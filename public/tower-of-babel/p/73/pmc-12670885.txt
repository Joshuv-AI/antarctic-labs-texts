Around the ThermoClock: A precision automated temperature control system for Ex Vivo circadian studies
Authors: Kaiyin Kelly Zhang, Dominic Locurto, Matthew Belden, Mark Price, Cathal J. Kearney, Meghan E. Huber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Temperature treatment is commonly used to manipulate circadian rhythms in cells and tissue cultures. However, it is often laborious and error-prone in prolonged studies. We present the ThermoClock, an Arduino-based temperature regulation system designed for precise, automated temperature control in ex vivo and in vitro studies, particularly circadian rhythm research. Built with off-the-shelf components and open-source software, ThermoClock is easy to fabricate, costing approximately $450 and requiring under 10 h to assemble. Its modular design enables simultaneous control of multiple conditions, reducing manual intervention and user error.Individual ThermoClock modules use a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller and off-the-shelf electronics to realize real time, precise temperature controls, while being cost-friendly and accessible to construct and operate.Assembled ThermoClock can operate up to five temperature modules, greatly enhancing experimental versatility and throughput. An Arduino script is provided to automate the temperature controls based on user-input temperature setpoint schedules.ThermoClock is designed to function in an incubator and shows significantly faster heating and cooling (p < 0.001) compared to a programmable incubator. It reaches the target temperature within five minutes after a setpoint change.

Background

Circadian rhythm is critical in maintaining homeostasis and regulating physiological functions via the rhythmic expressions of clock genes [[1], [2], [3]]. Disrupted circadian rhythms are linked to the pathogenesis of diseases, including cardiovascular disease, cancer, immune-related disorders, and pulmonary diseases [[4], [5], [6]]. Studies have also shown that oscillations of circadian genes in aged and diseased tissues are damped, implying that the lack of a robust circadian oscillation is an indicator of health problems [7]. Finally, there is substantial evidence for predictable time-of-day variations in physiological function and disease, which the pharmaceutical industry now integrates into treatment with chronotherapy (i.e., daily timed dosing) [5].

And yet, the majority of current biomedical research or therapeutic development studies are conducted on ex vivo models that are not circadian synchronized. In the absence of a hierarchical circadian system formed by central (in the suprachiasmatic nucleus) and peripheral (in peripheral organs) oscillators, cells in vitro become desynchronized from each other, leading to physiological readouts that may diverge from in vivo conditions [8]. This can jeopardize the accuracy and prospects of numerous biomedical technologies – including lab-on-a-chip and tissue engineering – and increases the financial and time costs for product development by forwarding or omitting candidate therapies untested under a circadian rhythm [9].

While synthetic glucocorticoids like dexamethasone have been used to reset the circadian time in explanted tissues and cell cultures [10,11], their effectiveness depends on glucocorticoid receptors availability. The potential toxicity and anti-inflammatory properties of dexamethasone also make it unsuitable for certain applications [12]. Meanwhile, temperature has been shown to be a potent and universal cue for resetting circadian rhythm in mammalian peripheral tissues [7,13,14]. There are two common temperature profiles used as thermal zeitgebers (‘time-givers’) for the circadian clock: rhythmic temperature cycling consistent with native body temperature cycles [14] and heat shock, which induces a series of acute protective responses to a proteotoxic event including resetting of the circadian clock [15]. Beyond circadian rhythms, heat pulse is also often needed to study heat shock proteins (HSPs) and heat shock factors (HSFs) [16], and to test heat-responsive drug delivery systems in vitro [17].

In both approaches for circadian rhythms, researchers need to set at least two incubators at different temperatures and manually alternate samples between incubators to achieve precise temperature steps — process that is labor-intensive, costly, and unsustainable for long-term studies. While programmable incubators are commercially available, they are costly and incapable of producing precise heat shock due to the long ramp time for heating and cooling.

Our aim was to develop an accessible temperature control system that can precisely automate thermal stimuli for cell culturing; this has applications for synchronizing of cultures to a circadian rhythm among other applications. In this study, we present the ThermoClock, a low-cost, open-source, Arduino-based platform that uses off-the-shelf components and is compatible with standard culture plates and dishes up to 40 mm in diameter. Compared to the expense of upgrading to commercially available systems, this system provides a much more economical solution for programmable temperature control. A distinctive feature of ThermoClock is its ability to assign specific temperature profiles to individual culture dishes – a function not found in existing products. Also, the system is fully cell culture compatible when operated within an incubator with active humidity and CO2 control. Designed to be easy to construct and integrate into standard laboratory equipment, ThermoClock offers flexible and precise thermal control, enhancing throughput for a wide range of cellular and tissue studies involving thermal stimuli.

Method details

System overview

The ThermoClock system presented here independently controls the local temperature of up to five multi-well culture plates, each following a user-defined temperature profile. The device is packaged in a custom machined acrylic ‘tray’ (467 mm W, 288 mm L, 25.5 mm D) that fits on a single shelf of a standard incubator. Each individual plate is equipped with its own temperature control module, consisting of a sensor (resistance temperature detector, RTD) and a resistive heating pad. Each temperature control module can be programmed to have its own temperature profile. The temperature regulation of all modules is managed by a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) feedback controller programmed on a microcontroller (Arduino Due), which is coupled to a micro-SD for data collection. The overall circuitry is assembled on a printed circuit board (PCB).

For each module i at time t, the controller calculates the error, ei(t) between the desired temperature Tdesired,i(t) and the measured temperature Tmeasured,i(t). This error, ei(t), is converted from a temperature range of −50 °C to 280 °C to a pulse-width modulation (PWM) signal, ui(t), in the range of 0 to 255 (i.e., duty cycle 0 to 100 %, which represents the percentage of a binary signal being active within a period). Note, e(t)and u(t) in Fig. 1 represent the vectors containing the ei(t) and, ui(t) from all modules. The PWM signal ui(t) drives a bipolar junction transistor (BJT), which modulates current ii(t) through the heating pad to control heat generation. The system logs data – t, Tdesired(t), and Tmeasured(t) – for all plates onto a microSD card for monitoring and analysis.Fig. 1ThermoClock system overview schematics. The microcontroller (Arduino DUE) receives the desired temperature vector, Tdesired(t), which contains setpoints of each temperature module, Tdesired,i(t). It also the measured temperature array, Tmeasured(t), which contains real-time temperature readings from each module, Tmeasured,i(t). The microcontroller calculates the error array, (t), which is the input to the proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control operating under the same gains for every module. The PID control output PWM signals, ui(t), which are sent to individual bipolar junction transistors (BJTs). BJTs regulate the current output, ii(t), to the heating pads, thereby controlling their temperatures and that of the well plates placed on top. The actual temperature of each module is measured using a resistance temperature detector (RTD), then sent back to the microcontroller for calculation and for logging onto a microSD card.Fig 1

Since the system is not equipped with gas or humidity regulation, it is recommended to use ThermoClock on cell cultures inside an incubator. Of note, the ThermoClock does not have an active cooling mechanism, thus the desired temperatures within your profile range should not be lower than the background temperature (e.g., temperature of the incubator housing the system).

Software

We developed a script using the Arduino Integrated Development Environment (IDE) to automate and centralize control over all ThermoClock temperature modules. The script is available in supplementary material and GitHub: https://github.com/umass-hrsl/ThermoClock.git. The script comprises three main sections:1.System Configuration: Defines constants, including pin assignments for RTD sensors and heating pads, PID gain values, and calibrated resistance values for RTDs.2.Temperature Schedules: Specifies desired temperatures and their corresponding time points.3.Control and Logging: Operates the control loop based on the schedules and logs measured and desired temperatures to a micro-SD card every 30 s.

To customize the script for a specific setup, users should update the pin assignments in Section 1 and the temperature schedules in Section 2. In Section 3, users must rename the data log file for each new experiment. Once the temperature schedules have been completed, the user can verify the performance by reviewing the text file that is saved to the in the microSD.

For calibration, place the ThermoClock in a well-calibrated incubator and run the script with a constant temperature setpoint significantly lower than the incubator's internal temperature (e.g., set the desired temperature to 30 °C when the incubator is at 36 °C). Once the incubator and sensor readings stabilize, adjust the RNOMINAL values (sensor resistance at 0 °C) for each module until the sensor readings match the incubator temperature. The system should be recalibrated each time when it is transferred to a new environment.

Method validation

We conducted three experimental tests to validate the system using temperature profiles that have previously been shown as effective in other circadian studies [7,14]. In all three tests, the system was fully enclosed in a standard incubator set at the lowest temperature in the profiles, and otherwise standard culture conditions were employed (5 % CO2 and 95 % humidity). In each experiment, system performance was evaluated for each module using three metrics:1.Steady-State Error was defined as the difference between the measured temperature and the programmed setpoint during the steady-state portion. We mainly focused on the error during active heating periods because the system does not include active cooling. However, steady-state errors during the cooling periods in Test 2 were evaluated to assess the impact of asynchronous heating between modules. For Tests 1 and 2, the steady-state temperature was defined as the average temperature during the final 30 min of each phase within the 72-hour cycle. For Test 3, it was defined as the average over the final 5 min of the 15-minute heat pulse. Of note, the incubator used in the tests for enclosure and comparison has a temperature fluctuation range of ± 0.1 °C and a uniformity of ± 0.3 °C within the chamber. Therefore, steady-state errors smaller than these values are considered acceptable.2.Heating Ramp Time was defined as the time required for the 10-minute moving average of the measured temperature to first reach and remain within ±0.5 °C of the steady-state temperature following a temperature increase (i.e., during heating periods). The moving average window began 2 min after the setpoint change to avoid underestimating ramp time due to initial transients.3.Cooling Ramp Time was defined as the time required for the 10-minute moving average of the measured temperature to first reach and remain within ±0.5 °C of the steady-state temperature following a temperature decrease (i.e., during cooling periods).

For statistical analysis, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to compare the means between individual modules, as well as between modules and the incubator control when applicable. Test 1: Synchronized Temperature Cycling Evaluation

The purpose of this test was to evaluate the system’s ability to deliver precise 24-hour entrainment cycles and benchmarked its performance against a commercial programmable incubator. The system was programmed to maintain 24-hour temperature cycles comprised of 12 h of 36 °C and 12 h of 38.5 °C for three cycles (i.e., 72 h total). This profile has been shown to be effective in entraining peripheral ex-vivo tissues [7,14]. We compared the performance of individual modules of the ThermoClock to an equivalently programmed commercially available incubator that enables users to preprogram temperature changes. The background incubator was set to 36 °C throughout the test.

As shown in Fig. 5A, all modules of the ThermoClock were able to respond to and maintain the temperature setpoints precisely following the input program. All ThermoClock modules showed a small steady-state heating error significantly below the range of temperature fluctuation in a typical incubator. An ANOVA revealed a statistically significant effect of module (ThermoClock modules 1–5 and incubator) on steady-state error during active heating (F(5, 12) = 4.125, p < 0.05). Tukey’s post-hoc tests showed that steady-state errors in Module 1,2, and 4 were significantly smaller compared to incubator steady-state error (Fig. 5C). Test 2: Independent Module Programming Validation

The purpose of this test was to validate that each module could be independently programmed to follow distinct temperature cycles, enabling simultaneous experiments under different entrainment conditions. To our knowledge, no existing device can simultaneously output multiple temperature trajectories within the same enclosure, thus no control is available for this test. The five-module system was programmed to maintain five different temperature cycles of varied period lengths and temperature steps simultaneously. These adjusted period lengths are used to entrain tissues and cell cultures to a non-24-hour period cycles to induce circadian disruption [11,12]. Thus, this setup mimics a scenario where a user wants a control plate (37 °C) and several other groups. All cycling groups were able to maintain at minimum three cycles of different periods (20-hour, 24-hour, 25-hour; Fig. 6A, B). The system was then programmed to deliver a temperature profile that applies a heat shock to cells by raising the temperature to 42 °C for 15 min and then returning itto 37 °C. The background incubator was set up at 37 °C throughout the test (Fig. 7A). Heat shock pulses to various temperatures have been shown to be effective in resetting the cellular circadian clock through the interactions of the activated heat shock proteins with circadian genes [18]. All ThermoClock modules reached the desired heat pulse temperature within an average of 2.5 min while the incubator was unable to reach the target temperature within 15 min (Fig. 7A). Major delays in response to setpoint change and overshoots were observed in the incubator, indicating that the incubator was unsuitable for performing heat pulses. Specifically, the incubator did not reach the setpoint during the observation period. As a result, the incubator data was excluded from the following statistical analysis.

A greater average steady state error, relatively to Test 1 and Test 2, was observed in the modules (−0.21±0.0192 °C), likely due to the rapid temperature change of 5 °C above background within 15 min – combined with the regulation by the enclosure incubator (Fig. 7B). A one-way ANOVA indicated a significantly smaller amplitude of error in Module 1 than in other modules (F(4, 10) = 13.17, p < 0.001). Overall, the steady-state heating errors are within the acceptable range. Similar to Test 1, significant differences in cooling ramp times between modules (F(4, 10) = 57.3, p < 0.001) were observed, with no significant differences in heating ramp times (F(4, 10) = 0.00, p = 1.00) (Fig. 7C).

Limitation

A potential limitation of the ThermoClock is the lack of an active cooling mechanism, which could impact applications with high sensitivity to the cooling procedure. This limitation could be overcome by including a thermoelectric (Peltier) cooling element. Alternatively, the background environment temperature can be set lower than desired system temperatures if faster cooling is needed. In the applications we have explored, this has not been necessary. For cell culture experiments, it is necessary to use the ThermoClock inside an enclosure with proper CO2 and humidity modulation to achieve optimal biological culture conditions. In our experience, we have not observed adverse effects on electronics over the course of use to date (∼1 year).

Conclusion

We present the design and development of a low-cost, Arduino-based temperature regulation system, ThermoClock, that enables convenient temperature treatments for ex vivo/in vitro studies involving circadian rhythm synchronization and entrainment, as well as other applications. The system uses off-the-shelf electronics, easy-to-machine hardware, and implements open-source libraries for the control software, making the system more accessible to researchers with little experience and knowledge in electrical engineering. Total build time for an inexperienced user is estimated to be under 10 h and cost at present market values were ∼$450.

Following successful fabrication, individual modules (up to 5 in this configuration) can be independently programmed to control temperature and time with high precision. We evaluated the accuracy and system response to setpoints commands via three metrics: steady-state error, heating ramp times and cooling ramp times. Each module of ThermoClock was equipped with PT100 RTDs (accuracy of ± 0.5 °C) and calibrated to the reading of the background incubator. When assessed against standard incubators, Thermoclock modules showed comparable or smaller steady-state errors during heating, confirming that the device can accurately operate within the intrinsic range of fluctuation of the background incubator. In tests against standard incubators, Thermoclock could reach and hold target temperatures in ∼10 % of the time it took the incubators. This rapid ramping enabled the ThermoClock to output short heat pulses — a temperature profile that couldn’t be achieved with the incubator. This improved performance is attributed to the heating source being localized close to the dish, whereas standard incubators need to heat the entire space. Additionally, our device offers the flexibility of independent control over each module – a functionality currently lacking in existing commercial products – allowing users to run both control and study groups in one trial. By contrast, individual incubators would need to be assigned for each desired profile in standard incubators. In test 2 and test 3, steady-state errors of the modules were consistently within the range of the background’s temperature fluctuation and variation, confirming both accuracy and stability of the system. Beyond the improved performance, there is the obvious advantage of significantly reduced cost, and not having to manually adjust incubators at defined times, helping to automate this aspect of experiments and limiting user-error.

ThermoClock can help researchers who are interested in exploring circadian rhythms in ex vivo and in vitro models to overcome many of the current challenges. Furthermore, it opens up avenues for experiments that were previously only achievable in vivo. In many 3D cell cultures and organ-on-chip studies, implementing the ThermoClock can mimic natural temperature oscillations and induce a circadian rhythm in the cultures, capturing the fourth dimension of time that is physiologically critical, yet sometimes overlooked in vitro/ex vivo studies. For example, we are now conducting studies that entrain various ex vivo cultures using ThermoClock and are observing promising results in rhythm synchronization and rhythm manipulations.

Beyond circadian research, ThermoClock can also be a powerful tool in studying heat shock responses and other temperature-dependent procedures. For other benchtop procedures (e.g., bacterial culturing), the system can be readily set up and used in an open space. The device can be easily transferred between different environments and does not require any permanent modifications to the system, making it a versatile tool. Implementation of the ThermoClock also increases experimental repeatability and reduces the impacts from larger incubator devices and manual manipulation variations.
