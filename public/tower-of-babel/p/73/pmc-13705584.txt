Mapping worker-led AI governance in the global cultural industries
Authors: Rafael Grohmann, Helena Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBetween 2022 and early 2026, hundreds of cultural workers’ organizations around the world have mobilized in response to generative AI. From the Hollywood strikes of 2023 to voice actors’ protests in Mexico City, from pan-European game workers’ declarations to the first workers’ appendix to Brazil’s national AI bill, these mobilizations are reshaping how AI is deployed across cultural industries. This article presents a tracker of worker-led AI governance, documenting 142 worker organizations in 34 countries across eight cultural sectors. The tracker’s evidence suggests that collective bargaining is one of eight repertoires documented in the field, and not the most frequent; that most organizations target the state rather than employers or AI companies; that workers’ claims cluster around compensation, consent and copyright, with unauthorized training on creative output as the most cited harm; and that voice acting, the sector with most presence of Majority World, mobilizes a cultural-identity register that tracks the sector’s position in global screen value chains.Keywords: worker-led AI governance, cultural industries, cultural labor, generative AI, collective action, global value chains, creative workers

Introduction

In May 2023, the Writers Guild of America opened a 148-day strike that produced the first enforceable collective agreement on generative AI. By the end of 2025, SAG-AFTRA had negotiated parallel terms for screen performers and video game workers, and the Alliance of Canadian Cinema, Television and Radio Artists had ratified a 3-year agreement cited as a template for AI performer protections. These North American agreements have become the main references for the scholarship on cultural workers and generative AI (Grohmann et al., 2025; Halperin and Rosner, 2025; May et al., 2026; Nielsen et al., 2026).

They are not, however, the whole field. In July 2025, hundreds of Mexican voice actors marched through Mexico City against unauthorized AI voice cloning (TechXplore, 2025); by February 2026, President Claudia Sheinbaum had submitted a legislative initiative recognizing the voice as an irreplaceable artistic tool (El Financiero, 2026). French game workers coordinated strikes against AI-driven layoffs, contributing to the first pan-European game workers’ declaration on AI (IWGB, 2025). The Association of Voice Artists of Mumbai documented members’ monthly project volumes falling from fifteen to six as AI voice cloning spread through the Indian industry in the absence of a national legal framework (The Hollywood Reporter, 2025). In Brazil, Dublagem Viva, association of the voice actors, submitted the first workers’ appendix to the national AI regulation bill (Campos Rocha et al., 2026; Dublagem Viva, 2024). In Hong Kong, the Labor Union of Dubbing assembled 180 signatories for a first collective position on AI voice cloning in the region (The Standard, 2026). Together with the Hollywood cases, these mobilizations in the cultural industries show that workers have been struggling to have a voice in the present and future of artificial intelligence in the world of work, in what we term as an ongoing process of worker-led AI governance (Campos Rocha et al., 2026; Grohmann et al., 2025), conceptualized in the next section.

This article presents a global tracker 1 of worker-led AI governance, documenting 142 worker organizations in 34 countries across eight cultural sectors. It presents the tracker as a mapping exercise that documents the geographic, sectoral, temporal, reportorial, and discursive contours of worker-led AI governance in the cultural industries. The tracker’s evidence suggests that collective bargaining is one of eight repertoires documented in the field, and not the most frequent; and that sectoral profiles differ along lines that track sectors’ positions in the global economy of cultural production and respective value chains.

The article contributes to scholarship on cultural workers and generative AI (Berg et al., 2025; Erickson, 2024; Halperin and Rosner, 2025), offering the first global mapping of collective responses to AI across eight cultural sectors and 34 countries, moving the analytical center of gravity beyond the North American paradigmatic cases that have dominated the literature, offering contribution to global perspectives on generative AI and cultural production (Poell et al., 2025). It also complicates and deepens the concept of worker-led AI governance by bringing global empirical substance.

Worker-led AI governance in the cultural industries

Research on generative AI in cultural industries has developed quickly since 2023, across three streams. The first analyzes AI’s impacts on cultural workers at the individual level, tracing anxieties, displacements and reconfigurations of creative labor (Berg et al., 2025; Erickson, 2024; Scasserra et al., 2025; Lee, 2024). The second examines copyright and policy debates around training data, authorship and remuneration across jurisdictions (Chateau et al., 2025; Kaye et al., 2025). The third, closest to the concerns of this article, examines workers’ collective responses to AI, and has concentrated on the 2023 Hollywood strikes as a paradigmatic case (Halperin and Rosner, 2025).

This article builds on the definition of worker-led AI governance advanced in Grohmann et al. (2025) and refined through (Campos Rocha et al., 2026): the capacity of workers, through collective forms of organizing, to intervene in and shape how AI is employed, managed and refused in the workplace, and, by extension, in the broader regulatory and public arenas in which AI is debated. It designates a collective and worker-centered intervention that draws on the institutional, structural, and discursive power resources available in a given context (Schmalz et al., 2018). The concept names an ongoing process in terms of “governance,” since they are struggling to establish terms of AI use at the workplace. Theoretically, this means, on the one hand, conceptualizing these worker struggles as taking shape in unfinished forms, and recognizing the need to understand these processes. On the other hand, by naming this as part of processes of “governance,” we join debates on platform and AI governance (Gorwa, 2019; Mao et al., 2025) to highlight that workers are not always heard in these discussions.

Cultural industries are a central site for understanding how workers push back on generative AI. They are among the sectors in which generative AI has been most rapidly and visibly deployed, in the form of text, image, voice and video generation systems. Cultural workers have also been among the first to organize collectively in response. The risk, however, given the visibility of a handful of cases, is that the analysis of worker-led AI governance becomes organized around a single paradigmatic case. The Hollywood strikes have functioned as such a paradigmatic case (Grohmann et al., 2025): a well-resourced sectoral union, exercising structural leverage over a global production chain, negotiating enforceable collective agreements on AI use in the workplace. This is only one possible configuration of worker-led AI governance in cultural industries, and it operates under conditions that most cultural workers worldwide do not share. Global perspectives on cultural production (Poell et al., 2025) emphasize that cultural industries are structured by global asymmetries, inequalities and differences that run through all dimensions of cultural labor, including worker-led AI governance: in the repertoires available to organizations, the institutional counterparts they can engage, and the framings through which AI harms are articulated. This article also joins scholarly contributions that the role of generative AI, rather than a complete disruption, has a series of continuities in cultural industries, with a historical role for technologies (Erickson, 2024).

Methods

The tracker documents worker-led organizations that publicly mobilized on generative AI in arts, culture and media – largely defined as cultural industries – between 2022 and March 2026. It was compiled from documents publicly available, such as organizational materials (union websites, collective agreement texts, policy submissions, press releases, open letters, manifestos), trade and general press in multiple languages, and gray literature from sectoral federations and collecting societies. This article reports on the tracker’s state as of early April 2026.

For each organization, the tracker records the main arguments the organization advances in relation to AI; the agreements or outcomes it has produced, where applicable; the negotiation type or repertoire of action it employs; and the date and location of the mobilization. Each entry has one or two key documents, and is supported by the organization’s official website and additional sources, including trade and general press. The full data collection, with the corresponding links for each case, was compiled in a spreadsheet. Both authors independently reviewed and validated the cases, and the verified dataset was published as a webpage within the project website Creative Labour & Critical Futures (CLCF): https://creativelabourcriticalfutures.ca/resource-files/tracker-ai-mobilization-clcf.html.

Organizations were included in the tracker on three criteria: they are organizing cultural workers collectively, whether in the more formal worker collectives, such as unions, or in more informal ways, in coalitions, campaigns and other worker collectives in arts, culture and media, broader understood as cultural industries; they have publicly documented mobilizations addressing generative AI; and they operate at the collective level. Relevant forms include unions, guilds, professional associations, collecting societies, cross-sector coalitions and formally constituted campaigns. Restricting the tracker to formally recognized trade unions would exclude Majority World 2 organizations, where collective action takes forms that do not always map onto Global North institutional models (Atzeni, 2021). The tracker covers eight sectors: film and television (34 organizations), music (28), voice acting (23), writing and publishing (16), visual arts (13), journalism (13), cross-sector initiatives (9), and video games (6).

The tracker was also designed with a participatory verification step. An initial version documenting 105 organizations was published openly in early March 2026 with a public-facing interface; all the organizations were contacted via email and invited to verify entries, suggest corrections and flag omissions. In the publication of the tracker, we invited people to submit suggestions for additions or edits. Responses produced corrections to specific fields and additions of organizations not initially captured, particularly in contexts where public documentation circulates outside the research team’s working languages. The procedure constitutes a form of participatory verification narrower than participatory action research in the full sense (Medrado and Verdegem, 2024), but consistent with our commitment to treating workers and worker organizations as research interlocutors. The tracker is also a resource for worker organizations, policy makers, and scholars.

Tracking worker-led mobilizations

The tracker documents 142 worker organizations in 34 countries across eight cultural sectors between 2022 and March 2026 (Figure 1). Film and television is the largest sector (34 organizations, 24%), followed by music (28, 20%), voice acting (23, 16%), writing and publishing (16, 11%), visual arts and journalism (13 each, 9%), cross-sector initiatives (9, 6%), and video games (6, 4%). Regionally, the field is unevenly distributed: Europe accounts for 41% of organizations, North America for 24%, Asia for 11%, Latin America for 11%, Oceania for 5%, Africa for 1%, and transnational initiatives for 7%. The United States leads with 24 organizations, followed by Canada (14), the United Kingdom (13), Spain (8), Japan (6), Australia, Germany, and France (5 each). Voice acting departs from the overall regional profile: 11 of its 23 organizations (48%) are in Latin America or Asia, against an average of 22% across the other sectors.

Geographic distribution of worker-led AI mobilizations in cultural industries, by region and country (N = 142, 2022–2026).

Temporally, the field grows sharply from 2022 to 2023 and sustains activity thereafter. The tracker records 6 mobilizations initiated in 2022, 58 in 2023, 43 in 2024, 30 in 2025, and 5 in 2026 (a partial year, until April). The 2023 peak follows the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 and the May–November 2023 Hollywood strikes, which gave international visibility to worker claims on AI.

Five of the six organizations mobilizing in 2022 are in visual arts, with the sixth in voice acting. The commercial releases of Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 2 in 2022 placed visual artists among the first cultural workers to confront large-scale unauthorized use of their creative output as training data. Mobilization around generative AI in the cultural industries, on the evidence of the tracker, began with visual artists responding to generative image systems in 2022, prior to the 2023 Hollywood strikes.

Each sector presents distinct characteristics, related to its history, identity, and positioning in relation to current debates around generative AI (Figure 2). Visual arts is the pioneer of the field, the only sector that mobilized at scale in 2022, before the Hollywood strikes, and the sector in which framings of generative AI as a system of unauthorized extraction remain most concentrated. Voice acting is the most regionally diverse sector and the only one in which cultural identity functions as a discursive resource in its own right; its profile is taken up at length below. Music is the most copyright-centered sector, organized through a dense infrastructure of collecting societies and licensing instruments. Film and television concentrates the largest share of bargaining activity and is the most institutionally consolidated sector in the tracker. Writing and publishing is the legal-action front of the field, with copyright litigation against large language model developers as its main repertoire (e.g., the Authors Guild class action in the United States and the SNE–SGDL–SNAC suit against Meta in France, data available in the tracker). Journalism articulates an editorial-ethical register, combining newsroom AI policies with lobbying activity. Video games center on advance notice, consultation and bargaining over AI deployment (as in ZeniMax Workers United and the French STJV). Cross-sector initiatives function as the field’s coordinating infrastructure, articulating claims across several sectors at once.

Sector profiles of worker-led AI governance in the cultural industries.

Organizations in the tracker combine repertoires of action (Figure 3). Policy and lobbying is the most common repertoire, engaged by 98 organizations (69%), followed by campaigns, awareness initiatives and petitions (77, 54%) and public statements, open letters and manifestos (64, 45%). Coalition and collective organizing is engaged by 56 organizations (39%), collective bargaining and agreements by 41 (29%), research and surveys by 14 (10%), strikes and protests by 13 (9%), and legal action by 12 (8%). The 142 organizations generate 375 action-instances, for an average of 2.64 repertoires per organization. Most combine two or three repertoires, and some combine five or more.

Repertoires of collective action in worker-led AI governance in the cultural industries.

Collective bargaining, a central instrument of traditional labor organizing, is engaged by 29% of the organizations documented here. It coexists with, and is often complemented by, forms of action that operate outside the direct employer-employee relation: policy submissions to state institutions, cross-sectoral coalitions, and public statements that intervene in the discursive construction of generative AI at work. This means that there are varieties of repertoires of worker-led AI governance, and worker collectives need to be analyzed not through frameworks of lack or absence (e.g., of strong collective bargaining) but through their own characteristics and frameworks. Collective bargaining requires a recognized bargaining unit, an employer willing or compelled to negotiate, and a legal framework that renders the resulting agreement enforceable. These conditions are unevenly distributed. The 20 formal collective agreements recorded in the tracker concentrate in film and television (9), journalism (3), voice acting (2), music (2), video games (2), and writing and publishing (2), and are located largely in North America or Europe. Other repertoires, such as public statements, manifestos, coalition-building and policy submissions, can be mobilized where formal bargaining is unavailable or weakened. The uneven distribution of repertoires mirrors the uneven distribution of institutional and structural power resources across the field (Schmalz et al., 2018; Campos Rocha et al., 2026).

This decentering of the direct worker-employer relation also appears in the data on where claims are directed, with an agenda more oriented toward states and governments, attempting to push for policy from below (de Peuter and Cohen, 2015) at national or local levels. Of the 142 organizations in the tracker, 85 (60%) direct claims at government institutions, legislators, or regulatory agencies. By comparison, 31 (22%) target studios or producers, 27 (19%) publishers, 25 (18%) AI companies directly, 23 (16%) platforms, 5 (4%) game companies, and 3 (2%) record labels. Targets are not mutually exclusive, as many organizations engage several at once. Workers engage the state, through legislative advocacy, policy submissions, public comment procedures, and coalition with government actors, more often than they engage any other institutional category. The policy orientation towards state and governments is uneven across sectors: voice acting, with limited bargaining access, engages the state at disproportionately high rates (Campos Rocha et al., 2026); music engages both the state and the courts; writing and publishing concentrates on legal action against large language model developers; Majority World organizations engage the state at higher rates than Global North organizations. This importance of states, governments and the policy level, on the one hand, reinforces the relevance of situating these mobilizations within debates on AI governance and, on the other hand, demonstrates the need for further research to understand these relations and workers’ power resources in relation to public authorities.

In terms of the worker demands and claims, compensation or pay is the most common, raised by 90 organizations (63%), followed by consent (84, 59%) and copyright or intellectual property (65, 46%) (Figure 4). Job loss and displacement are raised by 53 organizations (37%), transparency by 51 (36%), training data or data use by 33 (23%), likeness, voice, or image by 25 (18%), human oversight by 23 (16%), and explicit credit by 12 (8%). The cluster of compensation, consent and copyright functions as an important paradigm for the field. It circulates through transnational coordinating networks, such as SAG-AFTRA’s “Three Cs” (consent, compensation, control), the Graphic Artists Guild’s “Credit, Consent, Compensation,” CISAC’s “Authorization, Transparency, Remuneration,” and through comparable formulations in voice acting, music and writing. The three claims across the corpus indicate a shared vocabulary through which cultural workers, across sectors and regions, articulate their position on generative AI.

Dominant claims of worker organizations on generative AI in the cultural industries.

In terms of AI harms, unauthorized training on creative output is mentioned by 70 organizations (49%). Job displacement and automation, the next most cited, is raised by 35 (25%). Voice cloning without consent is cited by 19 (13%), digital replicas and deepfakes by 16 (11%), copyright infringement by 11 (8%), wage or royalty suppression by 4, erasure of authorship by 3, and biometric rights violations by 3. Unauthorized training is cited at roughly twice the rate of displacement and is the only harm cited by nearly half the field. This distribution complicates the framing of AI’s threat to cultural workers as one of replacement. The tracker registers this framing, but locates it as one concern among several rather than as the field’s center of gravity. Displacement is cited by a quarter of organizations, while the harm cited by nearly half is the use of creative work as training data without authorization. Worker mobilization in the tracker is oriented more intensively toward the upstream moment of data extraction than toward the downstream moment of output substitution, though both are present. Training data is also tractable through existing legal instruments, such as copyright, data protection, collective licensing. This framing therefore connects cultural workers’ mobilizations to wider debates on AI governance that exceed the bounds of sector-level labor disputes.

Sectoral variation also has a regional component. Taken as a whole, the field is concentrated in the Global North in absolute terms. The Majority World accounts for 23%, with 11% in Asia, 11% in Latin America, and 1% in Africa. Voice acting departs from this overall distribution. Of its 23 organizations, 6 are in Latin America, 5 in Asia, and 8 in Europe. The Majority World share is approximately 48%, against an average of 22% across the other seven sectors. Voice acting is the only sector in the tracker with a Majority World near-majority. All eight sectors are globally distributed, yet only one produces a more regional profile.

Position in global cultural value chains offers a way to analyze the pattern (Anwar, 2025; Poell et al., 2025; van der Vlist et al., 2024). Cultural production is organized through international division of labor that place different kinds of work activities in different positions within circuits of production and circulation, and these positions intersect with longer-standing patterns of dependency between anglophone cores and the regions that consume content in other languages (Mirrlees, 2025). Voice acting is in a particular intersection of these dependencies. It is subordinated from the vantage of the anglophone industries that originate the content, what Thomas (2024) describes as a “performer hierarchy” in which audio performers, and dubbing performers especially, are positioned beneath visual and “star” performers in the global circuit, and central from the vantage of the markets that consume that content in non-anglophone languages. Mexico, Argentina, and Colombia have served as the principal hubs of Latin American Spanish-language dubbing, with Mexico in particular operating as the longstanding center of dubbing for the region’s audiovisual market. Brazil dubs most of the international content consumed by Portuguese-speaking audiences, including across Lusophone African markets. Japan and South Korea anchor regional voice acting infrastructures in East Asia, including for anime. Voice acting is therefore a subordinated node in global screen value chains. These global positions account for the concentration of AI voice cloning pressure on voice actors outside the anglophone core. The technologies that threaten the labor of voice actors are produced in the anglophone industries that dubbing serves, and they are deployed in markets where dubbing is itself culturally constitutive.

Voice acting is the only sector in the tracker in which cultural identity function as discursive resources in their own right. Approximately 26% of voice acting organizations (6 of 23) frame AI voice cloning as an attack on cultural identity, linguistic heritage or collective memory, compared with 0% in music, writing, journalism, video games, and cross-sector initiatives, and under 10% in the remaining sectors. Dublagem Viva, the Brazilian association of voice actors, frames the displacement of Brazilian voice actors as an attack on collective memory, on the argument that voices associated with iconic characters over generations form part of national cultural heritage (Campos Rocha et al., 2026). AMELOC in Mexico positions the country’s share of Latin American dubbing output as a regional cultural resource. Doblatge Unida de Barcelona frames AI dubbing as a threat to Catalan cultural heritage. The framing is largely absent from anglophone North American voice acting mobilizations, which operate in the register of labor rights and intellectual property. Voice acting has also developed transnational coordinating infrastructures outside the anglophone axis. The United Voice Artists coalition, founded in 2023 and referenced by 10 of 23 voice acting organizations in the tracker, coordinates across Europe, Latin America, and Asia. While Campos Rocha et al. (2026) analyzed the case of Brazilian voice actors and the importance of discursive power, more research is needed to understand voice actors globally.

Therefore, the tracker documents a field in which collective bargaining is one repertoire among eight, in which the state is the principal counterpart of worker claims, in which the harm cited by nearly half the field is the use of creative work as training data, the main claims are around compensation and consent, and in which sectoral profiles track positions in global cultural value chains.

Conclusions

The tracker presented in this article is a snapshot of worker-led AI governance in the cultural industries as of April 2026, mapping a field that continues to develop quickly. The findings reported here are therefore offered as an initial cartography, opening lines for future research on the topic, especially because the tracker is a public resource for scholars, policy makers, and workers.

The first contribution of the tracker is to map the varieties of worker-led AI governance that have emerged across cultural industries worldwide. Across the 142 organizations documented, collective bargaining is one repertoire among eight and is not the most common. The 20 formal collective agreements on AI documented here, and the 29% of organizations engaged in bargaining activity of some kind, are part of a field in which 69% of organizations lobby government, 54% run campaigns, 45% issue public statements, and 39% coalition-build. Worker-led AI governance therefore takes plural forms, combining institutional, regulatory, and discursive modes of intervention in different proportions across different contexts. The field also offers empirical material for refining how the forms of power described in the power resources approach interact in practice (Schmalz et al., 2018). In a number of the cases mapped here, discursive power consolidates ahead of institutional and structural capacity.

The second contribution is to show how these varieties are patterned by position in global value chains. Sectoral profiles in the tracker differ along lines that track the positions that sectors occupy in the global economy of cultural production. Voice acting offers an interesting example, as framings generated in subordinated positions of the value chain circulate transnationally through coalitions like United Voice Artists. Visual artists, mobilizing in the absence of an identifiable employer counterpart, articulates AI as “extractivist.” In other words, these varieties of worker-led AI governance the tracker maps are shaped by the positions from which workers are located in global value chains.

The tracker also highlights a shared vocabulary of claims. Compensation, consent, and copyright are the three most cited claims of worker organizations on generative AI. The cluster circulates through transnational coordinating networks and through comparable formulations in voice acting, music, and writing. Its recurrence across sectors and regions indicates that worker-led AI governance is producing a normative grammar of its own: a way of articulating what is at stake in AI’s encounter with cultural labor that persists across the institutional and structural diversity the tracker shows. Some possible directions for future research involve understanding how this shared vocabulary evolves, how it interacts with regulatory and judicial processes already underway, and how it may broaden to include other claims, such as credit, transparency, oversight, and biometric protection.

As an initial mapping of worker-led AI governance in global cultural industries, the tracker has limits that are also openings for further research. African cultural workers appear in the tracker through only two organizations, both in South Africa. For us, this small coverage is not a simple reflection of low activity. Africa hosts a number of large and growing cultural industries, such as Nollywood, for instance (Simon and Sanson, 2025) and the contexts in which these industries operate may produce orientations toward generative AI that differ from the worker-oriented registers that dominate the tracker. Generative AI may be encountered, in some of these contexts, less as an object of worker collective contestation. Further research could examine whether and where this is the case, and what worker-led AI governance looks like under such conditions, questions that the present mapping opens but cannot answer. The tracker reports on publicly documented mobilizations, so organizations whose engagement with the topic is internal or whose visibility depends on languages not well represented in the source base may be under-represented. The article also does not address what these mobilizations have achieved in practical terms, which is a topic for future research.

The tracker offers an empirical foundation on which further research on cultural workers and generative AI can be developed. It maps the varieties of worker-led AI governance in the cultural industries between 2022 and April 2026, and indicates some patterns through which these varieties are shaped.
