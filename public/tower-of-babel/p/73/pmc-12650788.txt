Comparative outcomes of laparoscopic versus robotic-assisted laparoscopic techniques in the surgical staging of endometrial cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Atieh Karimzadeh, Ali Kazemi Abadi, Hani Ghadri, Sevil Hakimi, Leila Allahqoli, Veronika Günther, Afrooz Mazidimoradi, Hamid Salehiniya, Antonio Simone Laganà, Ibrahim Alkatout
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery (RALS) in endometrial cancer reduces blood loss and intraoperative injuries.•RALS lowers conversion to laparotomy and shortens hospital stay compared with conventional laparoscopy (CLS).•RALS requires longer operative time and is associated with higher short-term postoperative complications.•Substantial heterogeneity across studies highlights the need for high-quality randomized trials on RALS.Keywords: Conventional laparoscopic surgery, Robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery, Endometrial cancer, Complications

Introduction

The most prevalent gynecologic cancer is endometrial cancer (EC), which has a mortality rate of 5.1 % per 100,000 women in 2019 and is increasing by almost 2 % per year. Obesity, physical inactivity, and excess body weight are associated with more than 70 % of the cases (Eakin et al., 2023). Globally, there were 91,641 fatalities and 417,000 new cases of EC in 2020 (Mazidimoradi et al., 2024, Kivekäs et al., 2024). Patients with stage IVA and IVB have significantly worse outcomes, with five-year survival rates of only 17 % and 15 %, respectively (Mazidimoradi et al., 2024, Makker et al., 2021).

The primary treatment for early-stage EC typically involves surgical procedures such as total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO), and pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph node dissection (Wright et al., 2012, Abu-Rustum et al., 2023). Significant improvements in surgical methods for treating EC have been made during the last three decades. For EC staging procedures, a number of studies have shown that the laparoscopic approach has emerged as the preferred technique and is frequently considered as the gold standard. Compared to the conventional laparotomic approach, laparoscopy (LPS) has several advantages, such as less intraoperative blood loss, fewer postoperative complications, and shorter hospital stays. Notably, laparoscopy has been demonstrated to have comparable oncological results to laparotomy, confirming its efficacy in the treatment of EC (Mäenpää et al., 2016, Johansson and Chan, 2020, Fu et al., 2023, Bergstrom et al., 2018, Casarin et al., 2018, Mourits et al., 2010).

Since 2005, robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery (RALS), an advanced development of conventional laparoscopic surgeries (CLS), has been used in gynecological procedures to treat benign and malignant conditions (Xie et al., 2024 6). The primary robotic surgical system used in gynecological robot-assisted surgery is the Da Vinci® Surgical System, which was created by Sunnyvale, California-based Intuitive Surgical (Chandrakar et al., 2022). Surgeons can control robotic arms with this system's “master/slave” setup from a console that is located far away. Enhanced three-dimensional visualization, precise control of robotic arms with increased mobility, fatigue resistance, tremor reduction, improved surgeon ergonomics, and a relatively short learning curve are just a few of the noteworthy technical benefits that RALS offers. Its widespread use in a variety of thoracoabdominal surgical specialties has been made possible by these advantages (Moss et al., 2017 14, Lim et al., 2023). Given the increasing trend towards the use of RALS,our study aims to perform a comparative analysis of RALS and CLS in the management of EC, in order to better understand their relative efficacy, safety, and potential benefits for guiding future clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Protocol and guideline

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021) and Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) guidelines (Shea et al., 2017), and has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO number: CRD42024552197). Formal ethical approval was not necessary for this study.

Inclusion criteria

This study included all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies of women with EC, as defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-11 code 2C76 and confirmed through histological analysis, and compared surgical outcomes, intraoperative complications, and short-term postoperative complications of between RALS using the Da Vinci® Surgical System and CLS, both involving hysterectomy, BSO, and pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND), with or without para-aortic lymph node dissection (PaLND).

Exclusion criteria

The following types of publications were excluded from the analysis: case reports, case series, commentaries, studies published in languages other than English, editorials, letters, and articles lacking full-text availability. Additionally, studies that didn’t report the primary outcome (estimated blood loss or the duration of operation) or did not compare these outcomes between the RALS group and a control group of CLS were excluded. Furthermore, studies involving patients with gynecological indications other than the focus of the study were also excluded from the analysis.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were: (a) operative time, defined as the time (in minutes) from the initial incision to skin closure; and (b) estimated blood loss (EBL), measured in milliliter (ml) and recorded in the anesthesiology notes.

Additionally, the secondary outcomes included: (c) intraoperative complications: included conversion to laparotomy, urinary tract injury, gastrointestinal tract injury, nerve injury, and vascular injury; (d) short-term postoperative outcomes, included the length of hospital stay, the incidence of urinary tract infections (UTIs), wound infections, the need for blood transfusions, hernias, lymphoceles, vaginal cuff dehiscence, and thromboembolic events.

Search strategy

A computerized literature search was conducted using three electronic databases, PubMed, Scopus, and ISI Web of Science. The search used the keywords “Robotic Surgical Procedure”, “Endometrial Cancers” and “Laparoscopic Surgical Procedure” and encompassed studies published until June 2024. To optimize the search results, we employed MeSH keywords and Boolean operators (such as AND, OR). The reference lists of relevant papers were reviewed manually by one reviewer (A.K). For transparency and reproducibility, the search strategy and a comprehensive list of search terms can be found in Appendix 1.

Study selection

Following the elimination of duplicate articles, the eligibility screening process was conducted in two phases. First, H. Gh and A. KA evaluated the articles based on their titles and abstracts to determine their relevance to the research topic. Next, L. A. and A. K. carried out a comprehensive review of the full texts to further assess the articles' appropriateness for inclusion in the study. In instances of disagreement, a consensus was achieved through discussion. Each identified article was assessed using a standardized evaluation format that included criteria such as study design, methodologies, participant demographics, intervention specifics, and reported outcomes. To elucidate our selection process, we employed a PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1), providing a visual representation of how studies were screened and included in our review.

Data synthesis and extraction

The two reviewers (A. K and H. Gh) conducted an independent data collection process from the selected studies utilizing a tailored data extraction table created in Microsoft Excel 2016. The gathered data encompassed several essential components, systematically arranged to enhance understanding and facilitate comparisons. These components included demographic details (such as the study title, the name of the first author, publication date, country of origin, study design, surgical techniques (RALS versus CLS), type of the surgery (hysterectomy, BSO, and PLND, with or without PaLND), sample characteristics (including sample size, age (year), body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2), and past medical history), study-specific parameters (such as FIGO staging system, grading (well differentiated, moderately differentiated, poorly differentiated, unspecified), pathology findings (endometrioid serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma, adenosquamous, mucinous, sarcoma, mixed, unclassified), prior neoadjuvant treatments, and the number of lymph nodes dissected, and para-aortic and plevic lymph nodes dissected), as well as measured outcomes which covered operative time, estimated blood loss, and intra- and post operative complications. This data was organized and presented in tables, effectively summarizing both the characteristics of the studies and the extracted data.

Assessment of risk bias

In this study, we used the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) to assess the risk of bias in randomized controlled trials. RoB does not have specific predefined cutoffs but rather assesses the risk of bias in various domains of a study. For each domain, the RoB provides options to assign a judgment of “low risk,” “high risk,” or “some concerns” of bias (Sterne et al., 2019).

Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies. The quality of each study was rated using the following scoring algorithms: ≥7 points were considered as “good”, 2 to 6 points were considered as “fair”, and ≤ 1 point was considered as “poor” quality (Stang, 2010).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 4.2.3). Data were pooled using a meta-analysis focusing on the efficacy of RALS versus CLS both involving hysterectomy, BSO, and PLND, with or without PaLND in women with EC. A random effects meta-analysis was calculated due to expected heterogeneity was examined using the standardized mean difference (SMD) 95 % confidence interval [CI]), where SMD values of <0.2, >0.2 and <0.8, or >0.8 indicated small, moderate, or large effects, respectively (Cohen, 2013). Binary outcomes, such as the conversion to laparotomy or intraoperative complication were analyzed using pooled odds ratios (OR) and 95 % CIs. Between-study variability was compared with within-study variability (i.e., heterogeneity of effect size) using both the chi-squared (Q) test of heterogeneity and the I2 statistic, which measures the percentage of variation due to heterogeneity. An I2 value <25 % indicated low heterogeneity, whereas values between 35 and 50 % denoted moderate heterogeneity, and those above 50 % denoted high heterogeneity (Higgins, 2003). A forest plot was generated to visually present the data from the included studies.

For parameteres such as age, BMI, and number of dissected pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes, weighted mean was calculated. the mean values for each group were multiplied by their respective sample sizes and then divided by the total sample size. The pooled standard deviation (SD) for each group was calculated by combining the within-group variances, adjusting for the sample size, and accounting for the differences in means within each group. This method provided an estimate of the overall variability within each group, which was used to assess the spread and consistency of the data.

Possible publication bias was examined through visual inspection of funnel plots for all studies in all meta-analyses combined with the regression-based Egger test (Egger et al., 1997). All two-way statistical tests were considered with α = 0.05.

Results

Search results

In initial searches, 1299 publications across all the databases were found. 399 of which were duplicates. 704 publications were eliminated after a thorough screening of abstracts and titles based on factors like improper study design or lack of relevance to the research topic. Seven of the 196 articles that were originally identified for retrieval did not have full texts available. 22 studies failed to report the primary outcomes of interest after a thorough review of the remaining 189 articles, 94 studies were ruled out as they used study groups other than robotic versus conventional laparoscopy, 18 studies were eliminated since they included participants with conditions outside the purview of EC, and 22 studies failed to report the primary outcomes of interest (estimated blood loss or operative time). Furthermore, the surgical methods used in 34 studies did not meet the established inclusion criteria. At the end, 16 articles were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Mäenpää et al., 2016, Johansson and Chan, 2020, Chiou et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Romano et al., 2016, Hayashi et al., 2024, Gueli Alletti et al., 2016, Johnson et al., 2017, Mendivil et al., 2015, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010, Sun et al., 2021, Estape et al., 2012).

Synthesis of results

This systematic review encompasses a total of 16 studies involving 1343 patients in the RAL group and 1382 in the CLS group, from the years 2010 to 2023 (Mäenpää et al., 2016, Johansson and Chan, 2020, Chiou et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Romano et al., 2016, Hayashi et al., 2024, Gueli Alletti et al., 2016, Johnson et al., 2017, Mendivil et al., 2015, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010, Sun et al., 2021, Estape et al., 2012). Seven articles described patients who underwent hysterectomy, BSO, and PLND (Mäenpää et al., 2016, Johansson and Chan, 2020, Mendivil et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Romano et al., 2016, Hayashi et al., 2024, Gueli Alletti et al., 2016), while five outlined women who had hysterectomy, BSO, PLND, and PaLND (Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010). The study by Johnson et al. (2016, USA) included two subgroups based on surgical procedures: one group underwent hysterectomy, BSO, PLND and PaLND, while the other group underwent hysterectomy, BSO, PLND without PaLND (Johnson et al., 2017). In the study by Estape et al., patients in the RAL group underwent hysterectomy, BSO, and PLND, whereas patients in the CLS group had hysterectomy, BSO, PLND, and possibly PaLND (Estape et al., 2012). Patients characteristics

The meta-analysis included 11 studies reporting the mean age and BMI of the participants (Johansson and Chan, 2020, Estape et al., 2012, Chiou et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Romano et al., 2016, Hayashi et al., 2024, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010). The weighted mean age (± pooled SD) was 59.76 (11.22) years in the RALS group and 58.73 (12.08) years in the CLS group with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.54). The weighted mean BMI was slightly higher in RALS group (29.32 ± 8.50) compared to the CLS group (28.99 ± 8.34), although this difference was also not statistically significant (p = 0.49 and p = 0.63, respectively).

The meta-analysis included studies that reported the mean collected lymph node in participants (Chiou et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Hayashi et al., 2024, Lim et al., 2010, Estape et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011). The weighted mean (±pooled SD) of collected lymph nodes patients in RALS group was 24.21 (14.05), while in CLS group it was 25.47 (18.21), with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.66). Six studies reported the number of para-aortic lymph nodes collected (Johnson et al., 2017, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010). The weighted mean (± pooled SD) of para-aortic lymph in the RALS group was 8.64 (6.97) compared to 10.45 (9.17) in the CLS group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.68). Six studies also reported the number of pelvic lymph nodes collected (Johnson et al., 2017, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010) The weighted mean (± pooled SD) of pelvic lymph of patients in the RALS group was 21.09 (13.99), while in the CLS group it was 21.27 (14.11), with no statistically significant difference observed between the groups (p = 0.85) (Chiou et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Hayashi et al., 2024, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010).

Comparison of intraoperative outcomes of RALS and conventional CLS

The primary outcome of this systematic review was to compare operative time and estimated blood loss in two groups.

Comparison of operative time of RALS and CLS

A total of 2483 participants from 12 studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising 1225 patients in the RALS group and 1258 in the CSL group (Johansson and Chan, 2020, Chiou et al., 2015, Aiko et al., 2020, Hayashi et al., 2024, Johnson et al., 2017, Estape et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2012, Cardenas-Goicoechea et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2011, Jung et al., 2010, Lim et al., 2010). In the study by Johnson et al. (Johnson et al., 2017), operative times were reported separately based on the surgical approach, which led to the inclusion of two arms: (1) hysterectomy, BSO, PLND; and (2) hysterectomy, BSO, PLND with and without PaLND. The mean operative time was 170.04 min for the RALS group and 152.55 min for the CLS group. The data on operative time for both RALS and CLS are presented in detail in Table 2. The random-effects meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant SMD of 0.5 (95 % CI: 0.06 to 1.01; p = 0.0281), indicating that RALS was associated with a moderately longer operative time compared to CLS. However, substantial heterogeneity was detected among the included studies (I2 = 96.8 %, τ2 = 0.6727), suggesting considerable variability in effect sizes across studies, possibly due to differences in study design, patient populations, or surgical techniques (Fig. 2). The publication bias of the studies included in the operative time analysis is shown. While the bias estimate (−6.2059) suggests some degree of funnel plot asymmetry, the lack of statistical significance indicates that there is no strong evidence of publication bias or small-study effects in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Estimated blood loss of RALS and CLS

With 875 observations in the RALS group and 1029 in the CLS group, the meta-analysis consists of 8 studies encompassing 1904 observations (24,25,27,31–33,35,37). The mean estimated blood loss was 109.10 mL for the RALS group and 180.07 mL for the CLS group. Table 2 presents the estimated blood loss and study characteristics by individual study. The pooled SMD was −0.7 (95 % CI: −1.1 to −0.2; p = 0.0029), indicating a statistically significant reduction in blood loss in the RALS group compared to the CLS group. This moderate-to-large effect size suggests a clinically meaningful advantage for RALS. However, the I2 value of 93.9 % reflects substantial heterogeneity across studies, indicating considerable variability in effect estimates. The Q-test for heterogeneity was also significant (Q = 114.52, df = 7, p < 0.0001), supporting the presence of substantial inter-study differences (Fig. 3). The publication bias of the studies included in the estimated blood loss analysis shows that the bias estimate (6.2431) is positive, indicating a potential asymmetry in the funnel plot. However, because of the the small number of studies (k = 8) and the high standard error, the evidence remains weak and inconclusive (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Risk of bias assessment

Among the 16 studies for which risk of bias was assessed, 12 studies (70.6 %) were assessed as 'poor', indicating significant methodological weaknesses. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to compare RALS and CLS in terms of surgical outcomes, intraoperative and short-term postoperative complications. Our results showed that RALS was linked to fewer intraoperative complications, lower conversion rates, reduced blood loss, and a longer operating time. Nonetheless, it was observed that the RALS group experienced a higher rate of postoperative complications.

Since its introduction in 1992, the use of MIS for EC has grown in popularity. Research has demonstrated that MIS procedures show fewer postoperative complications and offer comparable oncological outcomes to open surgery (Corrado et al., 2015, Boggess et al., 2008). With its three-dimensional view, wristed instruments, and scaled movement capabilities, RALS is responsible for more than half of MIS procedures (Aiko et al., 2020). Robotic systems provide a stable platform that eliminates the challenges of manually maintaining instrument and camera positions, particularly in larger patients. Since receiving FDA approval for gynecologic surgery in 2005, these advantages have encouraged widespread adoption of robotic technology by gynecologic oncologists (Bell et al., 2008). Therefore; there has been a significant increase in the use of robotic technology, while CLS has seen a decline (Seror et al., 2014).

Limited randomized controlled trials have been performed to compare RALS with CLS for the treatment of EC. In a randomized controlled trial conducted by Corrado et al. (Corrado et al., 2015) which compared open surgery, laparoscopy, and robotic-assisted laparoscopy for EC, it was found that MIS outperformed abdominal surgery in terms of surgical outcomes. Within MIS, RALS demonstrated advantages over CLS in terms of intra- and post-operative complications, conversion rates, length of hospital stay, and re-interventions. However, RALS was associated with longer operative times, which runs counter to the results of Mäenpää et al.'s randomized controlled trial (Mäenpää et al., 2016). In their study involving patients with EC, it was observed that RALS was actually quicker to perform compared to CLS. Conversely, several retrospective studies indicate that surgery typically takes longer in RALS compared to CLS for EC (Boggess et al., 2008, Bell et al., 2008, Seror et al., 2014).When directly comparing RALS and CLS, Ind et al. found that the former had fewer conversions, shorter hospital stays, less blood loss, and fewer overall surgical complications than the latter (Ind et al., 2017). These results were consistent with a network meta-analysis and systematic review by Natarajaan et al., which included 99 studies involving 181,716 women and assessed 14 outcomes (Natarajan et al., 2024).

It is important to note that previous studies in the literature have often pooled outcomes from heterogeneous surgical procedures; for instance, combining hysterectomies performed with or without BSO, and with or without lymphadenectomy. Each of these variations can independently influence surgical outcomes, thereby introducing confounding factors (Barrie et al., 2016). To minimize such variability, our systematic review and meta-analysis specifically included studies in which patients underwent a uniform surgical approach consisting of hysterectomy with BSO and PLND, with or without PaLND. This allowed for a more precise assessment of the relative effects of RALS versus CLS without the confounding influence of differing surgical scopes or procedures. The surgical methods reviewed in this analysis are consistent with the previous accepted standard of care. It should be acknowledged that current practice has shifted toward the use of sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) as the preferred staging method, as recommended by recent ESGO and NCCN guidelines, due to its lower morbidity and shorter operative time. However, the body of literature available for inclusion in this analysis contained insufficient studies employing SLNB to enable a meaningful comparison. Consequently, we focused on the traditional comprehensive staging approach (hysterectomy + BSO ± PLND/PaLND) to ensure methodological consistency and comparability across studies. As a result, our pooled estimates for operative time, blood loss, and complications may be higher than what would be expected with contemporary SLNB-based surgical protocols.

However, our study has several limitations. Firstly, the choice between CLS and RALS may have been influenced by patient characteristics like advanced disease stage, surgeon skill, and institutional resources, which could cause selection bias or affect case allocation. These biases could affect the pooled data. For instance, despite fewer intraoperative injuries and conversions, our study showed that the robotic group demonstrated a higher pooled postoperative complication rate. This finding may be partly attributable to the biases mentioned above. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of most included studies may have introduced recall bias in reporting self-limiting complications and documentation bias in recording postoperative events, potentially inflating the observed complication rate in the robotic cohort.

Temporal heterogeneity among studies [2010–2023) represents another limitation. Earlier studies often used first-generation robotic systems and pre-ERAS perioperative pathways, whereas later studies reflect modern instrumentation and enhanced recovery protocols. These differences likely influenced operative times and surgical outcomes. Now a days, robotic-assisted procedures are increasingly performed on an outpatient basis in some centers, the pooled mean length of stay of approximately 2–3 days in our review likely reflects variability in institutional discharge protocols, patient comorbidities, and the inclusion of comprehensive staging procedures. In addition, many studies were conducted before the widespread adoption of enhanced-recovery-after-surgery (ERAS) pathways, which today permit earlier discharge for suitable patients (Altman et al., 2019).

Significant heterogeneity in outcomes, such as operative time (I2 = 96.8 %) and estimated blood loss (I2 = 93.9 %), as well as a high risk of bias—more than 70 % of the included studies were rated as “poor” in methodological quality—are among the study's limitations. Given the variability acrossstudies, the overall result should be interpreted with caution, as different studies may yield different outcomes.

Because most available data are retrospective and based on earlier robotic platforms, further high-quality prospective evidence is needed. A large RCT directly comparing modern robotic and conventional laparoscopic systems would provide the most definitive evidence, but such a study may be challenging to implement given surgeon expertise, equipment availability, and ethical considerations once proficiency is established. Alternatively, prospective multicenter registries and pragmatic comparative-effectiveness studies could yield timely, real-world insights into outcomes, costs, and patient-reported recovery. Furthermore, additional studies with more comprehensive patient-level data—including age, prior surgical history, and lymphadenectomy details and surgeon experince—are warranted.

Conclusion

Because RALS provides improved visualization without sacrificing oncological results, its use as an extension of MIS has been increasing in the field of gynecological oncology. According to the results of our study, RALS is linked to fewer intraoperative complications. Additionally, our findings indicate that RALS procedures typically require more time. To get a more complete picture of the real comparison between RALS and CLS, future research should try to address potential confounding factors like the type of surgery.
