Broadly potent spike-specific human monoclonal antibodies inhibit SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sub-lineages
Authors: Melanie R Walker, Alexander Underwood, Kasper H Björnsson, Sai Sundar Rajan Raghavan, Maria R Bassi, Alekxander Binderup, Long V Pham, Santseharay Ramirez, Mette Pinholt, Robert Dagil, Anne S Knudsen, Manja Idorn, Max Soegaard, Kaituo Wang, Andrew B Ward, Ali Salanti, Jens Bukh, Lea Barfod
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continuous emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern has rendered many therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) ineffective. To date, there are no clinically authorized therapeutic antibodies effective against the recently circulating Omicron sub-lineages BA.2.86 and JN.1. Here, we report the isolation of broad and potent neutralizing human mAbs (HuMabs) from a healthcare worker infected with SARS-CoV-2 early in the pandemic. These include a genetically unique HuMab, named K501SP6, which can neutralize different Omicron sub-lineages, including BQ.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1, by targeting a highly conserved epitope on the N terminal domain, as well as an RBD-specific HuMab (K501SP3) with high potency towards earlier circulating variants that was escaped by the more recent Omicron sub-lineages through spike F486 and E484 substitutions. Characterizing SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific HuMabs, including broadly reactive non-RBD-specific HuMabs, can give insight into the immune mechanisms involved in neutralization and immune evasion, which can be a valuable addition to already existing SARS-CoV-2 therapies.Subject terms: Molecular medicine, Immunology

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2, the etiological agent of coronavirus disease (COVID-19), has caused one of the most challenging pandemics in recent history, resulting in an estimated >7 million deaths since it emerged in Wuhan, China at the end of 20191–3. Throughout the pandemic, variants of concern (VoCs) (including Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta and most recently Omicron) have emerged and posed new challenges such as reduced susceptibility to vaccine induced immunity, escape from therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), and increased transmission rates4–9. Many of the substitutions present in these VoCs are located in the ~22 kDa receptor binding domain (RBD) of the spike protein, which is the primary target of protective antibodies and facilitates viral entry by interacting with angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) on host epithelial cells10–12. Compared to the Wuhan-Hu-1 strain, the Omicron BA.1 variant includes 15 substitutions in the RBD, many of which have been associated with immune evasion13–16. Furthermore, a diverse array of Omicron sub-lineages, including BA.2, BA.2.75, BA.5, BQ.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86, and JN.1, have continuously emerged. These sub-linages have additional spike substitutions which have resulted in resistance to neutralization that could lead to increased hospitalization and deaths17–19. Currently circulating sub-lineages include BA.2.86, which was first observed in August 2023, and gave rise to the JN.1 sub-lineage. By December 2023, JN.1 exhibited a sharp increase in frequency in Europe and the United States of America, becoming the dominant variant globally20–23.

Early in the pandemic, RBD-specific mAbs were used as an effective countermeasure to avoid further disease progression in those with severe COVID-1924–26. These mAbs have been found to target four regions by the Barnes classification and are divided into four classes; class 1 and 2 mAbs compete for binding with the ACE2 receptor, whereas class 3 and 4 mAbs bind distant from the RBD/ACE2 interacting surface9,26. Additionally, the epitopes of the class 1 and 4 mAbs require the RBD to be in an ‘up’ conformation, whereas epitopes of class 2 and 3 can bind irrespective of conformation9,26. As VoCs with substitutions in the RBD have continued to emerge, loss of mAb neutralizing activity and reduced efficacy of the current approved vaccines has been observed9,18,19,27–29. To date, there are no clinically authorized therapeutic antibodies effective against the BA.2.86 and JN.1 sub-lineages, as the only Omicron approved therapeutic mAb, Bebtelovimab, was found to be completely ineffective to both18,19,27–29. Thus, potent broadly neutralizing mAbs targeting conserved areas in the spike protein are urgently needed. Several antibodies have been found to target the N-terminal Domain (NTD) of the spike protein, primarily a single supersite consisting of variable loops flanked by glycans30. However, little is known about anti-NTD antibody responses or if highly conserved anti-NTD epitopes exist.

Here, we isolated and characterized cross-reactive human mAbs (HuMabs) from a Danish healthcare worker infected with SARS-CoV-2 early in the pandemic31. We report the isolation of broad and potent neutralizing HuMabs, including a novel and unique NTD-specific HuMab, which can neutralize Omicron sub-lineages BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 by targeting a highly conserved epitope. Furthermore, using a potent HuMab, we determine the structural basis of escape from BA.5 via cryo-EM and SARS-CoV-2 reverse-genetics analysis.

Results

Isolation and characterization of spike-specific SARS-CoV-2 HuMabs

In previous work, we screened plasma samples obtained early in the pandemic (March 2020) from 165 convalescent Danish healthcare workers for SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific IgG and spike-RBD/ACE2 binding inhibition31. Through this analysis we selected one subject (K501) who had spike-specific IgG, RBD-specific IgG and spike-RBD/ACE2 inhibition (Figure S4). Based on these data, PBMC’s were collected at the September 2020 time-point with the aim of isolating spike-specific HuMabs.

A total of seven SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific HuMabs were isolated using EBV immortalization and their binding to the target antigen was confirmed by ELISA (Fig. 1A)32. To characterize the ability of the anti-spike HuMabs to block ACE2 binding to the spike protein, the HuMabs were screened for in vitro inhibition and IC50 was determined (Fig. 1B and Fig. 1C). Four of the seven HuMabs (K501SP2, K501SP3, K501SP4, and K501SP5) competed with ACE2 for binding. No spike/ACE2 inhibition was observed with HuMabs K501SP1, K501SP6 and K501SP7.

Binding and competition of HuMabs specific for SARS-CoV-2 spike.

Seven HuMabs (each represented by a different color as shown in the key) were isolated from subject K501 and spike-specific binding by ELISA (A) and competition with ACE2 (B) were performed and inhibition (IC50) potency determined (C). HuMabs were sequenced and germline usage is shown in (D).

Sequence analysis of the seven HuMabs revealed a polyclonal response with all HuMabs being derived from different germlines (Fig. 1D and Table S1). Four HuMabs had kappa light chains (K501SP1, K501SP2, K501SP3, and K501SP5) whereas three HuMabs had lambda light chains (K501SP4, K501SP6 and K501SP7) (Table S1). Similar to previous studies33, the HuMabs isolated here had relatively low levels of somatic mutation which ranged between 92.4% and 96.9% identity to germline with CDRH3 length that ranged from 11 to 23 a.a. (Table S1). For the light chains, somatic mutation ranged from 94.1% to 98.6%.

RBD-specific HuMabs have cross variant binding and inhibition

The majority of the protective antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 have been shown to target the RBD and inhibit ACE2 binding. Therefore, RBD-specific binding ELISAs and ACE2 competition assays were performed on the WT variant of the virus to assess whether our spike-specific HuMabs bound to and inhibited the RBD from binding to ACE2. The same four HuMabs that inhibited spike/ACE2 binding (K501SP2, K501SP3, K501SP4 and K501SP5) bound to WT RBD (Fig. 2A) and inhibited its binding to ACE2 (Fig. 2B). No WT RBD binding or ACE2 binding-inhibition was observed with HuMabs K501SP1, K501SP6 and K501SP7, indicating that these HuMabs are not RBD specific and bind elsewhere on the spike protein.

Next, to assess the variant transcending capacity of the RBD-specific HuMabs, RBD-specific binding ELISAs and ACE2 competition assays were performed on Alpha, Beta, Gamma and Omicron (BA.1) variants. All four HuMabs bound to and competed with the Alpha variant, however, inhibition was reduced when compared to WT RBD (Figs. 2A, B). Only HuMabs K501SP3 and K501SP4 bound to the Beta, Gamma and Omicron variants, and competed with the ACE2 binding (Figs. 2A, B and S5), but with a reduced binding capacity compared to the WT (Fig. 2C). Overall, HuMab K501SP3 had the broadest and most potent inhibition capacity when analyzed in our RBD/ACE2 inhibition assay and therefore we measured binding affinity for this HuMab.

To assess the binding affinity of K501SP3, HuMab/RBD-binding was tested in a concentration titration series using a QCMbiosensor (Fig. 2D and S6) for the WT and the four VoCs. HuMab K501SP2 was also tested as a binding control. The results confirmed the binding and inhibition data above and showed that K501SP3 bound to all the VoCs tested in nM range, however with decreased affinity when compared to the WT where affinity was in the pM range (Figs. 2D and S6). As expected, HuMab K501SP2 bound to the WT and Alpha variant only.

Taken together, our data reports the discovery of a highly potent RBD-specific HuMab with the ability to bind and inhibit four VoCs. Furthermore, three spike-specific HuMabs (K501SP1, K501SP6 and K501SP7) that are unable to bind to RBD or inhibit RBD/ACE2 binding were found.

Spike-specific HuMabs have cross variant and Omicron sub-lineage neutralization

Encouraged by the RBD/ACE2 inhibition breadth and potency results presented above, and to determine whether both our non-RBD and RBD-specific HuMabs had broad and potent virus neutralization capacity, we next examined breadth of neutralization for each of the seven HuMabs. Neutralization was performed against ten SARS-CoV-2 isolates representing the WT D614G, Alpha, Delta and seven of the Omicron sub-lineages (BA.1, BA.2, BA.5, BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1), four of which (BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1) became dominant while undertaking this study. The analysis showed that HuMab K501SP6 had the broadest neutralizing capacity, since it neutralized all isolates tested including the more recent Omicron BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 isolates. These isolates could not be neutralized by HuMabs SP1, SP2, SP3, SP5 or SP7 nor the control HuMab Bebtelovimab at 10 µg/mL (Fig. 3, Table S2). Furthermore, the IC50 value of K501SP6 was higher than the other neutralizing HuMabs, with an average IC50 of 15.3 µg/mL across all variants tested (Table S2). HuMabs K501SP1 and K501SP7 did not neutralize any of the isolates at the dilutions tested. HuMabs K501SP2, K501SP3, K501SP4 and K501SP5 could effectively neutralize the WT D614G, Alpha and Delta isolates (Fig. 3, Table S2). However, HuMabs K501SP2 and K501SP5 were not found to neutralize any of the Omicron sub-lineage isolates at 100 µg/ml. K501SP4 was found to neutralize BA.2, BA.5, BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 Omicron isolates, albeit with an overall less potent average IC50 (28.6 µg/mL) than K501SP6. K501SP3 could effectively neutralize isolates representing initial variants tested but not the more recent variants BA.5, BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 isolates, which were not observed to be neutralized at 100 µg/ml.

Overall, HuMab K501SP3 was found to be potent, supporting results observed above using the RBD/ACE2 inhibition assay, however, this antibody failed to neutralize newer circulating variants. In conclusion, non-RBD specific HuMab K501SP6 was the broadest HuMab, inhibiting all isolates tested including more recent and currently circulating BQ.1.1, XBB.1, JN.1 and BA.2.86 variants.

Identification of epitopes targeted by K501SP6 and K501SP3

Non-RBD-specific HumAb K501SP6 showed broad neutralization towards all SARS-CoV-2 variants and Omicron sub-lineages tested including the currently circulating JN.1. To understand the structural basis for broad recognition of HumAb K501SP6, we solved the Cryo-EM structure of the Fab region of K501SP6 in complex with spike Omicron BA.1 (Fig. 4A, B). K501SP6 was found to bind the NTD of the spike protein through the CDRH3, L1, and L2 loop. The interaction was mediated through numerous hydrophobic and hydrogen-bond interactions between CDRH3, L1, and L2 of K501SP6, and the NTD (Fig. 4C and S2). CDRH3 interacted with residues stretching from a.a.163 to 170 [168-175] (residues are shown according to BA.1 numbering and WT numbering is shown in square brackets) of the NTD (β-sheet 10 and the start of the N4 loop), as well as Asn123 [125] (β-sheet 9) and Asp225 [228] (β-sheet 13). CDR L1 interacted with Gln168 [173] (β-sheet 10), and CDR L2 with Thr122 [124] (β-sheet 9), Gln168 [173] (β-sheet 10), and Pro169 [174] (N4 loop) (Fig. 4C and S2). Further, Tyr49 of the K501SP6 light chain interacted with Thr122 [124] (β-sheet 9). The HumAb had a buried surface area (BSA) of 867Å2 with a single protomer, with the heavy chain as the primary contributor with 691Å2. The BSA of the HumAb to the full spike trimer was 1450Å2, with the increased area being due to interactions between the HumAb and spike subdomain 1 (SD1) of protomer 2 (Fig. 4A and B). Notably, the HumAb therefore potentially has a tertiary protein structure epitope. However, insufficient resolution was reached to describe the SD1 epitope in detail. A logo plot based on the sequences of the spike proteins tested in neutralization assays revealed a complete conservation of the NTD, explaining the ability of K501SP6 to neutralize every tested variant (Fig. 4D).

Two other mAbs have been identified and structurally characterized that bind a similar quaternary epitope spanning the NTD and SD1, namely Liu_12-16 and Liu_12-1934. Their CDR H3 loops are characterized by interacting with β-sheet 13 of the NTD and the crevice between SD1 and the NTD. In comparison, the CDRH3 loop of K501SP6 is further away from the crevice and closer to β-sheet 10 and 13.

Given the potency of neutralization observed towards earlier circulating variants by HuMab K501SP3, further investigation was conducted into the observed BA.5 neutralization resistance. Firstly, to confirm that the neutralization observed was directed to the RBD, we performed virus neutralization assays using a panel of mutant viruses exhibiting the RBD of either the Alpha, Delta and Omicron BA.1, BA.2 or BA.5 variants. Neutralization of these viruses by K501SP3 showed comparable IC50 values to the respective variant culture isolates, and no neutralization of the BA.5 mutant was observed, validating that the substitutions in the BA.5 RBD as responsible for preventing neutralization by K501SP3 (Fig. 5A and Table S3). Compared to the Alpha, Delta, BA.1 and BA.2 spike proteins, the BA.5 spike protein contains substitution F486V. To investigate if this specific residue was responsible for abolishing the neutralization from K501SP3, neutralizations with four additional mutant viruses containing E484A (which is present in all Omicron sub-lineages and is associated with escape from mAbs35) were performed, which included F486V, E484A and F486V or the complete BA.5 spike without F486V (reversion to V486F). Neutralization of these viruses by K501SP3 showed that, compared to the WT D614G isolate, the IC50 values for the E484A, F486V and E484A + F486V mutant viruses were 2.5-fold, 7-fold, and 44-fold higher, respectively (Fig. 5B and Table S3). For the BA.5 mutant with a V486F reversion, neutralization was now possible with an IC50 comparable to the WT D614G isolate (Fig. 5B and Table S3).

Next, to understand the structural basis of both the potent neutralization, and resistance to BA.5 observed in HuMab K501SP3, we resolved a low-resolution Cryo-EM electron density map of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 spike trimer in complex with HuMab K501SP3 (Fig. 5C). Spike trimer model PDB:7DK6 was docked into the map using ChimeraX and Phenix used to fit the RBDs into the electron map densities. In the trimeric structure, the RBDs of subunits 1 and 2 were observed to be in “up” conformation with each binding to a Fab region of HuMab K501SP3. The RBD of subunit 3 was observed to be in ‘down’ conformation (Fig. 5C). Although the resolution was insufficient to map the K501SP3 contact point, map density in that region shows that the Fab regions of K501SP3 are bound on top of the RBDs particularly in the receptor binding motif (RBM) (438–506 a.a.), which mediates binding of the spike protein to human ACE2.

Together, these observations are consistent in showing that K501SP3 is bound to the RBM and that the mutations F486V and E484A are essential for neutralization resistance, with F486V having a major impact. Furthermore, these properties are suggestive of a Barnes classification class 1 binding antibody36.

Genetic analysis of HuMabs K501SP3 and K501SP6

To further characterize our broadest (K501SP6) and most potent (K501SP3) HuMabs, we investigated whether the genetic characteristics of each of these HuMabs were unique. The coronavirus antibody database (CoV-AbDab) was used to compare the sequences of our HuMabs to 12916 entries of antibodies known to bind SARS-CoV-237. Sequence analysis of HuMab K501SP3 revealed that it was derived from an IGHV1-58 heavy chain gene and an IGKV3-20 light chain with a CDRH3 region of 16 a.a. in length (Table S1). In contrast, HuMab K501SP6 was derived from an IGHV4-39 heavy chain, an IGLV1-40 light chain and a CRHR3 region of 21 a.a. Of the 12916 SARS-CoV-2 antibody sequences banked in the CoV-AbDab database, 105 use the K501SP3 combination of IGHV1-58 and IGKV3-20. Further analysis revealed that 81 antibodies had identical IGHV, IGHJ, IGLV, IGLJ gene usage and CDRH3 length to K501SP3 (Fig. 6A and Table S4). The closest antibody (P008-08138) shared 86% a.a. sequence identity in their heavy and light chains.

For HuMab K501SP6, a total of 49 antibodies used the combination of IGHV4-39 and IGLV1-4037. We were not able to identify any antibody with identical IGHV, IGHJ, IGLV, IGLJ gene usage and CDRH3 length to HuMab K501SP6. On further examination two antibodies with the same IGHV, IGLV and CDRH3 length as HuMab K501SP6, but alternate IGHJ and IGLJ usage, were identified. Another six antibodies with identical IGHV, IGHJ, IGLV, IGLJ gene usage, but different CDRH3 length, were also found (Fig. 6B and Table S4).

Comparison of HuMab K501SP3 with the emergency use approved therapeutic mAbs revealed that HuMab K501SP3 had the same combination of IGHV1-58 and IGKV3-20 as well as CDRH3 region length as Tixagevimab (COV2-2196) (Fig. 6C)39,40. The sequence identity to Tixagevimab for the heavy chain was 78.2% and for the light chain was 89.2%. No FDA approved therapeutic mAbs were found to have the same heavy and light chain genes as HuMab K501SP6.

Due to the breadth of neutralization and genetic uniqueness of HuMab K501SP6 the CoV-AbDab was used to search for HuMabs that target the NTD area of the spike protein and neutralize the Omicron sub-lineages BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1. Two other NTD specific HuMabs (C1717 and Liu_12-19) that neutralize both BQ.1.1 and XBB.1 were found. C1717 targets the NTD-SD2 and utilizes IGHV1-69 and IGLV1-4419,30 whereas Liu_12-19, discussed above, targets the NTD-SD1 and utilizes IGHV3-33 and IGKV2-2934.

Overall, limited antibodies with similar genetic characteristics to HuMab K501SP6 were observed, indicating genetic uniqueness. Furthermore, we found that HuMab K501SP3 gene usage and CDRH3 length are common for RBD specific HuMabs.

Discussion

To date, Omicron sub-lineages have reduced or abrogated sensitivity to neutralization by all emergency use authorization (EUA) mAbs41. Here, broad and potent neutralizing HuMabs against SARS-CoV-2 were isolated from a healthcare worker sampled in 2020, before the emergence of VoCs. Among these, HuMab K501SP6 showed broad neutralization against previous VoCs and Omicron sub-lineages, including recent and currently circulating BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 variants. This HuMab had unique genetic features and targeted a highly conserved epitope on the spike NTD. A second HuMab K501SP3 was identified, which had potent neutralization capacity against the earlier VoCs, but was escaped by BA.5, BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 Omicron sub-lineages. Through structural and experimental analysis, we show that this HuMab binds to the RBM and is evaded by BA.5 through substitution F486V and E484A, with the former having the biggest impact.

Overall, seven HumAbs (four RBD-specific and three non-RBD specific) were isolated and analyzed via virus neutralization assays. Interestingly, two of the seven isolated non-RBD specific HuMabs (K501SP4 and K501SP6) retained neutralization activity against BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 Omicron sub-lineages with similar potency across the variants. However, both of these HuMabs had relatively low potency when compared to previously published broadly reactive HuMabs, as has been previously described with non-RBD specific mAbs6,30. With the exception of the BA.5 and variants emerging after BA.5 (BQ.1.1, XBB.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1), RBD-specific HumAb K501SP3 could effectively neutralize a range of isolates quite competitively in regard to recently described antibodies19,39. Furthermore, SPR analysis showed that HuMab K501SP3 binds with high affinity, in the pM and nM range, to each VoC tested33,42.

Using structural analysis, we found that K501SP6 targets the NTD region of spike through CDR H3, L1, and L2 loop and binds to highly conserved residues Thr122, Asn123, Asp225 and residues spanning a.a.163 - 170 of the NTD. Furthermore, K501SP6 interacts with SD1 of protomer 2 and therefore potentially has a tertiary protein structure epitope. However, insufficient resolution was reached to describe the SD1 epitope in detail. This HuMAb is unique in its binding epitope on the NTD and its interaction with the SD1 of another promoter. Several studies have found that mAbs that target the NTD region have broad neutralization19,30,43 with the majority of NTD mAbs targeting the NTD supersite at the N1 (residues 14–26), N3 (residues 141–156), and N5 (residues 246–260) loops44. Interestingly, none of these areas overlapped with the K501SP6 binding site. However, the epitopes of neutralizing mAbs Liu_12-16 and Liu_12-1945 were found to be closely related to K501SP6 in that they bound to a quaternary epitope consisting of the NTD of one protomer and the SD1 of an adjacent protomer. Liu_12-16 and Liu_12-19 have both shown in vivo efficacy in an established Syrian hamster model and the neutralizing effect of Liu_12-16 and Liu_12-19 were shown to be due to steric hindrance of the RBD in the ‘up’ conformation. As K501SP6 shares a closely related epitope, it is possible that this mAb shares a similar mechanism of inhibition. Nevertheless, further investigation is needed to confirm this. Interestingly, K501SP6 was isolated from a convalescent donor, and Liu_12-16 and Liu_12-19 were isolated from a SARS-CoV-2 recovered/vaccinated individual. It is possible that SARS-CoV-2 infection induces antibodies towards different epitopes in the spike than vaccination and could explain why some people are able to neutralize the later VoC while others are not. Understanding where broad antibodies from convalescent individuals target could help in identifying novel antigens for vaccine design as well as new treatment and prevention options for COVID-19. Furthermore, characterizing the neutralizing epitope landscape of the spike protein and appreciating the contribution of non-RBD targeting neutralizing antibodies can inform mAb treatment as well as vaccine design. Further, utilizing different epitope binning groups could pave the way for dual antibody treatment.

Using recombinant viruses and structural analysis we confirmed that K501SP3 targets the RBD, specifically the RBM and has features of a Barnes classification class 1 antibody36. Through further analysis, we confirmed that both substitutions F486V and E484A were responsible for the lack of BA.5 neutralization, with F486V having a major impact. This is consistent with previous studies showing that cross-reactive neutralizing antibodies are evaded by these substitutions in the viral genome41,46,47.

For HuMab K501SP6, the combination of heavy and light chain was found to be unique when compared to other HuMabs. Furthermore, heavy chain IGHV4-39 has been found to be used preferentially in SARS-CoV-2 infection48,49. Of note is the length of HuMab K501SP6 CDRH3 region at 21 a.a. Other SARS-CoV-2 mAbs with long CDRH3 regions (20 and 21 a.a.,) have been shown to be able to target highly conserved residues, which less potent mAbs with shorter CDRH3 region targeting the same contact region cannot50. The heavy (IGHV1-58) and light chain (IGKV3-20) usage of HuMab K501SP3 were found in one EUA mAb, Tixagevimab, which is used in combination with Cilgavimab as the treatment Evusheld. However, similar to results observed here, Evusheld has reduced neutralization to Omicron sub-linages XBB, BQ.1 and BQ.1.1, due to substitutions at F486, R346 and K444-44641. For this reason Evusheld is no longer authorized for use in the United States51.

Limitations of this study include the relatively low potency of HumAb K501SP6. Nevertheless, affinity maturation or expressing these IgG antibodies as IgM could increase potency. Indeed, several studies have shown that IgM mAbs are highly potent and can outperform clonally identical IgG mAbs in terms of affinity to the VoC52,53. Furthermore, additional analysis is required to determine the exact contact residues on SD1.

Overall, we found one broadly neutralizing HuMab with unique genetic features that could be a valuable addition to already existing SARS-CoV-2 mAb therapies. Furthermore, a second HuMab was found with high potency towards earlier circulating variants but through F486V and E484A was escaped by Omicron sub-lineage BA.5. Characterizing neutralizing antibodies targeting SARS-CoV-2 spike can give us valuable insights into the immune mechanisms involved in potent neutralization and immune evasions of Omicron sub-lineages and may be useful in tackling multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants.

Materials and methods

Subject and sample characteristics

A paucisymptomatic subject (minor or no symptoms that did not seek medical care) was recruited from Bispebjerg Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark, during screening of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in healthcare staff employed in the Capital Region of Denmark in July 2020, as previously described31,54. Follow-up samples were collected in September 202031. Blood was collected in an EDTA vacutainer separated by centrifugation. Plasma was collected and stored at −80 °C until use. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated via density gradient, as previously described32.

Blood sample collection and ethics statement

Human research ethics approvals were obtained for all samples from the Regional Research Ethics Committees for the Capital Region of Denmark (Protocols H-20034367 and H-20025872) and Data Protection Agency (P-2020-357). All patients gave informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

All antigens were synthesized by Geneart and optimized for expression in the ExpreS2 platform, as previously described55. Briefly, Schneider-2 (ExpreS2) cells (ExpreS2ion Biotechnologies), were transiently transfected using transfection reagent (ExpreS2 Insect TRx5, ExpreS2ion Biotechnologies) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Recombinant SARS-CoV-2 antigens (RBD wild-type (WT) SARS-CoV-2, or its Alpha (B.1.1.7), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1) and Omicron BA.1 (B.1.1.529) variants, amino acids (a.a.) 319-591 of spike) had an N-terminal BiP secretion signal and a C-terminal C-tag (N-RBD-EPEA-C) used for purification55. The ACE2 protein (a.a.1–615)-C-terminal Twin-Strep-tag (Iba, GmbH) and the spike protein (a.a.16-1208)-Ctag (ΔTM-ΔFurin-CoV-PP-Ctag)) were N-terminally tagged with a BiP secretion signal.

Generation of human monoclonal antibodies

After isolation of PBMCs32, memory B cells were purified via magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS, Miltenyi) using CD22 microbeads (Miltenyi) as per manufacturer’s instructions. In 96-well plates, cells were immortalized at 100 cells/well with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), CpG and irradiated PBMCs, as previously described in Refs. 32,56,57. Immortalized cells were then screened via ELISA to determine wells with SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific B cells. Spike-specific polyclonal B cells were then expanded and cloned by limiting dilution in 384-well plates. Supernatants of growing clones were tested for spike-specific antibody by ELISA32,56,57. Cells from wells with positive supernatants were expanded and grown in medium supplemented with ultra-low IgG fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cytiva). Finally, antibodies were purified from the monoclonal B cell supernatants by affinity chromatography using a 5 mL protein G sepharose column (Cytiva) on the ÄKTAxpress system (Cytiva). Antibodies were eluted in 0.1 M glycine pH 2.8 and immediately neutralized with Trizma hydrochloride solution 1 M pH 9. This was followed by buffer-exchange into PBS using Amicon ultra centrifugal concentrators.

Antibody variable gene amplification and sequencing

RNA was extracted from 4 × 106 B cells using RNeasy plus kit (Qiagen). Multiplex reverse transcription (RT) was performed using Sensiscript RT kit (Qiagen) followed by PCR using Phusion HF master mix (New England Biolabs), Table 1 and as previously described in Ref. 58. Next, a nested PCR was performed using Phusion HF master mix, 1 μL of the amplified product from above and primers which cover the diversity of all Vγ, Vκ and Vλ sequences (Table 158,). PCR amplicons were purified with QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) and Sanger sequenced (Eurofins Genomics). Analysis of HuMab sequences was performed using IMGT V-quest (www.imgt.org)59,60. Phylogenetic analysis was performed with IMGT/PhyloGene45. Sequence alignments and pairwise distribution was performed on the HuMabs generated here and antibodies downloaded from the coronavirus antibody database (CoV-AbDab)37 using Geneious Prime 2023 (https://www.geneious.com).

Enzyme linked immunoassays

ELISAs were performed as previously described30. Briefly, microtiter 96 well plates, (Nunc Maxisorb, ThermoFisher Scientific) were coated with 2 μg/mL of antigen (spike from WT SARS-CoV-2, WT RBD SARS-CoV-2, or its Alpha, Beta, Gamma or Omicron variants) and incubated overnight. Plates were washed three times with PBS-T and then blocked for one hour with blocking buffer (5% non-fat dry milk in PBS-T). For HuMab generation, the bound antigen was incubated with cell supernatant from immortalized B cells. For binding ELISAs, the bound antigen was incubated with purified antigen-specific HuMabs (5-fold dilution of initial 40 μg/mL). This was followed by a 1-hour incubation with the desired detection antibody: anti-human IgG-AP (Sigma Aldrich, 1:1500), anti-human IgM-AP (Sigma Aldrich, 1:1500) or anti-human IgA-AP (Sigma Aldrich, 1:1500). Next, an incubation with 4-Nitrophenyl phosphate disodium salt hexahydrate tablets (Sigma Aldrich) dissolved in 1 × Diethanolamine Substrate Buffer (Sigma Aldrich) was performed. Color development and absorbance were measured at 405 nm.

For competition, blocking was performed with 5% w/v bovine serum albumin (Sigma Aldrich). The bound antigen was incubated for 1 h with HuMabs (5-fold serial dilution of initial 200 μg/mL) serially diluted in 1% bovine serum albumin. Recombinant ACE2 was added corresponding to 90% maximal binding for the WT and each VoC, as previously described (spike from WT SARS-CoV-2, 81 nM; RBD WT SARS-CoV-2, 54 nM; Alpha, 4 nM; Beta, Gamma, 14 nM, and Omicron, 54 nM)31. Bound ACE2 was detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated strep-tactin (IBA, 1:10 000 dilution) and 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. On each plate, a plasma pool of immune serum with an IC50 of 1/50 was used as a positive control and a non-specific HuMab as a negative control. ACE2 binding without antibody served as a “normal binding” control and was used to determine percent inhibition which was calculated using the formula % inhibition = 1 − (inhibited activity)/(‘normal’ binding)] × 100, after subtraction of background.

HuMab binding kinetics by Attana© Biosensor

Kinetic interaction experiments of RBD antigens (RBD SARS-CoV-2 WT and its Alpha, Beta, Gamma and Omicron (BA.1) variants) binding to HuMabs were performed using a quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) biosensor Attana Cell A200 instrument (Attana AB). HuMabs (25 μg/ml, diluted in 10 mM Na-acetate pH 4.5) were immobilized on a LNB carboxyl chip by amine coupling using EDC and S-NHS chemistry following manufacturer’s instructions. A non-coated LNB chip was used as reference. Six serial, 2 fold dilution series of RBD antigens starting at 32 µg/ml were prepared in HBS-T buffer. The HuMab-immobilized chip was regenerated with NaOH (50 mM NaOH for SP2 HuMab and 100 mM NaOH for SP3 HuMab).

All sensorgrams were recorded at 25 μl/min at 22 °C using an 84 s association and 300 s dissociation time and then regenerated to allow complete baseline recovery. Injection of running buffer was subtracted for each sensorgram and the kinetic parameters kon and koff were fitted using a 1:1 binding model using TraceDrawer software (Ridgeview Instruments AB).

Generation of SARS-CoV-2 viral stocks

Viral stocks from SARS-CoV-2 virus isolates representing the ancestral D614G variant (hereafter referred to as WT, DK-AHH1, clade 20 A, Genbank accession number MZ049597.1), an Alpha variant (DK-AHH2, clade 20I, accession number OK041529), a Delta variant (DK-AHH3, clade 21 J, accession number OP271297), an Omicron BA.1 variant (DK-AHH4, clade 21 K, accession number OP271296), an Omicron BA.2 variant (DK-AHH5, clade 22 C, accession number OP722493) and an Omicron BA.5 variant (DK-AHH6, clade 22B, accession number OP722492), were reported previously61–65. For the generation of viral stocks representing Omicron XBB.1.4 (DK-AHH7, clade 22 F, accession number OQ843560) and Omicron BQ.1.1.20 (DK-AHH8, clade 22E, accession number OQ843561), we inoculated Vero E6 cells (RRID: CVCL_0574) with a nasopharyngeal sample from a SARS-CoV-2 positive patient (Department of Microbiology, Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark)66. Culture supernatants were subjected to two serial passages in Vero E6 cells and the supernatants from the 2nd passage were passed through a 0.45 μm filter and stored at −80 °C until further use66. The near-complete SARS-CoV-2 genome sequence of viral stocks (passage 2 viruses) and infectivity titers were obtained as previously described64.

Generation of SARS-CoV-2 recombinant viruses

Using a previously described reverse-genetics platform based on the ancestral D614G (DK-AHH1) isolate67, a total of eleven SARS-CoV-2 mutant viruses were generated exhibiting modifications of the spike protein, the RBD or containing point mutations leading to different amino acid changes at position E484 and F486. Thus, a mutant virus exhibiting the entire spike sequence from the BA.5 VoC (and the V486F change), 5 RBD mutant viruses containing the RBD sequence from VoCs: Alpha, Delta and Omicron BA.1, BA.2 and BA.5, and three mutant viruses exhibiting spike amino acid changes: E484A, F486V, E484A + F486V) were generated. Two mutant viruses with the complete spike sequence from the BA.286 and JN.1 VoCs were also generated. Mutations were introduced by a megaprimer polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or using an infusion kit (Takara) into plasmids containing the whole SARS-CoV-2 sequence, as described previously67. Plasmids were prepared using QIA Spin miniprep or Large-Construct kits (Qiagen), and sequence-confirmed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) prior to in vitro transcription and transfection. Mutant viruses were rescued as previously described67. In brief, clones were subjected to in vitro transcription to generate RNA transcripts using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher) and RNA transcripts were transfected into Vero E6 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher). Propagation of virus was verified by observing cytopathic effect (CPE) in transfected/infected cultures with an inverted light microscope. For each recombinant, a viral passage was performed by infecting Vero E6 cells with supernatants obtained from transfection experiments (first passage). Viral stock supernatants were passed through a 0.45 μm filter and stored at −80 °C until further use. Viral stocks were titrated as previously described64 and the multiplicity of infection (MOI) causing complete infection of a well without CPE was determined for each virus: 0.03, 0.01, 0.06, 0.03, 0.03, 0.02, 0.08, 0.05,0.03, 0.15 and 0.3 for the Alpha-RBD, Delta-RBD, BA.1-RBD, BA.2-RBD, BA.5-RBD, E484A, F486V, E484A + F486V, BA.5-V486F, BA.2.86 spike and JN.1 spike mutants, respectively.

Virus neutralization assay

Neutralization was performed in 96-well plates containing 104 Vero E6 cells/well, plated 24 h prior to the assay. 10-fold serially diluted HuMabs or plasma were mixed at a 1:1 HuMab/plasma:virus ratio and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Virus input was adjusted to reflect MOIs of 0.02, 0.01, 0.1, 0.06, 0.07, 0.2, 0.05 and 0.05 for the DK-AHH1, DK-AHH2, DK-AHH3, DK-AHH4, DK-AHH5, DK-AHH6, DK-AHH7 and DK-AHH8 isolates, respectively. Bebtelovimab (ProteoGenix, CAS 2578319-11-4, PX-TA1750, 1 mg/mL) used at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL and plasma from a Corminarty® vaccinated, convalescent individual was used as a positive control for each isolate65. As negative controls, a non-SARS-CoV-2-specific HuMab, as well as plasma from the same Corminarty® vaccinated, convalescent individual at a pre-vaccination/infection time point was used65. Following the 1 h incubation, HuMab/virus or plasma/virus complexes were then added to Vero E6 cells in quadruplicate. After 48 h incubation, the cells were processed to detect viral specific antigen as described previously64 but with minor modifications: anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike rabbit polyclonal primary antibody (Abcam ab272504, Lot GR3409042-4) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody conjugated to HRP (Invitrogen, Lot UK293475) were used. Spots representing virus infected cells were counted using an Immunospot series 5 UV analyzer (Cellular Technologies). Single outliers of quadruplicates were calculated using a modified z-score system as previously described68 and were removed from further analysis; thus, a minimum of triplicates was used for all assays. Cryo-EM sample preparation

For the K501SP6 model, Omicron BA.1 was produced in human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells with six stabilizing proline (HexaPro) substitutions, a C-terminal T4 fibritin trimerization domain, and a Twin-strep-tag for purification (IBA)69. Supernatant was purified on 5 ml prepacked StrepTactin XT 4Flow columns (IBA) using biotin for elution according to manufacturer’s instructions. The K501SP6 mAb was digested with papain and purified on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 (Cytiva). Fab fragments were mixed with Omicron BA.1 in a 1.2:1 molar ratio (1.2 Fab per protomer of spike) and incubated for 1 h. Sample at a concentration of 1.2 mg/ml total protein was mixed with lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) for a final concentration of LMNG of 5 µM before sample deposition onto 1.2/1.3 300-mesh UltraAuFoil grids (EMS). Grids were glow discharged prior to sample deposition on a Pelco easiGlow for 60 s at 15 mA. Samples were incubated on grid for 5 s and blotted for 3 s with blotting force 3 on a Vitrobot Mark IV. Frozen grids were stored in liquid nitrogen until data collection.

For the K501SP3 electron density map, Omicron BA.1 spike trimer protein (Sino Biological) was complexed with mAb K501SP3 by mixing at 1:1.5 molar ratio spike:mAb K501SP3. Reduced graphene oxide grids (reduced graphene oxide fabricated on Quantifoil 1.2/1.3 Au grids, purchased from Shuimu Biosciences Ltd.) were glow discharged with Leica Coater ACE200 for 10 s at 5 mA current. The cryo-EM grids were prepared using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) operated at 4 °C and 100% humidity. Sample concentration at 0.1 mg/ml was applied to the grid, incubated for 60 s and blotted for 8 s with blotting force −8. Frozen grids were stored in liquid nitrogen until data collection.

Cryo-EM data collection, processing, and modelling

For the K501SP6 model, the complex sample dataset was collected on a Glacios 2 electron microscope (ThermoFisher) equipped with a Thermo Scientific Falcon 4i Direct Electron Detector. EPU was used to collect 3819 movies at a pixel size of 0.718 Å and a total dose of 60 e-/Å2 using a defocus range between −0.8 and 2 µm (Table 2). All data were processed in cryoSPARC 4.4.170. The movies (Fig. S1) were motion corrected and Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) parameters determined using cryoSPARC Live. An initial 277,775 blob picking particles were extracted with a box size of 672 px and cleaned up using 2D classification (Fig. S1). Ab-initio was performed, creating three volumes. A Topaz model was trained and used for extraction of additional particles and filtered alongside the blob picking particles using heterogenous refinement71. 3D classification was used to filter out spike trimer that had dimerized. Particles were re-extracted at 512 px. An additional round of 2D classification, ab-inito, and heterogenous refinement on spike + fab was used to have a homogenous particle subset consisting of 87,875 particles. Non-uniform refinement was utilized for the globally refined model.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data analysis was performed, and graphs created using GraphPad Prism Software (version 7.0, GraphPad). The 50% inhibitory concentration neutralization and inhibition titers (IC50) of HuMabs were calculated using non-linear regression (inhibitor vs normalized response [variable slope]). Throughout all analysis, statistical significance was defined as a p value less than 0.05 and results expressed as individual data points.
