Enhanced growth of 1-butanol producing Escherichia coli via a metabolic buffer
Authors: Haidong Yu, Haofeng Chen, Guojun Yang, Zhen Huang, Jieming Zhou, Dongjiang Lin, Junru Zhao, Yang Zhang, Jifeng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract1-Butanol is a C4 aliphatic alcohol that is widely used as a gasoline blendstock. In this study, we report an underexplored metabolic route for the de novo biosynthesis of 1-butanol in Escherichia coli. Specifically, the 1-butanol biosynthetic pathway comprises a redesigned Clostridium-derived pathway for butyryl-CoA synthesis, followed by a CoA-acylating aldehyde dehydrogenase (PduP from Salmonella enterica) and an endogenous alcohol dehydrogenase (YqhD from E. coli). We found that abolishing thioesterases to block the formation of butyrate byproduct would lead to deleterious effects on the cell growth. Upon introducing a metabolic buffering pathway towards polyhydroxyalkanoate biosynthesis, the toxicity caused by metabolic imbalance was alleviated, accompanied by enhanced cell growth and 1-butanol productivity. A similar strategy was also validated to be effective for improving the production of hexane-2,3-diol, a potential cosmetic component with high value. In summary, this work successfully demonstrates that the metabolic buffering strategy exerts a synergistic effect on enhancing biochemical production with improved carbon recovery efficiency.Keywords: 1-Butanol; Polyhydroxybutyrate; Metabolic buffering pathway; Hexane-2,3-diol; Synthetic biology

Introduction

Microbial production of biofuels from renewable resources is considered a sustainable solution to address the global energy and environmental concerns. Compared to the first-generation biofuel of ethanol, higher alcohols (C4 and C5) exhibit non-hygroscopic properties and higher energy densities. Furthermore, microbial synthesis of C4 and C5 alcohols using the genetically engineered Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been widely explored [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. For instance, 1-butanol, as a key organic compound, has been extensively studied in various fields, including catalytic synthesis [6], utilization as a catalytic reaction feedstock [7], application as an antisolvent, and deployment in fuel-related technologies [[8], [9], [10], [11]], owing to its excellent physicochemical properties. Particularly, in the fuel sector, 1-butanol is regarded as a promising clean energy carrier, and its high energy density, low hygroscopicity, and good compatibility with conventional fuels highlight the significant potential as a gasoline alternative [9,11].

Currently, two main biosynthetic pathways are employed for 1-butanol bio-manufacturing: the CoA-dependent pathway and the ketoacid pathway [12]. Among these, the CoA-dependent pathway is the first to be discovered and most extensively researched. Its natural biosynthetic process is known as the acetone-butanol-ethanol (ABE) fermentation [[13], [14], [15]], which also represents the first biotechnological route for large-scale industrial production of 1-butanol [10,16]. The ABE-based pathway relies on Clostridium acetobutylicum to convert starch or sugars into a mixture of acetone, 1-butanol, and ethanol under anaerobic conditions, with 1-butanol as the primary product [14,17]. This pathway involves multiple key enzymes, including a thiolase (Thl), a β-hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (Hbd), a crotonase (Crt), a butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (Bcd), and an alcohol dehydrogenase (AdhE). The entire pathway converts two molecules of acetyl-CoA into one molecule of 1-butanol, consuming NADPH as the reducing cofactor [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, besides 1-butanol, the ABE fermentation process typically generates multiple by-products such as acetone, ethanol, and lactic acid, which increases the cost and complexity of downstream separation and purification [14,17]. In addition, the core of 1-butanol production via the ketoacid pathway lies in using 2-ketoacids as key intermediates, which undergo two key reactions—decarboxylation and reduction—to generate 1-butanol. Based on the source of initial 2-ketoacids, this pathway is divided into two major branches: the threonine-derived type (the most mainstream) and the citramalate/2-ketoglutarate-derived type [21,22].

With the advancement of genetic engineering and synthetic biology approaches, E. coli has emerged as an ideal heterologous production host due to its ease of genetic manipulation and well-understood physiological characteristics [17]. The CoA-dependent 1-butanol biosynthetic pathway is frequently employed in E. coli for 1-butanol production. Shen et al. introduced the clostridial CoA-dependent pathway into E. coli, and achieved a titer of 15 g/L in the flask cultivation [23]. Moreover, the strategy of modifying the endogenous amino acid synthesis pathways has been applied for 1-butanol production in E. coli and S. cerevisiae [21,[24], [25], [26]], reaching ∼ 1 g/L and 835 mg/L 1-butanol, respectively, which leverages their inherent ketoacid intermediates. Comparative analysis of production titers reveals that the CoA-dependent pathway currently achieves substantially higher yields than the keto acid pathway in engineered strains for 1-butanol production. Furthermore, 1-butanol production in other non-model microorganisms, such as Lactobacillus [27] and cyanobacteria [12], has also been investigated through synthetic biology technologies. For instance, the 1-butanol biosynthesis in cyanobacteria was optimized at both the genetic and enzymatic levels, involving modifications to the 5′ regions of gene expression units to regulate transcription and translation, reshaping the carbon flux, and rewiring the central carbon metabolism of photosynthesis to enhance precursor supply, consequently developing processes to convert carbon dioxide into 1-butanol, achieving a maximum cumulative titer of 4.8 g/L [28].

In this study, we attempted to investigate the 1-butanol biosynthetic pathway that contains an acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase and an acetoacetyl-CoA reductase (PhaA and PhaB from Ralstonia eutropha), a R-specific crotonase (PhaJ from Aeromonas caviae), a trans-enoyl-CoA reductase (Ter from Treponema denticola), a CoA-acylating aldehyde dehydrogenase (PduP from Salmonella enterica), and an endogenous alcohol dehydrogenase (YqhD from E. coli). We found that E. coli cells harbouring the PhaJ-Ter-PhaAB and PduP-YqhD modules could successfully produce 4.98 g/L 1-butanol in flask cultivation. However, the endogenous thioesterases (TEs) would lead to a substantial accumulation of butyrate byproduct to 1.14 g/L. Further abolishing TEs such as acyl-CoA thioesterase I (YciA) and acyl-CoA thioesterase II (TesB) could increase the 1-butanol titer by 1.66-fold, with a substantial reduction in butyrate levels. However, the deletions of yciA and tesB resulted in deleterious effects on cell growth, primarily attributed to the metabolic imbalance caused by accumulating toxic intermediates such as butyryl-CoA. To address this issue, we implemented a metabolic buffering pathway by introducing a polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) biosynthesis module, which led to enhanced growth and 1-butanol productivity upon the PHB co-production. Moreover, we also demonstrated the certain applicability of this metabolic buffering strategy in improving another value-added component, hexane-2,3-diol (2,3-HDO), which involves butyryl-CoA as a pathway intermediate. Taken together, we envision that the metabolic buffering strategy might serve as a generalizable approach to improve biochemical production that encounters toxic intermediate accumulation.

Materials and methods

General

E. coli Top10 was used for routine plasmid construction. E. coli MR4.0 [29] with deletions of pta, pflB, ldhA, and adhE was used as the parental chassis to minimize the conversion of pyruvate to fermentation byproducts such as lactate and ethanol. All strains were maintained using Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 10 g/L NaCl) supplemented with the corresponding antibiotics. The concentrations of antibiotics were used as follows: ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and kanamycin (50 μg/mL). The restriction endonucleases, T4 ligase, and high-fidelity Phusion polymerase were all purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, USA). The oligonucleotides were synthesized by Sangon (Shanghai, China). Authentic chemical standards such as 1-butanol were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China).

Plasmid and strain construction

Genes encoding the acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase (PhaA, GenBank: J04987.1), acetoacetyl-CoA reductase (PhaB, GenBank: FJ897462.1), and poly (3-hydroxyalkanoate) polymerase (PhaC, GenBank: M64341.1) were PCR amplified from the genomic DNA of R. eutropha. Genes encoding the R-specific crotonase (PhaJ, GenBank: WP_168235189.1) and trans-enoyl-CoA reductase (Ter, Gene ID: 4GGO_A) were codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The gene encoding the CoA-acylating aldehyde dehydrogenase (PduP, GenBank: WP_061376584.1) was PCR amplified from the genomic DNA of S. enterica. The endogenous YqhD (Gene ID: 947493) was PCR amplified from the genomic DNA of E. coli MG1655. The gene encoding the acetolactate synthase (Ilv2C, GenBank: NM_001182608.1) was cloned from the genomic DNA of S. cerevisiae BY4741.

The oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. PhaAB from R. eutropha was inserted into the pETDuet1-PhaJ-Ter plasmid (synthesized by GenScript) between BglII-XhoI sites, to obtain the plasmid pET-PhaJ-Ter-PhaAB. Similarly, PhaCAB from R. eutropha was inserted into the pETDuet1-PhaJ-Ter plasmid between BglII-XhoI sites, to obtain the plasmid pET-PhaJ-Ter-PhaCAB. Plasmids pET-PhaAB and pET-PhaCAB were constructed by inserting the gene fragments into the pETDuet-1 plasmid between BamHI-XhoI sites. For constructing pRSF-PduP-YqhD and pRSF-Ilv2C-PduP, the BsaI-mediated two-fragment ligation approach was implemented as previously reported [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Briefly, the DNA fragments were PCR amplified from the genomic DNA, digested with BsaI, and then inserted into the recipient plasmid between BamHI-XhoI sites. All plasmids used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 2. E. coli MR4.0 derivatives with ΔyciA-ΔtesB were constructed by CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. Details of all genetically engineered strains and the resulting strains transformed with the corresponding plasmids are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Fermentative production of 1-butanol and diol

The genetically engineered strains with the corresponding plasmids were first inoculated into 2 mL of LB medium supplemented with the necessary antibiotics to prepare the fresh overnight seed culture. For the bioproduction of 1-butanol and 2,3-HDO, the modified M9 medium as previously reported [29], was used, which contains 40 g/L glucose and appropriate antibiotics. 1% seed culture was inoculated into 20 mL of modified M9 medium and incubated at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 3 h. Then, isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to the media to a final concentration of 100 μM to induce the expression of targeted genes under the T7 expression system, and the cell cultures were shifted to 30 °C and 250 rpm for 1-butanol and diol production. Samples were periodically taken to monitor the cell growth and metabolite concentrations of 1-butanol, PHB, and 2,3-HDO.

Analytical methods

Quantitative analysis of metabolites, including 1-butanol and diols, was performed using a gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (GC-FID; Nexis GC-2030 system). Separation was achieved using an SH-Rtx-5 capillary column (25 m × 0.32 mm × 0.5 μm). Fermentation samples were first extracted with ethyl acetate and filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane. For analysis, 1 μL of the organic phase was injected in splitless mode with an injector temperature set at 250 °C. Nitrogen, served as the carrier gas, was set at a column flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature of 40 °C (hold for 2 min), ramped at 15 °C/min to 250 °C (hold for 2 min). The FID detector temperature was set to 350 °C. The calibration curve was constructed using 1-butanol standards. Due to the unavailability of commercial diol standards, the isomer 1,6-hexanediol was used to plot an external standard curve.

PHB quantification was performed using GC-FID, wherein PHB was esterified by treatment with methanol, sulfuric acid, and chloroform. After obtaining freeze-dried cells, 1 mL of chloroform, 0.85 mL of methanol, and 0.15 mL of sulfuric acid were added. The mixed sample was then heated at 80 °C for 140 min. After cooling the reaction to room temperature, 0.5 mL of deionized water was added to achieve phase separation. The lower organic phase was collected, filtered, and subjected to GC-FID analysis. A detailed GC method was set according to the literature [34].

Results

Engineering E. coli for de novo production of 1-butanol from glucose

In this study, we redesigned the Clostridium-derived pathway that contains alternative enzymes, namely, the acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase and acetoacetyl-CoA reductase (PhaA and PhaB from R. eutropha) [[35], [36], [37]], the R-specific crotonase (PhaJ from A. caviae) [38], and the trans-enoyl-CoA reductase (Ter from T. denticola) [39], which are used to produce butyryl-CoA from the central metabolite of acetyl-CoA. It has been reported in the literature that PduP derived from S. enterica exhibits a relatively high catalytic efficiency towards butyryl-CoA [38]. Following the CoA-acylating aldehyde dehydrogenase (PduP) from S. enterica, butyryl-CoA is transformed into butanal and further to 1-butanol via the NADPH-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase YqhD (Fig. 1). Given the oxygen sensitivity and ferredoxin dependence of clostridial butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (Bcd), the Ter enzyme was introduced in this study to achieve efficient and irreversible conversion of crotonyl-CoA to butyryl-CoA [23]. To reduce the ethanol byproduct from AdhE-mediated acetyl-CoA reduction [40], we replaced the AdhE module with PduP-YqhD to improve the product specificity.

Schematic diagram of metabolic routes towards 1-butanol, butyrate, and PHB synthesis. PhaA: Acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase; PhaB: acetoacetyl-CoA reductase; PhaC: polyhydroxyalkanoate synthase; PhaJ: R-specific crotonase; Ter: trans-enoyl-CoA reductase; PduP: CoA-acylating aldehyde dehydrogenase; YqhD: NADPH-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase; TEs: thioesterases.

Based on this design, we constructed a dual-plasmid system including pET-PhaJ-Ter-PhaAB and pRSF-PduP-YqhD in the E. coli parental strain (Fig. 2a), and conducted the shake flask fermentation of 20 mL using 40 g/L glucose as the substrate. After 72 h of fermentation, the 1-butanol titer reached 67.21 mM (4.98 g/L) (Fig. 2b). Compared to the conventional AdhE-mediated butyryl-CoA reduction, the utilization of PduP-YqhD eliminated ethanol byproduct formation due to the high specificity of PduP for butyryl-CoA over acetyl-CoA (data not shown). Notably, in contrast with other butanal biosynthetic pathways that involve the decarboxylation of corresponding α-keto acids via the CimA-LeuABCD [41], the redesigned Clostridium-derived clostridial pathway would specifically produce 1-butanol rather than other branched-chain alcohols such as isobutanol derived from α-keto acids, therefore simplifying the downstream separation and purification processes.

Besides 1-butanol production, it was found that the accumulation of the by-product butyrate could reach 12.95 mM (1.14 g/L) (Fig. 2c), indicating that a part of the butyryl-CoA intermediate was diverted to the synthesis of by-product butyrate by endogenous acyl-CoA TEs [[42], [43], [44]]. In E. coli, there are a number of TEs that may directly limit the carbon flux of butyryl-CoA towards 1-butanol [45,46]. In particular, TEs encoded by TesB and YciA have been used to boost the production of 3-hydroxybutyrate [42,43] and medium-chain fatty acids [45]. Therefore, we chose to delete the main acyl-CoA TEs in E. coli, namely TesB and YciA, to reduce the metabolic flux loss towards butyrate. As shown in Fig. 2b and 2c, it was demonstrated that the deletions of yciA and yciA-tesB substantially reduced the accumulation of butyrate to 0.73 mM (0.07 g/L) and 0.64 mM (0.06 g/L), respectively. Meanwhile, the titer of 1-butanol was further increased to 108.98 mM (8.08 g/L) in the EC-ΔyciA and 111.32 mM (8.25 g/L) in the EC-ΔyciA-ΔtesB, respectively. Surprisingly, EC-ΔyciA-ΔtesB exhibited a substantial reduction in biomass accumulation (Fig. 2d). One possible hypothesis is that the growth inhibition was caused by high-level production of 1-butanol, which is toxic to the cells as a solvent compound. Alternatively, it is hypothesized that the accumulation of the intermediate butyryl-CoA caused by deleting key acyl-CoA TEs would lead to a toxic level within cells that resulted in the metabolic imbalance.

Toxic effect caused by the accumulation of acyl-CoA intermediates

To test the hypothesis that acyl-CoA accumulation is toxic to EC-ΔyciA-ΔtesB cells, we further constructed a set of plasmids expressing PhaAB, PhaCAB, PhaJ-Ter-PhaAB, and PhaJ-Ter-PhaCAB. As shown in Fig. 3a, it was observed that the expression of PhaAB to give the production of (R)-hydroxybutyryl-CoA could substantially inhibit the cell growth, whereas the conversion of (R)-hydroxybutyryl-CoA into PHB by the additional expression of PhaC restored the cell growth to a much higher level. Similarly, the PhaJ-Ter-PhaAB module that produces (R)-hydroxybutyryl-CoA, crotonyl-CoA and butyryl-CoA also inhibited the cell growth (Fig. 3a). However, since PhaC only converts (R)-hydroxybutyryl-CoA into PHB, the overflow might still lead to the accumulation of crotonyl-CoA and butyryl-CoA, and only a slight alleviation of growth inhibition was observed upon inducing PhaC (Fig. 3a). It is reasoned that the accumulation of acyl-CoA could deplete the CoA moieties required for other essential biochemical reactions such as acetyl-CoA (Fig. 3b), thereby disrupting the normal cellular energy metabolism. In contrast, diverting the metabolic flux to PHB synthesis would release free CoA to restore the normal metabolism with improved growth.

Co-production of PHB as a metabolic buffering pathway to improve 1-butanol synthesis

Next, we attempted to study the effect of PHB synthesis on 1-butanol production. As shown in Fig. 4a, the cell densities of engineered E. coli strains producing PHB were obviously higher than those without PHB synthesis. This phenomenon was reproducible in both the single-knockout strain (ΔyciA) and the double-knockout strain (ΔyciA-ΔtesB). Meanwhile, PHB-producing strains also showed a remarkable increase in 1-butanol production during the first 48 h, reaching 105.17 mM (7.79 g/L) and 111.79 mM (8.29 g/L), respectively (Fig. 4b). These represented an increase of 39.94% and 45.62% compared to the strains without PHB synthesis, respectively. Further analysis revealed that approximately 6 g/L of PHB accumulated in the PHB-supplemented strains after 72 h of fermentation. Therefore, the PHB-mediated metabolic buffering pathway had a beneficial effect on the 1-butanol production rate and overall yield. Notably, it is also noticed that all strains displayed reduced biomass from 48 h to 72 h after prolonged cultivation (Fig. 4a), which is likely attributed to the innate 1-butanol toxicity. Therefore, more efforts such as adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE), will be required to address the toxicity of 1-butanol for further titer enhancement in E. coli.

Expanding the PHB-buffering pathway for improving 2,3-HDO biosynthesis

Recently, our research team successfully established a microbial synthesis platform for branched-chain β,γ-diols based on the amino acid metabolism. The platform adopted the “aldehyde-hydroxy ketone-diol” synthetic route, enabling the efficient synthesis of various branched-chain diols, including 4-methylpentane-2,3-diol, 5-methylhexane-2,3-diol, and 4-methylhexane-2,3-diol. The key enzyme, acetohydroxyacid synthase (Ilv2C derived from S. cerevisiae), catalyzes the condensation of aldehydes with pyruvate, followed by the reduction of the intermediate product via endogenous aldo-keto reductases (AKRs) in E. coli, ultimately yielding corresponding diols [29]. In addition, we further expanded the Ilv2C-mediated carboligation for the synthesis of butanal-derived 2,3-HDO in E. coli (Fig. 5a). In brief, the butanal from the redesigned Clostridium-derived pathway is condensed with pyruvate via the downstream module of Ilv2C, followed by the reduction via endogenous AKRs to achieve 2,3-HDO production [47]. Therefore, 2,3-HDO can be produced heterologously in the EC-ΔyciA-ΔtesB using a two-plasmid system containing the PhaJ-Ter-PhaAB and Ilv2C-PduP modules.

To further investigate the impact of PHB synthesis on the 2,3-HDO production, the engineered strains expressing PhaJ-Ter-PhaCAB and Ilv2C-PduP modules were also constructed. A similar trend of improved cell growth during the diol production was observed (Fig. 5b), and the cell density of the strain with PHB synthesis was markedly higher than that of the control during the flask cultivation. Unlike the 1-butanol biosynthesis in E. coli, the 2,3-HDO is less toxic to cells, and the cell density continued to increase from 48 h to 96 h. In terms of 2,3-HDO product synthesis, the engineered strain with PHB synthesis reached 38.58 mM (4.56 g/L) after 96 h of fermentation, which was 23% improvement over that of the control strain (Fig. 5c). It is worth noting that during the early fermentation stage (24 h), the 2,3-HDO production in the control was slightly higher than that in the strain with PHB synthesis. It might be ascribed to the fact that the introduction of the PHB synthetic pathway competes for the limited carbon flux in the early stage, and diverts part of the carbon flux towards PHB synthesis, thereby delaying the synthesis of 2,3-HDO. As the fermentation process advanced, the engineered strain with PHB synthesis progressively surpassed the control strain in the later stage of fermentation (96 h), ultimately achieving a higher titer of 2,3-HDO.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed a previously underexplored 1-butanol synthesis pathway in E. coli. The use of PduP-YqhD addressed the generation of ethanol byproduct commonly encountered in the AdhE-mediated route. Subsequently, by knocking out acyl-CoA TE genes to reduce the formation of butyrate byproduct, the 1-butanol titer in the EC-ΔyciA-ΔtesB was increased by 1.66-fold. However, we observed that the deletions of yciA and tesB led to reduced cell growth due to the metabolic imbalance caused by the accumulation of acyl-CoA, such as crotonyl-CoA and butyryl-CoA. To alleviate the CoA-related intermediate toxicity, we introduced the PHB biosynthetic pathway to serve as a metabolic buffering strategy. By avoiding the excess acyl-CoA accumulation and releasing free coenzyme A, this metabolic buffering approach efficiently improved the cell growth and 1-butanol productivity. Furthermore, we applied this metabolic buffering strategy to the biosynthesis of 2,3-HDO and achieved a 2,3-HDO titer of 38.58 mM (approximately 4.56 g/L) after 96 h of fermentation, which was 23% higher than that of the control without the PHB-mediated buffering pathway.

In the future, dynamic regulation of the PHB synthesis to redirect the produced PHB into the 1-butanol synthesis pathway is expected to further increase the 1-butanol yield. For instance, the PHB depolymerase encoded by PhaZ has been reported to degrade PHB into 3-hydroxybutyrate [48], while the propionyl-CoA transferase encoded by Pct can convert 3-hydroxybutyrate into 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA [49]. Therefore, using the carbon-starvation inducible promoter PcsiD [50] to regulate the expression of PhaZ and Pct genes may enhance 1-butanol production during the later stages of fermentation through PHB degradation and the metabolic reflux mechanism. Alternatively, by attenuating the expression of the PhaC gene, we can explore the optimal balance between its buffering effect and carbon source consumption, thereby maximizing the yield of the target product.

Apart from the metabolic pathway optimization, the cytotoxicity of 1-butanol is a core bottleneck limiting its biosynthesis efficiency. The sustained accumulation of 1-butanol leads to widespread cytotoxicity by disrupting cell membrane structure and interfering with intracellular metabolic processes (such as damaging DNA, RNA, and protein functions) [51,52]. In our study, we also observed the reduced biomass with prolonged cultivation due to the innate toxicity of 1-butanol. Although cells activate a series of stress responses to counteract the solvent stress [53], these mechanisms are often insufficient to offset the damage, ultimately resulting in the low efficiency of 1-butanol biosynthesis. Through various strategies such as traditional chemical mutagenesis [54], construction and screening of genomic libraries [55,56], ALE [57,58], and genome editing [59], the cellular tolerance against 1-butanol might be much improved. Alternatively, the strategy for in situ separation of 1-butanol could be developed to mitigate the toxic impact of volatile 1-butanol during the fermentation process [60,61]. For instance, Lee et al. achieved a 1-butanol titer of 55.7 g/L in a continuous fermentation system integrated with the adsorptive recovery of 1-butanol [61].

In summary, our study presented a supplementary strategy for improving chemical productivity via a PHB co-production metabolic buffering module. Based on our results, it not only alleviated the growth inhibition caused by the accumulation of toxic intermediates but also significantly increased the production of 1-butanol and 2,3-HDO. Moreover, introducing easily separable products like intracellular PHB is also expected to enhance carbon recovery. Taken together, the successful application of metabolic buffering also highlights its broad applicability, offering a potential solution for other biosynthetic pathways that are constrained by the accumulation of toxic intermediates.
