Autonomous Motion Vision with Tri-bulk-heterojunctioned Organic Adaptation Transistor
Authors: Zepang Zhan, Weijie Wang, Weichen Bai, Chengyu Zhang, Wei Wang, Yutao Ge, Zixuan Liu, Yunjiang Zi, Liyao Liu, Xiaojuan Dai, Ye Zou, Daoben Zhu, Chong-an Di
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Motion vision is increasingly critical in scenarios such as autonomous driving and embodied intelligence. Existing sensors perform motion analysis and moving‑object extraction as separate tasks, which increases computation and storage needs to challenge highly efficient applications. Here, we design an organic tri-bulk-heterojunctioned adaptation phototransistor (OTAT) that integrates both functionalities in a single device. Notably, the OTAT incorporates a gate-adjacent semiconductor-insulator heterojunction as a photosensitive percolation channel, providing fine-tuned photoadaptation under illumination spanning six orders of magnitude. In imaging experiments, the device exhibits in‑sensor moving‑object extraction with a 45.3% highlight ratio, approaching the capability of the human visual system, while yielding motion analysis accuracy over 99.4% in both direction and speed detection. Importantly, the OTAT-based agents generated the lowest risk in multiple route-planning simulation. These results show that the OTAT effectively bridges the gap between motion extraction and analysis, marking a significant step forward for next-generation in‑sensor motion vision systems.

Introduction

The convergence of Internet of Things and artificial intelligence has generated exponentially growing data volumes that challenge conventional machine vision systems, particularly for tracking multiple moving objects in complex environments1–3. Traditional architectures separate data acquisition, storage and processing into discrete modules, introducing latency and necessitating trade-offs between computational efficiency and analytical accuracy4,5. It therefore creates challenging limitations especially pronounced in applications demanding real-time multi-object tracking, such as autonomous navigation and human-machine interaction6. Motion-perceiving sensors have emerged as promising solutions, offering reduced data redundancy and high temporal resolution7,8. These include motion tracking sensors that record persistent trajectories via non-volatile photoresponse memory9,10, motion detection systems that isolate moving objects through temporal differencing11,12 or event-triggered spiking13,14, and dynamic-active pixel sensors that capture both static and dynamic information while highlighting motion targets15 (Supplementary Note S1 and Supplementary Table S1). However, these approaches typically decouple motion sensing from feature analysis, incurring substantial computational overhead that complicates cross-frame data association and precludes real-time decision-making at the sensor level16,17.

Biological vision systems provide instructive principles through adaptation mechanisms that enable dynamic responses to visual stimuli7,18. Operating across timescales from milliseconds to minutes, feedforward visual adaptation selectively amplifies behaviorally relevant signals while attenuating redundant information, optimizing neural resources for efficient processing (Supplementary Note S2)19–21. Human eyes, for example, adapt to static patterns while maintaining robust responses to motion, enabling organisms to rapidly detect moving targets against stationary backgrounds through local computation22. So far, bio-inspired sensors exploiting photo-adaptation23–25, chromatic adaptation26 and spectral adaptation27–29 have been developed. Their time-dependent outputs enable in-situ extraction of dynamic features without external processing to reduce computational burden. Despite these advances, existing adaptation-based sensors primarily implement single-modality responses and lack integrated capabilities for background suppression and quantitative motion analysis. Current designs can attenuate static scenes or detect motion, but cannot concurrently extract motion information of speed and direction, while preserving scene context. Addressing this limitation requires a new generation of sensor architectures that combine feedforward adaptation with spatiotemporal feature extraction to enable autonomous recognition and analysis.

Here, we report an organic tri-bulk-heterojunctioned adaptation phototransistor (OTAT) that achieves autonomous motion vision by integrating adaptation mechanisms with motion analysis at the hardware level. Through creating a light-controlled vertical channel embedded within the dielectric, the device implements tunable adaptation dynamics that distinguish moving objects from static backgrounds, with dynamic salience enhanced by 45.3% relative to static features. Moreover, it integrates kinematic parameters with temporal response dynamics, enabling feedforward extraction of single-frame speed and direction with average errors below 0.6%. These enable OTAT-based agents to perform predictive planning under computational constraints with 40% collision risk reduction. The OTAT represents an emerging class of motion-perceiving visual sensors with an in-sensor motion analysis function, marking a significant leap toward hardware-level autonomous spatiotemporal perception.

Results

OTAT Design and Fabrication

Conventional motion sensors rely on non-volatile current responses or instantaneous discrimination between transient and sustained stimuli (Fig. 1), producing history-dependent outputs that lack temporal differentiability. By contrast, biological motion vision requires temporally differentiable signal evolution to encode transient dynamics and cumulative motion trajectories. Critically, the desired motion vision sensors should exhibit intensity-independent adaptation amplitudes while maintaining monotonic intensity-dependent baseline responses to preserve static-scene texture information. Dual-heterojunctioned organic active adaptation transistors (OAATs) have achieved temporally differentiable responses through photosensitive charge transport and intensity-dependent charge trapping/accumulation occurring at the top/bottom interfaces of the adaptive layer23,30. However, this also generates intensity-dependent adaptation amplitudes, which hinders the preservation of static information and the fidelity of contrast between different static features (Supplementary Fig. 1). We hypothesized that introducing a photosensitive vertical conductive channel (PVCC) between the gate electrode and adaptive layer would enable autonomous regulation of charge accumulation at the adaptive BHJ’s bottom interface. Under varying illumination, the PVCC dynamically modulates intensity-dependent charge leakage to compensate for light-intensity fluctuations, thereby maintaining adaptation amplitudes. Figure 1d shows the molecular structure and the functionality of three bulk heterojunctions. The active transport layer comprises PDPP3T:ITIC, combining high charge mobility, efficient photocarrier generation, and excellent photostability for sensitive photodetection (Supplementary Fig. 2). The adaptation layer employs PTB7-Th:COTIC-4F, whose fluorine-rich composition and robust photoelectric response enable precise control of interfacial electron-trapping dynamics. Critically, the third heterojunction layer is composed of PTB7-Th semiconductor and poly(vinyl-cinnamate) (PVCN) insulator. The resulting PVCC layer exhibits photogenerated-carrier-dependent conductivity, providing intensity-dependent charge leakage from the gate to the adaptation layer. Under illumination, the device operates with three synergistic processes: charge transport in the active layer, interfacial charge trapping at the adaptation layer, and vertical charge percolation through the PVCC. This combination produces intensity-independent adaptation amplitudes and time-differential motion encoding. Interestingly, the device is fabricated via scalable solution processing. Detailed device fabrication protocols are provided in Methods.

Dynamic adaptation performance

In the OTAT, the top two BHJ layers function identically to OAATs23,30, while the bottom semiconductor-insulator BHJ plays a critical role in enabling motion vision. To investigate the ratio-dependent electronic behavior of OTAT, we fabricated devices with varying PTB7-Th content in the PVCN matrix. Figure 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3 show the source-drain current (IDS) versus time under fixed bias conditions (source-drain voltage, VDS = − 1 V; gate voltage, VGS = − 3 V) and fixed-intensity illumination (1 mW/cm²). Devices with different blend ratios display distinct photoresponse behaviors. For instance, the device without PTB7-Th exhibits a sharp increase in IDS, followed by a pronounced decrease of more than 80% within 3 seconds, demonstrating typical adaptation behavior. As the PTB7-Th content increases from 1 to 9 wt%, the post-adaptation IDS gradually rises, with negligible decay observed at 9 wt% PTB7-Th. Meanwhile, the decay time constant displays negligible dependence on PTB7-Th content, remaining consistently within 1–5 s across all blend ratios (Supplementary Fig. 4). We also evaluated the transfer characteristics of OTATs with different PTB7-Th ratios (Fig. 2b). With increasing PTB7-Th content, the maximum hysteresis voltage decreases from 4.7 V at 1 wt% to 0.64 V at 9 wt%, concurrent with the increase in post-adaptation photocurrent. For desired motion vision, the sensor output should scale monotonically with light intensity under sustained illumination (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, we extracted decay amplitudes upon light irradiation at different intensities to assess the optimal blend ratio. As shown in Fig. 2c, the decay amplitude shows a pronounced dependence on PTB7-Th content. At low PTB7-Th ratios of 1 wt%, the devices exhibit large current decay amplitudes comparable to those with a pristine PVCN film, with mean values of 86.8% averaged over 0.001–100 mW/cm². As the PTB7-Th content increases to 3–7 wt%, the decay amplitude drops to 60.8%, 41.0% and 24.6% on average, respectively. At 9 wt% PTB7-Th, the decay amplitude decreases to 0.8%. In particular, at a PTB7-Th ratio of 5 wt%, the device maintains a stable decay amplitude of approximately 41% across six orders of magnitude in illumination intensity. This is complemented by a rapid adaptive response within 1–10 s (Supplementary Fig. 4) and a transient response on the order of 50 ms (Supplementary Fig. 5). This characteristic resembles motion preference in human vision, enhancing dynamic cues while retaining static content. Notably, by modulating the adaptation layer’s composition or introducing p- or n-type dopants, the characteristic adaptation timescale should be engineered to optimize device performance across varied operational contexts. Moreover, both the transient photocurrent and the post-adaptation current scale linearly with light intensity with R² > 0.99 (Fig. 2d), and the device shows prominent operational stability (Fig. 2e). Taken together, the stable adaptation, faithful intensity encoding, and task-relevant emphasis on motion make 5 wt% the optimal composition for motion vision applications.

Working mechanism of OTAT

Given the established mechanisms of the charge transport and adaptive BHJs23,30, we focus on elucidating the critical role of PVCC-BHJ composition in dominating OTATs since the bottom insulation layer plays a key role in adaptation performance (Supplementary Fig. 6). The coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CG-MD) simulation is utilized to investigate the three-dimensional phase morphology of PVCN:PTB7-Th BHJ films with varying PTB7-Th contents (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7). Details of the simulation setup and analysis are provided in Supplementary Note S3. We extracted the neighboring distances between conjugated beads of PTB7-Th donor or acceptor units, and fitted the resulting distributions to evaluate the spatial correlation of charge-transport pathways (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 8–9). At 1 wt% PTB7-Th, most conjugated beads remain well separated, with a characteristic spacing of 3.54 nm. As the PTB7-Th content is increased to 5 wt%, a distinct configuration emerges with a distance of 0.47 nm, close to the interchain spacing of PTB7-Th, and accounts for 22% of the total morphology. Further increasing to 9 wt% induces pronounced aggregation, the PTB7-Th phase evolves predominantly into a direct-contact configuration. Figure 3c schematically summarizes the evolution of the charge-transport in PVCN:PTB7-Th BHJ films as a function of PTB7-Th content. When semiconducting domains are isolated and widely separated, the blend film remains electrically insulating. As the PTB7-Th content reaches a critical threshold, inter-connected charge-transport pathways begin to emerge, enabling percolative charge transport across the film. Upon further increasing the PTB7-Th, these pathways evolve into a well-developed, continuous percolating network, which enables efficient long-range charge transport. The sandwiched device measurements well corroborate these processes (Supplementary Fig. 10). Below 3 wt% PTB7-Th, devices exhibit minimal current less than 1 μA across a voltage bias range from 0 to 8 V, comparable to that of pure PVCN. At 5 wt%, current density increases by about tenfold, signaling the onset of charge transport under applied bias. Further raising to 9 wt%, boosts the current by two additional orders of magnitude, attributable to the formation of a highly interconnected percolating network.

Having identified the critical role of PVCN:PTB7-Th BHJ, the OTAT thus operates with synergistic interplay among the three BHJs (Fig. 3d). Under illumination, excitons are generated and dissociated in both the PDPP3T:ITIC (BHJ-1) and PTB7-Th:COTIC-4F (BHJ-2) layers. The photogenerated holes from BHJ-1 accumulate at the BHJ-1/PVCN interface, enabling efficient hole transport. In parallel, electrons generated in BHJ-2 are driven toward the polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)/BHJ-2 interface and become trapped in the PVA layer, thereby enhancing hole accumulation at the BHJ-2/BHJ-3 interface. As holes build up, the potential drop across BHJ-3 (PVCN:PTB7-Th) increases, promoting vertical conductivity and facilitating hole percolation through the percolating network. Under continuous illumination, both the density of accumulated holes and the BHJ-3 intrinsic conductivity scale with light intensity, as higher light intensities simultaneously boost hole accumulation and hole percolation. This interplay establishes a dynamic balance between charge buildup and dissipation, which stabilizes gate-field screening and yields a well-defined, robust adaptation amplitude. Collectively, the OTAT achieves effective coupling between the dual-heterojunction adaptation process and the photoactive percolation mechanism, enabling autonomous motion vision.

To further verify the charge transport across the BHJ-2/BHJ-3 stack, we fabricated a metal-semiconductor-insulator-metal (MSIM) device (Fig. 3e) and measured its photocurrent under varying light intensities (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 11). As the PTB7-Th content increases, the MSIM device shows a maximum enhancement at 5 wt%. For instance, the photocurrent rises from 0.072 nA at 1 wt% to 1.41 nA at 5 wt% under 1 mW/cm² illumination (Fig. 3g). Beyond 7 wt%, however, the dark current increases sharply, from 0.063 nA at 5 wt% to 1.24 nA at 7 wt%. These trends together indicate that charge transport evolves with increasing PTB7-Th content. In parallel, introducing PTB7-Th into BHJ-3 progressively shifts its work function toward that of pristine PTB7-Th (Supplementary Fig. 12), thereby reducing contact resistance and enhancing vertical charge transport. We further employed Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to track the time-dependent surface potential of the top composite dielectric layer (Fig. 3h). Upon illumination, the magnitude of the surface-potential decay systematically decreases with increasing PTB7-Th content (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 13), mirroring the light-intensity-dependent adaptation of IDS. This corroborates the crucial role of the BHJ-3 conductivity transition in modulating the effective gate field during OTAT operation.

This coupled-mechanism framework is not restricted to the specific molecular systems demonstrated here, but can be generalized to a broad range of organic and even inorganic/two-dimensional materials. This versatility could thus enable rational design of tri-bulk-heterojunction adaptation transistors tailored to diverse environmental and operational requirements. For instance, broadband and/or narrowband semiconductors can be strategically designed to satisfy a wide range of spectral requirements for various optoelectronic and neuromorphic applications. Looking beyond, while this OTAT architecture is optimized for feedforward motion vision, the principles are fundamentally applicable to other motion-related sensory modalities. Motion tactile perception, as an example, could be designed by incorporating pressure-responsive charge percolation layers into mechanosensitive transistors. Keeping this inspiration in mind, the design principle establishes a general model case that may catalyze new pathways towards intelligent motion perception systems across multiple sensory areas.

Single-frame motion analysis

The unique dynamic behavior of OTAT enables real-time imaging with simultaneous velocity estimation, motion-target extraction, and static-background suppression. To demonstrate the in-situ velocity analysis capability, we encoded videos of a ball moving at controlled velocities into time-sequenced illumination patterns using a programmable laser (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Note S4). The generated patterns were then projected onto the OTAT device. During illumination, we recorded the time-resolved photocurrent of each pixel to obtain spatiotemporal photocurrent maps. These measurements enabled reconstruction of ball images at successive time points. Figure 4b shows reconstructed images of the moving ball at different time. For a static ball, the photocurrent decays uniformly with 1.1% deviation, producing no intensity pattern. When the ball moves at 0.1 m/s, however, the leading edge exhibits maximum photocurrent that decays exponentially with distance, consistent with OTAT’s temporal response (Supplementary Fig. 3). A clear intensity gradient emerges within 0.5 s, encoding velocity as an on-object brightness distribution. As velocity increases, both the high-photocurrent region and overall signal magnitude scale proportionally, generating distinct signatures for different speeds. We extract velocity from single-frame data by linearly fitting the logarithmic photocurrent (Fig. 4c). The slope directly yields speed, while motion direction is opposite to the gradient (Supplementary Note S5). Extracted velocities achieve excellent agreement with ground truth (Fig. 4d), with mean relative errors of 0.31% for direction and 0.52% for speed. High analysis accuracy was demonstrated under uneven illumination condition and multi-objects situation (Supplementary Fig. 14). Critically, this approach fundamentally differs from conventional methods. Because the photocurrent gradient forms directly on the moving object, velocity information is inherently bound to the target before computation. This eliminates the tracking and association steps, effectively preventing the identity switches that plague multi-object tracking by removing the cross-interference of ghost artifacts associated with frame-differencing. Nevertheless, occlusion and varying contrast conditions remain challenging for the current OTAT-based motion analysis framework. We anticipate that integrating deep learning techniques will further expand OTAT's motion analysis capabilities and extend its applicability to more real-world scenarios.

Aiming to demonstrate attention-like selectivity, we imaged a 10 × 10-pixel scene containing static pixels of varying brightness and one dynamic pixel following a defined trajectory (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Note S6). Photocurrent maps at two time points (Fig. 4e) reveal the device’s adaptive filtering mechanism. Initially, all pixels exhibit comparable photocurrents, 8.29 nA, for static pixels and 9.19 nA for moving-pixel, obscuring the moving target. Under sustained illumination, however, static-pixel signals decay to 4.91 nA while the moving-pixel signal remains stable at 8.98 nA (Supplementary Fig. 16). This selective suppression enhances the moving target by 45.3% relative to the static background, effectively isolating dynamic objects to motion-relevant regions. Therefore, OTAT simultaneously achieves dynamic target extraction and motion analysis—capabilities previously requiring separate sensor generations. As summarized in Fig. 4f, OTAT bridges dynamic vision by integrating sensing and computation in a single device. Its time-resolved photoresponse encodes velocity as an on-target photocurrent gradient within a single frame, eliminating inter-frame differencing and trajectory association. Moreover, adaptive suppression of static backgrounds enables attention-like target extraction and front-end static-dynamic decoupling. With these combined features, OTAT delivers target saliency, background suppression and quantitative velocity vectors bound to the correct object, which is essential for motion vision in complex environments.

Route planning simulation

Leveraging OTAT’s in-sensor motion–target extraction and velocity analysis, we investigated path planning in complex multi-object scenes. Path planning links perception to action and underpins autonomous driving31–33, warehouse logistics34 and unmanned aerial navigation35, where performance depends on decoupling static and dynamic scene components while tracking moving targets. We designed a simulation to compare routes generated from different sensing modalities (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Movie). Map information was processed according to each sensor’s response characteristics and supplied to the agent. In the preset navigation strategy, agent without sensors followed a fixed route computed from the first frame. Although this route was the shortest and nominally fastest, the lack of real-time perception and prediction produced multiple collisions, with an average risk of 0.41. With an organic phototransistor (OPT) sensor, real-time obstacle updates enabled collision-free arrival and reduced risk to 0.064, but the absence of predictive planning caused frequent emergency evasive maneuvers and reversals, increasing route cost. By contrast, OTAT-based information processing yielded a collision-free, low-cost path with an overall risk of 0.031, which is 92.4% and 51.6% lower than for the preset and OPT-based routes, respectively. To comprehensively evaluate OTAT’s potential for route-planning applications, we simulated 103 different route-planning tasks and compiled statistics on route length (Fig. 5e) cost, planning time cost (Fig. 5f) and collision probability (Fig. 5g) under three scenarios. Detailed simulation settings can be found in Supplementary Figs. 17–18 and Note S7. Among these simulated tasks, the preset strategy exhibited the highest failure rate. The collision probability reaches 52.5% in dynamic scenarios, rendering its theoretically low route cost of 1.21 and low time cost of 0.24 s impractical for real-world deployment. The OPT strategy, while achieving a 98.0 % collision-free success rate, suffered from inefficient pathing due to its inability to predict obstacle trajectories. The average route cost increased to 1.26 times the optimal distance, and the mean time cost increased to 5.54s per task. As the accuracy of velocity estimation via frame differencing relies heavily on the time interval, the strategy struggled to adapt to varying speed scenarios within fixed-interval simulations. This limitation compromised the stability of path planning quality, resulting in large standard deviations of 0.17 and 7.40 for route length and time cost, respectively. In contrast, the OTAT strategy demonstrated superior performance across all metrics. It maintained a 99.6% success rate with a normalized route cost of 1.22 and a low time cost of 1.89 s with low standard deviations of 0.15 and 0.35, respectively. The residual 0.4% failure rate is attributed to unavoidable cases, such as the starting point being directly next to a dynamic obstacle, where collision is inescapable regardless of prediction. Overall, OTAT provides robust feedforward perception in dynamic environments while minimizing external computing requirements, thereby enabling state-of-the-art motion vision capabilities for autonomous technologies.

In summary, we have designed and constructed OTATs that integrate motion analysis and moving-object extraction within a single device architecture. By incorporating a photosensitive semiconductor–insulator BHJ as the gate-adjacent PVCC layer, the OTAT achieves controllable adaptation dynamics. It not only produces a 45.3% differentiation ratio between static and dynamic visual inputs, but also enables a transient photoresponse speed of approximately 50 ms. These features closely mirror the perceptual characteristics and attentional allocation mechanisms of the human visual system. This facilitates in-sensor motion analysis, extracting both directional and velocity information from single-frame imaging with accuracy exceeding 99.4%. Simulations further demonstrate the application capability of OTAT in autonomous route planning, where seamless integration of static and dynamic environmental cues enables efficient navigation with minimized collision risk. By unifying sensing and processing at the device level, it offers a scalable, energy-efficient, and hardware-flexible avenue for in-sensor motion vision systems in next-generation intelligent vehicles, robotics, and embodied artificial intelligence applications.

Methods

Materials

PDPP3T, ITIC, PTB7-Th and COTIC-4F were purchased from Solarmer Materials Inc. Poly(vinyl-cinnamate) (PVCN) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

Fabrication of tri-bulk-heterojunctioned organic adaptation transistors

First, Corning Glass substrates were cleaned by ultrasonic waves with deionized water, ethanol, and acetone, respectively. Subsequently, 30 nm Al gate electrodes were then thermally deposited on the substrates at a pressure of 6 × 10−6 mbar. A 120-nm-thick PVCN layer with a variable PTB7-Th blend ratio was deposited onto the gate by spin coating at 5000 r.p.m. for 40 seconds from a chlorobenzene solution at a concentration of 50 mg/mL. Thereafter, the layer was UV-crosslinked for 20 minutes (UV Curer KW-4AC, wavelength = 254 nm). After crosslinking, the film was annealed at 100 °C for 1 h in a vacuum. A mixture of PTB7-Th:COTIC-4F (1:1 in weight ratio) was spin-coated (5000 r.p.m., 20 mg/mL in chlorobenzene) onto the PVCN. resulting in a film thickness of around 80 nm, and then annealed at 100 °C for 1 h.

The BHJ films were treated with oxygen plasma at a power of 24 W for 20 seconds. Following this, 50-nm-thick PVA (5000 r.p.m. for 60 s, 15 mg/mL in 1:1 mixture of deionized water and ethanol) and PVCN (applied at 6000 r.p.m. for 40 seconds, 30 mg/mL in chlorobenzene) were spin-coated as the top dielectric layer, respectively. The top PVCN was UV-crosslinked for 2 min and subsequently annealed at a temperature of 100 °C for a duration of 1 h in a vacuum. The top PVCN layers were modified with a self-assembled octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS, Sigma-Aldrich) layer at 120 °C for 3 h in a vacuum.

After mixing in chlorobenzene (10 mg/mL) and stirring for 1 h, PDPP3T:ITIC (1:1 in weight ratio) was spin-coated onto the PVCN dielectric (5000 r.p.m. for 60 seconds). This resulted in the formation of an active layer with a thickness of approximately 50 nm. The film was then subjected to annealing at 135 °C for a duration of 15 min in a vacuum environment. In order to enhance the transparency of the electrode, a 12-nm-thick layer of Au was deposited on the top BHJ film at a pressure of 6 × 10−6 mbar. The top BHJ film was exposed to oxygen plasma at a power of 24 W for 20 seconds. Subsequently, the top BHJ film was coated with 50-nm-thick PVA via the process of spin coating, as previously outlined.
