Comparative evaluation of long-term spinopelvic morphology after salter and pemberton osteotomies in DDH patients: follow-up of 8 years
Authors: Basri Pür, Sinan Yılar, İbrahim Dağ, Kamber Kaşali, Ahmed Arif Uzun, Eyüp Şenocak, Mehmet Demir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare the long-term effects of Salter and Pemberton osteotomies on spinopelvic alignment and the development of scoliosis in patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH).MethodsThis retrospective study included 127 patients who underwent unilateral Salter or Pemberton osteotomy between 2004 and 2012 at 1–3 years of age. Fifty-five patients treated non-surgically using a Pavlik harness or casting served as the control group. Patients were grouped into unilateral Pemberton osteotomy (Group 1), unilateral Salter osteotomy (Group 2), and controls (Group 3). Long-term radiographic outcomes were assessed using Cobb angle (CA), lumbar lordosis (LL), iliac tilt (IT), sacral tilt (ST), pelvic obliquity (PO), sacral slope (SS), and leg length discrepancy (LLD). Propensity Score Matching (PSM) technique was used to balance the demographic differences between groups, including age, sex, and follow-up duration.ResultsThe incidence of scoliosis was significantly higher in the Salter group compared to the Pemberton and control groups (p < 0.05). Significant deviations in iliac tilt and sacral tilt were also noted in the Salter group (p < 0.01). Spinopelvic parameters in the Pemberton group were more comparable to those of the non-operated control group. Logistic regression analysis revealed that increased sacral tilt, iliac tilt, and pelvic obliquity were independent risk factors for scoliosis (p < 0.01, p < 0.05, and p < 0.05, respectively).ConclusionsSalter osteotomy may increase the risk of long-term spinopelvic misalignment and scoliosis in patients with DDH. In contrast, Pemberton osteotomy appears to better preserve spinopelvic alignment. These findings suggest that long-term axial alignment should be considered during surgical planning in DDH management.

Introduction

The Pemberton periacetabular osteotomy (PPO) was first described by P.A. Pemberton in 1965, while the Salter osteotomy (SO) was developed by R.B. Salter in 1961 [1, 2]. Salter osteotomy involves a complete trans-iliac cut to reposition the entire acetabulum, whereas Pemberton acetabuloplasty is a partial osteotomy that utilizes the elasticity of the triradiate cartilage to increase acetabular coverage [3, 4]. The primary goal of both procedures is to support the maintenance of reduction and enhance acetabular coverage, thereby achieving long-term hip joint stability [5].

Some studies have reported that Salter osteotomy may lead to increased pelvic tilt and higher pelvic height [6, 7]. However, similar morphological changes following Pemberton osteotomy have not been sufficiently documented. Considering the anatomical and functional interdependence between the pelvis and spine through the sacroiliac joint, it is evident that alterations in one region may directly affect the other. This underscores the importance of not overlooking pelvic morphology when evaluating spinal deformities [8, 9].

While previous studies have primarily focused on parameters such as acetabular index and femoral head position, aspects such as the preservation of spinopelvic alignment, scoliosis development, and pelvic morphology have been largely neglected. This study aims to assess the long-term effects of Salter and Pemberton osteotomies on spinopelvic structures compared with the Pavlik harness or pelvic-pedal casting. Thus, it offers a broader perspective that considers not only local hip biomechanics but also axial skeletal alignment following surgery.

Materials and methods

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is a significant orthopedic condition that, if left untreated, can lead to long-term pelvic instability, secondary osteoarthritis, and spinal deformities. Pemberton and Salter osteotomies are commonly used surgical techniques in the treatment of DDH, primarily aiming to optimize acetabular coverage. The choice of osteotomy type was determined by factors such as the patient’s age, hip stiffness, the direction of acetabular deficiency, and the surgeon’s preference. Since this was a retrospective study, treatment selections were based on clinical evaluations and surgical practices at the time.

All cases were operated on by a single surgeon, and data from that surgeon’s cases were used. Surgical indications included dislocation or subluxation of the hip in patients over 18 months of age, or cases where conservative treatments (orthoses or spica casting) had been attempted but failed.

The control group consisted of patients diagnosed with DDH before 18 months of age and successfully treated with non-surgical methods. Treatment modality in these patients was determined based on age, hip stiffness, and radiological findings. Generally, Pavlik harness treatment was preferred for flexible hips under 6 months of age; in the 6–18-month age group, pelvic-pedal casting was used for cases where post-reduction stability could not be achieved or where Pavlik treatment had failed. Patients in whom reduction was successful and hip development was deemed adequate were included in the study.

All surgical procedures were performed according to the original technique descriptions by Salter and Pemberton. An anterior iliofemoral approach was used in all cases. During surgery, the ligamentum teres was excised, the transverse acetabular ligament was transected, and all intra-acetabular soft tissue obstacles were removed to allow full reduction of the femoral head.

In Salter osteotomies, a straight osteotomy was performed using a Gigli saw, starting from the greater sciatic notch and extending to the anterior inferior iliac spine.

In contrast, Pemberton acetabuloplasty involved a curved osteotomy extending to the posterior limb of the triradiate cartilage. The osteotomy line was not completed, leaving a hinge, and the mobilized fragment was directed downward, forward, and outward.

In our clinic, for both techniques, a slightly curved triangular iliac crest bone graft was split into two and placed side-by-side at the osteotomy site. This method is thought to enhance mechanical stability, particularly at the osteotomy site. In Salter osteotomies, the grafts were stabilized with two Kirschner wires, whereas in Pemberton osteotomies, no additional fixation was required.

Postoperatively, all patients were immobilized in a pelvic-pedal cast for two months as per our institutional protocol. Afterward, partial mobilization with an abduction orthosis was allowed for another two months. Full weight-bearing mobilization was initiated starting from the fourth postoperative month.

Radiographic evaluation

All patients underwent standing scoliosis radiographs and full-leg length radiographs. Additionally, sitting scoliosis radiographs were obtained to eliminate the compensatory effects of iliac tilt or lower extremity length discrepancy.

In our study, we evaluated scoliosis in all regions of the spine (lumbar, thoracolumbar, thoracic). This allowed us to comprehensively assess the effects of scoliosis in all three regions (lumbar, thoracolumbar, thoracic) and analyze the long-term effects of pelvic osteotomies on the entire spine.

Radiographic parameters included Cobb angle, lumbar lordosis, iliac tilt, sacral tilt, pelvic obliquity, sacral slope, and leg length discrepancy. Scoliosis was classified as structural or compensatory based on the comparison of standing and sitting radiographs. A curvature was considered compensatory if the Cobb angle was ≥ 10° on standing radiographs but decreased below 10° on sitting radiographs, indicating postural or pelvic tilt-induced deformity. In contrast, curves that remained ≥ 10° on both standing and sitting radiographs, particularly if accompanied by vertebral rotation or rigidity, were considered structural scoliosis. This classification was confirmed by consensus between two independent observers.

For statistical analysis, both structural and compensatory scoliosis cases were pooled under the variable “presence of scoliosis,” and compared to patients without scoliosis as a binary outcome.

All radiographic measurements were independently performed by two orthopedic surgeons experienced in spinopelvic morphology assessment. To evaluate measurement reliability, 30 patients were randomly selected and measured by both observers independently, and remeasured by the same observers after a two-week interval. Inter-observer and intra-observer agreement was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) calculated via a two-way mixed-effects model. ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.5 = poor, 0.5–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.9 = good, and > 0.9 = excellent reliability.

Cobb angle (CA)

On anteroposterior (AP) radiographs, the Cobb angle was measured as the angle between Lines drawn parallel to the upper endplate of the vertebra where the curvature starts and the lower endplate of the vertebra where the curvature ends. Curvatures greater than 10° observed on sitting AP radiographs were considered scoliosis. In cases where the Cobb angle was ≥ 10° on standing radiographs but fell below 10° on sitting radiographs, the curvature was classified as a compensatory spinal curve [10].

Lumbar lordosis (LL)

Measured on standing lateral radiographs as the Cobb angle between the superior endplate of L1 and the superior endplate of S1 [10].

Iliac tilt (IT)

Sacral tilt (ST)

Defined as the angle between the Line connecting the articular grooves of the sacral alae and the teardrop Line. Values greater than 0° were considered positive sacral tilt (Fig. 1) [11, 12].

Sacral slope (SS)

Measured on standing lateral radiographs as the angle between the superior endplate of the sacrum and the horizontal plane [10].

Pelvic obliquity (PO)

Evaluated using the Pelvic Height Increase Percentage (PHIP), calculated by measuring the vertical distance between the highest point of the iliac crest and the highest point of the acetabular roof on each side, using lines drawn parallel to the horizontal reference line connecting the lower teardrop points. If the operated side was labeled as A and the contralateral side as B, the PHIP was calculated with the formula: (A – B)/B × 100, to assess intra-pelvic obliquity (Fig. 1) [11, 12].

Leg length discrepancy (LLD)

Measured using orthoroentgenograms as the vertical distance from the center of the femoral head to the ankle joint line on both legs. A positive value indicated lengthening on the operated side, while a negative value indicated shortening [10].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 20. Data were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, percentage, and count. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests.

For comparisons between two independent groups, the Independent Samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. In comparisons among more than two groups, ANOVA was applied for normally distributed variables, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed ones. Post hoc analyses following ANOVA were conducted using the Tukey test when variances were homogeneous and Tamhane’s T2 test when variances were not homogeneous. For post hoc analysis after Kruskal-Wallis, one-way Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA was applied.

For categorical variables, when expected frequencies were > 5, Pearson’s chi-square test was used; when between 3 and 5, Yates’ corrected chi-square test; and when < 3, Fisher’s exact test. In comparisons involving more than two categorical variables, Pearson’s chi-square test was used when expected values were > 5, and Fisher-Freeman-Halton test when expected values were < 5.

The relationship between two continuous variables was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation analysis for non-normally distributed data.

To eliminate the influence of confounding factors such as age, sex, and follow-up duration, Propensity Score Matching (PSM) analysis was performed. This method allowed for the creation of comparable samples among the Salter, Pemberton, and conservative treatment groups with respect to key demographic variables, enabling a more reliable comparison of true differences in spinopelvic parameters.

Following PSM, analysis revealed that the Cobb angle was significantly higher in the Salter group compared to the control group (difference: 5,7°, p < 0.01). In contrast, no significant difference was found between the Pemberton and control groups (difference: 0,8°, p = 0.23). Di̇scussi̇on

This study is among the few that comparatively evaluate the long-term spinopelvic outcomes of Salter and Pemberton osteotomies performed for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). Our findings indicate that patients who underwent Salter osteotomy had a significantly higher rate of scoliosis development. In this group, both the average Cobb angle and associated parameters such as iliac tilt, sacral tilt, and pelvic obliquity were increased. In contrast, the Pemberton osteotomy group demonstrated more stable spinopelvic alignment, with results comparable to those of the conservatively treated group. Moreover, after controlling for confounding variables such as age, sex, and follow-up duration through Propensity Score Matching (PSM) analysis, Cobb angles remained significantly higher in the Salter group compared to both the Pemberton and conservative groups.

Salter osteotomy and increased risk of scoliosis

Patients who underwent Salter osteotomy had significantly higher mean Cobb angles (p < 0.05) and exhibited greater disturbances in spinopelvic parameters. Salter osteotomy directly affects the structure and functional integrity of the pelvis, disrupting the alignment of the spine in patients. Postoperative pelvic asymmetry and altered spinal load distribution create a foundation for the development of spinal deformities such as scoliosis. This condition is believed to be closely related to the patient’s postoperative recovery process in the long term. Several anatomical and biomechanical factors may contribute to this outcome:

Pemberton osteotomy as a more stable alternative

Patients who underwent Pemberton osteotomy demonstrated a more favorable spinopelvic profile. Notably, in the PSM analysis, there was no significant difference in Cobb angle between the Pemberton and conservative groups, suggesting better preservation of alignment. Lang Li et al. (2020) demonstrated that unilateral Salter osteotomy significantly reduced pelvic rigidity compared to the non-operated group. This biomechanical evidence aligns with our clinical findings, showing increased Cobb angles and a higher rate of scoliosis in patients who underwent Salter osteotomy [19].

Ezirmik and Yıldız (2011) compared Salter and Pemberton osteotomies during bilateral hip surgery in the same patients. They found significantly higher rates of avascular necrosis, leg length discrepancy, and complications in the Salter group. This is a noteworthy comparison as both procedures were performed by the same surgeon in the same session [18].

Liu et al. (2023) reported that Salter osteotomy increases pelvic height and shifts the acetabular center laterally and inferiorly, while Pemberton osteotomy results in only an inferior shift. This suggests that Salter osteotomy may disrupt pelvic symmetry to a greater extent [13].

Bellova et al. (2021), in MRI analyses of skeletally mature patients, showed that acetabular version and coverage remained within physiological limits following Pemberton osteotomy [17].

Agarwal and Rastogi (2021) also reported that Pemberton-type acetabular osteotomy caused minimal changes in pelvic parameters and yielded favorable functional outcomes [20].

Wang et al. (2016) conducted the first comparative study in the literature evaluating the spinopelvic effects of Salter and Pemberton osteotomies. According to their findings, both techniques initially increased pelvic height, iliac crest tilt, and sacral tilt; however, these parameters gradually remodeled and approached normal levels during long-term follow-up. They reported no significant difference in the incidence of scoliosis between the two techniques. Nevertheless, the absence of a control group in that study represents a notable methodological limitation, potentially weakening the strength of the comparison. Additionally, the high rate of compensatory curves observed in the study is worth noting [12].

Salter osteotomy, being a transiliac cut procedure, increases pelvic height more significantly compared to Pemberton osteotomy. This increase can lead to a greater leg length discrepancy (LLD) and pelvic obliquity, which may contribute to the development of compensatory scoliosis.

The question of whether scoliosis is compensatory is an important clinical consideration. If scoliosis is compensatory, correcting the LLD—such as through methods like elevated shoe insoles or epiphysiodesis—could restore pelvic balance and reduce the curvature. This hypothesis suggests that addressing LLD could play a key role in preventing or decreasing scoliosis development postoperatively, especially after Salter osteotomy.

Clinically, this highlights the importance of closely monitoring LLD and pelvic alignment in patients who have undergone Salter osteotomy. Early intervention to correct LLD might not only restore pelvic balance but also help reduce scoliosis, leading to better functional outcomes in the long term. This concept warrants further exploration in clinical practice, as it could provide valuable insights into optimizing outcomes for patients undergoing Salter osteotomy.

Our study has several limitations. First, since the findings are based on patients who are still undergoing growth, it is not possible to predict the potential effects of post-skeletal maturity remodeling. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether the observed changes in spinopelvic parameters are solely attributable to surgical intervention or influenced by the natural course of growth and development. Second, although the use of non-surgically treated patients as a control group is reasonable, these patients were younger at the time of treatment and had milder forms of DDH, which may limit their representation as an ideal control population. Finally, functional outcomes (such as back pain, gait disturbances, or daily activity limitations) could not be analyzed due to the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of systematic documentation of such data. This limitation restricts the ability to directly correlate radiographic findings with clinical symptoms. Future studies incorporating patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and functional assessments are warranted to address this gap.
