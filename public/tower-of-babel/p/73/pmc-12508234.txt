Portulaca oleracea-derived indoline amide ameliorates obesity and NAFLD in rats through Nrf2-dependent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms
Authors: Mona Eid Alshamari, Laila Naif Al-Harbi, Ghedeir M Alshammari, Maha H Alhussain, Ali Saleh, Mohammed Abdo Yahya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we evaluated the potential of indoline amide extract phenolic extract Portulaca oleracea (POIA-PE) in preventing obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in rats and depicted the possible mechanism of action. Adult male Wistar rats were utilized in this study and were divided (8/groups) into control, POIA-PE, HFD, HFD + POIA-PE (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg), and HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol (2 mg/kg). Treatments with POIA-PE were given by gavage, orally, and for 12 weeks (thrice/week). POIA-PE, at all tested doses, not only reduced body and fat weights but also lowered fasting plasma glucose and insulin, serum and hepatic levels of triglycerides and cholesterol, and serum levels of LDL-c in HFD-fed rats. They also prevented the increase in levels of malondialdehyde, tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, caspase-3, Bax, and mRNA, as well as the nuclear levels of NF-κB, but stimulated the levels of Bcl-2, total glutathione, heme oxygenase-1, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in the livers of HFD-fed rats. The increasing doses of POIA-PE also reduced the hepatic transcription of SREBP1, fatty acid synthase, and acetyl-CoA carboxylase and stimulated those of PPARα in HFD-fed rats. Mechanistically, POIA-PE reduced the mRNA and expression of Keap1 but increased the mRNA, cytoplasmic, and nuclear levels of Nrf2. All these effects were dose-dependent and were prevented by co-treatment with brusatol. POIA-PE can alleviate NAFLD by attenuating obesity, hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hepatic oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis through the stimulation of the Keap1/Nrf2 pathways.Keywords: Indoline amide, Portulaca Oleracea, NAFLD, Obesity, Oxidative stress, Nrf2Subject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Drug discovery, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Obesity, a widespread metabolic disorder characterized by elevated body mass index (BMI) due to excessive caloric intake and reduced physical activity, has reached epidemic proportions globally1. In Saudi Arabia, obesity prevalence stands alarmingly high, with rates soaring to 46%2. This condition significantly increases the risk of chronic ailments such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, insulin resistance (IR), and dyslipidemia, culminating in severe organ damage, including to the liver, kidneys, nervous system, and heart. These findings underscore the urgent need for effective interventions to combat this escalating health crisis.

On the other hand, Obesity plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), profoundly impacting metabolic health by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inflammatory cytokines3. Excessive calorie intake associated with obesity triggers adipose tissue expansion, leading to peripheral IR, heightened immune cell infiltration, and inflammatory adipose tissue responses, impairing its ability to effectively store excess fat4. Consequently, lipid accumulation occurs in the liver and muscles, accompanied by increased adipose tissue lipolysis because of IR4,5. These processes release free fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines/adipokines and enhance their influx to the liver6. Together, these mechanisms stimulate de novo lipid synthesis in the liver and initiate a cascade of hepatic events, including promoting endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, fatty acid oxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipotoxicity, and inflammation, which exacerbate oxidative stress via ROS generation6,7. These ROS not only damage the hepatocytes but also impair hepatic insulin signaling and initiate other pathological mechanisms such as inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis, which ultimately lead to liver fibrosis and NASH4,6. This comprehensive understanding indicates the critical need for targeted interventions to mitigate obesity-related hepatic complications and prevent severe liver pathology.

Recent research highlights nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) as a crucial mediator in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) development8,9. Nrf2 acts as a pivotal transcription factor in cellular survival, enhancing antioxidant defenses and maintaining cellular redox balance through the antioxidant response element (APE)10. Additionally, Nrf2 regulates glucose and lipid homeostasis and protects cells by modulating pathways involved in gluconeogenesis, lipogenesis, inflammation, and apoptosis, interacting with various receptors, proteins, enzymes, and transcription factors11–17. The activity of Nrf2 is regulated by its cytoplasmic inhibitor, Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), which promotes Nrf2 degradation via the proteasome10. Genetic studies demonstrate that Nrf2 deficiency exacerbates hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in murine models18–21. In NAFLD animal models, high-fat diet-induced downregulation of Nrf2 transcription and nuclear translocation correlates with increased Keap1 expression in liver tissues8,9,22,23. Conversely, therapeutic activation of Nrf2 shows promise in mitigating NAFLD symptoms and associated pathological changes24–26.

Portulaca oleracea (PO), also known as purslane or Rigla, is celebrated globally for its medicinal properties, utilized in various forms including raw, cooked, or as tea, with all parts—stems, leaves, and flowers—employed in traditional therapies27. The research underscores PO’s efficacy against parasitic infections, inflammation, and fever, highlighting its diverse pharmacological activities, including antibacterial, hypoglycemic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing properties28–30. Moreover, PO supplementation demonstrates significant anti-diabetic and anti-obesity effects in clinical trials and animal models, reducing weight gain, improving insulin sensitivity, and ameliorating hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia31–34. It also protects against liver damage and fibrosis by attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation, enhancing antioxidant defenses in high-fat diet-fed rats33. Furthermore, PO supplementation mitigates lipid peroxidation and inflammatory cytokines in conditions such as alcoholic fatty liver disease and STZ-induced diabetes. It attenuates renal injury in diabetic mice, highlighting its potential as a versatile therapeutic agent35–37.

In recent decades, extensive research has focused on uncovering the active compounds responsible for the pharmacological benefits of Portulaca oleracea (PO). Phytochemical screenings have identified high concentrations of flavonoids like quercetin and kaempferol, indoline amide glucosides (alkaloids), polysaccharides, and omega-3 fatty acids within PO28–30,38. Notably, water-soluble indoline amide glucosides, including 14 identified oleraceins (A, B, V, D, H, I, K, L, N, O, P, Q, R, S), exhibit potent antioxidant properties surpassing that of vitamin C, contributing to anti-inflammatory effects39–42. Studies highlight the neuroprotective potential of these phenolic indoline amides from PO, demonstrating their ability to prevent neural damage and cognitive decline in experimental models by inhibiting lipid peroxidation and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT)43. These findings underscore indoline amides from PO as a promising source of bioactive compounds with significant therapeutic implications for oxidative stress-related disorders.

Therefore, in this study, we first aimed to examine the protective effect of the total indoline amide extract isolated from PO on liver structure and hepatic markers of oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis, as well as on glucose and lipid metabolism in rats fed HFD. In addition, we examined whether this protection involves modulating the Keap1/Nrf2 antioxidant axis.

Materials and methods

Animals

The study utilized white albino rats weighing 130 ± 15 g, sourced from and housed at the animal facility of King Saud University, KSA. Rats were housed in standard laboratory cages furnished with suitable wood shavings, maintained under controlled conditions with a regulated temperature (20–22 °C) and humidity (40–60%). This ensured a stable and conducive environment throughout the experimental period, essential for Maintaining the health and well-being of the animals while minimizing external variables that could affect the study outcomes. The Light-dark cycle was always Maintained at 12 h for each rat. Diet and freshwater were provided ad libitum with free access during the adaptation and experimental periods.

Ethical statements

All experimental protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Committee (REC) of King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (Ethics Reference No. KSU-SE-22-115). The current study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Furthermore, the study followed the US National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication no. 85 − 23, revised 1996) and strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines and regulations stipulated by the Research Ethics Committee.

Accordance statement

The authors confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the full ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Diets

This study utilized two commercial diets: a control diet (CD) and HFD, both sourced from Research Diets, NJ, USA. The CD (D12450B) contains a total energy content of 3.85 kcal/g (16.1 kJ/g), comprising 10% fats, 70% carbohydrates, and 20% proteins. Its primary constituents (g/kg) include 200 g of casein, 50 g of cellulose, 350 g of sucrose, 315 g of corn starch, and 20 g/kg of fat source. The HFD (D12492) has a total energy content of 5.24 kcal/g (21.9%), comprising 60% fat, 20% protein, and 20% carbohydrates. Its Major ingredients are 200 g of casein, 50 g of cellulose, 0.0 g of cornstarch, 68.8 g of sucrose, and 245 g of fat source.

Extraction of Indoline amide from portulaca Oleracea

The entire PO plant was procured fresh from local markets and authenticated by a qualified faculty member from the Department of Pharmacognosy at KSU. Following the method described by Wang et al.43, the plant Material was washed, dried at 37 °C, and powdered after harvesting 10 kg. The powdered plant Material then underwent multiple extractions with 60% ethanol, each lasting one hour, repeated three times. The resulting extracts were concentrated under vacuum to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 g/ml. Subsequently, the supernatants (4 L) were processed using AB-8 Macroporous resin column chromatography, eluted with water and 50% ethanol (30 L each), and then dried in a water bath at 80 °C. The resultant solid Material was isolated, weighed, and dissolved in water to form a stock solution of 300 mg/mL for further analysis and experimentation. This extract was named PO indoline amide phenolic extract (POIA-PE).

Experimental design

The experiment involved seven groups, each consisting of eight rats. The first group served as the control, receiving a standard diet and distilled water as a vehicle. The second group received the standard diet and POIA-PE (300 mg/kg). The third group was fed HFD and administered distilled water (vehicle). The fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh groups were given HFD and co-treated with POIA-PE at concentrations of 100 mg/kg, 200 mg/kg, 300 mg/kg, and 300 mg/kg with brusatol (a selective Nrf2 synthesis inhibitor given two hours before POIA-PE), respectively. All treatments were administered orally three times a week for 12 weeks.

Selection of experimental regimen

Commercial HFD feeding for 12 weeks induced hepatic NASH in rats with reduced expression and activation of Nrf223. In addition, administration of brusatol at this selected dose suppressed the transactivation of Nrf2 through a non-Keap1-dependent mechanism in various tissues, including the liver, kidney, and intestine100–104. Changes in daily food intake will be monitored, and weekly body weight measurements will be recorded across all groups throughout the study duration.

Collection of blood samples and liver tissues

On the last day of the study, all animals underwent a 10-hour fasting period before being anesthetized with an 80:10 (v/v) ketamine/xylazine hydrochloride mixture. Blood samples of equal volume (1 ml each) were directly drawn from the right ventricle of each rat and divided into gel-containing or EDTA-containing tubes for serum and plasma collection, respectively. Following blood collection, samples were allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min before centrifugation at 500 x g for 10 min. The resulting serum and plasma were then carefully transferred into labeled Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C for subsequent biochemical assessments. At sacrifice, liver samples were collected under standardized conditions. To ensure consistency, tissue fragments (~ 200–250 mg) were excised from the left lateral hepatic lobe of each animal. This lobe was chosen due to its accessibility and homogeneity. The samples were divided appropriately for biochemical assays, RNA/protein analyses, and histological examination. All animals were processed in the same Manner to minimize variability. Additionally, selected Liver sections were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for subsequent histological examination.

Biochemical measurements in the serum and blood samples

Serum and plasma samples underwent biochemical analysis using ELISA kits designed specifically for rats. Insulin and glucose levels in blood plasma were assessed using ELISA kits (No. 589501, Ann Arbor, TX, USA, and No. 10009582, Cayman Chemicals, CA). Levels of glycosylated Hb were measured by an ELISA-specific kit (MBS2033689, MyBioSource, CA, USA). The HOMA-IR (homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance was calculated as (fasting insulin [ng/mL] × fasting glucose [mg/dL])/405107. Serum cholesterol levels (CHOL) were determined using the ECCH-100 kit from BioAssay Systems, CA, USA. ELISA kits from MyBioSource, CA, USA (MBS726298 and MBS014345), were used to measure low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-c) and triglycerides (TG). Serum glycerol levels were quantified using the ab65337 kit from Abcam, Cambridge, UK. Additionally, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were measured in serum using kits from MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA (MBS269614, MBS9343646), and Cosmo Bio, CA, USA (CSB-E13023r-1). Each measurement was conducted in duplicate for eight samples per group, adhering strictly to the protocols provided with each kit.

Hepatic lipid extraction and measurement

Liver lipid extraction was followed by Folch et al.108, using a 2:1 chloroform solvent mixture. Liver tissues (100 mg) were homogenized in 1 ml of this solvent, followed by the addition of an equal volume of chloroform, vigorous mixing, and centrifugation at 500 x g for 10 min. The resulting chloroform layer, containing Lipids, was extracted, the solvent evaporated under nitrogen, and the Lipid film was dissolved in 0.5 mL of isopropanol. This solution was used to measure FFAs, TGs, and CHOL levels using respective kits, with each analysis performed twice on n = 8 samples per group, adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Analysis of the liver homogenates

The tissue samples, weighing 100 mg each, were thoroughly homogenized in 0.5 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) to prepare cell homogenates for biochemical analysis. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatants were carefully retrieved and promptly stored at −70 °C until they were ready for analysis. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were employed to quantify tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (BMS622, ThermoFisher, UK), interleukin-6 (IL-6) (E-EL-R0015, Lab Science, TX, USA), malondialdehyde (MDA) (EK720188), total glutathione (GSH) (EK720816), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) (EK720658), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) (EK720889) (AFG Scientific, IL, USA. Rat-specific ELISA kits measured glucose-6-phosphatase (6-Pase) (MBS097902, MyBioSource, CA, USA), glucokinase (MBS453149, MyBioSource, CA, USA), and apoptosis markers Bax (E4513, BioVision, CA, USA), Bcl2 (LS-F11016, LS Bio, MA, USA), and caspase-3 (LS-F4135, LS Bio, MA, USA). All assays were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocols using n = 8 samples per group.

Biochemical analysis in the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions

We employed specific kits designed for isolating cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions from various tissues to extract nuclear proteins (50296, Active Motif, CA, USA). Rat-specific kits from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), including ab2072223 and ab133112, were used to assess the activities of Nrf2 and NF-κ in the nuclear extracts. The levels of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) were quantified using an ELISA kit (MBS7218529m, MyBiosource, CA, USA). Each experimental group consisted of 8 samples, and all procedures were strictly followed according to the protocols provided by the respective kit manufacturers.

Real-time PCR in adipose and hepatic tissue

The mRNA expression of Keap1, Nrf2, SREP1, FAS, ACC, and PPARα was assessed by qPCR. In our earlier study23, we detailed the primer gene number, forward and reverse sequences, and amplification size, which are shown in Table 1. After RNA was extracted from the frozen liver samples using the TRIZOL reagent, the first strand of cDNA was synthesized using the K1621 cDNA synthesis kit from Thermo Fisher. All amplification reactions were carried out under the manufacturer’s instructions and previous descriptions using a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad) and the SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix kit (172–5200, Bio-Rad)23. The expression of all target genes was normalized to that of the reference gene, β-actin using the transcription levels of every target. A total of six samples were used in each group for the amplification reactions.

Liver histology

Following fixation in formalin for 24 h, Liver tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and gradually rehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions. Nuclei were stained blue with hematoxylin for 5 min after rehydration, followed by counterstaining the cytoplasmic components pink with eosin for an additional 3 min, and then rinsing in flowing tap water. Subsequently, sections were cleared with xylene, mounted using a resinous medium, and dehydrated with graded ethanol solutions. Histological examination was completed by observing the slides under a light microscope, allowing for a detailed analysis of cellular architecture and tissue morphology109. For this part, three slides were prepared and analyzed per animal. All tissues were cut as semi-serial sections at approximately 50 μm intervals to provide a representative sampling of each liver fragment. All slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined by a blinded pathologist.

Statistical analysis

All collected data underwent statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism (Version 8). Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Analysis was conducted using Two-way ANOVA, and significance levels were determined using Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.05). Results are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) for all data sets.

Results

Alterations in adiposity and diabetic markers

HFD rats showed significantly higher body weights and fat deposition (masses) in their WAT and had higher BMI, Lee index, and adiposity index as compared to control or POIA-PE-treated normal rats (Table 2). They also had significantly higher plasma levels of glucose, insulin, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR. Only fasting glucose and HOMA-IR were significantly lower in POIA-PE-treated control rats compared to control rats administered the vehicle (Table 2). HFD rats that were fed with POIA-PE at increasing doses (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg) showed a significant and progressive decline in their final body, fat weights, Lee index, BMI, adiposity index, and HOMA-IR, as well as in their plasma glucose, insulin, and HbA1c as compared to the HFD-fed rats (Table 2). However, no significant changes in the levels of these parameters were seen when the control group was compared with the HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) (Table 2). On the other hand, final body, fat weights, Lee index, BMI, adiposity index, and HOMA-IR, as well as their plasma glucose, insulin, and HbA1c were significantly higher in HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol-treated rats as compared to the control, POIA-PE (100 m/kg), and HFD + POIA-PE (100, 200, or 300 mg/kg). No significant alteration in these measured markers was seen between HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol-treated rats compared to HFD rats (Table 2).

Changes in serum and hepatic lipid profile

Liver weights, as well as serum and hepatic levels of TGs, CHOL, and serum levels of LDL-c were significantly lower, while HDL-c levels were significantly higher in the livers of POIA-PE-treated rats (Table 3). The liver weights, serum and hepatic levels of TGs, CHOL, and serum levels of LDL-c were significantly higher, while HDL-c levels were significantly lower in HFD-fed rats as compared to control and POIA-PE-treated rats (Table 3). In a dose-dependent manner, liver weights, serum and hepatic levels of TGs, CHOL, and serum levels of LDL-C were significantly lower, while HDL-C levels were significantly increased in HFD + POIA-PE-treated rats that received increasing doses of the drug (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg). No significant alterations in liver weights and the levels of all these biochemical lipid markers were observed between the control and HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg)-treated rats. In addition, the liver weights, serum and hepatic levels of TGs, CHOL, and serum levels of LDL-c were significantly higher while HDL-c levels were significantly lower in HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol-treated rats as compared to the control, POIA-PE (300 mg/kg), and all other HFD-treated groups (HFD + POIA-PE (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg)-treated rats (Table 3). Interestingly, the liver weight and serum and hepatic levels mentioned above were not statistically different between the HFD and HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol-treated rats.

Alterations in liver function enzymes

Serum levels of ALT, AST, and γ-GTT were not significantly different between the control POIA-PE (300 mg/kg)-treated rats but were significantly increased in HFD-fed rats (Fig. 1A-C). The levels of all these enzymes were significantly decreased in a dose-dependent manner in the serum of HFD + POIA-PE (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg) as compared to HFD-fed rats and were significantly reversed in the serum of HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol-treated rats (Fig. 1A-C). No significant alterations in the levels of these enzymes were seen when the HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) was compared to control or POIA-PE (300 mg/kg)-treated rats or when HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg) + brusatol-treated rats were compared with the HFD rats (Fig. 1A-C).

Alterations in the hepatic levels of lipid peroxidation and antioxidant markers

The liver samples of the POIA-PE (300 mg/kg)-treated rats showed a significant decline in lipid peroxide levels, as measured by MDA, and exhibited significantly higher levels of GSH, SOD, and HO-1 (Fig. 2A-D). The levels of MDA showed a significant increase, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in the levels of GSH, SOD, and HO-1, in the livers of HFD-fed rats compared to control and POIA-PE (300 mg/kg)-treated rats (Fig. 2). The livers of HFD that were treated with POIA-PE (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg) showed a progressive decline in the levels of MDA and an increase in the levels of GSH, SOD, and HO-1 as compared to HFD-fed rats with the maximum increase to be seen with the highest dose (300 mg/kg) (Fig. 2A-D). No significant alteration in the levels of all the hepatic biochemical ends was observed between the control, POIA-PE (300 mg/kg), and HFD + POIA-PE (300 mg/kg)-treated rats (Fig. 2A-D). Discussion

This study investigates the therapeutic potential of total indoline amides extracted from Portulaca oleracea (PO) as a novel pharmacological agent for combating obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in rats fed a high-fat diet (HFD). The indoline amides extract demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing body weight, fasting glucose levels, and HbA1C, alongside mitigating total IR in the HFD-induced rat model. Moreover, it effectively attenuated obesity-associated hyperlipidemia and hepatic lipid accumulation. Mechanistically, the extract alleviated hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB activation, reducing levels of tumor TNF-α, IL-6, and MDA, and promoting Nrf2 activation. This activation was correlated with increased levels of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, GSH, and HO-1. Importantly, these beneficial effects were dose-dependent and abolished upon co-administration of the extract with brusatol, a selective Nrf2 inhibitor, underscoring Nrf2 activation as a pivotal upstream mechanism mediating the observed therapeutic benefits. This study highlights the promising therapeutic potential of PO-derived total indoline amides in mitigating obesity, peripheral IR, and NAFLD through modulation of Nrf2-dependent pathways.

The high-fat diet (HFD) animal model is widely recognized for its utility in studying obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), characterized by increased adiposity, inflammation, and reduced fat oxidation in white adipose tissue44,45. Crude extracts from PO have been extensively studied for their ability to reduce adiposity and body weight by enhancing lipid metabolism, including the promotion of fat oxidation mechanisms46. Additionally, PO-derived omega-3 fatty acids, such as alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), have been shown to increase energy expenditure and support fat metabolism47. This study explores indoline amides from PO as a novel therapeutic strategy to mitigate adiposity and improve metabolic parameters and IR in rats, independent of appetite modulation. While the complete spectrum of effects was not fully elucidated, the anti-obesity of PO indoline amides could be attributed to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in the WAT, consistent with the observed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects corroborated by findings in liver tissue from our rat model. Although no evaluated form of PO species exists, other studies utilizing indole amide from other plant resources may support our findings. Indeed, studies on indoline amides from Catharanthus roseus suggest they can reduce body weight and adiposity by modulating adipose tissue lipid metabolism and promoting adipose tissue browning48. Furthermore, research by Chen et al.7 highlighted the anti-obesity potential of indoline amides through their influence on adipocyte differentiation and lipolysis, suggesting promising therapeutic applications for conditions related to obesity. Human trials, albeit limited, have shown preliminary evidence, as indicated by Kim et al.49, suggesting that supplementation with indoline amides may modestly reduce body fat percentage and waist circumference, thereby suggesting potential benefits for weight management.

Due to peripheral IR, obesity is characterized by sustained hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia attributed to heightened hepatic gluconeogenesis, adipogenesis, and IR50. The exacerbated influx of free fatty acids (FFAs) and inflammatory cytokines, coupled with impaired hepatic fatty acid (FA) oxidation, is widely implicated in these metabolic perturbations, leading to increased hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation51. Moreover, in obese rodents with NAFLD, stimulated hepatic lipolysis is linked to the overactivation of the transcription factor SREBP1, which promotes the synthesis of FAs and TGs, along with reduced activity of PPARα, known for its role in stimulating FA oxidation and mitigating lipid accumulation52–54. In this study, treatment of rats with indoline amide extract from POI not only reduced fasting glucose and HbA1C levels but also ameliorated peripheral IR and decreased hepatic and serum levels of TGs, CHOL, and LDL-C. These effects coincided with the attenuation of hepatic SREBP1 activation and enhanced PPARα activity. Remarkably, the hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic effects of the indoline amide extract, along with modulation of these transcription factors, were observed in both serum and liver of control rats, suggesting efficacy under normal and metabolic stress conditions. Importantly, these effects were independent of changes in fecal lipid levels or serum insulin levels, implicating potential improvements in peripheral insulin sensitivity. However, as discussed later, these effects are primarily attributed to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of indoline amides, mediated through Nrf2 regulation.

Portulaca oleracea (PO) and its bioactive compounds are extensively studied for their potential in managing metabolic disorders like hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, hyperlipidemia, and IR. Research by Zhu et al.55, Chen et al.7, Al-Shebly et al.56, and Wu et al.20. highlights PO’s ability to reduce gluconeogenesis, enhance insulin release, and improve peripheral insulin sensitivity in animal models. Li et al.57 demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial that PO significantly lowers fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels in patients with type 2 diabetes compared to a placebo. Studies by Das et al.58 support the efficacy of PO in improving lipid profiles in both animals and humans, positioning it as a promising supplement for managing dyslipidemia. Human trials consistently show that PO supplementation reduces total cholesterol and triglyceride levels49. Mechanistically, PO’s soluble fiber, especially mucilage, aids in the excretion of bile acids and cholesterol, thereby reducing their absorption59,60. PO’s bioactive compounds, such as betacyanins, reduce fasting glucose and enhance insulin sensitivity in diabetic rodents61,62. Flavonoid fractions with quercetin and kaempferol from PO improve insulin resistance via AMPK activation63. Omega-3 fatty acids, notably alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), mitigate hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and inflammation via the AMPK/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway64. ALA-rich extracts enhance insulin secretion and beta-cell function65, while portuloside A improves insulin sensitivity and reduces hepatic glucose production66. Supporting our data, Indoline amides isolated from Catharanthus roseus and Vinca spp. exhibited promising effects on glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, dyslipidemia, and lipid accumulation in the liver and adipose tissue48,67. Li et al.68 also reported the role of indoline amide in enhancing insulin sensitivity through activating AMPK signaling. These compounds also possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which may alleviate oxidative stress and inflammation associated with peripheral and hepatic IR69. Animal studies support their role in improving glucose tolerance and reducing IR70.

Hepatic inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis are critical mechanisms in NAFLD and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)71–73. These conditions progress due to exaggerated hepatic ROS and inflammatory cytokine production post the development of peripheral IR, akin to a second hit promoting fibrosis, apoptosis, and hepatic IR74. Furthermore, ROS from endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial damage, along with antioxidant depletion, is the major pathway promoting hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis in NAFLD and NASH74,75. Several experimental and clinical studies have validated PO for its potent antioxidant properties, as evidenced by its effectiveness in scavenging free radicals and reducing oxidative stress markers. PO also boosts crucial antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and catalase76,77. In diabetic animal models, crude extracts from PO protected the brain, kidneys, and liver by enhancing antioxidant enzymes and reducing lipid peroxidation products78. However, many authors have attributed this antioxidant potential of PO to its high content of phenolic compounds (e.g., caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, quercetin, kaempferol) and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA)79–81. Also, Clinical trials confirm that supplementation of PO enhances antioxidant status and reduces oxidative stress markers in humans82. In addition to its antioxidant effects, PO extracts exhibit potent anti-inflammatory properties by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, through the inhibition of NF-κB activation83. Specific compounds, such as rutin and beta-carotene from PO, also display significant anti-inflammatory properties by reducing the expression of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)80. Clinical studies have shown improved inflammatory markers and health outcomes in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), following purslane supplementation82.

In this study, we also described indoline amide components of PO as potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic agents that could alleviate these pathological mechanisms in the livers of rats. This was validated by the ability of the PO, in a dose-dependent manner, to attenuate the increments in the levels of MDA, TNF-α, and IL-6, as well as the nuclear activities of NF-κB, while stimulating the levels of several endogenous antioxidants, including GSH, HO-1, and SOD1 in the livers of both the control and HFD-fed rats. In addition, treatment with the indoline amide, at increasing doses of the PO extract, significantly attenuated markers of apoptosis (e.g., caspase-3 and Bax) in the livers of HFD-fed rats in a progressive manner. In the livers of both the control and HFD-fed rats, it stimulated the levels of Bcl2. These results from the livers of the control and HFD rats indicate the independent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects of indoline amides through stimulating antioxidants, suppressing NF-κB, and upregulating Bcl2. Even not studied well, the protective effect of the phenolic extract of PO that contains indoline amides prevented memory loss, neural death, and cognitive impairment and enhanced learning abilities in senescent mice by decreasing the hippocampal levels of MDA and stimulating the levels of GSH, SOD, and CAT43.

Yet, it remained of interest to understand the precise molecular mechanism by which this indoline amide extract acts. For this reason, we have selected the Nrf2 molecules as our target given their emerging role in regulating glucose and lipid homeostasis, as well as oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. Indeed, Nrf2 is the chief transcription factor that enhances the expression of antioxidant enzymes in the cells10. In addition, Nrf2 enhances hepatic insulin signaling and reduces gluconeogenesis by inhibiting key enzymes, such as glucose-6-phosphatase (G6P) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK)17. Also, Nrf2 activators inhibited hepatic lipogenesis by decreasing the expression and activation of liver X receptor-a (LXRa), SREBP1, fatty acid synthase (FAs), and acetyl CoA carboxylase (ACC1)17,84–86. Moreover, Nrf2 stimulates FA uptake and oxidation by regulating CD36 expression and upregulating PPARα and other enzymes involved in β-oxidation16,18,87,88. Nrf2 is also considered a master anti-inflammatory factor that can directly inhibit NF-κB89–91. Additionally, Nrf2 suppresses NAFLD-mediated intrinsic cell apoptosis by upregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-211. In addition to this evidence, we supported our hypothesis by the novel reports that indicated a reduced expression of Nrf2 signaling in the livers of NAFLD animals, while activation of Nrf2 is an effective therapy to treat and prevent the progression of the disease by silencing oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis8,9,23–26.

For this reason, we measured the mRNA, cytoplasmic, and nuclear expression of Nrf2, as well as the transcription and protein levels of its canonical inhibitor Keap1, in the livers of all experimental groups. In parallel, we assessed the biochemical parameters and histological outcomes in rats treated with the highest effective dose of the indoline amide extract, either alone or in combination with brusatol. Brusatol is classically considered an Nrf2 inhibitor, as it suppresses nuclear translocation of Nrf2 without directly altering Keap1. In agreement with previous studies, HFD feeding markedly suppressed cytoplasmic levels and nuclear translocation of Nrf2, while concomitantly enhancing Keap1 transcription and protein abundance. Notably, hyperglycemia has been reported as a key driver of Nrf2 degradation in the cytoplasm, acting through direct stimulation of Keap1 expression92. Consistent with this mechanism, the decline in Nrf2 mRNA observed in the livers of HFD-fed rats in our study can be attributed to hyperglycemia-induced upregulation of Keap1.

By contrast, treatment with the POIA-PE not only restored cytoplasmic Nrf2 levels but also significantly enhanced its nuclear localization in both control and HFD-fed rats. These effects were closely associated with a concomitant downregulation of Keap1 transcription and protein expression, suggesting that indoline amides may exert their protective actions by repressing Keap1 expression. Importantly, co-treatment with brusatol completely abolished the protective effects of POIA-PE on glucose regulation, lipid and hepatic injury markers, histological improvements, and oxidative stress, inflammatory, and apoptotic endpoints. This pharmacological reversal provides strong evidence that the protective efficacy of POIA-PE is mediated primarily through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in a Keap1-dependent manner, and independent of its hypoglycemic effects. In support of this, the hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities of POIA-PE appear to converge mechanistically on the stimulation of Nrf2. These findings are further corroborated by the recent report of Cannavacciuolo et al.93, who demonstrated that oleraceins derived from PO leaves act as potent Nrf2 activators.

Hyperglycemia remains a key upstream driver of Nrf2 suppression in NAFLD, as it directly induces Keap1 expression and promotes Nrf2 degradation via the REDD1/GSK3/β-TrCP axis92. This mechanism likely explains the diminished Nrf2 activity we observed in HFD-fed rats. Beyond alleviating hyperglycemia, however, several additional upstream mechanisms may account for POIA-PE–mediated Nrf2 activation. First, electrophilic and phenolic phytochemicals in PO may directly modify redox-sensitive cysteine residues of Keap1—particularly Cys151, Cys273, and Cys288—thereby impairing its ubiquitin ligase activity and stabilizing Nrf2. Second, POIA-PE constituents may enhance p62/SQSTM1–dependent autophagic degradation of Keap1, a process well described by Ichimura et al.94 and Kageyama et al.95, ultimately liberating Nrf2 for nuclear translocation. Additionally, kaempferol, another flavonoid in PO, enhanced Nrf2 activation and induced the expression of phase II detoxifying enzymes96. Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an omega-3 fatty acid from PO has also been found to activate Nrf2, facilitating the transcription of antioxidant genes and enhancing cellular resistance to oxidative stress97. Third, PO has been shown to activate AMPK, which in turn can phosphorylate and upregulate Nrf2, further promoting its transcriptional activity98. Fourth, while brusatol was used in our study to suppress Nrf2, it is now recognized as a global inhibitor of protein synthesis rather than a specific Nrf2 suppressor99. This observation raises the possibility that POIA-PE may activate Nrf2 through mechanisms beyond Keap1 repression, potentially involving direct stimulation of Nrf2 expression. To definitively distinguish between direct Keap1 targeting and indirect metabolic regulation of Nrf2 by POIA-PE, future investigations should employ more selective tools such as ML385, a validated inhibitor of Nrf2, together with mechanistic assays probing Keap1–phytochemical adduct formation, p62–Keap1 interactions, and AMPK/GSK3 signaling activity.

Conclusions

This study underscores the therapeutic potential of indoline amides extracted from PO in combating obesity and NAFLD. Through their profound effects on reducing body weight, improving glucose metabolism, and attenuating hepatic lipid accumulation, indoline amides demonstrate promise as novel pharmacological agents. Mechanistically, their actions are mediated by activating the Nrf2 pathway, thereby enhancing antioxidant defenses and mitigating inflammation and apoptosis. These findings highlight indoline amides as a promising avenue for developing treatments targeting metabolic disorders and liver pathologies, warranting further exploration in clinical settings to validate their efficacy and safety.

Study limitations

Despite the protective effects of indoline amide extract against obesity and NAFLD demonstrated in this study, several limitations warrant consideration in future research. One important limitation is its reliance on an animal model (HFD-fed rats), which may not fully replicate human metabolic responses. While promising, translating these findings to clinical practice is hindered by the absence of robust human trials specifically evaluating the efficacy and safety of Portulaca oleracea–derived indoline amides. Mechanistically, although Nrf2 pathway activation is implicated in the therapeutic effects, the precise molecular mechanisms and potential off-target interactions remain unclear. Furthermore, the study focuses exclusively on indoline amides from P. oleracea, lacking comparative data with indoline amides from other plant sources, which restricts broader insights into their therapeutic potential. Dose-dependent effects are noted, but optimal dosing for humans and long-term safety profiles are yet to be established. The generalizability of results to diverse human populations is also uncertain. In addition, limited resources prevented the use of Western blotting for confirmatory validation of protein expression. Instead, we employed commercially validated ELISA kits, which are widely accepted as sensitive and reproducible screening tools and were supported by consistent histological findings. Nonetheless, future work with expanded resources should incorporate Western blotting and other confirmatory assays. Another limitation is the absence of phytochemical characterization of the extract (HPLC/LC-MS/MS profiling, quantitative standardization of indoline amides and phenolics). While we used a validated extraction method previously reported to yield indoline amide–rich fractions and confirmed biological activity through multiple functional endpoints, future studies should include full phytochemical profiling to strengthen reproducibility and translational relevance. Finally, direct histological confirmation of hepatic lipid accumulation by Oil Red O staining was not performed due to resource constraints. Although our conclusions are strongly supported by biochemical quantification of hepatic lipids, H&E-based histology, and consistent molecular markers, the addition of lipid-specific staining in future work will provide further validation and visual confirmation of steatosis.

Addressing these limitations through further research-including clinical trials, elucidation of upstream molecular mechanisms, advanced phytochemical characterization, confirmatory protein validation, and lipid-specific histological staining-will be essential for validating and expanding the therapeutic applicability of indoline amides in treating metabolic disorders and liver diseases.
