Meroterpenoids Isolated from Avicennia marina Fruit Fungal Strain Aspergillus sp.
Authors: Fan-Fan Wang, Xiaoxia Liu, Xuefeng Jin, Jing-Jing Tao, Ji-Wen Wu, Li-Ping Mo, Pingfang Tao, Xin-Xiu Nong, Feng Qin, Zhongqing Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
3 new diisoprenyl cyclohexene-type meroterpenoids, aspergienynes R-T (1–3), one new natural product (9), and six known compounds (4–8 and 10), were obtained from Avicennia marina (Forssk.) Vierh. fruit fungal strain Aspergillus sp. YLNU-Q11. These new compounds’ structures were elucidated using spectroscopic analyses, NMR/DP4+ calculations, and ECD studies. In the acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory assay, compound 8 showed moderate inhibitory activity (IC50 = 9.54 μM). Further molecular docking analysis suggested that hydrogen bonding interactions with residues TYR374, GLY79, and GLY150 are crucial for the binding of 8 to AChE.

Introduction

Diisoprenylcyclohexene-type meroterpenoids (DCMs) constitute a distinct subclass of meroterpenoids characterized by a cyclohexene core bearing two isoprenyl substituents. These metabolites are predominantly isolated from fungi, marine, and plant sources and show a series of biological activities, including cytotoxicity, antinonalcoholic steatohepatitis activity, antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. Their structural diversity, derived from varied substituents and an alkene-alkynyl-alkene moiety, together with notable pharmacological potential, has attracted considerable attention from researchers, , along with the ongoing identification of structurally novel DCMs from fungi in recent years.

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors prevent the hydrolysis of acetylcholine and are classified as reversible or irreversible. Reversible AChE inhibitors, including donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, are widely used to treat neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease. In contrast, numerous organophosphorus and carbamate insecticides function as irreversible AChE inhibitors and are widely applied in agriculture. , However, the toxicity and environmental concerns associated with some synthetic AChE inhibitors have prompted increasing interest in the discovery of alternative AChE inhibitors from natural sources. Therefore, the discovery of new, low-toxicity AChE inhibitors from natural sources is crucial for developing safer and more effective insecticidal agents.

Endophytic fungi have emerged as prolific producers of structurally diverse and biologically significant natural products. Their secondary metabolites, particularly meroterpenoids, constitute a rich source of chemically interesting scaffolds. In our previous studies, a series of DCMs isolated from an endophytic fungus displayed notable antinonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) activity, along with cytotoxic effects. These findings, combined with the distinctive structural features of DCMs, prompted a further investigation into this class of metabolites. Continued chemical exploration of endophytic fungi led us to examine Aspergillus sp. YLNU-Q11 was isolated from the fruits of the mangrove plant Avicennia marina (Forssk.) Vierh. This study resulted in the discovery of three previously undescribed DCMs and one new natural product, as well as six known compounds (Figure ). This paper reports the results of the isolation and structural identification of these metabolites along with their effects on acetylcholinesterase activity.

Results and Discussion

Aspergienyne R (1) was acquired as a yellowish oil. Its molecular formula was established as C16H22O4 based on the HRESIMS ion peak at m/z 270.1596 [M + H]+ (calcd for C16H23O4, 270.1591), corresponding to six dehydrogenation indexes. The 1H NMR of 1 combined with HSQC data showed that there were olefinic groups [δH 6.09 (d, J = 4.0 Hz), 5.28 (dd, J = 2.0, 1.0 Hz) and 5.25 (dt, J = 3.5, 1.6)], one methylene [δH 1.02 (dd, J = 6.9, 4.8 Hz) and 0.66 (dd, J = 9.4, 4.8 Hz)], three oxymethine groups [δH 4.54 (s), 4.08 (td, J = 4.0, 0.9 Hz), and 3.18 (d, J = 4.1 Hz)], one methine groups [δH 1.20 (dd, J = 9.4, 6.9 Hz)], as well as three methyl protons [δH 1.90 (t, J = 1.3 Hz), 1.32 (s) and 1.26 (s)]. The 13C NMR information (Table ) together with HSQC data suggested 16 carbon signals, four corresponding to olefinic carbons (δC 135.4, 128.4, 128.2, and 122.2), two alkyne carbons (δC 91.6, and 89.0), a methylene (δC 7.8), three oxymethine carbons (δC 77.9, 70.7 and 68.7), one methine group (δC 33.0), two nonprotonated carbon (δC 69.9 and 29.0), as well as three methyl carbons (δC 32.5, 30.4 and 23.6). The detailed 1D NMR information on 1 (Table ) was largely similar to those reported for aspergienyne M. The major differences were observed at C-1 (δC 68.7) and C-3 (δC 77.9), which showed upfield and downfield shifts of 5.4 and 4.8 ppm, respectively, indicating that 1 is a diastereomer of aspergienyne M. Analysis of 1H–1H COSY data in combination with crucial HMBC correlations enabled the establishment of the planar structure of 1 (Figure ). The proposed relative configuration was supported by coupling constant data and characteristic NOESY correlations. Specifically, the small coupling constant values of 3 J H‑3, H‑4 (4.0 Hz), along with the NOESY correlations between H-1 and H-3/H3-16, between H-4 and Ha-12, and between H-13 and H3-15 (Figure ) defined the relative configuration of 1 as 1S*,2S*, 3S*,4R*, 13R*. Finally, according to the comparison between theoretical calculation and experimental ECD spectra, the absolute configurations of 1 were assigned as 1S, 2S, 3S,4R, 13R (Figure ).

Aspergienyne S (2) was given the molecular formula of C16H22O5 from the HRMS (ESI-TOF) data at m/z 317.1360 [M + Na]+ (calcd for C16H22O5Na+, 317.1359), revealing six dehydrogenation indexes. The 1H NMR (CD3OD, 400 MHz) data of 2 (Table ) displayed one olefinic proton [δH 5.92 (t, J = 2.0 Hz)], and three methyl groups [δH 1.44 (s), 1.26 (s) and 1.22 (s)], of which one was a methylene group [δH 3.51 (s)]. The 13C NMR (CD3OD, 100 MHz), together with HSQC data, showed 16 carbons (Table ), such as two olefinic carbons (δC 139.2, and 129.0), two alkyne carbons (δC 94.2, and 82.4), two methylenes (δC 71.1 and 4.8), four methine carbons (δC 80.8, 71.2, 68.4 and 28.9), three nonprotonated carbon (δC 83.6, 69.6 and 35.7), along with three methyl carbons (δC 26.2, 25.3 and 24.6). Comparison of the 1H and 13C NMR information indicated that 2 was highly similar to biscognienyne E. The absence of the C-9/C-10 double bond in 2, replaced by two hydroxyl groups, was confirmed through key HMBC correlations from H-10 to C-8, C-9, C-11 and from H3-11 to C-8, C-9, C-10 (Figure ). Further comprehensive analysis of the correlations in the 2D NMR data supported this deduction and confirmed the 2D structure of 2.

The relative configuration of 2 was assigned as 1S*, 2R*, 3S*, 4S*, and 13S* via the large coupling constant J H‑3, H‑4 (8.2 Hz), together with the NOESY cross peaks from H-1 to H-3/H-13, from H-3 to H3-15, from H3-15 to H-13, and from H-12a to H-4/H3-16 (Figure ). To further confirm this assignment, quantum chemical NMR calculations were performed for two possible diastereomers, (1S*, 2R*, 3S*, 4S*, 9 R*, 13S*)-2a and (1S*, 2R*, 3S*, 4S*, 9S*, 13S*)-2b. The 13C NMR shifts calculated for 2b correlated closely with the test data (R 2 of 0.9986, and DP4+ of 99.92% for all data) (Figure ). The excellent agreement between the calculated and experimental ECD spectra (Figure ) confirmed the absolute configuration of 2 as 1S, 2R, 3S, 4S, 9 R, and 13S.

Aspergienyne T (3) was isolated as a yellowish oil. Its molecular formula, C16H22O5, was confirmed by HR-ESI-MS, which showed a peak at m/z 317.1363 (calcd for C16H22O5Na+, 317.1359), corresponding to six dehydrogenation indexes. The 1D NMR spectroscopic information on 3 (Table ) was highly similar to those of truncateol K. The main difference was the substitution of the chlorine atom at C-2 in truncateol K with a hydroxyl group in 3, accompanied by a variation in the position of one double bond and one hydroxyl group. The key 1H–1H COSY correlations (H-3/H-4/H-5) and HMBC correlations (H-1 → C-5, C-6, C-7; H-4 → C-6; H-5 → C-1, C-6, C-7) supported the proposed structure, while comprehensive 2D NMR and HR-ESI-MS data confirmed the planar structure of 3 (Figure ).

The relative stereochemistry of 3 (1S*, 3S*,4R*, 13S*) was determined from NOESY correlations H-1 ↔ H-12a, H-3 ↔ H-12b/H-13 and the small 3 J H‑3, H‑4 coupling (4.4 Hz) (Figure ). To further define the stereochemistry at C-2, two possible diastereomers, (1S*, 2S*, 3S*, 4R*, 13S*)-3a and (1S*, 2R*, 3S*, 4R*, 13S*)-3b, were evaluated using the same computational protocol as that applied for compound 2. Statistical analysis revealed that 3b showed better agreement with the test data (R 2 of 0.9997, and DP4+ of 100% for all data) (Figure ). The relative configuration of 3 was assigned as 1S*, 2R*, 3S*, 4R*, 13S*, along with the calculated ECD spectrum (Figure ) verified its absolute configuration as 1S, 2R, 3S, 4R, 13S.

5-Chloro-1,2,3-trimethoxybenzene (9) was isolated as a yellow oil. Its molecular formula C9H11ClO3 was confirmed through the HR-ESI-MS ion at m/z 203.0481 (calcd for C9H12ClO3, 203.0469). The 1H NMR data (CD3OD, 500 MHz) of 9 (Table ) showed two aromatic protons [δH 6.61 (s)] and three methoxy groups [δH 3.82 (s) and δH 3.74 (s)]. The 13C NMR and HSQC data suggested nine carbons, such as 6 aromatic carbons (δC 154.3, 137.8, 133.1, and 107.7), 3 methoxy carbons (δC 61.1 and 56.5). The NMR information (Table ) of 9 resembled those of 3, 4, 5-trimethoxyphenol, with the key distinction that the hydroxyl group in 3, 4, 5-trimethoxyphenol is replaced by a chlorine substituent in 9. The above speculation was confirmed by HR-ESI-MS data and the detailed HMBC correlations (Figure ). Thus, the structure of 9 was defined as depicted in Figure . Compound 9 represents a new natural product, as it is isolated from nature for the first time in this study, whereas previous reports have only described it as a synthetic compound.

At the same time, six previously reported compounds were identified as isosulochrin (4), chloroisosulochrin (5), monomethylsulochrin (6), dechlorodihydromaldoxin (7), pesteic acid (8), and 5-hydroxy-7-methoxy-4,6-dimethylphtahlide (10), by comparing the 1D NMR data with previously reported values.

In order to find a new low toxic acetylcholinesterase inhibitor insecticide from natural sources, the AChE inhibitory activities of products 1–10 were evaluated via the Ellman method, with chlorpyrifos as the positive control. This result indicated that 8 exhibited moderate inhibitory activity against AChE (IC50 = 9.54 ± 2.11 μM), whereas compounds 2 (IC50 = 24.30 ± 0.37 μM) and 5 (IC50 = 35.60 ± 1.25 μM) displayed weaker effects. In contrast, the remaining compounds showed no significant inhibition. For comparison, chlorpyrifos exhibited an IC50 value of 0.03 ± 1.06 μM. To clarify its binding interactions, compound 8 was docked with AChE. The results showed a binding energy of −5.78 kcal/mol and three hydrogen bonds with residues TYR374, GLY79, and GLY150, suggesting that these residues play a key role in its inhibitory mechanism (Figure ). The current research is limited to in vitro enzymatic activity assays; future studies require in vivo insect experiments to develop safer and more effective insecticides.

Conclusions

In summary, three new DCMs, one new natural product, and six known compounds, were separated from A. marina fruit fungal strain Aspergillus sp. YLNU-Q11. The structures of these new compounds were confirmed by detailed spectral analysis and quantum chemical calculation. Notably, compound 8 demonstrated significant acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory activity (IC50 = 9.54 μM). Based on structure–activity relationship analysis and molecular docking results, it is speculated that the phenolic hydroxyl group and flexible terminal double bond may be key structural features of compound 8 responsible for its AChE inhibitory activity, which will be further validated by synthesizing derivatives. This study provides additional chemical and bioactivity data for metabolites derived from mangrove endophytic fungi. Further studies, including structure–activity relationship analysis, toxicity evaluation, and in vivo validation, are still required to assess the potential of these compounds as lead AChE inhibitors.

Experimental Section

General

The measurement of optical rotation data was completed on a Jasco P-2000 polarimeter (manufactured by JASCO Company in Tokyo, Japan). A series of NMR data were acquired on Bruker AVANCE spectrometers operating at 400 and 600 MHz (Bruker, Bremen, Germany), and HR-ESI-MS spectra were obtained on an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF liquid chromatography–mass spectrometer, and all other instruments and experimental materials were identical to those described in our previous reports.

Fungal Material

The target fungus YLNU-Q11 was derived from A. marina fruits collected in Beihai, China in March 2024. Based on the ITS sequence homology analysis with known Aspergillus strains (similarity ≥99.3%) and morphological identification characteristics (bluish-green colonies, flask-shaped vesicles, uniseriate phialides, and rough spherical conidia), the strain was classified within the genus Aspergillus, and its rDNA sequence has been deposited in GenBank (accession no. OR999433).

Fermentation, Extraction, and Isolation

The producing strain was cultured for 28 days at 25 °C in 1000 mL conical flasks, each containing 80 g of rice and 80 mL of H2O supplemented with 0.5% sea salt. The resulting mycelia and solid medium were extracted with MeOH (3 × 10 L) for 2 days to afford a crude extract (18.5 g), which was subsequently partitioned with EtOAc (3 × 5 L). The EtOAc-soluble fraction (12.5 g) was fractionated based on silica gel column chromatography, eluting with a petroleum ether-EtOAc gradient (50:1–1:1), affording seven fractions (Frs. 1–7). Fr. 4 (3.25 g) was isolated using a Sephadex LH-20 column to afford 9 subfractions (Frs. 4.1–4.9). Next, subfraction 4.2 (0.56 g) was separated via semipreparative HPLC (45% MeOH–H2O, 7 mL/min) to yield products 4 (5.6 mg, t R 32 min), 9 (4.5 mg, t R 39 min), and 5 (3.9 mg, t R 45 min). Similarly, products 2 (5.1 mg, t R 27 min), 10 (5.8 mg, t R 34 min), 7 (7.8 mg, t R 46 min), and 3 (3.4 mg, t R 51 min) were acquired from Fr. 4.6 (0.32 g) by semipreparative HPLC (MeCN–H2O, 35:65, 7 mL/min). Fr. Six (1.52 g) was further purified based on reversed-phase C18 column chromatography (MeOH/H2O, 30% → 100%), to afford products 6 (6.3 mg), 1 (4.3 mg), and 8 (10.9 mg).

Quantum-Chemical NMR and ECD Calculations

The stereochemistry of compounds 1–3 was established using ECD spectral comparison, supported by NMR computational analysis, in accordance with reported protocols. , Detailed computational procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

AChE Inhibitory Activity Assay

The assay mixture (total volume 180 μL) consisted of PBS (0.1 mol L–1, pH 8.0), test compounds (1–100 μM), and AChE (0.1 U mL–1), and was preincubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Chlorpyriphos was used as a positive control, and a blank was included. The reaction was initiated by addition of 40 μL of a solution containing DTNB and ATCI. After continuing the incubation at 37 °C for another 20 min, a microplate spectrophotometer was used to measure the absorbance at 412 nm every 2 min, for a total of 10 min. AChE inhibitory activity was expressed as percentage inhibition according to the equation: inhibition (%) = [(E – S)/E] × 100, where E and S denote enzyme activity in the absence and presence of test compounds, respectively.

Molecular Docking

The AutoDock Vina 1.2.2 software was used for docking studies. The crystal structure of AChE (PDB ID: 1DX4) was prepared by removing crystallographic water molecules and the native ligand using PyMOL 2.3.0. Subsequently, polar hydrogen atoms were added, and AutoDock Tools was used to determine the charge values of each atom. Finally, the desired pdbqt file was obtained. The 3D structures of the small molecules were generated and energy-minimized using OpenBabel with the MMFF94 force field. Subsequently, these generated structures were converted to PDBQT format, and Gasteiger charge values were assigned to them for docking analysis. The center coordinates of this docking grid box are (x = 6.01, y = 66.82, z = 57.76). Its dimensions are 66.15 × 65.05 × 61.42 unit lengths, where each unit length corresponds to 1.0 Å. This grid box perfectly covers the area where the ligand binding site is expected to be located. The exhaustiveness parameter for the genetic algorithm search was set to 10. All other docking parameters remain at their default values. Docking calculations were carried out according to reported procedures. The analysis and visualization of ligand–protein complex results were completed using Discovery Studio 4.5 client.

Statistical Analysis

The data represent the results of at least three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate. Values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation.
