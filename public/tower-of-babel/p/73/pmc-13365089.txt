Immune microenvironment and its influence on CAR-T treatment outcomes in B-NHL
Authors: Danyang Li, Zhonghua Fu, Rui Liu, Fan Yang, Ting Zhang, Lixia Ma, Yuelu Guo, Miaomiao Cao, Biping Deng, Alex H. Chang, Xiaoyan Ke, Kai Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (B-NHL) is a highly heterogeneous tumor. Currently, CAR-T cells have been widely used in the treatment of B-cell lymphomas, with a complete response rate reaching 40%-60%. More and more studies have shown that the immune microenvironment plays an important role in the occurrence and development of B-NHL. However, it is still unclear what role the tumor microenvironment plays in the effect of CAR-T therapy on B-NHL. We analyzed the components of 44 tumor tissue biopsy samples from 36 patients with relapsed or refractory Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (R/R DLBCL) in our center to explore the immune cell components in the tumor microenvironment that correlated with improved clinical outcomes of CAR-T therapy. The median follow-up time was 8.5 months. We analyzed the impact of various cells on patient survival and identified multiple prognostic protective factors and risk factors. Among them, Treg cells (P < 0.009; HR = 0.32) are a key prognostic protective factor that associated with the efficacy of CAR-T therapy. Further analysis of tumor biopsy samples before and after CAR-T treatment in the same patients revealed that higher Treg cells were associated with the better CAR-T efficacy and were associated with sustained CAR-T persistence. There were more Treg cells in biopsy samples without CAR-T cells remaining after CAR-T treatment. The overall response rate in non-residual CAR-T cells group was 61.5% (n = 8), consisting of CR (30.8%, n = 4), PR (30.8%, n = 4), SD (7.7%, n = 1), and PD (30.8%, n = 4) compared to the residual CAR-T cells group (PR: n = 1, 25%).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05084-z.

Introduction

B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma is a group of heterogeneous malignant tumors, among which diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common type, accounting for approximately 30–40% [1]. The treatment of DLBCL requires individualized plans based on the patient’s condition, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy [2], CAR-T therapy [3], and stem cell transplantation [4], among others. For patients suitable for CAR-T therapy, 40%-60% achieve complete response, but some patients still relapse. The reasons for relapse are complex and require further exploration.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is composed of various cell components (T cells, NK cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)), extracellular matrix (ECM), soluble factors, and metabolites, which directly or indirectly regulate the proliferation, infiltration, and drug resistance of DLBCL [5]. Tregs and M2-type macrophages are often considered to promote immunosuppression and reduce anti-tumor immune responses in B-NHL [6]. In a study of gene expression data from DLBCL samples, it was found that Notch signal activation and IL-33 expression significantly increased the number of Treg cells [7], and patients with a higher proportion of Treg cells had a higher OS, suggesting that the number of Treg cells significantly affects the prognosis of DLBCL patients.

Current treatment strategies targeting the tumor microenvironment mainly include using immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 to restore the function of T/NK cells and enhance anti-tumor immune responses [8]. Or by blocking the signals of TME-related cytokines (IL-6, CXCL12, etc.) to interfere with the interaction between tumor cells and the microenvironment [9, 10].

CAR-T is a genetically edited T cell with the main characteristics of T cells. For patients who relapse after CAR-T therapy, the reasons for the failure of CAR-T treatment remain unknown. Most previous studies on the tumor microenvironment (TME) and the efficacy of CAR-T therapy relied on single-time-point biopsies (only conducted before the injection of CAR-T), which were unable to capture the dynamic changes of immune cell subsets during the treatment process. These changes might affect the treatment response or recurrence of the disease. However, continuous tumor biopsy flow cytometry has filled this gap. It can longitudinally track the immune cell populations (such as regulatory T cells, M2-type macrophages) before and after the injection of CAR-T, enabling us to directly correlate the temporal changes in the tumor microenvironment with clinical outcomes. Our research, on the cellular components of tumor masses in relapsed patients, and the study of the impact of their cell proportions on the efficacy of CAR-T treatment, provide new evidence for uncovering how the tumor microenvironment regulates the efficacy of CAR-T treatment for DLBCL.

Methods

Patients and sample collection

This study included 36 patients relapsed or refractory (R/R) DLBCL from February 2021 to January 2025 at Beijing GoBroad Hospital. All patients with DLBCL were diagnosed by experienced pathologists and the diagnosis was consistent with DLBCL diagnostic criteria. There were 20 male patients and 16 female patients, with a median age of 51 years (range, 26–73 years). The patients with central nervous system (CNS) involvement accounted for 2/36 (5.6%). The median follow-up time was 8.5 months (range:0.4–24.7 months). All patients who did not respond to first-line treatment or experienced a relapse sought further treatment at our hospital. The research protocol has been approved by the Institutional Review Board. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Beijing GoBroad Hospital in Beijing. And obtained the informed consent of all participants.

In this study, 44 samples were obtained through needle biopsy of the tumor. These samples were collected both before and after CAR-T treatment, and the tumor microenvironment was deeply analyzed using flow cytometry. Among these samples, 27 underwent tumor microenvironment testing prior to receiving CD19 CAR-T therapy. Of the 17 biopsy samples which underwent CD19 CAR-T therapy, 13 exhibited non-residual CD19 CAR-T cells in the tumor, constituting the non-residual CD19 CAR-T group (Figure 1).

Flow cytometry

The flow cytometry diagnostic laboratory of Beijing GoBroad Hospital examines the TME of B-cell lymphoma tissues using the multi-parameter spectral flow cytometer Cytek NL-CLC. The antibodies tested include: CD3/CD4/CD5/CD8/CD10/CD11b/CD14/CD16/CD19/CD20/CD22/CD25/CD30/CD33/CD38/CD45/CD45RA/CD56/CD70/CD79b/CD117/CD127/CD163/CD197; CD268/CD269/CD2/4/CD279/CD366/HLA-DR/Kappa/Lambda/CD19 antigen/7-AAD. It provides a detailed description of consecutive gating steps (live cells → CD45 + white blood cells → CD3 + T cells → CD4+/CD8 + cell subsets, etc.). The survival rate was evaluated by 7-AAD staining, and the bimodal phenomenon was excluded through FSC-A and FSC-H gating. The gating conditions for each fluorescent dye were set using the fluorescence minus one (FMO) control, and the specificity of the antibodies was confirmed using isotype controls. Gating was performed on CD3 (T cell marker) and specific antibodies against the CAR (antigen-specific PE) on CD19 + CAR+ cells to identify CAR-T cells in the tissue. This method complies with the diagnostic process specifications and has high reproducibility.

The frequencies of Tcm (central memory T cell), Teff (effector T cell), Tem (effector memory T cell), Texh (Exhaustion T cells), Tfh (T follicular helper cells), Tn (Naïve T cells), Treg (regulatory T cell), granulocytes, lymphocytes, mononuclear macrophage, M2 macrophage, MDSC (Myeloid-derived suppressor cells), CD3 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, CD4 + Tcm, CD4 + Teff, CD4 + Tem, CD4 + Tn, CD8 + T cells, CD8 + Tcm, CD8 + Teff, CD8 + Tem, CD8 + Tn and CD19-CAR-T cells were detected by flow cytometry. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1; BD Biosciences).

Efficacy evaluation

Evaluation of the therapeutic effect of the study: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD) were evaluated. We classified CR and PR as effective treatment and SD and PD as ineffective treatment.

Survival analysis

Overall survival (OS) was measured from the date of CAR-T therapy to the date of death or the last follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS) was measured from CAR-T therapy to the date of progressive disease or death due to disease. The optimal cut-off values for immune cell proportions were determined using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The value corresponding to the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) was selected as the threshold to categorize patients into high-expression and low-expression groups for survival comparison. Based on the derived cut-off, patients were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves (K-M curves) were then generated, and the differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. For variables with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were further calculated.

Statistical analysis

The demographic and other baseline data have been presented as frequencies and percentages. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the limited sample size, biopsy samples were analyzed as independent events for immune profiling. The potential clustering effect of paired samples from the same patients was not adjusted for in the survival analysis. The probabilities of OS and PFS were calculated by the K-M method and compared using log-rank test. The 95% CI for survival were calculated using the GraphPad Prism V.9.0 software. SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 were used for data analysis. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed adjustment for multiple comparisons (e.g., FDR) was not performed due to the exploratory nature of the study. Thus, p-values < 0.05 were considered nominally significant, and findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Results

Risk factors and protective factors of DLBCL in TME

The ROC curve threshold value with the highest sensitivity and specificity was used to group the B lymphoma patients with different immune cells proportion. Based on the above groupings, K-M curves was applied to analyze the overall survival clinical outcome of 44 samples in different immune cell groups of DLBCL patients. The overall survival clinical outcome with higher CD8 + T cells( P < 0.013; HR = 2.88), CD8 + Tcm (P < 0.03; HR = 3.1), CD8 + Tem (P < 0.002; HR = 4.18), CD8 + Teff (P < 0.029; HR = 2.9), M2 Macrophages (P < 0.013; HR = 3.01) infiltration proportion are worse than the patients with the lower (Fig. 2A). The specific cut-off values determined by ROC analysis were: CD8 + T cells (13.23), CD8 + Tcm (1.17), CD8 + Tem (17.33), CD8 + Teff (1.34), and M2 Macrophages (0.06). Protective factors and risk factors affecting the efficacy of CAR-T

In order to further clarify which cells affect the efficacy of CAR-T therapy, using the same analytical method, we compared the impact of cell levels in the tumor microenvironment (TME) before and after CAR-T treatment on the prognosis of patients. The results showed that in 27 samples before CAR-T therapy, CD8 + Tn, CD8 + Tem and M2 macrophages were risk factors for prognosis (Fig. 3A), while Tcm, Tcm, CD4 + Tcm and Treg were protective factors (Fig. 3B). In 17 samples after CAR-T therapy, CD3 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, CD8 + Tem, CD8 + Tcm and CD19 CAR-T were risk factors for prognosis (Fig. 3C), while CD4 + T cells, CD4 + Tem, Tem and Treg were protective factors (Fig. 3D). By comparing the changes in different cell levels in the TME before and after CAR-T treatment, Treg emerged as a protective factor for prognosis after CAR-T treatment, while M2 macrophages, which were risk factors before CAR-T treatment, disappeared after the treatment.

Higher Treg levels are correlated with CAR-T immune infiltration

To clarify whether Treg cells and M2 macrophages affect the efficacy of CAR-T therapy, we carefully compared the changes in cell content in the tumor microenvironment before and after CAR-T treatment. Among the 36 patients, six patients obtained tumor biopsy samples both before and after the CAR-T treatment. We found that the proportion of Treg cells in the tumor microenvironment of patients after CAR-T treatment was significantly lower compared to that before CAR-T treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). While the abundance of M2 macrophages did not change significantly post-treatment, their prognostic significance was lost. (Fig. 4B). To further verify whether Treg cells are involved in regulating the infiltration of CAR-T during the CAR-T treatment process and thereby affecting the efficacy of CAR-T treatment, We conducted an analysis of the immune microenvironment of the tumor masses after CAR-T therapy. We found that the proportion of Treg cells was higher in patients with non-residual CAR-T in the masses (p<0.05) (Fig. 4C). Four patients achieved CR and 4 patients achieved PR in 13 non-residual CAR-T patients, but only one patient achieved PR (25%) in residual CAR-T group (Fig. 4D). This indicates that the proportion of Treg cells is correlated with the infiltration of CAR-T cells in the tumor masses.

Discussion

Current research suggests that Treg cells play a negative regulatory role in tumor immunity and are important in the mechanism of tumor immune escape. However, there are differences in the clinical prognostic significance of the percentage or absolute number of Treg cells in peripheral blood or tumor tissues of patients with hematological malignancies. Some studies suggest that a low proportion of Treg cells in peripheral blood is a risk prognostic factor [11]. Although it is detected in peripheral blood, it is consistent with our conclusion. In this study, we explored the impact of Treg cells in the tumor microenvironment on the prognosis of DLBCL patients. Our findings suggested that higher Treg proportions in the TME correlate with increased non-residual CAR-T cell post-treatment, and non-residual CAR-T cell presence is associated with a higher CR rate. The microenvironment in the tumor mass and the entire blood system form an integrated whole with rich interactions, which also provides more support for our conclusion. The primary reason for performing a repeat biopsy after CAR-T therapy is often due to suboptimal treatment response, where significant residual tumors remain. Treg cells, as a prognostic protective factor, reasonably explain the observed decrease in Treg proportion in post-CAR-T therapy biopsies. Interestingly, our results indicated that higher Treg proportions in the TME were correlated with undetectable CAR-T cells in tumor biopsies post-treatment, and this ‘clearance’ of CAR-T cells was associated with a higher CR rate. The likely reason is that Tregs strongly promoted a rapid immune response, leading to rapid tumor clearance. Consequently, CAR-T cells also disappeared or ceased to proliferate in that local tissue.

In another study targeting classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and DLBCL, it was found that the frequency and absolute count of Treg cells in relapsed patients were significantly reduced, supporting the view that a higher peripheral blood Treg frequency is associated with better prognosis, better chemotherapy response, and lower recurrence rate in lymphoma patients [12]. In addition, some studies have reported that the concentration of Treg cells in the blood may be a good prognostic indicator for the 2-year overall survival rate of R/R DLBCL patients [13]. Su’s [14] study reported that the number of Tregs in the peripheral blood and tumor tissue of DLBCL patients significantly increased, and the number of Tregs decreased after treatment. However, some studies have shown that a high level of Tim-3 + Foxp3+ Treg cells is a prognostic indicator of poor survival in patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [15], which may be due to the different gene phenotypes of Treg cells and their significant functional heterogeneity. Additionally, some studies have reported that the proportion of Treg cells in patients with R/R B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia is significantly increased, and a high proportion of Treg cells is a potential risk factor for the response to CAR-T therapy [16]. This is different from our study in terms of the disease type, and the effects of Treg cells may differ. However, these uncertainties all reveal that Treg cells play an important role in tumor immunotherapy, and they regulate the anti-tumor immune response through multiple mechanisms. This discovery provides a theoretical basis for developing immunotherapy strategies targeting Treg cells.

As of now, the significance evaluation of Tregs is mainly concentrated in solid tumors, such as ovarian cancer [17] and breast cancer. The role of Tregs as an adverse prognostic factor has been established [18]. Treg cells are usually associated with tumor immune escape and poor prognosis. Treg-mediated immune suppression helps with tumor progression and the high infiltration of Treg cells in tumors, and is usually associated with various types of cancer (such as ovarian cancer, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and melanoma) with poor prognosis [19]. Researchers have revealed through different experimental models and clinical sample analyses that Treg cells inhibit the function of effector T cells by secreting inhibitory cytokines, cell contact-dependent mechanisms, and metabolic interference, etc.

Analyzing the mechanism of Treg action, it may be because Tregs can indirectly inhibit B cells by suppressing Th cells, which are crucial for B cell maturation. The deficiency of functional Th cells may lead to the inhibition of B cells. Secondly, Treg cells, as part of the anti-autoimmune system, can kill B cells that produce autoantibodies. Thirdly, Treg cells can control many other immune cells by inducing apoptosis. They are likely to kill malignant B cells in this way [20].

Despite the promising findings, our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size (n = 36), which may limit the generalizability of the findings and reduce the statistical power of subgroup analyses. The inclusion of paired samples from a subset of patients may introduce clustering bias, which was not adjusted for due to the small sample size. Second, consistent with real-world clinical practice, longitudinal biopsies were not available for all patients at uniform time points, introducing temporal heterogeneity that may affect the interpretation of immune dynamics. Third, our reliance on flow cytometry, while allowing for precise quantification, does not capture the functional states, spatial distribution, or transcriptional heterogeneity provided by single-cell or spatial profiling technologies. Fourth, the median follow-up of 8.5 months precludes the assessment of long-term outcomes such as durable remission and late relapse. Fifth, our findings are specific to CD19 CAR-T therapy and may not apply to other CAR constructs or targets. Finally, given the observational nature of this study, definitive causal relationships between TME changes and treatment outcomes cannot be established. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts, uniform sampling, and multi-omics approaches are needed to validate our findings.

In the future, the findings of this study may provide directions for developing new immunotherapy regimens by regulating the function of Treg cells to enhance the anti-tumor immune response. Analyzing which patients are suitable for using combination treatment [21] approach to improve the treatment effect and prognosis of lymphoma patients is of great significance, and providing a basis for the appropriate timing of CAR-T treatment for more patients with other types of lymphoma [22].

Conclusion

In the immune microenvironment, immune cells play different roles in CAR-T therapy for B-cell lymphoma. Among them, Treg cells act as a prognostic protective factor, not only predicting the overall survival of patients, but also being closely related to the complete response rate (CRR) of patients. Therefore, studying the interaction between CAR-T cells and other immune cells in the immune microenvironment has important clinical research value for improving the CRR and prognosis of B-cell lymphoma patients after CAR-T therapy.
