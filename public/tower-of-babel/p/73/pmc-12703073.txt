The Montecranon classification—a comprehensive treatment strategy for complex proximal ulna fracture dislocations
Authors: Christian Spross, Michael Strässle, Jesse Jupiter, Roger van Riet, Alberto G. Schneeberger, Andrew Choo, Denise Eygendaal, Kutalmis Albayrak, Jonas Grossmann, Vilijam Zdravkovic, Bernhard Jost
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplex proximal ulna fracture dislocations include Monteggia-like fractures and transolecranon fracture dislocations. Since 2011, we introduced a structured classification and treatment algorithm for these fractures in a Swiss teaching hospital, considering ligamentous structures, the coronoid process, and the radial head. This classification, named the Montecranon classification (MC), was validated by users with varying experience levels through a retrospective clinical analysis.MethodsAll consecutive patients treated for a complex proximal ulnar fracture dislocation between 2011 and 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. Fractures were classified according to the Bado, Jupiter, Mayo, and the new MC.For the clinical study, patients treated according to the MC were retrospectively analyzed including final range of motion, EuroQol 5-Dimensions-5-Levels Score, Quick DASH, Mayo Elbow Performance Score, analysis of complications/revisions, and radiographic assessment for posttraumatic arthritis. Patients included had at least one year of clinical and radiographic follow-up.For validation, 22 randomly selected cases were sent to 4 elbow experts, 3 young consultants, and 3 novice doctors.ResultsOf 43 patients (mean 55 years, range: 19-87) who suffered a complex proximal ulnar fracture dislocation, 30 patients met the inclusion criteria. Four patients (13%) had a transolecranon fracture dislocation, and 26 patients (87%) had a Monteggia-like fracture. The mean follow-up was 4.2 years (range: 1-10). At the final follow-up, the mean flexion-extension arc was 126° (range: 40-155), the mean Mayo Elbow Performance Score was 93 points (range: 70-100), and the mean qDASH was 19 points (range: 0-82). A total of 21 patients (70%) needed a secondary surgery after a mean of 364 days (range: 7-883). Whereof, hardware removal was performed in 12 (57%) patients. A significant correlation was found between decreased final range of motion and higher grade of MC fracture type.The interobserver correlation for MC was moderate for novice doctors and young consultants (kappa = 0.63 and 0.55) and poor for elbow experts (0.47). The intraobserver correlation was moderate to good in all groups (kappa = 0.76, 0.53, and 0.76). All participants found the MC helpful (22%) to very helpful (78%) for understanding the fracture and planning ORIF.ConclusionThe MC with special attention to involvement of ligamentous structures, the coronoid process, and the radial head, as well as an associated structured reduction technique, is helpful for the management of complex proximal ulna fracture dislocations. Despite the high rate of secondary surgeries, the final clinical outcome may lead to good to excellent results in 90% of the cases. However, young surgeons with less experience seemed to profit more from this classification than experts.

Complex proximal ulna fracture dislocations are challenging injuries that can be associated with persistent instability and poor functional outcomes.6,14,18

According to Monteggia in 1814, a Monteggia fracture is defined as a fracture of the proximal ulna with ligament failure of the proximal radioulnar joint and subsequent dislocation of the radial head.9,16 In 1967, Bado classified Monteggia fractures into 4 types depending on the direction of dislocation of the radial head.2 Jupiter further subdivided posterior Monteggia fractures (Bado type II), which are the most common in adults,16 focusing on the fracture location of the ulna and the involvement of the coronoid. They also included radial head fractures without dislocation (assuming that at least a part of the radial head was dislocated at the time of injury). This resulted in the often-reported Monteggia-like fractures (Mo(L)Fs): a fracture of the proximal ulna and the radial head without dislocation of the radial head. (5)

Another well-known problem is the confusion between Mo(L)Fs (Fig. 1) and transolecranon fracture dislocations (TOFDs) (Fig. 2), in which the radiocapitellar joint is subluxated or dislocated with an intact proximal radioulnar joint (7).Figure 1A Monteggia-like fracture with proximal ulnar fracture and dislocation of the radiocapitellar and ulnohumeral joint. Barlow and his colleagues from the Mayo Clinic defined a new classification which focused on the coronoid and merged the Mo(L)F and TOFD fractures for the first time in one classification. They presented an excellent interrater and intrarater agreement.3 In further studies, they were able to show that their transulnar basal coronoid type is the most difficult to treat with a high rate of complications and revision surgeries.12,13

Chapleau and colleagues introduced a novel classification of complex olecranon fractures highlighting the importance of identifying and fixing the fragments that contain the insertions of the collateral ligaments.5 As the aim of surgical treatment must be a stable elbow joint to enable early rehabilitation and prevent elbow stiffness, it is important to address all stability-relevant structures in such fractures.1,11,17,19

We have therefore developed a simple, new comprehensive classification which accordingly leads to a treatment strategy for complex proximal ulna fracture dislocations. We named it the Montecranon Classification (MC) as it merges Monteggia and Mo(L)Fs with TOFDs.

With this study, we retrospectively analyzed our own data after using this structured approach for more than 10 years. Furthermore, we validated the MC among international elbow experts, young consultants, and novice doctors.

Methods

All consecutive patients treated for a complex proximal ulna fracture dislocation in our level 1 trauma center in Switzerland between 2011 and 2022 were included in this retrospective study. Patients with less than 1 year of final follow-up, incomplete preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans, or surgical treatment not according to the below-mentioned approach were excluded from this study. A retrospective chart review and radiographic analysis was performed and included the classification of fractures according to Bado and Jupiter for Mo(L)F and according to the Mayo classification for TOFD. Patient's medical charts were reviewed for specific surgical steps, postoperative function, complications, and revisions. After defining the MC, all included fractures were classified accordingly by the 2 main authors (C.S. and M.S.) in a consensus reading.

For the validation study, 22 cases were randomly chosen, and their radiographic data (3D CT video and anteroposterior (ap)/lateral radiographs) were sent to 4 international elbow experts, 3 young consultants, and 3 novice doctors of our institute. None of the chosen participants were involved in the treatment of these fractures, nor were they familiar with the MC before this study. In the first step, the readers were asked to classify the fractures according to their usual classification. For the second step, a 10-minute instructional video presentation was sent to explain the new MC. After watching this video, the readers were asked to classify the same 22 cases according to the MC. Finally, in the third step, the same video and the same cases, in different orders, were sent again after four weeks to assess the intrarater correlation of the MC.

The surgical procedure was accordingly structured. Doing this, we realized that there was no need to distinguish between Mo(L)Fs and TOFDs, as the operative steps were similar. Thus, it was postulated that it would be possible to define a comprehensive classification, which merges all Mo(L)Fs and TOFDs in one. Furthermore, the process of classification should directly lead to the surgical plan described above. This is how the idea and name of the MC came up.

The Montecranon classification

The MC is applied on ap and lateral radiographs and CT scans including three dimensional (3D) reconstructions and comprises all Monteggia-, Monteggia-like, and TOFDs. It contains all proximal ulna fractures (shaft, metaphysis, or olecranon) with a radiocapitellar and/or a radioulnar dislocation and/or a radial head fracture. Furthermore, the MC constitutes a structured analysis of various but only treatment-relevant components of the proximal ulna and radius:1)involvement of the collateral ligaments (usually fracture parts on the ulnar side).2)coronoid fractures (usually Regan-Morrey ≥ type II).3)radial head fractures (usually Mason ≥ II)

Surgical treatment according to the Montecranon classification

All surgeries were performed by or under the supervision of 5 fellowship-trained shoulder/elbow surgeons.

The patient is placed supine with the arm over the body using a sterile pillow or an arm holder, using a sterile tourniquet, while making a dorsal incision and exposure of the ulnar fracture. In MC type 1 (Fig. 3) fractures only the ulna fracture is addressed usually resulting in a reduced and stable elbow. In MC type 2 fractures (Fig. 1), the joint block is not involved (no treatment-relevant coronoid fracture). Thus, the ulna can be reduced and fixed with a plate and the injured ligament can be fixed with a separate screw or osteosutures to the plate. In case of an MC type 3 or 4 fractures (Figs. 4 and 5), the joint block must be reduced first. This step may include a complex reconstruction of the coronoid with separate lag screws or pins (Fig. 6) usually approached through the ulna fracture. Once the joint block is reduced, the next step is reduction and realignment of the ulna. If this fracture is multifragmentary without anatomical references to the joint block, it can be reconstructed with separate lag screws from distal to proximal and finally to the joint block with anatomically preshaped plates. These plates help to restore the anatomical angles and axis of the proximal ulna without necessarily reducing all fragments anatomically (Fig. 7). Finally, possible ligament avulsions are addressed either with osteosutures to the plate or with separate lag screws as described above in type 2 fractures.Figure 6A Montecranon type 3. In case of an additional, treatment-relevant radial head fracture (suffix R to MC type), we dissect laterally subcutaneously through the Kocher interval and try to reduce the radial head to use it as a spacer (Fig. 5). If not possible, it is replaced with a prosthesis. In case of a stable reduction of the ulnar ligament avulsions, the radial head can also be exceptionally resected in such fractures. The radial head is treated as a last step during surgery. This brings us the advantage to assess primary stability after ulnohumeral joint reduction and stabilization. Furthermore, replacing the radial head as a last step, leaves a good radiographic view on the ulnohumeral joint and the coronoid during surgery. Functional and radiographic assessment at the final follow-up

At the final follow-up of at least 1 year, range of motion (ROM) was measured with a goniometer. Furthermore, a functional assessment was performed using the EuroQol 5-Dimensions-5-Levels Score (EQ-5D-5 L), the quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (qDASH) questionnaire, and the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS). The clinical examination was done by an independent physician which was not involved in the primary treatment. All patients were contacted for this study for a further final follow-up investigation.

On the final antero-posterior and lateral radiographs, osteoarthritis of the elbow was classified according to Broberg and Morrey.4

Statistical analysis

The statistics were performed using R Version 4.0.0 (R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The statistical significance level (alpha) was set at the P value of <0.05. Descriptive statistics included means with standard deviation, minimum, and maximum for numeric and proportions for categorical variables. Linear correlation was used to compare the clinical outcomes of the Montecranon approach. To assess the interrater and intrarater correlation, we used the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, R-package “irr,” Version 0.84.1″), classified according to the guideline of intraclass correlation coefficients for reliability research.10

Ethics

The Ethical Committee of the Kanton St. Gallen, Switzerland, approved the study (Ref. BASEC No. 2021-00612).

Results

Demographics

In the mentioned time span, a total of 43 patients with complex proximal ulna fracture dislocations were treated in our clinic. Eight patients were excluded due to incomplete follow-up and 5 further due to incomplete radiographic fracture data or surgical treatment not according to the MC. Thus, 30 patients met the inclusion criteria, whereof 19 patients were seen for final follow-up examination. Four patients (13%) had a TOFD (Mayo type 3A or B), and 26 patients (87%) had a Monteggia or Mo(L)Fs. The mean follow-up time was 4.2 years (50 months, range: 12-126 months). The mean age was 55 years (range: 19-87 years), and 40% were males. In 60%, the cause was low-energy trauma (eg, falling from a standing position). Nine patients (26%) sustained an open fracture, of which 5 were type 1, 2 each were type 2, and 3 according to the Gustilo-Anderson classification.7 All type 1 and 2 open fractures were treated with débridement, primary osteosynthesis, and wound closure. In the type 3 open fractures, one fracture was primarily débrided, reduced, and splinted, and treated with definitive osteosynthesis the following day. Another was débrided and treated with an external fixator, second look, and definitive osteosynthesis 2 weeks later (Table II).

Functional and radiographic results at the final follow-up

All patients had ROM values and radiographs at the final follow-up. The return rate for functional scoring was 60% (18 out of 30). The mean flexion-extension arc was 126° (range: 40-155, SD: 21), with a mean pronation-supination arc of 170° (range: 100-180, SD: 23). At the final follow-up, the mean MEPS was 93 points (range: 70-100 pts, SD: 12 pts) and a qDASH score of 19 points (range: 0-82 pts, SD: 26 pts). According to the MEPS result, 71% achieved excellent results, 18% good, and 12% fair. The quality-of-life index EQ-5D-5 L resulted in a mean value of 0.859 (range: 0.274-1.000, SD: 0.216). An overview is shown in Table II.

Overall, degenerative changes were found in 30 patients (100%). According to the Broberg and Morrey classification, mild degenerative changes were found in 25 patients (83%), moderate degenerative changes in 4 (13%), and severe degenerative changes in 1 case (3%). Surprisingly, the patient with severe degenerative post-traumatic elbow arthrosis had clinically good outcomes (MEPS, qDASH, EQ-5D-5 L) without hardware removal during the observational period.

Complications and revisions

There were 21 (70%) complications, 18 of which were surgically revised and 3 treated conservatively (Table II). 12 patients had soft tissue irritation with hardware removal. In 9 further cases, there was stiffness, secondary dislocation, nonunion, screw malposition, or complex regional pain syndrome.

Discussion

There are several old and new classifications for Mo(L)F and TOFDs. The MC is the first that merges all these fracture types focusing on stability and treatment-relevant structures which should be addressed during surgery. This study shows that such a structured approach was helpful for understanding and for planning the surgery of these complex fractures. Our clinical results show that even though the complication and revision rates are high, the final outcome usually results in stable and functional elbows with a high patient satisfaction in the majority of patients. Patients with higher MC fracture types showed a more restricted flexion/extension arc at the final follow-up. These findings show a clinical relevance of the MC, which can be important for the preoperative information and expectations of patients with such fractures. In the validation study, the MC was found to be very helpful for teaching younger colleagues who were not familiar with these complex fractures so far. Among high-level elbow experts, the MC showed a weaker interrater correlation which may be a downside, as maybe experts may have less need for such a new approach or have their own ideas of how to fix these fractures. However, the intrarater correlation was good to very good in all groups and the raters found the MC very helpful for planning the surgical steps. Thus, we found that the MC was still able to address the wide range of complexity of these fractures by merging them into one classification. This seems to be a helpful simplification for our clinical practice.

The classification of proximal ulna fracture dislocations has always been a challenge and the relative rarity of these fractures complicates solid scientific data acquisition and analysis. However, the wish for a simpler and more comprehensive classification for Mo(L)F and TOFD has been in many elbow surgeon's mind in the last years. The Mayo Clinic came up first with a truly simplified classification merging Mo(L)Fs and TOFDs by focusing on the coronoid. The upside of this classification is its simplicity which makes it very reproducible among surgeons.3 Furthermore, the authors proved that this new classification has a clinical relevance. In their latest study, they reviewed and reclassified the literature on this topic and found that their transulnar basal coronoid fracture dislocations were the most difficult to treat with inferior functional results in the literature.12,13 The MC also aims to reduce the complexity of fracture classification by merging Mo(L)Fs and TOFDs but compared to the Mayo Classification, the approach is different. The MC is putting the focus on all treatment-relevant structures from the beginning helping to plan the final surgery. This undoubtfully makes the MC more complicated than the Mayo Classification, which was also shown by the moderate interrater correlation among our expert elbow surgeons. This, however, can be explained by the fact that MC type I and II fractures already include most of the transolecranon fractures and the Monteggia-variant fractures of the Mayo Classification. Furthermore, the transulnar basal coronoid fractures include all the MC type III and IV fractures. This shows that the MC is more detailed and thus more complicated. On the other hand, the MC might be seen as an extension of the Mayo Classification in regard of further planning of the surgery. This was also reflected in our study by all raters finding the MC very helpful for understanding and planning of the surgery. Furthermore, the MC seems to be a good teaching tool for young surgeons without any experience in this specific field of elbow surgery so far.

Another advantage of this study is that we were able to assess the clinical relevance of the MC on our own patient collective. We showed a correlation of decrease of ROM with increase of the MC fracture type. This is an important finding which allows for better information of the patients which is in accordance with the findings of Nieboer et al.12,13

In the literature, there are few case series with a comparable sample size that studied the clinical outcomes of complex dislocation fractures of the elbow joint. Haller et al showed comparable clinical results after 2 years in 35 patients with a transolecranon fracture dislocation with a flexion-extension ROM of 107° and a pronation-supination of 137°.8 Nieboer et al was able to show comparable clinical results after an average of 3 years in 28 consecutive transulnar basal coronoid fracture dislocations with a flexion-extension ROM of 106° with a pronation-supination of 137° and a qDASH value of 11 points. There were also high osteoarthritis rates of 71%.13 Reinares et al showed comparable values of a flexion-extension ROM of 112° with a pronation-supination of 149° and a MEPS value of 87 points in 89 patients with an elbow fracture dislocation (posterolateral, posteromedial, or transulnar dislocation) after an average of 3.4 years.15

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The analyzed fractures were treated over a time span of 11 years resulting in roughly 5 fractures per year. Such small numbers of exposition for the surgeons reflects one of the problems of these fractures. However, the treatment was done by the most experienced surgeons available and only patients who were treated according to the above-mentioned concept were finally included in this study. This helped to improve the homogeneity of our collective to make it as reproducible and comparable for future studies as possible.

Furthermore, the return to clinic rate for objective scoring was low with only 60% but all included patients had a minimum of 1 year clinical and radiographic follow-up. However, the small number of cases did not allow for further analysis of subgroups of the MC fracture types. Finally, the retrospective design of the clinical study including the classification of the fractures according to the MC may have led to a selection bias.

Conclusion

The MC merges all complex proximal ulna fracture dislocations into one classification, which is a simplification for a complex injury. The final clinical outcome was successful even though the complication and revision rate were high in this collective. Patients with more complex MC types showed more restriction in the final arc of elbow motion, which implies a certain clinical relevance of this new classification. Furthermore, the MC helped surgeons of different levels of experience to better understand the fracture and to plan the osteosynthesis, focusing on the treatment-relevant structures. However, experienced surgeons seemed to profit less from this classification, which turned out to be a helpful teaching tool for colleagues with less experience in the treatment of such complex fractures.
