The Dermatoses of Pregnancy and Risk of Adverse Maternal and Fetal Outcomes

Stefano M. DiCenso

Background and Aims: The dermatoses of pregnancy (DOPs) are a distinct group of rare skin disorders that occur during or shortly after pregnancy.1 These conditions include pemphigoid gestationis (PG), polymorphic eruption of pregnancy (PEP), intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), atopic eruption of pregnancy (AEP), and pustular psoriasis of pregnancy (PPP). Given their rarity, studies investigating the effect of these disorders on maternal and fetal outcomes are few, low-powered, and uncontrolled. This study aimed to compare the risk of fetal and maternal complications in patients with DOPs to matched controls.

Methods: Within the TriNetX US collaborative network, pregnant patients aged 15 to 44 with at least one prenatal visit were divided into cohorts of each DOP and a control cohort that excluded patients with any DOP. The baseline characteristics of age, race, ethnicity, obesity, maternal comorbidities, nicotine dependence, and substance abuse history were identified between 5 years and 9 months before pregnancy. Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension (HTN), pulmonary HTN, chronic kidney disease, thyroid disease, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and the autoimmune diseases of lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis. All baseline characteristics were propensity-score-matched. The risk of complications in the 9 months following diagnosis with a DOP was compared, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results: Propensity-score-matching successfully mitigated all selected baseline differences between cohorts for each analysis.

Pemphigoid gestationis (N = 72,676): PG was associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia/eclampsia (odds ratio (OR) 1.24; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.196,1.288; P <0.0001), gestational HTN (OR 1.48, 95% CI (1.43,1.53; P <0.0001) gestational diabetes (GDM) (OR 1.58; 95% CI 1.53-1.63; P <0.0001), preterm birth (OR 11.37; 95% CI 1.3-1.45; P <0.0001), preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) (OR 1.58; 95% CI 1.52-1.64; P <0.0001), and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) (OR 1.28; 95% CI 1.23-1.33; P <0.0001).

Polymorphic eruption of pregnancy (N = 5,306): The risk of preeclampsia/eclampsia (OR 1.45; 95% CI 1.27-1.66; P <0.0001), gestational HTN (OR 1.57; 95% CI 1.39-1.78; P <0.0001), GDM (OR 1.19; 95% CI 1.044-1.361; P = 0.009), and PPROM (OR 1.39; 95% CI 1.21-1.6; P <0.0001) were increased with PEP.

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (N = 5,772): ICP was associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia/eclampsia (OR 1.46; 95% CI 1.29-1.67; P <0.0001), gestational HTN (OR 1.18; 95% CI 1.04-1.33; P = 0.0087, GDM (OR 1.29; 95% CI 1.14-1.45; P <0.0001), and preterm birth (OR 2.136; 95% CI 1.799-2.537; P <0.0001).

Atopic eruption of pregnancy (N = 576) and pustular psoriasis of pregnancy (N = 507): Neither AEP nor PP were associated with an increased risk of adverse maternal or fetal outcomes.

Conclusions: Our findings highlight significant associations between specific dermatoses of pregnancy and adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. PG, PEP, and ICP were each linked to an increased risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, and preterm complications, underscoring the need for heightened clinical monitoring in certain DOP patients. In contrast, AEP and PPP did not demonstrate increased risks of adverse outcomes. These results emphasize the importance of early identification of DOPs and may help identify individuals in higher need of specialized prenatal care.

Clinical Characterization and Inverse Probability Analysis of Sexually Transmitted Infections in Patients with Seborrheic Dermatitis

Amritpal Kooner, MA; Rawle A. Sekhon, MD; Sheehan Parvez, BS; Lindsey Lamb, MA; Peter Lio, MD

Background and Aims: Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a common, chronic inflammatory skin disorder that predominantly affects areas rich in sebaceous glands. Despite its well-established association with immunocompromised states, the relationship between SD and sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) remains underexplored. This study examines the prevalence of STDs in patients with SD using inverse probability analysis.

Methods: This cross-sectional study utilized data from the NIH All of Us Research Program, identifying SD cases via SNOMED and ICD-10-CM codes. Fisher’s exact test and unpaired t-tests were employed to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between SD patients and controls. Logistic regression models assessed the odds of STD comorbidities in SD patients, adjusting for potential confounders such as age, race, sex, income, and education.

Results: We identified 11,289 SD cases with a mean age of 63.1 years (SD 16.3), predominantly female (58%), and white (63.3%). SD patients had significantly lower income and educational attainment compared to controls (p < 0.01). The likelihood of having STDs such as Chlamydia trachomatis (OR: 2.20), Neisseria gonorrhea (OR: 2.02), herpes simplex virus (OR: 2.74), HIV (OR: 2.55), human papillomavirus (HPV) (OR: 1.98), and syphilis (OR: 2.58) was significantly higher in SD patients compared to controls (all p < 0.01). After adjusting for covariates, these associations remained significant.

Conclusions: Our findings suggest a robust association between SD and various STDs, particularly in immuno-compromised individuals. The high prevalence of SD in patients with HIV further emphasizes its potential as an early marker of immune dysregulation. These results advocate for increased dermatological and infectious disease screening in SD patients to enhance early detection and management of STD comorbidities. Further longitudinal studies are needed to establish causal relationships and better understand the underlying mechanisms of this association.

Assessing Barriers to Dermatologic Care and the Impact of Community-based Dermatology Education Among Women Experiencing Homelessness

Christine Li, Jake Nusynowitz, John Trinidad

Background and Aims: Homelessness in the United States remains a pressing issue, with over 650,000 persons experiencing homelessness (PEH) on a single night in 2023. Dermatologic conditions, including infections, inflammatory disorders, and skin cancers, are more prevalent among PEH. However, structural barriers such as financial constraints, lack of transportation, and fragmented care, along with individual factors like stigma and psychiatric comorbidities, hinder timely diagnosis and treatment. In addition, women experiencing homelessness may be further burdened by a history of sexual and/or physical abuse. Daily stress and safety concerns can prevent women experiencing homelessness from engaging in self-care practices and seeking medical attention that are critical for skin health. This study aimed to identify barriers to dermatologic care among women experiencing homelessness in Massachusetts and assess the impact of community-based dermatology education on health-seeking behaviors and skin health knowledge.

Methods: A one-hour dermatology educational session was delivered at local shelters and organizations supporting women experiencing homelessness in Massachusetts, such as Women’s Lunch Place and Abby’s House. The session covered basic skincare, sun protection, skin self-exams, and concerning findings for skin cancer. Participants completed anonymous pre- and post-session surveys in English, rating their familiarity with dermatology, likelihood of seeing a dermatologist, confidence in skincare, and comfort with self-exams on a 10-point Likert scale before and directly after the educational session. The survey also collected demographic data, barriers to dermatologic care, and optional free-text responses.

Results: Data from 26 participants across two organizations have been analyzed thus far, with anticipated data collection from at least two other organizations by the end of Spring 2025. The majority of participants identified as Black or African American (n=14, 53.8%), followed by White (n=8, 30.8%), Hispanic or Latino (n=4, 15.4%), and American Indian or Alaskan Native (n=3, 11.5%). Ages ranged from 35 to 78 years (average of 57.5 years). The most commonly reported barriers included long wait times (n=5, 19.2%), lack of insurance (n=4, 15.4%), preference for a primary care physician (n=4, 15.4%), uncertainty about when to see a dermatologist (n=3, 11.5%), preference for home remedies (n=3, 11.5%), and fear of COVID-19 (n=3, 11.5%). Participants showed improvement across all measured domains. Familiarity with dermatologic conditions increased by 2.2 points (5.3 to 7.5), likelihood of seeing a dermatologist by 2.5 points (4.8 to 7.3), confidence in skincare by 2.4 points (5.9 to 8.3), and comfort with skin self-exams by 2.8 points (5.7 to 8.5).

Conclusions: Our study highlights the significant structural and individual barriers that women experiencing homelessness face in accessing dermatologic care, including long wait times, lack of insurance, and uncertainty about when to seek a dermatologist. Despite these challenges, our preliminary data suggest that community-based dermatology education can improve skin health knowledge, increase confidence in self-care, and encourage engagement with dermatologic services. The observed increases in Likert scores across multiple domains indicate that even a brief, one-hour educational intervention can have a meaningful impact on participants’ health-seeking behaviors. As data collection continues, further analysis will help refine outreach strategies and inform policies aimed at reducing dermatologic health disparities among women experiencing homelessness.

Complications of Topical Timolol in the Management of Infantile Hemangiomas: A Systematic Review

Nicole Werpachowski

Background and Aims: Infantile hemangioma (IH) is the most common benign tumor of childhood affecting 4-5% of infants. Timolol has emerged as a widely used treatment for IH due to its efficacy and safety profile. Although systemic absorption is rare, timolol has been detected in urine and blood in some infants treated with topical timolol, raising concerns about potential adverse effects given its beta-blocker properties. This study aims to systematically review the literature on reported adverse effects associated with topical timolol for IH treatment.

Methods: A systematic review following the 2020 PRISMA guidelines was conducted. PubMed and MEDLINE databases (2000-2024) were searched, and studies reporting treatment-related adverse effects of topical timolol on IH were included.

Results: Twenty studies met inclusion criteria, comprising 1,780 patients treated with topical timolol for IH. Local adverse effects occurred in 4.7% of patients, including irritation, scaling, ulceration, pruritus, and skin texture changes. Systemic adverse effects occurred in 1.2% of cases, including bradycardia, bronchospasm, wheezing, hypothermia, and sleep disturbances. Our review did not find evidence that the severity/frequency of local adverse effects predisposed patients to systemic ones. Similarly, due to the heterogeneity of included studies, there was no pattern to suggest that longer treatment durations are associated with more systemic effects.

Conclusions: Topical timolol is generally well tolerated for IH treatment, with systemic adverse effects occurring infrequently. However, preterm infants and those with ulcerated or deep IHs may be at increased risk for systemic absorption and complications. Clinicians should monitor for potential cardiovascular and respiratory effects, particularly in these populations. Further research is warranted to better define risk factors for systemic absorption and establish optimal dosing guidelines for safe use in infants.

Variations in Melasma Risk Associated with Different Types of Synthetic Progestins

Amit Singal BA and Shari R Lipner MD, PhD

Background and Aims: Melasma is a hyperpigmention disorder that commonly affects women of reproductive age. Sex-hormones are thought to exacerbate the condition. Prior studies found that hormonal contraception exposure was a risk factor for melasma. However, the progestin component varies between oral contraceptive pill (OCP) formulations. Thus, we analyzed for associations between melasma and exposures to different synthetic progestin generations (1-4) using a large multicenter database.

Methods: The TriNetX research network was searched on 2/18/2025 for all female patients with vs. without melasma and with vs. without exposure to 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th generation progestins. Cohorts were propensity score matched for age, race/ethnicity. Chi-square and T-tests were used to compare demographics and prior progestin exposures. Risk ratio (RR) analysis was performed for melasma risk ≤5-years after progestin prescription. Those with melasma diagnosis prior to progestin exposure were excluded.

Results: Overall, 44,751 women with melasma and 44,751 matched-controls without melasma were included. After matching, demographics were similar between cases and controls (P>0.05). Those with vs. without melasma had higher prevalence of prior exposure to systemic contraceptives (21.3% vs. 8.5%, P<0.001), and systemic progestins (15.3% vs. 6.1%, P<0.001). Exposures to 1st generation (RR= 1.485, 95% CI 1.393-1.582), 2nd generation (1.619, 1.503-1.743), 3rd generation (1.549, 1.443-1.664), and 4th generation (2.331, 2.041-2.663) progestins were associated with increased risk of melasma compared to controls without prior systemic contraceptive exposure. Exposure to 4th generation progestins was associated with higher melasma risk compared to 1st (1.368, 1.222-1.532), 2nd (1.175, 1.057-1.307), and 3rd (1.264, 1.135-1.408) generation progestins. Exposure to 1st generation progestins was associated with lower melasma risk compared to 2nd (“0.842, 0.794-0.894), 3rd (0.853, 0.803-0.905), and 4th (0.747, 0.669-0.834) generation progestins.

Limitations include inability to assess dosing, route, duration of use, and compliance of hormone exposure. Given that most OCPs contain ethinyl estradiol, we did not stratify by estrogen component.

Conclusions: We found that all generations of synthetic progestins were associated with increased melasma risk compared to unexposed controls and that 4th generation and 1st generation synthetic progestins were associated with the highest and lowest melasma risks, respectively. Thus, OCPs containing 1st generation progestins may mitigate melasma risk associated with synthetic progestins exposure.

Hijab Related Hair Loss: Personal Perspectives, Concerns, and Barriers to Care Among Muslim Women in Southeast Michigan

Rana Chouaib, Marina Khreizat, Emman Dabaja, Yolanda Helfrich

Background and Aims: The hijab is a religious head covering worn by Muslim women worldwide, and existing literature suggests a potential link between hijab use and hair loss. However, limited research explores the mental health impact of hair loss on women who wear the hijab. This study aims to examine the perspectives on hair loss and experiences with dermatologic care among Middle Eastern and South Asian women who wear the hijab, with the goal of addressing their unique needs and preferences.

Methods: We designed an anonymous online survey using Qualtrics to community organizations, religious groups, and student-led organizations in Southeast Michigan. Inclusion criteria to participate included Muslim women over the age of 18 who currently wear or have worn the hijab and live in Southeast Michigan.

Results: A total of 203 participants met inclusion criteria, with 57.5% (n=96) identifying as Middle Eastern/North African and 22.2% (n=37) identifying as South Asian. Among 191 respondents who wore hijab, 54.5% (n=104) reported experiencing hair loss. Additionally, 65.2% (n=118) believed that women who wear the hijab are at risk for hair loss, and 61.8% (n=110) expressed concern about it. Daily anxiety about hijab-related hair loss was reported by 31.1% (n=55), while 54.5% (n=97) considered changing their hijab style due to hair loss concerns. Furthermore, 36% (n=64) reported an impact on self-esteem due to hair loss.

While 61% (n=108) were aware that dermatologists treat hair loss, nearly 90% (n=157) had never sought care. Reported barriers included personal time constraints (42.1%, n=72), doubts about dermatologic treatment efficacy (31.6%, n=54), lack of culturally competent care (21.1%, n=36), financial concerns (20.5%, n=35), and a lack of hijab-wearing medical providers (19.3%, n=33). Additionally, nearly 70% (n=121) were uncomfortable with a provider of the opposite sex examining their hair.

Conclusions: This study highlights the mental health impact, self-esteem concerns, and barriers to accessing dermatologic care for hair loss among women who wear the hijab. Addressing these concerns requires increased awareness and culturally competent dermatologic care tailored to the needs for women who wear the hijab. Further research is necessary to implement these strategies and ensure equitable care for Muslim women experiencing hair loss.
