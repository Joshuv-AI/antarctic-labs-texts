Methods of Immobilization after Achilles Tendon Rupture Repair: A Comparative Study in Rat Model
Authors: Tianliang Wang, Wenjie Wu, Jiaxiang Gu, Ruixing Hou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Achilles tendon (AT) is the most frequently ruptured in the human body. Literature describing different immobilization methods' impact on tendon healing after AT repair is lacking. We compare plaster cast, splint, and K‐wire to determine which is the most stable and has the fewest complications.MethodsSixty rats aged 5–6 months were selected to establish Achilles tendon injury in two hind legs model. After suturing the ends of the AT together with a modified “Kessler” method (Prolene 5–0). The skin incision was interrupted and sutured with 1–0 thread. Rats were divided into three immobilization methods (plaster cast group, splint group, and K‐wire group). In plaster cast group, the hind leg was cast with plaster in the extended position of the hip and knee joints, and the ankle joint was at 150°. Splint and K‐wire group used splints and 0.8‐mm K‐wires, separately. The fixed period was 4 weeks. The incidence of stability and complications (death, necrosis of the legs, necrosis of the skin, and incisional infection) were recorded. Differences were detected using the chi‐square test.ResultsWithin 4 weeks observation, K‐wires showed better stability (90%) compared with the other two ways (40% in plaster cast group, 65% in splint group; p < 0.05). Rats immobilized with K‐wires (10%) suffered significantly lower complications compared with plaster cast and splint group (15%; p < 0.05).ConclusionK‐wire has better stability, lower complication rate than other methods. Immobilization with K‐wire may be a promising tool in future clinical Achilles tendon rupture applications.Keywords: Achilles Tendon, Kirschner Wire, Tendon Immobilization, Tendon Rupture

Introduction

The Achilles tendon, which is the strongest and thickest tendon in the human body and influences many human movements due to its biomechanical characteristics, has the highest incidence of rupture among tendons, reaching up to 40 per 100,000 person‐years annually. 1 For decades, the orthopaedic literature has debated the best treatment options for acute Achilles ruptures. Previously, the debate focused on whether conservative treatment was superior to open surgical management, with both surgical and non‐surgical options posing their own set of risks and benefits. 2 In comparison to nonsurgical treatment, a study found that surgery restores calf muscle strength earlier, with a 10% to 18% strength difference favoring surgery at 18 months. 3 Surgery may also improve health‐related quality of life in the areas of bodily pain and physical functioning. Some researchers believed that surgical treatment is preferable in active patients who need to return to an active life as soon as possible. 4

As technology and techniques have advanced, the debate has shifted to include minimally invasive options that provide the same benefit of tendon apposition with less surgical risk. In comparison to the minimally invasive AT reconstruction, the open procedure demonstrated a lower rate of sural nerve palsy and postoperative palpable knot, whereas the minimally invasive reconstruction group demonstrated shorter surgery duration, a lower rate of postoperative wound necrosis, superficial and deep infections, and fewer scar tissue adhesions. 5

For acute midsubstance Achilles tendon ruptures, modern treatment options include conservative care (functional rehabilitation) and open, percutaneous, minimally invasive, or limited open Achilles repair. Despite rapid advances in understanding of Achilles tendon injuries, surgical strategies, and surgical techniques, the optimal treatment remains hotly debated. 6 In addition to surgical considerations, some have discovered that treatment choices made during the first weeks of immobilization and rehabilitation phase can also affect outcomes. 7 , 8

Currently, the traditional post‐surgery rehabilitation immobilization protocol involves immobilization with a plaster cast or splint. 9 Unfortunately, it was linked to an increased risk of additional issues like infection, adhesions, and altered skin sensitivity (numbness and tingling). 10 He et al. improved a new technique for recovering the length of the AT and ensuring the broken ends are in tight contact using Kirschner wires (K‐wires). 11 , 12 However, research on the effects of various immobilization methods on tendon healing after AT repair is lacking. The stability and complications of the AT repair immobilization process have not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, it's meaningful to study the efficiency and complication rate of various immobilization methods after AT repair surgery.

Furthermore, clinical results found evaluating the outcomes of AT rebuilding can be skewed due to variation in patient and injury characteristics, surgical technique, and rehabilitation protocols. Animal models can more precisely control for these variables, making them an important component of understanding in this area. The rat has been shown to be useful as an AT animal model for asking clinically relevant questions. 13 , 14

To improve efficiency, reduce complications following ATR surgery, and find better immobilization methods after ATR surgery. In the rat model, we compared various immobilization methods, including plaster casts, splints, and K‐wires, to determine which was the most stable with the fewest complications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

In animal treatment, Sodium pentobarbital and Penicillin were obtained from Sigma (P3761; P3032, China). Plaster cast, splint, and K‐wires were all purchased from Youke (shanghai).

Male Wistar rats were obtained from Vital River (China; 5–6 months, 350–400 g).

Animals

All animal experiments were carried out with the approval of Yangzhou University's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (ethical number DYYX‐2020‐003), and all procedures were followed in accordance with approved guidelines.

Rats were housed in a temperature‐controlled room (21° ± 2°C) with a 12 h dark/12 h light cycle. Water and standard laboratory chow were provided ad libitum. Rats were acclimated to the new environment for 7 days before starting the experiment.

Surgical Treatment

To begin, the rats were anesthetized with 3% sodium pentobarbital (0.1–0.2 mL/100 g; i. p.). After the rat was fixed in a prone position, skin preparation and disinfection were performed. Then incised the skin with an “S” shape. Two strands of AT were observed after separating the tendons bluntly. Cut off the thicker AT around 3 mm from the proximal tendon and 2 mm from the insertion of AT. The ends of the AT were sutured together using a modified “Kessler” method (Prolene 5–0). The skin incision was interrupted and sutured with 1–0 thread.

Following another disinfection, rats were divided into three immobilization methods: plaster cast, splint, and K‐wires. The rats in plaster cast group were fixed with casts in the straight position of the hip and knee joints, and the ankle joint was plantarflexed at 150°. Intraoperative AT anastomosis directly encapsulated with electrospun liposomes. In splint and K‐wire group, the rats were immobilized with splints and K‐wires, separately. The hind legs were fixed in the straight knee position and the ankle joint plantarflexed at 150°.

To prevent infection, Penicillin (60,000 U/d; 3 days) was injected after surgery. Dressing changes were performed every 3 days and lasted for 2 weeks.

Rat status was observed and recorded during the next 4 weeks, including stability and complications. To assess the stability of the immobilization device, we checked and recorded daily for detachment of the immobilization device. Then the shedding rate of every method is calculated to judge the stability. In addition, death, leg necrosis, skin necrosis, and incision infection were examined and recorded daily to evaluate the complications in every group.

The Postoperative Healing Evaluation

The tendon's condition was assessed. The adhesions on the tendons' palmar and dorsal surfaces were classified as follows: (i) No adhesions: There are no adhesions around the tendon, but some granulation tissue may be present; (ii) Membranous adhesions: only a few membranous adhesions have no effect on tendon gliding; (iii) Loose adhesions: These are thin, loose, soft fibers and tendons that are easily separated; (iv) Moderately dense adhesions: moderate texture with some tendon mobility.

The weights of the rats were monitored every 3 days.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). Differences were detected using the chi‐square test. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

The Stabilities of the Different Immobilization Methods

All rats were successfully sutured (Figure 1) and immobilized with plaster cast (Figure 2a), splint (Figure 2b), and K‐wires (Figure 2c) in rat models. In plaster cast group, the number of plaster loss is 12 (all occurred within 2 weeks after operation). In splint group, splint loss occurred in 10 legs of seven cases (mostly between 2 and 4 weeks after operation). In K‐wire group, there were two cases of K‐wires loss in unilateral leg (between 2 and 4 weeks after operation). K‐wires showed better stability (90%) compared with other two ways (40% in plaster cast group, 65% in splint group; p < 0.05) (unilateral exfoliation was also counted as one case).

The Complications in Different Immobilization Ways

The incisions healed well after the surgery. No skin necrosis occurred within 4 weeks in all groups. Unfortunately, one case of unilateral hind leg redness and swelling that occurred could be observed 1 week after the surgery in every group. Then the redness and swelling gradually subsided spontaneously 2 weeks after the surgery. These results indicated that there was no significant difference in incision healing and infection among the three methods.

In plaster cast group, there were two cases of rat death that occurred on the third day, without leg necrosis. In splint group, rats did not die but two cases of unilateral leg necrosis occurred (on the fifth and eighth day). In K‐wire group, there was no death that occurred, but one case of unilateral leg necrosis (on the second day after the operation). Rats immobilized with K‐wires (10%) suffered lower complications compared with plaster cast and splint group (15%; p < 0.05), shown in Table 1.

Adhesions to the Achilles tendon varied between rats. The incisions in the three groups of rats had light adhesions to the surrounding tissues 1 week after surgery. Two weeks later, the adhesions were grade III in plaster cast and splint group and grade II in K‐wire group. In week 4, the degree of adhesions was similar in plaster cast and splint group, but slightly lower in K‐wire group, with plaster cast and splint group scoring IV and K‐wire group scoring III‐IV.

Discussion

In our study, we observed that there was no significant difference in incision healing and infection among the three methods. Compared to other procedures, K‐wire is more stable and has a lower risk of complications. Immobilization with K‐wire may be a promising tool in future clinical Achilles tendon rupture applications.

Efficiency and Complications after ATR Surgery

One of the most frequent tendon injuries is acute Achilles tendon rupture, which affects 11–37 of every 100,000 middle‐aged persons annually. 4 There has been much debate over the optimal method to treat AT ruptures (ATR), with both surgical and non‐surgical treatments having different risks and advantages.

According to some studies, surgery restores calf muscle strength earlier than nonsurgical treatment. 3 This procedure may also improve health‐related quality of life in comparison to nonsurgical treatment in the areas of physical functioning and physical pain, though it is more expensive and associated with more complications than conservative treatment. However, performing surgical repair may result in complications such as wound infections and necrosis, which can lead to devastating soft tissue complications that may necessitate additional surgical reconstruction. In 1996, Assal et al. developed the Achillon® device, which offers a minimally invasive (MIS) approach in combination with a percutaneous suture, 15 based in part on the technique described by Kakiuchi in 1995. 16 This combination of a percutaneous and a mini‐open technique allows for direct visualization of the rupture site while reducing the risk of wound complications. 17 Orthopaedic surgeons now routinely use percutaneous minimally invasive procedures and can fully utilize surgical aid devices such as TenoligTM, Achillon®, PARS®, or the Dresden instrument in conjunction with ultrasound guided approaches. In general, most surgeons opt for operative management followed by cast immobilization in physically active patients 2–4 weeks before weight‐bearing. 18 Research on treatment options during the first weeks of immobilization is limited.

Comparison of Immobilization Methods

Cetti et al. first compared the postoperative mobile cast to the traditional rigid below‐knee cast, concluding that the postoperative mobile cast is a safer and more convenient option in clinical practice. 19 Recently, He et al. improved a new technique for recovering the length of the AT and ensuring the broken ends are in tight contact using K‐wires. Nevertheless, literature on the effects of various immobilization techniques on tendon repair following ATR is sparse. Our study compares plaster casts, splints, and K‐wires in three different ways, taking fixation stability, complications, and healing into account. We found that K‐wires showed a good clinical application prospect.

Regarding increasing the efficiency and decreasing the complications after ATR surgery, currently, the postoperative management of acute ATR attracts rising attention, 20 but many studies focus more on early rehabilitation. A few pieces of literature compared different immobilization methods in tendon rupture. 21 Literature describing different immobilization methods impact on ATR after surgery is lacking. In our study, we summarized the advantages and disadvantages of various fixation modalities. Plaster cast immobilization has the advantage of a good braking effect and no need for frequent replacement or reinforcement. Unfortunately, it is difficult to grasp the tightness. Too loose can easily result in plaster shedding, and too tight can easily interfere with rat feeding and digestion, resulting in rat death. The splint is simple, easy, and convenient for incision dressing change and does not affect rats eating and digestion causing fewer death cases in rats. While the tightness is also difficult to grasp: too loose and it's easy for splints to come off; too tight and it's simple for localized skin or leg necrosis. Over time, the adhesive tape will decrease, so it needs timely reinforcement and frequent operation. Advantages of K‐wires are narrow braking range, does not affect the feeding and digestion of rats, fewer death cases, and convenient incision dressing change. However, it is difficult to operate and technically demanding, and repeated operation can easily lead to necrosis of the rat legs. In addition, there is a risk of K‐wires tract infection.

Many clinical studies compare non‐surgical, open technique, and minimally invasive treatments. However, there is a scarcity of literature and research on immobilizations following treatments. The rat is the most widely used and practical animal model for studying the pathophysiology and pathogenesis of tendinopathy. 22 , 23 In our study, we summarized the experiences of the rat model in immobilization. When using plaster cast immobilization, the hind legs of rats are thin, and the diameter of the restraint needle should be 0.8 mm, which can ensure fixation stability and operation feasibility. Choose a suitable size (2 inches) of plaster or a large size of plaster for cutting and use. To avoid disturbing the foraging of the mice, the cast should not be too thick. The plaster margin should be rounded to avoid damaging the skin due to frequent rat activity. To prevent plaster shedding, make full use of the rat hip joint's “buckle” function. A layer of cotton paper can be placed inside the plaster so as not to affect the tightness. For splint fixation, a double‐layer splint is easy to cause local skin compression, and the use of a single‐layer splint is appropriate. Before immobilization, the splint should be shaped to prevent instability, and the adhesive tape should also be regularly monitored to detect shrinkage or failure of the tape in time. To prevent the skin damage caused by the continuous activity of the rat, the edge of the splint should be kept at an appropriate distance from the hip joint. In addition, Kirschner wire operators must be experienced, and the needle cannot be repeatedly punctured to avoid leg necrosis. To prevent the mouse from hooking on objects and falling off the wire, the exposed end of the wire should not be bent. During a dressing change, pay attention to the K‐wires track's disinfection.

Limitations and Strengths

Our study firstly evaluates the efficiency and complication rate of various immobilization methods after ATR surgery in animal models. We found that K‐wire may be a promising tool in future clinical Achilles tendon rupture applications. However, several limitations inherent to the study design exist and should be considered. First, we only compared three kinds of techniques; others, such as orthoses, were left out. Second, certain outcome measures, such as return to sport, stair climbing, strength, range of motion, pain, satisfaction, etc., cannot be assessed in research using rats. Third, our focus was primarily on assessing immobilizations in animal models. Nevertheless, numerous clinical trials have been carried out to compare non‐surgical, open procedures, and minimally invasive therapies. Regrettably, there is insufficient literature and research regarding immobilization post‐treatment. Consequently, we conducted this experiment using animal models. Moving forward, further clinical trials will be necessary to ascertain the effectiveness of various immobilization techniques.

Conclusion

In the rat model, we explored several immobilization techniques such as plaster casts, splints, and K‐wires after Achilles tendon rupture repair surgery. K‐wire was more stable and had less complications than previous techniques. In future clinical applications, K‐wire immobilization might be a feasible technique in the recovery of clinical Achilles tendon rupture surgery.
