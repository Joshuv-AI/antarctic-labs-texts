Chemical and non-chemical stressors in a postpartum cohort through wristband and self report data: Links between increased chemical burden, economic, and racial stress
Authors: Elise Hickman, Jenna Frey, Amanda Wylie, Hadley J Hartwell, Nicholas J Herkert, Sarah J Short, W Roger Mills-Koonce, Rebecca C Fry, Heather M Stapleton, Cathi Propper, Julia E Rager
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple external stressors are known to have adverse impacts on health and development. Certain groups are more vulnerable and/or more likely to be exposed to environmental, psychological, and social stressors simultaneously. Yet, few studies have examined combined exposure to environmental toxicants and psychosocial stress. Here, we integrated environmental chemical exposure data collected using silicone wristbands and self-report social stressor data within the Brain and Early Experience (BEE) perinatal cohort to understand co-exposure to environmental chemicals and social stress. Silicone wristbands were worn for one week by mothers throughout central North Carolina who were 6 months postpartum (n = 97). Exposure to 110 environmental chemicals across eight chemical classes was quantified on silicone wristbands using gas chromatography mass spectrometry. Social stress was evaluated using eight established self-report questionnaires (e.g., Brief Symptom Inventory, Perceived Stress Scale), quantifying experiences such as race-related stress, economic strain, and relationship conflict. Hair cortisol levels were measured as an additional metric of stress. The chemical exposure landscape and associations among chemical exposure, demographic characteristics, and social stress were characterized through individual variable analyses, cluster and data reduction, and compiled scoring approaches to comprehensively evaluate chemical and social stress burdens. We found that chemicals contain co-occurring patterns largely based on chemical class, with phthalates representing the chemical class with highest exposure and polychlorinated biphenyls the lowest. Chemicals showed differential exposure across racial groups, with diethyl phthalate, triphenyl phosphate, and tris(3,5-dimethyl phenyl) phosphate at higher levels in Black participants compared with White participants. Integrating social stressor profiling with chemical exposure data identified one particularly vulnerable subset of participants in which high chemical exposure burden coincided with high experiences of racism and economic stress. These findings demonstrate co-occurring chemical and social stress, warranting further investigation to better understand how these combined stressors may contribute to disparities in maternal and child health.Keywords: Exposure, Mixtures, Stress, Postpartum, Disparities, Silicone wristbands

Introduction

While the field of environmental health science has traditionally focused on characterizing environmental contaminants and limiting exposure to substances harmful to human health, there is increasing interest in quantifying the exposome and addressing individualized health concerns related to environmental exposures (Baccarelli et al., 2023). The exposome is characterized as an individual’s lifetime exposures to multiple types of stressors and the biological implications of these exposures (Wild, 2005; Wild, 2012). Improved exposure characterization is now needed to guide intervention efforts to alleviate specific stressors and exposure-related health outcomes. To that end, the term “precision environmental health” has been used to describe efforts combining four key areas of research to develop individual interventions to prevent disease (Baccarelli et al., 2023): 1) understanding disease mechanisms of environmental agents, 2) using ‘omics data to predict risk, 3) accounting for heterogeneity of susceptibility among individuals, and 4) developing biomarkers. One way to achieve increased precision in environmental health studies is to integrate advanced biomonitoring techniques and social determinants of health, allowing for more holistic determination of cumulative exposure and risk.

There are over 86,000 chemicals in commerce today (United States Environmental Protection Agency, 2022), yet the majority of these chemicals remain understudied in the field of environmental health research. For example, there are only about 400 chemicals with available human exposure data in the Center for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Report on Human Exposure to Environmental Chemicals, representing one of the most comprehensive exposure evaluations in the world (United States Center for Disease Control and Prevention). There is growing recognition of the need for “near field” exposure data collected in the home that captures consumer product use and individual activities (Wambaugh and Rager, 2022). The use of personal samplers—including silicone wristbands — in environmental exposure assessment has also recently grown in popularity, expanding exposure studies beyond more commonly analyzed environmental medias such as air, water, soil, and dust (Waclawik et al., 2022). Compared to human biomonitoring studies that have traditionally measured chemicals in hair, urine, or blood, studies using silicone wristbands can be more cost effective for larger cohorts, less invasive, more amenable for use in sensitive populations, and capable of capturing exposure information on parent chemicals rather than metabolites (Waclawik et al., 2022). Poly (dimethylsiloxane) is the most common silicone polymer and has numerous properties that support its utility in sampling devices, including the ability to sequestrate airborne compounds, sample moderately polar compounds despite being hydrophobic, and react minimally with environmental chemicals (Waclawik et al., 2022). As participants wear silicone wristbands throughout a study period, chemical concentrations accumulate, yielding measurements that can be used to reliably extrapolate time-weighted average (TWA) exposures (Waclawik et al., 2022; O’Connell et al., 2014).

Silicone wristbands were first used in exposure research in 2014 after the yellow Livestrong Foundation bands were popularized as a fashion trend by the cyclist Lance Armstrong a few years prior (Waclawik et al., 2022; O’Connell et al., 2014). Since then, a number of additional studies have been conducted using wristbands to assess chemical exposure (Waclawik et al., 2022). Organophosphate esters (OPEs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have been the most frequently analyzed chemical classes to date in silicone wristband studies (Waclawik et al., 2022). Notably, there have been a limited number (<20) of silicone wristband studies with pregnant persons, children under 6 years old, or mother–child pairs as specific study populations (reviewed in Waclawik et al. (Waclawik et al., 2022)). Most of these studies focused on a single specific class of chemicals (e.g., pesticides, plasticizers, flame retardants), and two were conducted in North Carolina (Hammel et al., 2020; Levasseur et al., 2021). Importantly, most research on environmental contaminants and child health outcomes has focused on exposure to toxic metals, primarily found in drinking water and food (Davis et al., 2019; Doherty et al., 2020). Assessing exposure to other chemicals found in the indoor living environment represents a critical knowledge gap that can be addressed using silicone wristbands.

Early life and pregnancy are particularly vulnerable life stages, as exposure to environmental contaminants in these windows can have implications for later-in-life health outcomes for both parent and child (Schoeters et al., 2011; Varshavsky et al., 2020; Landrigan et al., 2004). While pregnant people and children are considered as vulnerable populations in environmental risk assessments (Ferguson et al., 2017; Mastorci et al., 2021), the postpartum period is often overlooked, despite the increased risk for thyroid dysfunction, postpartum depression, altered immune response, and other hormone-related disease outcomes in the delayed postpartum (up to 6-months) period (Steiner et al., 2003; Sherer et al., 2017; Robinson and Klein, 2012). These health conditions can be further exacerbated by exposure to environmental contaminants (Varshavsky et al., 2020), and like exposure during pregnancy, exposure to contaminants during the postpartum period has been linked to adverse health effects for both the birthing parent and child (Landrigan et al., 2004; Mastorci et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2023). Endocrine disrupting chemicals are of particular concern, as this class of environmental contaminants affect typical hormone function (La Merrill et al., 2020; Jacobson et al., 2021). However, data are needed to adequately characterize exposure and assess risk are often limited in postpartum populations. Therefore, it is critical to better understand the landscape of chemicals that postpartum people may be exposed to in their everyday environments.

In addition to chemical exposures, social stress represents an important contributor to an individual’s exposome. Exposure to environmental and social stressors has been individually assessed and associated with adverse health outcomes among pregnant people and children (Chang et al., 2021; DeSocio, 2018; Cao et al., 2016; Wigle et al., 2008; Wylie and Short, 2023). While studies have explored the association between early-life exposure to one social stressor and one chemical stressor on later health outcomes (Vishnevetsky et al., 2015; Perera et al., 2018; Tamayo et al., 2017; Stroustrup et al., 2016), the combined effects of exposure to multiple stressors remains understudied, despite evidence of co-exposures having potential interactive effects (Padula et al., 2020; Wright, 2009). When characterizing exposure to multiple types of stressors, it is important to consider that historically marginalized groups such as communities that are predominately Black, Indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC) or low income are often at risk of being in closer proximity to a hazardous waste site than their White or more affluent counterparts (Morello-Frosch and Shenassa, 2006; Bullard et al., 2008). In addition to higher risk of environmental exposures, systemic racism, discrimination, a lack of resources, and persistent poverty are examples of factors that contribute to higher levels of social stress among these historically marginalized groups (Morello-Frosch and Shenassa, 2006; Eaves et al., 2023). Additionally, the postpartum period is a particular time characterized by altered functioning of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and fluctuating levels of reproductive hormones that play key roles in stress response and maintaining homeostasis (Schiller et al., 2015). Therefore, including social stress as a factor alongside chemical exposure data, particularly among postpartum parents and parents from historically marginalized communities, is needed to understand the interaction of social, environmental, and biological stress, capture cumulative exposure, and identify vulnerable subpopulations.

Here, we set out to explore the relationship between chemical exposure and social stress by leveraging silicone wristbands to measure chemical exposure and self-report questionnaires to measure social stress in postpartum participants in the Brain and Early Experience (BEE) cohort in North Carolina. Our study represents a novel approach to combining two types of stressors that have historically been analyzed separately, allowing us to characterize co-exposure to chemical and social stress in a population of postpartum persons and, by proxy, their infants’ exposure.

Materials and methods

BEE study cohort

The current study draws from the Brain and Early Experience (BEE) study, a longitudinal cohort following 203 mother-infant pairs from mid-pregnancy through childhood. Pregnant women were recruited and eligible to join the study if they were 18 years or older, an English speaker, pregnant with a singleton, living within 45 min of the study site (a large, public university in central North Carolina), and planning to remain in the same geographic area for the next three years. The pairs were eligible to enroll if the neonate was born at term (36 weeks and 4 days gestational age or older), weighed at least 5.5 lb, had no significant medical complications, and had no metal devices implanted that would impede magnetic resonance neuroimaging. Further extensive protocols for the BEE study and cohort are outlined elsewhere (Mills-Koonce et al., 2022). All procedures were approved by the institutional review board (#17–1914) at a blinded institution. Participants gave written informed consent at each study time point before participating in any data collection activity. Demographic data were collected as previously described (Mills-Koonce et al., 2022). Notably, race was self-reported, with the option for participants to choose “Other” and write in their race. Participants who identified two or more races were categorized as “Biracial/Multiracial.”.

Chemical exposure measures via silicone wristbands

Wristband preparation, distribution, and collection

Silicone wristbands were purchased from 24HourWristbands (24hourwristbands.com). Silicone wristbands were cleaned before distribution to study participants using 1:1 hexane:ethyl acetate (overnight, 100 °C) and 1:1 methanol:ethyl acetate (overnight, 100 °C) in a soxhlet condenser. Wristbands were transferred to cleaned aluminum foil (baked at 400 °C for 6 h) and then vacuum dried until dry (~24 h). Once dry, wristbands were wrapped tightly in cleaned aluminum foil before being distributed to participants. Participants were asked to wear the wristbands for 7 days and log the exact number of days the wristband was worn. Participants were provided with a piece of cleaned foil to wrap the wristband in and place back into an individual plastic sandwich bag after sampling. Wristbands were collected and stored in cleaned aluminum foil at −20 °C until all wristbands were ready for analysis

Mass spectrometry analysis of wristbands

Methods for the chemical extraction and targeted analysis of collected silicone wristbands can be found in a previous publication (Wise et al., 2020). Briefly, ~1 g segments of wristbands were cut and spiked with isotopically labelled internal standards and extracted via sonication (1:1 hexane:dichloromethane). Sample extracts were concentrated and cleaned with 8.0 g of water deactivated florisil (Acros organics Florisil, 100–200 mesh, fisher Scientific). Finally, sample extracts were concentrated and spiked with a set of isotopically labelled standards to measure recoveries. Samples were analyzed using a q exactive GC hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap GC–MS/MS system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) operated in full scan electron ionization mode. The QE-GC is equipped with a thermo TG-5SILMS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID, 0.25 μm film thicknesses) with helium as the carrier gas flowing at 1.3 mL/min and operated with a 1 μL split injection on a programmable temperature vaporizer (PTV) inlet. Field blanks (unworn clean wristbands) were extracted alongside samples for QA/QC purposes. Of the 110 analytes quantified, 58 had low level detection in field blanks and required blank subtraction (i.e., subtracting the average level measured in field blanks). In these cases, method detection limits (MDL) were set equal to three times the standard deviation of the levels in the field blanks following blank subtraction. If an analyte was not present in the field blank, MDLs were set to the lowest point detected on the calibration curve (typically around 1 ng/g). When detected in field blanks, levels were typically very low (e.g. Ranging from 0.05 – 5.0 ng) and were, on average, more than 10× lower than levels measured in samples. There were a few exceptions where field blank levels were higher, particularly the phthalates, which ranged from 0.58 – 1020 ng (TOTM and DEHP, respectively). Despite these higher field blank levels, measurements in the samples were still considerably higher and above MDLs. Wristband measurements are reported in units of nanograms of chemical per gram of wristband (ng/g; ppb)

Social stressor data collection

Social stress was evaluated through eight different questionnaires (indices). These questionnaires were used to measure components of social stress, including economic strain, racial discrimination, and interpersonal relationships. Seven of the measures included in the current study were self-report, and one (Household Grid/Income to Needs (HHI), which relates household income to economic needs, captured as a potential influence on social stress) included interviews by a trained research assistant. Table 1 describes in detail the social stressor questionnaires used in the BEE study, specifying where modifications were made to the original scales and calculation of summary variables from each index. Selected questionnaires are well established and widely used due to their strong psychometric properties, including high test–retest reliability, internal consistency, and validity (Sheikh et al., 2005; Lee, 2012; Ríos et al., 2022). We sought to provide a comprehensive overview of stressors rather than focusing on just one, and these questionnaires represent several common sources of stress that mothers may experience. Questionnaires were collected at the prenatal visit, 6-month visit (which occurred between 6 and 9 months postpartum), or both. Six-month data were used when available, and prenatal data were used for questionnaires that were not collected at the 6-month visit. Six-month visit data were available for all questionnaires except for Cohens Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and Experiences of Racism (RACD).

Hair cortisol levels as an additional measure of stress

As an additional metric of stress, levels of the stress protein cortisol were measured using hair samples of participants collected during the prenatal visit (Sauvé et al., 2007; Karlén et al., 2011). Research assistants used a small rubber hairband to combine approximately 150 strands from the posterior vertex area of the participant’s head. Hair was cut as close to the scalp as possible. The three centimeters closest to the scalp were analyzed for cortisol, as this hair reflects cortisol from approximately the previous three months (Sauvé et al., 2007; Karlén et al., 2011). Hair samples were stored in foil (with the hair root direction clearly marked and excess hair taped) at room temperature until the time of analysis. Samples were then processed in methanol extracts using a competitive radioimmunoassay at the University of Colorado Denver. Intra-assay coefficient of variation (1.17 %) and inter-assay coefficient of variation (5.12 %) reflected reliable assay results. It is important to note that this biomarker was collected from a subset of the participants (n = 78), as a portion of participants (e.g., participants with shorter hair, wigs, or locs) were not able to provide sufficient hair samples; and thus, this metric was not selected as a major focus of analysis, though it was included where possible.

Chemical data pre-processing

Outlier participants with a very high or very low number of chemicals detected were identified using a histogram (Fig. S1A). No outliers were detected using this method. To ensure that chemicals included in downstream analyses were adequately present in the environment, chemical data were filtered to include only chemicals detected in 20 % or more of participants within each cohort. This cutoff was selected by visual inspection of the distribution of chemical detection percentages (Fig. S1B) and through consideration of the racial demographics of the cohort, as ~20% of the participants were Black and ~20% were either biracial, multiracial, or self-identified other. Missing values (non-detects) were imputed using GSimp (Wei et al., 2018), a left-censored approach that leverages QRILC and elastic-net prediction. GSimp enables the user to set maximum imputation values for each variable, which we set as the minimum detection limit (MDL) for each chemical (Figure S2). Imputed data were adjusted to per-day values by dividing each value by the number of days each subject reported wearing the wristband (Waclawik et al., 2022), onwards referred to time-weighted average data (TWA). Data were log2 transformed to move distributions closer to normal and tested again for outliers using a visual approach based upon principal component analysis (PCA) coupled with a statistical filter determining whether any participant data were greater than 6 standard deviations from the mean, representing outlier detection methods previously employed when analyzing large HRMS datasets (Vitucci et al., 2023). No participants were identified as outliers. All subsequent analyses were carried out using detection-filtered, imputed, TWA, log2-transformed data

Social stressor data pre-processing

A total of 1220 answers to individual social stressor questions were missing from the dataset out of 15,908 possible observations (7.7 %). Outliers were first assessed with bayesian PCA (Stacklies et al., 2007), which is permissive of NAs, with no participants identified as outliers (Figure S3). Missing individual question level social stressor data were imputed using missForest (Stekhoven and Bühlmann, 2012), which does not rely on assumptions about the underlying data distribution, and summary indices were re-calculated following imputation. For indices where data were complete but dividing by zero resulted in a not available (NA) value, the NA was replaced with 0. Data were scaled, and outliers were identified using standard PCA to determine if any participant data was overall greater than 6 standard deviations from the mean, as the imputed values were used for downstream analysis (Vitucci et al., 2023) (Figure S3), and one participant was removed (for analyses involving social stressor data). Missing hair cortisol data were not imputed due to bias in sampling; and instead, available data were considered separately in a subset analysis.

Unsupervised pattern evaluation of chemical exposures

Hierarchical clustering (packages: vegan (Oksanen et al., 2022); cluster (Maechler et al., 2022); factoextra (Kassambra et al., 1.0.7 2020.) using Euclidean distance and correlation analyses (package: corrplot (Wei and Simko, 2021)) were used to evaluate patterns in chemical exposure data. Hierarchical clustering was carried out using two types of input data: 1) processed chemical data (as described in Chemical Data Preprocessing) and 2) processed chemical data standardized (Z-score normalized) such that the participant with the highest exposure for that chemical was assigned the highest positive value and the participant with the lowest exposure for that chemical was assigned the lowest negative value. Cluster numbers were chosen through evaluation of within sum of squares and average silhouette width plots (Figures S4 and S5) while considering interpretation of clusters in the context of the number of chemicals measured. Clusters were visualized using the pheatmap package (Kolde, 2019). Spearman correlation coefficients and associated p-values were calculated to assess the correlation between all chemical pairs and chemical pairs within each hierarchical cluster.

Chemical occurrence in products and functional use annotation

Chemical occurrence in products and functional use annotations were organized through manual query of the chemical functional use section of the CompTox chemicals Dashboard (Williams et al., 2017; United States Environmental Protection Agency; Phillips et al., 2017; Isaacs et al., 2016) which pulls information also available through the recently launched ChemExpo Knowledgebase (United States Environmental Protection Agency). A chemical was considered to be included in a harmonized functional use category if that category was present in the “Collected data on functional Use” list or if the predicted probability of associated functional use was greater than 0.5.

Evaluating differences in chemical exposures across demographic categories

Normality of chemical data was assessed using both qualitative (histograms, Q-Q plots) and quantitative (Shapiro-Wilk) approaches. Data for the majority of chemicals were non-normally distributed; therefore, non-parametric correlation analyses and statistical tests were used when possible. Spearman correlation coefficients and associated p-values were calculated to assess correlation between each individual chemical and continuous demographic variables (age, years with partner). Significant differences in chemical exposure between races were initially assessed via Wilcoxon test by comparing White participants to pooled group of Black, biracial/multiracial, and other participants. To further evaluate racial groupings while recognizing culturally sensitive best practices, including current guidance that discourages pooling of racial groups (Madowitz and Boutelle, 2014; JAMA Network, National Institutes of Health, 2024), we also assessed significant differences between four race categories (White, Black, biracial/multiracial, other) using a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. A Kruskal-Wallis test was also used to assess significant differences across educational attainment categories. Educational attainment categories represented the highest level of education completed as indicated in self-report questionnaires (High school or GED, Associate’s Degree, Four-Year Degree, Master’s Degree, Professional Degree or PhD). Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to collectively assess the association between covariates (age, race, Latina/non-Latina, educational attainment) and exposure to each chemical. For categorical tests and ANCOVA, p-values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995).

Relationship between social stress, chemical exposure, and demographics

Spearman correlation coefficients and associated p-values were calculated to assess the correlation between each individual chemical and individual social stressor questions or summary-level variables. We next leveraged k-means clustering to capture the entire landscape of social stress. Scaled individual social stressor questions were used as k-means cluster input. Prior to clustering, variables with no variability across participants were removed, resulting in a total of 137 social stressor variables (representing answers to individual questions) used as input for clustering. Cluster numbers were chosen through evaluation of within sum of squares, average silhouette width, and cluster plots (Figures S6) while considering interpretation of clusters in the context of the number of participants. Differences in individual chemical exposures between social stress clusters were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s posthoc test. P-values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Relationship between hair cortisol levels and chemical exposure

For a subset of participants (n = 78), hair cortisol data were available; due to the biased nature of hair cortisol data collection, missing data were not imputed in this analysis. Evaluating overall quality of the available, two outliers whose values were greater than 3 standard deviations from the mean were removed from the dataset. Results

Study demographics

Study demographics are reported in Table 2. The study cohort contained 97 participants, all of whom identified as women and who completed wristband chemical collection and demographic/social stressor questionnaires at approximately 6 months postpartum. Participants were on average 31 years old at the time of childbirth. A majority of participants were White (63.9%) and non-Latina/non-Hispanic (84.5%). Participants of all educational attainment levels were represented, with the highest proportion of participants having obtained four-year degrees (37.1%), followed by high school or GED (28.9%) and master’s degrees (15.5%). A majority of participants were in relationships (92.8 %), lived with their partners (86.6 %), and had been partnered for several years (mean = 7.5 years, standard deviation = 4.2 years). The majority of participants (86%) wore their wristbands for the full 7-day study period (Table S1).

Chemical detection and filtering

Of the 110 chemicals assessed in wristbands via HRMS, 95 were detected in at least one subject, and the number of chemicals detected per subject ranged from 30 to 66 (Figure S1, Table S2). Application of a 20% detection filter reduced the number of chemicals in the dataset to 62. Phthalates represented the majority of the top 10 chemicals by mean exposure, with the three chemicals with the highest mean exposure being: 1) DEHT, 2) DINP, and 3) DEHP. Descriptive statistics for each chemical (raw, imputed, and imputed TWA data) are presented in Table S3.

Chemical exposure patterns

To uncover chemical exposure patterns amongst measured chemicals in wristbands, we leveraged hierarchical clustering, an unsupervised algorithm that groups variables with similar patterns together. First, we performed hierarchical clustering on processed chemical data. This approach revealed four distinct clusters primarily driven by chemical concentration, demonstrating that the chemicals with the highest exposure were phthalates and some organophosphates, while PAHs, BDEs, and PCBs represented the chemical classes with the lowest exposures, paralleling descriptive statistics findings (Figure S4). This approach also demonstrated that the chemicals with the highest and lowest exposure were generally consistent across all participants. We next repeated hierarchical clustering using within-chemical standardized data to assess patterns of chemical co-occurrence independent of exposure level (i.e., do groups of chemicals show increased or decreased exposure together, across participants?). This approach revealed four distinct clusters that were unique from the clusters observed when hierarchical clustering was performed on non-standardized data (Fig. 2A). PAHs, BFRs, and phthalates all clustered together, while OPEs, alkylOPEs, pesticides, and PCBs were split between 2 or 3 chemical co-occurrence clusters. We next leveraged Spearman correlation |R| and p-values to determine whether chemicals within each cluster had higher degrees of correlation, demonstrating co-occurrence, in comparison to chemicals across the entire data set. These results demonstrated that within-cluster correlations had, on average, higher correlation coefficients, lower p-values, and a higher percentage of significant correlations than when the entire data set was considered together (Fig. 2B), indicating that the clustering results successfully identified co-occurring chemicals.

Product and functional use categorization to inform exposure sources

To understand potential sources of chemical exposure, we queried the EPA CompTox dashboard for chemical occurrence in products and functional use data, referred to as ChemExpo data (Williams et al., 2017; United States Environmental Protection Agency; Phillips et al., 2017; Isaacs et al., 2016). ChemExpo data were available for 48 of the 62 chemicals in the filtered dataset, with chemicals mapped to a total of 28 unique functional use categories obtained via analysis of the online CompTox database. The top functional use categories were 1) flame retardant (n = 23 chemicals), 2) plasticizer (n = 18 chemicals), and 3) antimicrobial, biocide, or fragrance (n = 10 for each) (Fig. 3A), with 45 present in at least one of the top 10 functional use categories. Most chemicals (n = 33) belonged to more than one functional use category, suggesting exposure from multiple sources, and chemicals generally clustered by chemical class (Fig. 3B). Associations between individual variables (demographics, hair cortisol) and chemical exposure

Because previous studies have demonstrated differences in exposure to environmental chemicals between people of different races or ethnicities, we next assessed demographic associations with chemical exposure (James-Todd et al., 2016). When racial groups were pooled to compare between White participants and the combined group of Black, biracial/multiracial, and other participants, there were three chemicals with significantly different (adjusted p < 0.05) concentrations and four chemicals with trends (0.05 < adjusted p < 0.1) towards different concentrations (Figure S6). White participants were exposed to higher levels of the pesticides chlorpyrifos and fipronil and lower levels of the pesticide cypermethrin; the plasticizers DEHP, DEHT, and DEP; and the flame retardant/plasticizer EDHPP (Figure S6). When race was stratified into four groups (White, Black, biracial/multiracial, other), there were two chemicals with significantly different (overall adjusted p < 0.05) concentrations among race groups – chlorpyrifos and DEP – and 12 additional chemicals with trends towards different (unadjusted p < 0.05) concentrations among race groups (Fig. 4). Post hoc testing indicated that there were significant differences between specific pairs of races for 10 of these 14 chemicals (Fig. 4). Notably, White participants were exposed to higher levels of chlorpyrifos, fipronil, and BDE 153, while Black participants were exposed to higher levels of DEHP, DEP, and TPHP (Fig. 4).

We found no significant correlations (adjusted p < 0.05) between individual chemical exposures and age or years with partner, Latina/non-Latina participants, participants with different educational attainment levels (Table S5). We also found no significant correlations (adjusted p < 0.05) between hair cortisol and individual chemical exposures or social stressor summary-level variables. Although race appeared to be the only covariate with significant differences in chemical exposure in analyses with individual covariates, we also used ANCOVA to consider the impact of all covariates together. In this analysis, race remained a significant covariate for chlorpyrifos (adjusted p = 0.009) and DEP (adjusted p = 0.0003) exposure when Black, biracial/multiracial, and other participants were pooled and a significant covariate for DEP (adjusted p = 0.005) and TDMPP (adjusted p = 0.04) when race was stratified into four groups (Table S6). Therefore, a major finding of this study is that, while some associations were identified when looking at variables at an individual-level, increased power was obtained when parsing data through aggregate scoring and integrative analyses as detailed in the next section.

Integrative analysis across social stress, chemical exposure, and demographic variables

An integrative cluster analysis was implemented to identify participants with similar patterns of social stress and to determine whether there was differential chemical exposure between social stress clusters (representing combined indices across groups of similarly distributed study participants). Three clusters of participants were derived using social stressor data as input for clustering (Figure S7D). Exposure to isodecyl diphenyl phosphate was significantly higher in Cluster 2 (n = 11 participants) in comparison with Clusters 1 and 3, with an overall BH-adjusted p-value of 0.049 (Fig. 5A). Exposure to nine additional chemicals was significantly different by overall Kruskal-Wallis raw p-value between social stress clusters (Fig. 5B), with a similar trend of increased exposure in Cluster 2 (pairwise p-values are reported in Table S7). We also observed a trend towards a higher overall chemical burden in Cluster 2 (p = 0.0899, Fig. 5C).

Further examining the characteristics of each social stress cluster, there were significant differences in overall social stress between clusters (mean of all questions, p = 1.5 × 10−9; mean of index summary-level variables, p = 4.1 × 10−5), and Cluster 3 had significantly lower overall social stress than Clusters 1 and 2. To understand whether these patterns were consistent across all indices of social stress, we next examined whether each of the index summary level variables were significantly different across clusters. Of the 26 summary level variables, 24 were significantly different between at least one pair of clusters (Fig. 5D). Participants in Cluster 1 reported overall highest social stress, while participants in Cluster 2 reported the highest economic and racial stress, and participants in Cluster 3 reported high stress related to co-parenting (Fig. 5D). Because the participants in Cluster 2 reported higher levels of racial stress, we assessed the demographic characteristics of the clusters and found that there were significant differences in race across the three groups (p = 0.0487), with Cluster 2 containing a disproportionately higher percentage of Black participants (Fig. 5E), suggesting co-occurring chemical and race- and economic-associated social stress.

Discussion

This study represents a unique integration of chemical exposure measurements captured using silicone wristbands, social stressors using self-report questionnaires, and participant demographic information across postpartum women. Primary findings included the following: (1) Chemical exposures co-occurred largely based on chemical class; (2) Likely exposure sources for measured chemicals included flame retardants and plasticizers; (3) Participants in different racial groups were exposed to significantly different levels of a subset of the chemicals measured; and (4) A subset of participants reported high racial and economic stress coinciding with an overall elevated chemical burden. Chemicals with differential exposure included diethyl phthalate (DEP), isodecyl diphenyl phosphate, and chlorpyrifos, present in products containing flame retardants, plasticizers, and biocides. Analysis approaches allowed for the identification of concurrent chemical and social stress indicators and lay the foundation for future investigations evaluating causation between these factors and downstream health outcome data that are currently being collected. To our knowledge, this is the first study leveraging both silicone wristband chemical exposure data and social stressor profiling to quantify co-occurring chemical and social stress in postpartum women.

Chemicals measured in silicone wristbands included brominated diphenyl ethers, organophosphates, alkyl organophosphates, phthalates, and pesticides. Phthalates were notably measured at the highest overall levels across study participants, followed by organophosphates. This finding parallels previous studies that have employed silicone wristbands as a metric of exposure, which have generally reported consistent detection of phthalates and organophosphates and that phthalates represent the chemical class with the highest overall exposure in populations such as firefighters and newborns (Hammel et al., 2020; Levasseur et al., 2021). Notably, these chemical classes have also been measured at high abundance in indoor environmental dust samples (Hammel et al., 2020; Rager et al., 2016). Here, we show emerging evidence for these exposures also occurring at relatively high levels in postpartum women. Phthalates are ubiquitous chemicals commonly found in personal care products, plastics, and household items (Wang et al., 2021). Phthalates are endocrine disruptors, and prenatal and early childhood exposure to phthalates has been associated with numerous endocrine-related health effects, such as pregnancy loss, preterm birth, reproductive system development, and precocious puberty, and with non-endocrine effects, such as neurodevelopmental alterations and respiratory diseases (Wang et al., 2021; Engel et al., 2021). There is also some evidence linking prenatal organophosphate exposure to neurodevelopmental outcomes (Sapbamrer and Hongsibsong, 2019). However, existing literature on phthalates and organophosphates largely focus on health effects directly pertaining to pregnancy or to the health of the child, and studies are needed to investigate whether and how exposure to these chemicals may impact the recovery of the birthing parent in the late postpartum period.

Differences in chemical exposures were also observed between racial groups. Black participants were exposed to significantly more cypermethrin, DEHP, DEP, and TPHP in comparison to White participants; conversely, White participants were exposed to significantly more chlorpyrifos, fipronil, and BDE 153. Differences in exposure to many chemical classes, including pesticides, flame retardants, parabens, bisphenols, metals, and phthalates, among participants of different races have been previously reported (James-Todd et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2020); however, some studies report similar levels of exposure across race or ethnicity groups, particularly for high molecular weight phthalates and bisphenols (James-Todd et al., 2016). A majority of these studies used human biomonitoring to detect exposure; here, we report similar findings using silicone wristbands as personal samplers. We hypothesize that personal care product use and the built environment may contribute to the observed differences in chemical exposure among racial groups (James-Todd et al., 2016; Collins et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). For example, feminine hygiene products may contribute to different exposure to DEP between Black and White participants (Branch et al., 2015). These race-based differences highlight the potential for different chemical exposure-associated health effects and resulting disease susceptibilities.

Participants in this study were grouped into data-derived clusters based upon metrics of social stress. Notably, the cluster of participants with high levels of racial and economic stress was also exposed to higher levels of specific chemicals (such as isodecyl diphenyl phosphate) and had an overall higher chemical burden than other social stress-based clusters. Although chemical and social stress have both previously been studied, this is one of the first studies to quantify concurrent chemical and social stress within a focused cohort of participants; further, this study is among the first to investigate this relationship in postpartum women. Concurrent chemical and social stress are hypothesized to interact, resulting in health outcomes that are worse than with either stressor alone. For example, studies in rodent models combining metal exposures and stressors demonstrated enhanced toxicity with concurrent exposure, including neurodevelopmental effects (Payne-Sturges et al., 2018). One proposed physiologic mechanism explaining these interactions is the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, as both environmental contaminants and social stressors have been found to activate the HPA axis (Payne-Sturges et al., 2018; Thomson, 2019). Chronic activation of the HPA axis contributes to increased “wear and tear” of biological systems, which is associated with numerous adverse health outcomes (Morello-Frosch and Shenassa, 2006; Thomson, 2019). Therefore, future studies are needed to investigate the cumulative effects of chemical exposure and social stress, particularly in postpartum women who are part of racial and ethnic minority groups.

It is important to consider the limitations of self-reported social stress. Though self-report provides important information about an individual’s perception of their experience, it may be biased by social desirability (reporting what is acceptable rather than true), issues with memory or insight, and understanding of the questions. Self-reported social stress may also be reported in relativity to that participant’s “normative” levels of stress or stress history; therefore, stress levels may be underreported in participants with generally high social stress or who have experienced high levels of stress in their past. Still, the social stress measures employed in this study were selected because they have strong psychometric properties, are commonly employed in social science research, and do not require participants to recall details or interpret complicated questions. Attempts were made to cover the most common areas of stress, but it is possible that there are other important stressors that were not included in these questionnaires. Relatedly, the range in values for many commonly used in social stress questionnaires, such as those used in our study, are relatively narrow, commonly ranging between 0 and 4 and on a Likert scale. This narrow and often discrete range can make it difficult to apply advanced computational methods and detect differences across participants. Additionally, hair cortisol data were only available for a subset of participants, limiting our statistical power and ability to integrate these data with other metrics of stress. Few social stressor variables were correlated with hair cortisol levels, paralleling previous studies (Torrecilla and Barrantes-Vidal, 2021; Wells et al., 2014; Hollenbach et al., 2019) and suggesting that hair cortisol and social stressor self-report questionnaires do not capture the same data. For chemical exposure collection, we were limited to detecting chemicals that are amenable to adsorption on silicone, and we focused our investigation on a defined set of chemicals in a targeted panel prioritized a priori and measured using GC–MS methods. We also recognize that choice of chemical detection threshold can influence downstream analysis, particularly correlations between chemicals (Bramer et al., 2024); however, our goal was to retain as much data as possible and maintain the ability to detect demographic sub-group differences for the exploratory analyses presented in this study. Future studies will integrate this type of exposure information with additional biological matrices from the same cohort (e.g., urine and blood chemical profiles) to further understand participant exposures.

We also recognize that there are other statistical methodologies that could evaluate potential cause-and-effect relationships, particularly in terms of mixtures exposures (Taylor et al., 2016); however, because we set out to identify more global patterns amongst groupings (chemical- and non-chemical-based groupings), we incorporated unsupervised machine learning and statistical distribution-based analyses as the study’s focus. We emphasize that here we have observed concurrent chemical and social stress, not causation between these two stressors. Future studies are needed to evaluate whether one type of stress causes the other, or if there are interactions, and/or they simply co-occur. Future studies are also needed to further investigate potential sources of differential chemical exposure, such as the built environment and personal care product use data, and to understand how concurrent chemical and social stress in postpartum women may impact their and their infant’s short- and long-term health.

Conclusions

Overall, this study contributes novel data related to chemical exposure patterns, demographic differences in chemical exposure, and co-occurring exposure to chemical and social stressors in a cohort of postpartum women. We found that participants that experienced the highest levels of economic and racial stress were also exposed to higher concentrations of individual chemicals and a higher overall chemical burden. These findings pave the way for future studies further investigating sources of differential exposure and health effects of these exposures, while highlighting the importance of considering both chemical and social stressors when evaluating the exposome.
