Hydrogel coatings for titanium implants: From smart stimuli-responsive strategies to multimodal bone regeneration
Authors: Aoao Wang, Zebin Gui, Li Ruan, Chaofeng Hu, Phuong Vy Tran, You Zhou, Jiao Jiao Li, Jiajia Xue, Rui Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTitanium implants are widely used in orthopedic surgery due to their excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties. However, their long-term clinical success is often limited by complications such as postoperative infection, chronic inflammation, and inadequate osseointegration. In recent years, hydrogels have been extensively explored for tissue regeneration because of their biological activity and functional versatility. One emerging application is the surface modification of titanium implants, in which hydrogel coatings can be engineered to improve implant adaptability and osseointegration under complex pathological conditions. This review summarizes the classification, fabrication methods, stimuli-responsive features, and emerging bone-regenerative applications of hydrogel coatings on titanium implants. We also discuss the prospects and challenges of functionalized hydrogel coatings for titanium implants in clinical translation. By examining the latest advances in this rapidly evolving research area, the review aims to catalyze innovation in implant-based bone repair and therapeutic applications.Keywords: Titanium implants, Hydrogel coating, Stimuli-responsive characteristics, Bone regeneration, Osseointegration

Introduction

Orthopedic implants are medical devices designed for use in orthopedic surgery to support, stabilize, or replace damaged bones or joints, and are extensively employed in nonunion repair, long bone fracture fixation, joint arthroplasty and arthrodesis [1,2]. In recent years, the demand for implantable orthopedic devices has soared due to accelerated population aging and the increasing incidence of traffic accidents, high-energy trauma, and bone tumors [3]. Recent epidemiological data indicate that approximately 2.2 million bone grafting procedures are performed annually worldwide, with the market for bone graft substitutes exceeding USD 2.5 billion [4]. This global demand reflects the urgency and complexity of therapeutic requirements in bone repair, driving ongoing research and innovation in implant design to address unmet challenges. Next-generation bone implants are expected not only to possess favorable biocompatibility and mechanical integrity as essential characteristics but also demonstrate enhanced adaptability to the peri-implant microenvironment, such as by promoting osseointegration, reducing infection risks, and improving postoperative recovery [5,6].

Titanium and its alloys have been widely used as orthopedic implants for over half a century due to their excellent mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility [7,8]. Pure titanium exhibits a unique combination of characteristics, including cytocompatibility, physiological stiffness closely matching cortical bone (with Young's modulus ca. 110 GPa), and long-term stability, positioning it as a material of choice for orthopedic applications [9]. Despite these advantages, the rigidity of pure titanium and its two-dimensional surface structure may hinder soft tissue integration [10]. Although titanium forms a native oxide layer (primarily titanium dioxide (TiO2)) through spontaneous passivation, which enhances biological acceptance [11], the unmodified titanium surface exhibits non-specific adsorption of serum proteins when exposed to physiological fluids [12]. This can compromise cell adhesion and proliferation or even trigger a local inflammatory response [13,14]. In addition, pure titanium lacks inherent antibacterial properties, making it vulnerable to bacterial invasion and colonization, which can lead to severe complications that eventually result in implant failure, such as periprosthetic inflammation, implant loosening, and detachment [[15], [16], [17]]. These limitations undermine the long-term clinical performance of titanium implants. Therefore, it is essential to develop surface modification technologies that enhance the bioactivity and functionality of titanium implants, with the aim of promoting tissue integration and improving clinical outcomes.

Hydrogels have emerged as a promising strategy for surface modification due to their unique characteristics and multifunctionality. Their three-dimensional (3D) porous network effectively mimics the extracellular matrix (ECM) of native tissues, providing an optimal microenvironment for cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [[18], [19], [20]]. When applied to titanium implants, hydrogel coatings form a “soft and moist” interfacial layer, providing excellent biocompatibility that leads to stable bioadhesion with host tissues while reducing friction and wear [21,22]. Beyond these physical properties, hydrogels can be engineered to exhibit multimodal stimuli-responsive behavior, including photothermal [23] and pH responsiveness [24], allowing them to adapt to diverse physiological environments and achieve controlled drug release. Their structures can also undergo dynamic modifications in response to ionic strength [25,26] or specific enzymatic activity [10], further extending their potential for orthopedic applications. Most importantly, hydrogel coatings offer remarkable functional flexibility, where they can be tuned to deliver application-specific functions such as antibacterial, antitumor, immunomodulatory, and pro-angiogenic properties, hence enhancing their adaptability in complex pathological environments.

This review critically examines the latest research advances in hydrogel coatings for titanium implants in bone repair (Fig. 1). In the context of a limited number of recent reviews on this emerging topic area [27,28], we present a focused discussion on the preclinical evidence reported for different types of hydrogel coatings, emphasizing their stimulus-responsive characteristics. First, we offer a comprehensive and systematic classification of both natural and synthetic hydrogel coatings alongside their fabrication methods, with dedicated discussions on interfacial mechanics and adhesion stability. Second, we present an in-depth analysis of stimulus-responsive hydrogels, categorized by endogenous stimuli including pH, ROS, enzyme, and temperature as well as exogenous stimuli such as light, ultrasound, magnetism, and electricity, followed by a discussion of multi-stimuli responsive synergistic systems designed to address complex pathological microenvironments. Third, we introduce different application scenarios of hydrogel coatings, including their antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, vascularization, and antitumor functions in osteogenesis, as well as emphasize multimodal integration strategies that combine these functions for enhanced bone regeneration. Finally, we present a critical evaluation of clinical translation challenges such as long-term biosafety and mechanical mismatch, outlining approaches for future work. This review may provide innovative guidance in the design of next-generation smart hydrogel coatings for titanium implants.

Types of hydrogel coatings

Hydrogels can be broadly classified into two categories based on their source materials: natural and synthetic hydrogels (Fig. 2) [29]. Natural hydrogels are derived from biological materials and exhibit excellent biocompatibility, with proteins and polysaccharides representing the most common sources [30]. Synthetic hydrogels are formed through physical or chemical crosslinking and can be further subdivided into ionic and non-ionic hydrogels depending on the presence of ionic functional groups within their chemical structure.

Natural hydrogel coatings

Protein-based hydrogel coatings

Collagen is a natural protein widely present in animal connective tissues, predominantly distributed in bones, skin, and cartilage [31]. As a hydrogel material, collagen offers several advantages including excellent biocompatibility, minimal cytotoxicity, and low immunogenicity [32,33]. Collagen molecules contain specific amino acid sequences, such as arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) sequence, which plays a key role in cell adhesion and ECM recognition [34,35]. Collagen hydrogels exhibit a 3D fibrous network that not only provides structural support for cell growth but also establishes a favorable microenvironment for cell proliferation and differentiation [36]. Coating titanium implant surfaces with collagen hydrogels has been shown to enhance osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [[37], [38], [39]]. In addition, collagen coatings can help to create an osteogenic microenvironment by mitigating inflammatory responses [40]. For example, Zhao et al. [41] developed multilayered collagen coating on titanium substrates using alkali surface treatment followed by polydopamine (PDA) coating and layer-by-layer self-assembly, leading to a covalently immobilized coating with greatly enhanced stability compared to simple coating by physical adsorption. This collagen coating was shown to activate multiple osteogenesis-related signaling pathways as well as convert macrophages from M1 (pro-inflammatory) to M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype, while simultaneously facilitating angiogenesis to support bone regeneration. The functionality of collagen hydrogels can be expanded to serve as a drug carrier for further enhancing the osteogenic capability of titanium implants. For example, Li et al. [42] constructed an alginate/mineralized collagen hydrogel and incorporated icariin, a flavonoid glycoside isolated from epimedium that has been shown to have osteogenic and angiogenic effects. The icariin-loaded alginate/mineralized collagen hydrogel (AMCI) was infiltrated into the porous 3D-printed titanium alloy scaffold (PTi) to fabricate the AMCI/PTi scaffold (Fig. 3). The coated scaffolds were shown to release icariin and mineralized collagen following in vivo implantation in rabbits, which synergistically promoted bone defect repair and angiogenesis.

Gelatin, as a hydrolyzed product of collagen, retains the RGD cell-adhesion peptide sequence and is therefore considered an excellent hydrogel material with inherent cell attachment sites [43]. Due to its high biocompatibility, cost-effectiveness, and low immunogenicity, gelatin is widely used in orthopedic and dental applications [44]. The gelation of gelatin is a reversible, temperature-driven physical process in which molecular chains aggregate to form gel networks upon cooling from high to low temperatures. The thermoreversible nature of gelatin requires careful handling during preparation and storage to prevent unintended dissolution or structural changes that may affect its stability and functionality in biological applications. Gelatin hydrogel coatings demonstrate good biocompatibility and have been shown to promote osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [45]. Moreover, they can be loaded with drugs or growth factors to further enhance the bioactivity of bone implants [46,47]. For example, Johan van der Stok et al. [48] loaded fibroblast growth factor-2 and bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) into a gelatin hydrogel coating on titanium implants, which significantly accelerated femoral defect repair in rats. Similarly, Chu et al. [49] incorporated Zoledronic acid into a composite hydrogel comprising methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) and polyacrylamide (PAAm), which was deposited as a bioactive coating on titanium alloy surfaces (TGPZ, Fig. 4). As Zoledronic acid inhibits osteoclast activity, this coating achieved dual regulation of bone regeneration by simultaneously enhancing bone formation and reducing resorption. When implanted in critical-sized bone defects in rat tibia, titanium implants coated with the drug-loaded composite hydrogel demonstrated superior osseointegration at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery.

Silk fibroin (SF) is a natural protein primarily derived from silkworm cocoons and can also be extracted from insects, with a molecular structure comprising symmetrically distributed crystalline and amorphous domains [50]. The crystalline regions mainly consist of β-sheet structures stabilized by strong hydrogen bonds, which confer high mechanical strength and toughness [51]. Meanwhile, the amorphous regions contain random coils and α-helix structures, contributing to the extensibility and elasticity of SF. This combination of structural features endows SF with excellent mechanical properties, enabling it to withstand external forces while maintaining shape integrity and ability to recover from deformation. SF-based hydrogel coatings have been shown to promote osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [52]. Additionally, SF hydrogels can serve as carriers for antibacterial agents [53], therapeutic drugs [54], and bioactive factors [55], thereby enhancing the multifunctionality and bioactivity of bone implants.

Polysaccharide-based hydrogel coatings

Sodium alginate (SA) is a naturally occurring polysaccharide extracted from brown algae, primarily composed of β-D-mannuronic acid and α-L-guluronic acid blocks [56]. SA readily forms hydrogels through ionic crosslinking with divalent cations such as Ca2+ [57]. The alginate backbone contains abundant hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups, enabling chemical or physical modifications to confer stimuli-responsive release behavior for bioactive molecule delivery, including through temperature-, pH-, and light-based triggers [58]. SA also exhibits some inherent antibacterial properties by affecting bacterial growth and metabolism through the alteration of bacterial membrane permeability [59]. However, its antibacterial efficacy is relatively limited and typically requires combination with other antibacterial components for enhanced performance. Accordingly, researchers have developed SA hydrogel coatings loaded with antibacterial agents [60,61] or antibacterial ions [62] to provide sustained antibacterial activity on titanium implants and reduce implant-associated infections (IAI). For example, Łucja Rumian et al. [63] used SA hydrogel for immobilizing gentamicin-loaded poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) microparticles on TiO2 implants, enabling both burst and sustained gentamicin release. Experimental findings demonstrated effective antibacterial activity of the SA hydrogel coating against Staphylococcus aureus, along with good cytocompatibility towards osteoblast-like cells (MG-63). SA hydrogel coatings can also be used as a drug carrier to further enhance the osteogenic capability of bone implants. For example, Wang et al. [64] developed a dopamine (DA)-loaded alginate-RGD (AlgR) coating on vaterite-modified titanium surfaces, whereby controlled DA release was observed to simultaneously inhibit osteoclastogenesis and enhance osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs (Fig. 5). Hence, this coating has potential to resolve the imbalance of bone remodeling and improve implant osseointegration in bone diseases such as osteoporosis.

Chitosan (CS) is a biopolymer extracted from chitin, mainly derived from the exoskeletons of crustaceans such as shrimp and crabs, representing the only naturally occurring cationic alkaline polysaccharide [65]. CS exhibits favorable histocompatibility, biodegradability, and low cytotoxicity [66,67]. It can form hydrogels through ionic gelation, physical crosslinking, and chemical modification [68]. CS hydrogels can promote osteogenesis through the incorporation of drugs such as pitavastatin [69] and quercetin [70], as well as bioactive substances such as insulin-like growth factor binding protein-3 [71] and BMP-2 [72]. In addition, CS possesses intrinsic antibacterial properties, with the ability to disrupt bacterial membranes and interact with negatively charged bacterial surfaces [73], causing intracellular content leakage and ultimately bacterial death [74]. To further improve antibacterial efficacy, CS hydrogel coatings are often loaded with antibacterial agents [75,76] or ions [77,78], hence conferring implants with sustained antibacterial effects and reducing the occurrence of IAI. For instance, Zhang et al. [79] developed a CS hydrogel loaded with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and catalase nanocapsules, which was used to surface functionalize titanium alloy plates (Fig. 6). Alongside excellent biocompatibility, the coating effectively inhibited the growth of S. aureus and E. coli as well as promoted osteoblast differentiation and osteopontin expression under chronic inflammatory conditions.

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a natural glycosaminoglycan predominantly found in various connective tissues such as skin, cartilage, and synovial fluid [80]. HA consists of repeating disaccharide units, with molecular weights typically ranging from hundreds of thousands to millions of Daltons [81]. HA hydrogels exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [82]. Titanium implants modified with HA hydrogels of different molecular weights have been shown to exert distinct effects on osteoblast behavior. For instance, Sankalp Agarwal et al. [83] evaluated osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation on titanium implants coated with high- and low-molecular-weight HA (respectively denoted h-HA and l-HA). DNA quantification indicated that h-HA immobilized on titanium surfaces significantly reduced osteoblast adhesion and proliferation over 14 days compared to l-HA. Meanwhile, h-HA was much more effective than l-HA in promoting osteoblast differentiation, evidenced by increases in ALP activity, total collagen production, and ECM mineralization.

Synthetic hydrogel coatings

Synthetic hydrogels have versatile potential for biomedical applications due to their wide range of raw material sources, simple synthesis methods, and controllable composition and structure. Based on the presence or absence of ionic functional groups in their chemical structure, synthetic hydrogels can be classified into non-ionic hydrogels (e.g., polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), PAAm, polyethylene glycol (PEG), poly-N-isopropyl acrylamide (PNIPAm), and PLGA) and ionic hydrogels (e.g., polyacrylic acid (PAA)). PVA contains numerous hydroxyl groups that confer good water solubility and hydrogen-bonding capacity [84], hence endowing PVA hydrogels with excellent biocompatibility and mechanical performance. PAAm is a long-chain polymer formed by free radical polymerization of acrylamide monomers, in which the amide groups along the polymer backbone readily form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and neighboring chains. This strong hydrogen-bonding interaction imparts PAAm hydrogels with high hydration capacity and exceptional water absorbency, allowing them to absorb several hundred times their own weight in water. However, despite these favorable physicochemical properties, their biomedical applications remain limited due to the potential toxicity associated with residual acrylamide monomers [85]. PEG is synthesized through dehydration polycondensation of ethylene glycol. The terminal hydroxyl groups enable facile chemical modifications, while the repeating ether linkages contribute to its hydrophilicity. PNIPAm is a thermoresponsive polymer made from free-radical polymerization of N-isopropylacrylamide. Its temperature sensitivity arises from reversible changes in polymer-water interactions. Below the lower critical solution temperature (LCST, at approximately 32 °C) of PNIPAm, the polymer chains are hydrated and soluble in water, while above this temperature, hydrophobic interactions dominate and result in chain collapse and precipitation [86]. PAA is a water-soluble polymer obtained by polymerization of acrylic acid monomers and is generally considered biocompatible. PAA hydrogels are pH-sensitive [87], whereby their degrees of shrinkage and swelling depend on solution pH. The unique physicochemical properties of different synthetic hydrogels offer opportunities to leverage them for a variety of applications in implant-mediated bone repair.

Natural-synthetic composite hydrogel coatings

Natural hydrogels excel in biocompatibility, ECM-mimicking properties, and low immunogenicity. These properties enable them to actively promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation when used as implant coatings. However, natural hydrogels have some inherent drawbacks such as weak mechanical strength, uncontrollable or rapid degradation, and batch-to-batch variability in material properties. In comparison, synthetic hydrogels exhibit several advantages including the capacity to allow precise structural manipulation, flexible molecular design, and highly reproducible fabrication. Hence, they enable controllable modulation of physicochemical properties such as swelling ratio, mechanical strength, and degradation rate for adapting to the complex microenvironment of bone regeneration. Nevertheless, synthetic hydrogel coatings usually require additional functional modifications, as their clinical translation is largely limited by insufficient intrinsic bioactivity, poor cell affinity, and degradation kinetics that are incompatible with the progression of bone repair. In this context, natural-synthetic composite hydrogels have been developed as high-performance coating materials to address the respective performance bottlenecks of single-material natural and synthetic hydrogels, which have been increasingly applied in the surface modification of titanium implants. The core design logic of such composite hydrogels is to synergistically integrate the biological advantages of natural hydrogels with the mechanical advantages of synthetic hydrogels, hence achieving a balance among bioactive properties and tunable mechanical characteristics, which is difficult to realize with single-material hydrogels.

Specifically, composite hydrogels can be made to retain the ECM-like structure, high cytocompatibility and cell recognition sites (such as the RGD sequence) of natural hydrogels, while at the same time possessing the adjustable mechanical strength, stable cross-linked network, controllable degradation rate, and good processing reproducibility of synthetic hydrogels. Such combinations simultaneously address the inherent problems of natural hydrogels, such as insufficient mechanical strength, rapid degradation, and poor interface stability, while overcoming the lack of bioactivity and poor cell affinity of synthetic hydrogels. In addition, the composite design can improve the adhesion strength between the hydrogel coating and titanium substrate, enabling the coating to maintain structural integrity under cyclic mechanical stress at weight-bearing sites. This feature is of great significance for the long-term stability of orthopedic implants. GelMA-PAAm composite hydrogel is a representative example of a natural-synthetic composite hydrogel applied as titanium implant coatings, which has been fully validated in preclinical studies. As a modified derivative of natural gelatin, GelMA retains favorable biocompatibility and cell adhesion sites, while synthetic PAAm provides high mechanical strength and a stable network structure through chemical crosslinking. In a representative study, Chu et al. [49] prepared a bioactive coating by loading zoledronic acid into the GelMA-PAAm composite hydrogel and applying it onto the surface of titanium alloy. Compared with single-material natural or synthetic hydrogel coatings, this composite coating led to significant enhancement in mechanical strength and interface stability. It also achieved dual modulation to simultaneously promote bone formation and inhibit bone resorption, which resulted in significantly improved osseointegration of titanium implants in rat critical-sized bone defects.

Based on the complementary properties of natural and synthetic hydrogels, researchers have established a unified material selection principle for applications in diverse bone regeneration scenarios. Specifically, natural hydrogels are preferred for conventional low-loading bone defect repair to maximize biocompatibility and osseointegration. Natural-synthetic composite hydrogels are suitable for weight-bearing bone repair sites to balance mechanical strength and bioactivity. Antibacterial natural hydrogels or functionalized synthetic hydrogels are better suited for pathological environments such as implant infection and chronic inflammation. Preparation and characterization of hydrogel coatings

Hydrogel coatings have attracted increasing research attention in recent years as an effective method to improve both the biocompatibility and functionality of titanium implants. The preparation methods for these coatings are mainly divided into three categories: sol-gel method, electrochemical techniques, and chemical vapor deposition (CVD). Each method offers a distinct set of advantages and challenges that directly affect the performance of the final hydrogel coatings.

Sol-gel method

The sol-gel method is among the most widely used techniques for preparing hydrogel coatings due to its simplicity and environmental friendliness. The process involves dissolving synthetic or natural polymers to form a sol, followed by gelation and solvent removal to generate a 3D hydrogel network. Common approaches for applying sol-gel coatings on an implant include dipping, spin coating, and spray coating. In the dipping method, implants are first cleaned and pretreated to remove surface contaminants and oxide layers, followed by immersion in a hydrogel precursor solution to form a uniform coating layer. The coating thickness and uniformity can be controlled by adjusting immersion time and solution concentration. While easy to implement, this method may produce variable coating uniformity or display inconsistent outcomes across studies [109,110]. Spin coating distributes precursor solutions by high-speed rotation, enabling control over thickness and uniformity, but is limited to coatings on planar or simple-shaped implants. Spray coating is a more flexible coating technique suitable for implants with complex geometries. A coating is formed by atomizing the hydrogel precursor solution onto the implant surface using a spraying device, whereby coating thickness can be controlled by adjusting spray distance, angle, and speed.

Electrochemical techniques

Electrochemical techniques provide an effective approach for preparing hydrogel coatings on implants, primarily through electrochemical deposition and electrophoretic deposition. Electrochemical deposition involves forming a coating on metal or alloy surfaces in aqueous or non-aqueous media. Due to its relatively simple process and high adaptability, this technology is becoming one of the most common methods for coating titanium implants [111,112]. The success of electrochemical deposition depends on precise control of current density and deposition time, which directly influence coating thickness, uniformity, and physicochemical properties. Meanwhile, electrophoretic deposition uses direct current to drive suspended particles onto electrodes of specific shapes. In this process, hydrogels need to be of the opposite charge to the substrate for effective electrostatic adhesion. Electrophoretic deposition allows rapid formation of uniform coatings with controllable thickness, making it suitable for implants with complex geometries [113,114].

CVD

CVD is an efficient technique suitable for preparing high-performance hydrogel coatings. The method introduces gaseous precursors into a reaction chamber, where chemical reactions occur on the substrate surface to form solid coatings. The key to CVD lies in controlling reaction conditions such as temperature and pressure, which directly affect coating quality and performance. CVD offers several advantages including the ability to achieve uniform coating deposition at relatively low temperatures, as well as precise control over coating thickness or composition through adjusting gas flow and reaction time. Additionally, bioactive molecules or nanomaterials that enhance implant biocompatibility and functionality can be introduced into hydrogel coatings during CVD [[115], [116], [117]]. However, high equipment costs and operational complexity remain significant barriers to large-scale applications.

Methods for the characterization of hydrogel coatings

Hydrogel coatings on titanium implants can be characterized using a variety of techniques [118]. Physicochemical characterizations reveal the morphology, structure, and composition of coatings as well as their mechanical properties or degree of adhesion on the implant. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) allows visualization of coating microstructure, including surface morphology and porosity, while transmission electron microscopy provides insights into the ultrastructure, such as nanoscale phase separation and crystal organization. Atomic force microscopy measures coating surface topography and roughness with nanoscale resolution. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy determines chemical composition and functional groups, whereas X-ray diffraction analyzes crystalline phases and crystal structures. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy can be used to determine the elemental composition of hydrogel coatings, and thermogravimetric analysis to evaluate the thermal stability of the hydrogel. Adhesion strength of the hydrogel to the titanium surface may be quantified using pull-off tests, which measure the force required to delaminate the coating. Cyclic loading tests are used to assess the fatigue resistance and coating integrity under repeated mechanical stress. Wet adhesion testing enables the measurement of bonding strength between the hydrogel coating and titanium substrate under hydrated conditions, providing a physiologically relevant evaluation of interfacial stability. Additionally, scratch testing or microindentation can be adopted for localized characterization of interfacial stability.

The biological performance of hydrogel coatings and their ability to functionalize titanium implants may be characterized through different methods depending on the key design features of the coating. For example, coatings loaded with bioactive factors can have their drug release profiles analyzed using ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy or high-performance liquid chromatography. Initial biological safety of the coating is often assessed using cytotoxicity assays, while cell-material interactions such as adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic induction can be evaluated through differentiation assays and histological or fluorescent staining for specific markers of cellular activity. The ultimate success of the implant and contribution of the hydrogel coating in enhancing bone repair is evaluated by in vivo implantation in animal models of bone defects, whereby the degree of osseointegration, inflammatory response, and bone remodeling may be measured through various techniques applied to the implanted tissues, most commonly micro-computed tomography, histological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Interfacial mechanics and stability of hydrogel coatings on titanium substrates

There is substantial mechanical mismatch between the rigid titanium substrate (Young's modulus ≈ 110 GPa) and compliant hydrogel coatings (typically ranging from 1 kPa to 100 kPa), posing an important challenge for weight-bearing orthopedic implants [119]. Under physiological loading, this modulus disparity generates concentrated interfacial shear stresses that can precipitate coating delamination, as well as induce fatigue-driven interfacial degradation under cyclic loading. Several reinforcement strategies have emerged to bridge this mechanical gap while maintaining biofunctionality of the hydrogel coating. For instance, Shao et al. [120] incorporated nano-hydroxyapatite and TiO2 nanoparticles (NPs) into PVA/PAAm composite hydrogels. The as-prepared composite coatings achieved a toughness of 2.47 MJ/m3 and a peel strength of 1.06 N/mm on titanium alloy substrates. Finite element analysis verified that this structure could reduce the maximum stress at the bone-implant interface by up to 33.3%. Double-network hydrogel architectures have also shown efficacy in improving tribological performance. Li et al. [121] grafted a Schiff base-crosslinked double-network hydrogel onto Ti6Al4V surfaces, achieving a 67.74% reduction in friction coefficient under 0.5 N load compared with uncoated controls, with stable low-friction behavior maintained at elevated load conditions. Beyond these mechanical enhancements, strategies to narrow the titanium-hydrogel modulus gap also include the use of porous titanium structures as an intermediate substrate, which reduce the effective modulus of the metal phase and simultaneously provide mechanical interlocking sites for hydrogel adhesion [122].

The in vivo durability of hydrogel-coated titanium implants critically depends on the long-term stability of interfacial adhesion. While physical adsorption through hydrogen bonding or van der Waals forces offers simplicity, this type of coating typically fails under sustained physiological loading due to insufficient bond strength and susceptibility to hydrolytic degradation. In contrast, covalent grafting strategies establish permanent chemical links between the coating and titanium surface, hence substantially enhancing adhesion strength and durability. For instance, Yuk et al. [123] achieved interfacial toughness exceeding 1000 J/m2 by covalently anchoring tough hydrogel networks onto titanium and other non-porous surfaces via silanation, a value that surpasses the ≈800 J/m2 benchmark of natural tendon-bone interfaces. This level of toughness is primarily attributed to the intrinsic adhesion from chemical anchoring combined with significant energy dissipation within the bulk hydrogel during detachment.

Contemporary hybrid approaches for stabilizing hydrogel coatings increasingly combine topographical and chemical anchorage. For example, anodized titanium surfaces with nanostructured TiO2 arrays (nanopores, nanotubes, or punch-through nanotubes) coated with PAAm hydrogel achieved a shear strength of 59.28 kPa due to the synergy of nano-mechanical interlocking [124]. Recently, an AA-CS-nHAP hydrogel deposited on TiO2 nanotube arrays demonstrated an adhesion force of 14.68 N, with wet-state adhesion strength reaching 182.93 kPa [125]. This coating also achieved 99.99% antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus and promoted BMSCs proliferation, illustrating that mechanically robust interfaces can co-exist with biological functionality. Plasma surface pretreatment represents another promising avenue for enhancing hydrogel coating adhesion. Oxygen plasma treatment of titanium was found to significantly increase surface hydrophilicity and reduce hydrocarbon contamination, facilitating more uniform hydrogel precursor wetting and enabling denser covalent coupling through silane-based adhesion promoters [126]. Collectively, these advanced surface engineering techniques, including plasma treatment, anodization-induced nano-mechanical interlocking, and covalent chemical grafting, provide a comprehensive toolkit for designing durable hydrogel-titanium interfaces with improved resistance to surgical handling and long-term physiological loading.

Hydrogel coatings with stimuli-responsive properties

Recent research has led to significant advances in smart stimuli-responsive hydrogels, which have found emerging applications as coatings on bone implants, imparting specific functions to the implant surface and enabling active responses to biological changes. These smart hydrogel coatings can alter their physical structures or catalyze chemical reactions in response to external stimuli or internal changes in the peri-implant microenvironment, thereby generating biological effects on cells and tissues [127,128]. Exogenous stimuli may include light irradiation [129], electric fields [130], magnetic fields [131], and ultrasound [132], whereas endogenous stimuli can involve temperature changes [133], elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [134], and pH decrease or generation of specific enzymes under pathological conditions such as infection, inflammation, and tumors [135]. By enabling dynamic responses to these stimuli, hydrogel coatings can confer a range of biological functions to otherwise bioinert implants [136,137]. Importantly, stimuli-responsive hydrogels are designed to rapidly detect and react to microenvironmental changes without damaging healthy cells and tissues, thereby significantly improving the therapeutic efficacy of coated implants while minimizing side effects or complications [138].

Endogenous stimuli-responsive strategies

Enzyme-responsive hydrogel coatings

Orthopedic implants are susceptible to bacterial invasion during or after surgery, which can lead to severe infection that compromises implant stability and function [139,140]. During invasion, bacteria secrete hydrolases such as hyaluronidase, lipase, and protease that disrupt host cell integrity [[141], [142], [143]]. These bacterial enzymes have high activity and specificity, creating an opportunity to design macromolecular substrates such as HA and gelatin as part of a stimuli-responsive system degradable by such enzymes. The chemical basis of this response lies in the enzyme-catalyzed hydrolysis of specific glycosidic bonds in HA or peptide bonds in gelatin, leading to network breakdown. The incorporation of these biocompatible and bioactive substrates into hydrogel coatings on implant surfaces provides a favorable microenvironment that supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [45,144]. Importantly, these macromolecular substrates can undergo rapid enzymatic degradation when bacterial invasion occurs, triggering structural changes in the hydrogel coating [[145], [146], [147]]. This enzyme-responsive property enables rapid and on-demand release of loaded antimicrobial agents from the hydrogel in the early stages of peri-implant infection, hence effectively combating bacterial colonization and preserving implant stability. For instance, Leng et al. [10] fabricated nanoflower-like zinc oxide (ZnO) structures on a titanium substrate, which were subsequently coated with a composite hydrogel comprising GelMA and hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA) (Fig. 7). The hydrogel remained stable under physiological conditions, effectively reducing ZnO cytotoxicity and supporting fibroblast behavior. Upon bacterial infection, hyaluronidase-triggered hydrogel degradation exposed the underlying ZnO nanostructures to exert antibacterial effects.

ROS-responsive hydrogel coatings

ROS are chemically reactive molecules generated during incomplete oxygen reduction, which include superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical, and singlet oxygen [134]. Under physiological conditions, ROS participate in various biological processes such as cell signaling, immune responses, cell proliferation and differentiation [148,149]. However, pathological conditions such as inflammation and tissue injury can lead to excessive ROS accumulation, causing oxidative stress and damage to surrounding tissues [[150], [151], [152]]. Hydrogel coatings designed to respond to changes in ROS concentration hence play a key role in enhancing implant biocompatibility, promoting osseointegration, and improving long-term stability. Such coatings respond through ROS-sensitive moieties within the hydrogel polymer network, whereby increased ROS concentration induces bond cleavage and structural rearrangement of these moieties. The hydrogel thus rapidly gains ROS scavenging and controlled drug release capabilities in response to microenvironmental changes in ROS levels, effectively alleviating inflammation and facilitating osseointegration [153,154].

As an example, Pu et al. [155] developed a ROS-responsive hydrogel coating for drug delivery by encapsulating vancomycin and BMP-2 into a hydrogel crosslinked by PVA and N1-(4-boronobenzyl)-N3-(4-boronophenyl)-N1, N3-tetramethylpropane-1,3-diaminium (TSPBA), aiming to enhance both the antibacterial and osteogenic performance of titanium implants. The TSPBA component in the hydrogel responds to ROS to induce hydrogel degradation and hence drug release. This hydrogel coating was shown to effectively inhibit the growth of S. aureus and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) while promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. In another study using similar hydrogel materials, Ma et al. [26] prepared a PVA-TSPBA hydrogel coating loaded with adenosine on a modified titanium substrate (Fig. 8). This coating responds to ROS in the early post-implantation stage and locally releases adenosine in an on-demand manner to regulate macrophage polarization. In the later stages of healing and following hydrogel degradation, strontium ions are released from the titanium surface and promote the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells. Interestingly, this hydrogel coating was also shown to exhibit glucose-responsive behavior, expanding its application to improving the treatment of bone-related complications in diabetic patients.

Thermo-responsive hydrogel coatings

Thermo-responsive hydrogels, also termed thermo-sensitive hydrogels, are extensively used to modify and enhance the functionality of bone implants. They undergo sol-gel transition in response to temperature changes, leading to effective modulation of hydrogel biocompatibility and drug release [156,157]. Thermo-responsive hydrogels alter their physical state with ambient temperature fluctuations according to the LCST. This transition is driven by temperature-dependent disruption or restoration of hydrogen bonds between polymer chains and water molecules, as well as enhanced hydrophobic interactions above the LCST. Specifically, below the LCST, hydrophilic groups within the hydrogel form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, hence maintaining aqueous solubility, whereas above the LCST, weakened hydrogen bonding together with increased hydrophobic interactions induce phase transition from sol to gel state [[158], [159], [160]]. This transition not only strengthens coating-implant interfacial adhesion but also optimizes the release profile of therapeutic agents. By rapidly forming a stable 3D network structure upon thermal stimulation, thermo-sensitive hydrogels provide a facile method to fabricate functional coatings on bone implants.

For instance, Chen et al. [161] developed a thermo-responsive hydrogel (I-OG) composed of oxidized starch, gelatin, and irisin. The I-OG hydrogel demonstrated excellent injectability and biocompatibility alongside precise thermosensitive responses. With its sol-gel transition point approximating body temperature, I-OG effectively buffered implant micromotion in mice with aseptic loosening while maintaining structural integrity during bone compression. Furthermore, the sustained release of irisin promoted peri-implant osteogenesis while simultaneously suppressing osteoclast activation and inflammatory responses. By filling gaps at the bone-implant interface, the hydrogel coating has potential to significantly enhance osseointegration and prevent aseptic loosening. In another study, Li et al. [162] loaded the osteogenic drug simvastatin into TiO2 nanotubes and subsequently constructed a thermosensitive CS-glycerin-hydroxypropyl methylcellulose hydrogel (CGHH) on the nanotube arrays (Fig. 9). At 37 °C, CGHH remained in a solution state that enabled controlled simvastatin release to sustain osteogenesis under physiological conditions. In contrast, under infection-induced hyperthermia, CGHH transitioned to a gel state that triggered rapid release of the pro-inflammatory molecule glycerol, hence promoting macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to provide immunomodulation. This temperature-mediated drug delivery behavior hence functioned as a thermosensitive “valve” mechanism that could concurrently enhance osteogenic and immunomodulatory response.

Thermo-responsive hydrogel coatings have also been employed to mitigate the discomfort and mechanical incompatibility issues arising from direct implant-soft tissue contact. As an example, Fu et al. [163] developed a thermo-responsive hydrogel coating using PDA as an adhesive matrix that incorporated three functional monomers: 2-(2-methoxyethoxy) ethyl methacrylate, oligo (ethylene oxide) methacrylate, and 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine. The coating was synthesized on modified titanium alloy Ti-6Al-4V through atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP). Due to its temperature-dependent swelling-deswelling behavior, this hydrogel coating absorbed water and expanded at 25 °C while undergoing dehydration-induced contraction at 37 °C. Within the body, this behavior could help reduce mechanical mismatch at the contact interface between titanium implant and soft tissues.

pH-responsive hydrogel coatings

Healthy bone tissue maintains a tightly regulated physiological pH range of 7.2-7.4, which represents the optimal microenvironment for normal bone metabolism and functional homeostasis. However, during bacterial infection, there is significant upregulation of pathogenic factors including phosphatases, phospholipases, proteases, and toxins. These changes are accompanied by a decrease in local pH to mildly acidic levels and localized temperature elevation, collectively establishing a pathogen-specific infectious microenvironment [164,165]. Such microenvironmental dysregulation is a major contributor to implant failure in orthopedics. pH-responsive hydrogels have emerged as intelligent biomaterials capable of dynamic structural adaptation in response to variations in environmental pH [166]. This property endows them with great application potential as bone implant coatings, due to remarkable advantages in enhancing biocompatibility, preventing and treating IAI, and promoting bone regeneration. The structural changes in pH-responsive hydrogel coatings under different pH environments can be leveraged to control the release rate of loaded drugs. Specifically, pH variations trigger protonation or deprotonation of ionizable moieties within the hydrogel, which modulates intermolecular electrostatic interactions and leads to network swelling or deswelling. The resulting changes in pore structure and mass transport pathways regulate the diffusion of encapsulated drugs, enabling controllable drug release kinetics across different pH microenvironments. This stimulus-responsive release mechanism enables preferential drug accumulation at the infection site, hence significantly improving localized therapeutic efficacy [167,168]. To adjust the pH-responsive range and functional performance of the coating, polymer precursors containing acidic (e.g., carboxyl or sulfonic acid groups) or basic (e.g., amino or imidazole groups) functional groups can be introduced into the hydrogel. Additionally, the hydrogel's response to pH changes can be finely tuned by adjusting fabrication parameters such as the degree and density of polymer crosslinking [169]. Collectively, the way in which pH-responsive hydrogel coatings modulate their spatial structure with environmental pH can be tuned to optimize drug pharmacokinetics and enhance bioavailability, hence helping bone implants achieve superior efficacy and safety in preventing infections while promoting osseointegration.

Li et al. [170] designed a pH-responsive hydrogel coating to prevent bacterial biofilm formation on titanium implants, which could be applied to improve bone healing in osteomyelitis. The hydrogel was composed of CS, gelatin, and aldehyde HA, deposited onto a titanium substrate using the dip-coating technique. To enhance coating mechanical strength and interfacial adhesion, nano-TiO2 particles were incorporated into the hydrogel. In addition, zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF)-8 encapsulating Ca2+ and vancomycin was introduced during coating fabrication, whereby these components are released due to ZIF-8 degradation under acidic conditions. Ca2+ release inhibits biofilm formation by interacting with bacterial Bap proteins, while vancomycin effectively kills free bacteria that have not formed biofilms. This pH-responsive mechanism actively enables the release of antibacterial components in an acidic environment characteristic of bacterial infection, thereby enhancing the antibacterial performance of titanium implants and reducing IAI risk. In another study, Choi et al. [171] developed a gentamicin-loaded, pH-responsive hydrogel-like coating named dfMAP@GENT, which was composed of mussel adhesive proteins (MAP), catecholic amino acid 3,4-dihydroxy-phenylalanine (DOPA), and Fe3+ (Fig. 10). This coating exhibited excellent self-healing adhesion and scratch resistance on titanium implants, effectively preventing commonly seen issues associated with mechanical damage, such as peeling and cracking, in conventional coating systems. Importantly, dfMAP@GENT could sequester gentamicin within the coating under normal conditions while rapidly releasing the antibiotic upon environmental acidification caused by bacterial infiltration. Specifically, the coating retained approximately 45% of loaded gentamicin under physiological conditions (pH 7.4) for over three weeks, enabling prolonged antibacterial efficacy. In response to local pH decrease induced by bacterial infection, the disintegration of DOPA-Fe3+ complexes triggered on-demand gentamicin release. At pH 5.0, an accelerated release rate was observed, reaching 80% of loaded antibiotic within the first 8 h. This hydrogel coating showed potential for preventing periprosthetic infection through its instant, on-demand antibacterial activity triggered by infection-associated pH changes, within in vivo models representing both immediate infection (during surgical implantation) and delayed infection (at 4 weeks after implantation).

Exogenous stimuli-responsive strategies

Light-responsive hydrogel coatings

Light-responsive hydrogel coatings leverage the photocatalytic activity and photothermal conversion characteristics of photosensitive materials. They can be used to initiate photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) under light irradiation [172], thereby enhancing the ability of implants to combat infection. PDT mainly utilizes gold, silver, or transition metal oxides as photosensitizers. Under light irradiation, these materials are excited from the ground state to the triplet state. The resulting energy transfer to molecular oxygen produces diverse ROS types, including hydroxyl radicals, superoxide radicals, and singlet oxygen. These ROS oxidize membrane lipids and proteins as well as intracellular DNA of bacteria, hence inducing membrane permeabilization, metabolic dysfunction, and eventual bacterial death [23,[173], [174], [175]]. Therefore, the application of light-responsive hydrogel coatings on titanium implant surfaces allows on-demand ROS production through PDT, which then oxidizes bacterial cell membranes and disrupts their metabolic pathways. These antibacterial effects reduce local inflammatory responses and lower the risk of peri-implant infection. For example, He et al. [176] developed a catechol-modified GelMA hydrogel containing photosensitizer Chlorin e6 (Ce6)-loaded mesoporous polydopamine NPs (GelMAc/MPDA@Ce6). This hydrogel exhibited strong adhesion to titanium substrates as a surface coating material and could also function as a wound dressing. Leveraging laser-triggered ROS generation by Ce6, GelMAc/MPDA@Ce6 demonstrated significant and rapid antibacterial activity when stimulated at 1 W/cm2 laser power. Notably, when the power was reduced to 100 mW/cm2, the hydrogel promoted fibroblast activation and accelerated wound healing. However, while light-responsive hydrogel coatings on implants can effectively eradicate bacteria when combined with PDT, the ROS generated by PDT may adversely affect normal bone tissue depending on their concentration [150,177,178]. Therefore, precise regulation of ROS production through rational coating design is essential to balance antibacterial efficacy with cytocompatibility.

Meanwhile, PTT is a technique that utilizes photothermal agents (PTAs) to convert light energy into heat, thereby eliminating abnormal cells or tissues [179]. Common PTAs include metal NPs (e.g., gold and copper NPs) [180,181], carbon-based materials (e.g., graphene oxide, carbon nitride) [182,183] and metal sulfides (e.g., copper sulfide, molybdenum disulfide) [184,185]. Light-responsive hydrogel coatings on titanium implant surfaces can elevate local temperature through PTT, disrupting bacterial structures to cause death or increase susceptibility to antimicrobial agents. For example, Ding et al. [23] fabricated tannic acid (TA)-D-tyrosine (Tyr) NPs with stable photothermal properties using ultrasound-triggered self-assembly technology (Fig. 11). These NPs were incorporated into a hydrogel comprising gum and gelatin, which was then coated onto titanium substrates. Under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, the hydrogel demonstrated excellent biofilm eradication and planktonic bacteria killing, attributed to photothermal-induced structural disruption and enhanced bacterial susceptibility to the antimicrobial agents TA and D-Tyr. In vivo results in a rat model of femur infection further demonstrated significant bacterial clearance, reduction of inflammation, and acceleration of titanium implant osseointegration. Additionally, Li et al. [186] formed a PVA hydrogel coating on red phosphorus-modified titanium implants through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. The hydrogel was modified with CS, PDA, and donors for nitric oxide release. Under 808 nm NIR irradiation, this coating generated photothermal effects and released nitric oxide, which reacted with superoxide radicals from the red phosphorus nanofilm to form peroxynitrite. The combined effects of peroxynitrite, hyperthermia, and superoxide achieved effective MRSA biofilm elimination of over 93.1%.

Electro-responsive hydrogel coatings

Electro-responsive hydrogels can alter their structure and properties under electric field stimulation to achieve specific functions. They typically contain conductive polymers such as polypyrrole, polyaniline, polythiophene, and poly3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene [187]. Under electric field stimulation, charged groups in the hydrogel matrix migrate or reconfigure, causing changes in conformational and mechanical properties that facilitate controlled release of encapsulated therapeutics [188,189]. Despite their potential, electro-responsive hydrogels containing conductive polymers are rarely applied as coatings on titanium implants, likely due to their poor biocompatibility. To address this limitation, Zhao et al. [190] developed a conductive polymer-free electro-responsive hydrogel coating for titanium implants, composed mainly of CS embedded with mesoporous silica NPs for controlled ibuprofen release (Fig. 12A). In vitro studies demonstrated that the application of negative voltage increases solution pH, thereby promoting CS dissolution and ibuprofen release. For example, a −5.0 V stimulus caused hydrogel pH to rise to 10, which significantly accelerated drug release. In contrast, lower negative voltages such as −1.0 V caused a slight pH increase and correspondingly slower drug release. By responding to external stimuli in the form of electric field modulation, electro-responsive hydrogels enable precise control of drug release when applied as a coating on titanium implants, overcoming the issues of uneven or poorly controlled release often experienced with other coating types.

Magneto-responsive hydrogel coatings

Magneto-responsive hydrogels are also a promising strategy for coating titanium implant surfaces. These coatings typically contain magnetic NPs such as γ-Fe2O3, Fe3O4, CoFe2O4, and FePt [192]. Upon exposure to an external magnetic field, the incorporated magnetic NPs are forced into directional alignment and spatial rearrangement. Such particle movement disturbs the hydrogel crosslinking network, thereby inducing changes in the 3D skeleton, pore architecture, and overall physical structure of the hydrogel that triggers stimulus-responsive behavior [193]. Current research on magneto-responsive hydrogel coatings for titanium implants remains relatively limited. In one such study, Zhuang et al. [191] incorporated iron oxide NPs (IOPs) with varying distributions into mineralized collagen coatings, co-deposited electrochemically on titanium substrates to create magneto-responsive coatings (O-IOP-MC) with hydrogel-like swelling and flexibility behavior (Fig. 12B). Under an applied magnetic field, deformation of the O-IOP-MC coating generated mechanical stimuli, which when transmitted to cells resulted in the activation of integrin-mediated RhoA/ROCK signaling. These effects were observed to promote MC3T3-E1 cell differentiation and osteogenic gene expression. Hence, the coupling of magneto-responsive hydrogel coatings on titanium substrates with non-invasive magnetic fields could safely and effectively enable regulation of cellular response and osteogenic differentiation through mechanotransduction.

Ultrasound-responsive hydrogel coatings

Ultrasound is a non-invasive and cost-effective form of energy wave that can penetrate tissues, with adjustable output power through frequency and intensity modulation [194]. Ultrasound-responsive hydrogel coatings exert therapeutic effects through cavitation and sonosensitizer activation, two distinct mechanisms that have both direct and indirect effects on bone regeneration. Ultrasound-induced cavitation triggers the formation, growth, and violent collapse of microbubbles in hydrogels, which generates localized high temperature, pressure, and microjets. This mechanical force can disrupt bacterial membranes and eradicate biofilms to reduce the risk of implant-associated infection, as well as accelerate controlled hydrogel degradation to enable the sustained release of osteogenic drugs or growth factors [187]. In addition, mild mechanical stimulation is beneficial for osseointegration by further promoting osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Meanwhile, embedded sonosensitizers such as Ce6 and manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanoflakes can be activated by ultrasound to produce ROS. High levels of ROS can inactivate planktonic bacteria and disrupt deep-seated biofilms, thereby helping to control persistent infections [195]. Subsequently, maintaining low-level ROS can help induce the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, thereby remodeling the microenvironment and making it more conducive to bone regeneration [196]. For example, Xu et al. [132] prepared an ultrasonic-responsive hydrogel coating on titanium implant surfaces, composed of modified CS, PVA, and sound-sensitive agent Ce6 as well as MnO2 nanoflakes to provide catalytic activity (Fig. 13). The ROS generated by ultrasound could effectively kill bacteria and release bacteria-associated antigens. In addition, Mn2+ provided immune-stimulatory effects, including by inducing dendritic cell maturation, enhancing antigen presentation, and activating T and B cell adaptive immunity, collectively conveying sustained antibacterial protection. Concurrently, a series of catalytic reactions facilitated by the coating could consume glucose and H2O2, thus alleviating the stress of the pathological environment. This multifunctional ultrasound-responsive hydrogel coating was shown to effectively eliminate bacteria and support osseointegration in a diabetic rat model of IAI.

Multi-stimuli responsive synergistic coating systems

The respective advantages and limitations of different stimulus-responsive hydrogel coatings shape their suitability for specific orthopedic applications. While endogenous stimuli-responsive systems, including those activated by pH, ROS, and enzymes offer targeted activation in pathological microenvironments without external intervention, they face challenges such as insufficient working duration, unintended activation due to physiological pH fluctuations, limited effective range, and unclear enzyme content dynamics under in vivo conditions. In contrast, exogenous stimuli-responsive systems such as those activated by light, ultrasound, magnetic field, and heat, enable precise spatiotemporal control through externally applied physical stimuli, but suffer from limitations including low tissue penetration for NIR light, significant attenuation for ultrasound, and cumbersome external setups with overheating concerns for magnetic fields. It is important to note that bone defect microenvironments rarely present single pathological cues. For example, infection triggers simultaneous pH reduction with elevated ROS levels and enzyme secretion. Hence, relying on a single type of stimulus for therapeutic release carries an inherent risk of “false-positive” activation, for instance, non-pathological fluctuations in pH or ROS levels can occur due to normal metabolic activities. Consequently, integrating multiple stimulus-responsive moieties into a unified hydrogel coating emerges as a pivotal strategy for designing intelligent systems to achieve precise and effective implant-mediated therapy.

By integrating two or more responsive mechanisms into a single system, multi-stimuli responsive hydrogel coatings enable on-demand, localized, and context-aware therapeutic functions that surpass the capabilities of single-stimulus systems. Among various multi-stimulus synergistic response strategies, pH-temperature dual-responsive hydrogel coatings have attracted considerable attention, as both pH and temperature are key physiological parameters that deviate from normal ranges during inflammation and bacterial infection, making them highly relevant signals for on-demand therapeutic activation. For example, Li et al. [197] synthesized a double-crosslinked amphiphilic co-network hydrogel directly on the Ti6Al4V surface by ATRP. They incorporated temperature-sensitive monomers and a pH-sensitive monomer, using stereocomplexed polylactic acid as a physical crosslinker and N,N′-methylene bisacrylamide as a chemical crosslinker. This coating exhibited efficient drug release properties and an energy storage modulus matching that of human soft tissues, offering a compelling platform for localized therapeutic delivery in response to both infection-induced pH reduction and inflammation-associated temperature elevation. Beyond pH-temperature dual-responsive systems, other multi-stimulus combinations have been developed to address specific clinical challenges. For instance, a pH and NIR light dual-responsive drug delivery system was developed by modifying TiO2 nanotube arrays with PDA and Fe3+. Under acidic infection-related conditions, the coordination bonds in the coating underwent cleavage to release loaded therapeutic agents, while NIR irradiation induced photothermal conversion to further accelerate drug release, hence enabling spatiotemporally controlled drug delivery applicable to combating peri-implant infections [198]. Jia et al. [199] developed a bio-inspired self-renewing hydrogel coating on titanium implants. This hydrogel coating can respond to low pH and high ROS associated with infection and inflammation by degrading to release TA and tobramycin, while exposing underlying micro/nanostructures to promote tissue integration. This adaptive strategy effectively eliminates infection, reduces inflammation, and accelerates tissue repair in diabetic conditions. Collectively, these multi-stimuli responsive hydrogel systems address the inherent limitations of single-stimulus strategies by enabling coordinated, context-adaptable therapeutic responses to complex pathological microenvironments spanning infectious, inflammatory, and tumor-associated conditions. Their development not only expands the functional adaptability of titanium implants but also establishes a versatile platform for personalized bone regeneration therapies.

Hydrogel implant coatings in bone regeneration

Despite the widespread clinical use of titanium implants in orthopedic and dental surgery, different bone regeneration scenarios impose distinct biological challenges that cannot be addressed by structural materials alone. Hydrogel coatings have therefore emerged as a functional surface modification strategy to endow implants with application-specific therapeutic capabilities. By incorporating bioactive molecules, cells, or responsive components within the hydrogel layer, implant surfaces can be engineered to address particular clinical needs. In bone repair contexts, these coatings have been designed to provide antibacterial protection against implant-associated infection, modulate inflammatory responses to support tissue integration, promote angiogenesis to enhance vascularized bone regeneration, and deliver antitumor agents following tumor resection. For instance, hydrogel coatings loaded with antibacterial components can effectively reduce postoperative infection risks and create a favorable microenvironment for osseointegration [27]. They can also be engineered to convey immunomodulatory capabilities, whereby the chemical characteristics of the coating are leveraged to induce specific immune responses that support osteogenesis [23]. Furthermore, hydrogel coatings can be used to encapsulate bioactive factors that promote vascularization and enhance blood supply, providing nutrients and oxygen for new bone formation [200]. Coatings can also be functionalized for tumor cell monitoring, creating optimal conditions for bone repair in cancer patients [201]. In summary, hydrogel coatings can provide titanium implants with multiple functions, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and antitumor properties, hence offering multidimensional support for bone regeneration with broad application prospects. Antibacterial osteogenesis

Infection is a major cause of bone implant failure [213]. Bacterial colonization on implant surfaces can trigger inflammatory responses that delay osseointegration, as well as lead to severe complications particularly after biofilm formation [214,215]. As conventional antibiotics often fail to effectively eradicate biofilm [216], engineered antibacterial strategies become critical for ensuring the functionality of orthopedic implants. Hydrogel coatings show considerable potential in mitigating implant infection through antibacterial surface modification. By tailoring the chemical composition and physical structure of hydrogels, stimuli-responsive behavior and controlled drug release can be achieved, thereby ensuring effective concentrations of antibacterial agents during early infection stages and reducing antibiotic resistance risks [217]. Additionally, the hydrophilic and porous structure of hydrogel coatings provide a favorable microenvironment for osteoblast adhesion and proliferation [208].

Many studies have focused on incorporating antibacterial agents into hydrogel coatings to enhance the antibacterial capacity of titanium implants. For example, Sille et al. [218] developed a PAAm hydrogel coating containing ampicillin (AMP) and AgNPs on titanium substrates. The synergistic effect of AMP and AgNPs endowed the coating with strong antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus. Additionally, some researchers have co-embedded antibacterial agents and bioactive factors in the same hydrogel coating to simultaneously impart antimicrobial and osteogenic capabilities. For instance, Cheng et al. [206] chemically conjugated catechol groups to GelMA to create GelMA-DOPA, thereby improving hydrogel adhesion to titanium surfaces. Subsequently, antibacterial peptide AMP HHC-36 and synthetic silicate NPs were loaded into the prepolymer solution to introduce dual antibacterial and osteoinductive functions. Experimental results showed sustained HHC-36 release over 21 days, effectively eliminating four representative bacterial species and preventing biofilm formation on titanium implants. Moreover, the presence of silicate NPs enhanced the osteogenic effect of the hydrogel coating. Recent research has also explored the integration of antibacterial agents with stimuli-responsive hydrogel coatings. For example, Ding et al. [211] developed an intelligent hydrogel coating decorated with ZIF-90-Bi-CeO2 NPs (Fig. 14). The coating was attached to the surface of PDA-modified titanium substrate through covalent or non-covalent bonds (hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces). This coating acted through the release of zinc ions and CeO2 NPs alongside the photothermal effect of Bi NPs, which collectively eliminated biofilms and inhibited the growth of planktonic bacteria. In vitro and in vivo results demonstrated effective implant osseointegration resulting from the antibacterial and anti-biofilm functions of the coating.

While antibacterial coatings can be effective for preventing implant-associated infection, their potential impact on osteogenesis must be carefully balanced. Certain antibacterial components, such as silver ions, exhibit concentration-dependent dual effects. At high local concentrations, silver ions can impair osteoblast activity, reduce ALP expression, and hinder matrix mineralization, ultimately inhibiting bone regeneration [219]. Conversely, optimized low doses of silver ions can achieve bacterial inhibition while promoting bone regeneration. Similar trade-offs are also observed with other metal ions and antibiotics [220]. Therefore, the design of hydrogel coatings should not simply seek high antibacterial efficacy but rather strive to achieve a synergistic balance between pathogen elimination and effective osseointegration. This requires precise control over the release kinetics and local concentration of antibacterial components delivered from the hydrogel.

Anti-inflammatory osteogenesis

Although titanium is widely regarded as a bioinert material, its implantation inevitably stimulates the host immune system during early implantation, which can lead to the accumulation of immune cells (e.g., macrophages and lymphocytes) and trigger an inflammatory response. While acute inflammation helps clear foreign bodies and repair damaged tissue, its uncontrolled progression may lead to chronic inflammation, tissue destruction, and implant failure, causing physical and psychological distress to patients. Among the earliest immune cells arriving at the injury site, macrophages play a crucial role in mediating the host response to implant insertion [221], as well as actively participate in the later stages of tissue repair through phagocytosis. During early inflammation, M1-type pro-inflammatory macrophages predominate and generate substantial levels of ROS in the microenvironment. Moderate ROS levels regulate endothelial cell growth and migration while activating MSCs to commence tissue repair [222]. However, excessive ROS causes sustained cellular and tissue damage, thereby impairing osteogenesis [223]. At later stages of inflammation, M1 macrophages transition to an M2 phenotype, which then secrete anti-inflammatory mediators to suppress inflammation and promote tissue regeneration [224]. It is worth noting that excessive or prolonged M1 polarization often results in chronic inflammation, which increases tissue damage and susceptibility to pathogenesis [225]. Therefore, it is essential to undertake rational design of biomaterials to scavenge excess ROS and regulate M1-to-M2 polarization. Such strategies can effectively control and alleviate inflammatory reactions, restore the normal tissue microenvironment, and provide a good biological foundation for implant osseointegration.

Utilizing the controlled drug release and stimuli-responsive properties of hydrogel coatings to “reprogram” macrophage phenotypes represents a novel strategy for resolving inflammation and promoting implant osseointegration. For example, Li et al. [25] constructed a smart responsive hydrogel coating containing phenylboronic acid-modified HA and PVA on Tβ4-loaded titanium implants to promote osseointegration through immunomodulation (Fig. 15). The borate ester bond not only enabled firm adhesion of the hydrogel to the implant surface and tissues but also conferred ROS-responsive characteristics. Tβ4 released from hydrogel degradation promoted the transition of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype and inhibited peri-implant fibrosis. This hydrogel coating hence enabled strong tissue-implant integration while modulating macrophage polarization to accelerate osseointegration. Additionally, Gao et al. [226] fabricated interleukin-4 (IL-4)-loaded TiO2 nanotubes on titanium substrates, which were subsequently coated with interferon-γ-containing carboxymethyl-CS hydrogel to achieve initial interferon-γ release followed by delayed IL-4 release. In vitro results suggested that the coating could simulate the early inflammatory response and later stimulate macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype during subsequent tissue repair. In addition, certain hydrogel coatings exert an anti-inflammatory effect while also having a potent effect on promoting osteogenesis through the embedded growth factors. For example, Wang et al. [227] developed a hydrogel coating composed of quaternary ammonium CS and HA, loaded with IL-4 and BMP-2, to regulate macrophage responses and enhance osseointegration. By enabling dual biomolecule delivery, this hydrogel effectively reduced inflammation through promoting the M2 macrophage phenotype, while also achieving superior regeneration and maturation of new bone compared to treatment with a single biomolecule. Bioinformatics data further suggested that IL-4 and BMP-2 could interact with each other through multiple signaling pathways to achieve a synergistic effect on enhancing osteogenesis.

Angiogenesis-assisted osteogenesis

Sufficient blood supply is crucial for successful implant osseointegration, as it provides oxygen and nutrients to support osteoblast proliferation, thereby accelerating new bone formation and maturation. Adequate vascularization can also reduce the risk of implant infection. Therefore, improving the blood supply around orthopedic implants is a critical part of ensuring their long-term effectiveness. Hydrogel coatings have emerged as a promising approach for promoting peri-implant vascularization and accelerating osseointegration. By mimicking natural ECM, hydrogels provide a favorable microenvironment for endothelial cell adhesion and proliferation, while their porous structure facilitates tissue infiltration and vascular ingrowth [228,229]. Moreover, hydrogel coatings can serve as a delivery platform for cells, growth factors, and therapeutic agents that promote angiogenesis. For example, Li et al. [207] developed an in vitro vascularized bone implant by fabricating allylated gelatin (GelAGE) hydrogel loaded with human MSCs (hMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) onto a titanium alloy substrate. This hydrogel coating was shown to support HUVEC expansion and vascular-like network formation while promoting osteogenic differentiation of hMSCs. Meanwhile, Huang et al. [230] designed a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-loaded thermosensitive collagen hydrogel coating on titanium alloy scaffolds, where sustained VEGF release increased vascular permeability, promoted HUVEC proliferation, and facilitated angiogenesis-mediated bone regeneration. Liu et al. [231] also reported a simvastatin-loaded hydrogel coating on titanium alloy scaffolds that significantly improved new blood vessel formation when implanted into rabbit tibial defects. Additionally, Che et al. [200] incorporated deferoxamine (DFO) into SA-mineralized collagen (SA-MC) to develop a hydrogel coating (DSA-MC) for titanium implants that simultaneously promoted osteogenesis and angiogenesis (Fig. 16). The DSA-MC released DFO, collagen, Ca2+, and phosphate ions to modify the local osteogenic microenvironment, hence effectively stimulating vascularized bone regeneration in rabbit distal femur defects.

Antitumor osteogenesis

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, characterized by high aggressiveness and metastatic potential [232]. Although surgical resection combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy has improved the 5-year survival rate to approximately 60-70%, more than 30% of patients still succumb due to postoperative recurrence [233]. Tumor recurrence is closely associated with the shortcomings of conventional intravenous chemotherapy, including poor targeting, severe toxicity, and limited efficacy [153]. To improve treatment outcomes for osteosarcoma patients, local chemotherapy has been proposed as an effective alternative, which delivers high drug concentrations directly to the tumor microenvironment and hence avoids systemic side effects. Hence, effective osteosarcoma treatment may rely on the development of functional implants that simultaneously achieve bone replacement and local chemotherapy delivery. Because these implants need to not only repair bone defects but also eliminate residual tumor cells through local drug release, multifunctional hydrogel coatings can offer a promising solution through controlled drug release. For example, Jing et al. [201] loaded a PLGA-PEG-PLGA thermosensitive hydrogel with cisplatin as an anti-osteosarcoma drug, which was infiltrated into a porous titanium scaffold to create a bone implant with combined anti-osteosarcoma and osteogenic functions (Fig. 17). The antitumor efficacy, biosafety, and bone repair capacity of the hydrogel-loaded implant were evaluated in tumor-bearing mice and rabbit bone defects. Hydrogel-loaded implants releasing cisplatin could reduce tumor volume by more than two-thirds (from 641.1 to 201.4 mm3) compared to unloaded implant controls while exhibiting negligible organ toxicity. Hence, delivering cisplatin from the hydrogel led to enhanced local antitumor effects while avoiding the adverse reactions (e.g., weight loss) observed in animals receiving systemic cisplatin. Although local cisplatin release led to suppression of osteogenesis at early healing stages (4 weeks), it did not significantly affect the long-term outcomes of bone regeneration at 8 weeks.

However, it should be noted that the vast majority of bone tumor-targeted drugs inhibit bone formation to some extent. Achieving a balance between anticancer activity and osteogenesis in hydrogel-coated titanium implants represents a key design challenge, which has inspired several problem-oriented design strategies. First, time-sequenced drug release profiles can be engineered, for instance to enable rapid local release of chemotherapeutics immediately post-implantation, which eradicates residual tumor cells, followed by sustained release of osteogenic factors to support bone repair. For example, Ji et al. [234] developed a dual-release 3D-printed porous Ti6Al4V implant that integrates an NIR-responsive photothermal hydrogel outer layer for on-demand chemotherapeutic release and a nanotube-based inner reservoir for sustained Zn2+ release. Meanwhile, the hydrogel exhibited near-complete degradation within 21 days, corresponding to the optimal therapeutic window following tumor resection. Second, composite hydrogel designs may allow the co-delivery of multiple drugs. For example, Chu et al. [235] developed a nanocomposite hydrogel for the sustained co-delivery of magnesium ions, anti-PD-L1 antibodies, and Hedgehog pathway antagonist vismodegib, achieving both remarkable tumor growth inhibition and enhanced osteogenesis in a mouse tibia osteosarcoma model. Third, the use of drugs with dual anti-osteosarcoma and pro-osteogenic effects, such as simvastatin, can directly integrate anticancer and bone-regenerative functions without complex delivery profiles. For example, Jing et al. [236] loaded a PLGA-PEG-PLGA thermosensitive hydrogel containing simvastatin into 3D printed porous titanium scaffolds to achieve both anti-osteosarcoma and osteogenic effects. Proteomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis combined with in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the hydrogel-loaded titanium implants conveyed antitumor effects by inducing ferroptosis in osteosarcoma cells through upregulating the expression of transferrin and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 2, while also enhancing osteogenesis by increasing BMP-2 expression.

Multimodal integration strategies for osteogenesis

Complex bone defect microenvironments often involve overlapping pathological cues such as infection, inflammation, oxidative stress, and tumor remnants, which cannot be fully addressed by single-function hydrogel coatings. Multimodal integration strategies may combine antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, or antitumor modules into one hydrogel system, hence enabling synergistic regulation of the bone repair microenvironment and simultaneous promotion of multiple key regenerative processes. These multimodal strategies can overcome the limitations of single-target therapy and adapt to complex clinical scenarios such as infected bone defects, diabetic osteopenia, and postoperative repair of osteosarcoma.

Recent advances have demonstrated that such multimodal integration can be accomplished through three principal hydrogel design strategies: material-based multifunctionality, architectural layering, and spatiotemporal sequential delivery. The material chemistry approach achieves multimodal effects by rationally incorporating multiple bioactive components into the hydrogel network. For instance, a bimetallic Zn/Co metal-organic framework incorporated into a GelMA dialdehyde starch hydrogel coating achieved pH-responsive on-demand release of Zn2+ for antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects alongside Co2+ for antioxidant and pro-angiogenic activity, while also delivering exosomes isolated from 3D-cultured MSCs to promote M2 macrophage polarization and osteogenesis [237]. This design enabled a single coating to simultaneously combat infection, modulate inflammation, and promote vascularized bone regeneration. Architectural layering offers another powerful integration strategy. A multilayered “sandwich” coating fabricated through layer-by-layer assembly onto titanium dioxide nanorod structures comprised graphene oxide, copper ions, and antimicrobial peptide KR12, effectively integrating antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and osteogenic functions within a single coating system [238]. The coating demonstrated multimodal functions, including anti-inflammatory effects by directing macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, enhancement of HUVEC migration and vascularization, and promotion of MC3T3-E1 osteogenic differentiation, while exhibiting an antibacterial inhibition rate of over 63% against E. coli and S. aureus. Such layer-by-layer architectures allow each functional component to be spatially segregated yet synergistically combined. Spatiotemporal sequential delivery strategies mimic the natural bone healing cascade by programming distinct therapeutic actions to occur at specific time windows. For example, a peptide-based inflammation-responsive coating incorporated anti-inflammatory peptide K23, angiogenic peptide K15, and osteogenic peptide Y5 into a single system, with the outermost K23 layer inducing M2 macrophage polarization during early inflammation, followed by matrix metalloproteinase-mediated cleavage that then exposed K15 and Y5 to promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis in later healing stages. This sequentially regulated coating achieved a 161% increase in push-out force and 207% increase in bone volume fraction compared with unmodified controls when implanted in rat femoral bone defects [239]. These multimodal integration strategies represent a paradigm shift from single-function surface modifications towards holistic biomimetic coatings that orchestrate the complex cascade of bone regeneration, addressing the interconnected challenges of infection, inflammation, vascular insufficiency, and osteogenic deficiency through a unified platform. Challenges and prospects

In summary, this review discusses recent progress in hydrogel coatings for titanium implants, focusing on their classification, fabrication routes, stimuli-responsive behaviors, and versatile functions in bone regeneration. Hydrogel coatings fabricated from natural, synthetic, or composite materials can create diverse biomimetic interfaces, transforming otherwise inert titanium substrates into dynamically bioactive surfaces. These coatings can be engineered to respond to endogenous stimuli, such as pH, ROS, and enzymes, as well as external physical triggers including light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields, enabling spatiotemporally controlled drug delivery and adaptive bioactivity. Their multimodal functions show promise for addressing critical clinical complications associated with bone defects, such as bacterial infection, chronic inflammation, poor vascularization, and tumor relapse. However, despite these favorable features, multiple obstacles may still hinder clinical translation, including mechanical mismatch, degradation control, long-term stability, and biosafety. The relative priority of addressing these challenges should be determined by the target application. For example, mechanical compatibility and interfacial stability are particularly important for load-bearing implants, whereas precise stimulus-triggered release and local therapeutic efficacy are more critical in infectious or tumor microenvironments. Meanwhile, standardized evaluation systems, scalable manufacturing, and industrial translation remain important long-term goals for future research.

A major limitation is the relatively weak mechanical performance of many hydrogel systems. Although the softness of hydrogels is beneficial for cell compatibility, their limited mechanical strength and wear resistance may lead to structural damage under physiological loading conditions, particularly in weight-bearing orthopedic implants. Future research should focus on developing mechanically reinforced hydrogel networks, including double-network hydrogels, nanocomposite hydrogels [240], and composite coatings reinforced with ceramic or metal NPs and graphene derivatives [241]. These nanomaterials can enhance mechanical robustness while simultaneously providing additional functionalities such as antibacterial activity or osteogenic stimulation.

Another important challenge is achieving stable and durable adhesion between hydrogel coatings and the titanium substrate. Delamination or degradation of the coating under surgical handling, cyclic mechanical stress, or long-term implantation could compromise therapeutic efficacy. To address this challenge, advanced surface engineering techniques may be adapted to improve hydrogel coating stability [242], such as plasma treatment, micro/nano-structured surface modification, and covalent grafting strategies. In addition, emerging manufacturing technologies such as layer-by-layer assembly, electrophoretic deposition, and 3D or 4D printing could enable more precise control over coating architecture, thickness, and the spatial distribution of functional components, thereby improving both mechanical integration and biological performance [243].

The design of stimulus-responsive hydrogel systems also presents challenges and opportunities. While hydrogel coatings sensitive to pH, enzymes, temperature, and ROS are relatively well established and have demonstrated the ability to release drugs in response to pathological microenvironments, achieving precise and reliable spatiotemporal responsiveness in vivo remains complex. The future research should focus on integrating advanced biosensing capabilities into hydrogel coatings, allowing implants to sense infection, inflammation, or tumor-associated signals and trigger localized therapeutic responses. This may be achieved by expanding current biosensing systems based on smart nanomaterials, such as magnetic NPs, PTAs, and catalytic nanozymes [244]. Next-generation stimuli-responsive hydrogel systems should aim to achieve precise external control over the spatial and temporal release of therapeutic agents as well as multimodal functions in regulating the bone regeneration process.

Optimizing the delivery of therapeutic agents remains another important direction for future work. Hydrogel coatings have shown potential for localized delivery of antibiotics, anti-inflammatory drugs, growth factors, nucleic acids, and anticancer agents [245,246]. However, maintaining the stability and bioactivity of these molecules while achieving sustained and controllable release remains challenging. Emerging delivery approaches involving NP carriers [247] or extracellular vesicle-based systems [248] may be leveraged to enhance the precision and durability of in vivo therapeutic effects. In particular, combining angiogenic and osteogenic cues with immunomodulatory strategies could further improve vascularized bone regeneration around implants. Moreover, although current progress in this area remains limited, the prospect of constructing hydrogels featuring bioactive or drug delivery gradients represents an exciting future research direction for the surface functionalization of titanium implants, as suggested by recent studies on related tissue repair scenarios [249,250]. However, in the design and construction of more complex hydrogel coatings that could involve multiple bioactive agents or stimulus-responsive mechanisms, there is a risk of causing competing release profiles or biological interference, where one agent or mechanism might inadvertently downregulate the activation pathway of another. A careful balance should therefore be considered when designing future multimodal hydrogel coatings to ensure compatibility among different bioactive agents and activation mechanisms.

Finally, comprehensive evaluation of biosafety and long-term performance of hydrogel coatings is essential for their clinical translation [251]. Many published in vivo studies on hydrogel-coated titanium implants report only short-term outcomes, leaving the long-term safety of degradation products insufficiently understood. For synthetic polymers, delayed inflammatory reactions have been reported to emerge months or even years after implantation, potentially due to the accumulation of acidic byproducts from hydrolytic degradation [252]. Notably, PLGA degradation gradually reduces local pH, which not only impairs cell viability but also drives macrophage polarization towards a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, thereby contributing to a chronic inflammatory microenvironment [253]. For PEG-based hydrogels, although their degradation products may be cleared through hepatic and renal pathways, the possibility of long-term retention and risk of organ accumulation remains poorly investigated [254]. Among nanomaterials, TiO2 NPs can reside in organs including the liver, spleen, and lungs for up to 12 months. These NPs can also induce elevated levels of tight junction proteins and pro-inflammatory cytokines in distal tissues such as the brain microvasculature, raising concerns over chronic low-grade systemic inflammation. Furthermore, TiO2 NPs have been reported to interfere with the cargo of osteoblast-derived exosomes and hinder osteogenic differentiation of hMSCs, potentially impairing bone regeneration at the implant site [255]. Meanwhile, despite inherent anti-inflammatory effects at low doses, ZnO NPs can elicit pro-inflammatory responses through zinc ion-mediated pathways if their release kinetics are not properly regulated [256]. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of conducting well-structured longitudinal studies with follow-up periods of ≥12 months. Rigorous evaluations of degradation kinetics, local tissue reactions, and systemic biodistribution of implant materials should be incorporated into the outcome measures. These data are essential for characterizing the long-term safety and performance of functionalized hydrogel coatings applied to titanium implants.

Overall, continued advances in hydrogel materials, nanotechnology, biosensing, and intelligent manufacturing are expected to drive the next generation of multifunctional hydrogel coatings for titanium implants. By integrating mechanical stability, responsive therapeutic delivery, and tailored biological functions, hydrogel coatings hold considerable promise for improving the clinical performance of titanium implants and expanding their applications in complex bone regeneration scenarios.
