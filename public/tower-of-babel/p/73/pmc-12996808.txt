Toxicological effects of volatile organic compounds are mediated by aggravated oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory processes in piglets
Authors: Evaristus Nwulia, Temitayo Olabisi Ajibade, Charles Etang Onukak, Oluwaseun Olarenwaju Esan, John Olusoji Abiola, Temidayo Olutayo Omobowale, Olanrewaju Samuel Olaifa, Olugbenga Olayinka Alaka, Olumayowa Olawumi Igado, Adedunsola Adewunmi Obasa, Godson Ana, Olubukola Christiana Omobowale, Maria Hipolito, Opeyemi Mercy Awofeso, Abimbola Idowu, Ademola Adetokunbo Oyagbemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anthropogenic and natural sources of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) have been associated with hematological, cardiovascular, and respiratory diseases. VOCs can also track directly from the nose to the olfactory bulb (OB). This study investigated whether inhaled VOC-induced toxicity increases free radical generation and oxidative stress biomarker levels along the olfactory neural pathway from the nasal mucosa to brain regions implicated in Alzheimer’s disease. In this study, twenty male six-week-old Landrace piglets were randomly divided into three groups and daily exposed to VOCs for eight weeks: Group A (unexposed piglets; n = 6), Group B (2 h exposure; n = 7), and Group C (3 h exposure; n = 7). The VOC mixture consisted of 5% formaldehyde, 5% benzene, 10% toluene, 10% xylene, and 70% water, and released at a steady-state Total VOC (TVOC) level of 1.0 mg/m3 range (0.6 – 1.8 mg/m3) in the test room. Biomarkers of oxidative stress, antioxidant status, and pro-inflammatory cytokines were assessed in the nasal mucosa, olfactory bulb, pyriform cortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus. Exposure of piglets to VOCs significantly elevated malondialdehyde, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) generation, nitric oxide, and acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity along the contiguous olfactory neural structures, from the olfactory mucosa in the nose to the olfactory cortex and adjoining hippocampus. There was also a time-dependent significant reduction in glutathione content, superoxide dismutase levels, and glutathione S-transferase activities in the olfactory regions of VOCs-exposed piglets. Olfactory neuroinflammation was evidenced by significant elevations of IL-6, IL-8, IFNγ, and TNF-α. In summary, exposure of piglets to volatile organic compounds resulted in a significant elevation of biomarkers of oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokines, and in depletion of the neuronal and systemic antioxidant defense systems. Furthermore, the observed olfactory neurotoxicity and neuroinflammation were more pronounced and prolonged after three hours of exposure to volatile organic compounds. Parallel patterns of neurotoxicity in the nasal-olfactory mucosa and the olfactory cortex suggest that the nose could serve as a window into pathophysiologic events underlying neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are toxic environmental pollutants emitted by natural and anthropogenic processes [1]. While VOCs are released from natural sources such as vegetation and volcanic eruptions, human activities, especially petrochemical refinement and vehicular emissions, constitute the major sources of VOC pollution in urban communities [2], [3]. Moreover, nonbiodegradable and anthropogenic VOCs, such as toluene and formaldehyde, are released into the environment from paints, varnishes, waxes, and solvents used in furniture making and in electronic devices [4], [5]. These emissions pose a significant threat to human health, particularly as more people spend prolonged periods indoors [4], [5]. The toxicological effects of VOCs arise from their chemical interactions with other compounds, such as nitrogen oxides, to form tropospheric ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate, which have devastating effects on the environment and human health [6], [7]. VOCs released into the environment reduce the water quality index, impair the normal functioning of major organs and systems in exposed humans and animals, and contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of many diseases, including those of the nervous system [8].

Generally, humans and animals are exposed to VOCs through the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and skin routes, with the greatest toxic effects observed in the respiratory, cardiovascular, endocrine, and central nervous systems [9], [10], [11]. The central nervous system is particularly vulnerable to VOC-induced toxicity because these compounds are highly lipophilic and easily cross the blood-brain barrier to exert their neurotoxic effects [12]. Under experimental conditions, sub-chronic exposure to VOCs was associated with heightened oxidative stress, altered expression of neurotransmitters and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors, and neurotoxicity in mice and rats [13], [14]. Likewise, neuroinflammation has been suggested as a possible mechanism of VOC-induced toxicity in a mouse model [15].

Previous in vitro studies have shown that VOC exposure induces oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and pro-inflammatory signaling in neuronal and glial cells [12], [16]. However, these models do not capture the complex anatomical and functional interactions along the nasal-olfactory-brain axis, nor the integrated neuroimmune responses that occur in vivo. Therefore, an in vivo model is required for system-level validation. This study was designed to investigate the specific oxidative and neuroinflammatory pathways involved in VOCs-induced neurotoxicity in piglets. The piglet was selected for its close anatomical, physiological, and immunological similarities to humans, thereby enhancing the translational relevance of the findings [17]. The results of this study may improve understanding of the roles of inflammatory cytokines in VOC-mediated neuroinflammation and provide a basis for developing interventions to manage neurodegenerative disorders in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Reduced glutathione (GSH), 2-dichloro-4-nitrobenzene (CDNB), 5,5′-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB), trichloroacetic acid (TCA), thiobarbituric acid (TBA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets, epinephrine, xylenol orange, Sorbitol, and acetylcholine iodide were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA). All other chemicals used for this study were of analytical grade.

Housing condition and animal care

A group of 20 male Landrace piglets, each 6 weeks old, was obtained from the Teaching and Research Farm of the University of Ibadan for the study. Upon arrival, the animals underwent a 1-week acclimatization period at the Department of Veterinary Medicine’s experimental animal unit at the University of Ibadan. The experimental piglets were housed in a spacious room measuring 10 m wide, 10 m long, and 15 m high, providing ample space for movement and social interaction. This generous enclosure ensured compliance with animal welfare standards and allowed for natural behaviors to be expressed. The animals were fed a carefully formulated pig grower mash, specifically designed to meet the nutritional requirements of growing piglets. This balanced feed contained an optimal blend of proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, and minerals to support healthy growth and development. The mash was provided at regular intervals throughout the day, with feeding schedules adjusted according to the piglets’ age and weight. Fresh, clean water was made available to the piglets ad libitum, ensuring they always had unrestricted access to drinking water. Multiple water dispensers were strategically placed throughout the enclosure to ensure easy access for all animals, regardless of their location within the room. The water supply was regularly checked and maintained to guarantee cleanliness and proper functioning of the dispensers.

To facilitate individual identification, each piglet was fitted with an ear tag during the acclimatization phase. The experimental protocol was initiated two weeks after the piglets’ arrival at the facility. This extended acclimatization period allowed the animals to fully adjust to their new environment before the commencement of any treatments or interventions.

Experimental design, animal exposure, and exposure device

In accordance with the 3 R principles [18], the study design was hypothesis-driven, used the minimum number of animals required for statistical power, and generated multiple biochemical and molecular endpoints from the same tissues to maximize data yield. We aimed to achieve a steady-state Total VOC (TVOC) level of 1.0 mg/m3 in the air sampled from the test room, which is a 400 sq. feet (i.e., ∼37 sq. meters) room roughly three meters high. This range was informed by levels we sampled from workplaces with high VOC levels in an unpublished survey and by data from international studies.

For this study, 20 piglets were randomly assigned to the following groups: Control (n = 6), 2 h of VOC exposure (n = 7), and 3 h of VOC exposure (n = 7). The test atmosphere was generated by aerosolizing a VOC mixture consisting of 5% formaldehyde, 5% benzene, 10% toluene, 10% xylene, and 70% water [14]. This mixture was dispersed using a modified version of the aerosol fogging system described by Omobowale et al [19]. The system comprised PVC pipes of varying diameters (12.5 mm, 18.75 mm, and 25 mm). A 20-liter cistern containing the VOC mixture was connected to a 1-horsepower pump via a 25 mm-diameter pipe. The pump was regulated by a sensor, programmed to activate for 20 s every half hour, supplying the VOC mixture to the foggers. At a room ventilation rate of 0.5 L/min and sensor-regulated dispersal for 20 s every hour, we achieved our desired TVOC levels of 1.0 mg/m3 range (0.6 – 1.8 mg/m3).

Animal ethics

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol of the University of Ibadan Animal Care and Use Ethics Committee (ACUREC) approved the study, and UI-ACUREC/24/104 was assigned, while the guide for the care and use of laboratory animals was also followed as previously reported by Garber et al [20].

Brain sample collection and preparation

At termination of the study, the piglets were humanely euthanized, followed by the harvesting of brain tissues. The piglets were anesthetized with 0.1 mL of xylazine/ketamine (v/v) via the intramuscular (IM) route. The overdose of xylazine/ketamine was used for euthanasia. Tissues harvested for histopathological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical assays included the nasal mucosa, olfactory bulb, pyriform cortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus.

Neuronal post-mitochondrial fractions preparation

Brain samples (olfactory bulb, pyriform cortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus) were quickly excised, rinsed, blotted with filter paper, weighed, chopped into bits, and homogenized with homogenizing buffer (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) using a Teflon homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min with a cold centrifuge at −4°C to obtain post-mitochondrial fractions (PMFs). The supernatants (PMFs) obtained were used for biochemical assays.

Biochemical assays

Determination Of brain and olfactory mucosal antioxidant defense status

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay was carried out using the method of Misra and Fridovich [18], with slight modifications by Oyagbemi et al [21]. The glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was also measured according to Beutler et al. [22], glutathione S-transferase (GST) was estimated by the method of Habig et al [23]. using 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene as the substrate, and the reduced glutathione (GSH) content was estimated by the method of Ellman [24].

Determination Of brain and olfactory mucosal markers of oxidative stress

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content was determined using the method described by Wolff [25]. Malondialdehyde (MDA), as a product of lipid peroxidation, was determined using the method described by Varshney and Kale [26]. Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity was evaluated using the Ellman method [24]. Nitric oxide was quantified using the method described by Olaleye et al [27].

Determination Of levels of proinflammatory cytokine markers in olfactory mucosa and brain

Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-6 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interferon gamma (IFNγ) were determined in serum using Bioss enzyme-linked immunosorbent (ELISA) assay kits following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the test of significance between two groups was estimated with Student’s t-test. The One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Turkey’s post-hoc test in GraphPad Prism 5.0 was also carried out, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Environmental stressors, including air pollutants, are known to induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses that contribute to neurological dysfunction [28]. Air pollution, particularly VOC exposure, has been associated with adverse neurological and psychiatric outcomes, including stroke, cognitive impairment, and neurodegenerative disorders [29], [30]. Consistent with these reports, our findings demonstrate dose-dependent increases in oxidative stress biomarkers and pro-inflammatory cytokines along the olfactory neural pathway.

Previous studies have shown that toxicant exposure can disrupt neuronal function and trigger neuroinflammatory cascades that promote neurodegeneration [31], [32]. The significant elevations in IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IFNγ observed in this study support the role of VOCs as potent inducers of neuroinflammation. In addition, the marked increase in lipid peroxidation, hydrogen peroxide generation, nitric oxide levels, and acetylcholinesterase activity, together with depletion of antioxidant defenses, indicates severe oxidative imbalance, a key pathological feature in neurodegenerative processes. These findings further support epidemiological evidence linking air pollution to increased incidence and severity of neurological disorders and highlight the olfactory pathway as a potential route through which inhaled toxicants may initiate central nervous system injury.

Oxidative stress is an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the antioxidant defense system, favoring ROS production [33]. The hallmark of oxidative stress is protein denaturation, lipid peroxidation, and nucleic acid oxidation [34]. Arrays of pathological conditions, including Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), have been associated with oxidative stress [31], [35]. Oxidative stress and neuroinflammation as pathophysiological mechanisms of most neurodegenerative diseases could also exacerbate memory loss, neurobehavioral deficits, and cognitive impairment in AD [36]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) is the enzyme at the first line of defense against oxidative stress [37]. The results of this study revealed that reduced SOD activity indicated oxidative stress due to the accumulation of superoxide anion radicals, which can initiate lipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Peroxidation of PUFA is the initial step in lipid peroxidation and free radical generation, resulting in the production of toxic aldehydes such as 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4HNE), malondialdehyde, and acrolein [38], [39]. We also observed a dose-dependent decrease in SOD activity, indicating that greater exposure led to higher superoxide anion radical production and greater tissue damage.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) plays a central role in detoxifying hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides and functions in concert with reduced glutathione (GSH) to maintain cellular redox balance [40]. Oxidative stress is known to deplete GSH, which may be accompanied by reduced activities of SOD, GPx, and GST. The present study found a decrease in GST activity in all tissues examined, except the hippocampus, where a significant increase was observed, especially at the 3-hour VOCs exposure time point. This region-specific increase may represent an adaptive detoxification response to sustained toxicant burden, as previously reported for GST induction under conditions of heightened oxidative and electrophilic stress [41]. This may suggest differential vulnerability and redox responsiveness of the hippocampus compared with other olfactory-connected regions.

The region-specific increase in GST activity observed in the hippocampus at the longer exposure duration may also reflect activation of endogenous cytoprotective pathways. A key regulator of cellular antioxidant defense is the Keap1-Nrf2-ARE signaling axis, which is activated by oxidative and electrophilic stress and drives phase II detoxification and the production of antioxidant enzymes [42]. Induction of this pathway has been reported as an adaptive response to toxicant exposure and may account for the selective upregulation of GST in the hippocampus despite widespread oxidative injury in other regions [42]. Given Nrf2’s central role in maintaining redox homeostasis and limiting oxidative damage in neurodegenerative conditions, its activation represents a potential protective mechanism against VOC-induced neurotoxicity [43]. These observations suggest that modulation of the Keap1-Nrf2-ARE pathway may be a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate the deleterious effects of chronic VOC exposure and warrant further investigation in future studies.

Toxicity associated with VOCs exposure was also assessed using AChE activity and nitric oxide (NO) content in tissues. This study found a dose-dependent, significant increase in AChE activity and NO levels, indicating neurotoxicity and nitrosative stress, respectively. This is in line with findings from previous studies, which have shown a positive correlation between increased AChE activity, nitrosative stress, and cognitive impairments [44], [45], [46]. Other studies have linked exaggerated elevated AChE activity and neurodegenerative pathology like AD [47], [48]. Therefore, the concurrent increase in AChE activity and NO observed in this study suggests that chronic VOCs exposure could further exacerbate neurotoxicity, promote cholinergic dysfunction and nitrosative stress, resulting in neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.

This study also found significant dose-dependent increases in IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IFNγ across the olfactory bulb, piriform cortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus, with the greatest increases observed following the longer exposure duration. This robust cytokine response indicates activation of neuroinflammatory pathways along the olfactory neural axis and is consistent with the marked oxidative imbalance observed in these regions. These reactive oxygen species are well-established triggers of glial activation, and the observed cytokine surge likely reflects coordinated responses of microglia and astrocytes, the principal immune effector cells of the central nervous system [49]. Therefore, the exposure-dependent increase in these mediators suggests that inhaled VOCs can initiate and amplify neuroinflammatory cascades that promote neuronal dysfunction and increase regional vulnerability to neurodegenerative processes.

Conclusion

In summary, inhalational exposure of piglets to volatile organic compounds resulted in a significant elevation of biomarkers of oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokines, accompanied by depletion of neuronal and systemic antioxidant defense systems. These observed neurotoxicity and neuroinflammation were more pronounced after three hours of exposure to VOCs. These findings demonstrate that the olfactory route is a vulnerable avenue for VOC-induced neurotoxicity and neuroinflammation, with greater injury observed following longer exposure duration. Therefore, this study provides mechanistic evidence linking environmental VOC exposure to pathways implicated in neurodegenerative processes and highlights potential public health implications amid increasing air pollution.
