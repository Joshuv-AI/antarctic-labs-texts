Cipepofol for adjunctive sedation during neuraxial anesthesia: a study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mi Chen, Lirong Chen, Yang Yang, Shuhua Huang, Yimin Ren, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCipepofol is a novel 2,6-disubstituted phenol derivative increasingly used for anesthesia and sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy and fiberoptic bronchoscopy. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of cipepofol for adjunctive sedation during neuraxial anesthesia, thereby providing evidence for its clinical application.MethodsThis was a randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial. Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the cipepofol group or the propofol group using a random number table. The target sedation level was defined as a Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score of 3–4. Patients allocated to the cipepofol group received an intravenous loading dose of 0.1 mg/kg cipepofol administered over 30 ± 10 s, followed by a continuous infusion initiated at 0.3 mg/kg/h to achieve the target sedation level. Those in the propofol group received an intravenous loading dose of 0.5 mg/kg propofol administered over 30 ± 10 s, followed by a continuous infusion initiated at 1.5 mg/kg/h. The primary outcome was the sedation success rate. Secondary outcomes included the following: the percentage of total study drug administration time during which MOAA/S scores were maintained at 3–4, 1–2, or 5, respectively; time from the start of the intravenous loading dose to the first occurrence of MOAA/S score < 4, and the proportion of subjects achieving MOAA/S score < 4 within 3 min; proportion of subjects requiring rescue sedation; the vital signs of subjects; number of dose adjustments and total sedative drug consumption; time from discontinuation of the study drug infusion to the first postoperative MOAA/S score of 5, length of stay in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), and recovery status at PACU discharge; satisfaction levels of the anesthesiologist, surgeon, and subjects.DiscussionThe purpose of this study is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of cipepofol as an adjunct sedative during neuraxial anesthesia. If cipepofol demonstrates beneficial properties comparable to propofol for adjunctive sedation during neuraxial anesthesia, it could serve as a viable clinical alternative.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry ChiCTR2500102635. Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

Neuraxial anesthesia is a commonly used anesthetic technique in clinical practice, suitable for most abdominal and lower body procedures, and can be cautiously applied to certain surgeries involving the neck, chest, and upper extremities. However, during surgery, patients may experience psychological distress such as tension, anxiety, and fear. This is particularly relevant for patients with cognitive dysfunction or those unable to cooperate adequately, as stimuli related to the surgical procedure—including visual, auditory, and olfactory cues—may trigger agitation or movement responses [1–3]. Such uncooperative agitation may interfere with surgical progression and carries a risk of injury to the operative site, which is particularly critical in orthopedic procedures [2]. To alleviate or eliminate patient anxiety and discomfort, sedative agents are commonly administered adjunctively during neuraxial anesthesia in clinical practice to enhance surgical safety and improve patient outcomes [4].

Currently, commonly used sedative agents in clinical practice include propofol, midazolam, dexmedetomidine, and remimazolam, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Propofol is characterized by rapid onset, potent efficacy, and quick recovery; however, it is associated with adverse effects such as hypotension, respiratory depression, and injection pain [5–7]. However, repeated administration for sustained sedation during surgery may lead to unpredictable sedation duration. Dexmedetomidine provides reliable sedative and analgesic effects, demonstrating superior efficacy to midazolam for procedural sedation during local anesthesia [8]. However, its action on α2-adrenergic receptors at both presynaptic and postsynaptic sympathetic nerve terminals can lead to vasoconstriction, vasodilation, and reflex bradycardia, resulting in transient hypertension, bradycardia, and hypotension [9]. While remimazolam has minimal impact on the circulatory and respiratory systems with limited fluctuations in blood pressure and heart rate, it exhibits a slower onset of action and longer recovery time compared to propofol [10].

Cipepofol is a novel 2,6-disubstituted phenol derivative that acts on γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors to promote chloride channel opening and induce neuronal hyperpolarization. Studies indicate that the introduction of specific substituent groups enables tighter binding of cipepofol to GABAA receptors [11]. Cipepofol exhibits a half-life comparable to propofol but demonstrates approximately fivefold greater potency [12]. Multiple studies have shown that cipepofol is non-inferior to propofol for induction and maintenance of general anesthesia, sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy, and ICU sedation, while reducing the incidence of adverse effects such as hypotension, respiratory depression, and injection pain [13–15]. These characteristics suggest its potential to benefit both clinicians and patients during neuraxial anesthesia.

In summary, cipepofol is a safe and effective agent for sedation and anesthesia. Compared with other sedative drugs, it demonstrates a reduced incidence of adverse effects such as hypotension, respiratory depression, and injection pain, indicating broad clinical application prospects. However, current evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of cipepofol for sedation during neuraxial anesthesia remains limited. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of cipepofol as an adjunct sedative during neuraxial anesthesia, thereby providing evidence for its clinical application.

Eligibility criteria for participants {14a}

Inclusion criteria are (1) males or females aged ≥ 18 and < 65 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II; (3) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 18 and ≤ 30 kg/m2; (4) preoperative vital signs: heart rate ≥ 60 and ≤ 100 beats/min; systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 90 and ≤ 160 mmHg; diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 60 and ≤ 100 mmHg; and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) ≥ 92%.

Exclusion Criteria are (1) Contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia (e.g., coagulation disorders); (2) History of cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, or gastrointestinal diseases, or history of alcohol or drug abuse within three months prior to surgery; (3) Allergy to propofol, cipepofol, or any excipients or anesthetic agents involved; (4) Use of any hypnotic, anesthetic, analgesic, or muscle relaxant medications prior to surgery; (5) Psychiatric disorders or inability to cooperate; (6) Other reasons considered inappropriate for participation by the investigators.

Eligibility criteria for sites and those delivering interventions {14b}

This is a single-center trial conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University.

Site eligibility: The trial will be conducted in the main operating rooms of the hospital, which are fully equipped for general anesthesia and advanced patient monitoring. The site has obtained ethical approval for this study (approval number: 2025[198]).

Intervention provider eligibility: Study drug administration will be performed by attending anesthesiologists who hold a valid Chinese anesthesia license, have ≥ 5 years of clinical experience, and have completed protocol training.

Intervention and comparator

Intervention and comparator description {15a}

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of cipepofol as an adjunct sedative during neuraxial anesthesia. Prior to study drug administration, neuraxial anesthesia was performed by an experienced anesthesiologist. After confirming satisfactory anesthetic effect (no painful expression or subjective pain upon clamping the surgical area), baseline MOAA/S score was assessed. Subsequently, an intravenous loading dose of the study drug was administered, followed by a continuous infusion at the initial maintenance rate.

The MOAA/S scale was used to assess sedation levels and guide dosage adjustments. The target sedation level was defined as an MOAA/S score of 3–4. Patients in the cipepofol group received an intravenous loading dose of 0.1 mg/kg cipepofol over 30 ± 10 s, followed by a continuous infusion initiated at 0.3 mg/kg/h. Patients in the propofol group received an intravenous loading dose of 0.5 mg/kg propofol over 30 ± 10 s, followed by a continuous infusion initiated at 1.5 mg/kg/h.

If the MOAA/S score was > 4, the infusion rate was increased by 0.4–2 mg/kg/h for propofol or 0.08–0.4 mg/kg/h for cipepofol. If the MOAA/S score was < 3, the infusion rate was decreased by 0.4–2 mg/kg/h for propofol or 0.08–0.4 mg/kg/h for cipepofol.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated intervention/comparator {15b}

If the MOAA/S score remained at 5 three minutes after the initial loading dose, rescue sedation was administered using half of the initial loading dose of cipepofol. Such patients were excluded from the study. In cases of severe bradycardia or hypotension, atropine (0.01 mg/kg) or ephedrine (0.1 mg/kg) was administered, respectively, with repeat doses permitted at intervals of no less than 1 min if necessary. The study drug infusion was discontinued at the end of surgery.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention/comparator {15c}

Not applicable. As this trial involves continuous drug infusion controlled entirely by the anesthesiologist during surgery, traditional “participant adherence” strategies are not applicable.

Concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {15d}

During the study drug infusion period, standard neuraxial anesthesia, monitoring, supportive care, and the use of rescue medications for hemodynamic instability (e.g., ephedrine) or pain (fentanyl) are permitted and documented.

The administration of any other sedative, hypnotic, or anxiolytic drug (e.g., midazolam, dexmedetomidine) is prohibited, as it would interfere with the primary outcome assessment.

Ancillary and post-trial care {34}

Participants will receive standard postoperative care after the trial procedures. In the event of any injury directly related to trial participation, the sponsor will provide all necessary medical treatment free of charge and appropriate financial compensation, in accordance with national regulations and the insurance policy terms.

Sample size {19}

Sample size was calculated using PASS 2021 software. As a non-inferiority trial with sedation success as the primary outcome, a non-inferiority margin of 8% was set based on previous literature. Assuming a sedation success rate of 99% for both cipepofol and propofol in regional anesthesia-assisted sedation, with a one-sided type I error of 0.025, statistical power of 90%, and a non-inferiority margin of 8%, a sample size of 66 patients was required. Accounting for a 5% dropout rate, we planned to enroll 70 patients undergoing regional anesthesia, randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio (35 patients per group).

Recruitment {20}

During the routine pre-anesthesia evaluation, the attending anesthesiologist will assess patients scheduled for surgery under neuraxial anesthesia based on the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible patients will then be invited to participate, and the study will be explained in detail using the approved informed consent form.

Assignment of interventions: randomization

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the cipepofol group or the propofol group using a random number table. Both researchers and participants were blinded to the treatment assignment.

No applicable. Both researchers and participants were blinded to the treatment assignment.

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}

The allocation sequence will be concealed in opaque sealed envelopes.

Implementation {23}

The personnel responsible for enrolling participants and those assigning participants to interventions will NOT have access to the random allocation sequence.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}

Participants, all clinical staff (anesthesiologists, surgeons), and study personnel (outcome assessors, data analysts) will be blinded to group allocation. Only the researcher preparing the drugs will be unblinded.

How will be blinding be achieved {24b}

An unblinded researcher will prepare the study drugs according to the randomization list. All syringes will be identical in appearance and labeled with identical, non-revealing labels.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

Unblinding is permitted only in medical emergencies affecting patient safety, following a predefined procedure conducted by the PI or designee, with mandatory documentation of the reason and process.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {25a}

At enrollment, demographic data (age, sex, height, weight, body mass index), medical history, vital signs, comorbidities, anesthesia technique, and surgical type were collected. During study drug administration, the following were recorded: anesthesia induction time, time to first MOAA/S score < 4, intraoperative MOAA/S scores at multiple timepoints and at the end of surgery, vital signs (HR, BP, SpO₂), surgery end time, drug discontinuation time, time to MOAA/S score of 5, PACU discharge time, Aldrete score at discharge, delirium assessment (using the Nu-DESC scale), and satisfaction levels of the anesthesiologist, surgeon, and subject. Investigators were required to ensure the authenticity, completeness, and accuracy of all data. All study records were to be fully completed. Upon completion of observation, case report forms were submitted to the principal investigator for review. Data managers entered the data into a database for organization and statistical analysis. Original research documents were archived by the principal investigator following statistical analysis.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {25b}

Given the short follow-up period ending at PACU discharge, a researcher will ensure real-time, complete data collection for all participants.

Data management {26}

Data will be collected using case report forms for quality control. All study data will be stored and backed up regularly. Paper documents will be archived in locked cabinets within the department. The overall data management process will be overseen by the principal investigator in accordance with institutional data governance guidelines.

Confidentiality {33}

All collected personal information will be de-identified and replaced with study codes. Research documents will be stored in locked facilities, and access to electronic data will be password-protected and restricted to authorized research personnel. After trial completion, all identifiable information will be securely retained for at least 5 years per institutional policy before being destroyed.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

Statistical analyses will be performed based on the intention-to-treat principle. Continuous variables that are normally distributed will be presented as mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup comparisons will be conducted using independent samples t-tests, while intragroup comparisons across different time points will be analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance, with post hoc comparisons performed using Tukey’s test. Categorical data will be expressed as frequencies/percentages (n/%) and compared between groups using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test. All analyses will be conducted using SPSS (version 26.0), and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants in the groups to which they were originally assigned.

How missing data will be handled in the analysis {27c}

For the primary outcome, a complete-case analysis will be performed initially. If the proportion of missing data exceeds 5%, multiple imputation methods will be considered for sensitivity analysis, with assumptions about the missingness mechanism stated.

Discussion

This study is a randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority clinical trial designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of cipepofol for adjunctive sedation during neuraxial anesthesia. The primary hypothesis is that cipepofol is non-inferior to propofol in terms of sedation success rate and the incidence of adverse events.

The findings of this study will inform the use of cipepofol for sedation during neuraxial anesthesia and provide evidence for its clinical application.

Compared to general anesthesia, neuraxial anesthesia requires less medication, reduces drug accumulation, and minimizes the impact of anesthetics on cardiac and pulmonary perfusion [16–18]. However, patients remaining conscious during surgery are more susceptible to tension, anxiety, and fear, which may induce stress and increase the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications as well as postoperative cognitive dysfunction. Since patients do not lose consciousness completely as under general anesthesia, they often experience significant anxiety during the procedure. Consequently, the requirements for sedative agents in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia are relatively high. The ideal sedative should not only provide a comfortable diagnostic and therapeutic experience but also demonstrate rapid onset, quick recovery after discontinuation, a predictable dose–response relationship, no accumulation, and minimal adverse effects [19].

Cipepofol, a short-acting intravenous sedative derived from structural modification of propofol, exhibits pharmacokinetic properties highly similar to propofol. As a highly selective GABAA receptor enhancer, it demonstrates approximately 4–5 times greater potency [20, 21]. Characterized by high efficacy, high selectivity, and a favorable adverse effect profile [19], cipepofol enables precise and controllable sedation depth, effectively alleviating patient discomfort. Its potentially more stable hemodynamic profile may further improve patient outcomes.

Recent studies have demonstrated that cipepofol is non-inferior to propofol for general anesthesia, with a significantly lower incidence of hypotension during administration. As an alternative sedative, it offers superior value, particularly in reducing side effects such as injection pain [22, 23]. These clear clinical benefits establish cipepofol as an important and better-tolerated sedative option, indicating broad application prospects. The use of sedatives during neuraxial anesthesia can effectively alleviate patient anxiety and agitation, thereby contributing to improved patient outcomes.

In summary, this study will evaluate the efficacy and safety of cipepofol for adjunctive sedation during neuraxial anesthesia and confirm its non-inferiority to propofol. The trial results are expected to provide evidence to support cipepofol as a new sedative option for adjunctive sedation during neuraxial anesthesia.
