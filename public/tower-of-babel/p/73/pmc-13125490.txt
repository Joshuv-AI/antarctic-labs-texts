Zero-shot English–Assamese neural machine translation via pivot-based cross-lingual embedding alignment and transfer learning
Authors: Basab Nath, Yonis Gulzar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neural Machine Translation (NMT) for low-resource languages such as Assamese faces significant challenges due to the scarcity of parallel corpora. To address this, we propose a novel zero-shot translation framework that leverages linguistic proximity between Assamese and Bengali, enabling English-to-Assamese translation without direct parallel data. Our approach combines a multilingual transfer learning architecture based on mBART with cross-lingual embedding alignment via Procrustes optimization, unifying English, Bengali, and Assamese into a shared semantic space. We further mitigate out-of-vocabulary (OOV) challenges through subword tokenization, embedding projection, and dynamic vocabulary expansion, while advanced language tagging mechanisms reduce off-target errors by enforcing explicit language distinctions during inference. We evaluate our framework on a curated English–Assamese dataset, achieving a BLEU score of 28.65–7.53 points higher than direct translation baselines. Human evaluations highlight significant improvements, with adequacy and fluency scores of 4.5/5 and 4.6/5, respectively, and an 8.4% error rate reduction. Ablation studies confirm the critical roles of embedding alignment and language-aware tagging, while inference speed analysis demonstrates a 26.9% improvement over baseline models. This work demonstrates that (pivot based) transfer learning and cross-lingual alignment is effective in bridging resource gaps for linguistically proximate languages.

Introduction

Cross-lingual communication has become a significant technology through Neural Machine Translation (NMT), that provides an efficient translation across several languages. Nevertheless, despite the progress of such multilingual NMT systems in mBART and mT51, these models still mainly rely on the existence of large parallel corpora for best performance. These models greatly benefited high resource languages, but low resource languages such as Assamese still suffer from some limitations in parallel data availability2,3. Not only do these directly affect direct translation models, but they make it more challenging for low resource language integration into multilingual architecture, thereby yielding suboptimal translation quality2,4. It comes with specific challenges translating lower resource languages as there are no linguistic resources, a higher chance for being off target, and maintaining semantic consistency5. These challenges are even more noticeable when expressed in Assamese, where we have limited resources such as monolingual corpora and bilingual dictionaries. However, Bengali presents a promising solution to these problems. Being it Indo Aryan language which is closely related to Assamese is Bengali has common grammatical structures and large chunk of its lexicon. This linguistic proximity opens up the possibility for cross lingual transfer learning where knowledge of the English Bengali translation models can be leveraged towards Assamese6.

Existing methods for low-resource NMT have explored multilingual training and transfer learning as potential solutions. Multilingual models, trained on multiple language pairs, can effectively share parameters between languages and generalise to unseen pairs through zero-shot learning7,8. However, these models often struggle with off-target translations and exhibit limited accuracy for languages without sufficient data representation9. Recent advances in cross-lingual embedding alignment techniques have shown promise in addressing these issues by mapping languages into a shared semantic space, enabling better representation and transfer for low-resource languages10.

Building on this motivation, several prior studies have sought to overcome the data scarcity problem through innovative multilingual and pivot-based approaches. These developments form the foundation of our proposed framework.

Johnson et al.11 has shown that multilingual models can reuse shared parameters between language pairs to generalize to unseen language pairs. To narrow down the semantic gap across languages, cross-lingual embedding alignment has become a popular approach. Ruder et al.14 reviewed approaches such as Procrustes optimization and adversarial training, noting their efficacy in common-space learning. More recent techniques, such as optimal transport based alignment15, the method we describe and implement in this paper, improve cross-lingual representations by minimizing Wasserstein distances between language pairs for better aligning them. These approaches work particularly well for low-resource languages, where an indirect pivoting through the middle language can side-step issues with data sparsity16. For instance, Nguyen et al. The use of Indonesian as a pivot language increased the quality of Javanese-Madurese translation to around 0.32% BLEU, proving that linguistic proximity is an advantage17 (2022). Pivot based approaches have drawn attention in low resource scenarios. Zhang et al. Dynamic vocabulary adaptation19 and sub word regularization15, act as a way to enable out-of-vocab (OOV) words in the system for VLSGs, allowing such systems to increase base tokenization performance across typologically similar languages e.g. Bengali and Assamese20. Language-aware mechanisms like explicit tagging have also been crucial. This development coincides with our approach that facilitates a comprehensive interoperability between tagging and embedding alignment, leading to an improvement in Assamese translation.

Pivot-based mapping of feature space across languages is presented, Li et al. Javed et al. introduced the idea of transformer-based reranking techniques to enhance the contextual and syntactic quality of low-resource translation, which once again proves the need to refine translations with the post-decoding methods. On a bigger scale, Yazar et al.24 and Zhang et al. surveys of low-resource NMT were quite extensive, highlighting such issues as off-target generation, morphological divergence, and insufficient parallel supervision as persistent. Simultaneously, Hongying et al. explained the idea of contrastive decoding and minimizing hallucinations in low-resource conditions using large language models, which further demonstrates the growing interest in decoding-time control techniques, as presented by Hongying et al.25. The new findings uphold the necessity of procedures with the optimal balance of representation alignment, target-language control, and robustness, which are the exact goals of our teachers in the proposed framework. In addition to the transfer at the representation level, recent works investigated prompt-based and adapter-based paradigms of low-resource and zero-shot neural machine translation. Our approach is representationlevel aligned and contrary to prompt-based approaches, such as AstraMT that we will discuss in Section 3.2, our model comprises one encoder–decoder architecture.

Adapter-based methods introduce lightweight, task-specific modules within largely frozen pretrained models to enable modular and parameter-efficient transfer across languages26,27. In parallel, prompt-based techniques leverage carefully designed natural-language instructions to steer large multilingual models toward desired translation behaviors28,29. While these approaches offer flexibility and efficient adaptation, they typically rely on task-specific supervision, curated prompts, or auxiliary tuning data. In contrast, our work focuses on embedding-level alignment and language-aware decoding within a unified encoder–decoder framework, enabling zero-shot transfer without introducing additional task-specific parameters or relying on handcrafted prompts.

Overall, from the literature it can be seen that that multilingual pretraining, alignment embedding, and language tagging are complementary to the achievement of robust translation between low-resource pairs. Nevertheless, a combined approach combining all three of the aspects transfer learning, alignment and target-aware decoding have not been studied thoroughly. This is precisely the gap which our proposed model is aimed at filling.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically examine the interaction between pivot-based transfer learning, cross-lingual embedding alignment, and language-aware decoding within a single multilingual encoder–decoder framework for zero-shot English–Assamese translation. While each of these components has been explored independently in prior multilingual and low-resource NMT research, their combined effect particularly in the context of an extremely low-resource Indic language without direct parallel data has not been empirically analyzed. By jointly integrating these mechanisms and validating their contributions through extensive ablation and human evaluation, this work provides new insights into how linguistic proximity and representation alignment can be effectively leveraged for robust zero-shot translation.

In this research, we introduce a language model for zero-shot English to Assamese translation based on a pivot language, Bengali. To create a shared semantic space for all three languages, we combine cross-lingual embedding alignment. Additionally, we also explore iterative back-translation to generate synthetic parallel data that improves over time. To mitigate off-target errors, we introduce an advanced language tagging mechanism, which adds explicit tokens and attention-based alignment. Language technologies are increasingly evaluated not only by intrinsic metrics but also by their real-world user impact30.

Background study

In this section, we present the key components that based our formulation for zero-shot English–Assamese neural machine translation. Our system is based on two important models: a multilingual sequence-to-sequence model, which leverages mBART; and a cross-lingual embedding alignment, in which we align sentences of English with Bengali and Assamese to map them onto a common semantic space. These are essential for properly leveraging transfer learning methods and zero-shot translation without English–Assamese parallel corpora.

mBART multilingual model

We choose the mBART31, a multilingual denoising autoencoder as the basis for our translation model. The mBART model is a Transformer-based architecture with twelve encoder and decoder layers, which are pre-trained on 25 different language pairs to learn cross-lingual representations. The encoder fully learns the source language sentence, and then the translation is fully decoded. The model leverages sentencePiece tokenization and a shared sub-word level vocabulary to increase parameter sharing and portability.

Cross-lingual embedding alignment

Lastly, to facilitate zero-shot transfer from English to Assamese, we align the embeddings of English, Bengali, and Assamese into a shared vector space by a process known as Procrustes-based alignment7 . We align embeddings in such a way that semantically relevant words across different languages must be closer to allow the model to generalize across different languages.

Given two embedding matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\text {en}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\text {bn}}$$\end{document} of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \times d$$\end{document} (for n shared word pairs and dimensionality d), we seek an orthogonal transformation matrix Q that minimizes the Frobenius norm:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q^* = \arg \min _Q \Vert E_{\text {en}} Q - E_{\text {bn}} \Vert _F^2.$$\end{document}

The optimal Q is obtained via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = U V^\top ,$$\end{document}where U and V arise from the SVD of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\text {en}}^\top E_{\text {bn}}$$\end{document}. The orthogonality constraint preserves relative distances within each language space, ensuring geometric consistency.

Since Assamese lacks extensive parallel data, direct alignment with English embeddings is infeasible. Instead, we exploit its linguistic proximity to Bengali. After aligning English and Bengali embeddings through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{\text {en-bn}}$$\end{document}, we extend this mapping to Assamese:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E'_{\text {as}} = E_{\text {as}} Q_{\text {en-bn}},$$\end{document}thus bringing all three languages into a unified embedding space.

This alignment enables semantically related words such as “river” (EN), “” (BN), and “” (AS) to occupy close positions in the shared vector space, forming the linguistic foundation for effective zero-shot translation.

Orthogonal Procrustes alignment is a stable and geometry-preserving mapping of embedding spaces but by design assumes that there exists linear relationship between languages. This assumption can be a limiting factor in capturing non-linear semantic shifts or morphologically rich constructions, especially for low-resource languages such as Assamese with compounding, inflectional variation and agglutinative behavior. More flexible cross-lingual alignment approaches have been recently investigated, where optimal transport-based methods are designed to minimize distributional divergence between embedding spaces that built upon Wasserstein distances. Such methods have demonstrated some success in characterizing non-linear relationships across languages. However, they usually rely on extensive bilingual lexicons/reliable cross-lingual distributional statistics which are currently not available for Assamese. Apparently, with such a high degree of data sparsity combined with the requirement for a stable alignment (while using only weak supervision), we adopted Procrustes-based alignment as a simple and robust computational means in this work.

Proposed methodology

This section described the proposed Zero-Shot English–Assamese Neural Machine Translation system, which utilized Bengali as a pivot language to support translation without direct English–Assamese parallel data. The novelty of this work lies in their unified integration into a single end-to-end framework tailored for zero-shot English–Assamese translation. The system leveraged various methodologies, including multilingual transfer learning using mBART, cross-lingual embedding alignment and OOV token dynamic handling, and language-aware decoding. Dataset summary

The dataset strategy was also constructed to confirm the main hypothesis of pivot-based zero-shot translation where the model learns the cross-lingual transfer patterns instead of learning them on large scale direct supervision. Two main datasets were used: (i) an English–Bengali parallel corpus of 50,182 pairs of sentences curated in various sources, such as FLORES-101 and OPUS to fine-tune on it, and (ii) a manually-curated English–Assamese evaluation corpus of 2147 sentence pairs of varying domains to test using zero-shot. The English–Assamese set of evaluation was sampled using publicly available Assamese news and Wikipedia content. A main sample of parallel sentence pairs was gathered by trustworthy sources, and the dataset was expanded with the help of a back-translation approach relying on an English–Assamese neural machine translation system (with the usage of the Google Translate API) to gain coverage in both domain and lexical terms. All sentence-pairs were retained in their original form and went through multiple quality-control steps. Furthermore, any sentence pairs with more than 250 subword tokens as well as of mixed script were discarded to provide clean Assamese Unicode text. Semantic consistency between source and target sentences was further verified through the use of cosine similarity between LaBSE embeddings.

We used various preprocessing steps to ensure that the data was highly filtered and relevant to the task. First, all sentences that exceeded 250 subword tokens were truncated. Second, lines containing a mix of scripts were filtered out, as were semantically similar pairs according to LaBSE embeddings for relevance scoring. The English–Bengali training corpus presented in Table spans four categories: news , Wikipedia, conversational, and technical texts, covering 42%, 28%, 15%, and 15% of the language spectrum, respectively, to provide more significant linguistic coverage for cross-lingual transfer. To ensure linguistic quality, the English–Assamese evaluation set underwent manual validation by three native Assamese speakers, who independently assessed translation adequacy and fluency. Substantial inter-annotator agreement was observed, with Fleiss’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.81$$\end{document}, indicating reliable quality control. Proposed framework

The framework worked in three key phases: (i) multilingual fine-tuning, in which mBART was trained using English–Bengali parallel data; ii) cross-lingual embedding alignment where Procrustes mapping aligned the representations of English, Bengali and Assamese; and (iii) zero-shot inference allowed direct English–Assamese translation conditioned on explicit (i.e., given as input) language tags. At inference, a source English sentence was tokenized, embedded and encoded at mBART’s encoder where the decoder, conditioned on the target-language tag lang:as, generates Assamese tokens autoregressively. During inference, the input English sentence was tokenized, embedded, and encoded by mBART’s encoder; the decoder, conditioned on the target-language tag \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\texttt {<}$$\end{document}lang:as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\texttt {>}$$\end{document}, generated Assamese tokens autoregressively. Cross-lingual embedding alignment is applied exclusively at the static input embedding level prior to Transformer encoding, while the mBART encoder–decoder architecture remains unchanged and learns contextual representations through self-attention. Before detailing the individual stages, we clarify the interaction between cross-lingual embedding alignment and the mBART model. Specifically, token embeddings for English, Bengali, and Assamese are mapped into a shared vector space using an orthogonal Procrustes transformation and then provided as inputs to the standard mBART encoder–decoder. Stepwise workflow of the proposed model

Step 1 (Preprocessing and tokenization). All corpora were normalized to Unicode NFC to ensure consistent encoding across scripts; character-level harmonization resolved orthographic variants (e.g., “ৰ” vs. “ৰ” “(r)”). Sentences were lowercased, diacritics preserved, and noisy entries removed. A joint entencePiece model (250k shared vocabulary) was trained on English–Bengali data and Assamese monolingual text to guarantee subword overlap. Source sentences were prefixed with language tags (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathtt {<2en>}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathtt {<2bn>}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathtt {<2as>}$$\end{document}) to guide training and inference.

Step 2 (Multilingual fine-tuning with mBART). The pretrained mBART-50 encoder–decoder was fine-tuned on English–Bengali parallel data by minimizing the negative log-likelihood:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}_{\text {trans}} = -\sum _{t=1}^{m} \log P(y_t^* \mid y_{<t}, X),$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_t^*$$\end{document} denoted the reference token and X the input sequence. This loss was optimized only during fine-tuning on English–Bengali parallel data and was not applied during zero-shot English–Assamese inference. To stabilize convergence, the first six encoder layers were frozen for two epochs and subsequently unfrozen. The Adam optimizer was used with a 2000-step warm-up followed by cosine decay, and early stopping was triggered by validation BLEU on a held-out English–Bengali split. This schedule encouraged adaptation to Bengali syntax while preserving cross-lingual generalization beneficial for Assamese. Fine-tuning multilingual pretrained models on pivot-language data is known to introduce potential risks such as catastrophic forgetting, where previously learned cross-lingual representations may be partially overwritten, as well as representational drift within shared multilingual spaces. In the context of zero-shot translation, such effects can undermine long-term generalization to unseen language pairs. To mitigate these risks, we employed partial encoder freezing during the initial training epochs together with a conservative learning-rate schedule, thereby encouraging the preservation of mBART’s multilingual priors while allowing higher layers to adapt to Bengali-specific syntactic and lexical patterns that are beneficial for Assamese transfer.

Step 3 (Cross-lingual embedding alignment). Static token embeddings were aligned across languages via orthogonal Procrustes mapping (as defined in Section ), learning \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{\text {en-bn}}$$\end{document} on English–Bengali embeddings and extending it to Assamese:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E'_{\text {as}} = E_{\text {as}} Q_{\text {en-bn}}.$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{\text {en-bn}}$$\end{document} was computed once using a bilingual English–Bengali seed lexicon and applied as a fixed orthogonal transformation to Assamese token embeddings; the alignment matrix was not updated during training and did not modify any mBART parameters. The mapping positioned semantically similar words (e.g., “river” / “নদি” (nodi) / “নদি” (nodi)) in proximity within a shared space, enabling zero-shot transfer. Step 4 (Handling OOV tokens). Rare or unseen words were segmented using Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) into frequent subword units. For truly novel tokens, embeddings were projected to the nearest known vectors using cosine similarity:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{\text {OOV}} = \arg \max _{e_i} \frac{E_{\text {aligned}}(e_{\text {OOV}}) \cdot E_{\text {aligned}}(e_i)}{\Vert E_{\text {aligned}}(e_{\text {OOV}})\Vert \, \Vert E_{\text {aligned}}(e_i)\Vert }.$$\end{document}

In practice, this nearest-neighbor substitution was applied only to rare tokens outside the shared subword vocabulary and was performed prior to decoding, ensuring compatibility with the fixed vocabulary of the mBART decoder. Dynamic vocabulary expansion periodically incorporated frequent Assamese subwords discovered during fine-tuning, improving robustness for domain-specific terms.

Step 5 (Zero-shot inference with language-aware decoding). At inference, each English input was prefixed with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathtt {<lang:as>}$$\end{document} and passed through the encoder–decoder. The aligned embeddings and shared multilingual representations guided the decoder to produce fluent Assamese without direct supervision. Decoding used beam search (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B{=}5$$\end{document}), length penalty (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha {=}1.2$$\end{document}), and temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau {=}0.9$$\end{document}). Early mixed Bengali–Assamese outputs were mitigated through tag conditioning and a restricted decoding vocabulary. The complete inference routine is summarized in Algorithm 1.

For the selected setup to be optimal and generalize stably across domains, extensive hyperparameter search was performed that encompassed six broad categories: architecture, training, regularization, embedding alignment, inference and cross-lingual-transfer. One-by-one tuning of each category was conducted with all other categories set to its default value for the purpose of isolating its contribution to model behavior. This investigation was designed to reveal what architectural and optimization decisions most mattered for translation quality and cross-lingual generalization. Several recurring trends surfaced during the search procedure. Among models included in the architecture group, mBART-50 offered the most well-founded trade-off between multilingual capacity and efficiency, and such a model was selected for this study (in particular when freezing early encoder layers during two initial epochs to ensure convergence). For training parameters, cosine learning-rate scheduling with 2000 warm-up steps and a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 10^{-5}$$\end{document} achieved smooth optimization without oscillations. Settings with dropout rate0.1 and label smoothing0.1 improved calibration, while reducing overfitting. For embedding alignment, Procrustes mapping with cosine similarity yielded the most coherent multilingual embedding space and nearest-neighbor replacement worked well for out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words. For decoding, a beam size of5 and length-penalty of 1.2 were found to provide the best trade-off between solution quality and efficiency during inference. Finally, in terms of target-language purity in zero-shot transfer, using Bengali as the pivot language with explicit language tags and partially shared embeddings produced the best results.

Results and analysis

This section comprises a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed zero-shot English–Assamese translation model with Bengali as an intermediate pivot employed for transfer learning. The evaluation was conducted based on both automatic metrics and human evaluations, complemented with diagnostic analyses to assess translation accuracy, fluency, and linguistic resilience. A test set of 2000 English–Assamese sentence pairs was prepared by sourcing extensive and varied sentences from English text to ensure wide linguistic coverage.

To address concerns regarding fairness of resource allocation, Table 5 additionally reports a direct English–Assamese supervision baseline trained on 50K parallel sentence pairs using the same mBART architecture (Baseline D). Despite access to substantially more parallel data, this capacity-matched baseline remains inferior to the proposed pivot-based zero-shot framework by 2.81 BLEU, highlighting the effectiveness of leveraging linguistic proximity and cross-lingual embedding alignment over increasing direct parallel supervision alone.

The automatic evaluation was conducted using four known metrics: BLEU32, SACREBLEU33, METEOR34, and Translation Edit Rate (TER)35. As illustrated in Table 5, our zero-shot model achieved a BLEU score of 28.65. The translation quality of the zero-shot method scored immense improvement over the baselines: +11.12 fiducial boost over direct English–Assamese application fine-tuning , +7.53 over multilingual mBART without linguistic tags , and +8.78 elevated than the conventional pipeline with Bengali pivot . Notably, the approach resulted in 91.7% BLEU fair score that would have been possible through complete direct supervision; this demonstrates the phenomenal zero-shot maximum potential of a model that has never been exposed to English–Assamese parallel data during the training process.

The performance gains are backed up by all other metrics: METEOR of 34.12, +10.67 over Baseline A application approach, and TER of 52.7% represented by − 8.7% absolute decrement. The substantial gap in performance between our model and Baseline B emphasizes the importance of explicit language labeling and alignment of cross-lingual embeddings. Moreover, an 8.78 BLAU lead over Baseline C indicates the significance of integrated learning over cascaded transformations that propagate errors and disrupt contextual coherence. Overall, the ablation trends illustrate crucial dependence between linguistic structure and cross-lingual transfer. Even though removing pre-training or layer depth, as well as regularization, did not cause dramatic performance decline, lack of mBART’s multilingual priors manifested considerably reduced contextual generalization, even with the specially designed pre-coded implicit alignment in the component analysis. Since regularization effect was relatively small, overfitting appears to be minor, considering the compelling diversity of the dataset. The patterns of the cross-lingual component analysis highlighted alignment and tagging as strongly synergic features; former improves the semantic mapping across languages, while tagging maintains implication fidelity for target language during decoding. Nevertheless, the ablation trends demonstrate that beam and length limitation, as well as some inference parameters, diminish their return over moderate values, suggesting that good decoding stability is achieved primarily though representation-level regularization and not search-space expansion. Therefore, informed by the component analysis and supported by ablations, we claim that linguistic proximity, embedding alignment, and explicit target conditioning are dominating factors for robust zero-shot translation performance.

Sentence-level error analysis

Beyond token-level language identification, we performed a qualitative sentence-level error analysis to gain insights into the character of remaining translation errors. Specifically, errors were classified into morphological and semantic ones, as these are the dominant failure modes in low-resource NMT of morphologically-rich language pairs like Assamese. The most frequently observed errors were morphological ones. These were things such as poor/faulty tense/aspect markers; missing or too simple-a-suffixes; subject-verb agreement going wrong in rare cases. The output in these examples was still often somewhat intelligible and semantically close to the source discourse but only at the surface-form level. For instance, the sentence “The river is wide” was a good semantic translation but had a morphological error of adjectival agreement whereas the errors on meaning level were more commonly found due to incorrect choice of predicate for longer sentences. Its rich inflectional morphology and the compound construction make it a typical for Assamese to be hard to model on low resource settings. Semantic bugs were much rarer, harder to resolve. These errors were given either category of an incomplete meaning shift, incorrect inappropriate predicate selection or omission as a secondary argument that upsets the phonological integrity causing skewed readings. Upon manual inspection it appeared that such errors mostly happened in longer or more complex sentences, especially when idiomatic expressions or abstract concepts were contained which have not been well represented in the pivot language data. This analysis indicates that the proposed model largely preserves sentence-level meaning, with remaining errors dominated by morphological realization rather than semantic misunderstanding. This finding complements the token-level confusion analysis, demonstrating that while language purity is effectively controlled, future improvements should focus on finer-grained morphological modeling rather than semantic alignment. While the token-level confusion matrix quantifies target-language purity, it does not capture the linguistic nature of translation errors at the sentence level. Discussion

The results indicate that linguistic proximity, explicit language tags and embedding alignment are the main contributors to developing an efficient zero-shot translation system for low-resource languages. In contrast with previous multilingual NMT works12,13,21, our approach obtains comparable or better BLEU score while keeping the target languages purely separated. Our ablation study shows that cross-lingual embedding alignment and tag conditioning work synergistically allowing for stable decoding, without direct supervision.

Notably, the framework generalizes over different domains including informal and code-mixed text from social media and shows that the shared semantic space learns these contextual and morphological variations between Bengali and Assamese well. In addition, the fact this domain robustness exists reinforces that our model is not merely driven by syntactic news but also utilizes the semantic transferability provided through shared lexical embeddings. Unlike the standard pivot systems, in which translation errors can easily be propagated, integration of alignment within a single encoder–decoder architecture permits the system to resynthesize meaning even for idiomatic or only partly code-mixed input.

Ultimately, another very interesting observation is that the model is robust to off-target translations, which has been a huge pain in previous multilingual zero-shot settings. The Assamese output has token-level accuracy of 97.3% which indicates that the Procrustes-aligned embeddings along with explicit language tags can efficiently constraint the decoder’s generative space. This provides a useful direction to generalizing interpretability approaches, e.g., the attention visualization or attribution mapping, to also investigate how linguistic separation is learned by the model during training.

The fact that Bengali has been relatively successful as a pivot language suggests that choosing an optimal pivot is not solely dependent on the large genealogical distance. Although Bengali and Hindi are both members of the Indo-Aryan language family, with Bengal having higher lexical overlap, closer phonetic realizations and stronger orthography associations with Assamese to better align cross-lingual embeddings. In contrast, Hindi exhibits higher divergence in surface vocabulary usage and distributional statistics, resulting in less optimal representation transfer when used as an intermediate pivot. These reasons explain the drop in performance we saw when using Hindi as a pivot in our ablation study. More generally, this study illustrates that pivot selection is inescapably biased and should focus on specifics of the linguistic and representational fit instead of relying solely on membership in language families. From a methodological viewpoint, in addition to the model assumptions they rely on, the proposed approach implies several modelling choices that need to be addressed. Specifically, orthogonal Procrustes alignment is based on the assumption that there is an approximately linear isomorphism between two embedding spaces of different languages. While this assumption has been found effective for closely related languages, it may at times not suffice to account for complex non-linear semantic shifts or in light of rich, morphologically rich phenomena like agglutinative constructions and compounding strategies in Assamese Recent alignment models are increasing in expressivity, with recent work such as those based on optimal transport for learning correspondences between complex non-linear relations being proposed to extend the range of relation that can be accommodated; however most methods rely on richer bilingual lexicons or very high quality distributional statistics, all of which are not available for Assamese at this stage. Then for the extremely lowlearning resource case, a linear Procrustes alignment model serves as rational tradeoff between theoretical flexibility and robustness of the alignment. Another potential limitation concerns representation stability in zero-shot transfer settings. Fine-tuning multilingual models can, in principle, lead to catastrophic forgetting or representational drift, undermining generalization to unseen language pairs. In our framework, this risk is mitigated by freezing early encoder layers during initial fine-tuning, restricting embedding alignment to static input representations, and preserving the core mBART encoder–decoder parameters. While these design choices promote stability, they do not fully eliminate the possibility of long-term drift under continued adaptation. Future work could explore adapter-based isolation or continual learning strategies to further safeguard multilingual representations during incremental training.

Despite the strong performance of the proposed framework, several limitations remain and point to promising directions for future work. However, some residual issues persist especially for out-of-domain terms and morphologically complex sentences-indicating potential benefits of combining the approach with contextualized post-editing or character-level modeling. For example, the introduction of character-level or syllable-level representations may contribute to robustness in low-agglutinative morphological formations as well as compound constructions that are common in Assamese morphology. In addition, the use of bilingual lexicons or domain-adaptive fine-tuning may further refine terminological consistency in specialized domains such as healthcare and legal text.

Conclusion

We in this work presented a novel zero-shot English–Assamese neural machine translation framework that strategically uses Bengali as a pivot language. This study proposed mBART-inspired multilingual transfer learning, Procrustes-transformed cross-lingual embedding alignment, and explicit language-aware decoding, through which we can effectively span the translation gap between English and Assamese without any direct supervision. Overall, the proposed model achieves a BLEU score of 28.65 and makes considerable improvements of 7.53–11.12 points over baseline, achieving 91.7% of the performance limit achieved by full direct supervision. Comprehensive evaluation in terms of automatic metrics, human assessment, domain sensitivity, and ablation study confirmed the stability of the proposed approach. Irrespective of the mode of text, the model performs well, but for challenging social media text types, the model benefits more by up to 77.6%. According to human evaluation results from two native Assamese linguistics, the model is very adequate , and is very fluent , practically resulting in a 8.4% reduce in the error. Ablation studies show that the resulting embedding alignment and language-aware tagging are the most important. They result in 3.84 and 6.11 better in BLEU, and second, the close linguistic distance between Bengali and Assamese yields 5.20 BLEU. The practical utility of our framework is further evidenced by its 26.9% faster inference speed compared to baseline approaches, making it suitable for real-world deployment. This work establishes that strategic pivot selection based on linguistic proximity, combined with careful cross-lingual representation learning, can effectively enable zero-shot translation for low-resource languages.

Even though this study showed promising results, there are some limitations. The evaluation was limited to English–Assamese translation, and performance on other low-resource Indic pairs may differ based on the linguistic distance, as well as, existing corpora. Moreover, for languages that do not share the script or have akin morphology, pivot-based alignment will need further tuning like character-level alignment or joint pretraining. Lastly, although this computation has been nicely optimized for a fast inference phase, mass deployments at scale on edge or mobile devices can also take advantage of additional model compression or quantization. In the future, we plan to expand this work to other low-resource language pairs in the Indo Aryan and Tibeto-Burman families, study dynamic vocabulary adaptation methods and advanced cross-lingual alignment operations that better model morphological and syntactic relations between cognate languages.
