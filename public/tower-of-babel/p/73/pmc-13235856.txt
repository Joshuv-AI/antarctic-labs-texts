Improved agreement between in-hospital and time-delimited mortality by including hospice discharges
Authors: Anica C Law, Nicholas A Bosch, Yang Song, Archana P Tale, Jason Nelson, Rishi K Wadhera, Amber E Barnato, Allan J Walkey
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Time-delimited mortality (ie, death within a defined time frame) is an unbiased outcome measure of critical interest to researchers, policymakers, and hospital administrators. However, identifying deaths occurring after hospital discharge requires linkage to national death indices (eg, Social Security data), which is often unavailable in inpatient databases. When time-delimited mortality is unavailable, in-hospital mortality—an “event-delimited” outcome—is often used instead. For instance, New York mandates that hospitals publicly report their in-hospital mortality for sepsis.1,2 However, in-hospital mortality rate is known to be biased by hospital discharge practices, as transferring patients to other sites shortly before death shifts mortality attribution away from the index hospitalization.3,4 Recent work in Michigan found that, among patients with sepsis, a composite outcome combining in-hospital mortality and discharge to hospice (“mortality/hospice”) better approximates 30-day mortality than in-hospital mortality alone.5 It remains unclear, however, (a) whether this improved concordance generalizes beyond Michigan and sepsis to critically ill patients nationwide, (b) whether concordance is similar for longer mortality horizons (eg, 60-day mortality), and (c) how these relationships have evolved in the United States over time. Filling these knowledge gaps is particularly important among critically ill patients, who are especially vulnerable to discharge bias4 due to high short-term mortality6 and frequent rates of transfers to other sites (hospice7 and long-term care hospitals8,9) prior to death. Therefore, among critically ill Medicare beneficiaries nationwide, we assessed composite mortality/hospice and in-hospital mortality outcomes to (1) compare their relative performance in approximating 30-day and 60-day mortality and (2) examine how concordance between each outcome and 30-day mortality has changed from 2011 to 2023.

Methods

Using the 100% Medicare Provider Analysis and Review File and the Master Beneficiary Summary File, we identified patients aged >65 years enrolled in fee-for-service Medicare for ≥1 year who were admitted to an intensive care unit (ICU) between 2011 and 2023, as described in a previously published study.7 Primary outcomes included 2 event-delimited outcomes (composite mortality/hospice and in-hospital mortality) and 2 time-delimited mortality metrics (30-day and 60-day mortality). To assess agreement between each event-delimited metric and each “gold standard” time-delimited mortality, we estimated accuracy metrics (primary: Cohen’s κ; secondary: sensitivity, specificity). Cohen’s κ measures agreement beyond chance, with <0.0 indicating no agreement/disagreement and >0.8 indicating almost perfect agreement.10 Given rising rates of discharge to hospice after critical illness over the past decade,7,11 we estimated annual κ for each event-delimited outcome vs 30-day mortality and assessed temporal trends with Spearman rank correlation tests. Analyses were 2-tailed (α = 0.05). This study was deemed exempt from review by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (#2022P000105) and Boston University Medical Center Institutional Review Boards (#H-41346).

Results

Between 2011 and 2023, 10 647 283 Medicare beneficiaries (mean [SD] age, 77.4 [8.4] years; 5 223 550 [49%] female) were admitted to an ICU; cohort characteristics have been previously reported.7 Among these, 1 716 620 (16.1%) died in-hospital and 619 827 (5.8%) were discharged to hospice; 2 567 721 (24.1%) died within 30 days and 3 046 155 (28.6%) died within 60 days of admission.

There was consistently better agreement between time-delimited mortality (30- and 60-day mortality) and mortality/hospice than with in-hospital mortality alone, without overlapping confidence intervals for Cohen’s κ (Table 1). For example, for 30-day mortality, κ increased from 0.72 for in-hospital mortality to 0.84 for mortality/hospice (ie, “almost perfect” agreement10). For both time-delimited mortality metrics, the sensitivity of mortality/hospice was higher than that of in-hospital mortality (eg, sensitivity for 30-day mortality: 83.7% vs 64.9%), with minimal loss in specificity (eg, specificity for 30-day mortality: 97.7% vs 99.4%). Finally, from 2011 to 2023, κ for mortality/hospice vs 30-day mortality increased (0.82 to 0.86, P for trend < .001; Figure 1), while κ for in-hospital mortality remained statistically unchanged over time (0.72 to 0.74, P for trend = .10).

Discussion

In this national cohort of patients with critical illness, combining in-hospital mortality with discharge to hospice better approximated both 30- and 60-day mortality than in-hospital mortality alone. Agreement between the composite outcome and 30-day mortality increased from 2011 to 2023, underscoring the importance of reassessing the performance of outcome measures as care programs (eg, general inpatient hospice), healthy policy, and discharge to hospice practices evolve.12 Our findings support the use of mortality/hospice as a pragmatic proxy for short-term mortality when time-delimited mortality data are unavailable for patients with critical illness, including by researchers using large multicenter electronic health records databases, policymakers designing pay-for-performance hospital measures, and administrators seeking to benchmark practices.

Our findings expand upon recent work by Prescott et al5 examining concordance of mortality/hospice with 30-day mortality among patients in Michigan with sepsis. Importantly, our findings demonstrate that (1) the composite outcome’s superior concordance generalizes nationally among older critically ill adults, (2) concordance improves with longer follow-up windows, and (3) as postacute discharge to hospice has increased, the value of our composite outcome to approximate 30-day mortality has increased over time.

Our study has limitations. First, findings among Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries admitted to an ICU may not be generalizable to other populations with different short-term mortality risks. Second, as thresholds for admission to an ICU vary widely between hospitals, cohort inclusion by admission to an ICU can result in inclusion of patients with a wide range of acute severity of illness. Third, elevated in-hospital mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic may have inflated agreement (κ) between in-hospital and time-delimited mortality (biasing our comparison of mortality/hospice and in-hospital mortality agreement toward the null). Finally, a composite outcome of in-hospital mortality and discharge to hospice may not be appropriate for all studies. Specifically, discharge to hospice may be viewed as a good outcome (ie, high-quality end-of-life treatment) in some circumstances and, as such, may deserve to be accounted for separately from the pool of “adverse” outcomes of in-hospital mortality.

Conclusion

When time-delimited mortality is unavailable for patients with critical illness, a composite outcome of discharge to hospice with in-hospital mortality should be considered a better pragmatic proxy for time-delimited mortality than in-hospital mortality alone.
