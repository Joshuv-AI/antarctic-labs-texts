Predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitors therapy in lung cancer
Authors: Jie Yao, Xuwen Lin, Xin Zhang, Mei Xie, Xidong Ma, Xinyu Bao, Jialin Song, Yiran Liang, Qiqi Wang, Xinying Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have changed the treatment mode of lung cancer, extending the survival time of patients unprecedentedly. Once patients respond to ICIs, the median duration of response is usually longer than that achieved with cytotoxic or targeted drugs. Unfortunately, there is still a large proportion of lung cancer patients do not respond to ICI. Effective biomarkers are crucial for identifying lung cancer patients who can benefit from them. The first predictive biomarker is programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), but its predictive value is limited to specific populations. With the development of single-cell sequencing and spatial imaging technologies, as well as the use of deep learning and artificial intelligence, the identification of predictive biomarkers has been greatly expanded. In this review, we will dissect the biomarkers used to predict ICIs efficacy in lung cancer from the tumor-immune microenvironment and host perspectives, and describe cutting-edge technologies to further identify biomarkers.KEYWORDS: Predictive biomarkers, lung cancer, immune checkpoint inhibitors, TME, PD-L1

Background

As one of the deadliest cancers, lung cancer is highly heterogeneous, with frenzied growth of cancer cells and concurrent alterations in the immune microenvironment. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitors, have shown encouraging efficacy in the treatment of various malignant tumors. By regulating the interaction between T cells and antigen-presenting cells or tumor cells, they contribute to the release of repressed immune responses. Compared with conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, ICIs have fewer systemic side effects and longer-lasting effectiveness. In addition, they have broader applicability than targeted therapies and are not limited to tumors with certain mutations.

Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated that ICIs can improve survival and antitumor response in lung cancer as monotherapy or combination therapy.1–3 In addition, some ICIs have been used in adjuvant and neoadjuvant therapy.4–6 Unfortunately, clinical outcomes are not always favorable. Resistance is an inevitable problem. Primary resistance means that the patient does not initially respond to immunotherapy. Although some patients may initially respond to ICIs, they eventually develop resistance, known as acquired resistance. The mechanisms of primary or acquired resistance overlap to some extent. In the CheckMate032 randomized cohort trial, the objective response rate (ORR) was 12% for nivolumab monotherapy and 21% for nivolumab plus ipilimumab, which means that more than half of the patients did not respond.7 At present, the reason for this situation is not yet well understood. In addition, there is no consensus on which biomarkers perfectly predict response to ICIs therapy. Therefore, there is great interest in identifying predictive biomarkers for ICIs to choose appropriate therapies and avoid unnecessary treatment-related adverse events. Although PD-L1 expression and tumor mutation burden (TMB) have been widely used to predict clinical outcomes, neither is perfect. The complex interactions between the tumor microenvironment (TME) and host immunity make it crucial to identify accurate and sensitive biomarkers.

This review provides a comparatively thorough description of predictive biomarkers for ICIs in lung cancer, broadly classifying them into three categories: tumor-related biomarkers, host-related biomarkers, and peripheral blood biomarkers (Figure 1). In addition, we discuss emerging cutting-edge technologies for identifying biomarkers in recent years.

Tumor-related biomarkers

PD-L1 IHC

PD-L1 expression measured by IHC has been widely used in the clinic. Many clinical trials have demonstrated that patients with PD-L1-positive tumors benefit more from ICIs than patients with PD-L1-negative tumors. However, there are many different definitions of the PD-L1 “positive” cutoffs. The Phase III PACIFIC trial evaluated durvalumab in patients with unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) after concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Durvalumab showed superior overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with placebo in all PD-L1 subgroups, except in patients whose tumor cells expressed PD-L1 less than 1%.8 In IMpower110 clinical trials, Atezolizumab significantly extended OS among NSCLC patients with high PD-L1 expression (≥50% of tumor cells or ≥ 10% of tumor-infiltrating immune cells).9 Moreover, due to the poor efficacy of immunotherapy in lung cancer patients with EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements, a number of clinical trials have excluded these patients regardless of PD-L1 expression.

Beyond that, several issues limit the predictive performance of PD-L1. First, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved ICIs have corresponding antibodies to assess PD-L1 expression with different measurement criteria. The spatial and temporal heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression has always been a controversial topic. The inconsistent of PD-L1 expression between primary and metastatic tumors was 22%,10 and between preoperative biopsies and corresponding surgical resection samples was 48%.11 Biopsy samples grossly underestimated PD-L1 expression. Archived specimens also affected PD-L1 detection and decreased the predictive value for OS compared with fresh specimens.12 PD-L1 levels also depend on histologic subtype. Although CheckMate032 found that nivolumab monotherapy or in combination with ipilimumab showed durable antitumor activity and manageable safety in small cell lung cancer (SCLC). However, PD-L1 expression was generally lower in SCLC compared to NSCLC, with 98% of SCLC patients having PD-L1 expression < 5%.7 There are studies using tumor expression data to identify SCLC subtypes to identify subtypes that benefit from immunotherapy.13

In conclusion, PD-L1 expression is a useful biomarker for selecting patients vulnerable to benefit from ICIs. However, its predictive performance is not perfect and it should be used as a crude predictive biomarker. Therefore, it is necessary to find a more sensitive biomarker or combine it with PD-L1 to improve predictive performance.

Mutation and genome instability

Tissue TMB

Tumor-specific somatic mutations can generate neoantigens, which are presented via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules to trigger specific T cells. Tissue TMB (tTMB) indirectly reflects the ability of neoantigen production by tumors and is another potential predictor of ICIs response. CheckMate026 clinical trial compared first-line nivolumab with chemotherapy in patients with untreated stage IV or recurrent NSCLC.14 When PD-L1 expression levels were at or above 5%, OS was similar between groups. However, when exploring the predictive performance of tTMB, the researchers found that patients with high tTMB (≥243 mutations) had prolonged PFS and improved ORR. In the pembrolizumab monotherapy first-line KEYNOTE-04215 and second-line KEYNOTE-01016 trials, higher tTMB (≥175 mutations/exome) was associated with improved outcomes. Table 1 summarizes other studies evaluating tTMB as a predictive biomarker for ICIs in lung cancer. Of interest, the predictive ability of tTMB disappeared in the randomized trials of pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy in KEYNOTE-021,33 KEYNOTE-189,34 and KEYNOTE-407.35 This suggests that tTMB may be a predictive biomarker for ICIs monotherapy, whereas tTMB has a limited predictive role when ICIs plus chemotherapy.

In conclusion, there are many practical issues when considering tTMB as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy. First, obtaining sufficient tumor tissue in advanced cancer patients is challenging, and tTMB is also affected by tumor heterogeneity. In addition, the optimal definition and quantification criteria for tTMB are unclear. There is no consensus on which mutations should be included, with some studies using all mutations and others using only nonsynonymous mutations or mutations that alter the protein sequence.36 Currently, the tTMB gives equal weight to each mutation, but numerous studies have demonstrated that some mutations produce antigens that are more readily recognized by the immune system, i.e., are of higher “quality,” and are more likely to induce an anti-tumor immune response.37,38 Finally, traditional whole exome sequencing (WES) to assess tTMB is the most accurate way, but the high cost limits its widespread use, so next-generation sequencing (NGS) is used in the clinic. However, differences in technology platforms have hindered comparisons among them. Similar to PD-L1, tTMB is not sufficiently perfect to predict ICIs outcomes in lung cancer.

MSI-H/dMMR

Mismatch repair (MMR) is a DNA damage repair mechanism for base mismatches, insertions or deletions that may occur during DNA replication or recombination, and is crucial for maintaining genome stability.39 Microsatellites are DNA sequences in the genome that repeat in tandem in a few nucleotides (mainly 1–6). Deficient MMR (dMMR) can lead to the mutations accumulation and neoantigens generation, especially in the microsatellite regions, resulting in high microsatellite instability (MSI-high, MSI-H).40 In 2017, pembrolizumab was approved for patients with unresectable or metastatic MSI-H/dMMR solid tumors, regardless of histology. MSI-H/dMMR is the first FDA-approved pan-cancer biomarker used to screen immunotherapy beneficiary populations. Multiple analyses used targeted NGS to quantify the prevalence of MSI-H in lung cancer.41–43 Yang and colleagues evaluated MSI in more than 5,000 patients with NSCLC and 300 patients with SCLC and found that the prevalence of MSI-H in SCLC was 1.9% compared to 0.58% in NSCLC. This study attempts to explore the clinical response of these patients receiving ICIs. They found that although clinical benefits were observed in a small subset of MSI-H NSCLC patients, this was not significantly different from the overall clinical benefits.44 Studies have explored the relationship between MSI-H and TMB. In an analysis of 1868 NSCLC patients, 14.1% (264/1868) had high TMB, but only 0.6% (12/1868) had MSI-H. There seems to be a difference in the biological significance between TMB and MSI-H.45 Combining these markers may improve the precision of selecting patients who may benefit from ICIs.

Tumor genotype

In addition to the classical biomarkers such as PD-L1 and TMB mentioned above, studies have shown that various mutations involved in tumorigenesis and progression may also have complex effects. Mazieres et al. conducted a retrospective study for patients with advanced NSCLC and oncogenic driver alterations treated with ICI monotherapy. In subgroup analysis, patients with KRAS and MET mutations had the best therapeutic outcome, while patients with RET, EGFR, and ALK mutations had the worst PFS performance.46 Zhang et al. found a significant correlation between NOTCH1/2/3 mutations and ICIs therapy rather than chemotherapy in the EGFR/ALK WT population of NSCLC, confirming del-NOTCH mut as a potential indicator of ICIs favorable response. This finding reveals the possibility of adding NOTCH inhibitors to ICIs for NSCLC patients to optimize treatment.47 A growing number of studies have confirmed that co-mutations in specific genes can affect the TME. For example, co-alterations of the NOTCH and DNA damage response (DDR) pathways could serve as novel predictors of immunotherapy in NSCLC.48 In addition, KRAS-positive tumors responded better to ICIs, positively correlating with CD8+ T cell infiltration, PD-L1 expression, and TMB.49–51 However, KRAS mutations are often accompanied with other co-mutations, resulting in different molecular profiles and outcomes. Immunotherapy was better in patients with co-mutations in KRAS and TP53, and poorer in patients with co-mutations in KRAS and STK11/LKB1, KEAP1/NFE2L2, and SMARCA4.52–55 Although the influence of tumor genotype in response or resistance to ICIs remains to be elucidated. However, in a retrospective clinical trial, combination chemotherapy with ICIs and anti-angiogenic agents improved survival in patients with non-squamous NSCLC, regardless of their EGFR or ALK genetic alteration status.56 Although prospective validation is needed, this finding suggests the possibility of extending ICIs to patients with driver mutations.

Tumor microenvironment

T-Lymphocytes and T cell receptor

In the 19th century, Stephen Paget proposed the “seed and soil” hypothesis. He likened cancer cells to “seeds” and the environment suitable for cancer cell growth to “fertile soil,” which is now described as the TME. The composition of TME includes both noncellular and cellular components, the former including blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and cytokines, and the latter including various immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and so on. Over the past few decades, scientists have dramatically increased their understanding of TME, with its various components that can either enhance or suppress the immune response.

Immune cells play a key role in the antitumor response and are broadly divided into cells involved in innate immunity and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes responsible for adaptive immunity. The density of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) is a key factor influencing the effectiveness of ICIs.57 A study that divided advanced NSCLC patients treated with ICIs into four groups based on TILs status and PD-L1 expression confirmed that both can help determine which patients may benefit.58 The level of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocyte infiltration also predicted the prognosis of ICIs.59 The response rate to PD-1 blockade in metastatic NSCLC patients was only 13.3% when the CD8+/CD4+ ratio was below 2, compared to 43% to 50% when the ratio was above 2. The cells subpopulation in the TME called tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) has received much attention recently. Trm cells express various checkpoint receptors, such as PD-1, CTLA-4, and Tim-3. After three cycles of neoadjuvant therapy in a patient with multiple early-stage lung cancer, increased Trm infiltration was observed in responding nodules, which may be a unique marker of early immune response to PD-1 blockade therapy.60 In addition, blocking PD-1 on Trm cells promotes cytolytic activity toward autologous tumor cells.61

In addition to the level of T cell infiltration, T cell receptors (TCR) have also been reported to be involved in ICIs response. Rossi et al. found that patients with high PD-1+CD8+TCR diversity prior to treatment showed better responses to ICIs, and patients with increased clonality after treatment had longer PFS than those with decreased clonality.62 In neoadjuvant immunotherapy, NSCLC exhibiting major pathologic response (MPR) showed higher TCR clonality and some clones were not detected before therapy, suggesting that ICIs may have induced the expansion of specific T cell clones.63 In conclusion, TCR diversity and clonality are potential predictors of ICIs therapy.

Tertiary lymphoid structures and B-Lymphocytes

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are ectopic lymphoid organs that develop in non-lymphoid tissues at sites of chronic inflammation, including tumors. Studies on the relationship between TLSs and clinical benefit in cancer patients suggest that TLSs are promising predictive markers for treatment with ICIs. TLSs in tumors are in different states, with mature TLSs forming germinal center. In a large-scale retrospective analysis of cancer patients treated with anti-PD1/PD-L1 antibodies, the investigators found that mature TLSs were associated with improved ORR, PFS, and OS independent of PD-L1 expression and CD8+ T cell density.64 In neoadjuvant therapy for lung cancer, both the maturation and abundance of TLSs were significantly associated with MPR.65 In addition to being predictive markers, TLSs promote lymphocyte infiltration and tumor antigen activation. As a result, a range of methods are being developed to induce TLSs formation, including the use of chemokines, cytokines or antibodies.66,67 In this context, TLSs induction in combination with ICIs provides new ideas for cancer therapy.

The function of B cells within TLSs is highly dependent on the degree of maturation of TLSs. In immature TLSs, B cells may be immunosuppressive whereas those in mature TLSs can undergo maturation, selection, amplification, somatic hypermutation and affinity maturation to acquire powerful anti-tumor effects.68 Bulk-RNA sequencing showed that B-cell associated markers were most differentially expressed between immunotherapy responders and non-responders.69 Intratumoral B cells, especially plasma cells, was predictive of OS in NSCLC patients treated with atezolizumab, but not chemotherapy.70

TLSs and B cells are important participants in the anti-tumor immune response. Currently, laboratories mainly detect the TLSs by hematoxylin and eosin staining. Multiplex immunohistochemistry and/or multiplex immunofluorescence assays were also used to detect maturation of TLSs by combination of CD21 and CD23 markers.71 In addition, a deeper understanding of TLSs at the single-cell and spatial levels will facilitate the clinical application of ICIs. For example, CD20+CD22+ADAM28+ B cells density predicted response to ICIs in NSCLC patients.72 Although the process of TLS formation and the exact mechanisms that predict ICI response are unknown, the available findings strongly support the view that TLSs and B cells are alternative markers of an active immune response.

Other TME components

TME also contains a series of cells involved in tumor immunity. CAFs influence other immune components through secreting various cytokines and chemokines, exerting immunosuppressive functions and causing tumor cells to evade immune surveillance.73 Available data strongly support that accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) contributes to immune tolerance and is associated with poor prognosis in lung cancer. Polymorphonuclear-MDSC (PMN-MDSC) proliferation and recruitment were significantly higher in non-responders than in responders after anti-PD-1 therapy, which may impair the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy.74 MDSCs can continue to differentiate into tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) in the TME. In NSCLC samples, low CD163+ TAM cell infiltration level was associated with longer PFS and OS under ICIs therapy.75 Natural killer (NK) cells can induce an antigen-independent immune response against malignant cells.76 And a subset of PD-1 expressing CD56bright NK cells was used to identify patients in lung cancer who responded well to ICIs.77 In addition, the understanding of neutrophil in TME is increasing. Neutrophils are recognized as immunosuppressive factors, and their role as inflammatory biomarkers predicting the effectiveness of ICIs will also be discussed in Part 3.

Host-related biomarkers

Microbiome

The microbiota is considered a crucial element involved in tumor immunity, and several studies have demonstrated that gut microbial diversity in NSCLC patients correlates with responses to ICIs.78,79 Antibiotics are known to alter the composition of gut microbiota, leading to ecological dysbiosis, which may affect the effectiveness of ICIs. Derosa et al. reported that the use of antibiotics within 30 days before the first dose of anti-PD-(L)1 in advanced NSCLC patients can reduce the efficacy of anti-PD-(L)1.80 Although it is uncertain how the microbiome affects the immune response, microbiota-derived metabolites play an important role in immunomodulation. For example, Mager et al. found that B. pseudolongum could enhance the immunotherapy response via the metabolite inosine.81

In addition to the gut, flora and metabolites in the blood and respiratory system are also potential predictors of response to ICIs. A recent study has shown that the measurement of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the exhaled breath of lung cancer patients using an electronic nose can identify responders and non-responders in ICIs. The association between VOCs and response assessment may be due directly to metabolites produced by tumor cells and/or immune cells/inflammatory hosts.82 Hatae et al. found that specific plasma metabolites in NSCLC patients before and after nivolumab treatment were highly predictive of clinical response.83 Future studies need to determine the composition of these microorganisms and the sources of their metabolites.

Other host-related biomarkers

There appears to be gender difference in the efficacy of ICIs in lung cancer patients. Surgically resected lung adenocarcinomas from female patients had a higher infiltration of tumor-associated immune cells than those from male patients.84 Several studies have shown that male patients have greater advantages in ICIs monotherapy, while female patients show more clinical benefit in ICIs plus chemotherapy.85,86 One hypothesis to explain this finding is that many genes on the X chromosome regulate the immune response (e.g., Toll-like receptor, cytokine receptor IL2RG, and transcription factor FOXP3).87,88 In addition, some studies have considered the impact of other factors such as age, weight, and human leukocyte antigen genotype on the efficacy of ICIs in lung cancer.89–92

Peripheral blood biomarkers

Currently, biomarkers are mainly based on tissue and no circulating biomarkers have been approved by the FDA to predict immunotherapy response. However, the availability of tumor tissue is limited, especially in patients with advanced cancer. Liquid biopsies are safer and more readily available than invasive tissue biopsies. By analyzing biomarkers in peripheral blood, we can assess immune status and thus predict the response of patients to ICIs.

Circulating PD-1 and PD-L1

PD-1 and PD-L1 are not only present on the cell surface but their soluble forms are also detected in the circulation of healthy individuals and cancer patients. Recent studies have attempted to elucidate the role of soluble PD-1 (sPD-1) and soluble PD-L1(sPD-L1) in ICIs treatment of lung cancer (Table 2). Study shows that NSCLC patients have higher sPD-1 and sPD-L1 than healthy controls.93 High sPD-L1 levels significantly predicted unfavorable PFS and OS in NSCLC patients treated with ICIs monotherapy, but had no predictive significance in ICIs plus chemotherapy.94 Even in patients with high cell surface membrane-bound PD-L1 (mPD-L1) expression, PFS and OS were shorter in patients with high sPD-L1 than in those with low sPD-L1. A study analyzed sPD-L1 concentrations at diagnosis and after treatment in patients treated with ICIs. The results showed that lower sPD-L1 levels after treatment were significantly associated with tumor regression, helping to identify responders at an earlier timepoint.95

The origin of sPD-1 and sPD-L1 and their role in tumor immunity have not been fully elaborated. It has been reported that sPD-1 may retain the ability to bind to mPD-L1, preventing it from binding to natural ligand PD-1, and thus enhancing the antitumor response. Thus, patients with high sPD-1 are more likely to benefit from treatment. In addition, sPD-L1 binds to membrane-bound PD-1 (mPD-1) to induce lymphocyte exhaustion, which would contribute to immune evasion of the tumor. sPD-L1 also competes with anti-PD-1 antibodies for mPD-1 on lymphocytes, which would reduce drug efficacy.96 Taken together, the results suggest that sPD-1 and sPD-L1 have ability to regulate immune homeostasis.

Platelets expressing PD-L1 were also detected in the peripheral blood of NSCLC patients, and platelet PD-L1 inhibits CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. In contrast to IHC-based PD-L1, platelet PD-L1 is thought to be superior in predicting responses to ICIs.97 In addition, exosomal PD-L1 is a new tool for the assessment of immunotherapy efficacy in lung cancer.98 In conclusion, A growing amount of research has shown that circulating PD-1 and PD-L1 can be used as biomarkers to predict ICI outcome and survival prognosis in cancer patients.

ctDNA and bTMB

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a component of circulating free DNA, which is shed by tumor cells undergoing apoptosis or necrosis. Avanzini et al. revealed a relationship between ctDNA and tumor evolution, determining the potential of ctDNA for cancer detection.99 In addition, the half-life of ctDNA is short (2 hours), allowing real-time detection of tumor cell changes in vivo, making it an ideal molecular biomarker for monitoring therapy response.100 In patients with metastatic NSCLC treated with ICI, a decrease in ctDNA level predicts prolonged survival. Surprisingly, ctDNA response precedes radiographic response and may provide earlier monitoring of efficacy.101 Another potential biomarker for is blood-based TMB (bTMB), which is the number of mutations per megabase (muts/Mb) found in the ctDNA sequencing region. Two studies consistently confirmed that high bTMB is associated with improved PFS in metastatic NSCLC patients receiving ICIs-based therapy.102,103 Although current studies provide some evidence for ctDNA and bTMB to predict the clinical benefit of ICIs, most of these are small-sample retrospective studies. The findings need to be validated in larger prospective studies to ensure their validity and reliability.

Inflammatory biomarker

Inflammation reflects immune resistance in cancer patients. Therefore, inflammatory response related biomarkers may provide clinical guidance for ICIs application. Over the past few years, several studies have demonstrated the predictive performance of early C-reactive protein, neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-lymphocyte ratio for checkpoint inhibitor efficacy in NSCLC.104–106 In addition, large retrospective analyses have specifically assessed the strong association between persistently high derived NLR (dNLR, neutrophils/[leucocytes-neutrophils]) and early ICIs failure.107 Other routine blood biomarkers such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) have also been demonstrated to be inversely connected to ICIs response and may even trigger excessive disease progression.108 These indicators are dynamic over the course of treatment, and their longitudinal evaluation may provide predicting efficacy. Combining dNLR and LDH, Mezquita et al. developed a new blood-based biomarker called the Lung Immune Prognostic Index (LIPI).109 Pre-treatment LIPI was associated with clinical outcomes in lung cancer patients treated with ICIs, suggesting that LIPI could serve as biomarker for identifying patients with potential ICIs benefit.110–112

Cytokines and chemokines

Cytokines are important regulators of immune activity and are involved in intercellular communication, immune regulation and inflammatory response. In the era of immunotherapy, a range of studies have focused on this. IL-8 is a member of the CXC chemokine family. Sanmamed et al. reported that in NSCLC patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy, serum IL-8 levels significantly decreased between baseline and best response and increased at progression.114 Another study came to the consistent conclusion that circulating IL-8 levels increase at time of progression and are associated with poor OS.115 In contrast, Boutsikou et al. concluded that elevated IL-8 levels resulted in better responses and longer survival in NSCLC patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy.113 In summary, the relationship between IL-8 levels and the clinic benefit of ICIs is controversial. The function of the cytokine INFγ also needs to be further verified. Several studies have reported that increased levels of IFN-γ are associated with improved response and prolonged survival in the treatment of ICIs, while decreased levels are associated with disease progression and immunotherapy-induced pneumonia.113,116 However, the report by Costantini et al. concluded that IFN-γ levels had no effect on ORR, PFS, and OS in nivolumab-treated NSCLC patients.117 In-depth studies of cytokines and chemokines can help to further improve the prediction of ICIs treatment.

Despite the positive results of blood-based biomarkers in predicting response to ICIs, many practical problems remain to achieve clinical application. First, detection and quantification of biomarkers remains a challenging task. Accurate and reliable results depend on the sensitivity and specificity of detection techniques. Secondly, inconsistent conclusions may be due to factors such as insufficient sample size and imperfect research design. To overcome these problems, larger studies and more standardized trial designs are needed.

Cutting-edge technologies for biomarker exploration

Several new techniques have been developed in recent years to better characterize TME and are promising tools for discovering biomarkers that predict response to ICIs. For example, single-cell sequencing technologies, which provide comprehensive information about the gene expression profiles of individual cells, have been used to investigate whether a certain cell subpopulation is connected to immunotherapy. Hu et al. found that neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 plus chemotherapy resulted in distinct transcriptome associated with clinical response. In MPR patients, there was a significant enrichment of FCRL4+ FCRL5+ memory B cells and CD16+ CX3CR1+ monocytes, which were predictive of the response to immunotherapy.118 These finding was accomplished by single-cell sequencing. Combining single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing, Liu et al. observed increased levels of precursor exhausted T cells in responsive NSCLC after anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting that expansion of such cells could improve treatment.119

Digital spatial profiler (DSP) is a spatial analysis method suitable for paraffin-embedded samples. Multiple studies have used DSP technology as a discovery tool to find spatial markers associated with ICIs. For example, DSP combined with immunofluorescence confirmed CD44 in tumor regions, but not immune regions, as a biomarker associated with anti-PD-1 therapy sensitivity in advanced NSCLC.120 New biomarker discoveries could help advance precision immunotherapy.

Tumor immunology research is increasingly focused on fully mining clinical data. Artificial intelligence based on deep learning algorithms can be used to extract image features and assess response to immunotherapy by quantifying clinical radiology and pathology images. In fact, radiographic images reflect complete information about the tumor in a noninvasive manner compared to traditional biopsy-based detection methods. Radiomics techniques confirmed the differences in CT images of lung cancer before and after treatment with ICIs.121,122 In addition, Artificial intelligence (AI) has facilitated the development of automated analysis techniques for tissue sections and has advanced the exploration of the distribution, function, and interactions of immune cells and tumor cells. Current studies are endeavoring to assess TILs,123 PD-L1,124 TMB,125 TLS126 and other predictors of ICIs in clinical sections using various convolutional neural network models.

Conclusions and future perspectives

In this review, we provide a pretty comprehensive analysis of ICIs predictive biomarkers from different perspectives. However, there is no agreement on which one is the most desirable and each of them has its own limitations (Table 4). For example, the method for determining the cutoff value of the continuous variables has not been standardized. Tumor heterogeneity cannot be ignored, which may reduce accuracy of prediction. In addition, the cost and convenience of biomarker testing also need to be considered. The immune response includes several processes such as antigen presentation and lymphocyte activation. Therefore, the combination of multiple biomarkers might improve predictive performance. In lung cancer, there was no significant correlation between PD-L1 and TMB, suggesting that they may be complementary biomarkers. For patients with low PD-L1 expression, TMB may be helpful in guiding patients to choose ICIs monotherapy or ICIs plus chemotherapy.
