Recreational Noise Exposure and Hearing Protection Practices Among Syrian Students in Damascus University: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ibrahim Alnader, Modar Doyya, Jameel Soqia, Yahia Ranjous, Samer Mohsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHearing loss is a global public health concern, with the World Health Organization projecting nearly 2.5 billion affected individuals by 2050. Work‐related and recreational noise contribute significantly to hearing impairment, yet there is limited research on this issue in Syria.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted with 391 participants from students at Damascus University, using convenience sampling at Damascus University, the largest university in Syria. A questionnaire, adapted from a UK study, was utilized to collect data on sociodemographic factors, beliefs about hearing loss, leisure noise exposure, and hearing protection devices (HPD) usage. Statistical analyses included logistic regressions to identify predictors of recreational noise exposure and HPD usage.Results45.8% of participants reported occasional recreational noise exposure, with 11.7% experiencing weekly exposure. However, HPD usage remained low at 8.4%. Logistic regressions revealed that gender, age, and personal hearing difficulty did not significantly predict noise exposure or HPD usage. Participants exhibited a high belief (98%) in genetic causes of hearing loss.ConclusionThis study unveils a gap in understanding and addressing recreational noise exposure among Damascus University students. The prevalence aligns with global trends, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions. Low HPD usage and prevalent beliefs in genetic causes of hearing loss underscore cultural influences and emphasize the necessity for culturally sensitive interventions. To advance public health, tailored interventions and educational programs within academic institutions are imperative. These findings lay the foundation for culturally sensitive public health initiatives.

Background

Hearing loss represents a widespread and disabling public health concern, as indicated by recent projections from the World Health Organization (WHO), forecasting that nearly 2.5 billion individuals will encounter some degree of hearing loss by the year 2050 [1]. Furthermore, nearly 90% of the people with hearing loss live in the low‐ and middle‐income countries [2]. WHO underscores the substantial contribution of work‐related, noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL), along with concerns pertaining to recreational NIHL, as primary causes of hearing impairment [2]. Furthermore, elevated noise levels, historically prevalent in occupational environments, are now becoming more pervasive in recreational settings [3]. NIHL is the gradual decline in auditory function following long‐term, repeated exposure to loud noise. NIHL emerges as the second most prevalent cause, following age‐related factors, of hearing loss in developed nations [4]. In the United States, 14.4% of adults contend with hearing difficulties, with nearly 10 million individuals specifically afflicted by NIHL [5, 6]. Similarly, in the United Kingdom, hearing impairment impacts the lives of 11 million individuals [7]. Research by Alnuman et al. revealed that 53% of Jordanian young adults exhibit symptoms indicative of hearing loss, with two‐thirds of this demographic participating in environments such as discos and nightclubs [8]. Similarly, a significant proportion of college students in Delhi, India, reported experiencing adverse health effects attributed to exposure to loud music [9]. Similar trends have also been observed among adolescents in Brazil [10]. A comparative study conducted in Pakistan revealed that among participants exposed to musical noise, 52% exhibited signs of hearing loss, while 13% reported experiencing tinnitus [11].

WHO has asserted that over 1 billion young adults face the risk of acquiring permanent hearing loss due to recreational noise exposure resulting from unsafe listening practices, including attendance at concerts, festivals, discos, and routine use of personal audio devices [1]. Furthermore, in their study on hearing, tinnitus, and exposure to amplified recreational noise, Metternich and Brusis identified a notably elevated risk of tinnitus and hearing loss, even in cases where people were exposed to brief durations of amplified music [12]. Over and above, noisy music and various types of recreational noise often lead to hearing loss, tinnitus, and hyperacusis, even when initial audiometric thresholds remain within the clinically normal range [13].

Despite NIHL being a preventable cause of hearing loss through the application of hearing protection devices (HPDs), such as ear muffs or earplugs [14], the utilization of HPDs during recreational exposure to loud events remains notably low at 8%, 9.8%, and 2.7% in the United States, Jordan, and India, respectively [8, 9, 15, 16]. Moreover, a study conducted in the United Kingdom found that students were largely unconvinced of the efficacy of hearing protection and identified several barriers to its use [17]. Nevertheless, studies indicate a potential for improvement in young adults' attitudes toward HPD usage, with an observed 8% increase in earplug use among participants in the United States of America in the MTV website survey subsequent to educational interventions highlighting the significance of HPDs [18]. Furthermore, a study conducted in South Africa identified a significant relationship between awareness of noise and attitudes. The findings suggested that the higher the level of awareness regarding leisure noise, the better their attitude and behavior, thus the lower the risk of hearing loss [19].

Significantly, there exists a lack of literature addressing the extent of recreational noise exposure and the adoption of HPDs in Syria. Consequently, our study seeks to evaluate the extent of recreational noise exposure and the use of HPDs among students at Damascus University.

Methods

Design

We conducted a cross‐sectional study to assess the level of recreational noise exposure among Syrian university students and their use of hearing protection. We chose Damascus University because it is the largest University in Syria with over 180,000 students from all over the country.

Participants

We employed a convenience sampling method to recruit participants for this study. Inclusion criteria included being a student at Damascus University and providing informed consent to complete the study questionnaire. Individuals who did not meet these criteria, as well as questionnaires containing incomplete or missing data, were excluded from the final analysis. After applying the exclusion criteria, the total number of participants was 391.

Data Collection

Data collection took place between February and April 2023. Paper copies were distributed in the libraries and lecture halls of various faculties. Each printed questionnaire was entered into Google Forms by two authors, with one author entering the data and the other verifying the answers. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before filling the questionnaires. The consent clearly stated that participation was optional and the collected data would be kept confidential and only used for research purposes. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Damascus University on 15/2/2023 with a serial number (1227).

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was adapted from a previous study conducted in the United Kingdom [20]. Minimal changes were made to the demographic questions to make them suit the characteristics of our community and sample that consists of university students. In particular, the race question was substituted with the city of residence, and the job question was deleted. The questionnaire was translated into Arabic three times by three of the authors, and each version was then retranslated into English by expert otolaryngologists. We chose the final Arabic version after comparing the translations to ensure clarity and accuracy. A pilot study was carried out with 50 students, and the questionnaire was checked according to the primary statistical study. Also, the survey was tested for reliability using Cronbach's α test, and the internal stability was found to be 0.82.

The questionnaire consisted of four parts. The first one consisted of five questions about sociodemographic factors and clinical hearing loss. The first three questions addressed gender, age, and city of residence, while the last two questions were derived from Benova et al. [21] and focused on hearing loss among participants and their families. The second part was adapted from Nguyen et al. [22] and comprised two questions regarding beliefs about the importance of genetic and behavioral factors in hearing loss. These two questions were dichotomized to No (not at all) and Yes (a little, somewhat, and a lot). In the third part, we defined “noisy leisure activity” as an activity in which you must raise your voice to be heard when standing 1 m away from the person you are speaking to. We gave the participants examples of it, such as live music, clubs, engine noises, and sports noises, and asked how frequently they had been exposed to such noises in the past year. In the fourth part, we asked how often the participants use hearing protection when exposed to these noises. The participants responded on a scale from 0% to 100% with a 10% interval.

Statistical Analysis

Google Forms was used to enter data online. Absolute (n) values were used to describe the variables. A two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Separate logistic regressions were conducted to identify predictors of recreational noise on only an occasional basis and the use of hearing protection during noisy recreational activities, respectively. In each regression, gender, age, and having a personal hearing difficulty were entered as predictors. Regression model assumptions were checked, and the model was fit for the analysis. We followed Assel et al. and SAMPL guidelines in our analysis [23, 24]. IBM SPSS Statistics v.24 was used in the analysis.

Sample Size

Sample size was calculated using Cochran's formula, considering Z = 1.96 (confidence level 95%), p = 0.5, q = 1―p = 0.5, and e = 5% (margin of error).

The sample size for this study was 385.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Three hundred and ninety‐one participants (n = 391) were randomly surveyed; 57.5% of the sample were females (n = 225), and 42.5% were males (n = 166). Mean age was 21.53 years (SD = 1.79). Most students (91%, n = 356) did not report a hearing loss, and all of them did not have people in their family with hearing loss (100%, n = 391). Approximately all (98%, n = 383) of the sample believed that hearing loss was caused by genetics whereas just 72.4% (n = 283) thought that hearing loss had a behavioral cause. Hearing Protection Use

Three hundred and eighty‐five participants (91.6%) reported zero use of hearing protection during noisy recreational activities in the last 12 months; just 33 students (8.4%) reported wearing hearing protection for noisy recreational activity. Discussion

It is firmly established that heightened sound levels can induce damage to the auditory system, leading to hearing loss in both animals and humans [25, 26]. Extensive research has demonstrated a correlation between damage and sound intensity, along with the duration of exposure to noise [27]. However, there appears to be a significant disparity in public concern regarding potential harm from recreational noise exposure compared to work‐related noise exposure. This is exemplified in recent literature and, more concerningly, in public competitions during national sporting events [27, 28]. A prior study conducted in Syria scrutinized the noise levels of dental equipment used at the University of Damascus Faculty of Dentistry [29]. The study revealed that the detected noise levels approached the risk threshold for hearing loss, set at 85 decibels [29]. Consequently, this study presents a new step within the Syrian context, aiming to quantify recreational noise exposure, assess the utilization of hearing protectors, and delineate their correlation with instances of hearing loss.

The study focused specifically on a cohort of Damascus University students. The investigation reported a prevalence of 45.8% for occasional recreational noise exposure. In contrast, among 73.4 million children in the United States, 7.9% were exposed to “very loud” recreational noise, primarily from sources such as music players, fireworks, lawnmowers, and firearms [30]. In the United Kingdom, 73.0% of individuals reported exposure to recreational noise in the past 12 months [20]. Notably, 11.7% reported weekly exposure, providing crucial insights into exposure frequency in this population. The study highlighted a low usage of HPDs (8.4%), aligning with the 8% reported by Dobie [4], as well as the 9.8% in Jordan [31]. However, this contrasts with Bhatt et al. [30], where 16.4% reported consistent use of protection, indicating the need for targeted interventions in specific populations. Additionally, only 2.1% reported wearing hearing protection for every noisy leisure activity in the United Kingdom [20], emphasizing a substantial gap between recreational noise exposure and hearing protector usage, consistent with studies in the UK [20], the US [32], and Belgium [33].

Given the high prevalence of recreational noise exposure and the low utilization of hearing protectors, targeted interventions are imperative to raise awareness and promote protective behaviors, especially among the younger demographic. The study advocates for the implementation of educational programs within academic institutions to enhance awareness about the risks associated with recreational noise exposure and the significance of hearing protection. A noteworthy finding in this study is the overwhelming belief (98%) in the genetic causes of hearing loss among participants. However, approximately half (49.8%) of the sample believed that hearing loss was caused by genetics whereas just 16.8% thought that hearing loss had a behavioral cause UK [20]. In the United States, conversely, a notable 75% of students demonstrated an understanding that exposure to loud sounds could precipitate hearing loss [34]. This unexpected revelation suggests potential cultural or educational influences on perceptions of hearing loss causes. The results underscore the critical need for public health campaigns targeting the prevention of NIHL, particularly in low‐income countries.

The findings emphasize the requirement for population‐specific strategies to address cultural or educational factors shaping beliefs about hearing loss causes, thereby informing the development of effective public health interventions. The study also uncovered a novel finding, with 11.7% reporting weekly recreational noise exposure, a facet not extensively explored in previous studies. This underscores the necessity for investigations into specific activities contributing to weekly exposure and their potential repercussions on hearing health. By shedding light on the impact of cultural beliefs on perceptions of hearing loss causes, the study contributes to existing knowledge, facilitating the tailoring of interventions to specific populations. Moreover, the identification of a high frequency of weekly exposure adds nuance to existing knowledge of patterns of recreational noise exposure, guiding the development of targeted interventions focused on reducing the frequency of exposure. The implication is that early interventions are necessary to ensure awareness of behavioral causes of hearing loss before exposure occurs.

Limitations

The inherent cross‐sectional design of the investigation poses constraints on establishing causal relationships, rendering it incapable of capturing evolving beliefs and behaviors over time. Furthermore, the study confines its scope to a specific age demographic comprising Damascus University students. The utilization of self‐reported data introduces a susceptibility to recall bias, as participants may not reliably recollect or disclose instances of noise exposure and adherence to protective measures. The low number of hearing protection users (8.4%) presents a limitation in the statistical analysis. Consequently, to bolster the robustness of future investigations in this domain, there is a compelling need for longitudinal studies and the incorporation of objective measures to assess noise exposure.

While recognizing these limitations, the significance of this study lies in highlighting the need to raise awareness about the potential risks associated with leisure noise exposure and to promote the use of hearing protection. Further research involving a larger and more diverse sample is necessary to better understand the scope of the issue across other Syrian cities and among different age groups.

Conclusion

Our study unveils a gap in understanding and addressing recreational noise exposure in the Syrian context, with 45.8% of Damascus University students experiencing occasional exposure. The prevalence aligns with global trends, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions. Notably, low hearing protector usage (8.4%) and widespread belief in genetic causes of hearing loss (98%) highlight cultural influences. To advance public health, tailored interventions and educational programs within academic institutions are imperative. Our findings, notably the 11.7% reporting weekly exposure, offer nuanced insights for future research and targeted strategies. This study lays the foundation for culturally sensitive public health initiatives, urging timely interventions to mitigate NIHL.
