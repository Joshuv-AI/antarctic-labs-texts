Improving pressure injury assessment skills of nursing students’ using in-situ simulation and moulage: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hatice Erdem Onder, Dilek Sari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is very important to use new teaching methods to improve the skills of classification and assessment of pressure injuries of nursing students who will provide direct care to patients after graduation. This study evaluated the effectiveness of in-situ simulation and moulage in improving nursing students’ pressure injury classification and assessment skills.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial was completed with 84 senior nursing students. Students participating in the study were randomly assigned to the in-situ simulation, in-laboratory simulation, and control group.ResultsIt was found that students in the in-situ simulation group had significantly higher knowledge test (post-test) scores than the control group (p < 0.05). Students in the in-situ and in- laboratory simulation groups had significantly higher correct classification percentages and performance scores for both laboratory and clinical assessment of pressure injury than students in the control group (p < 0.05).ConclusionMoulage is a useful tool for developing and transferring nursing students’ pressure injury classification and assessment abilities to the clinical setting when used with both in-situ and in-laboratory simulation methods.Trial registrationRetrospective registration was completed on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06406504, registered on 2024-05-06).Keywords: In-situ simulation, Moulage, Nursing students, Simulation, Pressure injury

Introduction

Pressure injuries (PI), which are often considered preventable but continue to occur, are a significant patient safety issue [1]. It has been reported that the prevalence of PI, a quality indicator in healthcare, varies from 3.4% to 32.4% worldwide and it is estimated that 60,000 patients die from PI annually [2, 3]. Although the prevalence of PI is accepted as an indicator of the quality of healthcare, especially nursing care1, Fulbrook et al. (2019) found that nurses’ level of knowledge about prevention of PI was inadequate, and the lowest score was obtained from the PI assessment sub-dimension [4]. Similarly, a descriptive, comparative multicentre study in Sweden found that registered nurses (55.8%) and undergraduate nursing students (54.3%) had lower PI classification and assessment skills using a PI knowledge assessment tool compared to other themes [5]. Nevertheless, the proper classification and assessment of PI is of crucial importance in the formulation of a treatment plan and the monitoring of progress [6].

The knowledge and skills of nurses in the classification and assessment of PIs are taught during nursing education and developed as they encounter PIs during clinical practice [7]. To develop these skills, providing students with sufficient clinical experience is essential; however, limited opportunities and challenges in psychomotor skill development arise due to high student-to-educator ratios, the complexities of the healthcare system, reduced clinical practicum hours, and patient safety concerns [8–10]. In addition, it is not possible to guarantee that all students observe and evaluate the patient’s PI, considering the difficulties such as not having a patient with PI in every clinical practice, and even if there is, the student is not given the opportunity to evaluate and participate in the care of the patient’s PI [11]. One strategy used by nursing programs to address this challenge is the use of simulation-based training (SBT) to provide clinical training for nursing students in a safe, controlled environment [12]. Standardized simulation techniques provide nursing students with repeated opportunities to develop essential skills within a controlled environment prior to clinical practice, thereby reducing the potential risk of patient harm [13].

Simulation applications are traditionally carried out in simulation centers established for simulation training. In-situ simulation, on the other hand, takes place in the participants’ actual patient care area/setting to achieve a high level of fidelity [14–17]. One of the ways to increase fidelity in simulation applications is through moulage [18]. Moulage, involving the use of make-up and materials that mimic body fluids is used to simulate the wound, disease, old age and other physical characteristics of the participants related to the scenario in order to achieve learning outcomes [14, 19]. Thus, the moulage in the simulation application increases the fidelity of the simulation scenario by enabling the participant to physically visualize the simulated findings specific to the scenario [20]. Many healthcare education experts propose that moulage can enhance the fidelity of simulation scenarios. Comparative studies examining moulage versus no moulage have demonstrated enhanced learner performance and greater immersion in clinical scenarios. However, limited research explores the extent to which moulage influences learners in simulation [18, 21]. Studies have combined simulation with moulage to address burns, complications related to intravenous catheters, skin aassessment, teaching of dermatologic conditions, trauma education, and endotracheal suctioning skills [22–27]. Despite its potential, very few studies have combined the use of simulation with moulage for the purpose of PI classification and assessment [10, 26].

Studies have reported that SBT increases students’ knowledge, skills and satisfaction with PI prevention and management, improves communication skills and is more effective than traditional didactic education [7, 10, 28, 29]. However, no studies have been identified in the literature examining the effect of in-situ simulation and moulage on improving PI classification and assessment skills. This study aims to compare the effects of in-situ simulation and moulage on improving PI knowledge, classification and assessment skills, and satisfaction and self-confidence among nursing students.

Research hypotheses.

H1

There is a statistically significant difference between the groups in post-education PI knowledge scores.

H2

There is a statistically significant difference between the groups in evaluation simulation skill performance scores.

H3

There is a statistically significant difference between the groups in clinical skill performance scores.

H4

There is a statistically significant difference between the groups in students’ satisfaction and self-confidence scale scores.

It is important to note that the hypotheses were designed to evaluate differences between the groups following the educational intervention. Therefore, improvements observed exclusively within the control group would not satisfy the criteria for hypothesis confirmation unless a statistically significant difference was also demonstrated between the groups.

Methods

Study design

A randomized controlled study design with pre-/post-tests and control group was employed. Data was collected from September 2021 to May 2023. Registration was completed in the Clinical Trials database (NCT06406504, 2024-05-06, retrospectively registered, ClinicalTrials.gov).

Study population and sample

The study consisted of 284 senior nursing students enrolled in a Nursing Faculty in Izmir, Türkiye, during the 2021–2022 academic spring semester. The nursing program is a four-year curriculum, and in the final year, students receive one day of theoretical instruction and four days of clinical practice per week. Participants were recruited voluntarily through announcements disseminated by class representatives via WhatsApp and verbal invitations delivered during class breaks. It was emphasized that participation would not influence their academic evaluation. The required sample size was determined based on the findings of Sezgünsay and Başak (2020) [10], which evaluated the effectiveness of moulage in enhancing nursing students’ clinical skills in pressure injury assessment. A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for one-way ANOVA comparing three independent groups. The parameters used were α = 0.10, power (1–β) = 0.95, and an effect size (f) derived from the study. The analysis revealed that a minimum of 84 participants (28 per group) would be needed. To account for potential dropouts, the sample size was increased to 90 students. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a senior nursing student, (2) no prior pressure injury classification experience, (3) no previous coursework on pressure injury, and (4) voluntary participation. During the study, six participants (two from each group) withdrew due to personal reasons, such as health problems and absenteeism, which prevented them from participating in the simulation training. As a result, the study was completed with 84 participants.

Randomization

After obtaining informed consent, students were randomly assigned to the in-situ simulation, in-laboratory simulation, and control groups in a 1:1:1 ratio. Randomization was conducted by an independent statistician who was not involved in the intervention or data collection. To ensure balanced allocation, stratified block randomization based on students’ Grade Point Averages (GPA) was used. A computer-generated random number sequence was created using a publicly accessible online tool (https://www.randomizer.org).

Allocation concealment was implemented by securing the randomization list and ensuring that group assignments remained blinded to both participants and outcome assessors until the conclusion of the data collection phase. Although blinding of the primary researcher was not feasible due to their involvement in delivering the prebriefing and debriefing sessions, participants remained blinded throughout the data collection, statistical analysis, and reporting phases to minimize performance and detection bias.

Data collection

Data were collected with a The Descriptive Characteristics Form, Pressure Injury Knowledge Test, Pressure Injury Assessment Form (student), Pressure Injury Skill Evaluation Form, and Students’ Satisfaction and Self-Confidence Scale.

The descriptive characteristics form

This form had two items about the sociodemographic characteristics of the students such as age and gender.

Pressure injury knowledge test (PIKT)

This test was created by the researcher following a literature review [30, 31]. The PI knowledge test consists of 15 multiple-choice questions on PI general knowledge (five questions), classification (seven questions) and PI assessment (three questions). The score range of the test is 0 to 100. The form underwent review by four experts in wound care and nursing education to evaluate content validity, including four faculty members specialized in Fundamentals of Nursing (two Professors and two Associate Professors). The content validity of the tool was calculated as 0.81.

Pressure injury assessment form (student)

The form was created by the researchers according to the literature and the “Bates-Jensen Wound Assessment Tool” available on the website of the Wound Ostomy Incontinence Nurses Association [32]. This form was used both to determine the accuracy of students’ classification and assessment of PI and as a facilitator. The form was used by students during simulation applications and clinical assessments. The form includes thirteen steps relating to PI classification, PI location and size, presence of tunneling and exudate, assessment of PI perimeter, maceration, induration and assessment of signs of infection. Content validity was ensured through review by four experts in wound care and nursing education. Experts provided qualitative feedback regarding item clarity and relevance, and necessary revisions were made accordingly.

Pressure injury skill evaluation form

Form was created by the researcher following a literature review [33, 34]. This form was used to determine students’ PI skill performance scores. The form contains a total of twenty-five items: preparation for implementation (7 items), PI classification (1 item), PI assessment (12 items), and post-implementation (5 items). The preparation for implementation (7 items) and post-implementation (5 items) steps of the form were filled in by the researcher while the student was practicing, while the PI classification (1 item) and PI assessment (12 items) steps were filled in after the student had completed the practice, taking into account the “Pressure Injury Assessment Form” used by the student during the practice. PI performance skills were evaluated based on whether the steps are: “correctly performed”, “performed incompletely”, and “not performed/incorrectly performed”. Correct steps are awarded two points, incomplete steps one point and unperformed/incorrectly performed steps zero points, with total scores ranging from zero to 50. The form underwent review by four experts in wound care and nursing education to evaluate content validity. The content validity of the tool was calculated as 0.92.

Students’ Satisfaction and Self-Confidence Scale (SSSC)

The 13-item scale, originally developed by Jeffries and Rizzolo (2006), was adapted into Turkish by Unver et al. (2017) [35, 36]. This scale is utilized to assess students’ self-confidence and satisfaction in a simulation learning environment. It comprises two sub-dimensions: “Satisfaction with Current Learning” and “Self-confidence in Learning.” Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.85 for “Satisfaction with Current Learning” and 0.77 for “Self-confidence in learning” [36]. In our study, Cronbach’s alpha values were found to be 0.87 for “Satisfaction with Current Learning” and 0.89 for “Self-confidence in Learning.” A higher total score indicates more self-confidence in learning and satisfaction.

Implementing research/procedure

The study adhered to CONSORT reporting guidelines, as shown in Fig. 1. INACSL Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice™ Simulation Design was used as a framework to design SBT [37]. Participants with prior standardized patient (SP) experience were selected for the study, and their consent was obtained. The SP was informed about the study and provided with a 2-hour training session on the simulation scenario. Figure 2 shows the simulation components.

Components

This research, designed to improve nursing students’ ability to classification and assessment PIs, was carried out in three stages (Fig. 1).

The first stage is the preparation stage. In this stage, the students who randomised were first given a PIKT (pre-test 1). After the form was completed, theoretical education was conducted with the participation of all students. The theoretical education included topics such as definition, prevalence, epidemiology, pathophysiology, risk assessment, classification, and assessment of PI. The PIKT (pre-test 2) was then administered to assess the effectiveness of the training.

The second stage is the simulation-based training stage. Two weeks after the theoretical training, the students participated in a 10 min education simulation of their group, choosing times that were suitable for research program. Control group students (n = 30) did simulation with a PI simulator on a SP in the simulation laboratory, in-laboratory simulation group students (n = 30) did simulation with moulage on a SP in the patient room in the simulation laboratory, and in-situ simulation group students (n = 30) did simulation with moulage on a SP in a clinic (Image 1). Simulation started with pre-briefing. Each student participated in an 8–10 min pre-briefing session that included orientation to the environment, sharing expectations about the scenario, and defining student role. Students were taken into the simulation one by one and their performances were recorded on video. During the simulation, students completed the “Pressure Injury Assessment Form (student)”. A single independent researcher evaluated their performance using a “Pressure Injury Skill Evaluation Form” After the simulation, the researcher reviewed the forms completed by the students, assessed whether they correctly performed the PI classification and assessment steps, and calculated their skill performance scores. Subsequent to each simulation, a debriefing session was conducted, with a duration of between 20 and 30 min. The education simulation concluded with students completing the SSSC.

The third stage of the study was the evaluation phase, which included two parts: “Evaluation Simulation” and “Clinical Evaluation”. The students’ “Evaluation Simulation” was carried out using the simulation in which they had been trained. The simulation lasted 10 min for each student. Students performances were scored with the same “Pressure Injury Assessment Form (student)” and “Pressure Injury Skill Evaluation Form”. Following the simulation, a debriefing session was conducted, and students completed the PIKT (post-test). Finally, performance evaluations of the students in the clinical environment were carried out. The students did evaluations on patients determined by the clinical nurse in charge before the practice. They did not provide any PI care or treatment. Each student conducted one assessment, which was evaluated using the same performance forms. Feedback was given after the assessments.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0 (SPSS, Inc., IL, USA). Descriptive data were presented using percentage, frequency, mean, and standard deviation values. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. ANOVA was utilized to make the comparison of independent groups of more than two for normally distributed data. Kruskal-Wallis analysis was employed to make the comparison of independent groups of more than two for non-normally distributed data. The time-dependent change of the measurements was evaluated by Friedman test. Bonferroni correction was utilized to make paired comparisons to find out the source of different findings between the PIKT and skill performance scores of the students.

Discussion

The effect of in-situ simulation and moulage on improving nursing students’ PI classification and assessment skills was investigated in the present study.

Pressure injury knowledge acquisition

Participants’ PI knowledge scores increased in all groups, but significantly more in the in-situ simulation group. While numerous studies have compared simulation fidelity, research on the effectiveness of in-situ simulation in nursing education remains limited. Yilmazer et al. (2022) reported a significant increase in knowledge among nurses trained in PI prevention using in-situ simulation [38]. Similarly, Lavelle et al. (2017) found that in-situ simulation training on medical emergency management in mental health clinics significantly improved the knowledge of healthcare professionals [39]. Furthermore, studies support that SBT using moulage improves nursing students’ knowledge [26, 40], which is consistent with our findings. It is likely that the in-situ simulation group more effectively bridged the gap between theory and practice, thereby facilitating knowledge retention. However, there was no significant difference in PI knowledge scores between the in-laboratory simulation and control groups, consistent with Sezgunsay and Basak (2020) [10]. All groups demonstrated higher mean knowledge scores at pre-test 2 and post-test compared to pre-test 1, suggesting that high-fidelity simulation (HFS) with SP contributed to overall knowledge improvement. Meta-analyses confirm that PI training significantly improves nurses’ PI knowledge [41, 42].

Pressure injury skill acquisition

As a result of our study, both in-situ and in-laboratory simulation were found to be effective in improving students’ skills in PI classification and assessment, as well as in transferring these skills to clinical practice. In clinical settings, nearly all students in the in-situ simulation group (92.9%) and most in the in-laboratory simulation group (89.3%) correctly classified PI, compared to 57.1% in the control group. Previous studies support the effectiveness of moulage in SBT. Sarmasoglu et al. (2020) found that moulage with in-situ simulation enhanced scenario engagement and improved patient management skills [23]. Similarly, Sezgunsay and Başak (2020) and Uzelli Yılmaz et al. (2021) showed that the use of moulage with SBT significantly improved PI classification skills in nursing students [7, 10]. Additionally, Monesi et al. (2022) highlighted the benefits of in-situ simulation in intensive care training, improving both technical and non-technical skills, while Rød et al. (2021) emphasized its role in increasing clinical competence and self-confidence [43, 44]. The popularity of HFS in nursing education is attributed to its ability to provide realistic learning experiences, enhancing both skills and knowledge [45, 46]. This study enhanced environmental realism through in-situ simulation, the physical fidelity was increased by ensuring that the SP characteristics match with the patient characteristics in the real world with moulage, and the functional reality was increased by performing a PI classification and assessment similar to the real PI with moulage. Although all three groups received HFS training, in-situ simulation and moulage were more effective in developing PI classification and assessment skills compared to PI simulators. The lack of a statistical difference between in-situ and in-laboratory simulation groups may be explained by the clinical design of the laboratory and the use of moulage in both settings. Moulage enhanced fidelity, allowing students to experience authentic PI classification and assessment, establish logical clinical connections, and reduce stress, leading to better performance. Thus, moulage proved to be a superior method for developing PI classification and assessment skills.

SSSC

Many studies compared the level of students’ satisfaction and self-confidence in low-fidelity simulation and HFS applications and indicated that those getting education via HFS had markedly greater self-confidence and satisfaction scores [26, 47, 48]. This can be attributed to the fact that students’ satisfaction and self-confidence levels rise as the realism of SBT practices increases.

These outcomes were similar to those of our study. In our study, the fact that all three simulation methods were HFS caused the level of students’ self-confidence and satisfaction to increase. SBT is an ideal education and training method because it allows students’ strengths and weaknesses to be determined and individualized education to be developed by giving immediate feedback on their performance [49].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single institution and included only senior nursing students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other educational settings or student populations. Second, the Pressure Injury Knowledge Test, Pressure Injury Assessment Form, and Pressure Injury Skill Evaluation Form were developed by the researchers. Content validity was ensured for all instruments through expert review. For the Pressure Injury Knowledge Test and the Skill Evaluation Form, the Content Validity Index was calculated. For the Pressure Injury Assessment Form (student version), experts provided qualitative feedback regarding item clarity and relevance, and necessary revisions were made accordingly; however, no quantitative validity index was calculated. In addition, no pilot testing of the instruments was conducted. This has been acknowledged as a limitation of the study. Third, only stage 2 and stage 3 PIs were simulated with moulage; thus, students did not have the opportunity to assess all stages of PIs. Fourth, although PIs are most common in the sacrum and coccyx region, they were not created in these areas to protect the privacy of the SP. Fifth, students were not able to assess the same patient during the clinical evaluation phase, which may have limited the consistency of performance evaluations. Finally, the study lacked a longitudinal follow-up to evaluate the long-term retention or sustainability of the SBT effects.

Conclusions

The study results indicated that all groups exhibited an increase in knowledge following SBT. However, the in-situ simulation group achieved significantly higher post-test knowledge scores than the control group. Furthermore, students in both the in-situ and in-laboratory simulation groups significantly higher skill performance scores than those in the control group who were trained with the PI simulator. This suggests that moulage is more effective than the PI simulator in developing PI classification and assessment skills. Students in the in-situ and in-laboratory simulation groups were more successful in accurately classifying PI and transferring their skills to clinical practice when assessing a real patient for the first time. However, no significant difference was found between these two groups in terms of PI classification and assessment performance score. This suggests that in-situ simulation may not be superior to laboratory simulation when the primary objective is psychomotor skill development. Instead, in-situ simulation is likely to be more effective in areas requiring collaboration, communication and teamwork. Therefore, the selection of an appropriate simulation should be based on a careful analysis of the intended learning outcomes.
