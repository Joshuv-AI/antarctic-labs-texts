Medical Device Industry Payments and Utilization of Corresponding Devices
Authors: Misop Han, Yuezhou Jing, Zeyad Hammadeh, Ethan N. Lee, Jenny X. Chen, Craig Cronin, Gerard Anderson, Amin Herati, Bruce J. Trock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs acceptance of payments from medical device manufacturers associated with physician utilization of the manufacturers’ devices?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study linking device industry payments and Medicare claims for 12 devices between 2015 and 2023, cumulative payments exceeded $136 million, and more than a half million device-associated procedures were performed. Physicians who accepted higher payments from a manufacturer had greater utilization of that manufacturer’s device.MeaningThese findings suggest that higher device-related industry payments to physicians are associated with increased utilization of corresponding devices.

Introduction

Financial relationships between physicians and industry remain pervasive. The Physician Payments Sunshine Act requires pharmaceutical and medical device manufacturers to disclose payments to physicians and teaching hospitals through the Open Payments Program (OPP).1,2 Between 2013 and 2022, industry payments to physicians totaled more than $12 billion, with more than one-half of US physicians having accepted at least 1 industry payment.3 Given concerns about potential conflicts of interest, it is important to understand how these payments impact clinical practice.4

Extensive research has shown that pharmaceutical industry payments are associated with higher prescribing costs, increased use of brand-name medications, and greater prescribing of a paying manufacturer’s products.5,6,7,8,9 Moreover, temporal analyses between pharmaceutical industry payments and prescribing strongly suggest that payments cause physicians to change their prescribing patterns.8,10,11 In contrast, far less is known about the influence of medical device industry payments, despite device companies directing a larger share of revenue toward physician payments.12 One major barrier has been the lack of accessible, device-specific utilization data at the physician level, in contrast to the readily available, physician-level prescribing data used in pharmaceutical studies.12

To address this gap, we linked the OPP dataset with a Medicare claims dataset and identified procedures involving devices with unique Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) codes. Using this linked dataset, we sought to evaluate the association between payments from device manufacturers and the volume of procedures performed with their corresponding devices by individual physicians in the Medicare population.

Methods

Data Sources

We conducted a cross-sectional study with a repeated-measures design linking device industry payments to physician-level utilization of select device-associated procedures captured in Medicare claims. Public data for industry payments were obtained through the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services OPP website for program years 2015 through 2023. Only general payments related to covered devices were included, which represent nonresearch, noninvestment payments or other transfers of value from applicable device manufacturers to physicians. Examples of general payments include food and beverage, travel and lodging, entertainment, speaking fees, and consulting fees.13 Payment data were then linked with the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System’s latest monthly dissemination file to accurately identify allopathic and osteopathic physicians and their specialties.14 Records missing National Provider Identifier or with National Provider Identifiers that did not match a current or deactivated entry in the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System were excluded.

Device utilization was measured using a nationally representative 20% random sample of Medicare claims from carrier and outpatient files spanning the same period.15,16 Absolute procedure counts and adjusted mean annual counts represent utilization observed within this 20% sample and were not multiplied to estimate national Medicare fee-for-service totals. All linkages between OPP, National Plan and Provider Enumeration System, and claims were performed using the physician National Provider Identifier. This study was not submitted to the institutional review board for approval because this study used a retrospective, deidentified, national data set. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Study Sample

The cohort for this study consisted of physicians who had performed any procedures associated with the devices of interest. We only included payments to clinically relevant specialists for each device (eg, urologists for prostatic urethral lift or otolaryngologists for balloon sinus dilation). Taxonomy and specialty definitions were obtained from the National Uniform Claim Committee code set.17 When multiple taxonomies were present for a physician, the primary taxonomy code was used.

We examined select devices and/or procedures across multiple specialties. We included generic devices and/or procedures that (1) can be identified with distinct procedure codes (Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] codes, also known as HCPCS Level I codes) or device codes (HCPCS Level II codes) in the claims data; (2) are mostly produced by a limited (<5) number of manufacturers; and (3) have corresponding payment and utilization data for the study period. Device-related payments were identified using the associated product name fields. In the OPP, payments generally listed 1 associated product per payment record. When multiple product fields were present or when a payment record could not be confidently assigned to 1 of the specified devices categories, the record was reviewed manually and excluded. The following 12 generic devices and/or procedures were included for this study: prostatic urethral lift, robotic-assisted waterjet ablation of the prostate, photoselective vaporization of the prostate, sacral neuromodulation, inflatable penile prosthesis, balloon sinus dilation, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, subcutaneous implantable cardioverter and/or defibrillator, transcatheter aortic valve replacement prosthesis, yttrium-90 microsphere-based selective internal radiation therapy and/or embolization, deep brain stimulation, and aqueous shunt implantation (Table 1). A wide spectrum of procedure-intensive specialties was represented, including urology, gynecology, colorectal surgery, otolaryngology, cardiology, radiology, neurosurgery, and ophthalmology.

Statistical Analysis

The primary exposure was the annual total general payments by each physician per device in the OPP. To improve interpretability and limit the influence of extreme values, total payments were grouped into 6 categories: $0, greater than $0 to $2500, greater than $2500 to $5000, greater than $5000 to $7500, greater than $7500 to $10 000, and greater than $10 000. The $0 payment reference category included physician years in which physicians performed the relevant device-associated procedure but received no corresponding general payments. Thresholds of $5000 and $10 000 were chosen, corresponding to current and prior US Department of Health and Human Services criteria for defining substantial financial conflicts of interest.18 All payment values were inflation adjusted to 2024 US dollars using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers: Medical Care in US City Average.19

The primary outcome was the annual utilization of each device-associated procedure, measured as the count of relevant procedure claims per physician, allowing for the assessment of differences in device utilization across payment categories. The association between annual general payments category and annual procedure counts was modeled as the incidence rate ratio (IRR) using generalized estimating equations with a Poisson distribution and a log link function. Models were fitted over 2015 to 2023, with calendar year as a covariate to account for longitudinal variations. Correlation of repeated counts within physicians over time were accounted using exchangeable working correlation structure. Goodness of fit and potential overdispersion were evaluated via Pearson χ2 test and deviance statistics. Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < .05.

We conducted prespecified sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, linear trend across payment categories was assessed by assigning each category its midpoint value and modeling payments as a single continuous variable in regression analyses. Second, lagged payment models were estimated in which prior-year payments were used to estimate current-year device utilization. Third, analyses were restricted to physicians with higher procedural volume (≥20 device-associated procedures during the study period). Finally, we grouped payments into 2 categories by nature of payment (compensation and gifts) by device to assess whether association patterns differed across payment types. Across these analyses, the magnitude and direction of associations between industry payments and device utilization were evaluated for consistency. Analyses were performed using STATA/MP statistical software version 18.0 (StataCorp) and SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

We evaluated the association between device-related industry general payments to physicians and utilization volumes for 12 generic devices between 2015 and 2023. The total aggregate payments for all examined devices were $136 671 793. The aggregate payments by device varied from $759 572 to $67 318 185. In the 20% Medicare fee-for-service sample, the total aggregate number of procedures using all examined devices during the study period was 518 166. The aggregate number of procedures by device ranged from 2212 to 149 230 (Table 1).

We assessed whether receipt of more payments was associated with greater device utilization compared with receipt of no payments in each device. The aggregate number of physicians performing procedures by device ranged from 547 to 6364. Overall, there was a statistically significant increase in device utilization by increasing payments for 11 of 12 devices (Table 2 and Table 3).

For example, for prostatic urethral lift for benign prostatic hyperplasia treatment, mean procedure volumes increased approximately stepwise with higher corresponding payment categories (Table 2). Compared with physicians who did not receive any industry payments, those receiving more than $10 000 had higher adjusted mean annual counts (10.6 [95% CI, 8.27-13.57] procedures vs 4.37 [95% CI, 8.27-13.57] procedures) with an IRR of 2.42 (95% CI, 1.89-3.12; P < .001). In addition, the type 3 P value in Table 2 indicates that the overall fit of the model was statistically significant (P < .001). Similarly, for hypoglossal nerve stimulation, physicians receiving more than $10 000 had higher adjusted mean annual counts than those receiving no payments (5.09 [95% CI, 3.65-7.11] procedures vs 2.16 [95% CI, 2.01-2.32] procedures; IRR, 2.36; 95% CI, 1.68-3.32; P < .001)

Meanwhile, for subcutaneous implantable cardioverter defibrillator for ventricular arrhythmia, compared with physicians receiving no payments, physicians receiving more than $10 000 had higher adjusted mean annual counts (1.87 [95% CI, 1.46-2.40] procedures vs 1.28 [95% CI, 1.24-1.32] procedures), with an IRR of 1.46 (95% CI, 1.14-1.87; P = .003) (Table 3). However, the overall fit of the model did not reach statistical significance (P = .10), possibly reflecting low power owing to the small number of physicians in the highest payment categories (5 in the $7500-$10 000 category and 9 in the >$10 000 category).

Although higher payments from device manufacturers were associated with greater utilization of the corresponding devices, the magnitude of this association varied across technologies. Among physicians receiving more than $10 000 compared with those receiving no payments, adjusted IRRs for device use ranged from 1.11 (95% CI, 0.64-1.94) for aqueous shunt implantation to 4.05 (95% CI, 3.34-4.92) for inflatable penile prosthesis, suggesting heterogeneity in the observed associations between industry payments and device utilization.

Results were robust across multiple prespecified sensitivity analyses. First, modeling payment categories as a continuous variable using category midpoints demonstrated statistically significant linear trends for most devices, supporting the use of a continuous specification (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Second, analyses using lagged payment variables—prior-year payments estimating current-year device use—produced comparable, yet mostly attenuated, effect estimates, suggesting diminishing payment-related associations with device utilization within a year and heterogeneous temporal relationships between payments and procedures by device, rather than a uniform direction across all devices (eTables 2 and 3 in Supplement 1). Finally, restricting the cohort to physicians with higher procedural volume (≥20 procedures) did not materially change the findings (eTables 4 and 5 in Supplement 1). Across sensitivity analyses, the magnitude and direction of associations between industry payments and device utilization remained consistent. Additional results are shown in eTables 6 to 15 in Supplement 1.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study linking device industry payments with device-specific utilization, physicians who accepted greater payments from a device manufacturer performed a higher volume of procedures using that manufacturer’s device, independently of device type or physician specialty. Although this positive association was consistent across most devices, the magnitude of increased utilization varied between devices.

These findings extend a growing body of literature demonstrating associations between industry payments and device use. Prior studies have reported associations between aggregate payments from multiple manufacturers of balloon dilation devices and procedure volume among otolaryngologists treating chronic rhinosinusitis,20,21 preferential use of cardiac devices produced by manufacturers providing the highest payments to physicians,22 and greater regional use of knee arthroplasties and percutaneous coronary interventions associated with payments.23 The present study expands this evidence by examining multiple devices across several specialties and identifies a consistent dose-response relationship between payment amount and physician-level device utilization. This finding may help explain prior research linking device industry payments with higher overall Medicare Part B billing amounts.12

Although the influence of pharmaceutical industry payments on prescribing behavior is well established,6,7,8,9 relationships between physicians and device manufacturers differ in important ways. Physicians frequently collaborate in the development and refinement of medical devices,24 and device representatives often provide procedural training and technical support for new technologies.12 Nonetheless, the present findings suggest that industry payments from device manufacturers are associated with physician utilization patterns in a manner similar to that observed for pharmaceutical industry payments. However, the clinical and economic interpretation of higher utilization is not necessarily uniform across product types. Device-related financial ties may raise concerns about overuse or preferential product selection by physicians, but they may also accompany training, technical support, and the diffusion of procedures that require specialized expertise. Without information on patient outcomes, complications, disease severity, and alternative treatment use in each case, the observed associations should be viewed as evidence of payment-associated utilization patterns rather than evidence that the utilization was clinically unjustified or economically inefficient.

Investigation of device-specific utilization is inherently challenging because many devices lack unique procedure-specific or device-specific codes in administrative claims data. For example, the da Vinci robotic surgical system, which has been associated with more than $300 million in payments to physicians over the past decade,3 cannot be reliably tracked in Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services claims because CPT and HCPCS codes do not distinguish robotic-assisted from conventional surgical approaches. Similar limitations apply to many orthopedic devices that lack specific CPT and/or HCPCS codes, despite device manufacturers having paid more than $1.3 billion to orthopedic surgeons in the same period.3 Consequently, this analysis was limited to a subset of devices with unique CPT and/or HCPCS codes. Nonetheless, the included devices in the present research accounted for more than $136 million in cumulative industry payments across a broad range of clinical indications, procedures, and specialties, supporting the generalizability of the observed association between industry payments and device utilization. These findings highlight the limitations of current Medicare claims and suggest that requiring standardized device codes on Medicare claims could improve transparency, enable more-precise assessment of device utilization, and strengthen postmarket surveillance and value-based payment evaluations.25

This study highlights several avenues for future research. Prior work has shown that industry payments related to antiplatelet drugs were associated not only with prescribing behavior but also with increased use of related cardiac procedures, such as coronary artery stent placement.26 It is unknown whether device industry payments similarly influence associated pharmacologic treatment patterns. In addition, temporal analyses in pharmaceutical studies suggest that payment-related effects on prescribing may attenuate within a year.10 Longitudinal studies examining the durability of associations between device industry payments and utilization are needed, although sensitivity analysis using lagged payment variables suggest a diminishing impact of device industry payments over time. Finally, within general payments, compensation payments (eg, consulting or speaking fees) have been shown to influence prescribing more strongly and are disproportionately directed toward key opinion leaders with many professional peers.27 Whether such compensation payments similarly affect device utilization, relative to other general payments, remains unknown.

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to evaluate associations between industry payments and device utilization across multiple devices and specialties. For most devices examined, higher industry payments were associated with increased utilization in a clear dose-response pattern with potential implications for clinical decision-making, health care spending, and transparency policy. Given the widespread use of these procedures in routine clinical practice, the findings are likely applicable to other procedure-intensive settings where physician compensation and device selection are closely linked.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this analysis was limited to Medicare fee-for-service claims. The observed differences in procedure volume may be different or have different economic implications than in commercial insurance or capitated systems where reimbursement rates and physician incentives may differ. Second, the analysis did not assess the impact of other types of industry payments, including research payments or physician ownership or investment interests.13 Third, many evaluated procedures were urology focused, reflecting author expertise, although the Medicare population disproportionately seeks urologic care, and 6 of the 7 nonurology procedures exhibited statistically significant linear trends.28 Fourth, although inaccuracies in OPP data have been reported, substantial financial penalties for misreporting may mitigate this concern.29 Fifth, this study cannot exclude the possibility of residual confounders, such as unmeasured patients preferences or clinical factors influencing device selection. Sixth, same-year models cannot establish temporal directionality. Payments may influence subsequent utilization, but manufacturers may also preferentially engage physicians who already perform high volumes of relevant procedures. Although lagged-payment analyses partially address this concern, reverse causality and bidirectional feedback between payments and utilization cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, higher payments from device manufacturers were associated with greater use of the corresponding devices, irrespective of device type or physician specialty. The magnitude of this association varied across devices. These findings highlight the need for continued transparency and evaluation of financial relationships between physicians and device manufacturers.
