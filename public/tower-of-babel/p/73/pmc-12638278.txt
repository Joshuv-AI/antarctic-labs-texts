Postnatal choline supplementation improves sleep in a rat model of developmental alcohol exposure
Authors: Jaclyn B Hanson, Jessica A Baker, Daniel S Whittaker, Jennifer D Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) is an umbrella term used to describe physical, neuropathological, and behavioral alterations caused by prenatal alcohol exposure. Caretakers often report that children with FASD experience sleep problems. However, interventions to improve sleep have not been well‐studied in this population. Interestingly, supplementation with the essential nutrient choline can attenuate many behavioral alterations associated with prenatal alcohol exposure. Moreover, choline is a precursor to acetylcholine, a neurochemical that is also involved in sleep–wake modulation. Using an animal model, this study investigated the effects of developmental alcohol exposure on sleep and examined whether postnatal choline supplementation can modulate sleep alterations associated with FASD.MethodsSprague–Dawley rat pups received ethanol (5.25 g/kg/day, 11.9% v/v) or sham intubations from postnatal days (PD) 4–9, a period of development equivalent to the human third‐trimester brain growth spurt. Subjects then received subcutaneous injections with choline chloride (100 mg/kg/day) or saline from PD 10–30. On PD 32–37, subjects were individually housed to measure sleep–wake behaviors using the PiezoSleep Adapt‐A‐Base System. Sleep parameters, including sleep time, bout length, and number, were recorded.ResultsEthanol‐exposed subjects, particularly females, slept less during the dark cycle. In addition, females exposed to developmental alcohol exhibited more night‐to‐night variability in sleep duration, consistent with findings from clinical populations. In contrast, choline supplementation increased sleep duration, particularly among males exposed to developmental alcohol. Choline specifically increased sleep bout duration in ethanol‐exposed subjects during the dark cycle, suggesting that choline can modify sleep patterns among subjects exposed to prenatal alcohol.ConclusionsThese results illustrate that alcohol exposure during late gestation may lead to sleep disturbances and suggest that postnatal choline supplementation affects sleep quality. Importantly, this nutritional intervention was administered after the alcohol insult, suggesting that nutritional supplements in children with FASD may impact sleep problems.Keywords: adolescence, alcohol, choline, development, sex differences, sleep, teratology, treatment

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol consumption during pregnancy can lead to serious alterations in the developing fetus known as fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD). It is estimated that FASD affects 1.1%–5.0% of children in the United States (May et al., 2018; Popova et al., 2023), with global estimates of 7.7 per 1000 population (Lange et al., 2017). FASD is recognized as a major public health concern due to its impact on school, work, daily life, and overall health. Prenatal alcohol exposure can alter a variety of behavioral and cognitive domains, affecting intellectual performance, executive function, learning and memory, language, visual–spatial ability, motor skills, attention, and activity levels (Wozniak et al., 2019). While these domains have been well‐described, other critically important domains such as sleep, which impact quality of life and may further exacerbate behavioral and cognitive problems, have been understudied.

Interestingly, pediatric sleep problems are more prevalent (as high as 86%) in children with neurodevelopmental disorders, such as Autism Spectrum Disorders, Down Syndrome, and Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) (Jan et al., 2008; Robinson‐Shelton & Malow, 2016) compared to the general pediatric population (28%–36%) (Meltzer et al., 2014). Recent studies report many infants and children with FASD have sleep disturbances, such as difficulties falling asleep, waking up early, decreased rapid‐eye movement (REM) sleep, and increased sleep fragmentation (Inkelis & Thomas, 2018; Ipsiroglu et al., 2019; Troese et al., 2008). Moreover, studies have shown that there are higher occurrences of sleep onset delay, night wakings, parasomnias, and daytime sleepiness in children with FASD compared to typically developing children (Chen et al., 2012; Dylag et al., 2021; Inkelis et al., 2024). One study that utilized naturalistic observations found that children with FASD had chronic insomnia; specifically, 93% of children with FASD presented with circadian rhythm sleep disorders and 85% with restless sleep and excessive daytime sleepiness (Ipsiroglu et al., 2019). Moreover, a recent clinical study in our laboratory found greater night‐to‐night variability in total sleep time, wake after sleep onset, and number of wake bouts, specifically in children 8–10 years (Inkelis et al., 2024).

Animal model studies examining sleep following prenatal alcohol exposure have paralleled clinical findings. Limited preclinical studies show that prenatal alcohol exposure results in sleep disruptions, including increased sleep latency and decreased sleep in adulthood (Allen et al., 2005; Volgin & Kubin, 2012). Moreover, studies report increased sleep fragmentation, as measured by increased number of sleep bouts but decreased sleep bout length (Allen et al., 2005; Volgin & Kubin, 2012), as well as significant delays in the ability to synchronize circadian rhythms after light/dark cycle shifts in adult subjects exposed to prenatal alcohol exposure (Allen et al., 2005; Farnell et al., 2004; Sakata‐Haga et al., 2006; Sei et al., 2003). However, these studies have mostly focused on sleep in adulthood and have only included males. One study that examined sleep in juvenile and adolescent subjects found similar sleep disturbances after prenatal alcohol exposure and, interestingly, found that sleep disturbances increased as subjects aged into adolescence (Ipsiroglu et al., 2019), similar to our recent clinical finding (Inkelis et al., 2024). Thus, prenatal alcohol exposure leads to sleep disturbances in both clinical and preclinical studies of FASD. Unfortunately, interventions for sleep problems in children with FASD have not been adequately pursued, even though sleep disturbances may exacerbate other key behavioral and cognitive symptoms of FASD (Inkelis & Thomas, 2018; Jan et al., 2010; Sadeh et al., 2002; Soja et al., 2024).

Choline is an essential nutrient known to have an important role in brain development (Zeisel & da Costa, 2009) and exerts long‐term benefits on cognitive and behavioral functions (Akison et al., 2018; Derbyshire & Obeid, 2020; Zeisel, 2006; Zeisel & Niculescu, 2006). Choline is being investigated as an intervention to help reduce the severity of FASD, with clinical studies showing beneficial effects of both prenatal and postnatal choline supplementation on brain development, as well as neurobehavioral and cognitive function in children with FASD (Ernst et al., 2022). Preclinical studies illustrate that early choline supplementation can attenuate many of the behavioral alterations associated with prenatal alcohol exposure (Monk et al., 2012; Thomas et al., 2007, 2009, 2010). It has been found that prenatal choline supplementation can target multiple developmental outcomes, mitigating alcohol‐related reductions in birthweights, motor impairments, and cognitive deficits (Thomas et al., 2007, 2009, 2010). Early postnatal choline supplementation appears to target hippocampal‐related behaviors, attenuating alcohol‐induced hyperactivity, spatial learning deficits, and trace eyeblink conditioning (Thomas et al., 2007; Thomas & Tran, 2012). Even adolescent choline supplementation can mitigate some of the effects of prenatal alcohol exposure, targeting behaviors related to the prefrontal cortex, such as working memory (Schneider & Thomas, 2016). The fact that choline supplementation can improve brain function, behavior, and cognition even when choline is given after the alcohol insult is of particular interest, as most interventions or treatments occur in school‐aged children after prenatal alcohol exposure has already occurred.

Importantly, choline is a precursor to acetylcholine, which has been shown to play a role in sleep–wake modulation (Holst & Landolt, 2018; Hut & Van der Zee, 2011; Murillo‐Rodriguez et al., 2012), and early postnatal choline supplementation leads to long‐lasting modifications in cholinergic function (Monk et al., 2012). Interestingly, an animal study has shown that choline supplementation during gestation improves sleep patterns and sleep‐dependent memory consolidation in adult offspring compared to those who received prenatal choline‐sufficient diets (Cheng et al., 2008). In fact, animal subjects that receive choline insufficient diets show less sleep than those on choline sufficient diets (Szymusiak et al., 1993), and low choline levels may serve as biomarkers of chronic sleep deprivation and sleep fragmentation in animal models (Feng et al., 2016). Consistent with preclinical studies, a recent clinical study found lower levels of plasma choline were associated with increased daytime sleepiness (Pak et al., 2018). Taken together, these results suggest that choline supplementation has the potential to modulate sleep disruptions in individuals with FASD.

This study used an animal model to investigate whether choline supplementation can attenuate the effects of developmental alcohol exposure on sleep. Development of circuits involved in sleep architecture (sleep onset and transitions between sleep states) occurs during the third trimester in humans, which correlates with the early postnatal period in rodents (Canal‐Corretger et al., 2001; de Groot et al., 2024; Frank et al., 2017; Sladek et al., 2004). In fact, regulation of sleep/wake states occurs from postnatal day (PD) 2–10 in rodents (Blumberg et al., 2014; Cirelli & Tononi, 2015). Thus, we used an animal model of FASD in which neonatal pups are exposed to alcohol during the third trimester equivalent brain growth spurt. To examine sleep, this study used the piezoelectric system, which detects body movements and respiratory rate in a noninvasive manner, enabling the detection of sleep–wake parameters (Topchiy et al., 2022; Vanneau et al., 2021). This system has been validated in both mice and rats and is shown to have an accuracy of up to 90% to discriminate sleep–wake patterns, compared to EEG/EMG recordings (Topchiy et al., 2022; Vanneau et al., 2021; Yaghouby et al., 2016). Thus, this study utilized the noninvasive PiezoSleep system to collect sleep–wake patterns in the home cage of male and female subjects exposed to developmental ethanol and postnatal choline supplementation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

All procedures and behavioral testing measures used in this experiment were approved by the San Diego State University (SDSU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and in accordance with the National Institute of Health's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male and female Sprague–Dawley rats were obtained from Charles Rivers Laboratories (Hollister, CA, USA) and bred at the Center for Behavioral Teratology Animal Care facility at SDSU. Mating was determined by the presence of a seminal plug and the day designated gestational day (GD) 0. Pregnant dams were singly housed in a standard cage on a 12:12 h light–dark cycle in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled room with access to food and water ad libitum. Food was solid rat chow (Standard Rodent Diet, Product #5001; Lab Diet, Richmond, IN, USA), which contains 1.37 mg choline/g. The age of the pups was determined according to gestational age; thus, GD 22 was noted as postnatal day (PD) 0. On PD 1, litters were pseudorandomly culled to eight pups (four males and four females, when possible). On PD 7, pups received paw tattoos with nontoxic veterinary black ink (STONE Manufacturing and Supply Company, Kansas City, MO, USA) diluted with saline for identification purposes and to keep investigators blind to treatment conditions. Subjects were weaned on PD 21 and were housed individually on PD 28.

Study design

On PD 4, subjects were randomly assigned to experimental groups. To control for potential litter effects, only one sex pair (one male and one female) per treatment group was used from each litter. Subjects received either developmental ethanol (EtOH) exposure via intragastric intubation or sham intubations from PD 4–9. Half of each litter received either choline supplementation or saline control via subcutaneous injections from PD 10–30 (Figure 1A). Thus, this study used a 2 (EtOH, sham) × 2 (choline, saline) × 2 (female, male) design, with at least 10 subjects per group. The number of subjects per group is as follows: EtOH + Choline: N = 26 [F = 13, M = 13], EtOH + Saline: N = 24 [F = 12, M = 12], Sham + Choline: N = 29 [F = 16, M = 13], Sham + Saline: N = 31 [F = 16, M = 15]. Subjects were acclimated to single‐housing from PD 28–32, prior to sleep testing. Subjects were then placed in sleep chambers on PD 32, and 4 full days (light and dark cycle) of sleep–wake behaviors were captured from PD 33–36, with subjects removed on PD 37 (Figure 1B).

Experimental procedure and timeline. (A) Ethanol exposure occurred from postnatal day (PD) 4–9 and choline supplementation from PD 10–30. (B) On PD 32, subjects were singly housed in standard cages placed on top of the PiezoSleep Adapt‐A‐Base to acclimate to the sleep chamber environment during the light cycle. From PD 33–36, sleep–wake behaviors were recorded. For each day, the light cycle was recorded from ZT 0 to ZT 12 (6:00 am–6:00 pm). Rats are nocturnal, so sleep more during the light cycle; however, they are polyphasic sleepers, sleeping for multiple bouts during both light and dark cycles. Subjects were removed during the light cycle on PD 37. ZT, zeitgeber time. Created using Biorender.com.

Ethanol exposure

From PD 4–9, which is equivalent to the human third‐trimester brain growth spurt, all subjects received oral intubations, as previously described (Kelly & Lawrence, 2008; Thomas et al., 2007). Subjects in the EtOH group received 5.25 g/kg/day EtOH (95%; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in an artificial milk diet (11.9% v/v, 0.0275 mL/g). Intubations were split into two equal doses, 2 h apart, followed by two milk‐only intubations to control for reduced suckling behavior during intoxication (Kelly & Lawrence, 2008; Thomas et al., 2007). To control for any potential stress effects due to the intubation procedure, controls received sham intubations.

Blood alcohol concentration

On PD 6, blood was collected to determine peak blood alcohol concentration. Ninety minutes after the second intubation, 20 μL of blood was collected from the tail (Kelly & Lawrence, 2008; Thomas et al., 2007). Plasma was analyzed for alcohol levels using the Analox Analyzer (Model AM1; Analox Instruments, Lunenburg, MA, USA). Blood was also collected from sham controls to control for potential procedural stress.

Choline supplementation

From PD 10–30, half of the subjects received subcutaneous (s.c.) injections of 100 mg/kg/day choline chloride (6.667 mL; Product Code: F3060030; IFN 7‐01‐228; BalChem, New Hampton, NY, USA). This dose has previously shown to be the most effective at reducing behavioral alterations associated with developmental EtOH exposure (Thomas et al., 2007). The remaining subjects received injections of isovolumetric saline vehicle (0.85% sodium chloride solution; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Choline supplementation occurred during a period equivalent to early infancy and childhood in humans (Sengupta, 2013).

Sleep–wake behaviors

On PD 32, subjects were individually housed in standard plastic cages with ad libitum access to food and water. Cages were placed on top of the Adapt‐A‐Base pad (Signal Solutions, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA), which has a sensor that can detect pressure changes due to movement and breathing rate (Topchiy et al., 2022; Vanneau et al., 2021). Sleep recordings were conducted in a dedicated sleep behavior room free from any other rodent husbandry activities. Subjects were checked daily for food, water, and health, but otherwise left undisturbed during sleep recordings. Sleep signals were defined as a periodic rhythm of the breathing motion (~3 Hz) with consistent, low amplitude. Wakefulness was defined as larger amplitude variations and frequency changes. Behaviors were recorded for four complete 24 h periods, including both light and dark cycles (lights on at 06:00 am). Outcome measures included sleep duration, sleep bout length, number of sleep bouts, sleep variability, and bout length variability.

In addition to sleep–wake behaviors, activity levels were also collected. Activity levels were measured as intervals between 0 and 3, exported in 3‐min bins, as previously described (Gamble et al., 2023). Briefly, an activity level of 0 is assigned for all intervals detected as sleep. Calibrated activity values less than or equal to 1 correspond to quiet wake and low activity states. Activity values near 2 correspond to slow head movements, slow locomotion, or gentle rearing. High levels of activity up to 3 correspond to movement associated with more rapid locomotion, rearing, or grooming. The total activity by day and of all 4 days combined was analyzed during the light and dark cycle, and the full 24 h period. Finally, circadian rhythms were monitored by fitting each day's activity to a sine wave and by observing activity levels in relation to light/dark onset. The peak of the resulting wave is the peak activity, also known as acrophase.

Data analyses and statistics

The PiezoSleep software (version 2.11; Signal Solutions, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA) was used to record sleep/wake states and activity levels as previously described (Topchiy et al., 2022; Yaghouby et al., 2016). Sleep parameters were calculated using the SleepStats analysis software (version 2.28r; Signal Solutions, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA). Activity levels were calculated and analyzed using Activity Statistics (version R2p28; Signal Solutions, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA). Acrophase was calculated using the ClockLabs analysis software (version 6.1.15; Actimetrics, Inc., Wilmette, IL, USA).

Data were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with Statistical Packages for the Social Sciences software (SPSS Version 29; IMB Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with significance levels of p < 0.05 and trend toward significance at p < 0.08. Data were analyzed with repeated measure ANOVAs with EtOH Exposure (EtOH, sham), Choline Treatment (choline, saline), and Sex (female, male) as between‐subject factors and Day as a within‐subject repeated measure. When applicable, Student Newman–Keuls follow‐up post hoc tests were used. Mean (M) and standard error of the mean (SEM) are shown in graphs, which were generated in GraphPad Prism 8 (version 8.0.1; San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

Blood alcohol concentration

Mean ± SEM blood alcohol concentrations were 295.79 ± 50.43 mg/dL for EtOH‐exposed subjects and 301.67 ± 54.68 mg/dL for EtOH‐exposed subjects treated with choline supplementation. Blood alcohol concentration did not differ between groups [F 1,42 = 0.131, p = 0.719].

Body weight

The EtOH dose used in this study models a binge‐like alcohol exposure which can lead to a transient lag in weight gain, with eventual catch‐up (Monk et al., 2012). Similar to previous studies (Thomas et al., 2007), there were significant Day × EtOH [F 1,88 = 30.271, p < 0.001] and EtOH effects [F 1,88 = 111.882, p < 0.001] in body weight over the course of intubations (PD 4–9), with EtOH subjects weighing less than sham controls beginning on PD 5 (Figure S1A) and continuing until PD 15. Similar to previous studies (Thomas et al., 2007), there were no effects of choline supplementation on offspring growth [F 1,88 = 0.131, p = 0.72] from PD 10–30 (Figure S1B,C). However, there was a significant Day × Sex interaction on PD 10–30 body weight [F 1,88 = 25.480, p < 0.001], with males weighing more than females from PD 23–30 (data not shown).

Sleep–wake behaviors

Overall sleep duration

Males slept longer than females across the 24 h periods, resulting in a main effect of Sex [F 1,102 = 13.606, p < 0.001] (Figure 2A,B). No effects of EtOH or Choline, nor any interactions, were found. However, splitting the data into dark and light cycles revealed alterations in sleeping patterns. During the dark cycle, males exposed to ethanol had increased sleep duration when given choline supplementation compared to those that received saline (p < 0.05), resulting in an EtOH × Choline interaction [F 1,102 = 4.631, p < 0.05] (Figure 2C,D). There were no significant differences in average sleep duration during the light cycle for females or males (Figure 2E,F).

No significant differences were found with average sleep duration during the light cycle in (E) females or (F) males.

Sleep bout length and number

The sleep duration of a rodent depends on both the length and frequency of sleep bouts. During the 24 h period, males had longer sleep bouts compared to females, producing a main effect of Sex [F 1,102 = 8.806, p < 0.05] (Figure S2A,B). Conversely, when collapsed across groups, males had fewer sleep bouts than females [Sex: F 1,102 = 5.109, p < 0.05] (Figure S2C,D). Interestingly, group effects in sleep patterns were observed during the dark cycle. During the dark cycle, there were main effects of Choline [F 1,102 = 4.525, p < 0.05] and Sex [F 1,102 = 22.975, p < 0.001] on bout length (Figure 3A,B). Although the interaction of Choline × Sex failed to reach statistical significance, a main effect of Choline on bout length among males during the dark cycle was significant [F 1,49 = 3.912, p < 0.05], an effect driven by increases in bout length among ethanol‐exposed male subjects [EtOH + Choline > EtOH + Saline, p < 0.05] (Figure 3B). Thus, choline supplementation increased sleep bout lengths among males, but not females. During the dark cycle, there was also a main effect of Sex [F 1,102 = 5.109, p < 0.05] and a Sex × Choline interaction on bout number that approached significance [F 1,102 = 3.495, p = 0.064] (Figure 3C,D). Males that received choline supplementation had fewer sleep bouts compared to saline vehicle, leading to a main effect of Choline among males [F 1,49 = 5.67, p < 0.05], but not among females (Figure 3C,D). This effect was driven by a reduction in sleep bouts among ethanol‐exposed males (EtOH + Choline < EtOH + Saline, p < 0.05). There were no significant differences in bout length (Figure 4A,B) or number (Figure 4C,D) during the light cycle for either males or females.

While humans are monophasic sleepers that normally sleep for one continuous period during the dark cycle, rodents are polyphasic sleepers with multiple bouts of sleep and wakefulness during both the light and dark cycles, although overall sleep levels are greater during the light cycle. Previous studies in rodents have analyzed sleep bout duration and number as an indicator of sleep consolidation or sleep fragmentation (Lee et al., 2023; Rayan et al., 2024; Sethi et al., 2015). Therefore, we examined the number of bouts during the light and dark cycles in multiple bout length intervals. As there were many main effects of sex, specifically during the dark cycle, we separately analyzed males and females for these analyses.

Male subjects exhibited many significant interactions of EtOH × Choline at multiple bout length intervals during the dark cycle (Figure 3F). Choline shifted bout duration among ethanol‐exposed subjects, reducing short‐duration bouts (30–240 s) and increasing long‐duration bouts (480–1920 s) during the dark cycle. Specifically, ethanol‐exposed males that received choline had fewer 30–60 s sleep bouts compared to all other groups [p's < 0.05], producing a significant interaction of EtOH × Choline [F 1,49 = 4.343, p < 0.05]. Choline also reduced bout lengths among ethanol‐exposed males during the 60–120 and 120–240 s sleep bouts [EtOH + Choline < EtOH + Saline, p's < 0.05], producing significant Choline effects during the dark cycle [F 1,49 = 6.647, p < 0.05; F 1,49 = 3.408, p = 0.07, respectively] (Figure 3F). During the medium bout length of 240–480 s, there was a significant EtOH × Choline interaction [F 1,49 = 4.114, p < 0.05] due to opposing, but nonsignificant, directional effects of choline supplementation in ethanol‐exposed males versus controls. Conversely, during the longer bout durations, ethanol‐exposed males treated with choline had significantly more 480–960 [EtOH × Choline: F 1,49 = 4.718, p < 0.05] and 960–1920 s bouts [Choline: F 1,49 = 4.245, p < 0.05] compared to nonsupplemented ethanol‐exposed males [EtOH + Choline > EtOH + Saline, p's < 0.05] (Figure 3F).

During the dark cycle, females that received choline supplementation had a significantly higher number of 240–480 s sleep bouts [F 1,53 = 6.414, p < 0.05] during the dark cycle compared to nonsupplemented females (Figure 3E). During the light cycle, neither EtOH nor Choline affected bout durations among females (Figure 4E). In contrast, EtOH reduced [F 1,49 = 4.292, p < 0.05] and Choline increased [F 1,49 = 4.688, p < 0.05] the number of 480–960 s sleep bouts in males during the light cycle (Figure 4F).

Sleep duration and bout length across days

Sleep duration during the 24 h period did not significantly change with Day (Figure 5A,B). However, during the dark cycle, choline supplementation significantly increased sleep duration compared with saline vehicle when collapsed across sex, resulting in a main effect of Choline [F 1,102 = 4.056, p < 0.05] (Figure 5C,D). In addition, there was a Day × Sex × EtOH interaction [F 2.732,278.669 = 3.184, p < 0.05] and a Sex × EtOH × Choline interaction [F 1,102 = 3.898, p = 0.05]. Splitting the data by sex revealed that females exposed to developmental ethanol slept less on Day 3 [F 1,55 = 5.774, p < 0.05] and Day 4 [F 1,55 = 4.138, p < 0.05] compared to controls, producing a Day × EtOH interaction among females [F 3,159 = 3.418, p < 0.05] (Figure 5C). However, the choline‐related increase in sleep duration among ethanol‐exposed males did not vary with Day (Figure 5D). Examining sleep duration during the light cycle revealed a Day × EtOH interaction [F 2.575,262.624 = 3.335, p < 0.05] (Figure 5E,F). However, follow‐up analyses did not detect any significant main effects of EtOH on any specific day when collapsed across sex. However, ethanol‐exposed females tended to sleep more compared to controls on Day 4. While this effect was not statistically significant, when comparing this result to the decrease in sleep duration in ethanol‐exposed females during the dark cycle, this might indicate a shift in circadian rhythmicity. Day did not interact with any other variables on sleep bout length (Figure S3).

Sleep duration and bout length variability

We recently reported that prenatal alcohol exposure increases night‐to‐night variability in sleep duration and other sleep measures (Inkelis et al., 2024). Therefore, it is important to see whether similar findings are present in animal models. Females exposed to ethanol showed more day‐to‐day variability of sleep duration across 24 h periods compared to controls, resulting in a significant effect of EtOH in females [F 1,53 = 3.82, p = 0.05] (Figure 6A,B). Furthermore, females had greater day‐to‐day variability in sleep compared to males, producing a main effect of Sex during 24 h periods [F 1,102 = 25.69, p < 0.001] (Figure 6A,B), the dark [F 1,102 = 33.51, p < 0.001] (Figure 6C,D), and the light cycles [F 1,102 = 8.00, p < 0.001] (Figure 6E,F).

Average sleep variability. Sleep variability during 24 h periods in (A) females and (B) males. Females exposed to developmental alcohol exhibited more day‐to‐day variability in sleep duration (EtOH > Sham, *p = 0.05) during the 24 h periods, but not during (C) the dark cycle in or (E) the light cycle. There were no effects among males during (B) the 24 h period, (D) the dark cycle, or (F) the light cycle. Females also exhibited more day‐to‐day variability in sleep duration compared to males during 24 h periods, the dark cycle, and the light cycle.

Over 24 h periods, bout length variability did not significantly differ with EtOH, Choline, or Sex (Figure S4A,B). However, subjects exposed to ethanol showed greater night‐to‐night variability in bout length compared to sham controls during the dark cycle, resulting in a main effect of EtOH that approached significance [F 1,102 = 3.48, p = 0.06] (Figure S4C,D). Females also exhibited more variability in bout length compared to males, leading to a main effect of Sex during the dark cycle [F 1,102 = 5.43, p < 0.05]. No significant effects were observed during the light cycle (Figure S4E,F).

Activity levels

EtOH exposure increased activity across the 24 h period compared to shams [F 1,102 = 3.97, p < 0.05]. Although the EtOH × Sex interaction only trended toward significance [F 1,102 = 3.376, p = 0.069], this effect was driven by the females [F 1,53 = 6.853, p < 0.05] (Figure 7A). During the dark cycle, females (Figure 7C) were more active than males (Figure 7D), resulting in a significant effect of Sex [F 1,102 = 11.877, p < 0.001]. There was also a significant interaction between EtOH × Sex [F 1,102 = 5.907, p < 0.05] and a trend toward a significant Choline effect [F 1,102 = 3.242, p = 0.07] during the dark cycle. Female subjects exposed to developmental ethanol had increased activity during the dark cycle compared to female controls [F 1,53 = 9.457, p < 0.01] (Figure 7C). Choline supplementation tended to reduce activity level in all groups except Male Shams. Although these effects did not reach statistical significance, trends were seen in females, regardless of developmental ethanol exposure [Choline: F 1,53 = 3.07, p = 0.08] (Figure 7C) and in males exposed to Ethanol [EtOH × Choline: F 1,49 = 3.204, p = 0.08] (Figure 7D). During the light cycle, there was a significant effect of Sex [F 1,102 = 11.105, p < 0.001], with males (Figure 7F) showing more activity during the light cycle than females (Figure 7E).

Circadian rhythm

Acrophase is used to measure circadian rhythm patterns by calculating the time during which peak activity occurs. Because many of the sleep problems in FASD can be classified as circadian rhythm sleep disorders (Ipsiroglu et al., 2019), it is important to see whether alterations in sleep patterns are related to alterations in circadian rhythm. There were no significant effects of Day, EtOH, Choline, nor any interactions found on acrophase (Figure 8). However, there was a trend toward a significant effect of Sex [F 1,102 = 3.217, p = 0.07], as females tended to have later acrophases compared to males (Figure 8A). While not significant, ethanol‐exposed subjects showed a pattern of delayed acrophase in both sexes; however, the ethanol‐exposed males that received choline supplementation did not have delayed acrophase and were more similar to controls (Figure 8B).

DISCUSSION

The current study aimed to examine the effects of developmental ethanol and choline supplementation on sleep in male and female adolescent subjects. We found sex‐specific effects of developmental ethanol exposure and choline supplementation on sleep. Specifically, early alcohol exposure affected sleep in females, but not males, whereas choline supplementation affected sleep predominantly in male subjects exposed to developmental ethanol exposure. Moreover, differences in sleep behavior were predominantly present during the dark cycle, which is the active period in rodents; limited effects of developmental ethanol or choline supplementation were seen during the light cycle. These results demonstrate that ethanol exposure during late gestation (3rd trimester equivalent) and early postnatal choline supplementation exert long‐lasting and sex‐dependent effects on sleep in adolescent animals.

Significant sleep changes associated with early alcohol were predominantly observed among the females. Females that were exposed to developmental ethanol slept less during the dark cycle on the last 2 days of testing (Days 3 and 4). This change occurred without significant changes in sleep bout lengths. Changes across days likely contributed to increased day‐to‐day sleep variability among ethanol‐exposed females. Interestingly, these findings parallel clinical data, as we have reported increased night‐to‐night variability in sleep duration among individuals with FASD, despite similar overall sleep durations compared to controls (Inkelis et al., 2024). Clinically, increased night‐to‐night sleep variability significantly mediates behavioral problems and impaired episodic memory (Soja et al., 2024), suggesting that this dysregulation contributes to performance in multiple domains. Increased day‐to‐day variability in sleep may represent poor sleep regulation and/or sensitivity to environmental zeitgebers. However, in the present study, ethanol‐exposed females were also hyperactive during the dark cycle; thus, reduced sleep may also reflect a general increase in arousal. It also cannot be discounted that this ethanol‐related reduction in sleep duration represents a change as the subjects became more familiar with the sleep chamber.

Sleep variability could also be related to hormonal fluctuations. Sleep patterns in females change over the course of the estrous cycle due to natural hormone fluctuations, an effect that is seen mainly during the dark cycle (active period) in rodents (Carrier et al., 2017). In the present study, we examined sleep from postnatal days 33–37, which is in the middle of puberty in rodents and during a developmental period in which prenatal ethanol exposure can alter hormones such as estradiol and progesterone (Sliwowska et al., 2016). Indeed, sex hormones play a role in sleep–wake organization during early development, and sleep–wake organization in female, but not male, rats is sensitive to changes in sex hormones (Mallampalli & Carter, 2014). This is also confirmed in clinical populations, in which women typically complain about sleep problems during periods of sex hormone fluctuation such as puberty (Mong & Cusmano, 2016). Females may also be more affected by maturational changes in circadian rhythm that occur during adolescence. For example, similar to clinical findings (Bailey & Silver, 2014), female rodents go through puberty‐related circadian shift delays at an earlier age than males (Dib et al., 2021). Although developmental ethanol exposure did not significantly alter acrophase, females displayed a later acrophase compared to males, and there was a nonsignificant tendency for ethanol‐exposed females to sleep more during the light cycle on day 4. Thus, reductions in sleep duration may relate to premature shift delays in circadian rhythmicity that are more robust among females. Finally, although ethanol affected sleep in females only, choline supplementation did not modify these effects, although there were trends for choline to reduce activity levels in both ethanol and control subjects during the dark cycle.

Surprisingly, we did not find effects of ethanol on sleep in males during adolescence. While there have a been limited studies of sleep using animal models of FASD, most have only investigated male subjects and reported ethanol‐related changes in sleep (Allen et al., 2005; Farnell et al., 2004; Ipsiroglu et al., 2019; Sakata‐Haga et al., 2006; Sei et al., 2003; Volgin & Kubin, 2012). Only one previous paper used both male and female subjects, although they did not analyze sex differences (Wilson et al., 2016). Importantly, most previous studies have only examined sleep in adult subjects exposed to prenatal alcohol (Allen et al., 2005; Farnell et al., 2004; Sakata‐Haga et al., 2006; Sei et al., 2003; Volgin & Kubin, 2012; Wilson et al., 2016). In fact, only one study focused on the effects of prenatal alcohol exposure on sleep during adolescence and reported delayed sleep latencies and less total sleep time in prenatal ethanol‐exposed males during preadolescence (PD 23–25) and adolescence (PD 35–36) (Ipsiroglu et al., 2019). Differences in results between the present study and the Ipsiroglu et al. (2019) study could be due to different developmental timing of ethanol exposure, as well as different methods of collecting sleep data. The Ipsiroglu et al. (2019) study gave pregnant dams an ethanol liquid diet from gestational days 1 through 20 (equivalent to human first and second trimester), whereas the present study administered ethanol in a milk diet to neonates on postnatal days 4 through 9 (equivalent to human third trimester). Furthermore, the previous study used video recordings to observe sleep behaviors for only 1 day, whereas the present study used computer software to analyze activity and breathing levels to determine sleep behaviors over 4 days. Importantly, the software used in the present study differentiates quiet resting and sleep. It may be more challenging to make that distinction in visual observations, although it is also possible that observations may pick up behavioral differences that the automated system does not.

In contrast to ethanol effects, we did find significant choline effects that were specific to ethanol‐exposed males. During the dark cycle, choline supplementation increased sleep duration and reduced activity among ethanol‐exposed males. The increase in sleep duration related to a shift in longer sleep bouts among ethanol‐exposed males supplemented with choline. Thus, although ethanol did not significantly alter sleep among males, choline increased sleep and reduced sleep fragmentation, particularly in ethanol‐exposed males. Given that choline's effects were most robust among ethanol‐exposed subjects, these data suggest that choline has the potential to improve sleep among individuals with FASD.

One limitation of the present study is that sleep behavior was measured for a limited number of days and only during a limited period of adolescence. Exploring sleep during adolescence is crucial as adolescence is characterized by many developmental psychophysiological changes, including dramatic changes in sleep behaviors (Colrain & Baker, 2011; Dib et al., 2021). Sleep problems including parasomnias, bedtime resistance, and symptoms of insomnia are higher for adolescents with FASD compared to typically developing adolescents (Ipsiroglu et al., 2019; Jan et al., 2010). Sleep disturbances may have profound implications in cognition and behavior, such as decreased performance in school and a higher risk for developing psychopathology and alcohol and drug abuse among others (Colrain & Baker, 2011). However, examining sleep behavior for a longer duration during this critical period of development and into adulthood would help elucidate how developmental alcohol affects sleep across maturation, how sex differences change with age, and how choline supplementation influences sleep.

Another limitation of the present study is that the sleep system required that the subjects be individually housed to collect sleep data. Because rodents are social creatures, separating the subjects may have caused stress, which may have influenced sleep. While the current study only tested for sleep, future studies may want to look at cortisol levels before and after collecting sleep measures to look at potential changes in stress. Finally, the activity data from the sleep system is based on overall motion energy signal, and more discrete measures of activity (i.e., wheel running) may provide more accurate means for analyzing activity level and circadian rhythmicity.

In sum, the present study was the first to examine the effects of postnatal choline supplementation on sleep in both males and females exposed to ethanol during development, and results illustrate that developmental ethanol exposure and early nutritional factors can have lasting effects on sleep behavior that are evident during adolescence. Importantly, we found sex‐specific effects of sleep on almost all sleep measures tested, demonstrating the importance of examining sex as a factor in sleep studies. Moreover, there were also important interactions of sex with developmental ethanol exposure and choline supplementation. While females were more susceptible to ethanol‐induced sleep alterations, choline supplementation did not mitigate these effects. In contrast, ethanol‐related reductions in sleep among males were not statistically significant; however, ethanol‐exposed males who received choline exhibited longer sleep durations, reduced sleep fragmentation, and decreased activity.

These findings illustrate that alcohol exposure during late gestation may disrupt sleep patterns among adolescents. Most clinical studies examine sleep in children with prenatal alcohol exposure (Inkelis & Thomas, 2018; Ipsiroglu et al., 2019; Troese et al., 2008), but few examine whether sleep problems persist, despite limited reports of poor sleep among adults with FASD (Coles et al., 2024). We previously reported that sleep problems become more pronounced as children with prenatal alcohol exposure age (Inkelis et al., 2024). Adolescence is a period of dynamic changes in sleep patterns and regulation (Carrier et al., 2017) and given that sleep problems can contribute to poor daytime functioning (behavioral, cognitive, emotional), it is important to examine sleep quality in adolescents with FASD and to determine if sex differences emerge during this period. If so, sex‐specific approaches to improving sleep among adolescents may be required. Moreover, early postnatal nutritional factors also have long‐lasting effects on sleep, suggesting that nutritional status can be an important regulator of sleep quality. Although there were complex interactions of sex, alcohol exposure, and choline on sleep behavior in the present study, the finding that choline specifically modified sleep in males exposed to developmental alcohol suggests that choline or other nutritional supplements may improve sleep quality in individuals with FASD. Choline supplementation has been shown to improve cognitive functioning among children with FASD (Ernst et al., 2022; Schneider & Thomas, 2016; Thomas et al., 2007, 2010) and it will be important to determine whether behavioral improvements relate to improvements in sleep. Given the importance of sleep on quality of life, elucidation of interventions that can improve sleep among individuals with prenatal alcohol exposure is of critical importance.
