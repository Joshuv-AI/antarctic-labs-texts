Integrating explainable artificial intelligence with multiomics systems biology and electronic health record data mining for personalized drug repurposing in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Mohammadsadeq Mottaqi, Pengyue Zhang, Lei Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by region- and patient-specific molecular heterogeneity, which hinders therapeutic design. In this study, we introduce PRISM-ML (PRecision-medicine using Interpretable Systems and Multiomics with Machine Learning), an open-source integrated analysis pipeline that combines interpretable machine learning with systems biology and electronic health records data mining to elucidate the molecular diversity of AD and predict promising drug repurposing opportunities. First, we integrated and harmonized transcriptomic (bulk RNA-seq) and genomic (genome-wide association study) data from 2105 brain samples, each with matched data from the same individual (1363 AD patients, 742 controls; 9 tissues), sourced from three independent studies. Random forest classifiers with SHapley Additive exPlanations identified patient-specific biomarkers; unsupervised clustering resolved 36 molecularly distinct subtissues (defined as clusters of samples within a brain tissue that share a specific expression pattern); and gene–gene coexpression networks prioritized 262 high-centrality bottleneck genes as putative regulators of dysregulated pathways. Next, knowledge graph-based drug repurposing predicted six Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs that simultaneously target multiple bottleneck genes and multiple AD-relevant pathways. Notably, in a large US de-identified insurance-claims database (n = 364 733), exposure to promethazine, one of the candidate drugs, was associated with a 57%–62% lower incidence of AD versus an active antihistamine comparator (adjusted hazard ratio 0.38; inverse-probability weighted 0.43; both P < .001), providing real-world support for its repurposing potential. In summary, PRISM-ML, as an explainable multiomics analysis pipeline, is readily transferable to other complex diseases, advancing precision medicine.Keywords: machine learning, computational biology, GWAS, Transcriptomics, personalized medicine, electronic health records

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting millions worldwide, with elusive underlying molecular mechanisms [1]. Current drugs offer only symptomatic relief, failing to target the disease due to its profound molecular heterogeneity across patients and brain tissues [2]. Multiomics technologies—integrating transcriptomics, genomics, and proteomics from real patients—offer a powerful means to unravel this complexity, capturing interactions missed by single-omics studies. However, translating these datasets into actionable therapeutic strategies remains a significant challenge, underscoring the need for innovative approaches beyond traditional methods [1].

Early AD studies focused on single-omics datasets, but these capture only a slice of the disease’s molecular landscape; even recent multiomics machine learning (ML) and deep learning models that nominate biomarkers or drug targets remain largely “black boxes,” masking their biological rationale [3–5]. A notable exception combined network analysis-based feature selection with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [6] to produce a transparent 10-gene panel from one microarray cohort [7]. Building on that idea, we developed PRISM-ML (PRecision-medicine using Interpretable Systems and Multiomics with Machine Learning), which applies SHAP at the patient level to quantify both the magnitude and direction of the contribution of each gene to an AD prediction, enabling truly personalized biomarker discovery beyond global feature-importance scores. This directional insight that SHAP provides is absent in traditional feature importance methods such as the mean-decrease-in-impurity of random forest (RF).

While SHAP excels at identifying patient-specific biomarker genes, it provides a static view of importance without capturing the dynamic interactions between these genes. To understand how these disparate biomarkers might collectively drive disease pathology, a systems-level approach is required. Network biology offers a complementary framework by modeling diseases as perturbations of complex molecular interactomes. Gene–gene interaction networks, particularly those derived from coexpression patterns, map the functional relationships between genes to reveal the underlying architecture of biological systems [8]. In such networks, only a small subset of genes acts as critical hubs or “bottlenecks”—high-centrality nodes that interconnect multiple functional modules [8]. The centrality-lethality rule suggests these bottleneck genes are often essential for network integrity; their dysregulation can lead to system-wide failures, making them exceptionally attractive therapeutic targets for complex diseases [9]. Therefore, integrating patient-specific biomarkers from SHAP with a network-based analysis of their interactions provides a more powerful strategy for identifying key regulatory points for intervention.

Moreover, many studies propose drugs for AD treatment without robust validation, often relying on cell lines or animal models that may not reflect human disease, while rarely considering patient-specific details crucial for personalized medicine—leaving a gap between computational predictions and clinical relevance [10].

To address these limitations, we developed PRISM-ML, a novel open-source analysis pipeline integrating interpretable ML, statistical analyses, network biology, and electronic health record (EHR) data mining to transform large-scale multiomics data into actionable insights on AD’s molecular complexity and real-world patient responses.

From matched RNA-seq and genome-wide association study (GWAS) profiles of 2105 postmortem brain samples (1363 AD, 742 controls) across 9 tissues, PRISM-ML first delineates molecularly distinct subtissues—defined as clusters of samples (patients) within an anatomical region that share a coherent expression pattern—to capture finer-grained pathological heterogeneity than is possible by analyzing broad tissue regions alone. It then pinpoints patient- and region-specific biomarkers and uses gene–gene interaction networks to prioritize “bottleneck” genes—high-centrality nodes that are critical for communication within biological networks and are therefore hypothesized to be powerful leverage points for therapeutic intervention [9]. A knowledge-graph screening linked these genes to six Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved multitarget drugs, whose protective association we confirmed in 364 733 real-world patients. By uniting molecular heterogeneity with population-scale evidence, PRISM-ML advances personalized, multitarget therapy design for AD and is readily extensible to other complex diseases.

This manuscript is organized as follows: The Results introduces the PRISM-ML analysis pipeline and presents findings on data processing, subtissue stratification, biomarker and bottleneck gene identification, drug repurposing, and EHR data mining. Discussion interprets findings, limitations, and therapeutic impact; Methods and Supplementary Materials details technical aspects.

Material and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

We integrated matched bulk RNA-sequencing and GWAS data from three large-scale AD brain tissue studies [Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) [11], Mount Sinai Brain Bank (MSBB) [12], and Mayo RNA-seq study (MAYO) [13]] obtained via the Accelerating Medicines Partnership® Program for Alzheimer's Disease (AMP-AD) Knowledge Portal [14] comprising 2105 postmortem brain samples (1363 AD and 742 controls) across 9 tissues from 835 unique individuals. Detailed data preprocessing and harmonization steps and dataset statistics are provided in the Supplementary Materials section 1 and Table 1.

Machine learning classification model and model interpretation

Each sample was classified via leave-one-out RF models using the 5000 most-variable genes, and SHAP scores defined sample-specific high-impact biomarkers; for hyperparameters and detailed interpretation, see Supplementary Materials section 2.

Subtissue identification and subtissue-specific biomarkers and genetic drivers

Confident AD samples per tissue were K-means-clustered into four subtissues using unions of patient-level biomarker sets as features, with subtissue-specific biomarkers defined by intersecting SHAP-derived lists within each subtissue (4–80 genes, see Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S1) and subtissue-specific genetic drivers identified from enriched mutation rates of 96 AD-associated variants via Fisher’s exact tests and permutations (see Supplementary Table S1). Clustering algorithms, validation metrics, Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) selection, and statistical details are provided in Supplementary Materials section 3.

Throughout the manuscript, subtissue refers exclusively to the K-means–derived cluster of AD patients within a given tissue; we avoid the generic terms cluster or subgroup when referring to these entities.

Statistical analysis and gene set enrichment

Differential expression was analyzed using Limma [15], identifying genes with |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and Bonferroni-adjusted P < .05 (see Supplementary Materials sections 4 and 8 for full statistical methods).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) [16]. We specifically queried the following annotated gene set collections: Gene ontology (GO) terms (including the Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component domains), Reactome pathways, and disease-associated terms from the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) and KEGG_DISEASE databases.

Gene–gene interaction network construction and critical bottleneck genes

Subtissue-specific coexpression networks were built using Weighted Gene Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA) [17] with Pearson correlations, retaining edges for ~0.5% density, and shortest paths connected biomarkers to genetic drivers to identify message-passing genes. Centrality metrics prioritized 262 bottleneck genes after filtering well-studied AD genes; parameter details and justifications are in Supplementary Materials section 5. A gene was classified as a “well-studied AD gene” if a PubMed search query [“Alzheimer’s disease” AND “(gene symbol)”] returned ≥10 publications. Genes meeting this criterion and genes ranked lower than these genes were excluded from further consideration as novel bottleneck candidates. The complete list of these well-studied AD genes, along with their PubMed citation counts, is provided in Supplementary Table S7. See Supplementary Materials section 5 for further details.

Drug target identification and repurposing drugs

We treated Bioteque precomputed embeddings [18] as 128-dimensional, L2-normalized vectors and scored gene–compound relatedness with cosine similarity for each metapath separately. Each metapath represents a distinct biological relationship (direct binding, codependency, coexpression, and protein–protein interaction pathways). The 128-dimensional embeddings capture both direct and multistep network relationships. For each bottleneck gene, we kept the top five compounds per metapath, then united results across metapaths (preserving the max similarity per gene–compound pair). We then set the threshold at cosine ≥0.65 (elbow of the empirical distribution; Supplementary Fig. S1b) and restricted to FDA-approved drugs, finally retaining compounds linked to ≥2 bottleneck genes to prioritize multitarget candidates. This analysis yielded six candidates; see Supplementary Materials section 6 for implementation details, metapath definitions, and thresholding details.

Pharmacoepidemiologic validation

A retrospective active-comparator cohort study using Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics Data Mart Database (2007–2021) compared promethazine (n = 353 856) to cyproheptadine (n = 10 877) exposure in individuals aged ≥60, employing Cox models and inverse probability weighting to estimate AD incidence risk [defined by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes]. Inclusion/exclusion criteria, covariates, and full model results are detailed in Supplementary Materials section 7 and Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Candidate drugs for validation were prioritized based on cohort size to ensure analytical robustness. We required a minimum exposed cohort size of 10 000 individuals to achieve sufficient power for detecting differences in AD incidence. Only promethazine met this criterion among the six candidate drugs identified by the PRISM-ML pipeline.

Results

Overview of the PRecision-medicine using Interpretable Systems and Multiomics with Machine Learning pipeline

In this study, we developed PRISM-ML, a four-stage integrated analysis pipeline that fuses transcriptomic and genomic information to dissect AD heterogeneity (Fig. 1). Leveraging matched bulk RNA-seq and GWAS data from 2105 postmortem samples spanning 9 brain tissues (see Table 1), PRISM-ML first identifies sample-specific biomarker signatures and captures AD region-dependent molecular variation. The analysis pipeline traces dysregulated genes and pathways through network analysis and predicts repurposable drugs followed by EHR data mining to validate drug effects.

PRISM-ML: integrating interpretable machine learning, multiomics systems biology, and EHR data mining for AD drug repurposing, showing (a) multiomics integration and subtissue identification in which harmonized bulk RNA-seq and GWAS data from 2105 postmortem brain samples (1363 AD patients and 742 controls) across nine tissues were used to train random forest classifiers with SHAP to derive ≈175 patient-specific biomarkers per sample and stratify each tissue into four molecularly distinct subtissues (36 total) whose shared high-impact genes and enriched AD-associated variants define subtissue-specific biomarkers and genetic drivers; (b) subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction networks connecting biomarkers and genetic drivers via intermediate “message-passing” genes (shortest paths), with topological metrics (e.g. betweenness centrality) prioritizing 262 high-centrality bottleneck genes enriched in synaptic transmission, ion transport, and extracellular matrix organization; and (c) knowledge-graph–based drug repurposing and pharmacoepidemiologic validation, in which six FDA-approved multitarget drugs (e.g. promethazine and disopyramide) are nominated and promethazine exposure in a cohort of 364,733 individuals is associated with 57%–62% reduced AD risk (adjusted hazard ratio 0.38; inverse probability weighted 0.43; both P < .001).

PRISM-ML comprises four steps. First, for each sample, a dedicated RF classifier was trained on the remaining 2104 samples via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). SHAP values [6] quantified the contribution of each feature (gene) to the AD-versus-control prediction, labeling genes exceeding a threshold as high-impact biomarkers, yielding a personalized set of ~175 genes per sample (Methods section 2).

Second, unsupervised clustering of AD expression profiles within each tissue partitioned samples into distinct “subtissues” to capture local patterns, resulting in 36 subtissues total. Subtissue-specific biomarkers were identified by selecting genes with consistently high SHAP magnitudes across AD samples in each subtissue. In parallel, subtissue-specific genetic drivers were pinpointed by comparing mutation rates of established AD genes against the background population.

Third, subtissue-specific gene–gene coexpression networks were constructed, linking biomarkers and genetic drivers via shortest paths. High-betweenness-centrality “bottleneck” (hub) genes—key mediators in dysregulated pathways—were prioritized as drug targets. The union across subtissues yielded 262 unique bottleneck genes (per-subtissue bottleneck lists are preserved and reported; the union is used only as a non-redundant catalog for drug inference).

Finally, knowledge-graph embeddings of 30 million biomedical relations [10] predicted six FDA-approved multitarget drugs (e.g. promethazine, disopyramide, nicardipine) modulating multiple bottleneck genes and AD pathways. In an independent de-identified insurance-claims cohort (n = 364 733) with active-comparator design, promethazine exposure was associated with 57%–62% lower AD incidence versus a matched antihistamine comparator (adjusted hazard ratio 0.38; inverse-probability weighted 0.43; both P < .001), supporting its repurposing potential.

PRISM-ML delivers an open-source, interpretable pipeline that unites patient-level biomarkers, region-specific pathology, and multitarget drug repurposing. By replacing black-box predictions with SHAP-based explanations and network context, it also uncovers molecular axes shared with comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease (Results section 5.2). The pipeline offers a scalable template for turning multiomics data into actionable therapies for AD and other complex disorders.

PRecision-medicine using Interpretable Systems and Multiomics with Machine Learning identifies patient-level Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers

First, we compiled a multiomics dataset by merging matched bulk RNA-sequencing data and individual-level genotype data (GWAS) from the same donors in three large-scale AD brain tissue studies: the ROSMAP [11], the MSBB [1, 2], and the MAYO [13]. This integration yielded 2105 postmortem brain tissue samples—1363 classified as AD patients and 742 as controls—originating from 307 males and 528 females (see Methods section 1). Within each tissue, all samples originated from a single cohort (see Supplementary Table S4); their distribution is summarized in Table 1. By ensuring that both the transcriptomic and genomic data were derived from the same individuals, this harmonized dataset provides a robust foundation for downstream analyses, capturing patient-level molecular variation across diverse brain tissues. Figure 2a presents a t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) plot visualizing the distribution of total samples in the data using 15 300 genes as input features.

We trained RF classifiers—using a LOOCV scheme with the top 5000 most variable genes across the dataset as features (details in Methods section 2). At each iteration, a model was trained on every sample except a single test case; the withheld sample was then classified as AD or control by the model. Table 2 summarizes the strong class-specific performance of the models. Additionally, we obtained a predictive score for each sample (score ranging from 0 to 1) determined by the RF classifier. The score indicated the likelihood of the sample belonging to the AD group.

SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis pinpoints personalized biomarker sets

To explain each model’s prediction, we applied SHAP [6] which, for each predictive model, computes a gene-level “importance” score reflecting how much that gene/feature influences the classification decision. For every held-out sample, we ranked genes by their SHAP value and flagged those genes that exceeded the cutoff (defined in Methods section 2) as sample-specific biomarkers (high-impact genes), indicating genes whose expression had a decisive impact on that sample’s classification (see Supplementary Fig. S1a).

Across the 2105 samples, this procedure yielded a mean of ≈175 high-impact genes per sample, capturing unique molecular signatures that separate the diseased sample from the control (healthy) samples. Figure 2b illustrates the distribution of SHAP values for several genes across all the models, highlighting the differential impact of genes on the decisions of the models across AD samples.

To improve analytical reliability, we retained only AD samples that the model classified with high confidence—those with a predictive score > 0.82 (the mean cohort predictive score). This filter produced 720 confidently classified AD cases, excluding lower-confidence “borderline” samples without introducing systematic bias. All downstream comparisons therefore focus on this robust subset, and we refer to their high-impact gene lists as patient-specific biomarkers. Figure 2c presents a t-SNE plot visualizing the distribution of the 720 robustly classified AD cases using 5000 highly variable genes as input features. The evident tissue-level segregation in both Fig. 2a and c suggests that the RNA-seq data inherently reflect tissue-specific differences.

High-SHapley Additive exPlanations genes conversion into tissue-specific biomarker sets

Using the 720 confidently classified AD patients, we grouped them by their tissue of origin (one of 9 brain tissues). Each AD sample had its own set of high-SHAP biomarkers within each tissue. We then intersected the biomarker sets of all AD samples belonging to that tissue, identifying “common” biomarkers—genes deemed critical across all individuals within the same brain tissue. On average, we found 1 to 36 such genes per tissue, with temporal cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex showing the greatest overlap, reflecting regionally conserved dysregulation (Fig. 2d). As shown in Fig. 2e, the tissue-specific biomarker sets display varying degrees of overlap across different brain tissues, with darker cells indicating more shared genes. These findings confirm that, despite patient-level variability, there are consistently influential genes within specific brain tissues, implicating shared mechanisms in AD pathology.

Tissue-specific biomarkers demonstrate clear biological significance

To evaluate the biological relevance of our RF–SHAP approach, we combined tissue-specific biomarker sets (56 genes total) and performed GSEA. Enriched pathways and GO terms included membrane-binding functions (e.g. integrin, heme, channel, symporter binding) and extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 2f and g), critical for synaptic integrity and disrupted in AD [19]. Dysregulated ion transport (Fig. 2h) aligns with Aβ-induced neuronal hyperexcitability [20], confirming our biomarkers’ link to AD pathophysiology and demonstrating that PRISM-ML identifies relevant molecular features, not random signals.

Subtissue-specific biomarkers and genetic drivers reveal regional heterogeneity in Alzheimer’s disease

Four subtissues per brain tissue capture finer molecular diversity

After identifying patient-specific biomarkers, we aimed to examine the molecular heterogeneity within each brain tissue at a more detailed level. For each of the nine brain tissues, we compiled a set of genes by combining the patient-specific biomarker sets from all samples within that tissue, taking their union to ensure that all relevant genes were included. We then applied K-means clustering to the transcriptomic profiles of these samples (within a tissue), using the expression levels of the compiled gene set as input features.

We term these molecularly coherent clusters “subtissues” to emphasize that they represent distinct transcriptional states within a single, anatomically defined brain tissue. A subtissue is therefore defined as a group of samples from the same brain tissue that are clustered together based on the expression of AD-relevant biomarkers, capturing a shared component of the molecular heterogeneity of the disease. It is important to note that these 36 subtissues were identified exclusively from the 720 confidently classified AD samples. Control samples were not included in this clustering analysis, as our goal was to resolve molecular heterogeneity specifically within the AD population.

Using clustering evaluation metrics (detailed in Methods section 3.1), we determined that four stable subtissues per tissue provided an optimal representation of molecular diversity. This process resulted in a total of 36 subtissues across the 9 brain tissues.

To ensure that the identified subtissues reflected biological rather than technical variation, we rigorously assessed potential confounders. We found no significant association between technical batch assignment and subtissue identity within any tissue after false discovery rate correction (all q > 0.05; Supplementary Table S5). Furthermore, analysis of postmortem interval (PMI) across subtissues revealed no significant differences (all P > .05) and effect sizes were negligible (epsilon-squared <0.06 for all tissues; Supplementary Table S6). These results confirm that the molecularly defined subtissues are not driven by these technical artifacts (see Fig. S3). Supplementary Materials section 3.1 provides further details.

Subtissue-specific biomarker sets expose localized Alzheimer’s disease mechanisms

To identify subtissue-specific AD biomarkers, we intersected patient-specific high-impact gene sets (~175 genes per patient from RF-SHAP analysis) across AD samples in each subtissue, yielding shared sets of 4–80 genes per subtissue (Fig. 3a). This size variation underscores distinct pathobiology across subtissues.

Aggregating biomarker sets across all 36 subtissues yielded 183 unique biomarkers (full list and per-subtissue statistics in Supplementary Table S1). Although global expression profiles naturally cluster by anatomical region (Fig. 2a), filtering with SHAP removes this baseline signal and retains only genes that separate AD from control within each region. Consequently, the biomarkers expose disease-specific disturbances—e.g. those unique to the cerebellum versus the neocortex—rather than constitutive tissue differences. The overlap heatmap (Fig. 3b) underscores this distinction: some biomarker sets are almost exclusive to a single subtissue, while others recur across several regions, capturing both local and shared molecular pathology.

Functional enrichment confirms the importance of subtissue biomarkers

To evaluate the biological significance of these 183 aggregated biomarkers, we performed GSEA. The full per-subtissue breakdown (e.g. the top genes in each subtissue) is available in Supplementary Table S1. This analysis showed associations of the gene set with diverse disease processes, including AD, type 2 diabetes, chronic renal failure, breast cancer, and hypertension (Fig. 3c). These links may reflect shared pathways in metabolic disorders and neurodegeneration—such as insulin signaling deficits and dysregulated apoptosis implicated in AD [21, 22]—or broader comorbid mechanisms. The significant enrichment of AD in the aggregated biomarker set shows that the RF–SHAP approach successfully prioritizes well-established AD genes, underscoring the reliability of the pipeline.

GO terms strongly enriched cell ion transport, differentiation, and angiogenesis, processes that are recognized as associated with the pathology of AD [20, 23] (Fig. 3d). The analysis also highlighted their involvement in cell secretion and extracellular matrix organization, processes critical in AD progression [19] (Fig. 3e). Moreover, reactome pathway analysis revealed that biomarkers participate in signal transduction, carbohydrate metabolism, and extracellular matrix organization, further underscoring their comprehensive impact on cellular and molecular functions relevant to AD [19, 24] (Fig. 3f).

Differential expression underscores additional Alzheimer’s disease-related genes

As a complementary approach, we used Limma [15] to compare AD versus control expression levels across the full cohort (2105 samples; 9 tissues), identifying 2347 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with an absolute log2-fold changes >0.5 at a Bonferroni-adjusted P value < .05 (Fig. 3g). This genome-wide differential expression analysis did not use tissue or subtissue stratification.

We then examined the overlap between our subtissue-specific biomarkers (derived exclusively from AD samples) and these DEGs (identified from AD versus control comparisons). Among the 183 subtissue-specific biomarkers, 160 (88 upregulated + 72 downregulated) were also significant DEGs, while 23 showed high SHAP importance without significant differential expression in the bulk tissue analysis. These 23 genes may exert regulatory effects through transcriptional or posttranscriptional mechanisms, as evidenced by several genes enriched in Neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 1 (NOTCH1) signaling or reported to have high brain expression. Taken together, these findings highlight the added value of integrating ML-based feature selection with standard expression-level contrasts to capture a broader spectrum of AD-relevant genes.

Genome-wide association study data pinpoint subtissue-specific genetic drivers

As AD progression is driven by both genetics and the environment, we next aimed to identify genetic signals in each subtissue. First, we used the individual-level genotype data available for all donors matched to our transcriptomic dataset to extract the lead SNPs corresponding to 96 well-validated AD risk genes, as reported in [25]. For every subtissue, we averaged each SNP’s risk-allele dosage and compared it with the AD cohort mean, showing clear subtissue-specific enrichment of AD variants and their parent genes (Fig. 3h). Permutation testing confirmed that these dosage skews constitute distinct genetic signatures rather than random noise, highlighting regional variation in the disease’s genetic architecture. Hereafter, we refer to these genetic signatures (genes) as genetic drivers.

Integrating these subtissue-level genetic drivers with our previously derived transcriptomic biomarkers provides a more complete snapshot of AD pathology. However, phenotypic outcomes arise from interplay among multiple genes and pathways. To explore how genetic drivers might modulate the biomarker pathways, we subsequently constructed subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction networks (see Results section 4).

Network analysis prioritizes critical bottleneck genes as therapeutic targets in Alzheimer’s disease

Subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction networks prioritize key mediators

To investigate how biomarkers and genetic drivers converge within each brain tissue, we constructed subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction networks (details in Methods section 5). Each network was derived from RNA-seq profiles of AD samples in each subtissue and represents a coexpression network where edge weights correspond to Pearson correlation coefficients calculated between gene expression levels of samples. Only edges with correlation coefficients above a certain threshold were retained, ensuring that the network captures the strongest coexpression patterns among genes.

In these networks, biomarkers (orange nodes) and genetic drivers (green nodes) are typically not directly connected. Instead, they are linked through intermediate genes (blue nodes), referred to as “message-passing” genes, which mediate signaling cascades influencing AD progression and affecting the phenotype (Fig. 4b). These message-passing genes were identified by computing the shortest paths between all pairs of subtissue-specific biomarkers and genetic drivers within each network. This approach selects genes that serve as critical connectors, potentially playing a key role in regulatory pathways, rather than simply identifying genes on the basis of high correlation or coexpression strength alone.

Prioritizing critical bottleneck genes through centrality metrics

In each filtered subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction network, which included only biomarkers, genetic drivers, and intermediate message-passing genes, we ranked the constituent genes by multiple centrality metrics (e.g. betweenness, and closeness). These metrics assess a gene’s connectivity and influence within the network, pinpointing those with the most significant roles in mediating cellular communication within the subtissue network. Genes consistently scoring high in connectivity and low in clustering were designated candidate hub genes, as they appear pivotal in bridging communication between biomarkers and genetic drivers within the filtered network. This initial analysis yielded 22–30 candidate hub genes per subtissue.

To prioritize underexplored therapeutic possibilities, we then filtered out well-studied AD genes (a gene set selected based on literature citation counts, see Methods) from each hub gene list, focusing on less-studied but higher-ranked genes. These critical bottleneck genes may offer novel insights into AD pathophysiology and could serve as new therapeutic targets. Figure 4d illustrates how these bottlenecks interact in the broader human proteome [26]. The number of critical bottleneck genes varied across subtissues (Fig. 4f), reflecting the heterogeneous genetic architecture of AD in different brain tissues. A complete listing of these bottleneck genes—including subtissue affiliations and annotation details—can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Ultimately, unifying the critical bottleneck genes from all subtissues yielded 262 unique drug target candidates.

While this union-based approach combines genes from across all 36 subtissues, it is a deliberate strategy to prioritize drug targets with broad therapeutic potential. All bottleneck genes are first prioritized within the network of each subtissue, using subtissue-specific biomarkers/drivers and centrality (see Methods). The union reported here is a non-redundant catalog for downstream drug discovery and functional annotation; it does not replace the per-subtissue results. We retain subtissue provenance of each gene (see Supplementary Table S1) and show that bottleneck set sizes vary by subtissue (see Fig. 4f), reflecting preserved regional distinctions. Drug-repurposing then operates at the gene level across subtissues to find multitarget compounds that can modulate disease-relevant nodes irrespective of where they arise. This approach therefore seeks to identify targets for drugs that could have efficacy in a wider AD population, rather than being limited to a specific molecular subtype associated with a single brain region.

To biologically contextualize the 262 bottleneck genes, we assessed their overlap with established AD mechanisms. First, we found that 51.1% (134 of 262) were also DEGs in AD versus control samples (Bonferroni-adjusted P < .05), directly linking their topological centrality to transcriptional dysregulation. The remaining genes may exert influence through protein–protein interactions or posttranslational mechanisms not captured by RNA-level changes. Second, bottleneck genes show high expression in brain, central nervous system, and cerebral cortex (Supplementary Fig. S4a), and their encoded proteins are enriched for cell junction and cell membrane compartments, particularly synapse, postsynapse, and axon (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. S4b). Together, these results indicate that bottleneck genes are not only central connectors in the GWAS↔biomarker subnetwork but also map to relevant locations and processes which are fundamentally disrupted in neurodegeneration.

Functional enrichment links bottleneck genes to Core Alzheimer’s disease pathobiology

GO/Reactome analysis of the 262 bottlenecks (Fig. 5a–d) converged on key processes and pathways disrupted in AD pathology: membrane and synaptic ion transport, cell adhesion, extracellular-matrix organization, secretion, actin-cytoskeleton regulation, axon guidance, and broader signal-transduction programs [20, 27, 28]. Many bottleneck genes are also linked to comorbidities such as diabetes and neuropsychiatric disorders, indicating shared pathways with AD. These insights position the genes as promising pharmacological targets, explored next in drug repurposing.

Drug repurposing suggests multitargeted strategies for Alzheimer’s disease treatment

Knowledge-graph analysis discovers FDA-approved drugs targeting bottleneck genes

To identify FDA-approved drugs targeting multiple bottleneck genes (n = 262), we leveraged the Bioteque biomedical knowledge graph [18], which integrates >150 data sources into a network of >450 000 nodes (including 20 108 genes and 137 396 compounds) and 30 million relationships, with precomputed embeddings capturing direct and multistep interactions (e.g. drug–gene–gene). We computed pairwise cosine similarities between bottleneck gene and drug embeddings across four metapaths representing diverse biological relationships (one-step, e.g. drug-interacting-gene; multistep, e.g. compound-interacting-gene-coexpressing-gene; details in Methods section 6 and Supplementary Materials section 6), retaining the top five compounds per gene per metapath.

Combining results (five compound-gene pairs per metapath) yielded 2355 high-confidence drug-gene pairs covering 225 genes and 1600 compounds. After filtering for cosine similarity ≥0.65 (indicating strong connections; Supplementary Fig. S1b) and drugs targeting ≥2 genes (details in Methods section 6), we prioritized six FDA-approved multitarget candidates: pyrantel (5 targets), promethazine (3 targets), disopyramide (3 targets), nicardipine (2 targets), 1-benzylimidazole (2 targets), and pagoclone (2 targets) (see molecular structures of these drugs in Fig. 5e). These drugs hold promise for AD by modulating multiple disease-related genes and pathways.

Predicted repurposed drugs show diverse but similar pharmacological profiles

Our knowledge-graph analysis identified six FDA-approved drugs—promethazine, disopyramide, pyrantel, nicardipine, pagoclone, and 1-benzylimidazole (Fig. 5e)—that, despite diverse primary indications, converge on AD-relevant mechanisms. Three cardiovascular agents include disopyramide (class Ia antiarrhythmic) [29, 30], nicardipine (dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker) [31–34], and 1-benzylimidazole (thromboxane A₂ synthase inhibitor) [35, 36]. The others are promethazine (H1-antihistamine with N-methyl-D-aspartate/dopamine D2 receptors (NMDA/D2) antagonism) [37], pyrantel (nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist) [38, 39], and pagoclone (partial gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptor (GABA-A) agonist) [40, 41]. Preliminary research provides mechanistic support for a potential association between these drugs and AD pathology. For instance, disopyramide, a class Ia antiarrhythmic agent and a Na+-channel blocker, suppresses network hyperexcitability—now recognized as an early, targetable defect in AD. Disopyramide aligns mechanistically with evidence that reducing aberrant firing can normalize circuit function [42]. Similarly, nicardipine, a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, has demonstrated neuroprotective effects by attenuating amyloid-β-induced toxicity in neuronal cultures, linking its pharmacological action to the amyloid pathway [43]. In a longitudinal cohort, calcium channel blocker use was associated with slower progression to dementia, and a deduction in Aβ production in APP-overexpressing cells [33].

Furthermore, 1-benzylimidazole has been investigated as a potent inhibitor of glycogen synthase kinase-3β, a kinase critically involved in both amyloid production and tau hyperphosphorylation, providing a strong rationale for the development of related benzimidazole derivatives against AD [44].

Pyrantel, as a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) agonist, aligns with AD-directed cholinomimetic strategies; [39] synthesizes experimental evidence that nAChR activation can dampen neuroinflammation and support cognition in AD-relevant contexts, motivating pyrantel’s mechanistic rationale pending Central Nervous System (CNS) exposure data.

Pagoclone, a partial GABA-A agonist, reduces neuronal hyperexcitability—a feature of early AD—and has been evaluated for cognitive effects in clinical studies [41], suggesting potential utility in managing AD-related agitation.

Promethazine, an H1-antihistamine with NMDA/D2 antagonism, has demonstrated neuroprotective properties in preclinical models, reducing excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and exhibiting amyloid-binding activity [37, 45–47]. Promethazine directly engages amyloid pathology and shows neuroprotection in cell and tissue models relevant to AD; [47] reported selective Aβ-plaque binding in human AD tissue, and [46] showed reduced glutamate-induced injury and oxidative stress in hippocampal neurons.

While each agent carries known caveats (e.g. antimuscarinic load with disopyramide and promethazine; uncertain CNS penetration for pyrantel), their complementary mechanisms offer a systems-level therapeutic palette: vascular protectors (nicardipine, 1-benzylimidazole), excitability dampers (promethazine, pagoclone, disopyramide), and cholinergic modulators (pyrantel). Together they illustrate how PRISM-ML moves beyond single-target thinking toward repurposed, multipathway intervention in AD. Detailed pharmacological profiles, including primary indications, mechanisms, AD rationales, and caveats for each repurposed drug, are provided in Supplementary Materials section 9.

Network-level convergence suggests pathways with potential Alzheimer’s disease relevance

Bioteque embeddings [18] revealed that each drug candidate maps most strongly (top-5 cosine hits) to molecular-function nodes involved in glycogenolysis, striated-muscle contraction, DNA methylation, and Ca2+-dependent regulation. Although classically muscle-centric, glycogen breakdown, and contractile signaling influence astrocyte energy supply and hippocampal circuitry [48, 49]; DNA methylation and Ca2+ homeostasis likewise govern neuro-inflammation and synaptic stability—key axes of Alzheimer’s pathology [50, 51]. These network insights highlight the multitarget potential of our candidates, paving the way for real-world validation of the effects of promethazine on AD incidence in the subsequent section.

Pharmacoepidemiologic validation: promethazine shows a reduced Alzheimer’s disease risk compared with an active comparator

Among the six candidate drugs predicted by our knowledge-graph analysis, we selected promethazine for pharmacoepidemiologic validation based on the availability of a sufficiently large exposed cohort in the database. To ensure robust and reliable effect estimation in our active-comparator design, we established a priori inclusion criterion requiring a minimum of 10 000 individuals in the exposed drug cohort. This threshold was chosen to provide adequate statistical power to detect a significant difference in AD incidence, a relatively rare outcome over a limited follow-up period. The exposed cohorts for the other five candidate drugs (disopyramide, pyrantel, nicardipine, pagoclone, and 1-benzylimidazole) fell below this cutoff. Consequently, we restricted our validation analysis to promethazine, which had a robust exposed cohort of 353 856 individuals, compared to an active comparator (cyproheptadine) cohort of 10 877.

To validate our drug repurposing predictions in real-world clinical settings, we conducted a retrospective pharmacoepidemiologic study (EHR data mining) using Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database (2007–2021). We employed an active comparator design to minimize confounding by indication—a critical consideration when studying drug effects in observational data. Rather than comparing promethazine users to nonusers (which could introduce bias due to different underlying health conditions), we compared promethazine exposure to cyproheptadine exposure, as both are first-generation H1-antihistamines with similar clinical indications but potentially different neurological effects [52].

After applying the inclusion criteria and wash-out periods (Fig. 5g), our final cohort comprised 353 856 individuals with promethazine exposure and 10 877 with cyproheptadine exposure. This active comparator approach helps control for confounding factors related to allergy treatment needs while isolating the specific effects of promethazine on AD risk. The demographics of the study population are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Using two complementary analytical approaches, both demonstrated considerable protective associations for promethazine compared with cyproheptadine. In the primary covariate-adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, promethazine exposure was associated with a 62% reduction in AD risk [hazard ratio (HR) = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.27–0.53, P < .001]. A sensitivity analysis using the inverse probability of treatment weighting to further balance baseline characteristics between exposure groups yielded consistent results (HR = 0.43, P < .001) (Fig. 5f). The consistency between these two analytical approaches—direct covariate adjustment and propensity score-based weighting—strengthens confidence in the observed protective association. The complete model coefficients and demographic characteristics are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

This considerable association between promethazine exposure and a reduced risk of AD, as evidenced by our robust covariate-adjusted Cox model, suggests potential neuroprotective properties that warrant further investigation. These findings could inform future clinical trials and healthcare strategies aimed at integrating promethazine into broader AD management protocols.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce PRISM-ML, an integrated pipeline combining interpretable ML with multiomics data to dissect AD’s molecular complexity, analyzing matched transcriptomic and genomic profiles from the same cohorts to identify patient-level biomarkers and 36 subtissues across nine brain regions, thereby highlighting localized diversity. We constructed subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction networks to prioritize 262 high-centrality bottleneck genes as potential AD progression drivers and used a knowledge-graph approach to predict six FDA-approved multitarget drugs for modulating multiple pathways (see Table 3), extending beyond prior studies through integration of multiple tissues, genomic variation, and real-world pharmacoepidemiologic validation via EHR data mining.

Our prioritization highlights bottleneck genes—high-betweenness intermediates that lie on shortest paths between genetic risk (GWAS) and patient-level biomarkers (SHAP). The substantial overlap (51.1%) with DEGs confirms that many of these topologically central genes are indeed transcriptionally dysregulated, validating their disease relevance. Importantly, the remaining bottleneck genes—which show high network centrality without significant expression changes—may represent critical regulatory nodes whose influence operates through protein-interaction networks or posttranscriptional mechanisms. The strong enrichment of these genes in synaptic function, cell membrane, axon guidance, and neural development pathways, and their considerable expression in brain tissue and central nervous system provides evidence that they are not random network features but rather reflect core neurodegenerative processes. While centrality alone cannot establish causality, the convergence of network position, transcriptomic change, and neuronal localization strengthens confidence in these candidates and clarifies the rationale behind our prioritization strategy.

Our research has several strengths that underscore its significance in AD research. First, by leveraging SHAP values [6], we address the interpretability gap often associated with ML models, clearly illustrating the contribution of each gene to the classification of each AD sample. Second, our focus on subtissue-specific changes rather than broad tissue-level analyses enabled a more precise dissection of the regional heterogeneity of AD. Third, integrating multiomics data from three cohorts offers a unified perspective on genetic and expression-level changes. Fourth, the LOOCV strategy ensures robust and personalized findings, addressing patient-specific disease manifestations. Finally, prioritizing novel bottleneck genes, rather than reiterating the well-studied AD genes, opens new therapeutic avenues.

Despite these advances, our study has several limitations. It focuses on transcriptomic and genomic data, excluding other omics layers like proteomics or metabolomics. One subtissue lacked sufficient samples for reliable network construction. The use of bulk RNA-seq cannot resolve cell type-specific contributions, an area for future single-cell studies. Moreover, since samples in each tissue are contributed by a distinct cohort, formal batch-correction was not performed; any global adjustment would have downweight true regional differences—the very signal the study aims to characterize. Statistical analyses confirmed that technical variation did not drive subtissue clustering. We found no significant associations between batch identity or PMI and subtissue assignment after false discovery rate correction (all q > 0.05) (see Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Table S6). This provides strong evidence that the subtissues reflect underlying biological rather than technical heterogeneity. Additionally, our drug repurposing relies on association-based knowledge graph embeddings, which require further validation to confirm interaction types (agonistic or antagonistic). Nevertheless, the pharmacoepidemiologic evidence for protective effects of promethazine underscores the clinical potential of our drug repurposing pipeline.

Three of the six repurposable drugs predicted by PRISM-ML—disopyramide, nicardipine, and 1-benzylimidazole—are used primarily for cardiovascular conditions, whereas promethazine, pagoclone, and pyrantel modulate neuronal or neuromuscular pathways. Mechanistic overlaps suggest that promethazine and pagoclone may alleviate neuropsychiatric symptoms and excitotoxicity, whereas nicardipine and 1-benzylimidazole could address vascular and inflammatory dysregulation, and that pyrantel might bolster cholinergic function. Their side effects, particularly anticholinergic properties, require careful consideration as one study [37] has shown that a high anticholinergic load can worsen confusion, causing delirium in older adults.

The real-world evidence from our pharmacoepidemiologic study supports the protective effect of promethazine against AD. Using an active comparator design with cyproheptadine, another first-generation H1-antihistamine, our analysis of 364 733 individuals demonstrated that promethazine exposure was associated with 62% risk of AD (HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.27–0.53, P < .001). This finding was further validated through sensitivity analysis via the inverse probability of treatment weighting (HR = 0.43, P < .001).

While these pharmacoepidemiologic results are promising, they should be interpreted within the context of certain limitations, including potential unmeasured confounding effects, possible misclassification in health insurance records, and limited generalizability to populations outside commercial or Medicare Supplemental health insurance plans. Notably, healthcare claims data are vulnerable to misclassification of mild or early AD, which can bias hazard ratios. Despite these limitations, our active comparator design represents a methodological strength that distinguishes this study from simple exposure-versus-unexposed comparisons. By comparing two drugs with similar indications but different mechanistic profiles [52], we reduced the likelihood that the observed differences reflected underlying patient characteristics rather than drug-specific effects. The consistency of the results across the two analytical methods (direct covariate adjustment and inverse probability of treatment weighting) further supports the robustness of our findings. Future studies should validate these results in more diverse cohorts and incorporate rigorous causal-inference methods to address potential residual confounding.

Moving forward, our findings set the stage for deeper investigation into these identified candidates and their biological interactions. Rigorous in vitro and in vivo assays can clarify whether each agent’s mechanism—agonistic or antagonistic—truly counter AD pathologies. In parallel, novel computational methods, including neural networks and transformer-based models with interpretability tools [53] could refine biomarker discovery, whereas expanded omics data layers (e.g. proteomics, metabolomics, and epigenetics) might yield a fuller picture of the molecular architecture of AD. Future investigations should incorporate single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, which can resolve the cell type-specific disruptions masked by bulk RNA profiling, leading to refinement of our understanding of molecular architecture of AD. Ultimately, PRISM-ML bridges foundational research and clinical application, paving the way for multipathway therapeutics tailored to individual profiles.

Conclusion

In this work, we developed PRISM-ML, an interpretable ML analysis pipeline integrated with multiomics data (bulk RNA-seq and genomics) to illuminate key molecular mechanisms underlying AD. By identifying patient-level biomarkers and constructing subtissue-specific gene–gene interaction networks, we prioritized critical bottleneck genes and highlighted new paths for therapeutic intervention. Our data-driven knowledge graph-based approach led to the discovery of six repurposable drugs that target multiple AD-relevant pathways simultaneously, marking a shift from conventional single-target strategies. Real-world evidence, including pharmacoepidemiologic validation of promethazine, underscores the clinical promise of our findings, although further experimental and clinical studies are needed to refine the mechanistic details and therapeutic efficacy.

As precision medicine gains momentum, our integrated analysis pipeline—combining molecular signatures, knowledge graph-based drug repurposing, and pharmacoepidemiologic validation—offers a powerful template for unraveling the heterogeneity of complex diseases and accelerating the development of targeted, multipathway drug treatments. While further experimental work is necessary to validate our findings, this study paves the way for transformative advancements in AD treatment and beyond, potentially accelerating the shift toward more personalized medicine in clinical practice.
