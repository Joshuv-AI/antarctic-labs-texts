Photobiomodulation therapy reduces diabetic periodontal damage through modulation of macrophage metabolism and functions
Authors: Pawuziya Abulizi, Hao Dong, Aimin Cui, Haonan Zou, Yuezhang Sun, Lulinxiao Nie, Bomiao Cui, Shimeng Xiao, Yi Ding, Wai Keung Leung, Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus disrupts macrophage function and immune homoeostasis, thereby increasing susceptibility toward periodontitis. PBMT shows emerging potential in oral rehabilitation. However, it remains underexplored for targeting diabetes-associated periodontal complications. This study aimed to investigate the biomodulatory effects of PBMT on hyperglycaemia-associated macrophage dysfunction, metabolic disorders, and periodontal tissue impairment. Immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages undergoing glucose transition and streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice received PBMT (980 nm, 3 J/cm²), insulin, or combined therapy. Pharmacological inhibition using specific inhibitors targeting FOXO1, AKT, and ERK was employed to elucidate signaling mechanisms. Cellular functions were assessed through Transwell migration, phagocytosis, and senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining. Metabolic parameters were evaluated via western blotting, ATP/LDH assays, and immunostaining. Tissue morphology was analyzed using micro-CT and H&E staining. PBMT enhanced macrophage phagocytosis, migration, and anti-inflammatory phenotype. The combined PBMT-insulin therapy demonstrated superior efficacy in restoring periodontal architecture compared to monotherapy. Mechanistically, PBMT attenuated FOXO1 hyperactivation primarily through MEK/ERK signaling. PBMT effectively counteracted hyperglycemia-induced metabolic dysregulation, restored macrophage function and phenotype, and mitigated subsequent periodontal tissue damage. The MEK/ERK/FOXO1 signalling pathway may represent a novel mechanism underlying the benefits of PBMT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10103-026-04818-3.

Introduction

Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) employs infrared or near-infrared light (wavelength range: 380–1070 nm; output power: <0.5 W) to penetrate deep tissues and activate cellular photoreceptors. This modality regulates mitochondrial metabolism and cellular signalling pathways, eliciting biological effects including anti-inflammatory responses, analgesia, and tissue regeneration [1, 2]. By modulating cellular metabolism and signalling cascades, PBMT accelerates tissue repair and demonstrates significant therapeutic potential for oral diseases, particularly those exacerbated by diabetes mellitus (DM) [3–8].

Diabetes mellitus (DM), is a global health challenge characterized by sustained hyperglycemia, which disrupts periodontal microenvironmental homeostasis and compromises gingival epithelial barrier function [9, 10]. In periodontal immunity, macrophages play a pivotal role in maintaining immune homoeostasis [11]. Hyperglycaemia drives periodontal damage through multiple mechanisms [12]: it induces macrophage pyroptosis via NLRP4 activation, promotes M1 polarization and cellular senescence [13, 14], and disrupts glucose metabolism through glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) [15] and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) [16] dysregulation, impairing chemotaxis and phagocytosis. These metabolic alterations shift macrophages toward aerobic glycolysis and trigger epigenetic changes that sustain chronic inflammation [17, 18]. Given the close connection between macrophage function and metabolic state, investigating PBMT’s effects on macrophage metabolism during hyperglycemia represents a crucial research direction.

Excessive activation of gluconeogenesis represents a key pathological feature of type 2 DM. Forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) is a crucial regulator of gluconeogenesis that plays pivotal roles in cellular senescence, glucose metabolism, and energy balance [19]. In periodontal tissues, FOXO1 exhibits dual regulatory properties: under physiological conditions, it maintains immune surveillance, tissue repair, and microbial balance [20], whereas in diabetic states, aberrant nuclear accumulation and enhanced transcriptional activity of FOXO1 drive multiple pathological processes [21, 22]. Notably, FOXO1 knockdown suppresses osteoclastogenesis, mitigates age-related alveolar bone loss, and shifts macrophage polarisation from M1 to M2, thereby enhancing osteogenesis and alleviating periodontitis [23, 24]. Despite these insights, the effects of PBMT on FOXO1 regulation and subcellular dynamics in macrophages during hyperglycaemia remain unexplored, highlighting a critical gap in our understanding of its therapeutic potential.

This study explored the biomodulatory effects of PBMT on hyperglycaemia-driven macrophage dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation in high glucose-treated immortalised bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) and streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice. Cellular-level analyses demonstrated that PBMT improved macrophage phagocytic function and reduced senescence, accompanied by a shift toward anti-inflammatory polarization. In diabetic mice, PBMT-insulin combination therapy improved gingival morphology more effectively than insulin monotherapy. Mechanistically, PBMT facilitates FOXO1 nuclear export via mitogen-activated protein kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway activation, reversing its pathological hyperactivation. These findings provide a novel theoretical foundation for phototherapeutic strategies against DM-associated periodontal pathologies.

Materials and methods

Animals and PBMT treatment in vivo

Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (Nanjing Model Animal Center, Nanjing, China) were housed at our centre under approved protocols (WCHSIRB-D-2024-586) and randomly allocated to five groups (n = 6 per group): control (N), diabetic (D), insulin-treated (DI), PBMT-treated (DL), and combined therapy (DIL). DM was induced through intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 150 mg/kg in pH 4.5 buffer), with diabetic status being confirmed by the blood glucose levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/L [25]. Starting at week 12, the DI and DIL groups received daily insulin injections (25–30 U/kg), while the DL and DIL groups underwent PBMT using a 980 nm laser at 0.1 W power and 3 J/cm² energy density (10 s per tooth, twice weekly for 4 weeks). The irradiation spot area was calculated as 0.33 cm² based on the energy density formula, with a working distance of 1.4 cm. Control mice received equivalent volumes of saline [26, 27].

Cell culture and PBMT treatment in vitro

iBMDMs (Fu Heng Biology) and RAW 264.7 macrophages (State Key Laboratory of Oral Diseases, Chengdu, China) were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) containing 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

iBMDMs were allocated into distinct groups. The control group (NG) received DMEM with 25 mM glucose. Hyperglycemic (HG) conditions were established using 45 mM glucose-containing DMEM for 6 days. The HG-NG transition group experienced initial exposure to 45 mM glucose for 3 days followed by 3 days in 25 mM glucose medium.

For PBMT treatment, cells cultured in 12-well plates (base area ≈ 4.4 cm²) were first maintained under high-glucose conditions for three days, then transferred to low-glucose medium and irradiated using a 980 nm semiconductor laser system (GigaaMedical, China). Based on the formula Energy Density = Power × Time/Spot Area, three energy densities (3, 6, and 18 J/cm²) were achieved using power outputs of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.6 W respectively, with a constant irradiation time of 132 s and working distance of 5 cm.

In the inhibitor experiments, cells were pretreated with specific inhibitors for 1 h prior to exposure to high-glucose (45 mM) conditions for 3 days, followed by 3 days in low-glucose (25 mM) medium. The inhibitors included: 1 µM AS1842856 (FOXO1 inhibitor, Ambeed, USA), 2 µM AKT inhibitor VIII (Ambeed, USA), and 10 µM FR 180,204 (ERK inhibitor, Ambeed, USA). For combination treatments, inhibitor-pretreated cells undergoing the same glucose transition received 980 nm laser irradiation at 0.1 W for 132 s during the low-glucose phase.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted from iBMDM cells or murine gingival tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cell lysates were centrifuged (14,000 × g, 10 min), and tissue samples were homogenized in liquid nitrogen, followed by sonication and centrifugation. Supernatants were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, denatured (100 °C, 6 min), and separated by SDS-PAGE. After transfer to PVDF membranes, blots were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (β-actin 1:10,000; β-tubulin 1:10,000; IL-1β 1:1,000; TNF-α 1:2,000; HK2 1:1,000; GLUT1 1:2,000; PKM2 1:1,000; DNMT1 1:4,000; FOXO1 1:5,000; AKT/p-AKT 1:2,000; ERK/p-ERK 1:5,000; MEK 1:5,000), followed by 1-h incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:50,000). Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ChemiDoc MP, Bio-Rad).

Micro-computed tomography (CT) assay

A µCT50 system (Olympus, Japan) was utilised to scan mandibular bones, followed by 3D reconstruction and bone density analysis using SCANO Visualiser (https://scano.app).

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunostaining

Mouse maxillae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Servicebio, China), decalcified in ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid, and embedded in paraffin for sectioning. H&E staining was performed using standard protocols. For immunohistochemistry, sections underwent antigen retrieval, peroxidase blocking, and incubation with primary antibodies followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, with DAB used for chromogenic development. For immunofluorescence, samples were processed similarly and incubated with primary antibodies against HK2 (1:200), PKM2 (1:250), ERK (1:50), p-ERK (1:100), F4/80 (1:200), and DNMT1 (1:200), followed by fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cy3 1:500, FITC 1:100). All images were acquired using confocal microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Transwell migration assay

Cells were resuspended in DMEM devoid of serum components and placed in the upper compartments of the migration chambers, whereas the lower compartments received medium enriched with 20% FBS. After a 12-h incubation, celluar fixation was performed using 4% PFA, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min and PBS washes; subsequently, the non-migrated cells were removed from the upper membrane. The migrated cells were quantified using a bright-field microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-GaL) staining

Cellular senescence was evaluated through SA-β-GaL staining kit (Beyotime, China). RAW 264.7 underwent fixation with 4% PFA for 15 min at ambient temperature. After PBS washing, cells were treated with staining solution and maintained at 37 °C under light-protected conditions for 16 h. Following additional PBS rinses, stained specimens were examined microscopically, with quantitative analysis of positively stained cells performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Latex bead phagocytosis assay

The red fluorescent latex beads (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were prepared by diluting in 1% bovine serum albumin (1:100 ratio), followed by a 30-minute incubation at 37 °C and 5-minute sonication. Subsequently, 2 mL of the prepared bead solution was introduced into the culture medium and maintained at 37 °C for 90 min. Following incubation, cellular uptake was quantified through flow cytometric, with data processing performed using FlowJo software (FlowJo, USA).

Flow cytometry

iBMDMs were collected and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per sample. For intracellular FOXO1 detection, cells were stained with FOXO1 antibody (Proteintech, USA) followed by FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, China). For macrophage polarization assessment, cells were stained with PE-Cyanine7 anti-CD86 (eBioscience, USA) and PE anti-CD206 (eBioscience, USA). All samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman, USA).

ATP and LDH content assay

ATP and LDH levels were measured using an ATP assay kit (Beyotime, China) and LDH assay kit (Solarbio, China). Standard curves were constructed, followed by iBMDM lysis on ice, sonication, and centrifugation. The supernatants were mixed with detection reagents, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and analysed using a microplate reader (ATP: 340 nm; LDH: 450 nm). The concentrations were calculated using standard curves.

Statistics

Each experimental procedure was replicated a minimum of three times independently. The analysis of data was conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA), with results presented as mean values accompanied by standard deviations. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed, while unpaired Student’s t-tests were utilized for two-group analyses. A threshold of P < 0.05 was established to determine statistical significance.

Micro-CT analysis revealed significant alveolar bone loss in group D, which was not mitigated by insulin monotherapy (DI). In contrast, PBMT (DL) and combined therapy (DIL) significantly reduced bone resorption and diminished occlusal surface attrition, restoring the microstructure to near-non-diabetic levels (Fig. 1c). Quantitative analysis demonstrated increased bone volume fraction (DIL > DI, P < 0.05) and trabecular thickness (DL > DI, P < 0.05), along with reduced trabecular separation across the treatment groups (P < 0.05 vs. group D, Fig. 1d).

Histological examination using H&E staining confirmed hyperglycaemia-induced (Fig. 1e), epithelial hyperplasia (69.6 ± 14.4 μm [group D] vs. 21.8 ± 5.8 μm [group N], P < 0.001) and shortening of epithelial rete ridge, consistent with established findings [28]. Insulin partially restored epithelial thickness (34.0 ± 18.8 μm [group DI], 27.1 ± 4.0 μm [group DIL] vs. group D, P < 0.001), while PBMT monotherapy (DL) further reduced hyperplasia (26.6 ± 6.6 μm [group DL] vs. group D, P < 0.001) and bone resorption (33.6% reduction vs. group D, P < 0.001). The combined therapy (DIL) reduced the interdental papillary area by 33.4% and bone resorption by 17.2% compared with insulin monotherapy (P < 0.05; Fig. 1f), indicating the superior short-term therapeutic efficacy of PBMT.

PBMT improves hyperglycaemia-induced macrophage phenotypic aberrations and dysfunction

PBMT (3, 6, and 18 J/cm², 980 nm) was applied to hyperglycaemic macrophages to evaluate functional recovery. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that high glucose (HG) conditions increased the proportion of M1-like (CD86⁺) macrophages and decreased the M2-like (CD206⁺) population (Fig. 2a). This shift persisted even after glucose normalization (HG-NG group). Importantly, all three PBMT treatment groups promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Furthermore, WB analysis of inflammatory mediators showed that PBMT at 3 and 6 J/cm² significantly reduced IL-1β expression, while all three energy densities (3, 6, and 18 J/cm²) exhibited a downward trend in TNF-α levels (Fig. 2b). These results further confirm that PBMT reduces pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype. IF revealed enhanced phagocytic uptake of fluorescent beads in PBMT-treated macrophages compared to that in glucose-normalised controls (HG-NG group, Fig. 2c), which was corroborated by flow cytometry showing a 57.8% increase in phagocytic cells at 3 J/cm² (P < 0.001, vs. HG-NG, Fig. 2d). Transwell assays demonstrated preserved migration under hyperglycaemia (P > 0.05, vs. NG), in which PBMT (3, 6 J/cm²) was significantly enhanced (P < 0.01, vs. HG), confirming its dual efficacy in restoring phagocytic and migratory capacities under hyperglycaemia (Fig. 2e). PBMT alleviates metabolic dysregulation in hyperglycaemia-stimulated macrophages and diabetic gingival tissues

As innate immune effectors, macrophages exhibit functional plasticity that is closely linked to their metabolic state. Based on the previous evidence that PBMT restores cellular function by reducing senescence and oxidative stress, we explored its metabolic regulatory effects under hyperglycaemic conditions. Metabolic profiling revealed that PBMT (6 J/cm²) elevated ATP production by 1.5-fold (P > 0.05) and reduced LDH release by 17% (P < 0.05), indicating that improved mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and suppressed aerobic glycolysis (Fig. 3a). WB analysis revealed persistent upregulation of HK2 and GLUT1 in hyperglycaemic macrophages (HG), which remained unchanged after glucose normalisation (HG-NG). PBMT (6 and 18 J/cm2) significantly downregulated HK2 and GLUT1, whereas pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) reduction was moderate, suggesting the ability of PBMT to counteract hyperglycaemia-induced glycolytic dominance (Fig. 3c). IF staining confirmed the PBMT-mediated normalisation of HK2 (cytoplasm) and PKM2 (cytoplasm) localisation (Fig. 3b). We also investigated hyperglycaemia-driven epigenetic dysregulation, particularly the overexpression of DNMT1, given its role in DNA methylation and immune homoeostasis [20, 29]. To further elucidate the pivotal role of FOXO1 in PKM2/HK2/GLUT1 regulation, additional experiments were conducted using the FOXO1 inhibitor. We examined the expression of key metabolic markers and found that PKM2 protein levels showed no significant changes across the groups. In contrast, both HK2 and GLUT1 expression were elevated under high-glucose conditions and were downregulated following PBMT. Notably, this downregulatory trend was further enhanced when FOXO1 was inhibited (Fig. 4e). Subsequently, we measured cellular ATP production and LDH levels. FOXO1 inhibition significantly increased ATP generation and reduced LDH release (Fig. 4f). Importantly, PBMT alone produced effects similar to those of FOXO1 inhibition. These findings suggest that PBMT may exert its protective effects against diabetic progression by regulating PKM2/HK2/GLUT1-related glycolytic metabolism through FOXO1.

Both in vivo and in vitro studies showed that PBMT treatment enhanced MEK and p-ERK levels, with no significant differences in total AKT protein or its phosphorylation levels contrast to untreated group (Fig. 5a, c). Additionally, we performed further validation by establishing cellular models containing AKT and ERK inhibitors. Both experiments demonstrated that PBMT significantly suppressed FOXO1 expression in the absence of AKT or ERK inhibitors (Fig. 5e, f). When AKT or ERK was inhibited, the PBMT-mediated suppression of FOXO1 was abolished. This indicates that the effects of PBMT were counteracted by FOXO1 upregulation induced by AKT or ERK inhibition. Further assays assessed AKT and ERK expression and phosphorylation levels. Results showed no significant differences in total AKT protein or its phosphorylation levels among groups. In contrast, phosphorylated ERK levels increased in the PBMT-treated group, consistent with the observed FOXO1 downregulation. In summary, our findings collectively indicate that PBMT primarily modulates FOXO1 expression via the ERK pathway, as evidenced by the loss of inhibitory effects upon ERK inhibition and the corresponding increase in ERK phosphorylation levels. Although AKT inhibition experiments confirmed AKT’s role in FOXO1 regulation, AKT phosphorylation levels remained unchanged after PBMT treatment, suggesting that the ERK pathway plays a more direct role in mediating PBMT effects (Fig. 5f, g).

Discussion

PBMT is a noninvasive therapeutic modality that demonstrates significant potentical in diabatic complications management without inducing thermal damage. Our findings reveal that 980 nm PBMT at 3 J/cm2 effectively restored gingival tissue morphology in diabetic mice, reduced cellular senescence burden, and normalized metabolic disregulation in both local tissues and macrophages. Notably, PBMT exhibited a unique capacity to ameliorate persistent hyperglycaemia residuals even after glycemic control. It may exert bioregulatory effects by modulating cellular function, phenotype, and metabolism via the MEK/ERK/FOXO1 pathway.

Building upon evidence that diabetes upregulates inflammatory and senescence-associated genes, thereby compromising macrophage function and periodontal immunity [30, 31], PBMT has gained increasing attention as a potential treatment modality. Growing literature indicates that PBMT confers beneficial effects in periodontal contexts by enhancing immune cell activity, suppressing inflammatory cytokine release, and restoring the periodontal microenvironment [32–34]. Consistent with this paradigm, our results demonstrate that PBMT conteracts hyperglycaemia-induced functional and phenotypic dysregulation in macrophages, aligning with our earlier research [11]. Importantly, we discovered that cellular abnormalities persisted after high-glucose stimulus withdrawal—a novel finding that expands the therapeutic scope of PBMT.

The metabolic reprogramming induced by PBMT represents a key mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects [35, 36]. In our study, PBMT at 3, 6 J/cm2 significantly donwnregulated glycolytic enzymes (HK2 and GLUT1), epigenetic regulator (DNMT1), and LDH production while enhancing ATP generation in macrophage, although tissue-level metabolic responses showed variation from cellular observations-potentially due to the complexity of tissue microenvironments, constrained laser parameters, and short observation periods, the combined PBMT-insulin therapy demonstrated superior efficacy in modulation metabolic parameters compared to insulin monotherapy.

FOXO1, a key transcription factor in diabetes pathogenesis, regulates cellular metabolism by promoting pyruvate-to-lactate conversion while suppressing acetyl-CoA synthesis [37]. Our study identified that hyperglycemia induces persistent FOXO1 overexpression, which PBMT effectively mitigates through an ERK-dependent mechanism. While FOXO1 activation involves complex post-translational modifications [38–40]. Our inhibitor-based experiments and uninhibited cellular models collectively demonstrated that while both ERK and AKT pathways possess the capacity to regulate FOXO1, 980 nm PBMT at 3, 6 J/cm2 modulates FOXO1 primarily through MER/ERK signaling rather than AKT pathway. Specifically, WB analysis revealed that PBMT significantly enhanced ERK phosphorylation without altering AKT activation, correlating with FOXO1 downregulation. This ERK-dominated mechanism was further confirmed by the abolition of PBMT-mediated FOXO1 suppression under ERK inhibition. This pattern aligns with previous reports of ERK-mediated FOXO1 regulation independent of AKT signaling, as demonstrated in insulin receptor-deficient models [41]. The observed signaling preference differs from reports of AKT-dominated FOXO1 regulation, highlighting the parameter-dependent nature of PBMT. While inflammatory models at different energy densities (0.5–10 J/cm²) show predominant AKT activation [42], our metabolic stress model primarily engaged the ERK pathway. FOXO1 inhibition experiments confirmed PBMT’s metabolic effects are mediated through FOXO1 regulation.

Our research group’s previous investigation employing 810 nm laser irradiation reveals distinctions from the current study utilizing 980 nm parameters [43]. The 810 nm wavelength exhibits enhanced tissue penetration characteristics compared to 980 nm irradiation, resulting in distinct biological responses due to their differential photon absorption properties. This physical divergence is further amplified by substantially different experimental paradigms: while the earlier investigation employed a P. gingivalis-induced diabetic periodontitis model, the present work utilizes a sterile diabetic model specifically designed to investigate metabolic dysregulation. These distinct pathological environments engage different molecular pathways—where 810 nm PBMT primarily activated the GLUT1/mTOR axis characteristic of inflammatory senescence in infected tissues, 980 nm irradiation predominantly engaged the MEK/ERK/FOXO1 pathway governing metabolic homeostasis. These wavelength-dependent and pathology-sensitive mechanistic variations underscore PBMT’s capacity to engage different biological pathways according to specific disease conditions.

In conclusion, this study provides the first evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of PBMT in ameliorating hyperglycemia-induced periodontal damage through regulation of macrophage metabolism and functional states. Significantly, our findings establish the MEK/ERK/FOXO1 signaling pathway as a key mechanistic pathway mediating the biological effects of PBMT. These results not only reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying PBMT’s action but also highlight its clinical promise as an adjunctive therapy for diabetic periodontal complications that respond inadequately to conventional treatments.
