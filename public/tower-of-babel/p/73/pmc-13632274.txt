Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition including incorrect functioning from an early age in three areas: communication, social interaction, and repetitive, restricted behavior and interests [1, 2, 3]. Global prevalence of ASD is estimated as 1–2%, with a predominance of men with a 4.2 median male-to-female ratio [2, 3, 4]. In recent years, numerous studies have been published in the field of etiology of ASD, and the genetic basis of ASD has become of greater interest among scientists all over the world. Various factors responsible for the occurrence of ASD were reported, like pre- and perinatal factors (advanced parental age at conception, nutritional deficiencies during pregnancy, prenatal exposure to some medications or endocrine-disrupting chemicals, previous fetal loss, maternal stress, infections, vaginal bleeding, gestational diabetes, hypertension, preeclampsia, proteinuria, birth trauma/injury, birth asphyxia, neonatal sepsis, metabolic problems – hypoglycemia, prematurity, low birth weight), environmental factors (exposure to air pollutions, pesticides), abnormalities in different brain regions, dysregulation of the gut microbiome, disruption of the gut-brain axis, immunological factors, genetic and epigenetic alterations, as well as gene-gene/gene-environment interactions [1, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Twin studies showed that ASD is highly heritable (70–90%) [6]. Genes associated with ASD encode proteins that are mainly responsible for synaptic signaling, neurogenesis, neuroplasticity of the brain, regulating chromatin remodeling, silencing gene expression, or the immune response [6, 7]. It is assumed that thousands of genes might be responsible for ASD. Most of them probably remain functionally not characterized or not identified [8]. In the literature, different types of genetic variation in ASD were identified, in coding and non-coding regions. Those genomic alterations encompass several structural variants (copy number variations – CNVs, larger insertions, inversions, uniparental disomies, tandem repeat expansions), common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), rare and de novo single nucleotide variants (SNVs), short insertions/deletions (indels), and mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) variants. Those variations might be inherited in both dominant and recessive patterns [9]. Rare genetic variation explains about 3% of ASD genetic risk, and common variation – SNPs might be responsible for 50% of ASD heritability [8]. For example, CNV at the 1q21 locus, mainly duplication, is found at high incidence in ASD. Region 15q1 is the second most common region affected by duplications in individuals with ASD. It was noted that 16p11.2 deletion and duplications confer a 10-fold increase for the risk of ASD [10].

There are several genetic diseases in which ASD has been described as one of the components of the phenotype (syndromic ASD). The most well-known reason for a single gene etiology of ASD is fragile X syndrome, which accounts for 1–2% of ASD patients [1, 11]. Other conditions in which ASD is described as a component of the phenotype include a.o. tuberous sclerosis complex, neurofibromatosis type 1, Rett, Angelman, Phelan-McDermid, Smith-Lemli-Opitz, Noonan, Down, Williams-Beuren, Sotos, Cowden, Moebius, Timothy syndromes, and sex chromosome trisomies (like XXX, XXY, XYY) [12–20]. Among genetic diagnostic tools that might identify genes associated with ASD risk, the following could be mentioned: multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), targeted gene sequencing, whole exome sequencing (WES), or whole genome sequencing (WGS) [20, 21].

The aim of the study is to analyze genetic abnormalities and correlations between genotype and phenotype in patients with ASD according to data from current literature.

Materials and methods

Studies available in the PubMed and Google Scholar databases were chosen through a literature search that was undertaken in November 2025. The following inclusion criteria were assumed: papers published from 2020 (to focus exclusively on the most recent five years of research and present the most current state of knowledge), available as full-text publications in English, with studies conducted on humans; only original papers and meta-analyses were eligible for inclusion in the study. Case reports, reviews, systematic reviews, letters to the editor, books, and documents were excluded from the review. To find articles, the following keywords were used: “autism spectrum disorder”, “etiology”, “genes”, “MLPA”, “microarrays”, “copy number variation”, next-generation sequencing”, “comorbidities”. Titles and abstracts were screened to determine whether they met the inclusion criteria. Furthermore, manual searching of retrieved studies was performed, in order not to miss matching studies. Finally, 53 articles entered the review. All 53 analyzed studies are included in the reference list and are cited in the Background and Results and Discussion sections.

Results and discussion

According to the reviewed literature, CNVs (both deletions and duplications) are one of the possible etiologies of ASD, including inherited and de novo variations. The method dedicated to the assessment of CNV is MLPA when there is a suspicion of CNV at a specific locus or CMA when there is a suspicion of CNV at an undefined location on chromosomes, that may not be included in the MLPA probe set. Currently, array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarrays are two types of CMA techniques used in the diagnosis process of submicroscopic chromosomal imbalances. About 10% of ASD individuals might present genetic susceptibility due to CNVs, a diagnostic rate of 5–10% [20].

Example CNV in association with genes that are involved in ASD etiology are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 [9, 20–31]. Protein functions of each gene were searched using GeneCards, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Men (OMIM), and Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) databases [32–34].

It was evaluated that from 130 to 234 CNV regions in the human genome are related to ASD [23]. In the study by Mkhitaryan et al., assessing 149 ASD children and 148 controls from Armenia, CNV in MLPA at 15q11-15q13, 16p11, and 11q13 regions, and mutations in SHANK2 and SHANK3 genes were noted as significantly implicated in ASD. Exacerbated ASD risk was noted in combination with prenatal factors and specific genetic variations. Higher mutation rates across multiple probes were found in females [21]. The following frequently reported CNVs are 7q11.23, 17p11.2, and 22q11 duplications [23].

In the study by Annunziata et al., 209 individuals with ASD, who were negatively tested for metabolic diseases, were diagnosed with aCGH. In 75 patients, 114 CNVs were found – 11 classified as pathogenic, 94 as variants of unknown/uncertain significance (VUS), and 9 as likely benign. Six CNVs were genomic disorders (del22q11.21, del15q11.2, del15q13.2q13.3, dup1q21.1q21.2, dup15q11.2q13.1, dup15q11.2), and five were CNVs involving MBD5, HUWE1, NRXN1, and CNTNAP2 genes, which are pathogenic for monogenic diseases [20].

Sandoval-Talamantes et al. analyzed aCGH results of 212 Spanish patients with ASD. Among them 99 (45.2%) carried CNVs: 34 deletions (34.34%) and 65 duplications (65.65%). Likely pathogenic and pathogenic CNVs were diagnosed in a total of 28 of 212 individuals (13.2%). Almost the same frequency was found among VUS (26 patients; 12.26%). Individuals with pathogenic variants carried a single CNV, and those with benign, likely benign, or VUS variants carried several of them. Authors suggested that the diagnostic process of ASD patients might involve a CMA as a cost-effective, first-line test [24].

In the study by Cucinotta et al., 329 Italian patients with idiopathic ASD were assessed using aCGH microarrays. Patients with known genetic syndromes, major dysmorphisms, malformations, and positive karyotypes were excluded. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic CNVs were identified in 50 patients (15.2%), whereas VUS were identified in 89 (27.1%). Frequencies of deletions and duplications in these classes were comparable. Among 139 CNVs, 115 (82.7%) were inherited (with similar frequency from maternal and paternal sides), and 24 (17.3%) were de novo variants. De novo and rare variants were associated with greater pathogenicity in comparison to inherited and common variants. Two individuals were diagnosed with rare de novo deletions located in 16p11.2 and 17q11.2 regions associated with high susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disorders. Five patients carried a rare pathogenic de novo 15q11.2-q13.1 duplication. The following genes recognized as candidates for ASD were found in the research group: TUBGCP5, CYFIP1, NIPA2, NIPA1, CTNNA3, MACROD2, IMMP2L, PARK2, LZTS2, LRP1, and TBX1. According to the literature, clinically relevant CNVs are identified in 9.3–29% of individuals with idiopathic ASD [25].

In the study from Turkey by Ayaz et al., MYT1L, PXDN, TPO, and AUTS2 genes were elucidated as strong candidates for ASD. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic CNVs in the 2p25.3, 7q11.22, 15q11.2q13.1, and 17p11.2 regions were noted in 5 patients with ASD. The following possible pathogenic duplications were seen together: 1p36.32, 8q24.3, and 9q34 [26].

Martucci et al. enrolled 130 Italian children with ASD and negative testing of the FMR1 gene into the study. In SNP-array testing, 61/130 cases (34%) tested positive for chromosomal imbalances, while 69/130 patients (53%) were negative. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs were diagnosed in 17 children (13%). GPHN, IMMP2L, and ZMYND11 were shown as ASD susceptibility genes [27].

Chehbani et al. assessed aCGH results of 98 Tunisian children and adolescents diagnosed with ASD. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs were found in 11.2% of patients, VUS in 26.5%, and the rest were classified as likely benign or benign. The following recurrent risk loci associated with increased risk of ASD were detected in the study: microdeletions 15q13.3, 16p11.2, 22q13.33, recurrent 15q11.1-q11.2 microduplication [28].

In the study by Calderoni et al., chromosomal microarray results from 90 females with ASD were evaluated. In the cohort, 20 individuals were diagnosed with one or two potentially pathogenic CNVs, including 16p13.2, 16p11.2, 15q11.2, and 22q11.21 regions. Statistically significantly lower repetitive and restrictive behaviors were noted in females with causative CNVs compared to those with non-causative CNVs or with correct CMA results. In the study, 11 of 21 disease-associated genes (ADARB1, ASIC2, CADM2, DMD, GRIN2A, GRM7, NEDD4, NRXN1, PCDH15, PTPRD, TRPM2) are involved in neurotransmission and synaptic structure, which confirm the neural basis of ASD [29].

Wright et al. conducted an analysis of chromosomal microarray genotyping and exome sequencing of 21,532 patients with ASD and 17,785 parents, identifying 1903 returnable likely pathogenic and pathogenic variants in 1861 individuals (8.6%). In the group, 58% were SNVs or indels, 36% were CNVs, and 4% were chromosome aneuploidies. Of the 1861 patients with a result, the most common were deletion that overlaps the 16p11.2 recurrent region in 3.1% (58) individuals, and the 16p11.2 duplication, which was carried by 3% (55) of individuals. The next most often found were 15q11.2 duplications in 2.3% (43) of individuals and 15q13.3 deletions in 2% (36) [30].

The 15q11-q13 region is a well-known locus associated with ASD, one of the most common [21, 25, 27, 31]. Small nuclear ribonucleoprotein polypeptide N (encoded by SNRPN) is linked to Angelman and Prader-Willi syndromes, and variants might impair ribonucleic acid (RNA) processing, neuronal communication, and synaptic function. ATPase phospholipid transporting 10 A gene (ATP10A) is involved in membrane integrity and phospholipid transport, and variants affect synaptic plasticity and connectivity. Gamma‐aminobutyric acid type A receptor subunit beta3 (GABRB3) mediates inhibitory neurotransmission, and variants underlie the excitatory‐inhibitory imbalance in epilepsy and ASD. UBE3A and HERC2 genes are involved in protein degradation [21, 25, 27]. OTUD7A and CHRNA7 genes are supposed to be the primary genes responsible for the phenotype of 15q13.3 microdeletion syndrome. OTUD7A encodes deubiquitinase, which stimulates dendritic growth and spine development, while CHRNA7 encodes the alpha 7 subunit of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, which regulates neuroinflammation and modulates the secretion of the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate and the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [28]. The 15q11.2 region contains 4 conserved genes, including NIPA1, NIPA2, CYFIP1, and TUBGCP5. CYFIP1 encodes cytoplasmic fragile X mental retardation 1 FMR1 interacting protein 1 that regulates cytoskeletal dynamics and interacts with fragile X mental retardation protein and translation initiation factor 4E. It is crucial in neuropsychiatric disorders through inhibition of FMR1 translation in the brain [31].

The second well-documented region in the literature is 16p11.2. Variants in seizure-related 6 homolog‐like 2 (SEZ6L2) are implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders and may contribute to behavioral and synaptic abnormalities [21]. Mitrakos et al. assessed 15 patients with 16p11.2 rearrangements, and 6 of them were affected by ASD; they presented with a deletion on the 16p11.2 region, mainly proximal. About 0.6-1% of all ASD cases are caused by 16p11.2 recurrent rearrangements, which makes it one of the most common genetic reasons for ASD [23, 27]. The 16p11.2 deletion comprises genes exerting important functions in neurodevelopment, like QPRT, MVP, TAOK2, MAPK3, and TBX6 [28]. The prevalence of 16p11.2 deletions was 0.3% (58 of 21,532 ASD patients), and it was the most common finding in the study by Wright et al. [30].

SH3 and multiple ankyrin repeat domains 3 (SHANK3) from the 22q13 region is a key synaptic scaffolding protein connected with ASD and Phelan-McDermid syndrome. SHANK proteins play a role in dendritic spine maturation and synapse formation. Variants might disrupt synaptic organization and signaling [21, 28]. ASD is noted in approximately 40% of Phelan-McDermid cases [28]. The SHANK2 gene (11q13 locus) encodes a synaptic scaffolding protein essential for glutamatergic synapse formation and function, and variants may lead to synaptic dysfunction, affecting social behavior, memory, and learning [21]. The FARP1 gene (chromosomal region 13q32.2) encodes a synaptic scaffolding protein. It regulates synapse function, morphology, and synaptogenesis and promotes dendritic growth and actin assembly [25].

Overall, studies based on MLPA and CMA consistently demonstrate that clinically relevant CNVs (pathogenic or likely pathogenic) are identified in approximately 14% of individuals with ASD, whereas VUS are detected at similar or even higher frequencies. The most frequently recurrent CNVs involve the 1q21, 15q11-q13, 16p11.2, and 22q11.2 regions. Although CNVs affect diverse chromosomal regions, many candidate genes converge on shared biological pathways, including synaptic transmission and plasticity, cell adhesion, neural connectivity, neurodevelopment, neuronal migration, intracellular signaling, cytoskeletal regulation, gene expression, ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, membrane trafficking, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis. Several genes (NRXN1, DOCK8, UBE3A, CHRNA7, and SEZ6L2) can be affected by both deletions and duplications, emphasizing the importance of gene dosage in ASD susceptibility. Moreover, many CNV-associated genes are also implicated in intellectual disability and language disorders, supporting the phenotypic overlap among neurodevelopmental conditions. Despite marked genetic heterogeneity, the recurrence of genes involved in synaptic structure and function supports the view of ASD as a disorder arising from convergent neurodevelopmental pathways rather than from isolated single-gene defects.

Next-generation sequencing is a novel sequencing method that can be approached using three patterns: sequencing of selected gene panels (genes potentially connected with specific phenotype), WES that includes exons/coding regions, and WGS that analyzes intronic/intergenic DNA regions. NGS is a useful tool for the detection of variants in the sequence of genes connected with ASD. So far up to 280 genomic regions that contribute to the development of ASD have been uncovered. It has been estimated that more than 1000 genes promote the pathogenesis of ASD – most of them are involved in neurotransmission [35]. It is estimated that rare gene variants account for approximately 10 to 30% of ASD cases. Genes found in studies relate to ASD through performing different functions, like neurotransmission, neuronal development, memory, and morphology of the nervous system. Particularly, the NF-κB signaling pathway might play a role in ASD through being a regulator in the development and maturation of the nervous system, functioning in synaptic plasticity, learning, memory, and affecting the brain homeostasis, neural connectivity, and inflammation [36].

Sandoval-Talamantes et al. assessed the results of a personalized NGS panel for ASD in 48 patients with ASD. In the cohort of patients being negative for chromosomal microarray analysis and other genetic studies, 12 pathogenic, probably pathogenic, and VUS-likely pathogenic variations in 11 patients, and 11 VUS in 10 patients were detected. It was estimated that isolated genes or loci as a reason for ASD is possible to be found in no more than 0.8% of ASD cases in particular cohorts. Among pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and VUS-likely pathogenic variants, all found in heterozygosity, there were 9 missense (75%), 2 frameshifts (16.66%), and 1 nonsense (8.33%). Among VUS variants, also in heterozygosity, 10 were missense variations (90.9%) and one was an in-frame insertion variant (9.1%). Authors divided genes affected by SNVs into 3 groups: associated with epilepsy (SCN2A, CACNA1A, EEF1A2), intellectual disability (MED13L, MED13), or genetic syndromes (NRXN1 related to Pitt-Hopkins-like syndrome, CREBBP related to Rubinstein-Taybi and Menke-Hennekam syndrome 1) [35].

In the study by Wright et al., the most common likely pathogenic or pathogenic SNVs/indels were loss-of-function and missense variants in SCN2A, SHANK3, and PTEN genes. The most common likely pathogenic or pathogenic recurrent variants were in PTEN NP_000305.3:p.(Arg335Ter), in ADNP NP_001269461.1:p.(Tyr719Ter), and in PTPN11 NP_542168.1:p.(Asn308Asp) [30].

Blázquez et al. performed WES in a cohort of 20 ASD patients and uncovered pathogenic variants in 9 genes strongly associated with ASD. In 90% of cases, the variants appeared de novo. Among those genes ADNP is involved in organogenesis and brain formation, WAC – in dimerizing, chromatin and RNA polymerase II complex binding, ASXL3 – in reading of posttranslational histone modifications and embryonic development, NR4A2 acts as a transcription factor, ALX4 is involved in embryonic development, ANKRD11 controls histone acetylation and gene expression during neural development, POGZ impairs the ability of the POGZ protein to bind to chromatin, BPTF encodes regulatory subunits in chromatin remodeling complexes with a function in DNA replication, transcription, and repair, and SHANK3 encodes a synaptic scaffolding protein [37]. SHANK (SH3 domain and ankyrin repeat-containing) proteins are the main anchoring proteins located in the postsynaptic density that play different roles in synaptic pathogenesis and maturation, elongation of dendrites, glutamate receptor transport, and neuronal signaling. SHANK1, SHANK2, and SHANK3 genes assemble glutamate receptors with other anchoring proteins, adhesion molecules, and elements of the cytoskeleton [38]. Inherited variants in the SHANK1 gene were reported to be more often transmitted by mothers and lead to ASD mainly in males [41]. SHARPIN is a component of the linear ubiquitin chain activation complex. Disturbance of synaptogenesis and signaling pathways might contribute to the pathogenesis of ASD. Wawer et al. suggested that expression of genes from the SHANK family and SHARPIN in the peripheral venous blood mononuclear cells might be used as a possible biomarker of ASD [38].

Khan et al. assessed results from SNP microarray and WES in 36 ASD families (26 complete trios, 8 small multiplex families, 2 proband-parent diads) from Pakistan, in which endogamous marriages are common. Overall, 16 rare or novel coding variants in 15 genes were identified (ARAP1, CDKL5, CSMD2, EFCAB12, EIF3H, GML, NEDD4, PDZD4, POLR3G, SLC35A2, TMEM214, TMEM232, TRANK1, TTC19, ZNF292). 10 homozygous variants (9 missense, 1 loss-of-function), 3 heterozygous de novo variants, and 2 variants in known X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder genes were detected [39].

Chinese scientists confirmed the neurobiological basis of ASD. They analyzed trios WES results of 369 patients, including Chinese probands and unaffected parents, and identified de novo 55 high-impact variants and 165 moderate-impact variants, and de novo CNVs containing ASD-related genes (like duplications of RAI1, UBE3A, deletions of TBR1, SHANK3, MECP2, GIGYF1). High-impact de novo variants (according to Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor) usually resulted in the shortening of protein products by frameshift-causing indels and the gain or loss of STOP codons. Moderate-impact variants were missense SNVs and in-frame indels, which usually did not shorten the sequence of proteins. Expression of genes with de novo variants was higher in the brain in banks of the superior temporal sulcus and the pre-central and post-central gyrus [40]. Neurodevelopmental basis of ASD was also described by Sleyp et al., who studied results of 14 patients with de novo missense variants in KLHL20. Patients presented with intellectual disability, ASD, febrile seizures/epilepsy, hyperactivity, and dysmorphic features. The gene KLHL20 encodes a protein that is part of a CUL3-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase, mediates the transfer of ubiquitin to the recognized substrates, and regulates synaptic development and neurite outgrowth in animal models. In 11 patients, a recurrent variant (NM_014458.4:c.1069G > A p.[Gly357Arg]) was diagnosed [42].

Genetic data from the literature suggested the role of DDX53, PTCHD1, and the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) PTCHD1-AS (PTCHD1 antisense RNA) (Xp22.11 locus) in ASD. Scala et al. identified rare, predicted damaging or loss-of-function variants in DDX53 in 8 ASD males and 2 females from 8 unrelated families. The authors also found gene deletion involving DDX53 and exons of the noncoding RNA PTCHD1-AS in a male and his mother with high-functioning ASD [43]. According to Fu et al., the greatest ASD risk was observed in variation that occurred anew, with less significant risk noted in inherited variants. De novo SNVs and indels were observed more frequently on the paternal allele; a maternal bias was detected for de novo CNVs [45]. Another study completed that homozygous inheritance was seen predominantly in Asperger syndrome, while de novo variants were mainly detected in patients with atypical autism. Variants inherited from mothers presented significant enrichment in intronic regions, showing a potential regulatory role [46].

In the study by Lee et al., assessing NGS panel data from 94 ASD patients, 266 genes containing nonsense, missense, frameshift, and splice site mutations were detected. Benign variants were excluded. Variants were most frequently noted in the following genes: TSC2 (12 times), RAI1 (9 times), CHD7 (7 times), and RELN (7 times). Patients carried from 0 to 34 variants, with 14.44 variants per child on average. Patients with mutations in ion channel-related genes presented higher total IQ and lower repetitive, restricted behavior-related scores and the mannerism subscale of the social responsiveness scale scores [48].

Lopes-Marques et al. performed a meta-analysis of 46,489 germline de novo mutations mainly associated with different developmental and psychiatric human inherited disorders. Mutations were annotated by the Human Gene Mutation Database. ASD emerged as a disease with the highest proportion of de novo mutations (45%). The following genes were shown to be associated with ASD and other neurodevelopmental phenotypes, like SCN1A, ANKRD11, KMT2A, SYNGAP1, SATB2, CHD7, STXBP1, SCN2A, and SHANK3 [49]. A similar set of genes is repeated in many studies concerning the genetic etiology of ASD. Chien et al. indicated NRG2 as the next gene connected with ASD. It plays a role in neurodevelopment, adult brain synaptic plasticity, neuronal migration and differentiation, glial proliferation and differentiation, and regulates the expression of neurotransmitter receptors [50].

Yao et al. performed a multi-trait joint analysis based on summary statistics of the five neuropsychiatric genome-wide association studies. Statistics from a meta-analysis of data from a Danish population-based cohort and five family-based cohorts of trio European patients’ samples (18,381 ASD cases and 27,969 controls) were included in the study. Genome-wide significance threshold was reached by 9 loci for ASD [51].

Sánchez Suárez et al. investigated WES results in 62 patients with ASD and 47 patients with ASD and intellectual disability, who were negative in previous genetic or metabolic testing. Diagnostic yield (sum of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants relative to the total number of variants) by proband in only WES was 3.2% in the ASD group and 12.7% in the ASD/intellectual disability/global developmental delay group [52].

Comprehensive understanding of ASD genetics requires WGS due to the ability to discover almost all pathogenic variations, including non-coding variation, in a single diagnostic test [9, 53]. WGS outperforms both CMA and WES and provides a modest increase in diagnostic yield [53]. Trost et al. analyzed WGS results from 5,100 patients with ASD and 6,212 non-ASD siblings/parents and identified ASD-associated rare variants. Overall, nuclear sequence-level variants constituted 52% of them, nuclear structural variants (CNVs, inversions, large insertions, tandem repeat expansions, uniparental isodisomies) – 46%, and mitochondrial variants (pathogenic variants, haplogroups, heteroplasmy) – 2%. The authors identified 134 ASD-associated genes and multiple ASD-associated variants. The following genes were most frequently affected in patients with ASD by dominant de novo and inherited protein-truncating variants and de novo damaging missense variants in PTEN, KDM5B, MIB1, and CHD8. Multiple translocations, trisomy 21, sex chromosome aneuploidies, and several other chromosomal abnormalities were diagnosed. The following CNVs were the most often noted: 16p11.2, 1q21.1, 15q11-q13, and 22q11.21. NRXN1, PTCHD1-AS, and AUTS2 were ASD-associated genes most affected by structural variants. Pathogenic variants in mitochondrial DNA genes were most frequently observed in MT-TL1 and MT-ND6 [9]. In another study, genome sequencing detected 100% of the pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants identified by CMA and exome sequencing. It also identified an additional diagnostic variant in 0.4% of ASD cases (1 SNV, 1 indel, 1 frameshift insertion, 5 structural variants, like balanced translocation) [53]. Zhou et al. analyzed inherited and de novo coding variants from exome or whole genome sequencing data of 35,130 new ASD cases from the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge and 7,665 cases from published ASD studies. The authors found 60 risk genes, 5 of which (NAV3, ITSN1, MARK2, SCAF1, HNRNPUL2) were not correlated with neurodevelopmental disorders. Results suggested that many ASD patients might benefit from the identification of additional moderate-risk genes, because loss-of-function variants in the 4 moderate-risk genes (NAV3, ITSN1, SCAF1, HNRNPUL2) presented with less cognitive impairment than 129 patients with loss-of-function variants in highly penetrant ASD genes (CHD8, SCN2A, ADNP, FOXP1, SHANK3) [54].

Overall, findings from NGS studies confirm the marked genetic heterogeneity of ASD. Although most genes are identified in only a small proportion of patients, clinically significant variants have been reported in genes such as SCN2A, SHANK3, PTEN, ADNP, CHD7, ANKRD11, NRXN1, RAI1, PTCHD1-AS, MECP2, and CHD8. Despite the diversity of affected genes, many variants converge on common biological pathways, including synaptic function and neurotransmission, neuronal development, cytoskeletal organization, ion channel activity, chromatin remodeling, epigenetic regulation, transcriptional regulation, and ubiquitination. Furthermore, many ASD-associated genes are also implicated in epilepsy, intellectual disability, developmental delay, and other syndromic neurodevelopmental disorders, highlighting substantial clinical overlap across these conditions. These findings suggest that genetically distinct variants may converge on shared neurodevelopmental mechanisms underlying ASD.

Moreover, epigenetic factors, like histone modifications, DNA methylation, and micro ribonucleic acid (microRNA) might play an important role in ASD predisposition. DNA methylation in genes essential for synaptic plasticity (BDNF) or neuroinflammation (IL6), by maternal stress in pregnancy, with mutations in epigenetic regulators (MECP2) may impair neuronal connectivity [21]. MicroRNA might affect the expression of genes associated with neurological pathways involved in ASD. Incorrectly reregulated miRNAs target biological pathways and specific genes, modifying their expression levels, for example the oxytocin receptor gene. Hypomethylation in five CpG sites in the promoter region of the gene coding for microRNA-142 results in effects on oxytocin receptor gene expression. In the central nervous system, the receptor is associated with roles in social, cognitive, and emotional behavior and autistic features. MicroRNA-142-5p decreases the translation of dopamine D1 receptors and negatively regulates the transcription of monoamine oxidase thus influences metabolism of neurotransmitters (serotonin and dopamine). MicroRNA-483-3p influences dendritic and synaptic development and contributes to ASD and impairments in intelligence and behavior [55].

Current guidelines for genetic diagnosis of patients with ASD recommend performing directed genetic studies in the first line (like MLPA), in case of a negative result, chromosomal microarray as a routine method, “gold standard”, then NGS panel testing, NGS WES, or even WGS as the last test. West trio analyses have proven a useful diagnostic tool. Diagnostic effectiveness could be improved by including mitochondrial DNA analysis. Fragile X testing (especially in boys and children with a suggestive family history of intellectual disability in males) and karyotyping also should be performed [35, 37, 56]. However, some studies suggest using NGS WES as a first-tier test instead of CMA for genetic diagnosis of ASD. In 101 Indian patient-parents trios diagnosed with ASD, 29.7% of them received a confirmed genetic diagnosis from WES, 2.9% from chromosomal microarray, and 0% from both FMR1 triplet repeat expansion and karyotype testing. Additionally, WES might lead to the simultaneous detection of SNVs and CNVs. Genes identified in the cohort were involved in synaptic formation, ubiquitination, chromatin remodeling, and transcription [44]. Mahjani et al. concluded that WES might be used as a first-tier clinical approach due to improvement in methods of detecting CNVs in sequencing data and when the co-occurrence of SNV and CNV in some patients is highly possible [47].

Conclusions

ASD is a condition with heterogeneous etiology, both genetic and non-genetic. Due to the possibility of similarities in the symptoms of ASD and other genetic disorders, accurate diagnosis is of great importance. Wider access to modern diagnostic methods such as MLPA, CMA, and NGS has significantly increased the number of ASD patients in whom the genetic etiology of the disorder has been uncovered. Although numerous structural and sequence variants have been identified, the reviewed studies demonstrate that many ASD-associated genes converge on common biological pathways involved in synaptic function, neuronal development, transcriptional regulation, and intracellular signaling. The findings also highlight the considerable overlap between ASD and other neurodevelopmental conditions, including intellectual disability and epilepsy, emphasizing the complexity of genotype-phenotype relationships. The description of genetic changes occurring in ASD patients contributes to a better understanding of the etiology of ASD and clarifies the correlation between genotype and phenotype. Knowledge of the genetic background in ASD would be applicable in the diagnosis, prevention, prediction of prognosis, and individualized treatment of patients from the earliest age. Further studies are needed to identify additional genetic factors and better understand their clinical significance.
