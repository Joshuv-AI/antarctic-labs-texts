Superior lesion detection on 3D FSE T1WI with magnetization transfer and CHESS preparation pulses in children with focal epilepsy: Preliminary results
Authors: Xing‐Chang Wei, Zarina Assis, Martin Sherriff, Walter J Hader, Juan Pablo Appendino
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn childhood drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, the identification of a magnetic resonance imaging lesion significantly affects the management and prognosis, although it is often challenging. Herein we report the preliminary results of a modified MR sequence, in which both magnetization transfer and chemical shift selective preparation pulses are added to a 3D fast spin echo T1‐weighted sequence to recognize focal cortical dysplasia. The scan time is short, and the images have expected uniform suppression of the background normal gray and white matter. We report four children with focal epilepsy, in whom the focal cortical and subcortical lesions are superiorly conspicuous on the aforementioned MR sequence compared to the high‐resolution fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery images obtained with typical epilepsy MR protocols.Keywords: drug resistant epilepsy, focal cortical dysplasia, magnetic resonance imaging, magnetization transfer

INTRODUCTION

In children with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, identification of a focal cortical lesion on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that is consistent with the seizure semiology and electroencephalogram (EEG) localization drastically affects patient management and outcome. However, focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), the most common etiology in children with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, often has subtle signal intensity and morphological changes on MRI. Many efforts have been made to improve the sensitivity of MRI in the detection of subtle cortical lesions, especially FCDs. Comprehensive MRI protocols have been proposed and widely adopted in clinical practice.1, 2 Yet, identification of such subtle structural lesions remains challenging.

Magnetization transfer (MT) effects or MT contrast in MRI is based on the interaction and exchange (i.e., transfer) in magnetization between hydrogen protons that are bound to macromolecules such as lipids and lipoproteins and those protons that are unbounded. In clinical MRI, it is achieved by adding a preparation radiofrequency (RF) pulse to the regular RF pulse sequences such as T1‐ or T2‐weighted spin‐echo or gradiant‐echo sequence. 3 While it has been extensively used to study brain disorders such as multiple sclerosis, 4 its application in detection of epileptogenic lesions is limited and not included in the recommended standard MR protocols for epilepsy.1, 2

In 1997, Girard et al. reported that spin‐echo T1WI with added MT preparation pulse revealed more cortical tubers in patients with tuberous sclerosis. 5 In 2006, a study by Pinto Gama et al. further revealed that spin‐echo T1WI with MT is also far superior to the other sequences in detecting white matter lesions in patients with tuberous sclerosis. 6 The beneficial effects of spin‐echo T1WI with MT in detection of FCD in children with seizures were demonstrated in a small case series by Kadom et al. 7 Of note, cerebral tubers in tuberous sclerosis are histologically indistinguishable from FCD with balloon cells (FCD type IIb). 8

Chemical shift selective (CHESS) is one of the four types of fat suppression techniques that are widely used in clinical MRI. 9 It utilizes the difference in resonant frequency of fat hydrogen protons and water hydrogen protons. Interestingly, this fat saturation preparation RF pulse has an unintended MT effect in multislice imaging sequences. 10 Based on this, Kusama et al. added CHESS to a 2D spin‐echo T1‐weighted sequence and reported two cases of FCD type IIb, where the FCDs were hyperintense on T1WI with CHESS and more conspicuous than on the standard T2‐weighted FLAIR images. 11 More recently, in a study by Fujii et al. on tuberous sclerosis, T1WI with CHESS was shown to be significantly superior to T1WI with MT for the detection of radial migration lines, while no significant difference was found between T1WI with CHESS sequence and T1WI with MT sequence for the detection of cortical tubers. 12

At the authors' center, the clinical 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens) has an option of adding both MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses to a standard 3D FSE T1‐weighted sequence (SPACE, Sampling Perfection with Application optimized Contrasts using different flip angles). We hypothesize that by adding both the MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses, as opposed to applying either of them, the MT effect will be more prominent and potentially result in a higher sensitivity to FCD. Also, using a 3D rather than a 2D sequence would allow a useful multiplanar reformation of the source images, hence, a better visualization of the FCDs. Since October 2022, after proper testing and optimization, we added such a 3D FSE T1‐weighed sequence with both MT and CHESS to our epilepsy brain MRI protocol. Herein we report our preliminary results on four cases with presumed diagnosis of FCD. The study was approved by the institutional research ethics board.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Imaging methods

The patients were scanned with our institutional standard epilepsy brain MR protocol. A 64‐channel head coil was used. The 3D FSE T1‐weighted SPACE sequence with MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses were performed in a sagittal plane with a TR of 400 ms, TE of 9 ms, echo train length of 44, slice thickness of 1 mm, NEX of 1.4, and a total scan time of 2 min and 45 s. The source images were reformatted into orthogonal axial, coronal, and sagittal planes with a 3‐mm slice thickness and 1‐mm increment. The conspicuity of the focal cortical and subcortical lesions on the 3D FSE T1W images with MT and CHESS was compared to that on the high‐resolution FLAIR images obtained with typical epilepsy MR protocols. In three of the four patients, the FLAIR sequences include a 3D acquisition in the sagittal plane with a 0.9‐ or 1‐mm thickness and a 2D acquisition in the coronal plane with a 2‐ or 3‐mm thickness. The source images of the 3D FLAIR sequence were reformatted into orthogonal axial, coronal, and sagittal planes. In the other patient, the FLAIR sequences include an axial and a coronal 2D acquisition with a 2‐mm slice thickness.

Patient 1

This is a 5‐year‐old girl with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy and frequent seizures. A brain MRI performed elsewhere at the age of 32 months showed a possible left posterior insula FCD. A limited MRI prior to stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) electrode placement was done in our center. A 3D T1‐weighted SPACE sequence with MT and CHESS was performed at this time, which showed the FCD more clearly compared to the standard high‐resolution FLAIR images (Figure 1). A laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) targeting at the visible lesion was performed 1 week after the repeat MRI. On the 9‐month postsurgical visit, the child was seizure‐free and recovered from transient postprocedure right arm and hand weakness.

Superior lesion detection on 3D FSE T1WI with MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses. On the standard high‐resolution FLAIR images (upper row) obtained at the age of 32 months, a subtle subcortical focal lesion is seen in left posterior insula (circles, A–C). The lesion is minimally hypointense on the 3D MPRAGE T1WI (not shown) and cannot be appreciated on T2WI, DWI, or postcontrast MPRAGE T1WI. Such a subtle lesion could easily be overlooked in daily practice. The location of this lesion fits the seizure semiology and was confirmed to be epileptogenic on subsequent SEEG. On the reformatted images of 3D FSE T1WI with MT and CHESS (lower row) obtained 1 day before placement of SEEG electrodes, the lesion is much more conspicuous (arrows, D–F) and is hyperintense, as opposed to being hypointense on MPRAGE T1WI. No corresponding hyperdensity can be appreciated on CT scan (not shown). MRI performed 9 months after LITT procedure showed focal necrosis in this region (not shown). Patient 2

This is a 10‐year‐old boy with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy since the age of 3 years. Multiple brain MR scans were performed elsewhere, the most recent of which was done at the age of 5 years and showed a subtle focal cortical lesion in the left anterior insula, probably an FCD. He was referred to our center for further investigation including SEEG. A limited MRI was performed locally 2 days prior to SEEG electrode placement. A 3D T1‐weighted SPACE sequence with MT and CHESS was included, which showed the FCD more clearly compared to the standard high‐resolution FLAIR images (Figure 2). An LITT procedure targeting the visible lesion was performed 9 days after the repeat MRI. Purified neuronal DNA analysis of neurons and astrocytes collected from the two anterior insular depth electrodes identified a somatic missense MTOR variant. 13 On the 6‐month postsurgical visit, only rare nondisabling, brief sensory seizures with preserved awareness were reported.

Patient 3

This is a 12‐year‐old girl with frequent, single‐type, focal seizures. The seizure semiology includes waking up from sleep, right‐arm numbness, impaired awareness, and tonic posturing. She also had episodes of right arm numbness during wakefulness. Her interictal EEG was unremarkable suggesting a deep epileptogenic zone. She was recently started on levetiracetam. Her MRI showed a probable FCD in the left postcentral gyrus, which is better seen on the 3D FSE T1WI with MT and CHESS than on the FLAIR images (Figure 3).

Patient 4

This is a 3‐year‐old girl with focal epilepsy since the age of 10 months. Seizure semiology comprises gasping breath at onset, followed by rhythmic twitching of bilateral orbicularis oculi and right facial muscles, then irregular respirations with neck hyperextension and purposeless arm movements for 5–20 s without postictal confusion. Awareness was preserved. Sleep EEG showed focal epileptiform discharges in the left central head region. Her seizures were treated with levetiracetam. Her MRI showed a probable FCD in left middle frontal gyrus, which is better seen on the 3D FSE T1WI with MT and CHESS than on the FLAIR images (Figure 4).

RESULTS

The new MR sequence of 3D FSE T1‐weighed SPACE with added MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses can be run with a scan time of <3 min. The normal cerebral cortex and white matter are uniformly suppressed and have lower signal intensity than on our standard 3D FSE T1WI without MT or CHESS. Both the gray and white matter appear darker on images with minimal gray‐white matter differentiation. The obtained source images have 1 mm thickness and appear noisy, but the reformatted images with 3 mm thickness have an acceptable signal‐to‐noise ratio (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4).

All patients in this report have clinical diagnoses of FCD based on seizure semiology, EEG localization, and MRI findings. Two of them are further supported by SEEG and postsurgical outcomes. In all four patients, the cortical/subcortical lesions are visible on standard high‐resolution FLAIR images, but the lesions are more conspicuous and appear larger on the 3D T1WI with MT and CHESS (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4).

DISCUSSION

In this report, we demonstrated the feasibility of adding both the MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses to a 3D FSE T1‐weighted sequence. In the previous studies,5, 6, 7, 11, 12 either an MT or a CHESS preparation RF pulse, not both of them, was applied. In those studies, the preparation RF pulses were added to 2D spin‐echo T1‐weighed sequences, as opposed to a 3D sequence in the current studies. Those 2D scans have a slice‐thickness of 3–5 mm, which essentially makes the images reformatted into other planes useless for the detection of subtle cortical lesions like an FCD. The 3D acquisition allows for multiplanar reformatted images with high signal‐to‐noise ratio and sufficient anatomical details. Yet, the total scan time of the sequence is <3 min. Compared to the 3D FSE T1WI that most neuroradiologists are familiar with, the signal intensity of the background normal gray and white matter is obviously suppressed, and the gray‐white matter differentiation is decreased (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4), which is most likely due to the doubled MT effect from both the MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses.

The results presented here show the benefits and great potential of this 3D FSE T1‐weighed sequence with MT and CHESS. On the multiplanar reformatted images of the modified MR sequence, the cortical and subcortical focal lesions are larger and more obvious and noticeable than on the high‐resolution FLAIR images acquired with standard clinical epilepsy MR protocols. The increased conspicuity may be partially due to the suppressed signal of the background normal brain tissue. The increased conspicuity enhanced the diagnostic certainty of focal cortical lesions in patients 1, 2, and 4 (Figures 1, 2 and 4). In patient 3, even though a subcortical lesion is unequivocally seen on the FLAIR images, the larger area of signal abnormality seen on the new sequence changed the estimation of lesion size, which may alter the surgical intervention technique (Figure 3).

The limitations of this report include the lack of histopathologic information on the MR lesions. Nonetheless, all the focal lesions on MRI fit the seizure semiology and EEG localization. In patients 1 and 2, the MR lesions were found to be epileptogenic on invasive SEEG, and the patients became seizure‐free or nearly seizure‐free after the LITT procedure. In the two cases reported by Kusama and colleagues, the lesions seen on the 2D FSE T1WI with CHESS were confirmed to be FCD on histopathology. 11 Like their cases, the lesions in our cohort are also hyperintense on the T1WI, but the biophysical basis of the T1‐hyperintensity is uncertain. In the study by Kimura and colleagues, 14 nine out of the 26 FCDs with transmantle sign have hyperintense T1 signal and corresponding hyperdensity on CT. The T1‐hyperintensity was attributed to a richer density of balloon cells. In the current report, none of the three patients who had brain CT scans had corresponding hyperdensity on CT. Further studies are needed to assess whether such a discrepancy is because of a higher sensitivity of the new sequence, and whether it can reveal FCD lesions earlier before degenerative calcifications had occurred.

While the preliminary results presented here demonstrated the feasibility of the new MR sequence and its benefits of higher lesion conspicuity with higher diagnostic certainty, further studies with histopathological correlation are needed to better understand the underlying biophysical basis of the signal change. Further studies with larger sample sizes are also required to further assess its impact on the diagnosis and management of children with epilepsy.

CONCLUSION

The 3D FSE T1WI with MT and CHESS preparation RF pulses can be performed in a clinically feasible time frame. The preliminary results reported here demonstrated its potential of providing higher conspicuity of FCD compared to the high‐resolution FLAIR images obtained with typical epilepsy MR protocols.
