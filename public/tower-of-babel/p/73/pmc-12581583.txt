Integrative effects of melatonin and putrescine on vase life and physiological traits in cut alstroemeria ‘Amatista’
Authors: Nazdar Mirzaei Esgandian, Zohreh Jabbarzadeh, Reza Darvishzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the physiological, biochemical traits, molecular responses, and vase life of cut Alstroemeria hybrida ‘Amatista’ in response to preharvest applications of putrescine and melatonin. A factorial experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design with three replications. The treatments included foliar sprays of putrescine (0, 1.5, and 3 mM), melatonin (0, 50, and 100 µM), and four postharvest sampling times (0, 5, 10, and 15 days). Measured parameters included chlorophyll index, relative fresh weight, solution uptake, ion leakage, malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity, vase life, and the relative expression of chlorophyllase (CHL) and ACC oxidase (ACO) genes. Postharvest senescence was associated with a time-dependent decline in chlorophyll index, water content, and solution uptake, along with increased ion leakage, MDA, H₂O₂ levels, and PPO activity. However, preharvest application of putrescine and melatonin effectively delayed these detrimental changes. Notably, the combined application of 1.5 mM putrescine and 50 µM melatonin resulted in the greatest improvement in postharvest performance, extending vase life from 16.00 to 23.66 days and enhancing physiological and biochemical traits. Moreover, gene expression analysis indicated that these treatments suppressed the expression of CHL and ACO, suggesting a molecular mechanism involved in delayed senescence. Overall, the findings highlight the potential of putrescine and melatonin as eco-friendly preharvest treatments to improve the postharvest quality and longevity of cut Alstroemeria flowers by modulating key physiological, biochemical, and molecular processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07530-6.Keywords: ACC oxidase, Chlorophyllase gene expression, Polyamine, Plant growth regulation, Senescence, Vase life

Introduction

Alstroemeria hybrida, commonly known as the Peruvian lily, belongs to the family Alstroemeriaceae and is highly valued as a cut flower for its vibrant colors and long-lasting blooms. Petal colors range from purple, yellow, orange, peach, pink, red, to bicolor patterns, making it popular in both domestic and international markets. This herbaceous perennial propagates mainly through rhizomes and produces erect stems with glabrous leaves and terminal, umbel-shaped inflorescences, contributing to its high ornamental appeal [1, 2].

Over the past two decades, numerous commercial hybrids of Alstroemeria have been developed, positioning the genus among the top 10 cut flowers worldwide. It is primarily cultivated in the United States, England, Colombia, and the Netherlands, thriving in pots as well as sub-temperate to temperate climates. Alstroemeria flowers are available year-round, producing approximately 50–60 blooms per plant annually, with vase life lasting up to two weeks, making them highly valued by florists and consumers alike [3].

Among these cultivars, Alstroemeria ‘Amatista’ has gained notable popularity in commercial floriculture due to its vibrant flower color, extended blooming period, and strong market demand. Although precise production statistics are limited due to private greenhouse operations, estimates indicate that Alstroemeria represents one of the top 10 cut flowers globally, with ‘Amatista’ being among the most cultivated hybrids in key production regions. Despite its commercial success, like other cultivars, ‘Amatista’ is susceptible to rapid postharvest deterioration, including water loss, oxidative stress, and ethylene-induced senescence [4]. These challenges highlight the necessity of investigating preharvest strategies to enhance vase life and maintain flower quality, addressing both scientific questions and market demands.

Physiological processes such as premature senescence, reduced water uptake, increased ethylene production, and oxidative stress markedly shorten the vase life and commercial value of cut Alstroemeria flowers. Key contributors to postharvest deterioration include wilting, impaired water absorption, heightened sensitivity to exogenous ethylene, oxidative membrane damage, and leaf chlorosis [5, 6]. Leaf senescence, a form of programmed cell death, is characterized by irreversible physiological and biochemical changes, including the upregulation of senescence-associated genes (SAGs), chlorophyll degradation, and cellular disintegration [7]. Among these changes, leaf yellowing is typically the earliest visible symptom, largely attributed to the degradation of chlorophyll–protein complexes [8]. Previous studies have identified several chlorophyll catabolic enzymes—such as chlorophyllase 1 and 2, pheophytin pheophorbide hydrolase, and pheophorbide a-oxygenase—as key players in chlorophyll breakdown. Their activity is significantly upregulated during senescence and in response to stress, and is tightly regulated by senescence-associated phytohormones and abiotic factors [9]. Chloroplasts are among the earliest organelles targeted for degradation, where the activation of these enzymes at the chloroplast membrane facilitates chlorophyll catabolism. Simultaneous dismantling of chloroplast structure further intensifies pigment degradation in aging leaves [10].

To mitigate these challenges, the application of environmentally safe and effective growth regulators has attracted considerable interest. In particular, putrescine and melatonin have emerged as promising agents for enhancing postharvest quality through modulation of physiological, biochemical, and molecular pathways in ornamental plants. Melatonin is a multifunctional molecule in plants, playing critical roles in abiotic stress tolerance, seed germination, root development, and fruit ripening [10–12]. Functioning as a potent antioxidant and free radical scavenger, melatonin protects plant tissues against oxidative damage [13, 14], mediating its effects through regulation of gene expression and crosstalk with plant hormones—including abscisic acid, ethylene, gibberellins, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid—as well as signaling molecules such as hydrogen peroxide, hydrogen sulfide, and nitric oxide [15–17]. Melatonin has been shown to delay senescence, enhance cellular antioxidant defenses, improve ATP production, reduce activity of senescence-related enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase, suppress ethylene biosynthesis by downregulating ACO, reinforce aquaporin activity, maintain cell wall integrity, and decrease chlorophyll degradation [13, 18–22]. Its efficacy in maintaining relative water content, minimizing ion leakage, and alleviating oxidative stress has been demonstrated in cut flowers such as tuberose (Polianthes tuberosa) [23], carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus) [24], and Alstroemeria, where postharvest application of melatonin (100 and 200 µM) prolonged vase life from 12 to 20–21 days [25].

Polyamines, including putrescine, are low-molecular-weight nitrogenous compounds that play pivotal roles in physiological and developmental processes, including cell division, organogenesis, flowering, fruit maturation, leaf senescence, and responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses [26–28]. Under stress, endogenous polyamine levels often increase, contributing to cellular homeostasis by stabilizing membranes, scavenging ROS, and regulating gene expression and enzyme activities. Polyamines also interact with phytohormones and ethylene biosynthesis, thereby influencing growth and senescence mechanisms [27]. Previous studies have demonstrated that polyamine treatments, such as spermidine, spermine, and putrescine, can extend vase life and improve water relations in cut flowers [29, 30], highlighting their potential as postharvest longevity agents.

Notably, melatonin and putrescine may exhibit synergistic effects due to their complementary mechanisms. While melatonin modulates gene expression, antioxidant defenses, and hormone signaling, putrescine primarily stabilizes membranes and scavenges ROS. Their combined action is hypothesized to more effectively mitigate oxidative stress, maintain water balance, delay chlorophyll degradation, and suppress senescence-related signaling than either compound alone [31]. Despite these promising prospects, information on combined preharvest application in Alstroemeria is limited, and the precise molecular mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to evaluate the physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of Alstroemeria hybrida ‘Amatista’ to combined preharvest foliar application of melatonin and putrescine, with the aim of enhancing vase life, improving postharvest quality, and elucidating the potential mechanisms contributing to delayed senescence.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

In this study, Alstroemeria cv. Amatista, propagated through rhizome division, was used. Plant material was obtained from a commercial greenhouse in Varamin, Iran. Originally developed by Royal Van Zanten (The Netherlands), this cultivar features deep purple flowers, dark green foliage, and flowering stems averaging 70–90 cm in height. The typical vase life of ‘Amatista’ is approximately two weeks. Individual florets measure about 7.5 cm in length and 15–20 cm in diameter [2]. The general morphology of the ‘Amatista’ cultivar is shown in Fig. 1.

The Amatista cultivar used in the present study

Growth medium and environmental conditions

Plants were grown in a soilless system using a substrate consisting of 70% cocopeat and 30% perlite. To reduce salinity, the cocopeat was thoroughly leached for one week before being mixed with perlite and filled into plastic pots (7 L volume; 19 cm height; 24 cm top diameter). During cultivation, greenhouse temperatures were maintained at 21–25 °C during the day and 13–17 °C at night. Light intensity ranged from 10,000 to 15,000 lx on cloudy days to 30,000–50,000 lx on sunny days, while relative humidity was kept at approximately 60% during the day and 70% at night.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted as a factorial arrangement within a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications during the preharvest stage under controlled greenhouse conditions using a soilless cultivation system. The experimental factors included three melatonin concentrations (0, 50, and 100 µM), three putrescine concentrations (0, 1.5, and 3 mM), and four postharvest sampling times (0, 5, 10, and 15 days after harvest). Treatment concentrations were selected based on preliminary trials and previous reports [32, 33].

To prepare the melatonin solution, the required amount was first dissolved in 0.5 mL ethanol, followed by 0.5 mL Tween-80, and then diluted to the final volume with distilled water. Melatonin and putrescine were applied as foliar sprays at two-week intervals over two months, totaling four applications per treatment. Control plants received foliar sprays of distilled water containing ethanol and Tween-80 in the same proportions to ensure consistency across treatments.

Nutrient management program used in this study

During the first week after rhizome planting, root-promoting fertilizers were applied to support early root development. A complete fertilizer ([10–52]– [10]) was applied at 100 g per 100 L of water, along with Basilar at 100 mL per 100 L, over a two-week period. Postharvest measurements

For postharvest assessments, cut flowers were harvested early in the morning. Flower stems bearing 1–2 florets at the opening stage were selected and immediately transported to the laboratory. Lower leaves were removed, and stem bases were recut under running water to a uniform length of 50 cm (initial stem lengths ranged from 60 to 80 cm). Prepared stems were then placed in vases containing 250 mL of distilled water supplemented with 4% sucrose. Flowers were maintained under controlled conditions of 22 °C, 70% relative humidity, and a 12-hour photoperiod with a light intensity of 13 µmol m−2 s−1 throughout the vase life. Sampling was performed every five days until the end of the vase life period (Fig. 3).

Alstroemeria flower stems during vase life assessment under controlled environmental conditions

Determination of physicochemical parameters

Evaluation of relative fresh weight (RFW) of flowers

To determine relative fresh weight (RFW), flower stems were weighed immediately after harvest (the first day). Subsequently, stems were removed from the vase solution at five-day intervals throughout the vase life, and fresh weights were recorded using an analytical balance. Relative fresh weight was calculated following Joyce and Jones [34] and expressed as the ratio of the fresh weight at each sampling time to the initial fresh weight, according to the following formula:

RFW = FWi/FW0.

where FWi​ is the fresh weight at the sampling time (g) and FW0​ is the fresh weight on the day of harvest (g).

Measurement of relative solution uptake of flowers

Relative solution uptake (RSU) was determined by placing flower stems in 250 mL of holding solution and recording the decrease in solution volume every five days. RSU was calculated following Aalaei et al. [35] and expressed as milliliters per gram of initial fresh weight using the following formula: RSU = WUi/FW0.

where WUi​ is the volume of solution absorbed on the sampling day (mL), and FW0​ is the initial fresh weight of the stem on the first day (g).

Petal relative water content (RWC)

Petal discs (1 cm²; three per replicate) were weighed to determine fresh weight (FW) using a precision balance (± 0.0001 g). The discs were then floated in distilled water at 4 °C for 4 h, gently blotted, and reweighed to obtain turgid weight (TW). Samples were subsequently oven-dried at 72 °C for 48 h to determine dry weight (DW). Relative water content (RWC) was calculated following Chakrabarty et al. [36] using the formula: RWC = FW-DW/TW_DW×100.

Leaf chlorophyll index

Chlorophyll content was estimated non-destructively using a chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502 Plus, Konica Minolta, Japan). Measurements were taken on the middle portion of fully expanded young leaves. Three readings per leaf were recorded from three leaves per plant, and the average was used as the SPAD value [37].

Petal ion leakage

Ion leakage was determined using 0.5 g of petal tissue, which was rinsed with distilled water and incubated in 15 mL of distilled water at 40 °C for 30 min. The initial electrical conductivity (EC1) was measured, after which samples were heated at 100 °C for 10 min, cooled to room temperature, and the final conductivity (EC2) recorded. Ion leakage (%) was calculated following Lutts et al. [38] using the formula: Ion leakage (%) = (EC1/EC2) × 100.

Petal malondialdehyde (MDA) content

To determine malondialdehyde (MDA) content, 0.5 g of petal tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. Then, 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of a solution containing 20% TCA and 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min and immediately cooled on ice. After a second centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min, absorbance was measured at 532 and 600 nm. MDA content was calculated using the following formula and expressed as µmol g⁻¹ fresh weight [39]: MDA (µmol/g FW) = (A532 – A600/155)×100.

Petal hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content was determined following Velikova et al. [40]. Briefly, 0.5 g of fresh petal tissue was homogenized in 3 mL of 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 15 min. The reaction mixture consisted of 0.5 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide, and 0.5 mL of the supernatant. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 35 °C for 1 h, and absorbance was measured at 390 nm. H2O2 concentration was expressed as µmol g⁻¹ fresh weight.

Petal polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity was assayed based on catechol oxidation following Pizzocaro et al. [41]. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.5 mL of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 4.6) containing 50 mM catechol as substrate and 300 µL of methanolic enzyme extract. The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min, and absorbance changes were recorded at 420 nm over a 3-minute period. One unit of PPO activity was defined as an increase of 0.001 absorbance units per minute per mL of enzyme extract. Vase life

Vase life was defined as the period during which cut flowers retained their commercial and ornamental quality, remaining suitable for market sale. For Alstroemeria, vase life was determined as the time until 50% of the leaves exhibited yellowing or 50% of the florets had abscised [42]. Flowers were monitored daily to accurately assess these parameters.

Relative expression of target genes

To evaluate the expression of CHL (chlorophyllase) and ACO (1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase) genes, petal samples were collected at four postharvest time points (0, 5, 10, and 15 days), with three biological replicates per time point. Gene expression levels were quantified to investigate molecular changes during the vase life.

Primer design

Target gene sequences were identified through a comprehensive literature review and verified via BLAST searches against the Alstroemeriaceae and Liliaceae genome databases. Specific primers were designed using Gene Runner software and validated through NCBI BLAST analysis. Primer synthesis was performed by TAG Copenhagen (Table 2).

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from 80 mg of petal tissue using the Gene X RNA Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen, homogenized with 800 µL of TRIzol reagent, and thoroughly vortexed before incubation at room temperature. Subsequently, 300 µL of chloroform was added, followed by vortexing, incubation, and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was carefully collected, mixed with precipitation buffer, and applied to the RNA purification column. After sequential washing and centrifugation steps, RNA was eluted in 100 µL of DEPC-treated water preheated to 65 °C. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and samples were stored at − 80 °C until further use.

RNA quantification and quality assessment

RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The absorbance ratios at 260/280 nm and 260/230 nm were used to assess RNA integrity and purity, and RNA concentration was reported in ng/µL.

cDNA synthesis

First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the SMOBIO cDNA Synthesis Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5 µL of total RNA was mixed with 1 µL of Oligo dT/Random Primer Mix and DEPC-treated water to a final volume of 10 µL (Mix A). This mixture was incubated at 70 °C for 5 min and then immediately chilled on ice for at least 10 min. Separately, 4 µL of 5× reverse transcription buffer (containing DTT and dNTPs), 1 µL of reverse transcriptase, 1 µL of RNase inhibitor, and 4 µL of DEPC-treated water were combined (Mix B). Mixes A and B were then combined and incubated sequentially at 25 °C for 10 min, 42 °C for 50 min, and 85 °C for 5 min to terminate the reaction.

Real-Time PCR analysis

Gene expression levels were quantified using a Real-Time PCR kit (Sinaclon, Iran). Each 15 µL reaction contained 1 µL of cDNA, 0.5 µL of each forward and reverse primer, 7.5 µL of 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, and 5.5 µL of PCR-grade water. Reactions were gently mixed to avoid bubble formation. Primer annealing temperatures were optimized through melting curve analysis and verified by gel electrophoresis to confirm specificity and correct amplicon size. Amplifications were performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at primer-specific temperatures for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. Gene expression analysis was carried out using the comparative Ct method (ΔΔCt) as described by Livak and Schmittgen [43]. First, ΔCt values were calculated as the difference between the Ct of the target gene and the Ct of the reference gene (GAPDH): ΔCt = Ct (Target) – Ct (Reference). Subsequently, ΔΔCt values were obtained by comparing the ΔCt of each treatment with that of the calibrator (control sample). Relative gene expression levels were expressed as 2^–ΔΔCt. Primer efficiencies were validated prior to analysis to ensure reliable quantification. All qPCR reactions were performed with three biological replicates, each including three technical replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS software version 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The experiment was conducted as a factorial arrangement in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three factors: melatonin concentration, putrescine concentration, and sampling time. The complete set of treatment combinations included all possible levels of these three factors and is presented in Table S1 (Supplementary Material). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the main effects and interactions. Mean comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at a 5% significance level. For molecular data, mean comparisons were carried out using the Student–Newman–Keuls (SNK) test.

For multivariate analyses, including heatmap visualization, Pearson’s correlation analysis, and principal component analysis (PCA), R Studio software (version 2024.09.0 + 375) was used. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were calculated along with their corresponding p-values to assess the strength and significance of relationships among measured traits. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied. Correlation analyses were performed using data obtained from three biological replicates per treatment.

Results and discussion

Relative fresh Weight, relative solution uptake of flowers, and relative water content of petals

Throughout the postharvest period, all treatments exhibited a gradual decline in relative fresh weight, solution uptake, and petal water content. However, flowers pre-treated with putrescine and melatonin displayed a markedly slower rate of reduction compared to the control. The combined application of these compounds was particularly effective in maintaining the relative fresh weight over the entire vase life. The most effective treatment, 50 µM melatonin combined with 3 mM putrescine, sustained the relative fresh weight of the petals for up to 15 days after harvest (Fig. 4A). This same combination also resulted in significantly higher relative solution uptake, peaking at 2.93 mL g⁻¹ initial fresh weight on day 5, whereas the control showed the lowest uptake (1.09 mL g⁻¹ fresh weight) by day 15 (Fig. 4B). Similarly, the decline in petal relative water content occurred more gradually in the melatonin and putrescine-treated flowers. While most of these treatments were not significantly different from each other in terms of water content, the control consistently exhibited the lowest values on days 5, 10, and 15 postharvest (Fig. 4C).

The present study revealed that relative fresh weight, relative solution uptake, and petal relative water content gradually declined over time in cut Alstroemeria flowers. Nevertheless, preharvest application of putrescine and melatonin significantly mitigated these reductions. Senescence in cut flowers is typically accompanied by water loss, which disrupts the balance between water uptake and transpiration, reduces cell turgor pressure, and ultimately compromises flower freshness. Maintaining proper water relations and adequate uptake after harvest is therefore critical for prolonging vase life, as any disturbance in water balance accelerates senescence and diminishes marketability. Relative fresh weight provides a reliable measure of plant water status, directly influencing both flower opening and the progression of senescence. Postharvest changes in water relations are often associated with bacterial blockage or the formation of air embolisms within xylem vessels [44]. In addition, decreases in relative fresh weight may result from reduced vase solution uptake [5]. These findings are consistent with those of Aalifar et al. [45], who reported similar declines in fresh weight during the vase life of carnation flowers.

Putrescine, a key polyamine in plants, plays a vital role in prolonging postharvest longevity and maintaining the quality of cut flowers by regulating water balance and enhancing relative water content. One of the major challenges in cut flower preservation is the rapid loss of water from tissues due to transpiration and evaporation. Putrescine alleviates this water loss primarily by promoting stomatal closure through the suppression of ethylene activity and by increasing cellular resistance, which together reduce transpiration rates and help retain water within petal cells [46]. In addition, putrescine stabilizes cell membranes by binding to membrane phospholipids, thereby minimizing ion leakage and preventing excessive water loss. This membrane stabilization is further supported by enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes, which protect cells from oxidative damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) typically generated during postharvest stress [47, 48]. Moreover, polyamines like putrescine can directly interact with water molecules, thereby increasing the water retention capacity of cells. Several studies have confirmed these mechanisms, showing that putrescine application improves relative fresh weight and water uptake in cut flowers, including Lisianthus (Eustoma grandiflorum), Lily (Lilium spp.), and Chrysanthemum morifolium, ultimately enhancing vase life and overall postharvest quality [47, 49, 50].

In this study, petal relative water content (RWC) progressively declined during the vase life, reflecting a reduction in cellular hydration status. RWC is a crucial physiological indicator of plant water status and is closely associated with cell turgor and metabolic activity [51]. Exogenous application of putrescine has been shown to enhance water uptake and reduce transpiration in cut flowers, thereby maintaining higher RWC levels [30]. Mechanistically, putrescine improves vascular conductivity by alleviating xylem blockages, which commonly result from microbial growth or embolism formation postharvest [52]. In addition, putrescine stimulates the biosynthesis of osmolytes, which help retain cellular water under stress conditions [25]. Furthermore, it enhances drought tolerance mechanisms that collectively support water balance maintenance [53]. These physiological effects likely underlie the observed stabilization of petal water content in the present study. Consistent with this, Vijayalaxmi et al. [30] reported that spermidine treatments significantly increased RWC and reduced petal wilting in cut Gerbera flowers, further highlighting the beneficial role of polyamines in postharvest water regulation.

The results of this study indicated that melatonin application markedly mitigated the decline in relative fresh weight, solution uptake, and petal relative water content during the vase life of cut Alstroemeria flowers. In cut flowers, parameters associated with water retention and uptake are closely linked to vase life, as disruptions in water relations often result in incomplete flower opening, poor bloom development, and accelerated stem wilting [54]. Melatonin enhances water retention by reducing oxidative stress, including electrolyte leakage, modulating hormonal balance, promoting water uptake, suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and stimulating protein synthesis [55]. Furthermore, melatonin regulates stomatal aperture through the modulation of aquaporin gene expression and other factors affecting stomatal conductance, thereby controlling transpiration and maintaining tissue hydration [56]. These mechanisms likely explain the improved water relations observed in Alstroemeria petals in this study. Similar beneficial effects of melatonin on maintaining fresh weight and relative water content have been reported in other ornamental species; for example, treatment of cut carnation flowers with 0.1 mM melatonin significantly increased relative fresh weight compared to controls [24], while application of 250 µL L⁻¹ melatonin in cut tuberose flowers prevented declines in relative water content of both leaves and petals [23].

While previous studies have established the efficacy of either putrescine or melatonin in maintaining postharvest water relations in a limited number of ornamental species, the present study provides novel insights by demonstrating the superior effectiveness of their combined preharvest application in cut Alstroemeria. Our findings reveal that this approach not only improved relative fresh weight, solution uptake, and petal water content but also introduced a proactive preventive strategy. This strategy ensures that flowers enter the postharvest phase with enhanced physiological resilience, a significant advancement beyond earlier work that primarily focused on postharvest treatments. Thus, our findings advance the understanding of how preharvest interventions can be leveraged to bolster water balance mechanisms and significantly delay senescence in commercially important cut flowers.

Chlorophyll index

Both putrescine and melatonin applications significantly enhanced the leaf chlorophyll index before harvest and effectively slowed chlorophyll degradation during the postharvest period. All tested concentrations, applied individually or in combination, contributed to chlorophyll retention, although the magnitude of their effects varied with concentration. The highest chlorophyll index values were observed in treatments combining 1.5 mM putrescine with all melatonin levels, as well as in the 3 mM putrescine treatment without melatonin, with no statistically significant differences among these groups across all sampling times. As shown in Fig. 5, the chlorophyll index declined over time, reflecting the natural senescence process, yet this reduction was substantially slowed in treatments containing putrescine and melatonin, particularly those with 1.5 mM putrescine alone or combined with melatonin. The results of this study showed a progressive decline in chlorophyll content in cut Alstroemeria flowers during the postharvest period. However, treatments with 1.5 mM putrescine combined with melatonin significantly slowed chlorophyll degradation compared to the untreated control and other treatment groups. Senescence in plants, particularly in harvested cut flowers, is a tightly regulated and complex physiological process characterized by a gradual loss of photosynthetic capacity, dismantling of chloroplasts and chlorophyll molecules, catabolism of essential macromolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, and remobilization of nutrients to developing tissues. Postharvest senescence is typically accelerated following detachment from the parent plant, with Alstroemeria flowers displaying similar patterns [57]. A major postharvest challenge in cut Alstroemeria is rapid chlorophyll degradation leading to leaf yellowing, which substantially reduces the aesthetic and commercial value of the flower stems despite preserved floral bud vitality. Consequently, maintaining foliar greenness is a critical quality attribute during postharvest handling and marketing of Alstroemeria [58]. The observed reductions in leaf chlorophyll and associated yellowing in this study are in agreement with Lopez-Guerrero et al. [59], who reported significant chlorophyll loss in Eustoma grandiflorum exposed to various vase solutions.

Preservation of chlorophyll content is one of the key quality attributes during the postharvest phase of cut flowers. Loss of leaf greenness primarily results from chlorophyll degradation, a process largely catalyzed by the enzyme chlorophyllase [60]. In addition to this enzymatic activity, disruptions in endogenous hormonal balance and impaired water transport to the upper stem and floral tissues also contribute significantly to the accelerated decline of leaf greenness [61].

Putrescine application effectively contributed to the preservation of leaf greenness in cut Alstroemeria flowers. As a major polyamine, putrescine plays a vital role in maintaining chlorophyll content in postharvest ornamental flowers [62]. A key factor driving chlorophyll degradation after harvest is hormonal imbalance, particularly elevated ethylene production, which accelerates senescence and programmed cell death. Putrescine has been reported to inhibit both ethylene biosynthesis and action, thereby reducing chlorophyll loss [63]. Furthermore, putrescine supports chlorophyll retention by maintaining water content in floral tissues, as water deficit is a primary trigger for chlorophyll degradation [64]. In addition, the antioxidant properties of polyamines suppress reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, further protecting chlorophyll molecules from oxidative damage [65]. These findings are consistent with those of Vijayalaxmi et al. [30], who showed that polyamine treatments preserved chlorophyll content in cut Gerbera flowers, supporting the observations of the present study.

The results of the present study indicated that melatonin application significantly slowed the decline of the chlorophyll index during the postharvest period. Previous studies have shown that melatonin enhances levels of key chlorophyll biosynthesis precursors, such as protoporphyrin and magnesium protoporphyrin, thereby improving photosynthetic capacity and increasing carbohydrate reserves in plants. As a result, melatonin-treated plants exhibit greater tolerance to various stressors, particularly postharvest stresses, by providing additional energy through enhanced photosynthetic efficiency to better cope with adverse conditions [66, 67]. Furthermore, postharvest application of melatonin has been reported to downregulate the expression of chlorophyll degradation-related genes and suppress the activity of enzymes such as chlorophyllase, while simultaneously upregulating genes involved in stress signaling and response pathways [66, 68]. This regulatory effect contributes to delayed leaf senescence. In the present study, melatonin likely mitigated chlorophyll degradation through similar molecular mechanisms, as supported by data showing reduced CHL gene expression. These findings are consistent with Brito et al. [69], who reported that 100 µM melatonin preserved chlorophyll content in leaves of Hippeastrum hybridum. Similarly, Zhou et al. [55] observed that foliar application of 1 mg L⁻¹ melatonin-maintained leaf greenness in cut carnation flowers. Moreover, SeyedHajizadeh et al. [4] demonstrated that melatonin effectively preserved chlorophyll in Gerbera, corroborating the results of the present study.

The combined preharvest application of putrescine and melatonin proved highly effective in preserving chlorophyll content and enhancing leaf quality in cut Alstroemeria, thereby delaying visible senescence. This finding extends the scope of previous research, which has largely documented the postharvest effects of these compounds applied individually on a limited range of species. By demonstrating the superior, synergistic efficacy of a preharvest strategy, our study introduces a more proactive approach for ensuring the physiological resilience and commercial appeal of cut flowers from the very start of the postharvest phase.

The findings of this study revealed a progressive increase in oxidative parameters—including ion leakage, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) accumulation—over the postharvest period. Cut flowers experience multiple physiological stresses after harvest, with oxidative stress, dehydration, impaired ATP synthesis, and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) being particularly detrimental. These stressors disrupt cellular homeostasis primarily by damaging biological membranes, especially the plasma membrane, which increases membrane permeability and promotes ion leakage [70]. Under such conditions, membrane lipids undergo peroxidation, and membrane-associated proteins are degraded, ultimately compromising membrane integrity. This damage facilitates the efflux of essential ions, including potassium (K+), calcium (Ca2+), and other solutes from the cytoplasm. A well-established relationship exists between increased membrane permeability and flower senescence, as elevated ion leakage contributes to loss of cellular turgor, petal wilting, and an overall decline in ornamental quality [71].

Flower senescence is closely linked to increased membrane permeability and the excessive accumulation of free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS), processes largely promoted by ethylene biosynthesis and signaling. Overaccumulation of ROS induces oxidative damage to cellular components, particularly membrane lipids, resulting in the formation of toxic byproducts such as malondialdehyde (MDA)—a well-established secondary product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation. MDA is widely recognized as a reliable biochemical marker of senescence, as lipid peroxidation generates both lipid peroxides and MDA [47]. Accordingly, MDA levels serve as a key indicator of oxidative stress and the extent of membrane structural deterioration during postharvest aging in plants. In cut flowers, enhanced lipid peroxidation is among the earliest and most irreversible signs of senescence, activating phospholipid-degrading enzymes such as phospholipase and lipoxygenase. This enzymatic activity increases membrane permeability and compromises membrane integrity [72], leading to leakage of essential solutes and a subsequent decline in cellular turgor. As senescence progresses, sustained ion leakage and MDA accumulation further accelerate physiological deterioration, resulting in wilting, discoloration, and reduced vase life [5]. Consistent with these mechanisms, the present study showed that postharvest progression significantly increased ion leakage and MDA content in petals of cut Alstroemeria ‘Amatista’ flowers. These observations align with previous reports; for example, Zulfiqar et al. [23] noted a marked rise in MDA content in cut tuberose (Polianthes tuberosa) flowers during the vase life period. Importantly, exogenous application of putrescine and melatonin—either individually or in combination—substantially mitigated the time-dependent increases in ion leakage, MDA accumulation, and hydrogen peroxide content, indicating that both compounds effectively alleviated oxidative stress, preserved membrane stability, and delayed senescence in cut Alstroemeria flowers.

Polyamines, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, are small aliphatic amines with multiple positive charges that play a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis under stress conditions. These cationic molecules interact electrostatically with negatively charged phospholipid head groups in cellular membranes and with pectic substances in cell walls, enhancing membrane compactness and reducing permeability. Such biophysical stabilization of membranes is particularly important during postharvest senescence, when membrane deterioration is a major contributor to quality loss in cut flowers [30]. By preserving membrane integrity, polyamines effectively limit ion leakage. Beyond their structural roles, polyamines serve as potent modulators of the cellular antioxidant system. Exogenous application of putrescine has been shown to upregulate key ROS-scavenging enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and protecting cellular membranes from oxidative damage [73]. This dual functionality—both structural and biochemical—positions polyamines as essential regulators of oxidative stress mitigation during postharvest handling. Furthermore, polyamines influence the transcriptional regulation of stress-responsive genes and suppress ethylene biosynthesis, a hormone strongly associated with the onset of senescence. By modulating ethylene signaling, polyamines contribute to delayed chlorophyll degradation, reduced petal wilting, and extended vase life. In line with these protective roles, Farooq et al. [74] reported a significant reduction in ion leakage following polyamine treatment in cut Antirrhinum majus flowers, consistent with the present findings in Alstroemeria. Overall, these results underscore the multifaceted role of polyamines as membrane stabilizers, ROS scavengers, and modulators of hormone signaling pathways, all of which collectively enhance postharvest performance and delay senescence in ornamental species.

Lipid peroxidation, reflected by malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation, is a well-established marker of oxidative damage to cellular membranes during plant senescence. In the present study, putrescine treatment markedly reduced both MDA content and electrolyte leakage, indicating decreased lipid oxidation and enhanced membrane stability. These results are consistent with previous findings in cut Lisianthus flowers, where putrescine effectively lowered MDA levels and preserved membrane integrity [47]. The antioxidant activity of polyamines, particularly putrescine, plays a central role in mitigating oxidative stress, either through direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or by enhancing the activity of enzymatic antioxidants [75]. One mechanism involves the direct neutralization of ROS, which are known to initiate peroxidative degradation of membrane-bound polyunsaturated fatty acids. Alternatively, putrescine may act indirectly by modulating the expression or activity of ROS-scavenging enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase and catalase. Regulation of lipoxygenase (LOX) activity is also critical for maintaining membrane integrity. Senescing floral tissues typically exhibit elevated LOX activity, which accelerates lipid peroxidation and membrane deterioration. Previous studies have shown that exogenous putrescine can downregulate LOX activity in cut flowers [47], thereby reducing membrane damage and delaying senescence. Supporting this, Mohammadi et al. [57] demonstrated that increased LOX activity correlates with higher membrane permeability and advanced senescence in Gerbera flowers. In line with these observations, the present study found that reduced LOX activity following putrescine treatment likely contributed to lower MDA content and decreased ion leakage in Alstroemeria petals. This is further corroborated by Jowkar et al. [76], who reported that treatment with spermine and spermidine significantly decreased MDA levels in cut Rosa hybrida cv. Dolce Vita, highlighting the multifaceted role of polyamines in postharvest quality maintenance. Overall, these findings suggest that putrescine alleviates oxidative membrane damage through multiple mechanisms, including ROS scavenging, suppression of LOX-mediated lipid peroxidation, and enhancement of antioxidant defense pathways. Such protective effects contribute to delayed floral senescence and extended vase life in cut Alstroemeria.

In the present study, melatonin application significantly reduced electrolyte leakage, malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content in cut Alstroemeria flowers, highlighting its strong protective role against oxidative stress and membrane deterioration during postharvest senescence. Detachment from the mother plant induces abiotic stress, which often triggers excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, compromising membrane stability and accelerating cellular damage. Consistent with previous reports, melatonin effectively attenuates MDA accumulation and ion leakage, thereby preserving membrane integrity under various stress conditions [77, 78]. These protective effects arise from both direct and indirect antioxidative mechanisms. Melatonin’s amphiphilic structure allows it to localize within both lipophilic and hydrophilic cellular compartments, enhancing its ability to scavenge a wide spectrum of ROS and synergize with diverse antioxidant systems [78]. Its hydrophilic functional groups directly neutralize cytotoxic ROS intermediates, thereby preventing the initiation and propagation of lipid peroxidation in cellular membranes [79]. Moreover, melatonin has been reported to downregulate the expression and activity of lipid-degrading enzymes associated with β-oxidation, contributing to the stabilization of membrane lipids and improved cellular resilience [80]. Collectively, these multifaceted actions position melatonin as a central regulator of cellular redox homeostasis.

The reductions in MDA, H2O2, and electrolyte leakage observed in this study are likely attributable to melatonin’s ROS-scavenging activity, its inhibitory effect on oxidative lipid degradation, and its ability to maintain membrane structural and functional integrity. These findings are in agreement with Zulfiqar et al. [23], who reported decreased electrolyte leakage in cut tuberose flowers following melatonin application, and with Wang et al. [32], who observed significant reductions in both electrolyte leakage and MDA accumulation in two Paeonia cultivars (‘Qi Hua Lu Shuang’ and ‘Da Fu Gui’). The consistency of these results across diverse ornamental species underscores the broad-spectrum efficacy of melatonin in mitigating oxidative membrane damage and enhancing postharvest longevity. Overall, the present study reinforces melatonin’s potential as a promising biostimulant for improving physiological stability and marketability of cut flowers during vase life.

Building on the established role of putrescine and melatonin in alleviating oxidative stress, our study reveals the pronounced superiority of their combined preharvest application. In cut Alstroemeria, this dual treatment served as a powerful preemptive defense, significantly curbing ion leakage, lipid peroxidation, and H₂O₂ accumulation throughout the vase life. This robust mitigation of oxidative damage directly resulted in enhanced membrane integrity and a consequential delay in senescence. Our preharvest strategy thus moves beyond the reactive measures of prior postharvest studies, offering a foundational and practical method for bolstering physiological resilience and extending vase life, which directly translates to greater commercial value.

The results of this study indicate that both the progression of postharvest time and the application of putrescine and melatonin were associated with a reduction in polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity. Putrescine, a key member of the plant polyamine family, is known to stimulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, superoxide dismutase, and ascorbate peroxidase, which protect cellular components from oxidative damage [81, 82]. By enhancing the antioxidant defense system, putrescine helps maintain cellular integrity and reduces oxidative stress. Simultaneously, putrescine effectively inhibits oxidative enzymes like PPO, thereby limiting phenolic oxidation and preserving tissue antioxidant capacity throughout the postharvest period [83, 84].

In this study, the application of putrescine at concentrations of 1.5 and 3 mM significantly reduced PPO activity in cut Alstroemeria flowers, contributing to the maintenance of petal quality and delaying tissue browning during the vase life period [84]. These findings are supported by previous studies reporting similar protective effects of polyamines on phenolic metabolism and postharvest quality in ornamental species. For instance, Farooq et al. Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) is a ubiquitous enzyme present in fungi, bacteria, and plants, including ornamental flowers, where it plays a key role in the oxidative degradation of phenolic compounds. Elevated PPO activity at the cut surface promotes the formation of brown pigments, contributing to vascular blockage and impaired water uptake, ultimately reducing flower longevity [85]. In the present study, putrescine application significantly suppressed PPO activity in cut Alstroemeria flowers. This reduction is likely due to the antioxidant properties of putrescine, which mitigate oxidative stress by enhancing the activity of key antioxidant enzymes. In addition, polyamines such as putrescine can inhibit ethylene biosynthesis, consequently reducing ethylene-mediated induction of PPO activity [86].

Polyamines, which carry positive charges at physiological pH, can interact with anionic groups within the active site of PPO, inducing conformational changes that reduce the enzyme’s catalytic efficiency. Additionally, polyamines can form stable complexes with phenolic substrates, limiting PPO access and decreasing the rate of phenolic oxidation even when the enzyme remains active [29, 87]. By activating antioxidant enzymes and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, polyamines help prevent non-specific PPO-mediated phenol oxidation. Supporting these findings, do Nascimento Simões et al. [87] reported that spermidine application (2 mM) in cut Anthurium flowers effectively suppressed PPO activity, consistent with the present results.

Similarly, melatonin treatment in this study reduced PPO activity while helping maintain petal phenolic content, thereby contributing to prolonged flower longevity. Melatonin is known to modulate ROS levels and reinforce antioxidant defenses, which collectively limit oxidative damage and phenolic oxidation [88]. Naghiloo et al. [89] also reported that melatonin application in cut Anthurium flowers reduced PPO activity, supporting the results observed here. Melatonin-induced upregulation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes has been reported in Rosa rugosa, leading to improved coloration and quality, which aligns with the protective effects observed in the present study [90]. Collectively, through antioxidant activation, hormonal modulation, and reinforcement of defense mechanisms, melatonin contributes to reduced PPO activity and enhanced postharvest quality in ornamental flowers.

This study provides a novel contribution by demonstrating that the combined preharvest application of putrescine and melatonin significantly suppresses PPO activity in cut Alstroemeria. This finding is crucial, as unchecked PPO activity is a key driver of enzymatic browning and senescence. While previous research has often examined these compounds in isolation, our work identifies a specific optimal concentration range (1.5 mM putrescine with 50 µM melatonin) for maximizing this inhibitory effect. This offers a targeted and practical preharvest strategy to delay deterioration and maintain the aesthetic quality of cut flowers.

These results indicate that the synergistic action of putrescine and melatonin effectively delays senescence and enhances postharvest longevity in cut Alstroemeria flowers.

The cut flower industry represents an important segment of horticulture, where both initial visual quality and vase life are critical determinants of consumer satisfaction and repeat purchases. Enhancing postharvest longevity directly improves market value and economic returns for floricultural products [91]. Alstroemeria, valued for its vibrant color diversity and distinctive floral morphology, is highly popular in the global cut flower market. Nevertheless, this species, like many other ornamental flowers, faces significant postharvest challenges that compromise quality and commercial appeal. Premature leaf yellowing is a major postharvest issue in Alstroemeria, which markedly diminishes ornamental value. This senescence symptom is typically associated with reduced chlorophyll biosynthesis and increased activity of chlorophyll-degrading enzymes. Vase life is primarily governed by two physiological factors: ethylene and water uptake. Ethylene, a plant hormone, accelerates senescence in numerous flower species, shortening postharvest longevity [92]. Simultaneously, impaired water uptake—often caused by vascular occlusion—leads to wilting and further reduces vase life. Such vascular blockages have been extensively documented in species like Rosa spp., where they critically limit postharvest performance [5]. Cut Alstroemeria flowers not only produce ethylene but also exhibit high sensitivity to exogenous ethylene [93]. Therefore, strategies that mitigate ethylene effects while promoting water uptake are of substantial economic and practical importance for extending the postharvest life and marketability of these ornamental flowers.

Polyamines share the common biosynthetic precursor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) with ethylene and are well-known for their role in delaying senescence. They achieve this, in part, by inhibiting ACC synthase, a key enzyme in ethylene biosynthesis, thereby reducing ethylene production. In addition, polyamines contribute to the stabilization of cellular membranes by preventing lipid peroxidation, enhancing membrane integrity and resilience under postharvest stress [29, 94]. Previous studies have demonstrated the senescence-delaying effects of polyamines in various cut flower species. For example, Haq et al. [29] reported that polyamine treatments in roses increased leaf sugar, protein, and RNA contents, collectively mitigating the activity of peroxidases and other senescence-related enzymes. In line with these observations, the present study found that putrescine application substantially improved key physiological and biochemical parameters in cut Alstroemeria flowers, including relative fresh weight, petal relative water content, and both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant activities. Simultaneously, indicators of oxidative stress—such as electrolyte leakage, malondialdehyde (MDA), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content—were significantly reduced. These physiological and biochemical improvements likely underlie the observed extension of vase life in putrescine-treated Alstroemeria flowers. Supporting evidence from other ornamental species corroborates these results: Bandara et al. [95] reported that putrescine extended the vase life of orchid flowers to 25.6 days, Jowkar et al. [76] observed enhanced postharvest longevity in cut roses following polyamine application, and Vijayalaxmi et al. [30] documented a 4–5 day increase in vase life in Gerbera flowers treated with polyamines. Collectively, these findings highlight the effectiveness of polyamines, particularly putrescine, in mitigating senescence and enhancing postharvest quality in cut flowers.

The present study demonstrated that melatonin application, particularly at 50 µM, significantly prolonged the vase life of cut Alstroemeria flowers. A primary factor limiting postharvest longevity in cut flowers is oxidative stress, which induces cellular degradation, compromises quality, and shortens shelf life. Oxidative stress is characterized by the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anions, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals, which can damage cellular membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids. Plants rely on efficient defense systems, notably antioxidant enzymes, to counteract these detrimental effects [96]. Melatonin mitigates oxidative stress by enhancing the activity of key antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidases (POD), which scavenge ROS and prevent cellular damage. In addition to enzymatic defenses, melatonin modulates non-enzymatic antioxidants, including phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which directly neutralize free radicals and further reduce ROS levels [97]. In the current study, melatonin-treated flowers exhibited higher total phenolic and flavonoid contents, likely contributing to the observed reduction in oxidative injury. The reinforcement of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems by melatonin was associated with improved physiological traits, such as maintenance of petal relative water content, delayed wilting, and preservation of flower coloration and visual quality—key determinants of postharvest performance [98]. These results are in agreement with previous studies: Lezoul et al. [24] reported that melatonin extended the vase life of Dendrobium orchids by up to 10 days; Wang et al. [32] observed a 1.6-fold increase in vase life in Paeonia cultivars; and Zulfiqar et al. [23] documented enhanced longevity in Polianthes tuberosa following melatonin treatment. Collectively, these findings underscore melatonin’s multifaceted role in mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing postharvest longevity in ornamental cut flowers.

Importantly, this study advances previous work by being the first to systematically evaluate the combined preharvest application of putrescine and melatonin in cut Alstroemeria flowers—a strategy not extensively explored for this species. The identification of a synergistic treatment (1.5 mM putrescine combined with 50 µM melatonin) as the most effective regimen provides crucial insights for optimizing vase life extension protocols. By demonstrating that this combined treatment simultaneously supports multiple key postharvest processes—including the maintenance of water relations, chlorophyll preservation, reduction of oxidative stress, and suppression of PPO activity—this study offers a more holistic and mechanistic understanding of how preharvest priming enhances longevity. These findings address a significant knowledge gap and provide a practical, evidence-based strategy for improving the postharvest quality and marketability of Alstroemeria.

Chlorophyllase gene expression in cut flowers typically rises as senescence progresses, reflecting its central role in chlorophyll degradation and leaf aging [60]. During the early postharvest period, flowers maintain a relatively stable physiological status, resulting in low chlorophyllase expression. However, advancing senescence and the accumulation of oxidative stress, along with the weakening of antioxidant defenses, trigger a marked upregulation of this gene, accelerating chlorophyll breakdown and the loss of green pigmentation [99].

Putrescine, a principal polyamine, plays a pivotal role in delaying plant senescence and mitigating its detrimental effects on postharvest quality. Its application has been shown to downregulate chlorophyllase gene expression, thereby slowing chlorophyll degradation in cut flowers [100]. As a biosynthetic precursor to spermidine and spermine, putrescine regulates polyamine metabolism and indirectly delays senescence through its antioxidant activity and modulation of stress response pathways [101]. By mitigating the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), putrescine suppresses the signaling cascades that would otherwise activate chlorophyll catabolic genes such as CHL [102, 103]. Furthermore, putrescine interacts with hormonal pathways, particularly ethylene and abscisic acid (ABA), both of which are key regulators of chlorophyllase expression during senescence. By modulating ethylene biosynthesis and signaling, putrescine reduces the transcription of chlorophyll catabolic genes. While some studies suggest it may influence transcription factors involved in chlorophyllase regulation, any direct interaction remains speculative and requires further investigation [104–106]. Beyond gene-level effects, putrescine helps maintain membrane and chloroplast integrity, preserving photosynthetic machinery and preventing the generation of intracellular senescence signals that would otherwise enhance chlorophyll degradation [105]. Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to the preservation of leaf greenness, delayed senescence, and improved postharvest longevity in cut flowers.

Melatonin is a multifunctional molecule that exerts potent antioxidant, anti-senescence, and growth-regulating effects in plants. In cut flowers, melatonin has been shown to delay leaf senescence and inhibit chlorophyll degradation primarily through the downregulation of chlorophyllase gene expression [106]. This regulatory effect is largely mediated by its ability to mitigate oxidative stress: melatonin directly scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), thereby preventing ROS-induced activation of chlorophyll catabolic genes such as chlorophyllase [107]. Additionally, melatonin suppresses ethylene biosynthesis and signaling, which are major promoters of leaf senescence and chlorophyllase expression, leading to further downregulation of these genes [108]. Melatonin also stabilizes chloroplast ultrastructure by protecting thylakoid membranes, thus preventing retrograde signaling that would otherwise upregulate chlorophyllase expression [21]. Genomic studies indicate that melatonin modulates transcription factors associated with stress and senescence, resulting in reduced expression of chlorophyllase isoforms CLH1 and CLH2 [106]. Furthermore, melatonin influences hormonal homeostasis by increasing cytokinin levels and decreasing abscisic acid (ABA) concentrations, promoting chlorophyll maintenance and delaying senescence, since cytokinins repress while ABA induces chlorophyll degradation gene expression [109]. Collectively, these multifaceted actions of melatonin contribute to the preservation of leaf greenness and enhanced postharvest longevity in cut flowers.

ACC oxidase (ACO) is a pivotal enzyme in ethylene biosynthesis and plays a central role in regulating plant senescence. In cut flowers, ACO expression typically increases over time, particularly during the senescence phase. This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) into ethylene, a plant hormone that accelerates senescence. Upregulation of ACO leads to elevated ethylene production, which in turn hastens flower senescence and shortens vase life. High ethylene levels induce rapid quality deterioration and adverse visual changes in cut flowers. Moreover, ACO expression is often triggered by environmental stresses such as water deficit, mechanical injury, and temperature fluctuations, further enhancing ethylene synthesis and promoting postharvest senescence. Therefore, the progressive increase in ACO expression over time serves as a reliable indicator of enhanced ethylene biosynthesis and the advancement of floral aging [110, 111].

Putrescine, a principal polyamine in plants, can mitigate the expression of the ACO gene in cut flowers through multiple, interconnected mechanisms. Since S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) serves as a common precursor for both polyamine and ethylene biosynthesis, elevated putrescine levels may redirect SAM toward polyamine synthesis, thereby limiting substrate availability for ethylene production and downregulating ethylene biosynthetic genes, including ACO [96–116]. Additionally, polyamines are known to activate anti-ethylene signaling pathways that suppress the transcription of ethylene biosynthesis and signaling genes. Putrescine also exerts antioxidant effects, stabilizing cellular membranes and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, which can otherwise induce stress-responsive ethylene biosynthesis and ACO activation [103]. Emerging evidence further suggests that polyamines may influence epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling, potentially leading to the repression of ACO expression, although such mechanisms remain underexplored in ornamental plants [111]. Collectively, these multifaceted actions highlight putrescine’s central role in delaying ethylene-mediated senescence by directly or indirectly repressing ACO expression and reducing ethylene production.

Melatonin, like putrescine, plays a crucial role in modulating senescence in cut flowers by suppressing the expression of ACO gene through both direct and indirect mechanisms. It interferes with ethylene signaling pathways by inhibiting ethylene perception or downstream transcription factors, thereby downregulating ethylene biosynthetic genes including ACO [116]. As a potent antioxidant, melatonin scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS), whose accumulation in petal cells promotes senescence and positively regulates ACO expression. By mitigating oxidative stress and reducing ROS levels, melatonin suppresses ROS-mediated signaling cascades that would otherwise enhance ACO transcription [117]. Moreover, melatonin interacts with other phytohormones such as abscisic acid (ABA), salicylic acid (SA), and jasmonic acid (JA) to finely tune hormonal crosstalk, supporting the maintenance of floral freshness and delaying senescence partly through suppression of ethylene-related genes like ACO [118]. Melatonin also downregulates ethylene biosynthesis genes, including ACS and ACO, via inhibition of transcription factor activity or through epigenetic modifications such as histone acetylation and methylation. By stabilizing membrane integrity and preventing ion leakage—hallmarks of senescence—melatonin further inhibits the activation of stress-responsive pathways that would otherwise upregulate ACO expression [119]. Collectively, these multifaceted actions highlight melatonin’s central role in repressing ACO expression and delaying ethylene-mediated senescence in cut flowers.

This study provides a novel, mechanistic [120] advancement by [121] demonstrating [122] that preharvest [123] application of putrescine and melatonin synergistically downregulates key senescence-related genes in cut Alstroemeria. Specifically, our results show that the combined treatment significantly suppresses the expression of both CHL and ACO. While the role of these compounds has been studied individually, their combined preharvest effect at the transcriptional level represents a significant leap in understanding. The identification of an optimal combination (1.5 mM putrescine with 100 µM melatonin) that co-represses these two distinct senescence pathways offers a powerful new strategy for delaying deterioration and improving postharvest quality in ornamental flowers.

The correlation matrix revealed strong interrelationships among the evaluated postharvest traits in Alstroemeria cut flowers. Vase life was positively correlated with relative water content (RWC), chlorophyll index and relative solution uptake (RSU), while it showed strong negative correlations with ion leakage, malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), ACO and chlorophyllase gene expression, and PPO activity. These patterns indicate that maintaining water status, membrane stability, and antioxidant defenses is crucial for prolonging vase life. Moreover, the close association between oxidative stress markers (ion leakage, MDA, H2O2) and the expression of senescence-related genes (CHL and ACO) underscores their central role in the progression of floral senescence. Collectively, these results suggest that interventions that mitigate oxidative damage and downregulate senescence-associated genes can effectively enhance postharvest longevity in Alstroemeria (Fig. 12) .

Principal component analysis (PCA)

The PCA biplot clearly illustrates the effects of various preharvest melatonin and putrescine treatments on the postharvest traits of Alstroemeria ‘Amatista’. The first two principal components (PCs) together explain 75.9% of the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 60.7% and separating treatments primarily based on oxidative stress markers (e.g., MDA, H₂O₂, ion leakage, ACO gene expression) along the positive axis. PC2, representing 15.2% of the variance, differentiates treatments according to water status and antioxidant enzyme activities. Treatments combining 1.5 mM putrescine with 50 µM melatonin (Put 1.5 mM + Me 50) and 3 mM putrescine with 50 µM melatonin (Put 3 mM + Me 50) clustered closely with quality-associated traits, including relative water content (RWC), vase life, and PPO activities, relative solution uptake (RSU), and chlorophyll index, indicating strong improvements in physiological performance and postharvest longevity. In contrast, the untreated control (Put 0 mM + Me 0) and the high-dose melatonin-only treatment (Put 0 mM + Me 100) were closely associated with senescence-related parameters, reflecting their lower postharvest quality (Fig. 13 ) .

The integrated analyses of heatmap, correlation matrix, and PCA consistently showed that the combined preharvest application of 50 µM melatonin and 1.5 mM putrescine had the strongest positive effect on physiological traits and postharvest quality of Alstroemeria ‘Amatista’. PCA further confirmed the distinct association of this treatment with key quality indicators. Overall, these results highlight the synergistic and dose-dependent role of melatonin and putrescine in enhancing performance and delaying senescence in cut Alstroemeria flowers.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that preharvest foliar application of putrescine (1.5 and 3 mM) and melatonin (50 µM) significantly enhanced the vase life of cut Alstroemeria flowers. Both 1.5- and 3-mM putrescine produced comparable improvements across most physiological and biochemical parameters, while 50 µM melatonin consistently exerted the most beneficial effects. Among all treatments, the combination of 1.5 mM putrescine and 50 µM melatonin was the most effective, significantly increasing relative solution uptake and relative fresh weight, thereby maintaining turgidity and delaying senescence. These treatments also suppressed electrolyte leakage, MDA and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation, and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity. Chlorophyll degradation progressed more slowly in treated flowers, accompanied by downregulation of senescence-associated genes, including CHL and ACO. Collectively, these physiological, biochemical, and molecular effects underlie the extension of vase life from 16 to 23.66 days. Considering both efficacy and cost-effectiveness, the preharvest application of 1.5 mM putrescine combined with 50 µM melatonin is recommended as an optimal strategy to improve postharvest quality and prolong the longevity of cut Alstroemeria flowers.
