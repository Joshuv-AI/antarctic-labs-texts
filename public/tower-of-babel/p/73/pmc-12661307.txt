Synthetic cells by the numbers
Authors: Chana G Sokolik, Maya Bar-Dolev, Ron Milo, Katarzyna P Adamala, Michael Levy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCan we build a living cell from non-living molecular components? This foundational question drives the field of synthetic cell engineering, having already reconstituted key cellular processes within synthetic environments, and now advancing toward their integration into systems of increasing complexity. At this stage of gradual complexification, it is essential to construct a general quantitative picture of synthetic cells that can clarify the capabilities and limitations of current systems and help guide future design efforts. Here, we adopt the “by the numbers” approach to provide a quantitative and intuitive overview of synthetic cell properties. Focusing on liposome-based systems, we compile and contextualize numerical estimates from the literature and use them to reason about the structural, biochemical, and functional characteristics of these synthetic constructs. Through this quantitative lens, we aim to highlight both the current performance achieved and the key challenges that remain in the path toward building autonomous synthetic cells.Subject areas: mathematical biosciences, experimental models in systems biology, synthetic biology

Introduction

The field of synthetic cell engineering is driven by a profound yet simple question: Can we build a living cell? More specifically, can we assemble a self-replicating, autonomous, and evolving system from non-living molecular components? This pursuit is more than an exercise in bioengineering; it challenges us to rethink fundamental questions about life itself. What defines life? How do the properties of living systems emerge from the interactions of their molecular parts? What can we learn about the origins of life by attempting to recreate it? And can we develop new types of living systems?

The journey toward building synthetic cells has already produced remarkable milestones. Researchers have reconstituted key cellular functions, such as gene expression,1,2,3 DNA replication,4 phospholipid biosynthesis,5 and chemical energy production,6,7 within artificial compartments like lipid vesicles. Recently, efforts have shifted toward integrating multiple functions into single systems, pushing the boundaries of complexity.8,9 For example, one recent study successfully combined gene expression, DNA self-replication, and membrane biosynthesis within cell-sized liposomes.8 These achievements on multiple fronts bring us closer to creating synthetic life, especially when we recall that the overarching goal of the field is the bottom-up assembly of a minimally complex living system rather than reproducing the full complexity of natural cells.10,11

A dimension that remains underexplored, however, and could critically contribute to the achievements of this field, is the quantitative characterization of synthetic cells. Numbers related to various processes, such as reaction rates, energy requirements, or molecular concentrations, exist in the literature, but are scattered across studies without integration into a cohesive framework. As a result, even simple questions like “how close are synthetic cells to living cells?” can be difficult to address, especially for non-specialists. We argue that constructing a general quantitative picture of synthetic cells will not only clarify their relationship to biological cells but also provide valuable intuition about their capabilities, limitations, and areas for improvement. Ultimately, such an approach could guide the design of more efficient and robust synthetic systems.

Here, we will explore synthetic cell engineering through the lens of biological numeracy, applying the “by the numbers” approach to this field.12,13 We will summarize key measurements into simple order-of-magnitude estimates and use these numbers to reason about synthetic cells’ performance relative to natural cells. We aim to provide a clear and accessible quantitative overview of synthetic cells’ properties while highlighting areas where further progress is needed.

Synthetic cell engineering is an inherently diverse field. Some systems aim to mimic protocells,14,15 the primitive ancestors of modern cells, while others embrace hybrid designs that incorporate non-biological components16,17,18 or novel strategies unavailable during evolution.19,20,21,22 Some studies use lipid vesicles as cellular compartments to reproduce lipid-based biological membranes,3,23,24 while others assemble polymersomes,17,25,26,27 coacervates,28,29 or microfluidic compartments to model the cell volume.30,31,32,33 This wild diversity presents a significant challenge for drawing a general and representative quantitative picture of synthetic cells. Therefore, we decided to limit the scope of this work and focus our analysis on the most common and biologically relevant types of synthetic cells: phospholipid-based vesicles capable of recapitulating gene expression activity.

We compile data about the properties and functions of these synthetic cells (Figure 1), and explore them through a set of questions focused on their structure, activity, and complexity.

About the compartment

What sets the size scale of synthetic cells?

While synthetic cells aim to mimic simple prokaryotic cells, their dimensions often reach well beyond the bacterial regime. Typical synthetic cells range from ∼1 μm up to 30 μm or more, encroaching on the size scale of eukaryotic cells.6,9,20,21,24,34,35 The upper limit is largely dictated by the constraints of liposome fabrication techniques used to form the synthetic cells and the requirement to stay at the cellular dimension level. In contrast, the lower bound arises not from fabrication limits, but from the molecular census of the reactions these compartments are meant to support. A concentration of 1 nM corresponds to roughly 1 molecule per femtoliter, or equivalently, 1 molecule per μm3. Since key components in cell-free gene expression systems, including template DNA, often operate at nanomolar concentrations, a compartment must be at least ∼1 μm in diameter to reliably encapsulate even a single copy of them. Gene expression has been reported in sub-micron vesicles.36 However, only a small fraction of such vesicle population was active, emphasizing the highly stochastic nature of encapsulation at this scale. Therefore, the statistical constraint on molecular encapsulation sets a hard lower bound on compartment size.

Regarding the size variability of synthetic cells, it is intimately tied to the method used to generate the liposomes that encapsulate them.27 Bulk methods such as thin film hydration24,37 and phase transfer techniques3,9,34 are widely used due to their simplicity and versatility, but they typically produce vesicle populations with diameters ranging from submicron to tens of microns, spanning more than an order of magnitude. In contrast, microfluidic-based approaches offer a level of precision more akin to engineered systems, routinely achieving standard deviations on the order of ∼1 μm or less.20,21 The choice of a vesicle formation method is therefore a key design parameter: bulk methods enable a single experiment to explore a broad range of size-dependent phenomena, whereas microfluidics achieves precision and reproducibility.

How complex is the composition of synthetic cell membranes?

Biological membranes are among the most complex and compositionally diverse structures in the cell, comprising a finely tuned mixture of both lipids and proteins. More than 1,000 distinct lipid species can be identified within a single eukaryotic cell (BNID 107243), and this diversity extends down to the level of individual organelles, which maintain unique lipid profiles despite constant exchange via vesicular trafficking. Prokaryotic membranes, though less complex, still contain tens of distinct lipid structures, with compositions that dynamically shift during the bacterial growth cycle.38

Against this biological backdrop, synthetic cell membranes are far simpler. Most systems have a static composition, employing between 1 and 6 phospholipid species, often selected from a small toolbox including dioleoylphospha-tidylcholine (DOPC), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3- phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), dioleoyl-phosphaHdylglycerol (DOPG), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1′-rac-glycerol) (POPG), and cardiolipin.4,5,9,24,39,40 Cholesterol is frequently used as well.40,41 Should we expect these synthetic membranes to play the role of natural ones or are they too simplified to do so? Or asked differently, is the complexity of biological membranes a critical feature for life? Recent work on the minimal cell JCVI-syn3A, derived from Mycoplasma mycoides, suggests otherwise. This engineered cell maintains viability with a remarkably simple lipidome composed of just two components: a diether PC and cholesterol.42 This finding highlights that membrane complexity, while biologically prevalent, is not strictly essential for basic cellular function.

Beyond lipids, proteins constitute a substantial and functionally critical fraction of cellular membranes. In bacteria, over 200 distinct kinds of membrane proteins contribute to the membrane composition (BNID 106256), accounting for ≈70% of the total membrane mass (BNID 106255). As a useful rule of thumb, the protein density on the membrane surface is estimated to be ∼105 proteins/μm2.43 In comparison, synthetic cells recapitulate only a narrow slice of this complexity. The current record for simultaneous membrane protein synthesis in a synthetic cell stands at seven different proteins.5 While many studies have incorporated membrane proteins into synthetic systems, few report quantitative densities. Where measured, densities of de novo synthesized membrane proteins range from 1 to 700 proteins/μm2,44,45,46,47 orders of magnitude lower than their natural counterparts. These numbers highlight both the strides made and the gaps that remain in engineering functional membranes in bottom-up synthetic cells. Higher protein densities are expected to be needed for crucial cellular functions, like energy production and replication.

Do synthetic cell membranes permit nutrient uptake from the environment?

One of the challenges in synthetic cell engineering is enabling the system to exchange materials with the environment and achieve continuous nutrient supply to sustain long-term activity. The pioneering work of Noireaux and Libchaber in 2004 addressed this question by equipping synthetic cells with membrane channels.3 In this work, α-hemolysin monomers from Staphylococcus aureus, expressed within the synthetic cells, self-assembled on the membrane into heptameric pores with a diameter of ≈1.4 nm. This pore size imposes a molecular weight cutoff of ≈3 kDa, allowing the diffusion of small metabolites such as amino acids and nucleotides, while confining larger biomolecules inside the vesicle. The dual effect of feeding fresh nutrients and clearing inhibitory waste products, most notably inorganic phosphate, led to a dramatic increase in protein production: both the total yield and the duration of expression were enhanced by roughly an order of magnitude, with sustained activity for up to 4 days. Since then, the use of α-hemolysin has become widespread, though not universal, in synthetic cell designs.48,49,50,51

This capacity for continuous nutrient exchange is of particular importance since key components, such as amino acids and nucleotides, are typically used at millimolar concentrations in cell-free systems, beyond which they begin to inhibit gene expression.1,2,52,53 Connecting the synthetic cell to an effectively infinite reservoir allows nutrient concentrations to be maintained below their inhibitory thresholds, while still providing the flux needed for prolonged function.

But how many nutrient molecules actually flow through a single channel? A simple estimate can be made using Fick’s law in the form J=−D∂c∂z≈DΔcL. For a channel with a radius of r ≈ 0.7 nm (i.e., same 1.4 nm diameter discussed above for α-hemolysin), the pore cross-sectional area is A = πr2 ≈ 1.5 × 10−6μm2. Assuming a membrane thickness of L ≈ 4 nm (BNID 112585), a typical diffusion coefficient of D ≈ 500 μm2/s,54,55 and a concentration gradient of Δc ≈ 1 mM ≈ 106molecules/μm3, we obtain a flux of roughly J × A ≈ 105 molecules/s/channel. This implies that a modest channel density, on the order of 1 channel/μm2, is sufficient to exchange the nutrient molecules present at 1 mM concentration inside a 1 μm vesicle in just 1 s. A 10 μm vesicle would require about 10 channels/μm2, with the required channel density increasing linearly with vesicle diameter, since it is obtained by dividing the total number of nutrient molecules in the vesicle (proportional to vesicle volume) by the vesicle surface area. In other words, even limited membrane permeabilization can fully support the metabolic demands of synthetic cells operating in the diffusive regime. This will be confirmed in our discussion of metabolite turnover times in synthetic cells.

Other membrane proteins have also been incorporated into synthetic cells, either to study channel behavior or to add specific functionality. For instance, the E. coli mechanosensitive channel (MscL) has been reconstituted to enable synthetic vesicles to respond to osmotic pressure differences on the order of 100 mOsm.56,57 These pores open under tension to relieve excess pressure, enhancing membrane robustness and reducing the likelihood of bursting during osmotic stress.

About gene expression

How many proteins can synthetic cells synthesize, and is it enough to achieve full protein doubling toward reproduction?

At the heart of synthetic cell engineering lies the ability to synthesize proteins using encapsulated cell-free gene expression systems. These systems act as a simplified cytoplasm, responsible for protein production. But how much protein can they actually produce? And is it realistic to envision the doubling of the full protein content, an essential condition toward whole-cell reproduction?

Two main gene expression systems dominate the field: E. coli cell extract48,58,59 and the PURE (protein synthesis using recombinant elements) system.1,2 The E. coli extract contains a rich ensemble of 500–1,000 different types of proteins,60,61,62 present at a total concentration of ≈10 mg/mL,63 or ≈300 μM, assuming an average protein mass of ≈30 kDa (≈5 × 10−20 g). For comparison, native E. coli cells contain roughly 3,000 different types of proteins (BNID 102087), both cytoplasmic and membrane-bound, at a total concentration in the range of 200–320 mg/mL (roughly 7–11 mM) (BNID 100041, BNID 108263), making the extract about 20–30 times more dilute than its biological counterpart.

The PURE system, by contrast, is a minimal gene expression platform fully reconstituted from individually purified components of E. coli. It includes approximately 36 enzymes required for transcription and translation.1 With the addition of the 54 ribosomal proteins, the system comprises a total of ≈90 different proteins at a total concentration of ≈2 mg/mL1, or ≈70 μM, about 100 times more dilute than the E. coli cells.

The usual biochemical units, like mg/mL and μM, offer limited insight when trying to build an intuitive picture of synthetic cell systems. Instead, we find it useful to normalize quantities to the volume of a typical E. coli cell, approximately 1 fL (1 fL = 1 μm3), which serves as a natural reference point. When expressed in this way, a femtoliter of E. coli extract contains roughly 200,000 protein molecules, while the same volume of PURE system holds closer to 40,000, both below the estimated ≈3 million proteins in native E. coli (BNID 115702), including ≈2 million in the cytoplasm (BNID 108534).

How many proteins can these gene expression systems synthesize? While yields vary depending on preparation and conditions, a femtoliter of E. coli extract typically supports the synthesis of ≈40,000 proteins (≈2 mg/mL),58,59,64 and most reconstituted PURE systems yield ≈2,000–10,000 proteins (≈0.1–0.5 mg/mL),2,9,65,66,67,68 although higher values, up to 26,000 (≈1.3 mg/mL), have been reported under optimized conditions.69,70 In both cases, the amount of protein produced generally corresponds to about 20% of the initial protein inventory, suggesting that these systems remain below the threshold needed to double their proteome. Without continuous feeding, these systems fall short of protein self-reproduction.

Still, reaching full protein doubling capability is not out of reach. A recent work reporting the development of an optimized E. coli extract achieved the synthesis of ≈300 μM of green fluorescent protein (GFP) inside 1–20 μm vesicles equipped with α-hemolysin pores for nutrient exchange.48 This level of protein production approaches the threshold for proteome doubling. In this case, enabling exchange with the environment doubled expression output.

What does this mean from the perspective of individual ribosomes? In both E. coli extracts and the PURE system, ribosomes are present at concentrations of ≈1–3 μM, corresponding to roughly 600–1,800 ribosomes per femtoliter.1,2,58 By comparing the total protein output to the number of ribosomes, we estimate that each ribosome synthesizes ≈20–70 proteins in E. coli extract and ≈1–17 proteins in the PURE system over the course of a typical reaction. For comparison, the ≈20,000 ribosomes in a fast-growing E. coli cell must each produce ≈150 proteins over a 20-min doubling time in order to replicate the ≈3 million proteins that make up its proteome. These numerical comparisons highlight how far in vitro systems have come, but also how far they still have to go to match the biosynthetic throughput of living cells.

Finally, we point to a recent and promising addition to the synthetic cell toolbox: a cell extract derived from the near-minimal synthetic bacterium JCVI-syn3A.71 While E. coli has long served as the foundation for cell-free gene expression and bottom-up assembly strategies, it remains a highly complex system to fully reconstruct. In contrast, JCVI-syn3A represents one of the simplest known life forms capable of autonomous replication.72,73 It offers an attractive radically simplified alternative based on a minimal chassis. Yet, the initial implementation of JCVI-syn3A-based extract exhibits modest performance, with protein expression yields of only ≈9.3 μg/mL,71 roughly 200-fold lower than typical yields from E. coli extracts. While these early results fall short of practical expression levels, the system’s simplicity holds promise for synthetic cell assembly, if and when the yields and efficiency are further optimized.

How fast do synthetic cells produce new proteins?

The capacity of synthetic cells to express proteins is not only defined by how many proteins they can make, but also by how fast they can make them. In minimal medium, E. coli divides roughly every 40 min, synthesizing its ≈3 million proteins over that time. This corresponds to an average production rate of ≈80,000 proteins per minute per cell volume. How do synthetic cells perform relative to this rate?

The protein production rate of cell-free systems can be evaluated from time-course measurements of fluorescent protein synthesis. Even though reported kinetics may vary across studies and preparation protocols, typical expression rates in systems based on E. coli extract fall in the range of ≈0.15–0.4 mg/mL/h,58,59,60 corresponding to ≈50–130 proteins synthesized per minute per E. coli-sized volume, roughly 700-fold lower than the rate achieved by E. coli. The reconstituted PURE system operates at an even slower pace, producing proteins at ≈0.05–0.2 mg/mL/h,2,9,33,41 or ≈15–70 proteins per minute per E. coli volume.

These expression rates provide a useful basis for estimating the time required for synthetic cells to double their protein content, assuming, of course, that the reaction is efficient enough or continuously fed to reach full protein doubling. In other words, what is the minimal doubling time of a synthetic proteome? Recalling that a femtoliter volume of E. coli extract and PURE system contains roughly 200,000 and 40,000 proteins, respectively, we estimate a doubling time on the order of ∼1,000 min, so less than a day in both cases. This timescale is similar to that of slow-growing bacteria or mammalian cultured cells.

It is important to note, particularly in the case of PURE-based systems, that protein doubling reflects only the minimal translational machinery. Achieving full autonomous self-replication will demand the expression of a broader set of functions, well beyond the ≈90 proteins that constitute the PURE system, and therefore will require a longer timescale than the ∼1,000 min estimated here.

What is the turnover time of metabolites in synthetic cells?

The rate of protein synthesis also dictates the overall level of chemical activity within synthetic cells. One useful measure of this activity is the turnover time of metabolites, the characteristic timescale over which a metabolite pool is fully replaced through its consumption and replenishment. The turnover time is given by the ratio of the metabolite pool size to its utilization rate. In E. coli, the metabolic machinery operates at a remarkable speed compared to the doubling time of the cell. The entire ATP pool is replaced every ≈2 s (BNID 109701), while most amino acid pools are replenished in under a minute (BNID 101622).

Synthetic cells, by contrast, exhibit significantly slower turnover. In typical cell-free systems, ATP and amino acids are generally present at concentrations in the millimolar range, corresponding to about 106 molecules/fL. Given a protein synthesis rate of ≈100 proteins/min/fL, and assuming an average protein length of 300 amino acids, this translates to the consumption of ≈30,000 amino acids and ≈120,000 ATP molecules per minute per femtoliter (assuming 4 ATP equivalents per peptide bond). Under these assumptions, the turnover time is roughly 20differentaa×106aa/fL30,000aa/min/fL≈600min for the full pool of amino acids and about 106ATP/fL120,000ATP/min/fL≈10min for ATP, about two orders of magnitude slower than in E. coli.

The limited pool of tRNAs within synthetic cells provides another example of turnover dynamics related to protein synthesis. With a molecular weight of ≈26 kDa (BNID 110969), tRNAs exceed the ≈3 kDa size exclusion limit of α-hemolysin pores and are effectively trapped inside the vesicle once encapsulated. Acting as essential mediators of translation, tRNAs shuttle amino acids to ribosomes in a cycle that involves charging by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and subsequent peptide elongation. In E. coli, this cycle is completed in under a second (BNID 105275). What is the timescale of this process in synthetic cells?

In extract-based systems, a femtoliter-scale volume contains approximately 8,000 tRNA molecules (corresponding to ≈0.2 mg/mL) that are used at a rate of 30,000 per minute, as discussed earlier, assuming an expression rate of 100 protein/min/fL. The corresponding turnover time for the tRNA pool then ≈8,000tRNA/fL30,000tRNA/min/fL≈0.25min, or 15 s.

Minimal recombinant systems such as PURE, however, often include a 10-fold higher tRNA concentration (≈80,000 per fL). At a typical expression rate of ≈50 protein/min/fL, the use of ≈15,000 charged tRNAs per minute yields a turnover time ≈80,000tRNA/fL15,000tRNA/min/fL≈5min. While these back-of-the-envelope rough estimates should be adjusted to follow the specific composition and activity of different cell-free reaction recipes, they provide a quantitative sense of the activity level reached in an artificial cell.

To what extent do synthetic cells reproduce the macromolecular crowding of living cells?

Macromolecular crowding is one of the most striking physical features of the cellular interior, affecting all biochemical processes, including crucial to life ribosome biogenesis.74,75 In E. coli, macromolecules occupy an estimated 20%–40% of the cytoplasmic volume (BNIDs 105814, 106973), a level of crowding where the average spacing between biomolecules approaches their own dimensions. This crowded state shapes the very rules by which cellular chemistry operates, from diffusion and reaction kinetics to the assembly and stability of multicomponent structures.

Cell-free gene expression systems, and by extension synthetic cells, do not recapitulate this level of macromolecular crowding. As previously discussed, protein concentrations reach approximately 10 mg/mL in extract-based systems, and around 2 mg/mL in recombinant systems like PURE. Using a partial specific volume of ≈0.73 mL/g (BNID 104272), a standard value reflecting the volume occupied by 1 g of protein, these concentrations correspond to macromolecular volume fractions φ of ≈0.7% and ≈0.15%, respectively. Spontaneous crowding of gene expression machinery has been reported inside vesicles formed in a diluted non-reactive gene expression reaction, triggering internal protein synthesis.76,77,78 However, only a small fraction of vesicles, less than 0.5%, exhibited this spontaneous and unexpected overcrowding and activity. These φ estimates therefore highlight the dilute nature of most synthetic cell interiors, and the need for strategies to better emulate natural cellular crowding.

To partially bridge this gap, researchers have turned to macromolecular crowding agents such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), dextran, and Ficoll, sometimes combined with inert proteins like bovine serum albumin (BSA). These additives can raise the effective volume fraction inside synthetic cells to 1%–5%, closer to biological levels. Yet, this comes at a cost: several studies have reported a reduction in cell-free protein synthesis efficiency by as much as 20%–60% under these crowded conditions.9,58

Despite this drawback, crowding was reported to play an active role in shaping biochemical organization also in synthetic systems. Several studies show enhanced self-assembly of complex, multicomponent structures under crowded conditions. The FtsZ-ring,9 ribosomal subunits,79 and even full bacteriophage particles such as T758 have all assembled more efficiently in crowded synthetic environments. In the striking case of the T7 phage composed of ≈500 protein subunits, the addition of 4% PEG increased the assembly yield by three orders of magnitude.58

Crowding is not limited to the cytoplasm; it also extends to the two-dimensional environment of the cell membrane. As noted earlier, in bacteria, ≈70% of the membrane mass comes from membrane-associated proteins. This level of 2D crowding was recently mimicked in synthetic cells by incorporating PEGylated lipids into the membrane bilayer.63 Each PEG chain, acting as a flexible polymer with a root-mean-square end-to-end distance of ≈6 nm, sterically excluded an area corresponding to 65–80 phospholipids. At a concentration of ≈1% lipid-PEG, the membrane became nearly fully coated. Remarkably, this engineered surface crowding drove the self-assembly of the bacterial actin homolog MreB into a sturdy network along the membrane, ultimately reshaping spherical vesicles into rod-like synthetic cells.34 These observations reinforce the notion that both volume and surface crowding, despite potential drawbacks, may be essential for promoting the emergence of higher-order structures within synthetic systems.

About DNA

How much DNA does a synthetic cell contain?

The genetic information embedded within a synthetic cell will ultimately define its complete identity. This synthetic genome must encode all the components necessary for the core processes of cellular life, and in particular, autonomous self-replication. But how many genes are needed to cross that threshold, and are we equipped to synthesize and encapsulate a genome of that size80?

Mycoplasma genitalium is among the simplest known organisms capable of independent replication, with a genome of ≈580 kbp encoding ≈480 genes. Similarly, the fascinating minimal cell JCVI-syn3.0, derived from the more complex Mycoplasma mycoides and engineered to retain only the essential genes for viability and replication, was reduced to 531 kbp and 473 genes.72 Together, these organisms suggest that a minimal genome for a free-living synthetic cell would require on the order of 500 genes.

Thanks to advances in DNA chemical synthesis and assembly, constructing a genome of this scale is now possible. Hierarchical assembly approaches, starting from short synthetic oligonucleotides and progressively combining them into larger fragments, have already enabled the synthesis of a complete bacterial genome81 (≈1 Mbp) and even an entire yeast genome82 (≈11 Mbp).

Yet, encapsulating a full minimal genome inside a lipid vesicle presents physical and technical challenges. Long DNA molecules, spanning hundreds of kilobases, are mechanically fragile, prone to shearing from pipetting or extrusion. As a result, synthetic cells typically carry much shorter DNA templates in the 1–10 kbp range,5 sufficient to encode <10 genes.5,8 These templates are used at nanomolar concentrations, typically in the 1–10 nM range9,39,58 (∼1–10 μg/mL assuming 1 kbp DNA), values tuned to optimize expression in cell-free systems. Given these concentrations, a 10 μm-diameter vesicle encapsulates roughly 300–3,000 DNA molecules.

The DNA concentration in both bacteria and eukaryotic nuclei reaches ∼10 mg/mL (BNID 106487, 100043), about three orders of magnitude greater than in typical synthetic cells. While such comparison highlights the limitations of current systems, it may overlook a more appropriate frame of reference. Rather than comparing to modern cells with advanced DNA-packaging systems, it may be more instructive to compare synthetic cells to hypothetical protocells, which by definition operated with far simpler genetic programs.

Finally, in terms of informational complexity and circuit design, the sophistication of synthetic circuits continues to advance. Cell-free expression systems based on E. coli extracts have been expanded to function with the bacterium’s seven native sigma factors, as well as phage-derived RNA polymerases such as T7 and T3.83 These capabilities have enabled the implementation of diverse gene circuits, including logic gates,83,84 activation cascades,30 feedback loops,85,86 bistable switches,30,87,88 and genetic oscillators.31,89 While most synthetic circuits within synthetic cells still consist of only 2–4 genes, recent work has demonstrated the successful operation of a 10-gene pathway, an important step toward the regulatory complexity of natural cells.40

Can a synthetic cell replicate its DNA and potentially evolve?

A defining prerequisite for both cell division and evolution is the ability to replicate genetic material while simultaneously expressing it. In E. coli, this task is carried out by the replisome, a dynamic multiprotein complex comprising ≈15 core proteins and several auxiliary factors. Together, they achieve DNA replication at an impressively fast rate of ≈750 bases/s (BNID 104120), with a processivity exceeding 50 kb, and an error rate as low as ≈10−10 mutations/bp/replication (BNID 111229). Reconstituting such a fast, accurate, and intricately coordinated system in a minimal synthetic cell, where every component must be expressed and assembled de novo, is a clear challenge.

Yet nature provides a spectrum of replication strategies beyond those found in modern bacteria, and synthetic systems are free to borrow solutions from across the tree of life. In fact, some of the most promising candidates come from the viral world, where DNA replication systems are often radically streamlined. Currently, the replication machinery of the bacteriophage φ29 has been identified as particularly promising. This system operates on linear DNA templates and exhibits several layers of simplification.90 First, φ29 replication proceeds via a strand-displacement mechanism that eliminates the need for lagging-strand synthesis and Okazaki fragment processing. Second, it replaces the conventional RNA primer with a terminal protein that serves as a built-in primer. Third, and perhaps most impressively, the φ29 DNA polymerase achieves a processivity exceeding 70 kb91 without the aid of a sliding clamp, while maintaining a fast elongation speed of ≈50–100 bases/s and an error rate on the order of 10−5–10−6 mutations/bp/replication.92

The φ29 DNA replication machinery has been successfully reconstituted within synthetic cells using just four proteins.4 A 3.2 kb linear DNA fragment encoding the φ29 DNA polymerase and its terminal protein was self-replicated inside liposomes, following expression in a reconstituted PURE system supplemented with two purified auxiliary proteins serving, in the replication process, as single-stranded and double-stranded DNA-binding proteins. In this minimal setup, the synthetic cell was able to express the very proteins required for its own DNA replication, achieving an important step toward self-sustained genome duplication.

This success might establish a foundation for the development of a minimal unit capable of perhaps the most defining feature of life: the capacity to evolve. Can we recapitulate Darwinian evolution in synthetic cells? Remarkably, a recent study leveraged the φ29 DNA replication system to sustain multiple rounds of expression and replication of the previously described 3.2 kb DNA template in synthetic cells.93 Two experimental strategies were employed to emulate cell division. In one approach, synthetic cells were disrupted after each round, DNA extracted, PCR-amplified, and re-encapsulated. The DNA sequence diversified, accumulating 4–8 mutations per molecule by round 11. In a second, more integrated approach, fresh lipid vesicles containing PURE components were added at each round. Freeze-thaw cycles triggered vesicle fusion and fission, redistributing replicated DNA into new compartments. This semi-continuous setup maintained full-length DNA replicators for at least five cycles.

Going beyond 3 kb, the φ29 polymerase has also been used, although not within a vesicle, to replicate a multipartite genome totaling 116 kb,94 nearly a quarter of the estimated 500-gene minimal genome discussed earlier. This synthetic genome included 11 plasmids encoding all E. coli translation factors, ribosomal RNAs, an energy regeneration system, and the enzymes for transcription and DNA replication. The largest individual plasmid reached ≈30 kb. These replication experiments were performed in a PURE system specifically optimized for DNA amplification, allowing the concurrent expression and replication of the encoded genes, and achieving a partially self-replicating in vitro translation system.

Toward higher complexity

Can synthetic cells build their own membranes?

Cells have developed a variety of strategies to divide autonomously, from the tightly regulated binary fission of E. coli and most bacteria, to mechanisms such as budding, multiple fission, or asymmetric division.95,96,97 These mechanisms vary widely in complexity, some relying on elaborate protein-based machinery, while others are relatively simple and may even occur without dedicated division proteins. Perhaps the simplest strategy, one that may echo the behavior of early protocells, is the blebbing mechanism observed in wall-deficient L-form bacteria, which divide by simply overproducing membrane.98,99 Despite their diversity, all of these strategies share one common requirement: the synthesis of new membrane to create daughter compartments. Can synthetic cells reproduce this essential function? More specifically, can they reproduce membrane growth under the control of their own genetic material and do so at levels sufficient to double their initial surface area?

Different approaches have been proposed to achieve membrane growth, often involving the uptake of lipids from the environment100 or the fusion with smaller vesicles101 mediated by DNA hybridization40,102 or soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor proteins.23,103,104,105 These strategies, which rely on recruiting lipids rather than synthesizing them de novo, have successfully led to membrane expansion. However, by decoupling membrane growth from the cell’s genetic program, they fail to capture the self-sufficient nature of biological cells. A more life-like strategy consists of encoding and expressing inside synthetic cells the machinery that will actively drive the production of phospholipids from simple precursors.

But how much lipid should a synthetic cell actually produce to double its surface area? Given that each phospholipid occupies roughly 0.5 nm2 (BNID 106993), a spherical vesicle with a diameter ranging from 1 μm to 30 μm would require the synthesis of approximately 107–1010 molecules to duplicate its membrane, corresponding to a phospholipid concentration inside the vesicle of roughly 1 mM–40 mM. We highlight that smaller vesicles, with their higher surface-to-volume ratios, demand proportionally more lipid synthesis per unit volume.

Different phospholipid synthesis pathways have been reconstituted inside synthetic cells by expressing the genes encoding lipid-producing enzymes.5,106 In particular, the Kennedy pathway, the central route for phospholipid production in E. coli, has been successfully expressed from a single 10.5 kb DNA construct encoding seven enzymes, leading to the production of six phospholipid species (DOPE, DOPG, DPPE, DPPG, POPE, and POPG).5 Yet, the total concentration of phospholipid end products reached only up to 20 μM, about two orders of magnitude below the estimated requirement for membrane doubling and insufficient to produce detectable membrane expansion. A variant of the pathway improved yields to ≈100 μM, but still failed to induce observable morphological changes.107 More recently, a study using the same pathway in large populations of synthetic cells (≈4 μm in diameter) revealed substantial heterogeneity in lipid synthesis: while the average yield across the population was around 0.7% of the total lipid content, a subset of high-performing synthetic cells reached ≈7%, corresponding to ≈700 μM.8 These results demonstrate the feasibility of coupling DNA-programmed expression to lipid biosynthesis in a way that respects the logic of cellular metabolism, but the achieved yields remain below the threshold necessary for self-growing and self-replicating systems.

In contrast, a simplified strategy has repurposed the soluble enzyme FadD10 to bypass the complexity of canonical pathways and generate phospholipids de novo.108 This approach succeeded in producing vesicles that grew dynamically, transitioning from small unilamellar to larger multilamellar structures. Although not implemented directly within synthetic cells, this approach was coupled to the cell-free expression of FadD10 in a PURE system reaction supplemented with lipid precursors and resulted in the formation of vesicles ranging from 1 to 5 μm in diameter, demonstrating that dynamic membrane growth is within reach.

Can synthetic cells produce energy, and how much?

Energy consumption is a defining trait of life and an unavoidable feature of out-of-equilibrium systems like biological and synthetic cells. In synthetic cells, this energy is typically supplied through a combination of transmembrane feeding (as discussed earlier) and internal ATP regeneration systems that rely on high-energy phosphate donors as secondary energy sources. These systems are designed to maintain ATP at physiological concentrations of ≈1–3 mM by continuously recycling ADP and AMP back into ATP. Regeneration substrates such as phosphoenolpyruvate, 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA), or creatine phosphate are commonly provided at concentrations of 10–30 mM or higher,1,2,53,58 effectively multiplying the available energy supply by a factor of >10, and thereby extending the longevity and productivity of synthetic cells.

But can synthetic cells harvest energy autonomously, without relying on preloaded chemical fuels? In a striking proof of concept, artificial photosynthetic cells have been developed that generate ATP directly from light, using it as a substrate for transcription and as an energy source for translation.6 This system relies on specialized artificial organelles encapsulated within the synthetic cell cytoplasm. These organelles are small proteoliposomes (100–200 nm in diameter) that incorporate two key membrane proteins: the light-driven proton pump bacteriorhodopsin and the F0F1-ATP synthase. Upon illumination, bacteriorhodopsin creates a proton gradient across the proteoliposome membrane, which ATP synthase then exploits to synthesize ATP. Roughly 11,000 proteoliposomes were encapsulated into a 10 μm synthetic cell, occupying ≈4% of the internal volume. Each proteoliposome contained ≈4,900 copies of bacteriorhodopsin and ≈30 ATP synthases, collectively producing ≈51,000 ATP molecules after 6 h of illumination. The resulting ATP concentration within the synthetic cell reaches ≈1.8 mM, a level comparable to that found in biological cells (BNID 111006).

However, this cell-like final concentration can be misleading. To assess the efficiency of the system and whether it is energetically self-sufficient, we must consider the rate of ATP production rather than the final concentration achieved. We recall that, if protein synthesis proceeds at ≈100 proteins/min/fL, then the synthetic cell has to consume ≈120,000 ATP/min/fL. Are the artificial photosynthetic organelles capable of meeting this demand? If we make the simplifying assumption that ATP production proceeds at a constant rate over the 6–h illumination period, then the total ATP yield (51,000 molecules per 10 μm vesicle) leads to an average ATP synthesis rate of ≈3,000 ATP/min/fL, only a few percent of the estimated demand. On a per-enzyme basis, this translates to an ATP production rate of ≈5 ATP per ATP synthase per minute, far below the >100 ATP per ATP synthase per second observed in natural systems (BNID 111975). Therefore, although this system represents a major step forward in engineering light-powered synthetic cells, significant optimization is still required to achieve full energetic autonomy.

A complementary strategy has also been demonstrated by encapsulating intact bacterial chromatophores from Rhodobacter sphaeroides into 20 μm vesicles.109 These native organelles (<100 nm in diameter) house the complete photosynthetic apparatus and, under illumination, serve as highly functional ATP-generating modules. Importantly, the chromatophores retain their physiological lipid microenvironment, preserving activity. Bulk measurements indicate an ATP production rate reaching up to ≈100 ATP per ATP synthase per second, very similar to natural systems. Although this approach has not yet been combined with full protein expression, it highlights a promising top-down path toward constructing synthetic cells with autonomous energy production.

How many cellular functions can a synthetic cell incorporate simultaneously?

The bottom-up construction of synthetic cells follows a modular logic. Each module, whether gene expression, DNA replication, membrane biosynthesis, energy generation, motility, etc., is reconstituted and optimized in isolation, with the goal of integrating them into increasingly complex assemblies, eventually building a full, cohesive, and self-replicating system. In this hierarchy of increasing complexity, gene expression has emerged as the foundational module, routinely used to drive other processes. But where does the field currently stand on this path toward integration? Can we quantify the complexity already achieved?

A simple yet informative indicator for estimating functional complexity is the number of distinct biological modules, particularly genetically encoded ones, that have been simultaneously integrated into a single system. While this count is admittedly schematic, it provides a concrete measure of progress in assembling increasingly life-like synthetic cells. Most existing studies have paired gene expression with one additional function, such as DNA replication,4 membrane biosynthesis,5 or membrane deformation.39,110 Only recently have systems emerged that successfully combine gene expression with two distinct modules in a single synthetic cell, marking a new threshold in functional integration.

One notable example is the development of synthetic cells that simultaneously support gene expression, DNA self-replication, and membrane biosynthesis.8 In this system, a synthetic linear genome encoding the φ29 DNA replication machinery (two genes) and a phospholipid biosynthesis pathway (four genes) was expressed inside liposomes. This led to the concurrent replication of the genome and production of the phospholipid dioleoylphosphophatidylserine (DOPS) in ≈8% of the liposome population. This experiment demonstrates the successful coordination of three key cellular modules, information processing, genome duplication, and membrane growth, that form the backbone of cellular self-maintenance and inheritance.

Another example pushes the boundaries of spatial self-organization, focusing on the development of a minimal divisome inspired by the E. coli division machinery. In E. coli, the MinCDE protein system forms a self-organized reaction-diffusion oscillator that drives dynamic pole-to-pole protein oscillations, establishing a spatial gradient with high Min protein concentrations at the poles and low concentrations at midcell.111,112 This gradient inhibits the polymerization of the tubulin homolog FtsZ at the poles, thereby guiding its assembly into a contractile division ring, the FtsZ-ring, at the cell center. This process, which combines synthesis, assembly, and active positioning of the FtsZ-ring and represents the early stages of E. coli divisome formation, has now been reconstituted inside synthetic cells.9

A total of five genes, three encoding the MinCDE proteins and two encoding a minimal division module composed of FtsZ and its membrane anchor FtsA, were co-expressed inside lipid vesicles. The integration of these three modules, gene expression, the Min system, and the FtsZ-ring system, led to the emergence of a complex protein dynamics coupled to vesicle deformation. Newly synthesized FtsZ initially formed isotropic filament networks that progressively condensed into ring-like structures positioned at the vesicle equator under the influence of Min oscillations. These oscillations and FtsZ-ring formation engaged in a positive feedback loop, reinforcing one another and resulting in coordinated vesicle constriction of up to 19%.

In this striking system, spatial information, mechanical force, and dynamic feedback converge to produce life-like behavior. This reflects a prototypical characteristic of living cells: the emergent properties arising from interactions between various components allow for a level of organization and complexity that exceeds that of the individual components themselves. Complexity is not merely a matter of adding parts. It is the result of interactions between modules. With synthetic cells now integrating up to three functional modules, we are beginning to witness the first glimpses of this emergent organization.

Summary

The development of synthetic cells has made major strides toward recapitulating the fundamental characteristics of living systems. In this article, we have presented these progresses through the lens of biological numeracy, assessing the current state of liposome-based synthetic cells and positioning them within a clear numerical context relative to natural cells. Although some aspects of synthetic-cell biochemistry already approach concentrations and efficiencies seen in robust natural cells, several key areas still require major improvement. Achieving self-replication and self-sustained metabolism will require bringing synthetic minimal cells closer to the biochemical efficiency of living systems. While comparing synthetic cells directly to modern cells helps us understand current limitations, modern cells may set an overly stringent benchmark, given their complex evolutionary adaptations. As mentioned previously, the field aims at assembling a living system of minimal complexity rather than reconstituting faithfully complex biological cells. In this context, protocells would provide a more appropriate reference system, lowering the requirements for synthetic cells.

Our “numbers-first” approach should be regarded as a complementary tool, not a replacement, alongside other valuable methods to describe and analyze synthetic cells. We hope that it will be adopted by the synthetic cell community to help design, benchmark, and evaluate new synthetic systems.
