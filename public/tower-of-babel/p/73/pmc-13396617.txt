Primary ovarian synovial sarcoma in an adolescent mimicking a germ cell tumor: A case report and diagnostic challenge
Authors: Dyah Listyaningtyas, Hari Nugroho, Bagus Ngurah Brahmantara, Heriyawati, Ayu Farikha Nandiaty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary ovarian synovial sarcoma is an exceedingly rare malignancy that may mimic more common ovarian tumors, particularly in adolescents, posing significant diagnostic challenges.Case presentationA 14-year-old girl presented with progressive abdominal distension and respiratory compromise. Imaging revealed a large mixed solid–cystic adnexal mass with ascites and pleural effusion. Serum CA-125 was elevated, while AFP and CEA were normal. The patient underwent right salpingo-oophorectomy and omentectomy. Histopathology showed a monophasic spindle cell neoplasm with focal cartilaginous differentiation. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated diffuse nuclear positivity for SS18 and TLE1, with co-expression of BCL-2, CD99, and EMA, while inhibin, calretinin, and WT-1 were negative. The positive SS18 immunohistochemical staining provided additional diagnostic confirmation of synovial sarcoma, supporting the diagnosis in conjunction with the characteristic histopathologic findings. The patient deteriorated rapidly and died before initiation of chemotherapy.ConclusionPrimary ovarian synovial sarcoma is a rare and aggressive tumor that can mimic germ cell neoplasms. Accurate diagnosis requires careful integration of histopathology and immunohistochemistry. Although molecular confirmation remains the diagnostic gold standard, SS18 fusion-specific immunohistochemistry provides a reliable diagnostic surrogate when molecular testing is unavailable.

Introduction

Synovial sarcoma is a malignant mesenchymal tumor accounting for approximately 5–10% of all soft tissue sarcomas and predominantly affects adolescents and young adults, typically arising in the deep soft tissues of the extremities near joints (Thway and Fisher, 2014; Ladanyi, 2001).

Primary involvement of the ovary is exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of well-documented cases reported in the literature (Rahman et al., 2021). Due to the absence of specific clinical and radiologic features, ovarian synovial sarcoma is frequently misdiagnosed as more common ovarian neoplasms, particularly germ cell tumors or other spindle cell sarcomas (Gupta et al., 2023).

Accurate diagnosis relies on histopathologic evaluation supported by immunohistochemistry. Molecular detection of the SS18 (SYT)-SSX fusion resulting from t(X;18)(p11;q11) remains the diagnostic gold standard for confirming synovial sarcoma (Thway and Fisher, 2014; Coindre et al., 2003). However, a recent study demonstrated that SS18 fusion-specific immunohistochemistry is a highly sensitive and specific surrogate marker for molecular confirmation, particularly in diagnostically challenging cases or when molecular testing is unavailable (Lasota et al., 2024).

Case presentation

A 14-year-old girl presented with a two-month history of progressive abdominal distension, dyspnea, and abdominal pain. She had no significant past medical or family history. Menarche occurred at age 13 with regular menstrual cycles, and no abnormal uterine bleeding was reported.

Initial ultrasonography revealed a large mixed solid–cystic intra-abdominal mass of unclear origin, suspected to be a dermoid cyst, with associated mild bilateral hydronephrosis. Laboratory investigations demonstrated anemia (Hb 9.5 g/dL), leukocytosis (23,680/μL), thrombocytosis (771,000/μL), hypoalbuminemia (2.65 g/dL), and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (609 U/L). Tumor markers demonstrated elevated CA-125 (229.2 U/mL), while AFP (1.04 ng/mL) and CEA (0.18 ng/mL) were within normal limits. Additional tumor markers including β-hCG, inhibin B, and CA 19–9 were not obtained.

Computed tomography of the abdomen revealed a massive mixed solid–cystic lesion measuring approximately 16.0 × 25.1 × 30.6 cm arising from the adnexa, with ascites and bilateral pleural effusion. Chest imaging demonstrated bilateral pleural effusions, more prominent on the right side, with associated pulmonary infiltrates suspicious for pneumonia versus metastatic involvement. The patient developed respiratory compromise requiring bilateral chest tube insertion. Surgical findings and management

An urgent exploratory laparotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, a giant mixed solid–cystic tumor measuring approximately 30 cm was found arising from the right ovary, with dense adhesions to the omentum, abdominal wall, sigmoid colon, and rectum (Fig. 2). The patient underwent right salpingo-oophorectomy, partial omentectomy, sharp adhesiolysis, and abdominal drainage. Intraoperatively, the mass could not be removed intact because of its large size and dense adhesions. Residual tumor tissue measuring approximately 10 × 5 × 2 cm remained adherent to the posterior uterus and rectum, raising suspicion for local infiltration. Peritoneal cytology was not obtained due to the difficulty of tumor mobilization and fragmentation during resection. Although hysterectomy and rectal resection were considered, these procedures were deferred because the patient was only 14 years old and fertility preservation was strongly prioritized. Furthermore, the initial intraoperative impression favored a germ cell malignancy, which is generally highly chemosensitive in adolescents, thereby influencing the decision to avoid radical surgery during the initial procedure. No lymphadenectomy was performed, as comprehensive staging surgery was not prioritized based on the presumed diagnosis of a chemosensitive germ cell tumor.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings

Histopathological examination revealed a malignant spindle cell neoplasm composed of relatively uniform spindle cells arranged in intersecting fascicles, with elongated nuclei, fine chromatin, and scant cytoplasm. Areas of increased cellularity alternated with less cellular regions, accompanied by focal hemorrhage.

Importantly, the tumor showed monophasic morphology, composed entirely of spindle cells, with focal heterologous cartilaginous differentiation. No true epithelial component was identified. These findings raised a broad differential diagnosis, including sarcomatoid transformation within a germ cell tumor and embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma. No evidence of malignancy was identified in the omental specimen.

Given the morphologic overlap with other spindle cell neoplasms, an extended immunohistochemical (IHC) panel was performed. The tumor cells showed strong nuclear positivity for TLE1, along with diffuse cytoplasmic expression of BCL-2 and focal membranous positivity for CD99. EMA demonstrated focal membranous staining, supporting epithelial differentiation. In addition, focal nuclear expression of myogenin and cytoplasmic desmin positivity were observed, along with partial membranous expression of CD56.

Markers associated with sex cord–stromal differentiation were negative, including inhibin, calretinin, and WT-1, while SMA was also negative, arguing against smooth muscle differentiation.

Taken together, the overall morphologic and immunophenotypic profile—particularly the combination of SS18, TLE1, BCL-2, CD99, and EMA positivity in the appropriate histologic context—is highly supportive of synovial sarcoma. Although focal expression of myogenic markers introduced a potential diagnostic pitfall, the staining pattern was limited and did not support rhabdomyosarcoma.

To further confirm the diagnosis, SS18 fusion-specific immunohistochemistry was subsequently performed and demonstrated strong diffuse nuclear positivity in the tumor cells. Outcome and follow-up

Before adjuvant chemotherapy could be initiated, the patient exhibited rapid disease progression, manifested by recurrent abdominal distension and progressive clinical deterioration. Despite comprehensive multidisciplinary management involving pediatric oncology, cardiology, thoracic surgery, and urology, her condition continued to decline, and she died less than three months after the initial diagnosis.

In the present case, serum CA-125 levels were elevated, while AFP and CEA were within normal limits. Elevated CA-125 is a nonspecific finding and may reflect peritoneal irritation or extensive intra-abdominal disease rather than epithelial ovarian malignancy (Lin et al., 2025). On imaging, synovial sarcoma typically presents as a heterogeneous soft tissue mass with variable solid and cystic components and may exhibit locally aggressive features. However, these imaging characteristics are nonspecific and frequently overlap with those of other neoplasms, limiting their diagnostic specificity (Blay et al., 2023).

Histologically, synovial sarcoma demonstrates considerable morphologic heterogeneity, classically presenting as monophasic spindle cell or biphasic epithelial and spindle cell patterns (Blay et al., 2023). In the present case, the tumor exhibited a monophasic spindle cell pattern with focal heterologous cartilaginous differentiation, without a recognizable epithelial component. This unusual feature may further complicate the diagnosis and broaden the differential, particularly toward teratoma or other sarcomas with heterologous elements. Therefore, immunohistochemistry plays a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis. In the present case, strong nuclear expression of TLE1, together with positivity for BCL-2, CD99, and EMA, provides strong supportive evidence for synovial sarcoma within the appropriate morphologic context (Rekhi et al., 2012). Although focal expression of focal myogenic markers such as myogenin and desmin may be observed, this represents a recognized diagnostic pitfall and should be interpreted with caution, as aberrant myoid differentiation has been described in synovial sarcoma and should not outweigh the overall morphologic and immunophenotypic profile (Qassid et al., 2016). Molecular detection of the SS18-SSX fusion remains the diagnostic gold standard for synovial sarcoma (Thway and Fisher, 2014; Coindre et al., 2003). However, the increasing availability of SS18 fusion-specific immunohistochemistry has substantially improved diagnostic accuracy. A recent study demonstrated that this antibody exhibits excellent sensitivity and specificity for synovial sarcoma and can serve as a practical surrogate for molecular testing in routine clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings (Lasota et al., 2024). In the present case, the tumor demonstrated strong diffuse nuclear SS18 positivity, providing additional diagnostic confirmation and further supporting the diagnosis established by the characteristic histomorphology and immunophenotypic profile (Thway and Fisher, 2014; Lasota et al., 2024).

Due to the rarity of primary ovarian synovial sarcoma, no standardized treatment guidelines exist, and management is generally extrapolated from soft tissue synovial sarcoma protocols. Recommended treatment typically consists of maximal surgical resection followed by ifosfamide- and doxorubicin-based chemotherapy, with or without radiotherapy in selected cases (Rahman et al., 2021; Blay et al., 2023). Although synovial sarcoma is considered relatively chemosensitive compared with other soft tissue sarcomas, particularly in younger patients, outcomes remain poor in advanced-stage or incompletely resected disease. Reported survival ranges from several months to a few years, with adverse prognostic factors including large tumor size, incomplete surgical resection, metastatic spread, and delayed systemic therapy (Rahman et al., 2021; Blay et al., 2023). The aggressive clinical course observed in our patient is consistent with previous reports of advanced ovarian synovial sarcoma.

Given the rarity and complexity of this malignancy, multidisciplinary management involving gynecologic oncology, pediatric oncology, pathology, thoracic surgery, radiology, and supportive care teams is essential for optimizing diagnosis and treatment planning (Rahman et al., 2021; Blay et al., 2023).

Differential diagnosis

Because ovarian spindle cell neoplasms in adolescents demonstrate substantial morphologic overlap, integration of clinical findings, serum tumor markers, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry is essential to establish an accurate diagnosis. The diagnosis of primary ovarian synovial sarcoma is particularly challenging due to its overlap with several ovarian neoplasms, especially in adolescents. One of the main differential diagnoses is immature teratoma with sarcomatoid differentiation, which may exhibit spindle cell morphology and mimic synovial sarcoma. However, germ cell tumors are frequently associated with elevated serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels and demonstrate a distinct immunophenotypic profile, lacking the characteristic markers of synovial sarcoma (Birbas et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025). The presence of heterologous cartilaginous differentiation may mimic a teratomatous component; however, the absence of other germ cell elements and the characteristic immunophenotype support synovial sarcoma (Thway and Fisher, 2014).

Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma represents an important differential diagnosis, particularly in tumors expressing myogenic markers such as myogenin and desmin. However, rhabdomyosarcoma typically demonstrates diffuse nuclear myogenin expression and lacks epithelial marker expression such as EMA (Parham and Ellison, 2006). In contrast, synovial sarcoma may exhibit focal myogenic differentiation alongside epithelial marker and TLE1 positivity, representing a recognized diagnostic pitfall (Rekhi et al., 2012; Qassid et al., 2016; Lim and Oliva, 2017).

Sex cord–stromal tumors, including granulosa cell tumors and Sertoli–Leydig cell tumors, may also mimic synovial sarcoma. These tumors typically express inhibin, calretinin, and WT-1, all of which were negative in this case, effectively excluding this category (Rekhi et al., 2012; Lim and Oliva, 2017).

Other spindle cell tumors, such as leiomyosarcoma and fibrosarcoma, should also be considered. Leiomyosarcoma typically demonstrates diffuse smooth muscle marker expression, including SMA and desmin, whereas fibrosarcoma lacks epithelial differentiation and TLE1 expression (Thway and Fisher, 2014; Nasiri et al., 2017).

In the present case, the combination of monophasic spindle-cell morphology, strong TLE1 positivity with co-expression of BCL-2, CD99, and EMA, together with negative sex cord–stromal markers and normal AFP levels, favored synovial sarcoma over other differential diagnoses (Thway and Fisher, 2014; Coindre et al., 2003; Rekhi et al., 2012).

Conclusion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of primary ovarian monophasic synovial sarcoma with heterologous cartilaginous differentiation, which may resemble germ cell tumors and other spindle cell sarcomas. Comprehensive immunohistochemical evaluation, including SS18 fusion-specific immunohistochemistry when available, is essential for establishing an accurate diagnosis and distinguishing this rare malignancy from other ovarian spindle cell neoplasms. Although early diagnosis remains challenging because of the nonspecific clinical presentation and rapid tumor progression, careful integration of clinical, radiologic, histopathologic, and immunohistochemical findings may facilitate earlier recognition and timely multidisciplinary management.
