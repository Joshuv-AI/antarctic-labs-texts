Chemotherapy switch for localized pancreatic cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Esther N Dekker, Raja R Narayan, Mohamed A Ahmami, Anis Meddouch, Eva M M Verkolf, Anne M Gehrels, Marc G H Besselink, Casper H J van Eijck, Marjolein Y V Homs, Bianca Mostert, Grainne M O’Kane, Roeland F de Wilde, Johanna W Wilmink, Eileen M O’Reilly, Motaz Qadan, Bas Groot Koerkamp
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with localized (that is non-metastatic) pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma with an inadequate response or toxicity to first-line chemotherapy may benefit from chemotherapy switch. The aim was to explore the available data on the use and effect of chemotherapy switch, as reported in the literature.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in Embase, MEDLINE (Ovid), the Web of Science, Cochrane, and Google Scholar on 1 December 2023. The main outcomes were the proportion of patients who underwent chemotherapy switch and the carbohydrate antigen 19-9 response and resection, R0 resection, and ypN0 resection rates after chemotherapy switch. Data were pooled using a random-effects model.ResultsA total of five retrospective studies, representing 863 patients with localized pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, were included and 226 of the 863 patients underwent chemotherapy switch. In four studies, first-line chemotherapy consisted of 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin/irinotecan with oxaliplatin (‘FOLFIRINOX’) and patients were switched to gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel. Reasons for chemotherapy switch included an inadequate biochemical, clinical, or radiological response, or toxicity. Three studies compared patients who underwent chemotherapy switch with patients who only received first-line chemotherapy and found that the proportion of patients who underwent chemotherapy switch was 20.5% (95% c.i. 10.5% to 36.3%). The pooled resection rate after chemotherapy switch was 42.0% (95% c.i. 16.6% to 72.5%). Two studies compared the chance of resection after chemotherapy switch versus first-line chemotherapy alone and found a risk ratio of 0.88 (95% c.i. 0.65 to 1.18). Two studies, with a combined total of 576 patients, found similar postoperative survival for patients who underwent chemotherapy switch and patients who only received first-line chemotherapy.ConclusionOne in five patients with localized pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma underwent chemotherapy switch after an inadequate response or toxicity to first-line chemotherapy. The pooled resection rate after chemotherapy switch was 42% and similar in overall survival compared with first-line chemotherapy only. Three ongoing trials are investigating chemotherapy switch in patients with an inadequate radiological or carbohydrate antigen 19-9 response.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a leading cause of cancer-associated mortality, with a 5-year survival of 13%1. Localized (that is non-metastatic) PDAC is classified as resectable, borderline resectable (BR), or locally advanced (LA), based on the extent of vascular contact2. The combination of systemic chemotherapy and surgical resection results in the best long-term survival3. Neoadjuvant and induction treatment has become the standard of care for patients with BR and LA PDAC. However, the role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in resectable PDAC is unclear and is under investigation in the ongoing ALLIANCE 21806 (NCT04340141) and PREOPANC-3 (NCT04927780) trials4,5. The most frequently used neoadjuvant regimens are (modified) 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin/irinotecan with oxaliplatin ((m)FOLFIRINOX) and gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel4,6.

At restaging, radiological imaging and the serum biomarker carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) level are used to assess treatment response. After first-line chemotherapy, it is unclear whether patients with stable disease on imaging or an inadequate decrease in CA19-9 benefit from chemotherapy switch. Additionally, treatment-related toxicity may prevent patients from completing first-line chemotherapeutic regimens7.

In 2019, a retrospective study from MD Anderson identified 25 patients who switched to gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel after first-line FOLFIRINOX for localized PDAC8. The main reasons for switch of chemotherapy were an inadequate response on imaging, and/or CA19-9, or toxicity. A total of 21 patients (84%) demonstrated a response after chemotherapy switch and 11 patients (44%) underwent a subsequent resection. Since then, several studies have investigated chemotherapy switch for localized PDAC. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to investigate the outcomes of patients with localized PDAC who underwent chemotherapy switch.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic literature review was performed according to the PRISMA guidelines (Table S1)9. MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, the Web of Science, and Google Scholar were searched by a biomedical information specialist until 1 December 2023, using the search terms ‘pancreatic neoplasms’, ‘neoadjuvant therapy’, ‘failure’, ‘switch’, ‘response’, ‘second-line’, and applicable alternatives (Table S2). In addition, references of all retrieved articles were manually reviewed for additional relevant studies. Quality of evidence was determined independently by E.N.D. and R.R.N., and rated based on the Newcastle–Ottawa scale10. A score of zero to nine was assigned to each study using an evaluation process that considered patient selection, comparability, and outcome.

Study selection and data collection process

Eligible studies included patients with localized PDAC who underwent neoadjuvant or induction chemotherapy switch. Studies with patients who underwent chemotherapy switch only after distant progression or only in the adjuvant setting were excluded. Titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (M.A.A. and A.M.) and irrelevant articles were excluded. The remaining articles underwent full-text review to exclude case reports, studies with overlapping study populations, and articles written in a language other than English. Disagreements were resolved with the involvement of a third reviewer (E.N.D.). For every eligible study, information was extracted on study characteristics, patient characteristics, and patient outcomes using a standardized data form.

Data analysis

The statistical software package R (version 4.2.1), with its metaprop function, was used for the statistical analyses11. Meta-analyses were performed using a random-effects model. The heterogeneity of the aggregated study findings was evaluated using the inconsistency statistic (I2) and the associated chi-squared test for heterogeneity; additionally, 95% confidence intervals for I2 were computed. Additional analyses were performed for studies comparing patients who underwent chemotherapy switch versus patients who only received first-line chemotherapy. The risk ratio (RR) for the chance of resection was calculated by dividing the resection rate after chemotherapy switch by the resection rate after first-line chemotherapy alone12. P ≤ 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

The search strategy resulted in 2537 records, from which 1080 duplicates were removed. The remaining 1457 records were screened for eligibility by looking at titles and abstracts. A total of 58 full-text articles were evaluated, of which 5 articles were eligible for inclusion (Fig. S1)8,13–16.

Study characteristics

All five of the included studies were retrospective studies; four from the USA and one from Japan (Table 1). Patients were included from 2009 to 2020. The sample size varied between 8 and 468 patients. Two studies only included patients who underwent chemotherapy switch8,14. Three studies also included consecutive patients who only received first-line chemotherapy for non-randomized comparison with patients who underwent chemotherapy switch13,15,16. Three studies included patients with resectable, BR, or LA disease8,15,16, one study included patients with BR or LA disease13, and one study included patients with only LA disease14. The studies used different radiological resectability criteria. One study only included patients who underwent surgical resection13.

Based on the Newcastle–Ottawa scale, four studies were of good quality (that is a score of six to nine) and one study was of moderate quality (that is a score of three to five) (Table S3).

Treatment regimens

In three studies, first-line chemotherapy consisted of FOLFIRINOX8,15,16. In one study, first-line chemotherapy involved chemotherapy regimens based on either 5-fluorouracil (FOLFIRINOX or Folinic acid, fluorouracil and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX)) or gemcitabine (gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel)13. The first-line chemotherapy regimen was not reported in the study from Japan14. In all studies, restaging took place every 2–3 months during neoadjuvant therapy to evaluate treatment response. In three studies, patients were switched to gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel8,15,16. In one study, patients were switched to either a 5-fluorouracil- or gemcitabine-based regimen13 and, in the remaining study, patients were switched to (m)FOLFIRINOX14. In four studies, selected patients received radiotherapy after neoadjuvant chemotherapy8,13,15,16.

Reasons for chemotherapy switch

Alva-Ruiz et al.13 included 139 patients who underwent chemotherapy switch. The most common indications were non-metastatic local disease progression on radiological imaging (increase in tumour size or vascular involvement) in 58 patients (41.7%) and CA19-9 increase in 54 patients (38.8%). A total of 35 patients (25.2%) had a lack of objective response, based on clinical factors (increased pain score or weight loss), CA19-9 levels (no decline from baseline), radiological assessment (no decrease in tumour size or vascular involvement), and metabolic assessment (no decrease in tumoral PET avidity). Treatment-related toxicity was noted as the reason for chemotherapy switch in 27 patients (19.4%)13. There were multiple reasons for chemotherapy switch in 38 patients (27.3%). Fong et al.15 observed 22 patients who underwent chemotherapy switch due to a poor treatment response. Details regarding the nature of the poor responses were not provided. Kobayashi et al.14 observed eight patients, but did not report the reasons for chemotherapy switch. Thalji et al.16 observed 32 patients who underwent chemotherapy switch; 23 patients (71.9%) had no CA19-9 response (12 of 23 patients had increasing CA19-9 levels), 4 patients (12.5%) had local disease progression on radiological imaging, and 5 patients (15.6%) switched because of toxicity. Vreeland et al.8 included 25 patients who underwent chemotherapy switch. The reported reasons included a poor response in 16 patients (64.0%), toxicity in 6 patients (24.0%), and both a poor response and toxicity in 3 patients (12.0%). A poor response was defined as either the absence of a greater than or equal to 50% decrease in serum CA19-9 or the absence of a radiological response, where any positive radiological development was recorded as a response8.

Carbohydrate antigen 19-9 response

Three studies reported the median CA19-9 level at diagnosis, ranging from 162 to 487 U/ml8,13,16. In addition, Thalji et al.16 and Vreeland et al.8 reported median CA19-9 levels before chemotherapy switch of 247 and 574 U/ml respectively. After chemotherapy switch, the median CA19-9 levels decreased to 56 and 50 U/ml respectively (Table 2)8,16. Additionally, Alva-Ruiz et al.13 and Thalji et al.16 reported the normalization of serum CA19-9 levels (defined as CA19-9 less than 37 U/ml and CA19-9 less than 35 U/ml respectively) in 64 of 100 patients (64.0%) and 11 of 32 patients (34.4%) respectively. Furthermore, Thalji et al.16 reported that 26 of 27 patients (96.3%) without a CA19-9 response to first-line chemotherapy had a CA19-9 response after chemotherapy switch.

Tumour response on imaging

Two studies reported the response on imaging after chemotherapy switch (Table 2)8,16. Thalji et al.16 reported a response on imaging, defined as a decrease in the primary tumour size or an improvement in the relationship of the tumour with adjacent blood vessels, in 20 of 32 patients (62.5%). In the study by Vreeland et al.8, 17 of 25 patients (68.0%) had a response on imaging after chemotherapy switch, defined as any positive response, whereas 3 patients (12.0%) had stable disease, 2 patients (8.0%) had a mixed radiological response, and 3 patients (12.0%) had disease progression8.

Resection and pathological examination

The pooled resection proportion was 42.0% (140 of 226 patients; 95% c.i. 16.6% to 72.5%) (5 studies) (I2 = 81.8%; P < 0.001) (Table 3 and Fig. 1a). The pooled proportion of patients who underwent R0 resection out of all resected patients after chemotherapy switch was 92.1% (129 of 140 patients; 95% c.i. 86.4% to 95.6%) (4 studies) (I2 = 0%; P = 0.674) (Fig. 1b). The pooled proportion of patients with ypN0- status after chemotherapy switch was 55.3% (83 of 135 patients; 95% c.i. 36.6% to 72.6%) (3 studies) (I2 = 68.7%; P = 0.041) (Fig. 1c). Tumour response in resected specimens was evaluated in patients after chemotherapy switch in three studies8,13,16. The pathological response rates varied because different definitions were used (Table 3).

Overall survival

The median overall survival (OS) for patients who underwent chemotherapy switch varied considerably across all studies (Table 4). Vreeland et al.8 reported the median OS for three subgroups. Among patients who underwent resection, the median OS was 23.7 months. For the ten patients who had a radiological or CA19-9 response after chemotherapy switch, but did not undergo resection, the median OS was 15.9 months. The median OS was 5.6 months for the four patients who neither responded after chemotherapy switch nor underwent resection8.

Outcomes after first-line chemotherapy alone versus after chemotherapy switch

The pooled proportion of patients who underwent chemotherapy switch after first-line chemotherapy was 20.5% (193 of 830 patients; 95% c.i. 10.5% to 36.3%) (3 studies) (I2 = 94.8%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1d). Three studies compared the outcomes of patients who underwent chemotherapy switch with those of patients who only received first-line chemotherapy13,15,16. In the study by Alva-Ruiz et al.13, the proportion of patients with CA19-9 normalization after first-line chemotherapy only or after chemotherapy switch was similar (55.3% versus 64.0% respectively; P = 0.125). Thalji et al.16 compared CA19-9 response in patients after 4 months of chemotherapy, which was 82% after first-line chemotherapy and 97% after chemotherapy switch (P = 0.04). Fong et al.15 reported a resection rate of 59.1% (137 of 232 patients) after first-line chemotherapy compared with 22.7% (5 of 22 patients) after chemotherapy switch (P < 0.010). Thalji et al.16 reported resection rates of 68.4% (52 of 76 patients) after first-line chemotherapy and 75.0% (24 of 32 patients) after chemotherapy switch (P = 0.490). The calculated RR for resection was 0.88 (95% c.i. 0.65 to 1.18) after chemotherapy switch, based on pooled data (from 2 studies) involving a total of 362 patients15,16.

In terms of major pathological tumour response rate (College of American Pathologists (CAP) score 0/1) in resected specimens, Alva-Ruiz et al.13 found no significant difference between the first-line treatment and chemotherapy switch groups (31.3% versus 30.0% respectively; P = 0.900). Thalji et al.16 reported a higher complete or near complete response rate using the Ryan score of 30.8% for the first-line treatment group compared with 8.3% for the chemotherapy switch group.

Two studies compared OS after first-line chemotherapy and after chemotherapy switch. Alva-Ruiz et al.13 reported a median OS of 41.4 months for the first-line treatment group and 36.4 months for the chemotherapy switch group (P = 0.940). Likewise, Thalji et al.16 reported a median OS of 44 months versus 44 months for patients who received all intended neoadjuvant treatment and surgery in the first-line treatment and chemotherapy switch groups.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis included five studies, representing 863 patients with localized PDAC, and 226 of the 863 patients underwent chemotherapy switch. Pooled analysis found that 20.5% of patients (1 in every 5) on first-line treatment underwent chemotherapy switch. The primary reasons for chemotherapy switch were stable disease or local progression on radiological imaging, insufficient decrease or increase in CA19-9 levels, or toxicity to first-line treatment. The pooled resection rate for patients after chemotherapy switch was 42%. Moreover, patients who underwent chemotherapy switch were only 12% less likely to undergo a resection (RR 0.88). The median OS after resection was similar in the two studies that compared patients who received first-line chemotherapy and those who underwent chemotherapy switch.

Guidelines recommend continuation of first-line chemotherapy for patients demonstrating tolerability without disease progression. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN; version 2023-2) guideline recommends consideration of alternative neoadjuvant systemic treatment in patients with resectable and BR PDAC who experience disease progression, defined by increasing CA19-9 levels or enlargement of tumour mass on imaging, but remain eligible for surgical resection. This recommendation is similar to that in the setting of metastatic disease, in which second-line chemotherapy is recommended after disease progression2. The European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) Clinical Practice Guideline also recommends second-line chemotherapy for disease progression in metastatic disease, but does not specify management for patients with localized PDAC who experience disease progression whilst on first-line chemotherapy17.The current clinical practice for chemotherapy switch varies among countries. In the Netherlands, chemotherapy switch is only reimbursed when patients with localized disease progress to metastatic disease. In the USA, the criteria for chemotherapy switch vary among centres. In the metastatic setting, stable disease is a favourable outcome and second-line chemotherapy is typically only initiated after disease progression. In the setting of localized disease, the threshold to switch chemotherapy is generally lower, because a response is more important to increase the odds of a complete resection. In the absence of disease progression, the authors recommend 2–4 months of first-line chemotherapy to fully understand its therapeutic efficacy. The timing of chemotherapy switch should be informed by performance status, serum CA19-9 trajectory, toxicity, and imaging.

Serum CA19-9 is currently the most important circulating biomarker to determine the response to first-line chemotherapy in patients with PDAC. In a systematic review and pooled analysis including 17 studies representing 3335 patients with localized PDAC who received systemic treatment, CA19-9 decrease was strongly associated with improved OS; a CA19-9 decrease of more than 50% had an HR of 0.43 (95% c.i. 0.29 to 0.56; P < 0.010) and CA19-9 normalization had an HR of 0.52 (95% c.i. 0.42 to 0.63; P < 0.010)18. Truty et al.7 also found that CA19-9 normalization was an independent favourable factor for OS, with an HR of 0.49 (95% c.i. 0.30 to 0.90). Future studies should investigate the optimal cut-offs for serum CA19-9 response for chemotherapy switch.

The studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis were all retrospective. Three ongoing non-randomized phase II trials may provide better insights into the role of an adaptive approach during neoadjuvant treatment in patients with localized PDAC (Table S4). The NeoOPTIMIZE trial is evaluating the effectiveness of early switching from mFOLFIRINOX to gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel in patients with resectable, BR, or LA PDAC19; in this trial, patients with toxicity to mFOLFIRINOX or who demonstrate radiological disease progression, CA19-9 decline less than 30%, or CA19-9 increase after four cycles are switched to gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel plus losartan for two more cycles. Another ongoing phase II trial is focusing on patients with resectable and BR PDAC20; after four cycles of mFOLFIRINOX, patients are switched to gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel for four cycles, depending on radiological response, CA19-9 response, and chemotherapy toxicity. In the third ongoing phase II trial (NCT03322995), patients with resectable and BR PDAC undergo chemotherapy switch in the absence of a ‘significant’ decline in CA19-9 levels or stable disease on radiological imaging; patients with radiological local disease progression receive chemoradiation.

Nevertheless, an RCT is required to confirm whether chemotherapy switch improves OS. Such an RCT should include patients with an inadequate response to first-line chemotherapy, who remain fit to undergo chemotherapy switch and subsequent surgical resection; patients in the intervention arm should switch after an inadequate response (for example CA19-9 decrease less than 20%) and patients in the standard-of-care arm should only switch when there is disease progression (that is increase in CA19-9 or increase in tumour size). At the same time, RCTs should investigate whether a planned chemotherapy switch regardless of treatment response could improve OS. Finally, promising results were reported in a retrospective study by AlMasri et al.21, in which the adjuvant regimen was based on the CA19-9 or pathological response to the preoperative regimen; thus, RCTs should also investigate this approach.

Liquid and tissue biomarkers are promising for personalized treatment of PDAC. Predictive biomarkers could determine the best regimen for a specific patient, rather than an empirical strategy requiring chemotherapy switch. Several promising biomarkers have been identified for selecting patients for FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine-based treatment22–24. For example, elevated human equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (hENT1) mRNA expression by tumours leads to an improved response rate and OS in patients receiving gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel25. Patients with mothers against decapentaplegic homolog 4 (SMAD4) alterations have an increased risk of metastases during neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX26. GATA-binding factor 6 (GATA-6) expression may also predict chemotherapy response27,28. However, these and several other potential predictive biomarkers for PDAC have not been validated in RCTs. In an ongoing phase II trial (NCT04683315), patients with the classical transcriptional subtype receive mFOLFIRINOX, whereas patients with basal subtype receive gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel29. In the ongoing PASS-01 phase II trial (NCT04469556), patients with metastatic PDAC are randomized to mFOLFIRINOX versus gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel; the primary aim of the PASS-01 phase II trial is to find and validate predictive biomarkers30.

The main limitation of the present study is the inclusion of only five retrospective studies. This limitation affects the overall quality and strength of the evidence provided. Second, there was considerable heterogeneity regarding resectability status at diagnosis and in the criteria for chemotherapy switch. Due to these limitations, it is challenging to draw definitive conclusions regarding the true benefits of chemotherapy switch or to determine criteria to guide chemotherapy switch.

In conclusion, about one in five patients with localized PDAC in this meta-analysis underwent chemotherapy switch after inadequate response or toxicity to first-line chemotherapy. The pooled resection rate after chemotherapy switch was 42% with similar OS compared to first-line chemotherapy only. The systematic review particularly underscored that randomized controlled trials are needed.
