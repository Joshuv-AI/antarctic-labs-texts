Superparamagnetic MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 lipid-coated small nanoparticles: Synthesis, physicochemical characterization and biocompatibility assessment in drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Aline S Perez, Denise Selivon, Antonio M․Figueiredo Neto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), particularly manganese ferrite (MnFe2O4), have emerged as promising candidates for biomedical applications due to their tunable magnetic properties, biocompatibility, and functionalization potential. In this study, we synthesized superparamagnetic MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 core-shell nanoparticles (5.8 nm inorganic core, ∼10 nm lipid-coated) functionalized with oleic acid (OA) or soy lecithin (Lec) to enhance biocompatibility. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to combine this unique hybrid core-shell structure with lipid coatings and evaluate its safety in an animal model. To characterize the MNPs we provided structural, magnetic, and physical-chemistry studies using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), dynamic light scattering (DLS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), magnetization hysteresis, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements. To assess acute and chronic nanotoxicity, fruit flies from parental and F1 generations were fed a diet containing MNPs at concentrations of 0.0, 0.1, and 1.0 mg/mL, and were evaluated throughout all developmental stages. The findings revealed that the MNPs showed no signs of toxicity at any of the concentrations tested. We combined hybrid core-shell superparamagnetic nanoparticles with organic lipid-coated that exhibit unique physicochemical characteristics, confirming their low in vivo nanotoxicity in Drosophila melanogaster and supporting their potential as biocompatible, magnetically responsive and small-sized platforms for biomedical application such as drug delivery due to their biocompatibility, magnetic properties, and physicochemical stability.Keywords: Superparamagnetic nanoparticles, Soy lecithin, Oleic acid, Nanotoxicity, Drosophila melanogaster, Biocompatibility

Introduction

Ferrite magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) have received attention in recent years due to their unique features, given their applications in drug delivery, magnetic resonance imaging, clinical diagnosis, and tumour treatment by hyperthermia [1,2]. Indeed, materials of nanometric dimensions (1–100 nm) are already approved for clinical use and numerous products are being evaluated in clinical trials [3]. When particles are sufficiently small, that is, <10 nm, including the ligand shell such as lipids, polymers, proteins, etc., which typically corresponds to a core just a few nanometers in size, they can be considered in a "quasi-molecular regime". In this state, the biomolecular surroundings interact so quickly with the particle surface that complex cellular and immune recognition processes are often significantly reduced [4]. However, there are toxicological concerns and ethical issues that accompany nanomedicine that always have to be considered and properly investigated [5]. The nanomaterials’ sizes are in the same range of dimension as proteins, membrane receptors, antibodies, and nucleic acids, among other biomolecules. These characteristics, together with their high surface-volume ratio and the possibility of tuning their properties, make nanoparticles powerful tools for medical applications such as imaging, diagnosis, and therapy [6].

The purpose of applying nanoparticles will require design, composition, functionalization, and dimensions that must be considered. The MNPs’ characteristics perhaps lead to some limitations including high nanotoxicity, unfavorable pharmacokinetics or challenging drug incorporation, depending on the composition and synthesis method. The synthesis methods and conditions employed during the synthesis are the primary factors that determine the caracteristics of the MNPs [[7], [8], [9]]. There are several MNP synthesis techniques, but the most common include thermal decomposition and chemical coprecipitation. The latter is a classical method used for decades in preparations to form precipitation from homogeneous solutions. Generally, the precipitating agent is added to the aqueous solution of the precursor to yield products during the reaction and does not require high temperatures (80 – 100 °C). It is a simple method performed to achieve relative control of MNP size, with uniform shape. Table S1, in Supplementary Material, presents a comparison of the synthesis methods that lists the required temperature, reaction time, and sizes obtained.

Although there is wide range of known magnetic materials with exploitable heating efficiency, biomedical applicability is strongly restricted by their biocompatibility. Ferrimagnetic nanoparticles are preferred due to their well-known characteristics as soft magnetic materials, which exhibit low magnetic anisotropy constants. This property enables a highly responsive behavior to alternating magnetic fields, making them exceptionally suitable for applications in magnetic hyperthermia and other therapeutic technologies [10]. Following this concept, it seems a proper strategy to incorporate other ions such as Co2+, Mn2+, and Ni2+ into typical ferrites (MFe2O4, where M=Co, Mn, Ni…), in an effort to attain tunable magnetic features [11]. Here, we chose modified MnFe2O4 ferrite nanoparticles by adding a thin surface layer of Fe2O3 based on their low inherent nanotoxicity, physical and chemical stability, ease of synthesis, and tunable magnetic properties. In general, typical ferrites dispersed in water can easily aggregate due to attractive forces in nanoscale. To improve stability, surface decoration can be a convenient approach to avoiding aggregation, particle decantation and favoring interaction with complexes (e.g. micelles, vesicles) for drug delivery [12].

In the present study, we describe the synthesis of small size (<6 nm) core-shell MNPs, with a structure that enhances their magnetic response, which is crucial for biomedical applications. Furthermore, we added an organic layer to the MPNs, making them hydrophobic, increasing their biocompatibility. Lipid-coated magnetic nanoparticles (Fe3O4) were previously reported by De Cuyper et al. [13]. In our approach, however, we employ lipids such as oleic acid, a fatty acid naturally found in olive oil, nuts, and soybeans, and soy lecithin, a complex mixture of phospholipids, triglycerides, and glycolipids derived from soy. Specifically, we employed a high-purity (>95 %) l-α-phosphatidylcholine fraction from soy lecithin. Herein, we report the design and synthesis of an innovative hybrid nanoplatform. At its core, a superparamagnetic nanoparticle with a tailored Fe2O3-shell architecture provides superior biocompatibility. This core is seamlessly encapsulated by a lipid layer, chosen for its biomimetic properties to ensure colloidal stability, enhance biocompatibility, and facilitate potential drug loading due its size, offering a multifaceted solution for advanced applications in combined diagnosis and therapy.

To characterize the MNPs we provided structural, magnetic, and physical-chemistry studies using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), dynamic light scattering (DLS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), magnetization, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements. Furthermore, we provide an in vivo nanotoxicity study using the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster animal model, which is an excellent model system to address biomedical questions and insights from studies in D. melanogaster that can directly impact our understanding in relation to humans [14]. Nanotoxicity tests were based on negative geotaxis climbing, crawling, dark-light preference, mortality rate, and changes in mechanical and motor patterns assays. To assess acute and chronic nanotoxicity, fruit flies from the parental and F1 generations were fed a diet containing MNPs at concentrations of 0.0, 0.1, and 1.0 mg/mL, and were evaluated throughout all developmental stages.

Material and methods

Chemicals

Ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH - 27 %), iron III chloride (Cl3Fe - 98 %), manganese II chloride tetrahydrate (Cl2Mn·4H2O - 99 %), iron III nitrate nonahydrate (FeN3O9·9H2O - 98 %), chloroform (CHCl3 - 99 %), and oleic acid (C18H34O2 - 99 %) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Nitric acid (HNO₃ - 65 %), acetone, and ethylic alcohol were supplied from Lab Synth. l-α- phosphatidylcholine (Soy - 95 %) was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. The aqueous solutions were prepared with deionized water Type I (Millipore Milli-Q Gradient quality).

Synthesis of MnFe2O4

The MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 nanoparticles were carried out in three basic steps: 1) ferrite magnetic nanoparticle synthesis, 2) then the chemical surface treatment, and 3) the coating of the particles with oleic acid or soy lecithin. A MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 nanoparticle stock solution was prepared by coprecipitating aqueous metallic solutions, modified from [15]. 1) 20 mL Cl3Fe [0.5 mol/L] with 2.5 % HCl [1 M] and 10 mL Cl2Mn [0.5 mol/L] with 2.5 % HCl [1 M] mixture in 400 mL NH4OH alkaline medium [0.6 mol/L]. Fig. S1 in Supplementary Material illustrates these steps. The metal salt solutions were rapidly added into the NH4OH solution at 80 °C under vigorous agitation for 30 min. At the end a black precipitate was formed at the bottom of the beaker. This precipitate was left under the action of a magnet (diameter: 12 cm, thickness: 1 cm) until it reached room temperature. All excess alkaline solution was removed and 75 mL of HNO3 [2 mol/L] was gently added to control polydispersity, polish the surface of nanoparticles, and eliminate any by-products. After 4 h, excess nitric acid was removed and 2) 150 mL of Fe(NO3)3 [0.5 mol/L] was added and boiled at 100 °C for 40 min. Excess nitric acid (or ferric nitrate) was removed by a permanent magnet that decanted the nanoparticles onto the edges of the beaker, leaving the liquid in the central region of the container, and with a pipette the liquid can be removed. After cooling, the excess ferric nitrate was removed and the resulting precipitate was washed 3–5 times with acetone. After complete evaporation of the solvent, 5 mL of distilled water were added. The pH was adjusted to 2 (24 h - dialysis) for the stock solution. Part of the stock solution was dried in a vacuum oven (70 °C) for 24 h for analysis. On average, the concentration of MNP was 30 mg/mL.

MNP coating

The last step of the synthesis initiates with an aqueous colloidal dispersion of MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 (pH = 2, HNO3) obtained previously, diluted in 30 mL of distilled water and 20 mL of oleic acid (or 0.05 g of soy lecithin). The mixture remains under vigorous stirring for 5 h at 30 °C, forming a dark, shiny, and dense mixture. Then, the mixture is washed ten times with ethyl alcohol to eliminate free lipid molecules. The washes are interspersed with centrifugations of 4 min at 300 rpm at 25 °C. The final precipitation is left in a vacuum oven for 48 h at 45 °C.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The micrographs were obtained in a JEM2100 electron microscope (JEOL) using a carbon type-A 400 mesh Cu or 300 mesh lacey carbon film Cu/Ni. The MNPs were dispersed in deionized water or chloroform following this procedure: nanoparticles were dissolved in a convenient solvent (0.01 mg/mL) and then laid over the grid and let to dry. The histogram was obtained by pixel measuring the diameter of the MNPs using ImageJ software. A Gaussian or lognormal function fit was performed to obtain the mean diameter of the size distribution. EDS spectra were measured simultaneously to investigate the composition of the image nanoparticles.

To investigate whether there were MNPs in D. melanogaster excrement, ten animals (five females and five males) for MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec were left for three days in a medium with MNPs (1 mg/mL). Next, the feces were collected and dissolved in distilled water. This liquid was properly placed on the grid and allowed to dry.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

To gather deeper insights about the surface composition of the lipid-coated MNPs, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements were performed using a Perkin Elmer FTIR spectrometer (model Frontier). The spectra were recorded in absorbance mode from 4000 to 650 cm−1 using five scans at 4 cm−1 resolution. The synthesized sample was dried for 48 h at 45 °C. 0.1 g of powder was macerated before measurement.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The hydrodynamic diameter of the MNPs was measured using DLS (NanoBrook 90 Plus from Brookhaven Instruments Corp.) at a scattering angle of 90° and wavelength of 657 nm. The samples were diluted, 0.05 mg/mL, with distillated water for MnFe2O4 and chloroform to MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec buffer, placed in glass cuvettes at room temperature (25 °C). Four measurements of 4 min each were carried out for each sample. Data analysis was caried out using the Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS) method. Magnetization

The magnetic hysteresis properties of the MNPs were evaluated using a conventional vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; 4500, EG&G, MD, USA). The measurements were performed at room temperature under an applied magnetic field ranging from −15 to +15 kOe, at 290 K.

Drosophila melanogaster strain and culture conditions

The stock of wild-type D. melanogaster was cultivated and maintained in the Laboratory of Evolutionary Studies of Fruit Flies, at the Institute of Biosciences of University of São Paulo. The flies and larvae were cultured at 22 °C ± 1 °C on standard food (containing water, banana, agar, and nipagin [methyl-p-hydroxybenzoate]), 60 % relative humidity and were exposed to a 12-hour dark-light cycle.

Treatment of drosophila melanogaster with MNPs

For nanotoxicity assays, three MNPs (naked MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 _OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec) were formulated in the diet. Two different concentrations (0.1 and 1.0 mg/mL) for each MNP dispersed in food (40 °C) and 5 mL of the mixture were placed in 8.5 cm high and 2.3 cm diameter vials. For each drug concentration, five replicates (five females + five males each vial) were carried out and triplicates of control (untreated) animals were performed. The animals were separated in the final pupal stage and placed in a treated/control vial. Considering that one D. melanogaster typically eats 0.0015 mL of food/day [14], the maximum dose of MNPs administered to the flies (1.0 mg/mL MNPs) was 0.0015 mg per day. By studying the parental and the F1 generation, we can investigate acute nanotoxicity and chronic nanotoxicity, respectively. Since the 48-hour exposure of the parents represents 4 % of their life (considering 50 days), we can observe possible immediate effects of exposure to the nanoparticles studied here.

Survivability assay

The survivability assay determined the number of days of fly survival, measured in groups of ten flies/concentration of each type of nanoparticle or control group (parental and F1). The experiment was performed in triplicate, together with untreated or control flies. Phenotype observation

The nanomaterial’s character was examined by checking phenotypes to determine whether it harmed the model organisms. Fifty adult flies were screened for phenotypes in their eyes, wings, bristles, and abdomens. The images were taken with a stereo microscope (Leica MC 120 HD).

Negative geotaxis climbing assay

Negative geotaxis climbing assay was performed, modified from [18], on adult flies on the 10th day after hatch. The tubes were marked to 15.5 cm distance. Ten flies were taken per group (untreated group, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec) at concentrations of 0.1 and 1.0 mg/mL. The number of flies reaching a vertical distance of 15.5 cm was measured for each group. The measurement was repeated three times for each group, with intervals of 15 min.

Dark-light larvae assay

This experiment detected an early photoreceptor deficiency using the approach described in [19]. Briefly, a Petri dish (5.5 cm diameter) was divided into four quadrants, with the opposite quadrant being colored black (two quadrants were white). Then, 2.5 % agarose was added and let to set. Ten third instar larvae from both the control and the treatment vials (untreated, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec) at concentrations of 0.1 and 1.0 mg/mL were kept in the dark for 3 h before the experiment began. The larvae were placed in the center of the dish at 25 °C. The Petri dish was uniformly illuminated (white LED light, 6500 K, 9 W, 50 Hz), and the larvae were given 5 min to move freely between the dark and light sections. After 5 min, the larvae in the dark and light sections were counted.

Crawling assay

For this assay, we evaluated third instar larvae (n = 15 per group), which is the final larval stage before pupation, exposed to MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec nanocomposites at concentrations of 0.1 and 1.0 mg/mL, along with control specimens. The larvae were transferred to agar plates prepared as standardized crawling substrates, at 25 °C, after washing with pure water to remove food particles. Five larvae were positioned at the center of fresh agar plates, overlaid with graph paper for path tracing. The apparatus was prepared using a Petri dish of 70 × 15 mm containing 2.5 % solidified agar. We created a track in the agar and recorded the movement of individual wandering third instar larvae placed within it. The average crawling speed of each larva was calculated based on the distance traveled over 60 s, as measured from the recordings.

Statistical analysis

For data with approximately normal distribution, the One-Way ANOVA test was applied, in a comparison between the treated sample and the control. All statistical calculations were performed using the PAST software (Hammer, O., Harper, D.A.T., Ryan, P.D.2001). P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Physical characterization of MNPs

Fig. 1A) shows XRD patterns at room temperature. They confirm the presence of single-phase MnFe2O4 spinel type when compared to (JCPDS, 73–1964, graphic in Supplementary Material – Fig. S2) for both naked and coated MNPs. The thin maghemite layer (γ-Fe2O3) formed on the surface of the nanoparticle, a structure with a core, composed of ferrite, and a maghemite shell, is given the name of core-shell structure. It is not observed by the XRD technique, but it is shown in high-resolution transmission electron microscopy [21]. It is also demonstrated that the lipid layer on the MNP shell does not alter the peaks in the XRD spectrum. In all the analyzed diffractograms, no intensity peaks related to any other type of crystal structure were found, which indicates that there are no significant amounts of impurities of any nature in the studied samples. The grain size can be estimated from the (311) XRD peak using the Scherrer Eq. (1). The measured DXR, Lattice (a), and Density values are listed in Table S1 in Supplementary Material. The crystallite sizes (DXR) for the synthesized samples are presented alongside their associated experimental uncertainties. The naked MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 nanoparticles exhibited a mean size of 5.6 ± 0.9 nm. A consistent increase in the calculated size was observed for the lipid-coated samples, with values of 6.1 ± 0.9 nm and 6.1 ± 0.9 nm for the oleic acid (MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA) and lecithin (MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec) functionalized particles, respectively. This apparent increase is attributed to differences in sample packing rather than actual crystallite growth. The presence of the lipid coating acted as a lubricant, facilitating a more compact and homogeneous arrangement of powder within the sample holder. This superior packing reduces instrumental broadening contributions, which can lead to a slight narrowing of the diffraction peaks (a decrease in the full width at half maximum, β). Lattice values, 0.849 ± 0.003 nm, calculated here, for all samples studied, are compatible with the 0.842 nm found in the literature [22,23]. Density was estimated according to Eq. (3). The value found, 5.00 g/cm3, for naked MnFe2O4 is compatible with the literature, 4.94 (g/cm³) [3].

From Fig. 1B-D), the average diameters (hydrodynamic diameter) obtained for MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec calculated on the basis of relative intensity normalized by number were 10.5 ± 2.9, 14.6 ± 3.4, and 12.8 ± 2.1 nm, respectively. The observed diameter differences between Lec-coated and OA-coated MNPs are equivalent, within the estimated error. This is also due to the low resolution of the DLS technique. We can also observe that, in the bar graph for MNP_OA, there is a small contribution from some larger particles (intensity normalized by volume), while in the same graph for MNP_Lec we do not see the same contribution. When compared with the average diameter values obtained by TEM, summarized in Table 1, higher values are observed. This is expected, since the autocorrelation curves receive contribution from aggregates as can be observed in relative intensity normalized by intensity ( %), which increases, on average, the diameters found. Furthermore, in the DLS technique, the MNPs are in a solution where a solvent layer is formed around the particle, thus the scattered light contains the information of the particle diameter added to this solvent layer (i.e., the hydrodynamic diameter). On the other hand, in the TEM technique for example, the particles are dry, and the aggregates are distinguishable from the individual particles, allowing a better measurement of the average diameter.

The analysis of TEM micrographics and their corresponding size distributions, Fig. 1E-G), shows the average sizes of MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec at 5.8, 5.8, and 5.9 nm, respectively. This indicates that the average size of magnetic nanoparticles remains the same since the synthesis of stock MNPs for all variations is the same. From the images it can be observed that the nanoparticles, morphologically, are slightly granulated, that is, they have a rough surface and can be described as approximately spherical. Besides that, the interplanar distance of the fringes observed in some microscopy images, Fig. S3 in Supplementary Material, were estimated from the FFT (Fast Fourier Transformed - ImageJ Software) at 0.25 nm. The value of 0.25 nm found from the micrographs can be confirmed by the XRD data for (311) Miller index (2θ = 35.31°) from the equation (d-spacing), d=a/h2+k2+l2 and considering the lattice parameter (a) already calculated by Eq. (1).

The size distribution results obtained here with the different techniques used are complementary, since considering the specificities of each technique we see that by DLS the average diameter found is larger than that found by TEM and XRD, since in dynamic light scattering the aggregates contribute to the final diameter values. While the average diameter values found by TEM and XRD converge, in both the aggregates do not have the same contribution to final value found. Furthermore, only in DLS do we have the nanoparticles suspended in a solvent, from which we can investigate the aggregation behavior and their hydrodynamic radius.

Now, considering that an OA molecule has approximately 2 nm in length [24], an MNP of approximately 6 nm has a final diameter increased by 4 nm, therefore 10 nm with the OA layer. Fig. S4, in Supplementary Material, illustrates the inorganic and organic core-shell. While MNPs functionalized with soy lecithin of approximately 2.5 nm length [25], the final diameter of the MNP is approximately 11 nm. Additionally, we also carried out Zeta Potential (ZP) measurements for MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 (stock dispersion in water) at pH 2 and 25 °C, which resulted in a ZP of + 40 mV. This value is compatible with a stable colloidal dispersion in water.

Magnetic characterization

Unlike ferromagnetic materials, superparamagnetic ones do not retain magnetization after the removal of the magnetic field, avoiding undesirable side effects. Therefore, ferrite nanoparticles are widely used in the biomedical field. The hysteresis loops, Fig. 2 (A-C), show the superparamagnetic behavior for all the samples in which remanence and coercivity are close to zero. Saturation magnetization (Ms) represents the maximum value of magnetization when the material is under a magnetic field high enough to align the magnetic moments of a sample. A relatively low Ms value, in this case, indicates a reduced nanoparticle size and possible surface effects (such as disorder in the orientation of spins), which can reduce the magnetic saturation value. It is known that the larger the particle diameter, the greater the interaction between the particles [26]. This effect can be a disadvantage, for example, in the application of magnetic hyperthermia therapy.

Therefore, the results we obtained for the nanoparticles studied here indicate that they are good candidates for medical applications. The saturation magnetization values found for our nanoparticles (20.51 ± 0.05 emu/g for naked MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 and 26.80 ± 0.05 emu/g for MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 _OA) are slightly higher than those typically reported in the literature for similar manganese ferrites. This difference can be attributed to the presence of the additional iron oxide (Fe2O3) shell, which contributes to the overall magnetic moment. For naked MnFe2O4 nanoparticles with a diameter of approximately 18 nm, Yang L. et al. measured a saturation magnetization of 21.1 emu/g at 300 K [27]. Islam et al. demonstrated that naked MnFe2O4 ferrites of 6 nm exhibit a magnetization saturation of 12 emu/g at 300 K [28]. Aghdam et al. showed that for MnFe2O4 ferrites of approximately 9 nm coated with a mixture of OA and oleylamine the magnetization saturation found was 19 emu/g at 300 K [26]. Considering that magnetization saturation depends on the type of nanoparticle, size, shape, coating, and measurement temperature, we did not find values in the literature for MNPs (∼6 nm) coated with soy lecithin. Magnetic coercivity (Hc) is the intensity of the magnetic field required to reduce the magnetization of the material to zero after saturation. A very low value suggests that the nanoparticles are in the superparamagnetic regime, where there is no significant hysteresis, and the magnetic moments can quickly realign and with the external field with small interaction between the particles. Remanent magnetization (Mr) indicates the magnetization retained after the external field is removed. Values close to zero are also observed.

Chemical characterization

Fig. 3A shows the infrared spectrum for MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA. The spectrum presents characteristic peaks of OA, confirming the successful coating. Table S3 Fig. 3 (Supplementary Material) lists the corresponding absorption peaks and their assignments. The peak at 3006 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the cis C—H stretch of the alkene group (=CH), while the bands at 2922 cm⁻¹ and 2854 cm⁻¹ arise from symmetric and asymmetric CH2 stretching vibrations, respectively. The absence of the 1710 cm⁻¹ peak (C = O of free OA) in MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA suggests covalent bonding between OA and the nanoparticle surface. New peaks at 1535 cm⁻¹ (C = C stretch) and 1407–1409 cm⁻¹ (CH3 deformation) indicate interactions between OA and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3. Additionally, the 1094 cm⁻¹ and 1049 cm⁻¹ bands correspond to C—O-C stretching, further confirming OA chemisorption. The spectrum of naked MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 lacks organic peaks, as expected, while MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA retains most OA signatures with slight shifts due to surface binding. These results verify the successful functionalization of MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 nanoparticles with oleic acid.

Furthermore, Table S3 shows key absorption bands in lecithin appearing at 3010 cm⁻¹ (CH₂ stretching), 2957 cm⁻¹ (asymmetric CH3 stretching), and 2923 cm⁻¹ (antisymmetric CH₂), while symmetric CH₂ stretching is observed at 2852 cm⁻¹. The carbonyl (C = O) stretching vibration at 1735 cm⁻¹ in lecithin shifts slightly to 1730 cm⁻¹ in MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec, indicating interaction with the nanoparticle surface. The broad band at 1627 cm⁻¹ (shifted from 1650 cm⁻¹) suggests hydrogen bonding or OH stretching perturbations due to coating. Notably, the phosphate-related vibrations asymmetric PO2⁻ (∼1236 cm⁻¹) and the P–O–C stretching region (1200–970 cm⁻¹) are prominent in lecithin and appear modified in MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec, with the phosphate I band shifting from 1070 cm⁻¹ to 1053 cm⁻¹, confirming chemisorption. The absence of some pure lecithin peaks (e.g., 3010 cm⁻¹, 2957 cm⁻¹) in MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec suggests structural rearrangement upon binding, while retained bands (e.g., 2925 cm⁻¹, 2854 cm⁻¹) verify the presence of lecithin hydrocarbon chains. These spectral changes collectively demonstrate the successful coating of MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 nanoparticles with soy lecithin, mediated by phosphate and carbonyl interactions. The formation of soy lecithin coating on the surface nanoparticles was confirmed through the comparison of soy lecithin and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3 spectra shown in Panel 3 (c). Absorption bands located at 2852, 2957, and 3010 cm−1 arise due to the symmetric CH2 stretching and the asymmetric CH3 and CH2 stretching, respectively. Carbonyl stretching vibration at 1735 cm−1. Antisymmetric CH2 at 2923 cm-1. Highly overlapped PO2− and P–O–C infrared active vibrations in the region between 1236 and 968 cm−1, with a maximal absorbance at 1063 cm−1. The peak at 1632 cm⁻¹ is commonly associated with the angular deformation mode of water (δ H—O-H). However, because these particles were peptized with nitric acid, this peak also has a significant contribution from the asymmetric deformation mode (ν4) of nitrate ions (NO3⁻) adsorbed on the surface to balance the positive charge. This NO3⁻ signature is not observed in the spectra of the coated MNPs, indicating the replacement of these ions by organic surfactants.

Nanotoxicity investigation

D. melanogaster (fruit fly) is a powerful model organism in toxicology due to its genetic tractability, short life cycle, and high conservation of key metabolic and neurological pathways with mammals. Phenotypic assays in D. melanogaster provide rapid, cost-effective, and reproducible insights into the sublethal effects of toxicants, making them invaluable for preliminary nanotoxicity screening before vertebrate testing [29].

The negative geotaxis climbing assay can describe the behavioral changes that occur in adult flies (parental and F1 generation) in response to gravity, since climbing is an innate behavior of D. melanogaster. The flies always try to climb vertically against gravity, so they showed negative geotactic behavior. This assay can reflect the neurodegeneration in the Drosophila model. Here, the climbing speed of both the parental and F1 generations did not show significant differences, ANOVA, p-value > 0.05. This indicates that the MNPs studied here at concentrations of 0.1 and 1.0 mg/mL are very slightly toxic or non-toxic, since they did not cause motor damage or anomalies in gravity behavior. The result of the climbing assay is plotted in a graph shown in Fig. 4A-B). Climbing ability was not significantly changed in the treated flies compared to the control flies of the setup, ANOVA, p-value > 0.05. Survival assay and the mean survival (50 days) are shown, respectively, in Fig. 4C-D) for parental and F1 generation, and did not significantly change in the treated flies compared to the control flies. The numbers of larvae, pupae, and hatched individuals were comparable with those observed for the control sample.

D. melanogaster larvae tend to avoid light exposure. To investigate light-depending behaviors and early larvae defects in light-sensing neurons, simple dark-light preference tests can be applied. Fig. 4E) shows dark/light preference (F1 generation). The assay tests the photophobic behavior of the larvae and compares the light or dark preference of a group of larvae moving freely in a Petri dish. This behavioral assay provides information about the sensitivity, integration, and temporal plasticity of the visual system. The D. melanogaster larval eye is the main organ for light perception. Each eye is composed of 12 photoreceptors, which cover the larval body wall and have been identified to respond to noxious light intensities [14,30]. The assay did not find significant changes in dark/light preference, ANOVA, p-value > 0.05, between treated flies and control flies.

The larval crawling assay is a well-established behavioral test used in D. melanogaster research which explores neuronal abnormalities in an early larva stage for neuronal mechanosensory investigation. Since larval movement is controlled by the nervous system, this assay provides valuable insights into neural and motor function. The healthy larvae move in a straight line, whereas abnormal larvae zigzag and sometimes slow down. When placed on a flat surface, larvae exhibit rhythmic peristaltic waves with brief pauses, a locomotion pattern governed by complex neural circuits. These circuits include motor neurons, sensory feedback pathways, and central pattern generator interneurons [31]. The crawling assay serves as an effective tool for assessing muscle contraction dynamics and overall locomotor behavior in Drosophila larvae [32,33]. The locomotor behavior of D. melanogaster larvae constitutes a sensitive and reliable parameter for detecting neurotoxic effects resulting from exposure to chemical agents or materials employed in toxicological studies. Deviations from these established patterns offer quantifiable indicators of neurological dysfunction. The larval crawling experiment is used as a method for evaluating neuronal activity. Experimental studies on crawling larvae show that increasing MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec concentrations, shown at Fig. 4F), did not affect their crawling speed, ANOVA, p-value > 0.05.

Wing venation pattern, eye coloring, thorax bristle count, and abdomen structure were all monitored to see if there were any phenotypic alterations due to the treatment with the nanocomposite (See Fig.S5, Supplementary Material). There were no remarkable differences or defects in first-generation D. melanogaster between treated and control groups.

To investigate the elimination pathways of MNPs in D. melanogaster, we carefully collected feces from ten animals per concentration, which were dissolved in distilled water and analyzed by TEM. Fig. S6 in Supplementary Material shows micrographs (TEM) and the respective energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) measurements. It was possible to confirm the presence of MnFe2O4 nanoparticles in three conditions: animals were exposed to A. MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, B. MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and C. MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec. The respective EDS spectra show the presence of Fe and Mn (the contribution of Cu comes from the grid used in the measurement). The presence of MNPs in the feces indicates an elimination pathway. Nanoparticles with approximately 6 nm diameter undergo efficient urinary excretion due to the pore size limit of glomerular filtration in the kidneys [34]. This is an indication of low or no accumulation of material in the organism. In parallel, we saw in the nanotoxicity assays that the animals treated with MNPs did not present differences when compared to the control animals (untreated). Table S4 with the quantification ( %) of the elements found in the EDS are presented in Supplementary Material.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to combine, successfully, this hybrid core-shell structure with lipid coatings and evaluate its safety in a whole organism model. We successfully synthesized, functionalized, and physicochemically characterized the MNPs. XRD data confirmed the presence of single-phase MnFe2O4 spinel type. We obtained small MNPs with average diameter of 5.8 nm (stock synthesis) analyzed by TEM and DLS. From the FTIR data we were able to confirm the functionalization of the MNPs with oleic acid or soy lecithin. While the saturation magnetization (Ms) values we obtained at 290 K were relatively high compared to previous reports, the system exhibited low remanent magnetization, a clear sign of its superparamagnetic nature. Ms values are compatible with the size of the MNPs and their coating, however it can be refined by increasing the F2O3 layer. An in vivo nanotoxicity study performed with D. melanogaster and phenotypic analyses demonstrated that the nanocomposite did not impair neural activity or developmental phenotypes at either the highest or lowest concentrations tested. MnFe2O4@Fe2O3, MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_OA, and MnFe2O4@Fe2O3_Lec are suitable for various biological applications, as they do not cause genotoxic or cytotoxic effects in D. melanogaster. The resulting material is proposed as a potential candidate for biomedical applications such as drug delivery (micelles, vesicles, and emulsions). Based on the nanotoxicological results in D. melanogaster, the nanocomposite is considered safe and non-toxic; however, further assessments in other organisms are required before its widespread application in biomedical systems.
