Colony-stimulating factor-1 as a potential therapeutic target in asthenozoospermia
Authors: Yongyong Wang, Qi Zhou, Cong Huang, Xiaodong Li, Haining Liu, Zhongyi Sun, Baoquan Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthenozoospermia (AZS), a common cause of male infertility, is characterized by significantly reduced sperm motility, though its precise etiology remains unclear. Recent research highlights seminal plasma proteins’ critical role in regulating sperm function. Colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1), an essential cytokine for immune regulation and cell proliferation, has been implicated in sperm functional regulation, spermatogenesis, and maturation.MethodsSeminal plasma samples from healthy men and AZS patients were collected using density-gradient centrifugation. Proteomics-based data-independent acquisition (DIA) identified four differentially expressed proteins: CD40, CSF-1, MCP-1, and IL-20. To provide an exploratory, proof-of-concept verification before large-scale validation. Subsequent validation in an independent small cohort confirmed a robust association between elevated seminal plasma CSF-1 levels and AZS. An in vitro sperm culture system assessed CSF-1’s function of sperm from AZS patients were treated with the CSF-1 inhibitor Pexidartinib, while recombinant CSF-1 was supplemented in sperm from healthy donors.ResultsPexidartinib treatment significantly increased sperm motility in AZS patients, whereas recombinant CSF-1 supplementation significantly reduced motility in healthy donor sperm. CSF-1 inhibition elevated intracellular ATP levels, suggesting disruption of mitochondrial energy metabolism as the mechanism for impaired motility. Proteomic profiling and functional assays demonstrated that seminal plasma CSF-1 induces mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby decreasing sperm motility.ConclusionSeminal-plasma CSF-1 is a potential pathogenic factor in AZS. Its overexpression suppresses sperm motility by impairing mitochondrial energy metabolism. CSF-1 represents both a diagnostic biomarker and a promising therapeutic target (e.g., via Pexidartinib) for the clinical management of AZS. These findings provide a foundation for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-025-01476-y.

Introduction

Asthenozoospermia (AZS) is a common cause of male infertility, accounting for approximately 40% to 50% of all cases reported worldwide the condition is defined by a marked reduction in progressive sperm motility. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen, AZS is diagnosed when the proportion of sperm exhibiting progressive motility (PR) is less than 32% or when the combined percentage of progressive and non-progressive motile sperm (PR + NP) is less than 40%. One of the primary challenges to natural fertility is the reduced motility of sperm, which is unable to penetrate the cervical mucous barrier or reach the oviduct to fertilize the oocyte [1]. The frequent co-occurrence of AZS with other semen abnormalities, such as oligozoospermia and teratozoospermia, further exacerbates subfertility [2, 3]. In addition to its direct impact on fecundity, AZS has far-reaching consequences for both individuals and families, as it adversely affects psychological well-being and quality of life. AZS is a disorder that is highly complex and heterogeneous, involving multiple etiological factors, including genetic predisposition, environmental stressors, endocrine disruptors, and lifestyle behaviors. The pathogenesis of this disorder is still unknown [4].

The relationship between sperm function and the composition of seminal plasma has been the focus of recent proteomics advancements [5–7]. Seminal plasma, which is primarily derived from the seminal vesicles (~ 65% of ejaculate volume) as well as the testes, epididymides, prostate, and vas deferens ampullae [8] Seminal plasma does not participate in spermatogenesis (a process confined to the testis), but provides the physical and metabolic environment necessary for post-ejaculatory sperm survival, motility maintenance, and fertilization competence in the female reproductive tract. It contains a variety of proteins that are closely associated with sperm function and provides the physical and metabolic environment necessary for sperm survival and motility. Additionally, it enhances the fertilization environment within the female reproductive tract [9]. Seminal-plasma proteins play an important part in reproduction [10]. Firstly, they protect and nourish sperm, providing energy and nutrients that are essential for motility and viability [11] and protecting sperm from hostile conditions such as acidity and immune attack [12]. Secondly, they influence sperm motility and navigation: proteins such as secreted phosphoprotein 1, fructose-bisphosphate aldolase, and malate dehydrogenase 1 modulate swimming patterns and velocity [13, 14], while calcium-binding proteins and fibronectin may act as chemotactic signals that guide sperm toward the oocyte [15, 16]. Most seminal plasma components are decapacitating factors, and their gradual removal during cervical transit is a prerequisite for sperm capacitation and fertilization ability. Notably, seminal plasma from patients with AZS demonstrates significant proteomic changes [17]. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1, interleukin-8, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, and tumor necrosis factor-α [18, 19], can induce oxidative stress, disrupt the seminal microenvironment [20–22], and significantly impair sperm quality. In contrast, the depletion of antioxidant enzymes and glutathione typically results in sperm being more susceptible to reactive oxygen species-mediated damage [23], which in turn promotes DNA fragmentation and flagellar dysfunction [24, 25]. Therefore, it is imperative for understanding the expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms of seminal-plasma proteins to comprehend the pathophysiology of AZS.

Colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1) is a homodimeric glycoprotein [26] that controls the growth and survival of cells by regulating multiple signaling pathways. By binding to its receptor CSF-1R, CSF-1 regulates the proliferation, differentiation, and function of macrophages [27–29]. CSF-1-dependent macrophages are indispensable for tissue development, homeostasis, and innate immunity [30]. Due to its central immunological function, CSF-1 has been extensively investigated in the context of immune responses and inflammation, and it plays a role in the development of numerous diseases [31–33]. Nevertheless, recent research indicates that CSF-1 serves functions within the reproductive system that were previously neglected. CSF-1 is expressed in the testis, epididymis, and other reproductive organs, suggesting that it may play a role in spermatogenesis and sperm maturation [34]. Preliminary research on a related family member, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF/CSF-2), indicates that CSF proteins can affect sperm motility by modulating energy metabolism, inflammatory pathways, and oxidative stress [35, 36]. The impact of CSF-1 on sperm motility has not yet been examined. It is essential to note that the concentration of CSF-1 in normal seminal plasma is approximately 1.7 ± 3.5 ng mg⁻¹ [37], which suggests that it may be a significant regulator of sperm movement. Therefore, the identification of novel therapeutic opportunities may be achieved by clarifying the function of CSF-1 in AZS.

To investigate whether CSF-1 regulates physiological processes that contribute sperm motility, we conducted in-vitro validation experiments and We first screened for differentially expressed proteins via DIA proteomics, then validated the expression of CSF-1 (one of the top candidates) in an independent cohort to confirm its association with AZS. Although the etiology of asthenozoospermia is complex and multifactorial, involving genetic, environmental, and immune-inflammatory factors, we focused on investigating the role of the seminal plasma protein CSF-1 in this process, aiming to provide a novel therapeutic target for the treatment of asthenozoospermia.

Sample collection and storage

Semen samples that were eligible for inclusion in subsequent experiments were collected in the Andrology Laboratory under standard conditions, flash-frozen, and stored at −80 °C. The laboratory was subjected to ultraviolet light the day prior to sampling in order to reduce microbial contamination.

Baseline semen analysis

Computer-aided semen analysis (CASA; Suiplus Semen Analyzer II, Beijing Suijia Software, China) and Gram staining were performed on fresh ejaculates as part of a routine clinical evaluation.

Sperm were isolated from seminal plasma using a density-gradient method established by the Department of Reproductive Medicine, Qingdao Municipal Hospital, following semen collection and baseline evaluation. All samples were equilibrated overnight in a pre-warmed incubator, and approximately 4 mL of each working solution was prepared for each patient. Two density layers, 80% and 40% (CooperSurgical, ART-2040、ART-2080), were freshly prepared. The 80% gradient medium was placed at the base of a 5 mL centrifuge tube, and an equal volume of the 40% medium was delicately layered on top. Initially, 1.5 mL of fluid was added. The prepared gradient was meticulously transferred onto the fresh semen samples using a Pasteur pipette after allowing them to liquefy at room temperature for 15–20 min. The tubes were centrifuged at 400 × g for 15 min. Immediately following centrifugation, the supernatant (seminal plasma plus gradient medium) was discarded, and the sperm particle was transferred to a new tube that contained 2 mL of sperm-washing buffer (CooperSurgical, ATR-1020). Residual contaminants were eliminated by conducting an additional spin at 300 × g for 10 min. The seminal-plasma fraction of the recovered supernatant was stored at −80 °C for subsequent proteomic analysis.

DIA quantification by nano-LC–MS/MS

DIA quantification

Sample preparation

Lyophilized peptide fractions were re-dissolved in mobile phase A (2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid), centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials.

Liquid-chromatography separation

Peptides were first enriched and desalted on a trap column and then separated on an analytical C18 column (Thermo Scientific) using a Thermo UltiMate UHPLC system at a flow rate of 300 nL min⁻¹. Mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile. The gradient was programmed as follows.

Mass-spectrometry acquisition

The eluent was introduced into a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer that was operating in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode and was coupled to a nano-electrospray ionization source.

DIA mass-spectrometry acquisition

The peptides that were separated by nano-HPLC were ionized using a nano-electrospray (nanoESI) source and analyzed on a Q-Exactive HF (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The discharge voltage was 1.6 kV. The maximal injection time was 50 ms, and full MS scans were acquired from m/z 400 to 1250 at a resolution of 120,000. For data-independent acquisition, the m/z spectrum was divided into 50 equal windows. The Orbitrap detected fragment ions at a resolution of 30,000 with incremental collision energies of 22.5, 25.0, and 27.5 eV. Fragmentation was accomplished through higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). The automatic gain control (AGC) was configured to operate at 1 × 10⁶.

DIA data processing

Raw files were processed in Spectronaut 5. Retention times were aligned with indexed retention-time (iRT) peptides, and mProphet scoring was used for precise ion matching. A target-decoy strategy controlled the false-discovery rate at 1%, yielding quantitative peptide lists. Proteins showing |fold change| > 1.5 and p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Statistical analysis

Principal-component analysis (PCA) was performed with FactoMineR v2.11 using default settings. For hierarchical clustering, Euclidean distance matrices were computed with the dist function, followed by average-linkage clustering with hclust.

In-vitro sperm culture and evaluation of the CSF-1 inhibitor

Motile sperm was first enriched by the optimized density-gradient procedure described above. Fertilization medium was equilibrated overnight at 37 °C in an incubator set to 6% CO₂. The final concentration of motile sperm was adjusted to 5 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹. For each assay, 0.5 mL of the sperm suspension was transferred to a 5 mL culture tube; tubes containing only the suspension served as untreated controls. All tubes were incubated under the same CO₂ conditions.

To test the effect of CSF-1 signaling blockade, cultured sperm were treated with Pexidartinib (a selective CSF-1 receptor [CSF-1R] inhibitor, MCE, HY-16749) at the indicated concentrations, (Pexidartinib exerts its effect by binding to CSF-1R and preventing CSF-1-mediated receptor activation, rather than directly neutralizing CSF-1) and progressive motility (PR) was measured after incubation. Identical procedures were applied to sperm from healthy donors and from AZS patients.

Statistical analysis

PR values were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.5 and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group differences were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for pairwise comparisons.

Measurement of ATP levels in sperm cells

Cell preparation

Motile sperm isolated by density-gradient centrifugation were transferred to a 10-cm dish and briefly exposed to trypsin to dissociate cell clumps, then collected into a 15-mL tube. Digestion was quenched with culture medium, and cells were pelleted (1 200 rpm, 3 min). After removing the supernatant, the pellet was aliquoted into 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes and washed again under the same conditions. Each tube then received 200 µL of ATP assay lysis buffer. Samples were pipetted repeatedly to ensure complete lysis and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected; 0.7 mL of lysis buffer was reserved for blank controls.

ATP assay

Intracellular ATP was quantified with a commercial kit (ATP Assay Kit, Beyotime, S20026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reagents were thawed on ice. An ATP standard curve (0.01–10 µM) was prepared by serially diluting the stock standard in lysis buffer. The working solution was freshly mixed at a 1: 9 ratio (reagent: dilution buffer). After pre-equilibrating the luminometer, 100 µL of working solution was dispensed into each well and allowed to stabilize for 4 min. Twenty microlitres of sample, standard, or blank was then added; plates were gently shaken, and luminescence was recorded within 2 s. All steps were completed within 10 min, and reagents were kept on ice until use.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.5. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-group comparisons used the Student’s t-test; multi-group comparisons employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate or more.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 51 participants were enrolled: 24 in the NORMO group (PR>32%) and 27 in the AZS group (PR<32%). Detailed demographic and clinical data are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Age and body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly between the two groups. Conversely, statistically significant group differences were observed in all semen parameters, including ejaculate volume, progressive motility, non-progressive motility, immotile sperm percentage, sperm concentration, and sperm morphology.

Proteomic differences between seminal plasma from healthy men and AZS patients

Twenty-four seminal-plasma samples (12 NORMO, 12 AZS) were profiled by Olink® proximity-extension assay at Novogene. Only 10 NORMO and 12 AZS samples remained after the exclusion of two samples that failed quality control. The protein abundance data were log-transformed and presented as normalized protein expression (NPX) values.

The data stability was confirmed, and no outliers were identified through orthogonal partial-least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and accompanying boxplots (Fig. 1A, B). In comparison to the NORMO group, the AZS seminal plasma exhibited substantially higher levels of CD40, MCP-1, and CSF-1, as well as a significantly lower level of IL-20 (Fig. 1C, D). Boxplots (Fig. 1F) illustrate the inter-group differences for each protein. The STRING network analysis (http://string-db.org) revealed robust interactions between CCL20, CCL13, and CD40, suggesting the presence of a coordinated inflammatory module in AZS (Fig. 1E). The highest diagnostic accuracy (highest AUC) was obtained by IL-20, as indicated by the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Using the same analytical workflow, we next profiled seminal plasma from an additional mini-cohort comprising two Normozoospermia men and two AZS patients. Given the relatively small cohort, this analysis was considered exploratory, aimed at assessing protein expression trends rather than definitive statistical inference. Quality-control assessment confirmed data stability and the absence of outliers; results are summarized in the boxplot (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Within this four-sample set, three proteins—IL-5, IL-18R1, and CSF-1—were up-regulated in AZS samples, whereas IL-1 α was down-regulated (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Boxplots of NPX values provide an intuitive view of these inter-group differences (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

CSF-1 inhibition improves sperm motility

Intersecting the two differential-protein datasets identified CSF-1 as the protein most strongly associated with AZS. To verify its functional relevance, we obtained a new set of ejaculates from 10 Normozoospermia men (NORMO group) and 10 AZS patients (AZS group). Motile sperm were adjusted to 5 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹ and incubated for 6 h with recombinant CSF-1 at 2, 10, 50, or 250 ng mL⁻¹. Progressive motility (PR) was then measured. In both groups, PR declined substantially as CSF-1 concentration increased, CSF-1 reduced progressive motility in both groups at all tested concentrations (2–250 ng/mL), but no clear dose dependence was observed. This may be due to saturation of CSF-1 receptors on spermatozoa even at the lowest concentration (2 ng/mL) (Fig. 2C, D). These findings demonstrate that elevated CSF-1 directly impairs sperm motility.

Next, to determine whether pharmacological blockade of CSF-1 can rescue motility, we cultured sperm from three AZS patients with the CSF-1 inhibitor (Pexidartinib, MCE, HY-16749) at 1 µM or 10 µM; untreated sperm served as controls. PR was recorded instantaneously (0 h) and after 24 h of incubation. Pexidartinib treatment markedly increased PR at 0 h, and the improvement persisted after 24 h (Fig. 2E).

Lastly, sperm tracks were observed under a microscope (20 × objective, 10 × eyepiece) in the control, 1 µM, and 10 µM groups after 24 h of culture. As Pexidartinib concentration increased, forward trajectories became progressively longer and more linear, indicating that progressive motility was enhanced in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2GI and Supplemental Video 1and Supplemental Video 2 and Supplemental Video 3).

CSF-1 blockade significantly increases sperm ATP levels

Sperm motility is highly dependent on mitochondrial synthesis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP); therefore, mitochondrial structure and function are essential for the efficient production of ATP [38]. In human sperm, mitochondria generate the energy that propels flagellar motion by forming a helical sheath around the mid-piece [39]. Prior research has demonstrated that asthenozoospermic sperm exhibit a significantly attenuated mitochondrial membrane potential, diminished ATP-synthase activity, and a lower overall ATP output [40]. Additionally, the ATP content is positively correlated with swimming speed [41].

To determine whether CSF-1 impairs motility by suppressing mitochondrial energetics, we assessed intracellular ATP following its pharmacological inhibition. Three AZS patients’ motile sperm were cultured with the CSF-1 inhibitor Pexidartinib at 1 µM or 10 µM for 24 h. Untreated cells served as controls. As illustrated in Fig. 2F, the 10 µM dose resulted in the most significant increase in ATP levels among both inhibitor groups. These findings suggest that the inhibition of CSF-1 restores mitochondrial ATP generation, establishing a mechanistic connection between elevated CSF-1, reduced ATP supply, and impaired sperm motility in AZS.

Discussion

In this study, we discovered that CSF-1 is significantly elevated in the seminal plasma of men with AZS and is inversely correlated with progressive motility using DIA-based proteomics. The functional assays conducted with in-vitro sperm-culture system confirmed that CSF-1 directly impairs motility and reduces ATP production, thereby emphasizing a mechanistic link between sperm dysfunction and CSF-1 over-expression.

CSF-1 is important for its ability to regulate macrophage growth and differentiation [42]. However, there is growing evidence that it also plays a significant role in the male reproductive tract [43], with significant expression in the testis and epididymis [44, 45]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report aberrantly elevated seminal-plasma CSF-1 in AZS patients, thereby supporting the hypothesis that CSF-1 is involved in the regulation of motility and spermatogenesis. The compromise of sperm function by CSF-1 could be attributed to a variety of non-mutually exclusive mechanisms: AZS is multifactorial, and CSF-1 may interact with other factors, while immune dysregulation (e.g., CD40 upregulation) may further disrupt the seminal microenvironment.Local genital-tract inflammation is a frequent occurrence in AZS, and CSF-1 is a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine [46]. Chronic inflammation ultimately undermines sperm viability and kinematics [47–49], elevates oxidative stress [50], and impairs the micro-environment of the testis and epididymis [51, 52]. In other words, the overexpression of CSF-1 may exacerbate pre-existing inflammatory insults, thereby exacerbating motility loss. The sperm membrane’s lipid peroxidation is the primary mechanism by which excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) causes reduced motility. CSF-1 has the potential to increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly in an inflammatory environment [53–56]. Thus, elevated CSF-1 may induce or exacerbate oxidative stress in AZS, thereby further reducing ATP output and flagellar motion. Collectively, these results establish CSF-1 as a critical mediator of impaired motility in AZS by means of inflammatory, oxidative, and metabolic pathways. Future studies should investigate CSF-1’s crosstalk with antioxidants and immune modulators to develop combinatorial therapies. Consequently, targeting CSF-1 may provide a potential therapeutic approach for the restoration of sperm function in patients who are affected.

In addition, the efficacy of flagellar motion necessitates an uninterrupted energy supply, and mitochondrial dysfunction is closely associated with the loss of motility. It is well-established that CSF-1, through its cognate receptor CSF-1R, activates numerous intracellular cascades, such as the PI3K/AKT [53, 57–59] and MAPK [60, 61] pathways. These signaling axes are essential for cellular energy metabolism. We have confirmed this mechanistic connection through our ATP assays: the inhibition of CSF-1 resulted in the restoration of mitochondrial ATP production and, consequently, progressive motility. It is also possible for CSF-1 to affect the local micro-environment of the testis and epididymis, which in turn affects the maturation of sperm. Within these organs, CSF-1 has the potential to interact with stromal cells, Sertoli, and Leydig [45, 62, 63]. The hypothesis that excessive CSF-1 expression could disrupt this cellular equilibrium, resulting in incomplete sperm maturation and diminished motility, is warranted by dedicated follow-up studies.

When considered in conjunction, our results indicate that CSF-1 is a prospective therapeutic target for AZS. Current clinical interventions, including lifestyle modification, hormonal manipulation, and antioxidant supplementation [64–67], provide only partial alleviation and fail to address the underlying molecular defect. Our data focus on the post-ejaculatory role of seminal plasma CSF-1 in impairing sperm motility. While CSF-1 is expressed in the testis, this study did not investigate testicular CSF-1 expression, and we cannot exclude potential pre-ejaculatory effects (e.g., on spermatogenesis). Future studies should compare testicular CSF-1 levels between AZS and normozoospermic individuals. Clinical benefits have been demonstrated in numerous malignancies and inflammatory disorders by small-molecule CSF-1/CSF-1R inhibitors (Pexidartinib) [68–70]. Pexidartinib showed a substantial improvement in progressive motility and ATP levels in AZS sperm, thereby demonstrating that pharmacological blockade of CSF-1 signaling could serve as the foundation for a targeted therapy for male-factor subfertility.

There are constraints associated with this study. Initially, the sample size was modest; to validate CSF-1 as a biomarker and therapeutic target, it is necessary to conduct larger, multi-center cohort study. Second, the precise downstream effectors that connect CSF-1 to mitochondrial dysfunction are yet to be determined; future research should investigate the PI3K/AKT- and MAPK-dependent events in germ cells. Furthermore, in order to determine the complete therapeutic potential of CSF-1 inhibition in reproductive medicine, it will be crucial to investigate its role in other male reproductive disorders, including azoospermia and teratozoospermia, in light of its extensive immunomodulatory functions.

Meanwhile, it should be noted that the small-scale validation (n = 2 per group) was conducted as an exploratory, proof-of-concept experiment, designed to confirm the feasibility and reproducibility of our findings before proceeding to a resource-intensive validation study. This approach allows for efficient prioritization of candidate targets while minimizing unnecessary ethical and logistical constraints. Future research will involve larger, adequately powered cohorts to substantiate these observations and further elucidate the mechanistic role of CSF-1 in AZS.

Conclusion

This is the first study to demonstrate that the seminal plasma of men with AZS contains a significantly elevated level of CSF-1. We provide preliminary evidence that CSF-1 is an essential regulator in the onset and progression of the disorder, primarily by disrupting cellular energy metabolism. These findings suggest that CSF-1 is an innovative target for therapy for the clinical management of AZS. In conclusion, our research offers new perspectives on the pathogenesis of diminished sperm motility and lays the foundation for the establishment of CSF-1-based therapies. Our study links CSF-1 to asthenozoospermia, offering a new perspective for understanding its multifactorial etiology. The CSF-1 may act synergistically with other known pathological factors, such as oxidative stress or local inflammation, contributing to reduced sperm motility. Future research should integrate these multiple factors to develop a more comprehensive model of asthenozoospermia pathogenesis. In addition, although this study highlights the potential role of CSF-1 in asthenozoospermia, its precise molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. The CSF-1 may exert its effects by activating downstream signaling pathways through its receptor CSF1R, such as the PI3K/AKT or MAPK pathways, thereby influencing sperm mitochondrial function and motility. Future studies should focus on validating these proposed pathways using approaches such as Western blotting or specific pathway inhibitors. Although Pexidartinib has shown potential in inhibiting CSF-1 in in vitro experiments, its use as a clinical therapeutic agent requires careful evaluation. The drug’s safety profile, optimal dosage, and long-term treatment outcomes remain unclear. Moreover, its potential effects on other sperm functions, such as fertilization capacity, warrant systematic investigation. Therefore, rigorous preclinical and clinical trials are essential to assess its potential risks and benefits before clinical application. In addition, the limited sample size may introduce potential data bias. In subsequent investigations, we plan to expand the sample pool and adopt more rigorous statistical methodologies to further validate the proteomic findings reported in this study.
