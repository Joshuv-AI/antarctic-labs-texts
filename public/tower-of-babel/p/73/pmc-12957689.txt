Beyond persistence: SERS-driven strategies for PFAS detection and monitoring
Authors: Dong Hwan Nam, Hayoung Kim, Hongyi Ban, Özce Durak, Jaejun Park, John D. Fortner, Seunghyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstarctThis review explores surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS)-driven strategies for the detection and monitoring of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a class of nearly 15,000 synthetic compounds known for their exceptional chemical stability and environmental persistence. Due to the high bond dissociation energy of carbon-fluorine bonds, PFAS accumulate globally in water and soil, posing severe risks to human health, including cancer, liver toxicity, and immune suppression. As regulatory bodies like the U.S. EPA establish stringent maximum contaminant levels as low as 4 ng/L (ppt level), there is an urgent demand for rapid, cost-effective, and portable sensing platforms to complement traditional, high-cost analytical techniques like LC-MS/MS. SERS is highlighted as a powerful analytical tool capable of providing ultra-sensitive "molecular fingerprinting" by amplifying Raman signals through localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) on metallic nanostructures. This paper categorizes the fundamental mechanisms of SERS-based PFAS detection into three primary interaction strategies: (1) host-guest inclusion using molecular cavities like β-cyclodextrin or MOFs, (2) covalent bonding between PFAS functional groups and substrate surfaces, and (3) physical adsorption driven by hydrophobic and electrostatic forces. Despite its potential, practical SERS deployment faces challenges such as weak PFAS-surface affinity and spectral interference from complex environmental matrices. To address these, the review discusses emerging advancements, including fluorophilic surface functionalization, deep learning-based spectral deconvolution, and the integration of microfluidic platforms for real-time monitoring. Ultimately, these SERS-driven innovations provide a critical pathway toward achieving on-site, high-throughput quantification of trace PFAS in diverse water resources.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Per-and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) comprise a class of nearly 15,000 synthetic organic compounds characterized by a hydrophobic fluorinated carbon backbone and a hydrophilic head group containing functional groups such as carboxylic and sulfonic acids [1]. Their amphiphilic nature, together with the extremely high bond dissociation energy of the carbon-fluorine bond (485 kJ/mol) [2], underpins the exceptional chemical stability of PFAS. Consequently, PFAS have been widely used in consumer goods and industrial processes since their commercial production began in the 1950s, with applications ranging from hydraulic fluid additives, aqueous film-forming foams, cosmetics, firefighting foams, and fluoropolymer coatings in architectural materials [3, 4]. However, the chemical, thermal, and biological resilience that makes PFAS so useful also underlies their persistence in the environment. PFAS have accumulated in air, soil, groundwater, aquatic organisms, crops, and surface waters, resulting in long-term ecological and human exposure [5–12].

Recent assessments underscore the global scale of PFAS contamination. In Europe, the “Forever Pollution Project (FPP)”, a cross-border collaborative project involving 29 journalists and seven scientific advisers, identified 22,934 known PFAS contamination sites, including 20 PFAS manufacturing facilities, and 21,426 “presumptive contamination sites” [13]. In the U. S., Salvatore et al. identified over 57,000 presumed PFAS-contaminated sites, spanning military bases, industrial facilities, airports, and wastewater treatment plants [14]. Similar trends of PFAS levels in water resources, exceeding the limits set by the Swedish National Food Agency, have been observed in several Asian countries, such as China, Japan, and South Korea [15, 16]. Widespread PFAS contamination poses a serious threat to global public health. In the U. S., epidemiological studies estimate 0.4–1 million people are exposed to 7.0 × 10− 8 g L− 1 combined PFAS in drinking water systems, while 18–80 million are exposed to levels 1.0 × 10− 8 g L− 1 above health-based thresholds [17]. Human exposure to PFAS has been linked to a range of adverse health outcomes, including cancer, liver toxicity, immune suppression, chronic kidney diseases, thyroid dysfunction, and developmental disorders [18]. PFAS have been detected in livestock and wildlife from different areas through contamination of water, soil, air, substrates, or food [19]. Such exposure can lead to immunotoxicity, neurotoxicity, genotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity, and endocrine disruption, particularly in organisms at higher trophic levels due to biomagnification [20, 21]. In response, regulatory frameworks are tightening. In 2024, the U. S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has set maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) as low as 4 ng/L for perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS), and 10 ng/L for perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), and hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (HFPO–DA) (commonly known as GenX), while also classifying PFOA and PFOS as hazardous substances under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability Act (CERCLA) [22, 23]. Similarly, in 2023, the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) published a “universal PFAS restriction proposal” submitted by four EU Member States (Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden) and Norway, which aims to ban 10,000 PFAS in the EU by 2026–2027 [13, 24] shown in Fig. 1.

Current technologies

With increasingly stringent regulatory thresholds, PFAS detection and monitoring technologies must deliver both high sensitivity and accuracy.

Currently, the most common methods for PFAS detection include liquid chromatography (LC) or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) or tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) [25–27]. However, these techniques are primarily suitable for aquatic metrics and involve high costs, complex workflows, and lengthy analysis time, which limits their feasibility for real-time applications [28]. Gas chromatography (GC) is less popular than LC because it only detects volatile, semi-volatile, and neutral PFAS, and its limits of detection (LOD) depend on the detector employed. Particle-induced gamma ray emission (PIGE), combustion ion chromatography, and fluorine-19 nuclear magnetic resonance (19F NMR) are commonly used for untargeted analyses of total organic fluorine (TOF) and total fluorine (TF, including both organic and inorganic fractions). The first two techniques only measure total fluorine and cannot specify individual PFAS compounds [29], while 19F NMR suffers from low sensitivity and requires relatively high PFAS concentrations for detection [30]. Therefore, there is a growing demand for portable, rapid, and cost-effective sensing platforms capable of detecting PFAS at parts-per-trillion levels in complex matrices.

PFAS molecules are neither optically nor electrochemically active and cannot be measured directly using low-cost spectroscopic methods like UV-Vis, fluorescence (FL), or electrochemical technologies. To overcome this limitation, advanced electrochemical, colorimetric, and optical spectrophotometric approaches have been recently developed (Table 1).

Colorimetric assays detect PFAS through the formation of ion pairs between anionic PFAS molecules and organic dyes (e.g., methylene blue) or nanoparticles (NPs, e.g., Fe3O4 or Au) [31, 32]. Pretreatment steps are required to minimize interferences from common nonfluorinated anionic surfactants, such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and sodium dodecyl benzenesulfonate (SDBS) [30]. The produced ion pairs are subsequently extracted, and the intensity of the vibrant color is proportional to the PFAS concentration [33]. Based on this principle, a portable commercial kit, AstkCARE™, has been developed and integrated with a smartphone app for the visual, preliminary identification of PFOA and PFOS [34]. FL- and luminescence-based detection methods offer higher sensitivity than colorimetric assays. These techniques use organic dyes that emit FL upon binding to specific functional groups in PFAS, such as sulfonates and carboxylic acids. However, their performance can be compromised by interference from heavy metal ions in aquatic sample matrices, and chromatographic methods are typically required for confirmation.

Electrochemical assays detect PFAS through charge transfer processes occurring at the electrode-solution interface [35]. Since PFAS are electrochemically inactive molecules, their detection typically relies on a secondary mediator that undergoes oxidation or reduction at the electrode surface. When PFAS interacts with the electrode, the mediator exhibits a proportional and measurable change in potentiometric or voltametric signals [36]. A typical mediator used to form molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) is o-phenylenediamine (o-PD), which can be electropolymerized onto a gold electrode [30, 37]. Another emerging electrochemical approach involves nanoporous metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which can act as ideal sensing probes due to their vast surface areas and suitability as electrode extensions. The affinity of PFOS toward MOF-modified electrodes has been demonstrated on a lab-scale microfluidic flow-through platform [38]. However, these electrochemical techniques generally suffer from limited selectivity, potential electrode fouling, and matrix interferences.

Depending on the detection method, diverse sensor materials supported by nanotechnology have been designed to generate measurable electrochemical, optical, or colorimetric signals under controlled experimental conditions. Among these sensing technologies, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) has emerged as a powerful analytical tool owing to its high sensitivity, selectivity, rapid detection capability, and non-destructive nature.

This review focuses on SERS as an emerging technology for PFAS detection. We first outline the fundamentals and methodologies of SERS, highlighting the key factors for optimizing Raman signal output. Then, we discussed the proposed mechanisms of SERS-based PFAS detection with particular attention to the physicochemical interactions between PFAS molecules and SERS substrates.

Theory

Fundamentals of SERS

Raman spectroscopy is a technique that analyzes the vibrational modes of molecules by detecting the inelastically scattered light when the molecules are irradiated with a monochromatic light source. It offers several advantages, such as short acquisition time, non-overlapping spectral features, and the ability to analyze multiple analytes simultaneously. However, conventional Raman spectroscopy exhibits an inherently weak scattering signal, limiting its applicability in detecting trace-level analytes [51–53].

To address this limitation, SERS employs plasmonic nanostructures that intensify the local electromagnetic (EM) field through localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) to amplify Raman signals [54, 55]. These enhanced fields considerably increase the Raman scattering cross-section of nearby molecules and enable ultrasensitive detection down to trace or even single-molecular levels. In addition to its exceptional sensitivity, SERS offers molecular fingerprinting, fast and non-destructive measurement, and compatibility with label-free detection, thereby offering wide applications in biosensing, environmental monitoring, and chemical analysis [56–60]. The enhanced local EM field in SERS primarily occurs in regions of nanogaps, sharp edges or tips, or between aggregated nanoparticles, also known as “hotspots.” EM enhancement and chemical enhancement are two main mechanisms that contribute synergistically to the SERS effect [61–65]. EM enhancement is the dominant mechanism, arising from the near-field amplification due to LSPR, and it can enhance the Raman signal by factors of 106–1011. Chemical enhancement is based on charge transfer between the analyte and the metal surface or changes in molecular polarizability due to metal-molecule interactions, typically contributing a more modest enhancement on the order of 101−103. Because the EM mechanism provides substantially larger enhancement factors, many SERS substrate designs focus on optimizing plasmonic features, such as interparticle gaps, sharp nanostructures, and surface roughness, to generate a higher density of EM hotspots.

Background on SERS applications in PFAS detection

Among the many PFAS, perfluoroalkyl substances, with a fully fluorinated carbon backbone, are more persistent in the environment and the human body and have more documented adverse health effects compared with partially fluorinated polyfluoroalkyl substances [66]. Common perfluoroalkyl substances can be classified into two major categories: perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCA) and perfluoroalkyl sulfonic acids (PFSA). Structurally, both PFCA and PFSA consist of a fully fluorinated carbon chain attached to a polar, negatively charged, anionic head group. PFCA contains a carboxylate group (–COO⁻), while PFSA contains a sulfonate group (–SO3⁻).

The strong C–F bonds in PFAS give rise to distinct vibrational signals in Raman spectra. Particularly, symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of CF2 and CF3 groups generate prominent peaks in the 700–1300 cm⁻¹ region. The exact positions of these peaks may vary slightly depending on the molecular environment and the location of the head group. These characteristic vibrational modes can be used to detect and differentiate PFAS species, provided that sufficient molecules are adsorbed near the plasmonic hotspot of the substrate [67].

PFAS molecules can be distinguished by analyzing their vibrational modes and characteristic Raman peaks. As shown in Fig. 2, all PFAS compounds exhibit common Raman peaks at 600, 715, and 1370 cm− 1, while all except PFNA also share a peak at 1296 cm− 1. Although overlapping peaks are observed within the 520–1325 cm− 1 range, each compound possesses a clearly distinguishable fingerprint region, with characteristic peaks at 1043 and 1136 cm− 1 for PFOS, 630, 675, and 763 cm− 1 for PFOA, 754 and 837 cm− 1 for PFNA, and 427, 918, 978, and 1422 cm− 1 for PFDA [67].

The peaks at 660, 715, 803, and 1100 cm−1 are likely attributed to CF3 vibrations, while the 1370 cm−1 band corresponds to C-F or COO− vibrations. Notably, PFOS uniquely contains a sulfonic acid group (SO3H), resulting in characteristic peaks at 1043 and 1136 cm−1 [68].

PFOA, PFNA, and PFDA share a similar structure containing a carboxyl group (COOH), differing only in the number of carbon and fluorine atoms. Increasing the carbon chain length has little effect on the vibrational modes of CF2 and CF3 but separates the C-C vibration mode.

Challenges in SERS-Based PFAS detection for environmental samples

SERS offers short acquisition times, multiplexing capabilities, and ultrahigh sensitivity for PFAS detection. Nevertheless, its practical deployment remains challenging due to inferior reproducibility, signal stability, analyte-capture efficiency, and environmental durability. SERS offers short acquisition times, multiplexing capabilities, and ultrahigh sensitivity for PFAS detection. Nevertheless, its practical deployment remains challenging due to inferior reproducibility, signal stability, analyte-capture efficiency, and environmental durability. In addition, the robustness of SERS-based PFAS sensing remains a critical concern, because complex environmental matrices can introduce substantial background noise and interferents (e.g., salts, natural organic matter, and co-contaminants) that degrade signal fidelity and exacerbate reproducibility limitations across substrates, batches, and measurement conditions [70].

A fundamental limitation arises from the inherently weak interaction between PFAS molecules and conventional plasmonic surfaces such as gold or silver. PFAS compounds are chemically inert and dominated by strong C-F bonds and hydrophobic perfluoroalkyl chains, resulting in minimal chemisorption or physisorption onto metal surfaces. Consequently, analyte coverage within localized EM hotspot regions is low, leading to weak signal intensity and inconsistent enhancement factors.

Since PFAS compounds are present at extremely low concentrations in water and exhibit weak affinity toward metal surfaces, drying or solid-phase extraction (SPE) is often required to preconcentrate analytes near hotspots. While these methods enhance sensitivity, they introduce variability due to nonuniform analyte distribution and “coffee-ring” effects during evaporation. Moreover, co-concentration of salts and organic matter produces background interference that can obscure the target signal [71]. These labor-intensive steps are incompatible with continuous or in situ sensing, limiting the practicality of SERS for PFAS monitoring.

Environmental water samples often contain strongly FL species, such as surfactants and dyes, that can mask weak Raman features. In addition, certain metal nanostructures exhibit intrinsic photoluminescence, further complicating spectral interpretation. These background emissions can easily obscure the already weak signals of PFAS, making their bands difficult to resolve. To address this, various mitigation strategies have been developed, such as applying FL-quenching surface coatings, using longer-wavelength excitation to suppress FL, and engineering SERS substrates to minimize or eliminate intrinsic photoluminescence.

Selectivity in complex environmental matrices also remains a critical limitation for SERS-based PFAS detection. Environmental and industrial samples typically contain diverse PFAS analogues along with natural organic matter and surfactants that generate overlapping Raman features. Conventional one-dimensional Raman spectra alone are insufficient to discriminate among PFAS species in such mixtures.

Reproducibility is another major obstacle for quantitative SERS measurements. Signal enhancement in SERS arises from localized hotspot-nanoscale junctions or interparticle gaps where EM fields are maximized. In conventional colloidal or roughened-film substrates, the random distribution of these hotspots causes large signal fluctuations, often exceeding 30%−50% even in replicate measurements [67]. This variability complicates calibration and undermines the quantitative reliability of SERS at trace concentrations.

Finally, moving from laboratory-scale demonstrations to practical, deployable SERS systems is hindered by several issues related to scalability, cost, and lack of standardization. Many high-performance substrates rely on lithographic fabrication or colloidal synthesis that produce variable results across batches. Moreover, inconsistencies in measurement protocols, such as laser power, acquisition time, and optical alignment, impede data comparison among studies.

In summary, despite notable progress, SERS-based PFAS sensing continues to face several interconnected challenges, including weak PFAS-surface affinity, inevitable FL and matrix interference, nonuniform hotspot distribution, complex sample preparation requirements, limited selectivity, insufficient substrate durability, and a lack of standardization. Overcoming these limitations requires interdisciplinary approaches that can integrate surface chemistry, nanofabrication, and data science to design robust, selective, and reproducible SERS systems suitable for real-world environmental monitoring.

SERS for PFAS detection

Extensive research has been conducted to enhance the sensitivity of SERS substrates by generating abundant plasmonic hotspots, optimizing nanoparticle assemblies, and engineering ordered arrays. Notably, PFAS detection using SERS has been reported with detection limits in the range of 10− 9–10− 11M. These performances leverage the inherent advantages of SERS-high sensitivity and rapid acquisition-and have significantly advanced SERS-based biosensors and chemical detectors. These developments have significantly advanced SERS-based biosensors and chemical detectors. Considering the extreme chemical stability and hydrophobicity of PFAS, SERS systems for PFAS detection must be deliberately engineered to promote selective and effective interactions between PFAS molecules and the sensing substrate. In SERS-based PFAS detection, adsorption or binding of PFAS molecules to the substrate surface is a prerequisite for Raman signal enhancement and determines the detection performance. Previous studies have classified the PFAS-substrate interactions into three types: (a) host-guest inclusion, where PFAS are trapped within molecular cavities such as β-cyclodextrin or MOF pores; (b) covalent bonding, involving the formation of chemical or hydrogen bonds between PFAS functional groups (e.g., –COOH, –NH₂, –OH) and the substrate; and (c) physical adsorption, where PFAS physiosorbed onto plasmonic surfaces (Au and Ag) through hydrophobic or electrostatic interactions.

The selective interactions between PFAS and the SERS substrate directly govern the detection selectivity and sensitivity, ultimately determining the analytical reliability of PFAS-targeted SERS systems. In the following subsections, representative examples are discussed based on these three fundamental PFAS-substrate interaction mechanisms.

Among various strategies to enhance the selectivity of SERS-based PFAS sensors, the host-guest inclusion approach has garnered significant attention. It utilizes molecular hosts such as β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) and MOFs to capture PFAS molecules within confined hydrophobic microenvironments. These hosts can preconcentrate perfluorinated chains via hydrophobic and fluorine-fluorine (F–F) interactions. Accordingly, a growing number of studies have demonstrated PFAS adsorption and detection in realistic environmental matrices using these host–guest systems [74, 75]. For example, Francesca et al. reported a host–guest-based sensing strategy that exploits the interaction between graphene oxide (GO) and PFAS for detection [76]. In addition, wei et al. investigated PFAS capture and detection from chrome-plating wastewater, reporting rapid removal within 2 min (98% for F53B, 65% for PFOS, and 55% for PFHxS), and demonstrated substrate reusability via methanol washing [77]. Collectively, these studies illustrate the practical utility of host–guest inclusion strategies by bringing PFAS molecules closer to plasmonic hotspots and thereby amplifying Raman signals.

For example, as shown as Fig. 4, a β-CD/Ag nanocomplex-decorated MOF achieved a remarkably low LOD of 1.7 × 10− 10 M for multiple PFAS species (PFOA, PFOS, PFBA, PFHxA, and PFNA) over a wide detection range from 10− 7 to 10− 10 M [72]. Likewise, a core-shell Au@MIL-4 F nanocomposite featuring a gold core and a fluorinated MOF shell achieved a LOD of 3.8 × 10− 11 M and successfully detected PFOA, PFOS, PFBA, PFDA, PFBS, and PFDS within 10− 3–10− 10 M (shown as Fig. 5) [73]. Such inclusion-based architecture combines molecular recognition and plasmonic enhancement, offering superior selectivity and sensitivity for PFAS detection. However, further improvements in diffusion kinetics and desorption resistance are needed for stable real-sample analysis shown in Fig 6.

Covalent bonding-based systems achieve specificity through the formation of chemical or hydrogen bonds between PFAS functional groups and the SERS substrate. Typical interactions involve the carboxyl (–COOH) or sulfonate (–SO₃⁻) groups of PFAS and amine (–NH₂), hydroxyl (–OH), or thiol (–SH) groups on the substrate surface. Importantly, the selectivity of these approaches is fundamentally supported by chemical and hydrogen-bonding interactions between PFAS functional groups and surface functionalities, which stabilize the analyte’s adsorption geometry (orientation) and thereby enable selective binding. These chemical interactions stabilize the analyte orientation and enable selective binding, which is particularly useful for detection in complex environmental matrices.

A notable example is the self-assembled p-phenylenediamine nanoparticle (SAp-PD) sensor combined with a silver nanograss (SNG) substrate [78]. The amine groups on SAp-PD chemically interacted with the carboxylic ends of PFOA, inducing a π-conjugation distortion observable via SERS signal attenuation. The system achieved an LOD of 1.28 pM (in deionized water), 1.6 nM (in tap water), and 1.69 nM (in frying pan extract), with a working range of 1 mM–1 pM. Notably, the applicability to real samples is demonstrated in Fig. 7, providing practical evidence that this chemistry-guided strategy remains effective beyond idealized laboratory matrices. Furthermore, to mitigate the risk of false positives, the study did not rely solely on SERS readouts; instead, cross-validation using LC–MS was performed, and the agreement between the two methods is presented in the Fig. 7e, f data. This approach demonstrates that rational chemical functionalization can translate molecular reactivity into quantifiable spectral responses. Covalent bonding strategies offer superior stability and molecular specificity but require precise control of surface functional density to avoid steric hindrance or signal attenuation.

Physical adsorption is the most fundamental and commonly employed mechanism in SERS-based PFAS detection. In this process, PFAS molecules are physiosorbed onto plasmonic surfaces (Au or Ag) via hydrophobic interactions of the CF2 chains and electrostatic forces between charged PFAS head groups and the metal surface. Despite its nonspecific nature, this adsorption places PFAS within the intense EM regions near hotspots, yielding strong SERS signals without involving any chemical interactions.

As shown in Fig. 8, AgNPs and Au@Ag nanorods arranged in a sandwich-like array achieved an LOD of 1.0 × 10− 4 g L− 1 and a detection range from 10− 4 g L− 1 to 10− 2 g L− 1 (PFOA) [79]. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 9, an aerosol jet-printed Au NP array demonstrated an LOD of 1.2 × 10− 9 M for PFOA and 9.6 × 10− 10 M for PFOS, covering a broad detection range from 10− 4 to 10− 9 M [68]. Another example, as shown in Fig. 10, it is the hollow Ag nanocluster substrate, which achieved an LOD of 1.1 × 10− 5 g L− 1 and a linear range up to 400 g L− 1 [80]. Such adsorption-driven systems exhibit excellent signal amplification and simplicity, although their selectivity is inherently limited. However, adsorption-based SERS approaches can exhibit substantial signal variability depending on substrate architecture, because hotspots are often formed heterogeneously and the measured intensity becomes highly sensitive to the analyte–hotspot distance. To mitigate this limitation, it is essential to develop substrates with more uniform nanostructures that yield consistent hotspot distributions and thereby improve signal reproducibility. Future efforts should focus on hybrid designs that combine plasmonic geometry optimization with controlled surface chemistry to achieve both high sensitivity and specific PFAS recognition.

Challenges and perspectives

SERS has gained significant attention owing to its exceptional sensitivity, molecular specificity, and rapid analytical capability, even in complex environmental matrices. However, developing SERS-based PFAS sensors and implementing them in real-world settings remains highly challenging.

Limitations and current mitigation strategies

PFAS are chemically inert and therefore exhibit only weak chemisorption or physisorption on conventional (plasmonic) analytical surfaces, leading to low SERS signals and inconsistent enhancement factors in aqueous media. To mitigate these limitations, numerous studies have functionalized SERS substrates with PFAS-capturing species, such as cationic ligands, ion-pairing polymers, or molecularly imprinted layers, to increase interfacial affinity and preconcentrate analytes within plasmonic hotspots, thereby improving detection performance.

Indirect SERS detection via ion pairing with cationic dyes has also been explored. In this approach, anionic PFAS (e.g., –COO− and –SO3−termini) form sparingly soluble complexes with Raman-active cations such as methylene blue, ethyl violet, or crystal violet. The resulting pairs accumulate on SERS-active surfaces and yield intense reporter signals that serve as proxies for PFAS concentration. For example, Zoi G. Lada et al. achieved an LOD of 5 × 10− 9g L− 1[81] (Fig. 11) by combining chemical preconcentration through ionic pairing with EM amplification. However, adsorption mediated by charged reporter dyes or surfactants only provides semi-quantitative information at intermediate concentrations, as the Raman signals originate primarily from the reporter molecules rather than the PFAS themselves, thereby limiting analyte-specific accuracy.

To improve analyte specificity, interfacial chemistry has been engineered to enable direct capture of PFAS within plasmonic hotspots. For instance, Xu et al. employed silica cartridges functionalized with perfluoroalkyl phases (e.g., –(CH2)2–C8F17), which selectively partitioned and retained PFAS from PFHxA to PFDA via F–F interactions, achieving recoveries greater than 95.3% [82]. (Fig. 12) Such substrates exhibit higher capture efficiencies and improved measurement reproducibility. Nevertheless, the development of multifunctional interfaces that selectively concentrate, orient, and confine PFAS within the near field remains a central research objective.

Quantitative SERS measurements of PFAS often suffer from poor reproducibility because commonly used colloidal and roughened-film substrates often generate fluctuating signals. This variability arises from the stochastic distribution of hotspots, where EM enhancement is confined to nanoscale gaps (“hotspots”) in plasmonic structures, leading to spatially inconsistent signal amplification. As a result, nonuniform intensities are often observed even for repeated measurements on the same substrate, undermining calibration at fixed analyte concentrations and complicating robust quantitation. Accordingly, substrates for SERS-based PFAS detection need to have spatially uniform hotspot arrays. Lithographic or template-assisted fabrication can achieve precisely defined nanostructures that generate reproducible and consistent signals; however, their high cost and limited throughput hinder broad deployment. To address these constraints, low-cost fabrication strategies capable of producing substrates with uniform and reproducible SERS enhancement are urgently needed.

Perspectives and future

Despite substantial progress toward qualitative and quantitative SERS, two major challenges still hinder the development of PFAS sensors: (i) fundamental detection chemistry and (ii) system-level integration. Accordingly, future work should focus on addressing these critical aspects to enable reliable, scalable, and field-deployable SERS-based PFAS sensing technologies.

Given the ultralow levels of PFAS in natural waters, highly sensitive capture strategies are essential to enable accurate detection. Indirect SERS approaches based on ionic pairing with cationic dyes can detect PFAS at intermediate concentrations; however, achieving quantitative accuracy remains challenging because the Raman readout originates primarily from the molecular reporter rather than the analyte. These constraints motivate the development of PFAS-selective adsorbents that directly preconcentrate the target within the plasmonic near field. One promising direction leverages fluorophilicity in conjunction with hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions. For example, polymer gels containing perfluoropolyether (PFPE) segments and quaternary-ammonium sites are regenerable sorbents that rapidly isolate a broad range of PFAS (Fig. 13) [83]. Through selective hydrophobic/fluorophilic partitioning and electrostatic attraction at ion-exchange sites, these gels effectively capture PFAS, achieving > 85% uptake within 30 s at 1.0 × 10− 4 g L− 1 and > 99% uptake within 120 min. In parallel, several studies have enhanced electrostatic attraction toward anionic PFAS, aiming to increase their local concentration near metal surfaces, stabilize orientation, and improve SERS response. For instance, Karolina et al. fabricated a complementary electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)-SERS platform by depositing gold onto a porous scaffold. In the conductive, plasmonic substrate, gold was functionalized with –C6H4–NH2 groups to promote affinity for the –COO− termini of PFOA and GenX (Fig. 14) [84]. Using this approach, SERS exhibited close-to-linear response over the 10− 6–10− 10 M range and achieved LODs down to 10− 10 M. Raman mapping over 1 mm × 1 mm indicated consistent signal intensity, indicating uniform surface capture. Such adsorption-based strategies overcome the low reactivity and weak surface affinity of PFAS and significantly enhance the SERS signals, supporting quantitative analysis.

Because SERS signals are amplified at plasmonic hotspots, ensuring a uniform hotspot distribution is critical for reliable measurements. As noted above, Quantitative Raman analysis requires repeated measurements with minimal variation, which in turn depends on the uniform distribution of EM hotspots in terms of density and geometry across the sensing area. Although lithographic and template-assisted nanofabrication can achieve such uniform structures, their high cost and limited throughput hinder large-scale adoption. To mitigate these constraints, low-cost routes have gained increasing attention, including NP self-assembly on prepatterned supports and conformal metal coatings on nanostructured scaffolds [85]. For example, Lee et al. produced large-area SERS substrates by combining anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) with gold NPs. After preparing an AAO substrate with spatially uniform porosity, a gold film was deposited for SERS, and gold NPs were then inserted into the pores, yielding large-area uniformity and strong SERS signals (Fig. 15) [86]. The enhancement factor reached ~ 107, and the LOD for benzenethiol was ~ 10− 9 M. In another study, Yeo et al. used dielectrophoresis (DEP) to arrange plasmonic particles along the perimeters of circular hole patterns, producing enhanced SERS signals with LODs down to 10⁻10 M and demonstrating scalability to large areas (Fig. 16) [87]. In addition to large-area fabrication, suppressing FL interference is also critical when analyzing environmental samples. Raman signals are often obscured by intrinsically fluorescent substances in aqueous or organic-rich matrices, which typically generate much stronger and longer-lived background signals. Several mitigation strategies have been reported to address this issue. For example, longer-wavelength excitation (e.g., 785–1064 nm) can reduce the fluorescent background (Fig. 17) [88]. Integrating FL-quenching interlayers, such as graphene oxide or hexagonal boron nitride, into SERS substrates can suppress background emission and improve signal-to-noise ratios (Fig. 18) [85].

The intrinsic spectral similarity across PFAS homologues, combined with the presence of natural organic matter, salts, and surfactants in environmental waters, poses significant challenges in their separation and analysis. In recent years, deep learning has been applied for the rapid extraction of subtle features from complex data. By leveraging curated spectral libraries along with chemometrics and machine learning, SERS-based PFAS detection can achieve spectral deconvolution, compound-level discrimination, concentration prediction, and interference compensation. In practice, Juhong et al. proposed a demultiplexing deep-learning model for PFOS SERS, in which Raman peak intensities were first extracted via algorithmic analysis, followed by machine learning to evaluate signal-to-noise ratios and reconstruct PFOS spectra [89]. Average cross-correlation and mean absolute error (MAE) were used to compare demultiplexed/denoised outputs with ground truths, yielding a high average cross-correlation of 0.9622 ± 0.0667 and a low average MAE of 0.0034 ± 0.0024. In complementary work, Chen et al. used DFT to compute Raman spectra for 40 major PFAS, assigning peaks and vibrational modes. After adding noise to the calculated spectra, a PFAS database was constructed, and PCA and t-SNE were then employed to differentiate structures and isomers [69]. These approaches are expected to support qualitative discrimination among structurally similar PFAS and enable quantitative analysis through signal comparison.

Finally, integrating microfluidics with SERS has been proposed to improve PFAS measurements in environmental samples. By driving dilute solutions through confined channels that traverse designed plasmonic hotspots, microfluidic flow increases encounter probability and hotspot occupancy without relying on drying or drop-casting processes [52]. This approach avoids coffee-ring deposition and spatially heterogeneous film formation, improving signal uniformity and quantitative precision. Microfluidic platforms also offer control over residence time and shear, facilitate the integration of selective interfacial chemistries (e.g., cationic/fluorophilic porous coatings) for on-surface preconcentration, and allow simple washing/regeneration to mitigate fouling. Combined with the intrinsically short SERS acquisition times, this flow-based operation supports online monitoring while maintaining compatibility with complex aqueous matrices shown in Fig. 19.

Limitations of SERS-based PFAS detection and strategies to overcome them

PFAS are among the most tightly regulated contaminants. Despite the availability of multiple analytical approaches, robust quantification of PFAS at ultratrace levels in environmental waters remains difficult. SERS-based PFAS detection offers short integration times, molecular specificity, and multiplexing potential. However, its translation to real-world applications is hindered by weak PFAS–Au/Ag interfacial affinity, low Raman cross-sections for perfluoroalkyl backbones relative to resonant or aromatic reporters, matrix-derived FL/photoluminescence, stochastic hotspot statistics that undermine reproducibility, and variability introduced by drying or SPE–based preconcentration. Recent advances, including fluorophilic/cationic or porous interfacial coatings to promote capture, low-photoluminescence or FL-quenching interlayers, more uniform and manufacturable substrates, and quantitative workflows (ratio-to-reference normalization, calibration transfer, and machine-learning models trained on curated spectral libraries), have effectively mitigated these barriers. With standardized reporting of optical parameters and matrix composition, together with external validation against LC–MS/MS, SERS gradually transitions from qualitative detection to accurate quantification of trace PFAS in complex waters.

Recent studies increasingly integrate microfluidic chips with SERS, substantially advancing quantitative, high-throughput, and time-resolved measurements [90]. Consequently, these platforms hold great potential as a powerful tool for on-site, real-time PFAS monitoring.
