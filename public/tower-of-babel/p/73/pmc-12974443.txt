Wild vegetables contribute to improving the diversity and sustainability of vegetable diets in China
Authors: Li Huang, Linlin Huang, Yajie Jiang, Lijuan Tian, Jinlong Chen, Siwei Hu, Lihua Zhou, Cheng Jin, Suhui Ma, C. Y. Jim, Yongchuan Yang, Zhiming Zhang, Zhiyao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study provides an assessment of the current market supply of cultivated vegetables and explored the potential role of wild vegetables in enhancing dietary diversity in China. We found that the overall diversity of cultivated vegetables remains relatively low. Regions with higher income levels exhibited greater diversity of cultivated vegetable, but the availability of common cultivated vegetables showed little regional variation. Wild vegetables exhibit much greater species diversity with significant regional variation. They serve as an essential supplementary food source, particularly in areas with limited diversity of cultivated vegetables. Expanding the use of wild vegetables presents a valuable opportunity to improve the diversity and sustainability of vegetable diets in the future.

Vegetables are essential to the human diet, providing nourishment and key micronutrients that support overall health (1–4). A diverse vegetable intake is particularly important, as it contributes to a balanced diet and helps prevent health problems linked to micronutrient deficiencies (5, 6). Nevertheless, many populations worldwide—especially in low-income regions—continue to consume less than the amounts recommended by dietary guidelines (5, 7, 8). Improving both the diversity and accessibility of vegetable diets remains a pressing challenge for global agriculture and food systems.

Economic development and urbanization have significantly influenced how people eat (9, 10). Today, most people rely on markets to purchase cultivated vegetables. Advances in agriculture and transportation have improved supply chains, ensuring a relatively stable vegetable supply for both urban and rural areas (11). However, investment in vegetable research and development remains limited compared to staple crops such as cereals (12–14). Global vegetable production lags behind demand, and most countries are still not self-sufficient (15–18). Additionally, high prices limit access, especially for low-income communities that often prioritize cheaper, calorie-dense foods over vegetables, contributing to poor health outcomes (4, 19, 20). Therefore, expanding affordable access to vegetables remains an urgent priority for policymakers and a continuing global challenge.

Even in high-income countries, the supply of cultivated vegetables continues to face persistent challenges. Although approximately 1,500 vegetable species are recognized globally, a small cohort has persistently dominated mainstream consumption (21–24). Additionally, the supply chain habitually prioritizes high-yield, easily stored, and transportable varieties (25). Such practices have significantly reduced species diversity and may lead to homogenization of the vegetable supply across regions (26). Moreover, most rare cultivated vegetables that could enhance dietary diversity are often expensive and accessible only to affluent consumers in major cities, contributing to regional inequality in accessibility to vegetable diversity (27). Therefore, it is necessary to assess whether the current supply systems can adequately support the need for diverse vegetables and ensure equitable access to these resources across different regions.

Before the rise of modern agriculture, people primarily relied on gathering wild plants to meet their vegetable needs (28). Currently, the practices and customs of collecting and consuming wild edible plants remain common worldwide (29–33). For instance, ethnic minority communities in southwestern China continue to harvest and consume hundreds of wild plant species as vegetables (34–36). Globally, an estimated one billion people still depend—at least in part—on food gathered from their surrounding environments (37). In some low-income or indigenous communities, up to 75% of vegetable and fruit intake comes from wild harvesting rather than cultivation or purchase (38). Collecting wild vegetables is one of the low-cost yet effective ways to obtain essential nutrients. Studies have shown a strong relationship between the diversity of wild vegetables harvested from local landscapes and both micronutrient intake and overall dietary diversity (32, 39–41). Thus, even in a modern food system dominated by cultivated crops, wild vegetables continue to play an important role in human diets. However, the expansion of industrial agriculture, urbanization, habitat fragmentation, and overharvesting increasingly threaten both the consumption of wild vegetables and the transmission of traditional ecological knowledge (42, 43). Due to these constraints, the understanding of species diversity of wild vegetables, edibility knowledge, and foraging skills on a larger geographical scale are declining even in traditional communities. Thus, the potential of wild vegetables to enhance the diversity and equity of local vegetable diets will increasingly be curtailed.

China is the world’s largest producer and consumer of cultivated vegetables, with nearly 1 billion tons produced and a per capita consumption of 113.6 kg in 2023 (China Statistical Yearbook 2024). In addition, as one of the most biodiverse countries globally, China has a long history of using wild edible plants (44). The tradition of eating wild vegetables remains widespread in many regions today (36, 45–47). Thus, China presents a valuable opportunity to examine the diversity of both cultivated and wild vegetables, as well as the regional distribution of supply and equity in access to these resources. This study investigated the species composition and sales volume of cultivated vegetables across 2,983 supermarkets in 330 cities in China (Fig. 1). Additionally, we compiled a comprehensive database of 2,461 wild vegetable species based on hundreds of field studies and authoritative literature, documenting their edible parts, harvesting periods, and geographic distribution. We also collected micronutrient data for a range of common cultivated and wild vegetables from authoritative published resources. We aimed to assess the current state of urban vegetable supply and evaluate the potential of wild vegetables to enhance dietary diversity. Specifically, we addressed three key questions: 1) How many and which cultivated and wild vegetables are currently available through markets and wild collection? 2) How equitable is people’s access to the diversity of cultivated vegetables across regions? 3) What contribution can wild vegetables make toward improving the diversity and sustainability of vegetable diets?

Results

Composition, Sales Volume, and Production of Cultivated Vegetables in China.

We recorded 164 cultivated vegetable species across 53 families. Family and species representations were highly skewed. The dominant families, such as Brassicaceae, Fabaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Asteraceae, Solanaceae, and Amaryllidaceae, contributed 50.0% of the total species and 73.9% of the total sales (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We identified 52 common cultivated vegetables, accounting for more than 95% of total sales, considerably dominated by a small number of popular vegetables (Fig. 2A). Leafy vegetables were the most prevalent, representing 35.4% of species and 30.9% of sales, followed by fruit and stem vegetables. Notably, nonnative species comprised 50.6% of the total species and contributed 68.3% of total sales. The overall species richness remained relatively stable across seasons, ranging from 141 to 160 species. Seasonal species composition remained quite consistent, with a high mean interseason Bray–Curtis similarity index of 0.83 (Fig. 2B).

The 580 key vegetable-producing counties are distributed across China’s regions, with concentrations in the densely populated northern, central, southwestern, and southern regions (Fig. 2C). These counties each produce between 0.5 and 5.5 million tons of vegetables annually, jointly accounting for over 60% of the nation’s total vegetable output. Over the past 30 y, the Chinese government has launched four stages of the “Vegetable Basket Project” to strengthen national and regional vegetable supply. These programs have aimed at expanding production, facilitating cross-regional distribution, improving quality, and advancing cultivation technologies. In this period, annual vegetable production in China has increased steadily from 100 million tons to nearly 1 billion tons, with an average annual growth rate of approximately 10% (Fig. 2D).

Species Diversity and Nutrient Content of Wild Vegetables.

We documented 2,461 species of wild vegetables across 183 families. The three most common families, Asteraceae, Poaceae, and Fabaceae, represented 22.3% of the total species (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Among the large cohort, we identified 107 species as common wild vegetables, such as Portulaca oleracea, Taraxacum mongolicum, Capsella bursa-pastoris, and Pteridium aquilinum var. latiusculum (Fig. 3A). By source area, most wild vegetable species were gathered and consumed locally, available in the markets in only a few cities or recorded in a limited number of references. Although 130 wild vegetable species were sold in supermarkets nationwide, they accounted for approximately 0.05% of the total vegetable sales volume. Most wild vegetables were harvested directly by local people from the wild and not routed through commercial channels.

Herb was the predominant growth form among wild vegetables, followed by shrub, tree, and climber. The edible parts varied by growth form, with herbs primarily harvested for stems and leaves, while trees and shrubs were mainly consumed for young leaves, buds, and flowers (Fig. 3B). Wild vegetable diversity varied seasonally, with the highest species richness observed from April to July (Fig. 3C). Nutrient analysis showed that wild vegetables generally contained higher levels of key micronutrients than cultivated ones. On average, they had 3.25 times more carotene, 1.71 times more thiamin, 3.87 times more riboflavin, and 2.07 times more niacin (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

Species Diversity and Composition Similarity of Cultivated and Wild Vegetables.

The species richness of cultivated and wild vegetables displayed distinct geographical patterns across China (Fig. 4). The species diversity of cultivated vegetables varied significantly across cities, ranging from 40 to 116 species with an average of 73.6 species (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Generally, species richness was higher in the southern, eastern, and northern regions than in the northeastern and northwestern regions (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). However, the species richness of common cultivated vegetables showed less variation between cities, with an average of 49.3 species (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Over 85% of surveyed cities offered more than 90% of these common species (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Wild vegetables exhibited significantly greater species richness than cultivated ones in most cities. In total, 132 cities recorded over 500 species of wild vegetables per city (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Overall, their diversity was high in southwestern, central, and southern cities (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C).

The diversity of cultivated vegetables was primarily associated with socioeconomic factors, showing significant positive correlations with human footprint index (HFI) and per capita disposable income (PCDI) (Fig. 4 D and E and SI Appendix, Table S1). In contrast, the species richness of wild vegetables was mainly shaped by environmental conditions, available plant resources, and cultural diversity, showing significant positive correlations with elevation range (EleR), mean annual precipitation (MAP), natural forest coverage (NFC), and the abundance of intangible cultural heritage (SI Appendix, Table S1). Notably, it showed no significant relationship with income levels (Fig. 4F). The piecewise structural equation modeling (SEM) explained 93% and 96% of the regional variation in species diversity of cultivated and wild vegetables, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Overall, natural environments indirectly influenced the species diversity of wild vegetables by affecting the local available plant resources and human culture (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). This effect was not found for cultivated vegetables (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B).

The mean Jaccard index for common cultivated vegetables was significantly higher than cultivated and wild vegetables (Fig. 5A). Similarity in species composition of wild vegetables showed a clearer distance decay pattern (distance between a pair of cities) than cultivated and common cultivated vegetables (Fig. 5B). Nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination plots indicated geographical regions expressing distinct spatial differentiation for wild vegetables but not for cultivated vegetables (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Both socioeconomic factors and environmental factors had a stronger predictive power in explaining macrospatial variations in the species composition of cultivated vegetables (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). In contrast, natural environmental factors were more influential in explaining the macrospatial variations in the species composition of wild vegetables (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D).

Discussion

This study offered an assessment of the current market supply of cultivated vegetables and explored the potential role of wild vegetables in enhancing dietary diversity in China. Our analysis revealed that the species richness of cultivated vegetables remains relatively low despite three decades and four stages of the national Vegetable Basket Project, which has resulted in a 10-fold increase in production since the early 1990s. This pattern faithfully reflects the highly restricted bond between humans and plants, attaching tenaciously to a tiny domesticated species collection. The current vegetable markets remain persistently dominated by a small number of common species. Regions with higher income levels exhibited greater diversity of cultivated vegetables, primarily due to the presence of rare species with very low sales volumes that are not usually available in low-income regions. Fortunately, the availability of common cultivated vegetables showed little regional variation, suggesting an equitable and spatially homogeneous supply regime across large expanses of the country. The complex supply chain of cultivated vegetables has successfully delivered similar common species cohorts nationwide.

In comparison, we found that wild vegetables are represented by a significantly larger species pool marked by considerable interregional species variations. Their consumption, albeit in small quantities by individual species, denotes an important supplementary food resource, especially in areas with limited cultivated vegetable diversity. Additionally, the wide distribution and rich diversity of wild vegetables make them a valuable source of germplasm and essential micronutrients for humans. Thus, for local communities, wild vegetables offer important food security and nutrition, especially during periods of food shortage, and signify persistent adherence to traditional food preferences in the countryside. Overall, our findings provided a comprehensive understanding of the current status and limitations in cultivated vegetable supply, highlighting the untapped potential of wild vegetables to diversify and enrich human diets.

Regional Inequality and Equality in Cultivated Vegetable Supply.

Although China is the world’s leading producer and consumer of vegetables, our analysis showed that the species richness of cultivated vegetables remains below expectations despite the vast endowment of vegetable germplasm resources (48). This was largely due to a supply system prioritizing the development of new varieties within a limited number of existing species rather than introducing a diverse range of entirely new vegetable species from other countries (49, 50). In addition, we found that a limited number of common species dominate the markets, while rare vegetables with low sales volumes but high species diversity remain underutilized. This pattern reflected the enduring global preference in the supply systems for vegetable species that share common traits: high and stable yields, ease of storage and transport, and enjoy competitive pricing and assured market demands (14, 25). Furthermore, we observed a marked regional disparity in vegetable species diversity, with economically developed areas having significantly greater diversity than less developed regions. This difference was primarily driven by the availability of rare species, which are more likely to be supplied to high-income regions where market demand can sustain the premium price. These findings underscore the need to expand the range of rare vegetable species in the supply chain and to reduce regional inequalities in access to diverse vegetable resources.

Fortunately, the supply of common cultivated vegetables showed little difference across Chinese cities. Our analysis revealed a high level of species similarity among cities, with over 80% offering 90% of the same common vegetables. Jaccard-distance analysis further supported this finding, which showed that geographic distance had a relatively minor effect on the similarity of common vegetable species compared to all cultivated and wild vegetables. These results suggested that residents in both economically developed areas with favorable climates and less developed regions with harsher environments have comparable access to common vegetables. This consistency and equality in supply composition can be largely attributed to national efforts such as the Vegetable Basket Project, establishing key vegetable-producing counties, advances in greenhouse technologies, improved transportation infrastructure, and supportive government distribution policies (51, 52). Together, these country-wide measures have helped ensure equitable access to common cultivated vegetables across regions, regardless of local economic or environmental conditions. Accordingly, it has fulfilled the objective of cross-regional distribution of common vegetables to achieve a national-scale level playing field under the Vegetable Basket Project.

Due to the widespread availability of common vegetables across China, the current supply system has led to notable homogenization. Markets in different cities tend to offer highly similar vegetable selections, with limited options beyond a core group of common species. For example, 40% of cities—mostly in lower-income regions—offer fewer than 20 species of rare vegetables. While national policies and supply systems have succeeded in ensuring equitable access to mainstream vegetables, they have done relatively little to improve overall species diversity, particularly in economically underdeveloped and remote areas. This heavy reliance on a limited pool of vegetable species may pose several potential risks. For example, the production of common cultivated vegetables may fluctuate due to pests, diseases, extreme weather, or soil degradation, undermining the stability of supply, just like the main food crops (53–57). The increasing frequency of extreme weather events due to climate change may aggravate such impacts. In addition, this limited species assemblage restricts opportunities to improve dietary quality and enhance nutritional diversity, as well as food systems transitions (58). Finally, although common cultivated vegetable varieties have advantages in yield, the limited range of their key micronutrient content cannot be ignored (59). Therefore, increasing vegetable species diversity should be a central goal of future agricultural and food policy initiatives. The natural and cultural barriers to the wider cultivation of selected rare vegetable species can be resolved by research.

The Potential of Wild Vegetables in Enhancing Dietary Diversity.

Our findings highlight the considerable potential of wild vegetables to broaden vegetable diets. The species assemblage of wild vegetables is more than 15 times that of cultivated ones, providing an abundant yet largely untapped resource. This rich diversity is closely linked to China’s extensive botanical wealth, varied ethnic cultures, and long-standing tradition of consuming wild plants (36, 44, 60, 61). Many of these species are widely distributed and commonly found, offering a sustainable and accessible food source for local communities (36, 50, 62). Unlike cultivated vegetables, wild vegetables originated from a broader range of plant forms, including herbs, shrubs, and trees. Notably, the young shoots of certain tree species, such as Toona sinensis and Aralia elata, have gained particular popularity as wild vegetables.

Additionally, the diversity of wild vegetables is especially valuable between April and June, when most cultivated vegetables are not yet in season. During this period, the availability of wild vegetables is high, providing a crucial dietary supplement for local people (32). Furthermore, wild vegetables contain significantly higher concentrations of key micronutrients compared to their cultivated counterparts, a trend consistently supported by previous studies (63, 64), suggesting that wild vegetables offer an additional and valuable reservoir of dietary micronutrients (65).

The consumption of wild vegetables is widespread across China, particularly in regions with high biodiversity and biocultural diversity, accompanied by relatively low-income levels. This pattern is also observed globally, highlighting the critical role of abundant plant resources in providing wild foods for local populations (33). Wild vegetables are commonly consumed in lower-income areas, helping to mitigate the limited diversity of cultivated vegetables and contributing to a more varied vegetable diet (41, 66). Gathering wild vegetables not only provides an affordable source of nutrition but also serves as a supplementary income for local residents. This additional income can improve access to a broader range of cultivated vegetables and other foods (31, 67). Thus, wild vegetables play a key role in supporting dietary diversity and phytonutrient intake in these communities (41, 68).

Moreover, the consumption of particular wild plants, often rooted in local cultural traditions, provides resilience against the impacts of modernization and economic development, ensuring these plants continue to play a vital role in dietary systems for the present and future (30, 69). However, challenges arise in regions with both lower income and limited biodiversity, such as western Sichuan and northwestern China, where the availability of diverse wild vegetables is constrained. These areas also face restrictions in the variety of cultivated vegetables. Thus, efforts should focus on increasing the variety of vegetable foods available to local populations to effectively address nutritional disparities. Besides transporting them from the better endowed regions, agricultural technology can be developed to produce a variety of vegetables in arid and harsh climates.

Strategies for Sustainable Use of Wild Vegetables in Our Diets.

The rise of modern agriculture has significantly reduced the diversity of plant species relied upon for human sustenance and health, particularly impacting wild and semidomesticated fruits and vegetables (70, 71). As essential contributors to agricultural biodiversity, wild vegetables and the related biological cultures play a vital role in enriching local diets (32, 72). However, realizing their full potential while ensuring their sustainable use remains a persistent challenge. To address this issue, we propose four key areas of action for managers, practitioners, and scientists to guide future efforts.

First, research on the domestication and cultivation of some potential wild vegetable species can be strengthened. Wild vegetables represent a vast global pool of genetic resources, offering promising candidates for cultivation and potential benefits. Selecting and breeding species with desirable traits, such as climate resilience, high yield, and rich nutrient content, from the common wild vegetable pool can expand the cultivated vegetable repertoire. The ingrained human inertia of relying heavily on a small pool of domesticated organisms as food can be overcome by stepping up modern domestication efforts. To prevent the excessive collection of wild vegetable plants, it is essential to establish a firm basis for producing the domesticated varieties before embarking on supply expansion and demand promotion.

Second, the supply chain can be enhanced to stimulate consumer demand for wild vegetables. The cultivation and supply systems for wild vegetables can be rationalized to facilitate delivery to the urban markets. Our study identified over 100 wild vegetable species already available in markets, with some successfully commercialized. In certain ethnic minority regions of China, local wild vegetables have gained popularity among residents and tourists, forming a core part of regional tourism products (36, 73). Despite this progress, the broader supply system remains underdeveloped. Further advancements are needed in nutritional analysis, cultivation techniques, storage, and preservation, as well as the development of diverse edible products. On the demand side, consumer acceptance of wild vegetables is often bolstered by cultural ties and traditions. To further promote consumption, wild vegetables should be incorporated into dietary guidelines and integrated into food supply systems. However, commercialization must also ensure equitable access, enabling broader populations to benefit from these resources.

Third, the conservation of wild vegetable genetic resources can be strengthened. As the supply of wild vegetables grows, protecting their genetic diversity must remain a priority. Overharvesting can diminish genetic variation and increase extinction risks for specific species (50). In situ conservation strategies, such as establishing nature reserves and community-based initiatives, are vital for safeguarding wild vegetable resources. Additionally, ex situ conservation efforts, particularly through botanical gardens and gene banks, should focus on preserving key wild vegetable species and their genetic materials, ensuring their long-term sustainability.

Fourth, there is a need for more comprehensive research, cataloging, and documentation of wild vegetable plants. Although many case studies highlight their diversity, large-scale investigations at the national level are lacking (74, 75). Moreover, traditional ecological knowledge about collecting and consuming wild plants remains underdocumented (29). Thus, addressing these gaps should be a priority in future research efforts on wild plants, which is important for preserving a diverse pool of wild vegetables for future generations.

Materials and Methods

Definition of Cultivated and Wild Vegetables.

We defined cultivated vegetables as plant species with a long history of domestication and widespread cultivation primarily for human consumption. These vegetables typically have well-developed industrial supply chains in China and are widely available in markets. In contrast, wild vegetables refer to species that are not intentionally planted or domesticated, but are foraged from natural environments and consumed as food. Some species may also be cultivated for nonfood purposes, such as landscaping or ornamental use. In such cases, they are still considered wild vegetables in our study. Additionally, a few traditional vegetable species, cultivated in very limited areas and consumed exclusively by local communities, are also included in the wild vegetable category. Our definitions of “cultivated” and “wild” vegetables serve as practical and functional frameworks for this research but are not without limitations. For example, some species classified as cultivated vegetables may still be harvested from the wild in certain contexts. As such, these definitions are neither absolute nor universally applicable. Future studies should refine or adapt these categories based on specific circumstances or additional data.

Survey on Cultivated Vegetables in Fresh Supermarkets.

We collected data on vegetables sold in fresh supermarkets across various cities in China using the Meituan Waimai App, the largest and most widely used platform for purchasing vegetables in the country. We selected the top 10 fresh supermarkets with the highest sales in each city. For cities with fewer than 10 supermarkets, we included all available supermarkets. From July 2024 to April 2025, we recorded detailed information, including the species list and monthly sales data, for all vegetables sold in these supermarkets. This time frame encompassed all four seasons, ensuring a comprehensive overview of the vegetable varieties available throughout the year. It is important to note that the vegetable species data and their corresponding sales figures on the App are publicly accessible and do not involve any personal customer information. This process yielded vegetable lists and monthly sales data from 2,983 fresh supermarkets across 330 cities in China (Fig. 1), resulting in more than 700,000 pieces of data. We divide these cities into seven geographical regions, including southern, southwestern, central, eastern, northern, northeastern, and northwestern. We standardized the species names of all cultivated vegetables using Species 2000 China (2024 edition) (http://www.sp2000.org.cn/) and the Flora of China (https://www.iplant.cn/). For consistency, only species and their variants were retained. Species accumulation curves indicated near-complete coverage of cultivated vegetable diversity in our dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A).

We obtained annual data on cultivated vegetable production in China from 1994 to 2023 using the China Statistical Yearbook. Additionally, we gathered information on key vegetable-producing counties as of 2023, including their annual production volumes, from publicly available government data. Over the past few decades, China has implemented four stages of “Vegetable Basket Project” to increase and improve vegetable supply. We collected and organized the implementation period and the main purpose of each project stage.

Data on Wild Vegetables in China.

To document wild plants used as vegetables by local communities in China, we conducted an extensive literature review and compiled data on species identity, edible parts, consumption methods, and collection periods. Data sources were as follows: 1) Published articles. We searched the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Web of Science using keywords such as “wild vegetables,” “edible plants,” “resource plants,” “ethnobotany,” “traditional food,” “wild food plants,” “indigenous vegetables,” and “wild edible plants.” This exercise yielded 762 articles, from which we retained only high-quality studies published within the past 30 y. We extracted over 20,000 records of wild vegetables from 496 selected articles, including field survey data from 248 locations and ethnobotanical surveys involving 28 ethnic minority groups in China. 2) Books. We searched online for books related to wild vegetables using the same keywords. The exploration identified 19 books documenting wild vegetable species in China. For example, the book Wild Vegetable Resources in China records more than 1,200 wild vegetable species, including their edible parts, collection times, and nutrient content. All relevant information from these books was incorporated into our database. 3) Flora of China. We also reviewed the utilization information of all vascular plants listed in Species 2000 China from the Flora of China, screening for species identified as wild vegetable plants. All collected data were consolidated into a comprehensive database containing 27,774 records from 520 independent sources. Species names were standardized using Species 2000 China and Flora of China, retaining only valid species and botanical variants. Species accumulation curves indicated that the database captures nearly all wild vegetable species currently documented in China (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B).

Based on these records, we determined the edible parts and collection months for each species. Edible parts were categorized into seven groups: leaves, stems, buds, roots, flowers, fruits, and others (e.g., bulb, bark, and seeds), with some species having multiple edible organs. Collection periods were mapped to specific months, and we calculated the number of wild vegetable species available for harvest each month. To assess the geographic distribution of wild vegetable species richness, we compiled county-level distribution data from the China National Herbarium, the Atlas of Woody Plants in China: Distribution and Climate (76), and provincial plant distribution records from 18 provinces (https://www.biodiversity-science.net/CN/subject/listSubjectChapters.do?subjectId=1744939693122&sortOrder=latest), totaling over 680,000 county-level entries. These were aggregated to the city level. By overlaying species distributions, we generated species lists and calculated wild vegetable richness for each city. These lists reflect the potential diversity of wild vegetables in each location, rather than the species actively collected by residents.

Classification of Vegetable Groups.

Based on their monthly sales, we classified cultivated vegetables into common and rare species. Species contributing to the top 95% of cumulative monthly sales were designated as common cultivated vegetables, while those accounting for the remaining 5% were classified as rare. Cultivated vegetable species were further grouped by origin: those native to China were designated as “native species,” while those introduced from other countries were labeled as “nonnative species.” The origin of each species was determined using the Flora of China. Wild vegetables were categorized using their distribution range and frequency in the data sources. Species present in more than 10% of the surveyed cities and appearing in over 10% of data sources were considered common wild vegetables. All other species were classified as rare wild vegetables.

Data on the Nutrient Content of Cultivated and Wild Vegetables.

We focused on four essential nutrients that are critical to human health and must be obtained through diet: carotene and three B vitamins—thiamin (B1), riboflavin (B2), and niacin (B3) (65, 77). Nutrient data for both cultivated and wild vegetables were primarily sourced from the Institute of Nutrition and Health, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (https://nlc.chinanutri.cn/) and the Nutrient Composition Table of Chinese Food (sixth Edition). To supplement this, we searched the literature using the keywords “wild vegetables + nutrients” on the CNKI and Web of Science. To ensure data reliability, we only included studies that reported nutrient content based on direct measurements using standardized methods. This process yielded 78 articles, from which we extracted 154 nutrient data points covering 54 wild vegetable species.

Explanatory Variables.

To investigate the drivers of diversity patterns and species composition of cultivated and wild vegetables, we selected explanatory variables across four major categories: socioeconomic development, cultural diversity, natural environment, and plant resources availability.

The socioeconomic category included four variables: human population density (HPD), cropland coverage (CLC), PCDI, and HFI. Human population data were derived from China’s seventh national census (2021). Land cover data for 2020 were obtained from Yang and Huang (78), and PCDI data were sourced from official government reports for each city in 2023. The cultural diversity category comprised three variables: the proportion of minority population (PMP), intangible cultural heritage abundance (ICHA), and traditional village abundance (TVA). PMP data were derived from China’s seventh national census (2021). TVA data included 8,155 villages officially recognized by the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development. ICHA data comprised 3,610 national-level and 17,720 provincial-level entries, as designated by the Ministry of Culture and Tourism. All values were calculated at the city level.

The natural environment category included four variables: mean annual temperature (MAT), MAP, mean elevation (EleM), and EleR. Climate data (1970–2020) were obtained from the WorldClim 2.1 database at 2.5′ spatial resolution. For each city, we calculated the average of each climate variable by aggregating values from 1 km × 1 km grid cells. Elevation metrics were computed from digital elevation models at the same resolution. The plant resources availability category included two variables: NFC and vascular plant diversity (VPD). NFC data for 2020 were sourced from Cheng et al. (79). The county-level distribution data for vascular plants in China were from the China National Herbarium, the Atlas of Woody Plants in China: Distribution and Climate (76), and provincial plant distribution records from 18 provinces (https://www.biodiversity-science.net/CN/subject/listSubjectChapters.do?subjectId=1744939693122&sortOrder=latest). Based on the distribution data of each species, we calculated the species richness of vascular plants in each city.

Preliminary analysis revealed strong correlations among several explanatory variables (Spearman’s r > 0.70). To minimize multicollinearity, we retained only one variable from each highly correlated group (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). In total, nine variables were included in the final analysis: two related to socioeconomic development (HFI, PCDI, CLC), three related to cultural diversity (TVA, ICHA, PMP), two related to natural environmental conditions (MAP, EleR), and one related to plant resources availability (NFC).

Data Analysis.

We first analyzed the species compositional characteristics of both cultivated and wild vegetables. The similarity in cultivated vegetable species across different seasons was evaluated using the Bray–Curtis similarity index, which accounts for the relative sales volume of each species. This index was calculated using the R package “vegan” (80). To compare the micronutrient content of cultivated and wild vegetables, we applied the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Geographic patterns in vegetable species richness were visualized using ArcGIS 10.2 (81), and differences among geographical regions were assessed through ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons using the Tukey HSD test.

We modeled vegetable species richness against explanatory factors using linear mixed models (LMMs) using the R package “nlme” (82), incorporating geographic region and province ID as random effects. Spatial autocorrelation of the model residuals was tested and found to be insignificant, so we did not include spatial structure in the LMMs. We then used the R package “glmm.hp” to quantify the relative importance of each explanatory variable on vegetable diversity (83). Additionally, we used a generalized additive model (GAM) to examine the relationship between vegetable diversity and PCDI using the R package “mgcv” (84)

We constructed a piecewise structural equation modeling (piecewise SEM) to investigate the hypothesized causal relationships among vegetable diversity with the natural environment (MAP, EleR), socioeconomic development (PCDI), plant resources availability (NFC), and cultural diversity (ICHA) at the city scale using the R package “piecewise SEM” (85). Piecewise SEM incorporates several linear or nonlinear models into a single analysis pathway using directional separation (d separation) tests to statistically identify causal relationships and missing links (i.e., pathways) in a directed acyclic graph. An initial path model was built based on our conceptual path diagram (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). We used LMMs to connect the variables, with geographical region and province ID being included as random effects. We refined the original model by dropping nonsignificant links, starting with the least significant link, and continuing stepwise until changes in the AICc were <2.

The similarity in species composition of vegetables among different cities was assessed using the Jaccard similarity index (J). The index is calculated using J = a/(a + b + c), where a is the number of species unique to the first city, b is the number of species unique to the second city, and c is the number of species in common at both cities. J ranges between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating greater similarity of species composition between two cities. GAM were used to model the relationships between similarities in vegetable communities and geographical distance. Geographic distance was calculated as the Euclidean distance between two cities, determined from their latitude and longitude coordinates using the R package “geosphere” (86). To examine differences in the mean Jaccard similarity index among the three groups of vegetables, we performed ANOVA and multiple comparisons. To further characterize compositional patterns of cultivated and wild vegetables in the sampled cities, we conducted NMDS analyses using the R package “vegan” (80). The NMDS was based on Jaccard distance, and stress values were used to assess the goodness of fit. In addition, the explanatory variables were added to the analyses, and squared correlation coefficients (r2) were calculated to assess the contribution of these variables to species composition.
