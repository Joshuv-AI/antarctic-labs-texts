Exploring teamwork in fluid multiteam systems (MTS): a qualitative analysis of team effectiveness in public health emergent response teams in Wales, UK
Authors: Eva Krczal, Doris A Behrens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives The research aims to understand key contributors to multiteam system (MTS) effectiveness by qualitatively exploring team members’ experiences working in a fluid MTS, their ability to effectively collaborate with component teams, and the effect of social dynamics on collaboration and cooperation.DesignIn-depth qualitative case study with semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions and open-ended answers from an online survey. Thematic analysis was applied.SettingA COVID-19 Test, Trace and Protect Service in the UK was formed as a partnership between a local health board, Public Health Wales and public sector organisations.ParticipantsSenior managers from different partner organisations, as well as current and former staff members from various positions and teams, were recruited via the service’s project management office.ResultsThe study identifies a strong influence of situational strength on team functioning, whereby the pandemic situation fuelled the teams’ commitment to the common purpose and promoted a shared identity across the teams. Further, the study highlights the key role of leadership in enabling MTS effectiveness through the establishment of effective governance structures, role-modelling (supporting that all voices are heard), and enhancing a psychologically safe working climate. Lastly, the study demonstrates the impact of social dynamics on team functioning, whereby team commitment, engagement and a shared team identity appeared to promote mutual support, communication and cooperation across component teams.ConclusionResults hold lessons for managers tasked with leading fluid MTS: communication of a clearly defined overarching purpose and aim, alignment of individual team contributions to the overarching aim alongside feedback cycles and acknowledgement of individual team efforts, selecting team members with the ability to cope with volatile, uncertain and ambiguous circumstances; selecting team leaders with inclusive and compassionate leadership styles, the establishment of collaborative governance structures and the introduction of staff well-being measures for coping with work stressors.

Background

Teamwork has become an essential service delivery feature to coordinate care within and across health organisations.1 2 Reaching public health goals usually requires integrated efforts that cannot be addressed by one health team individually but by multiple teams forming a multiteam system (MTS). According to Weaver et al (p.2), MTS are ‘networks of interdependent teams working toward overarching system goals in addition to local team goals’.3 MTS are often formed to accomplish complex, multifaceted, ambiguous and time-sensitive tasks4 and are proposed as effective structures to deal with dynamic and complex environments.5

For catastrophic events, such as disasters or outbreaks, emergent response teams are formed quickly to provide ad-hoc relief. A typical characteristic of such teams is fluidity, as they are formed temporarily for critical and time-sensitive tasks and are disbanded once their mission has been accomplished.6 Managing emergent response teams requires coordinating expertise, people, resources and tasks across organisations and sectors while dealing with a high degree of uncertainty, volatility and time pressure.7 Team members usually share no or limited common history of working together. The initial orientation stage is compressed; team members have limited time to get accustomed to each other and the task because they have to start and perform immediately.8 The lack of stability and the absence of previous experiences in working together pose a challenge to team effectiveness, as familiarity between team members has been demonstrated to enhance the quality of relationships, team cohesion and team functioning.9 10 Operating in a multiteam context poses additional challenges to team members regarding collaboration and teamwork as they have to coordinate activities within the team and across component teams.11

MTS effectiveness

Research on MTS effectiveness is rooted in team literature. The literature describes team effectiveness as a multidimensional construct comprising team performance (results), team functioning and team viability.12 13 According to Zajak et al (p.3), effectiveness results from a combination of (1) ‘what the team is able to accomplish (results/team performance outcomes)’, (2) ‘how the team functions while working together on a daily basis (team functioning)’ and (3) ‘whether the team believes they would be able to continue successfully working together in the future (viability)’.14 Team functioning comprises the two interrelated dimensions of teamwork and taskwork.14 Taskwork refers to the actions of the team members needed to complete the task and achieve the goals. In contrast, teamwork refers to the collaboration processes necessary to coordinate team members’ actions.15 16 In the context of MTS, team effectiveness relates to both team functioning within a team and team functioning across a system of component teams.17

Input, mediator and output (IMO) models serve to conceptualise team effectiveness.18 19 Figure 1 displays the IMO framework extended for MTS. The input factors displayed in the left box are considered antecedent factors that affect team members’ interactions.19 MTS input factors comprise the composition and organisation of the component teams involved in achieving the overarching aim.11 They typically include team member characteristics, team composition, task structure, organisational structure and MTS leadership.20 21

IMO framework for MTS (adapted from Shuffler and Carter 201817). ABC, attitudes, behaviours, cognitions; IMO, input, mediator and output; MTS, multiteam system.

These inputs affect team output performance through mediators, which are commonly categorised along the ABC (attitudes, behaviours, cognitions) framework,8 21 displayed in the middle box of figure 1. The first domain, attitudes, describes ‘how teams feel’.13 Attitudes comprise affective states representing team members’ feelings, attitudes and emotions, as well as motivational states referring to the intensity of members’ effort toward task accomplishment.22 The second group, behaviours, represents team processes, that is, ‘what teams do’.13 According to Marks et al,23 team processes include transition processes (mission analysis, goal specification, strategy formulation and planning), action processes (coordination, monitoring progress, systems and team members) and interpersonal processes (conflict management, motivation and confidence building, affect management). The third domain, cognitions, represents team members’ beliefs or thoughts and describes ‘what team members think’.13 Team cognition includes a shared understanding of the purpose and aim of the MTS, team members’ roles and abilities and how to cooperate and accomplish the task.24 25 For example, team members develop shared mental models on how the entire system needs to interact to accomplish the overarching goal.26 Attitudes and cognitions, both representing team emergent states, develop over time as they emerge gradually from team members’ interactions.8

Finally, the right box of the IMO model represents the outcomes of team performance (see figure 1), such as team productivity, viability or member satisfaction.12 The I-M-O sequence is commonly displayed as a linear path. However, advocates of the IMO model recognise a cyclical causal feedback loop.18 23 Authors argue that team processes and outputs are expected to affect future team processes and emergent states. Moreover, team inputs, processes and emergent states are proposed as dynamic and overlapping constructs that have reciprocal relationships with one another and team outcomes.19 20 22 The multilevel nature of emergence renders team processes and states inherently complex.11 MTS add a layer of complexity to teamwork as individual team members are embedded within teams that are connected with other teams of the system, resulting in a complex network of team relationships and task interdependencies.5

Previous research predominantly explored teamwork in hospital settings, studying medical emergency teams,27 28 surgery teams29 or intensive care teams.30 Team members from the same organisations can build on some commonalities such as well-established structures, standardised procedures or a shared organisational culture. Challenges in coordination and collaboration increase when team members derive from different health organisations or sectors and are working across multiple teams. In a multiteam context, the IMO framework needs to be extended by a systems perspective accounting for teamwork processes and psychological states across teams.4 There is scarce evidence on understanding and improving teamwork processes, as well as delivering and coordinating care among multiple teams.11 Current team literature points to a need for additional research on teams in such highly complex and dynamic settings to gain a better understanding of how team effectiveness unfolds within this specific context.31 32

The present study addresses this gap by qualitatively exploring team members’ experiences working in an MTS, their ability to effectively work together with component teams (taskwork) and the effect of social dynamics on collaboration and cooperation (teamwork). Thus, the research aims to understand what factors facilitate or hinder fluid MTS effectiveness. The insights from our findings extend and challenge prior research on fluid MTS in healthcare. We draw evidence-based recommendations for how MTS functioning can be supported.

Methods

Methodology

The present study adopted a qualitative explorative approach to generate a new understanding of MTS functioning in a specific context.33 We selected a temporary public health partnership operating as an MTS to explore in-depth enablers and barriers to reaching MTS effectiveness.34 Non-directive questioning was used to provide study participants with the opportunity to be more discursive, propose themes vital to them, reflect on their experiences and offer opinions.35

This study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist, which is provided as a supplementary file.

Context

The study examined team functioning within the Gwent Test Trace Protect Service (GTTPS), which was founded as a partnership between the local health board and Gwent’s local authorities. MTS effectiveness relates to the performance output of the GTTPS, which was produced jointly by the component teams. The service has proved successful, exhibiting a 98% success rate until the onset of the Delta variant (ie, the service followed up 98% of the contacts within 24 hours of being identified as a close contact of a positive case).36 Thus, the GTTPS presented an excellent case for studying the factors contributing to MTS effectiveness. For a detailed description of the GTTPS structure and evolution, see Behrens et al.36 Team fluidity refers to the dynamic and unpredictable working conditions during pandemic waves. In peak times, the staff comprised more than 400 employees recruited or temporarily deployed. The membership period was uncertain, resulting from unclear predictions on the duration of the pandemic and the service. Team members were recruited from different professions and sectors (public service providers and the private sector), many of them sharing no common history in working together. Further, teams faced environmental extremeness induced by the pandemic (novel virus, population-level health threat) and team performance was associated with serious consequences of failure.37 The contextual factors were considered when designing and conducting the research.38

Sampling and recruitment

After ethics approval had been received, recruitment of study participants started. The selection of study participants for the interviews and focus groups followed a maximum variation sampling approach to capture experiences and interpretations from multiple perspectives.39 Study participants were recruited across all partner organisations (AHUB, Public Health Wales, five Gwent Local Authorities), professions and job roles, ensuring gender parity. Interviewees held leading positions, and focus group participants were selected from different organisational hierarchies and positions. Study participants were invited by the service’s project management office via email to join an MS Teams meeting, receiving a short explanation of the purpose and a suggested date. All invited individuals agreed to participate. Before starting the interviews or focus groups, the interviewer outlined the purpose and topics covered and obtained verbal informed consent from participants, including consent to participate and to audio-record the session. Interviews and focus groups were conducted by members of the research team who were not part of the service’s operational management structure.

Additionally, 570 current and former GTTPS team members were invited to participate in an online survey. Of the former GTTPS members, those still employed by the National Health Service (NHS) - who thus had an active NHS email account) were contacted. Study participants received an email with a survey link explaining the purpose and topics covered. Completion of the survey was taken as evidence of informed consent. The two open questions from the survey were analysed for this study, while the quantitative responses were ignored.

Data collection

Data were collected between October 2021 and February 2022 through semi-structured interviews, two focus groups, one feedback workshop and an online survey. EK and DAB, who have experience in qualitative interviewing, conducted all interviews and focus groups via Microsoft Teams. EK is an independent senior university researcher; DAB is a university professor and was an epidemiologist within the GTTPS. Individual interviews lasted 30–45 min; focus groups lasted approximately 90 min. A final 90 min feedback workshop on preliminary findings was used to consolidate the results and collect further data. DAB conducted the workshop via Microsoft Teams and recorded information as unstructured notes. The workshop served as a form of member checking to validate and refine emerging themes and to check the credibility of the researchers’ interpretations.

Researchers used an interview guide for the interviews and focus groups to maintain consistency across the questioning. The interview guide focused on participants’ reflections on their working experience within the GTTPS and covered three main themes: organisation, communication and leadership.

The online survey questionnaire comprised 65 closed and two open questions and was conducted using Microsoft Forms. Overall, 33% of the service’s current and past workforce responded to the survey. Answers and comments from the first open question ‘What changes did you observe in your daily work over time?’ and from the second open question ‘Is there anything else you wish to share about how GTTPS has developed?’ were used for this study.

Data analysis

Trusted individuals experienced in generating protocols from within the NHS audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim interviews and focus-group discussions. Data were coded by EK and discussed with DAB, aided by MAXQDA, version 2020. Thematic analysis was applied to identify the contributors to MTS effectiveness.40

After reading the entire data set (interview and focus group transcripts, survey responses to the two open questions), the researchers shared initial ideas for organising the data and the specific fields of interest. Four macro-themes from the IMO framework were used to guide further analysis (see figure 1). These macro-themes were (1) ‘Inputs’, (2) ‘Team attitudes’, (3) ‘Team behaviours’ and (4) ‘Team cognitions’. During the coding process, researchers generated codes representing a particular feature or idea that emerged from the material. The codes were attached to the corresponding text segments and filed under a category name corresponding to the central theme. Continuing with the next transcript, researchers used codes from the previous transcript or created new codes whenever novel constructs arose from the data. After researchers had independently coded 50% of the manuscripts, a mutual thematic map was developed in a joint session. Data coded in the first round were reviewed and, eventually, text segments were recoded or reassigned into different themes. After generating and sorting codes into themes, the second half of the data were analysed. In an iterative process, codes were reviewed and revised. After all transcripts had been coded, the coded segments for each theme were reviewed to verify coding consistency, ensuring that all subthemes were clearly defined and mutually exclusive. The final thematic map was revised based on the data set as a whole, particularly to ensure that the themes reflect all the ideas and meanings within it. This final step also considered the notes from the feedback workshop.

Patient and public involvement

No patient or public involvement.

Inputs

Clarity on structure, roles and purpose

Study participants emphasised that clarity on the purpose and the aims of the service enabled effective communication and cooperation across the teams. Data analysis revealed two mechanisms. First, with clarity on the overarching aim, teams developed a shared understanding of the task and the purpose. One senior manager emphasised: “I think we've all always had a common understanding of the overarching aims and objectives of the service and what we wanted to deliver.” (Interview 7: 7/3).

Second, the clear mission focus enabled the teams to channel their behaviours towards task accomplishment. One senior manager reflected: “I think [it] worked well, and I think people again focused on the priority, which is contact tracing, contacts, advising, looking after our citizens.” (Interview 9: 1/16).

Participants’ perceptions of clear (enough) definitions of team member roles varied. For example, one interviewee stated: “we [were] all sort of quite clear on our roles and responsibilities” (Interview 7: 2/2), while another interviewee remarked: “I'm still, I'm confused about individual roles” (Interview 4: 1/7). Some interviewees described defining positions and job roles as a permanent process of finetuning, as the service was constantly adapting to the pandemic situation. The common purpose and the appreciative working climate emerged to compensate study participants for unclear roles and responsibilities. One interviewee stated: “[…] with a common purpose, and I think that understanding kind of it is there. People are very tolerant and appreciative of individuals.” (Interview 4: 6/7)

Team competencies and abilities

Study participants attributed the diversity of qualifications, skills and experiences of team members to service effectiveness. The “pool of expertise” (Interview 4: 13/23) emerged to inspire and motivate team members. For example, one senior manager outlined:

There [are] all sorts of expertise, and if you just spend a bit of time getting to know people and their backgrounds […] it’s really, it’s fascinating. That’s what I’ve really enjoyed (Interview 4: 13/23).

Study participants considered the recruitment process a vital instrument for selecting candidates with the ‘right’ skill set. Apart from professional expertise, study participants highlighted the coping skills of team members, for example, being “accustomed to dealing with uncertainty” and “with situations that we have not encountered before” (Interview 10: 1/12). One contact tracer stressed the ability to “adapt to the challenges and change we face almost daily” (Survey participant 152, Contact tracer 4).

Further, study participants noted that diversity in professional backgrounds supported the appreciative work climate and service adaptability, both contributing to service effectiveness. One interviewee explained:

It’s also been useful […] to provide a bit of a sounding board, really, and to look at things from a different angle, rather than being wholly health-focused. It’s been helpful with people with other skill sets providing there (Interview 11: 1/16).

Team leadership

Leadership emerged as a prominent contributor to service effectiveness. Study participants stressed the importance of strong leadership relating to the task: “I would describe it as strong. I think it’s been clear. I think it’s been decisive” (Interview 7: 9/2). Study participants described effective leadership as being committed to the purpose, providing a clear direction and promoting a two-way dialogue. One senior manager explained:

You need to listen to the team while giving up clear direction to what you expect of them, and that way, you will get [the] most out of the team (Interview 10: 6/2).

Further, senior managers stressed the importance of involving all team members, obtaining feedback and acknowledging individual efforts, thus promoting inclusive leadership. A senior manager outlined:

People have been putting in a lot of time and effort listening to the feedback that’s coming from the teams, making sure that we affect change as well. So it’s not just that it’s one way; we’re not just pushing information out to the teams and expecting them to deliver. (Interview 11: 11/6).

Next, data revealed that leadership affected team attitudes by enhancing interpersonal relationships. Senior managers stressed in the interviews that it was important for them to build high-quality relationships, promote a team approach and provide support and advice for teams and team members. One senior manager stressed the importance of being ‘supportive’ rather than too ‘directive’ or ‘controlling’ when managing multiple teams (Interview 1: 6/23–35).

Further, data analysis revealed expressions of compassionate leadership, for example:

They [team leaders]’ve been approachable, they’ve been contactable and […] they’ve very much bought into the ethic of the one team approach (Interview 7: 9/3).

Another interviewee added: “Lots of support from the managers […] sort of making sure that their coaching and training has been regular, being available on the end of a camera and a laptop to be able to provide support. So it’s those human things that I think have helped to keep the service ticking over” (Interview 14: 10/6).

Team attitudes

Affective commitment and teamwork engagement

Study participants attributed team affective commitment and team task engagement to service effectiveness. One senior manager reported that teams worked ‘very focused on protecting our communities’ and explained:

I suppose the appreciation of what the risk was, what was at stake by not acting quickly. You know at the end of the day, we’re talking about the potential for […] like life or death, really (Interview 11: 2/3).

The expressions of study participants were related to affective commitment, which is defined as ‘the employee’s emotional attachment to, identification with, and involvement in the organisation’.41 For example, a survey participant remarked: “It’s been great to work within the service […] I’ve really enjoyed being part of the service” (Survey participant 13, Senior Public Health Practitioner: 4). Study participants were committed to making an impact to community well-being or in the words of two participants “being part of the response” (Interview 4: 13/31) and to “keep our residents as safe as possible” (Survey participant 190, Contact tracer: 4).

Other expressions found in the data related to teamwork engagement are referred to as a ‘shared motivational emergent state of work-related wellbeing’ when the team members are enthusiastic and highly attached to the task, willing to put extra effort into work and persistence.42 A focus group member outlined how the emergency-fuelled team task engagement:

It was a very, very serious situation, and people were prepared to go above and beyond what they normally do, go outside of their own skill sets and learn new things and do new things, things that normally would take a long, long time, months or years. Those people just decided, well, this is what I’m going to do (Focus Group 2: 4/4).

Other statements suggested that team members inspired and motivated each other. For example, a focus group member noted: “You’ve got that passion throughout” (Focus group 1: 2/14).

Another focus group member outlined how the overarching aim, team commitment and engagement supported collaboration within and across teams: “We are there to safeguard our community, and everybody is 100% dedicated to the cause. You’ve got that common theme there that everybody wants to work together” (Focus group 1: 2/5).

However, data analysis indicated an adverse effect whereby high commitment and engagement may lead to emotional exhaustion. For example, one survey participant mentioned the “emotional strain of the job” (Survey participant 42, Contact tracer: 4), and another survey participant claimed to feel sometimes “very unappreciated and uncared for” (Survey Participant 32, Administration Support: 4). One interviewee reflected: “We all stepped up to that; worked incredibly long hours. I still don’t think, we got the resilience within the structure […] working a reasonable shift […] without the expectation that they will have to work ridiculously long hours just to get the job done” (Interview 9: 2/12).

Team trust and cohesion

Study participants discussed how trusting relationships between team members and teams affected service effectiveness. Trusting relationships emerged to enhance collaboration across the teams: “We’re comfortable with each other. We don’t always agree, we don’t always get on, but there’s a trust there” (Interview 9: 6/3).

Further, trust was considered the basis for the intense information exchange between the teams. A senior manager reflected that the pandemic stimulated trusting relationships and willingness to share information between component teams:

That does require a high degree of trust amongst the partners to be prepared to share information, and maybe in ordinary times, it would take more time to communicate. There may be reservations about sharing it. (Interview 3: 5/12).

The trust-building processes within the service have been reported in detail elsewhere.43

Team cohesion emerged as another essential contributor to MTS effectiveness. Study participants’ expressions indicated high levels of team cohesion. Cohesion refers to the social bonds between team members, resulting in members’ tendency to stay together and remain united.44 For example, one contact tracer claimed: “I love working with my team, and I don’t want it to end” (Survey participant 45, Contact tracer: 4).

A focus group member outlined how the common aim united all team members into one team:

And at one point, it was over 500 employees that we all work together for the common aim. And that’s what brings us together […] in our daily chats, we always put #TeamGwent. One team and that’s what we are. We are one team working together for a common goal. (Focus group 1: 2/5).

Also, similarities between team members emerged to promote team cohesion. One survey participant reported:

Working with a remote-based team was initially challenging, but we developed a strong team ethos very quickly, probably because we have similar values and beliefs. (Survey Participant 148, Clinical Lead Manager: 4).

Cohesion emerged to buffer the high level of stress experienced by team members. A senior manager stressed: “We were just good for each other, good to each other and good for each other, really.” (Interview 4: 10/17).

Team psychological safety

Study participants described a psychologically safe working environment and associated the working climate with service effectiveness. Team psychological safety is defined as ‘a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking’.45 Following Edmonson,45 a climate of mutual respect and trust among team members nurtures their confidence to raise their voices without being embarrassed, rejected or punished for speaking up. A senior manager emphasised:

All team members can and are allowed to put their contributions in […] irrespective of where they are in the hierarchy; it’s allowed to have a say because that’s the only way a set-up like this can work (Interview 10: 1/8).

Obtaining different opinions was considered to support effective decision-making, or in the words of a team leader, to “add strength, […]. It can give you opportunities […] you haven’t thought of yourself” (Interview 9: 7/3).

Another senior manager highlighted that a working climate characterised by mutual acceptance stimulated organisational learning:

The more experienced people giving advice to the less experienced, and I think it was that acceptance, which is [that] we were all pulled apart very thinly (Interview 9: 1/19).

A focus group member agreed:

It’s a culture of acceptance and change. It’s a culture of ‘it’s OK to put your hand in the air and say no, this isn’t working’ […] It’s a pleasure to be in those meetings where you can say ‘Sorry’. Not sure I quite understand that. […] We just accept change and help each other through it rather than watching people stand alone and go on (Focus Group 1: 2/21).

Further study participants attributed the psychologically safe working climate to service adaptability, which was considered another crucial success factor of the service. One interviewee described the “can-do attitude” and a “preparedness to take some risks at the beginning [of the pandemic]” (Interview 4: 1/10). According to the interviewees, the common approach was to accept failure and to constantly try to change and improve processes. A senior manager reflected: “I don’t feel there are any times where it [the service] failed; I think the service has had to adapt a lot and evolve a lot, and it’s been, it’s very good at doing that”. (Interview 3: 6/14).

Further, study participants experienced that team members’ ideas or proposed changes were welcome and (if considered reasonable) implemented very quickly. For example, one interviewee stressed:

If you’ve got a good idea and it’s possible, and […] we understand what the risks are, let’s go with it […] It was kind of, oh, this is amazing, you know. Look, we’ve changed this. We’ve done this [in] 24 hours and it’s all changed and happened (Interview 4: 1/7).

A contact tracer concluded, “The service has been successful because the teams’ superpower is their ability to roll with the changes and remain focused and enthusiastic” (Survey participant 186, Contact tracer: 4).

However, some study participants experienced emotional strains due to the fast pace of change and adaptations. A senior manager mentioned that constant changes negatively affected team attitudes. The participant explained “that keeping up with all the changes” can be “frustrating and difficult for morale” and concluded, “So I think if you asked me what would I change, it would be a more stable environment for them to operate in with less change less often.” (Interview 14: 5/11).

Team behaviours

Communication and coordination

Frequent regular meetings were adopted by teams as a core means for communication and coordination where the unfamiliar situation and the service’s network structure explain their high recurrence rate: “So, we had three times a day Scrum meetings here, which were a way for us to provide situational awareness to everybody so that we didn’t have to write 10- or 15-page emails all the time to tell everybody what was going on” (Interview 2: 3/15).

Study participants considered creating situational awareness and keeping everyone involved a key feature of multiteam functioning:

We've been sharing things really right when changes happen. We email it out and give people time to consider the changes and then have a briefing to make sure we’ve got a collective understanding and whether that’s within your team or whether that’s across the different organisations with senior management or the various meetings that go on. (Interview 7: 8/5).

Study participants reported that teams created an intense communication network, which contributed to service effectiveness. A senior manager stated, “The networking communication was a really positive thing, and it was a real strength to it” (Interview 4: 1/10).

Another interviewee pointed out a somewhat unique communication feature: “There are people in leadership positions reporting to other people in leadership positions in different organisations. That simply does not happen in very many places, does it?” (Interview 2: 3/16).

Analysis suggested that communication between team members at different hierarchical levels of the MTS lent itself to lateral consultation rather than top-down commands and supported collaboration between component teams. A survey participant reported: “I feel no barriers between each team and would happily seek support and advice from any partner” (Survey participant 89, Senior manager: 6).

Lateral communication between the various teams was manifested in the governance structure, which represented multi-organisational strategic coordination and leadership groups. In particular, the contribution of the Coordination Unit to service effectiveness was highlighted by study participants. This entity was responsible for the coordination of the activities of the component teams and was linking the teams with leadership groups and partner organisations. The ‘Head of Service’ was located at the Coordination Unit and underpinned the Coordination Unit’s approach to offer support and advice to the teams rather than to exert management control.

Referring to a mature stage of the service, some study participants raised the issue of ‘over-governance’ and ‘too much formalism’, which was perceived to hamper agility, flexibility and innovation. While at the initial phase, team members were “able to do all of that without any ratification and authorisation around it” (Interview 4: 11/15–16) 18 months later “it seems like it’s all creeping back to the old way of doing things” (Interview 4: 2/7). A survey participant noted:

There is an element of ‘over-governance’ and ‘top-down’ directions which has stifled innovation and prevented agile responses to quickly developing situations. TTP has become over-technical and has lost sight of the simple principle of contact tracing (Survey participant 90, TTP manager: 4).

Relationship building and mutual support

Meetings also served to build relationships and become familiar with other team members. Study participants emphasised that they invested time and effort in building relationships, also by virtual means. For example, participants mentioned virtual “coffee & connect” rounds (Focus group 1: 6/43, Interview 9: 8/9) or virtual social evening meetings (Focus group 1: 6/45).

Familiarity emerged to support the teams to overcome conflicts and enable effective decision-making: “It’s effective on the back of the fact that we know each other reasonably well, and we’ve established that through our regular teams’ meetings” (Interview 9: 6/3).

The principle of mutual support emerged clearly from data analysis to guide component teams’ cooperative behaviours. Study participants considered mutual support a key contributor to service effectiveness. One senior manager stressed: “We’ve always supported each other […], but it’s never really been done in such a significantly structured [way]” (Interview 14: 3/13). The teams practised ‘mutual aid’ in helping each other to respond to local needs (daily decisions). With the support of the Coordination Unit, on a daily basis, personnel resources were allocated throughout the local teams to respond quickly to emerging clusters.

Further, some participants mentioned the Buddy Scheme or the “decompression call” (Interview 9: 8/11) where team members provide advice and emotional support to each other: “[…] the Buddy Scheme is an excellent platform for sharing and caring” (Survey participant 180, Contact advisor: 4). Mutual support emerged from the transcripts to contribute to a positive working climate and team cohesion.

Exchange of skills, knowledge and information

Study participants emphasised the positive effect of information, skills and knowledge exchange on service effectiveness. They also highlighted teams’ willingness to share information, expertise and advice. An interviewee called the teams a “library of information” (Interview 8: 3/6).

Study participants reported relying on multiple communication channels (Meetings, Emails, Team Chats, SharePoint), which created some deficiencies in the distribution of information. Some study participants expressed concerns about the regularity, accuracy and relevance of information. For example, one survey participant noted: “too many senior people giving different information” (Survey participant 43, Contact advisor: 4). Another survey participant outlined the problem from a team lead’s perspective:

Information is constantly changing and communicating this, and then absorbing and retaining it has been difficult at times (Survey participant 103, Team lead: 4).

An interviewee from a senior level reflected:

“There’s lots of emails, lots in meetings going on and I think there’s things flying in from all quarters and there’s quite a lot of duplication in communication, but I supposed from our perspective it’s better too much communication than […] things getting missed” (Interview 7: 6/6) and mentioned the role of the Coordination Unit that “has helped in circulating the key messages and the scripts and the SOPs (Standing Operating Procedures) and everything else along with other key changes that come along” (Interview 7: 6/9).

Decision-making

Study participants highlighted the teams’ ability to make fast decisions. For example, one interviewee noted: “It was unusual for us to be taking decisions at that scale so quickly” (Interview 1: 2/3). One interviewee explained that the absence of formal procedures enabled fast decisions:

“The groups had to work quite differently because we haven’t had as much time as we would normally have to write papers. So we’ve had to be very pragmatic about the amount of information that’s required to make a decision. So, we’ve had to manage the risk around that and sometimes had to make decisions very quickly based on small amounts of information and then, maybe, we’ve had to wrap the governance around afterwards” (Interview 3: 5/12).

Data analysis revealed interconnections with team affective states, in particular, team psychological safety. During discussions, participants stressed that they paid attention and that each member was allowed to express views and opinions. When coordinating the teams’ activities, study participants appreciated that teams had some flexibility to make locally-based decisions. One senior manager explained that to be “able to make their own decisions […] people were more relaxed about that” (Interview 9: 1/20). Another senior manager agreed, “If you start to take control away from them [local teams] I don’t see that would work” (Interview 1: 6/35).

Further, study participants discussed the importance that all teams understand the reasons behind each decision. One interviewee explained:

If you’ve got the science and explanation behind it, it’s much easier to get your staff to come on board with things if they’re given the clear reason why. Again, they may not agree with it, but you got a clear reason or rationale as to why that decision is being made (Interview 7: 11/4)

Team cognitions

Developing a shared understanding of task, purpose and aims

According to study participants, shared cognitions supported service effectiveness. For example, one senior manager stressed that the teams had a “shared mindset” and added: “I think we’ve just all [tried] to find a common ground which we did quite quickly, really” (Interview 7: 7/3).

Informal communication was considered crucial to reach an understanding of other teams and how they operate:

Lots of informal meetings, lots of informal discussions, which kind of helped build a knowledge base really. […] I think the informal stuff has been just so important as the formal meetings, to get to know how people tick and get to know how each organisation ticks and understand the strengths and weaknesses and knowledge and experience that different people have and to be able to pick that up as and when you need to (Interview 7: 8/3).

Study participants experienced that formal communication might hamper taskwork due to different abbreviations, terminologies or interpretations used by the component teams. One senior manager outlined:

That can be a challenge because we all see the same document. We’ll all talk about the same process, but […] six people reading the same thing will see opportunities for them and the difficulties for them and interpret it the way they understand it. […] Usually, my experience is that there will be a flurry of questions which will be kind of practical delivery type questions […]. Well, actually, what does this actually mean? (Interview 9: 7/3).

Discussion

Conducting an in-depth analysis of a contact tracing service organised in multiple interdependent teams, this research explored contributors to fluid MTS effectiveness and their interconnections. Figure 3 summarises core contributors to MTS effectiveness and some barriers identified from this study.

Main contributors and barriers to MTS effectiveness. MTS, multiteam system.

The study has three main findings. First, our results suggest a strong influence of contextual factors on team functioning. As proposed by Schmutz et al,37 situational strength appeared as a strong contextual variable that impacted teams’ inputs, attitudes, behaviours and performance. In this case, a shared enemy: the SARS-CoV-2 virus appeared to affect team attitudes and behaviours. The notion of protecting the health and lives of family, friends and neighbours, as well as the task’s challenge and the opportunity for personal growth, emerged to evoke affective commitment and team task engagement. Further, the health threat posed by the pandemic appeared to unite component teams, increase team cohesion and promote supportive behaviours.

Second, consistent with previous research, our results highlight the role of leadership as a crucial promoter of MTS effectiveness.46 Leaders in MTS must perform multiple roles, including team building and establishing coordination both within and across teams.47 The challenging task itself might have inspired public health leaders to go for new approaches outside the traditional structures and procedures of the public sector. Our results demonstrate that several mechanisms induced by leadership facilitated the alignment of component teams’ efforts towards the overarching aim.

Results indicate that the collective response was guided by a clear and shared purpose and aim. Leaders provided a clear direction for component teams by aligning goals and synchronising their prioritisation. The team focus and the strong goal orientation emerged to promote a shared identity and team cohesion. Further, clarity on the alignment of the aims and goals across the teams appeared as an essential mechanism for coordinating team activities and developing a shared mental model.

Job roles were less clear than the service’s purpose and aim. Previous research proposed clarity of roles and responsibilities as a key contributor to MTS effectiveness. Unambiguity and inconsistency around members’ roles and responsibilities have been reported to negatively affect MTS performance48 49 as they may have created conflicts or professional silos, reduced trust and divided members of healthcare teams.32 50 In this study, participants felt compensated for the lack of clarity in job roles by the positive working climate and the strong commitment to the service’s purpose and aim. Thus, this research demonstrates that deficiencies in one factor can be compensated by others.

Next, consistent with previous empirical work, psychological safety was identified as a key contributor to team effectiveness by promoting directly and indirectly structured team learning processes.51 Our findings confirm the role of leadership in promoting team psychological safety.51 Consistency in leadership styles appeared to enable psychological safety to penetrate the entire MTS. Starting from the top management, leaders displayed a commitment to the purpose and a willingness to support team members, acknowledging their skills and opinions. We found descriptions of compassionate and inclusive leadership in qualitative data. The latter has been demonstrated to promote psychological safety in healthcare teams as it combats the negative effects of position hierarchy and professional status.30

Developing psychological safety can be challenging for fluid teams operating under stressful conditions. Previous research reported that in high-stress situations, emotions may run high, conflicts may arise and feelings may be hurt, which hindered healthcare teams from developing trust and psychological safety.32 This research presents different findings. Leaders likely promoted an atmosphere of mutual trust and interest in each other’s opinions by admitting that they did not have all the answers in this unpredictable pandemic situation. Following Brown,52 their exemplary behaviour might have encouraged team members not to hide their vulnerabilities and imperfections. Besides, shared uncertainty when facing the pandemic might have flattened professional hierarchies as proposed by Schilling et al53 and encouraged team members to express their opinions and views irrespective of their position and status. Also, personal engagement, relatedness and commitment have been proposed by previous research to form another driver for successfully building a psychologically safe working environment.51 54 Our findings reveal a reciprocal relationship: personal engagement and commitment were nurtured by team members’ perceived personal growth under challenging circumstances, which relates to the learning enabled by a sufficient level of psychological safety.

Another driver shaped by leadership relates to the organisational structure. The findings of this study emphasise the necessity for introducing coordination mechanisms to provide an organisational foundation for the MTS.55 The team approach was manifested in governance structures (coordination unit, leadership groups). These structures kept the communication flow lateral and underpinned the collective approach in decision-making. Involving all teams in decision-making has been demonstrated to enhance effective coordination across teams.3 Findings also emphasise the necessity to coordinate the flow of information throughout the system. The constant exchange of information is considered essential for MTS operating in high-stakes and complex settings to update shared mental models.48 Previous research showed that deficiencies in the information exchange can hamper team performance, whereby time pressure and high-stress working may lead to distractions, failure to listen or withholding information to speed up the process.32 56 This case showed that the intense networking communication created situational awareness and familiarity between the teams and enabled the development of shared cognitions in a compressed period. Our results confirm previous research demonstrating that transactive memory (knowing the other’s knowledge, skills and behaviour patterns) within teams and across the wider system of component teams increased performance via the quality of communication.57

However, at a mature stage of the MTS, we observed that the increasing level of governance and formalism hampered MTS effectiveness. Engaged team members highly committed to the purpose likely need to feel ‘enough air to breathe’ to perform. If governance becomes too rigid, there is a risk of undermining key promoters of MTS effectiveness. Then, overregulation and over-structuring could imply higher expenses and staff disengagement with the overarching purpose.

The third main finding relates to the impact of social dynamics on team functioning. Fluid teams are expected to have lower levels of familiarity and cohesion compared with permanent teams, due to time constraints, limited interpersonal interactions and missing opportunities to engage in relational-oriented communication.8 58 59 This case demonstrated that teams quickly managed to develop a strong team ethos across the component teams. Study participants displayed a willingness to spend time and effort in integrating informal chats in virtual environments and offering information and support to others. The results of our research suggest a strong influence of the team context, that is, the shared threat of the pandemic situation. The feeling of ‘being in it together’ has been demonstrated by another study conducted during the pandemic to unite quickly formed healthcare teams.53 The desire for social interactions with teammates might also be induced by feelings of loneliness and isolation outside working hours during lockdowns.53

The findings of this study illustrate a reinforcing cycle of team affections and behaviours. Team members’ commitment to the purpose and aim and offering (emotional) support to teammates created a collaborative working climate that stimulated learning processes and social bonding, which reinforced commitment, engagement and team cohesion. Similar results have been presented by previous research. Schilling et al60 reported that team cohesion enhances socially supportive behaviours such as mutual aid in communicating problems and support in coping with stress. Learning from one another through the open sharing of information and new experiences and helping to cope with day-to-day processes are all factors that contribute to a shared identity.54 61

Another finding from this study that deserves attention relates to a potential adverse effect of team commitment and engagement. Fluid teams are commonly exposed to high levels of stress and situational strength.37 The findings of this study indicate that high levels of engagement and commitment combined with long working hours, high workload and other common work stressors for fluid teams may lead to emotional (and physical) exhaustion.

Strengths and limitations

The results of this study contribute to a deeper understanding of the institutional and relational factors that affect the performance of connected health teams. They provide valuable insights for collaborations between health teams who share the overarching aim of offering the best possible care to the patient. There are limitations which have to be considered when interpreting results. Designed as an in-depth case study, caution has to be paid when interpreting the results. Restricting the sample of former GTTPS members for the online survey to those with an active NHS email account may have led to an overrepresentation of individuals with more stable or ongoing employment in the NHS, potentially biasing the results and reducing the representativeness of this sub-sample. Next, a key characteristic of a fluid MTS is its uniqueness in context and task. It is crucial to consider that the pandemic has played a significant role in fostering collaboration and support across teams and fueling the engagement and commitment of the team members. Further, the insights emerging from our findings shed light on the multi-layeredness of factors contributing to MTS performance, paving the way for future research on fluid multiteam effectiveness. There may be differences in how team effectiveness unfolds within teams and between teams. This research aimed to capture critical factors both within and across teams by focusing on the integral role of team processes and emergent states for effective service delivery. We hope future research on fluid MTS, especially through a mesolevel lens, extends our findings. For example, identifying mechanisms enabling the creation of a shared identity among multiple teams or exploring the mechanisms of relationship building and networking across teams presents promising approaches to understanding MTS effectiveness.

Conclusion

This case study analysed the key drivers and challenges to fluid MTS effectiveness. Results hold lessons for managers tasked with leading fluid MTS.

A clearly defined common aim and a clear alignment of individual team contributions to the overarching aim, alongside feedback cycles and acknowledgement of team efforts, might foster a shared identity and unite multiple teams. Further, strategic communication to inform the public about service performance might induce public recognition of the teams’ efforts.

Next, selecting candidates with the right mindset and skillset includes some specific competencies for members of fluid teams. Among them are team orientation (in particular, openness to other teammates’ views and opinions, mutual acceptance and the willingness to invest time to support and advise teammates) and the ability to thrive under volatile, uncertain and ambiguous circumstances. Further, inclusive and compassionate leadership presents a vital ingredient for shaping a collaborative and supportive organisational climate.

Managers are advised to create support/coordination units and groups with collective decision-making authority to manage an MTS effectively. Building on the findings of this case study, managing an MTS involves constantly monitoring and re-evaluating the flow of information throughout the system. Further, we recommend paying attention to the level of multiteam governance. There seems to be a need to reconsider the degree of governance continuously to ensure some self-direction and agility for the teams.

Finally, in the context of fluid MTS, it is vital to assist team members in coping with work stressors and mitigating the risk of burnout. Staff well-being measures may include, for example, advising team members to take breaks, supporting each other or seeking advice from team leaders if the workload is too heavy. Team leaders are recommended to integrate self-care and mindfulness in daily practice as well, so that exhaustion does not impair the working morale of those managed.
