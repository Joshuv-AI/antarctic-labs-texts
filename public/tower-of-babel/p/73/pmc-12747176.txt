Challenging the electrophysiological interpretation of mirror visual feedback in stroke
Authors: Brijesh Sathian, Javed Iqbal, Hanadi Al Hamad
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We want to praise Zhuang et al. (2025) for their study on how mirror visual feedback (MVF) changes electrophysiological readings in subacute stroke patients (Zhuang et al., 2025). Their study about MVF affecting motor and parietal brain cortices adds value to stroke rehabilitation research. Nevertheless, the findings presented in the study are questioned since they don’t match previous and current research, which may affect both their clinical and scientific reliability.

The study concludes that maneuvering difficulty following stroke inhibits the activity of the contralateral primary motor cortex, which can be noticed by the decline in mu band ERD. According to this theory, the effects of MVF are wrong because it actually helps mirror neurons and keeps the brain functioning balanced. According to a 2022 study by Bai et al., MVF enhanced the level of M1 excitability in people having subacute stroke, as illustrated by stronger MEP responses recorded through TMS (Bai et al., 2022). This opposes the argument about inhibition, showing that the effect on ERD may result from the brain creating alternative ways to work rather than stopping some brain functions.

Yet, according to the authors, MVF would reduce activity in the parietal cortex, especially at low mu frequencies, which is not what research discoveries have shown about MVF raising the level of parietal activity that relates to focusing on space. According to Chang et al., in 2023, activated parietal cortex during MVF is related to enhanced visuospatial processing in patients who had a stroke (Chang et al., 2023). The narrow goal of the study on mu band suppression could overlook the important impact of other regions in the parietal lobes on MVF, thus restricting the relevance of the inhibition idea.

The authors argue that the reduction of ERD in the low mu brain wave frequency range can show how successful stroke rehabilitation is. Even so, studies have found that recovering movement is connected to enhanced ERD, not less ERD. Research carried out by Cha et al. in 2025 points to the fact that better performance in grasping tasks is linked to significant ERD in the ipsilesional motor cortex and the left and right parietal cortices (Cha et al., 2025). So, it is possible that the study’s biomarker claim could be incorrect about the nerves controlling recovery.

It is stated by the authors that MVF leads to an increased balance between left and right mu activity when measured using lateralization index (LI). However, the idea does not fit with recent studies that reveal liking a song has varied effects on interhemispheric brain function. A study conducted in 2016 by Ito et al. points out that the laterality of the lesion is a major factor affecting the LI scores of patients having a stroke (Ito and Liew, 2016). After analyzing fMRI results, the researchers learned that LE volumes fluctuate according to size, position, and various settings of the data, making it challenging for the brain hemispheres to stay balanced in different people. The main claim of this study does not account for the important differences between individual patients.

The fact that the study emphasizes low mu band and ignores the role of low beta ban suppression is a problem since beta-band low suppression is a key sign of sensorimotor activation. Fong et al. indicated in their 2021 paper that the reduction in beta activity (12–16 Hz) located in the C3 part of the brain shows a stronger relationship with motor problems and arm function than the reduction in mu activity (6 Hz) (Fong et al., 2021). The asymmetry in low beta ERD during the MVF was also tied to increased arms control, which may mean that low beta ERD is more sensitive to changes caused by motor tasks than the mu rhythms. Thus, the study is not able to provide strong conclusions about MVF’s effects on the electrical activity of the heart.

Overall, Zhuang et al.’s findings opposing the effects of MVF on inhibition, on knowable biomarkers, and hemispheric balance are conflicting with recent data that reveal MVF leads to increased excitability in the brain, may not be balanced between brain hemispheres, and indicators can be found in beta band activity. Considering these discrepancies, the authors should try to resolve them by using tools such as TMS and fMRI to check the correctness of EEG findings. In the next step, experts should investigate how different lesions in stroke patients can enhance MVF in rehabilitation. It is important to provide these explanations to progress clinical practice and make science’s findings stronger.
