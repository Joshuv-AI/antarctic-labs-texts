Left Ventricular Intimal Sarcoma Masquerading as a Benign Mass
Authors: Qi Ouyang, Yong Jiang, Xia He, Wei Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary cardiac sarcomas are rare yet aggressive, and their preoperative diagnosis is particularly challenging, as specific subtypes such as intimal sarcoma can radiologically mimic benign masses like organized thrombus.Case SummaryA 45-year-old man presented with exertional dyspnea. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large, calcified left ventricular mass causing outflow tract obstruction, leading to a preoperative diagnosis of organized thrombus. Postresection histopathology indicated a malignant mesenchymal tumor, and MDM2 amplification confirmed intimal sarcoma.DiscussionClassic benign imaging features (calcification, avascularity) can camouflage a high-grade sarcoma. We emphasize the need to recognize specific “red flag” features and advocate for integrating MDM2 testing into the diagnostic workflow for ambiguous left-sided masses to guide lifesaving radical resection and avoid catastrophic management delays.Take-Home MessagesThe classic imaging triad of a calcified, avascular, left-sided cardiac mass can be misleading and in rare cases may mask an aggressive intimal sarcoma. MDM2 genetic testing is critical for definitive diagnosis, guiding lifesaving radical resection over inappropriate and hazardous conservative management.

History of Presentation

A 45-year-old man was admitted with a 1-week history of exertional chest tightness and dyspnea, which worsened with activity and was relieved by rest. Physical examination revealed a grade III systolic murmur at the cardiac apex. His vital signs were stable. Medical History

The patient's medical history included poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus. He had no history of cerebrovascular or cardiovascular disease, and no prior surgeries.

Differential Diagnosis

The primary differential diagnoses based on initial imaging were as follows:•Organized thrombus: This was the leading preoperative diagnosis, strongly supported by the mass's avascularity on both contrast echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), as well as its calcified appearance.•Benign tumors (eg, fibroma/hemangioma): These were considered less likely. Typical fibromas or hemangiomas often appear as well-defined, homogeneous masses on imaging and typically demonstrate significant contrast enhancement, which was absent in our case.•Malignant tumors (eg, intimal sarcoma): A key malignancy to rule out, given the mass's size, obstruction, and heterogeneous appearance, though its avascularity was highly atypical.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) were performed. Initial TTE identified multiple heterogeneous masses within the left ventricular cavity. The largest mass, measuring approximately 41 × 31 mm, exhibited scattered calcification with acoustic shadowing and caused significant obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), with a peak velocity of 4.1 m/s. Subsequent TEE, complemented by three-dimensional imaging, provided superior anatomical delineation. It confirmed the presence of irregular, sessile masses with well-defined borders, and three-dimensional reconstruction vividly depicted their spatial extent and relation to the LVOT. Left ventricular contrast echocardiography identified 3 irregular filling defects. The LVOT mass exhibited minimal mobility, clear boundaries, and an encapsulated appearance (Figure 1E).

Myocardial cardiac MRI showed multiple masses with mixed signals and calcification. The masses demonstrated no enhancement during first-pass perfusion or on delayed enhancement sequences (Figures 1F to 1H).

Management

The patient underwent resection of the left ventricular mass combined with mechanical mitral valve replacement. Intraoperative TEE was used for real-time monitoring to ensure complete resection.1

Gross examination of the resected specimen revealed a firm, gritty mass with extensive ossification and calcification. The cut surface was heterogeneous with areas of tan-white viable tissue, hemorrhage, and necrosis (Figures 2A and 2B). Microscopically, the tumor was composed of atypical spindle cells set within an osteochondroid matrix. The combination of widespread necrosis and ossification provided the pathological correlate for its avascular, thrombus-mimicking imaging phenotype. An external consultation confirmed intimal sarcoma via immunohistochemistry (vimentin+++, SATB2+++, Ki-67 25%+) and, crucially, molecular pathology (MDM2 gene amplification by FISH [fluorescence in situ hybridization]) (Figures 2C and 2D).Figure 2Gross and Microscopic Pathologic Correlation of the Resected Tumor(A and B) Gross specimen views of the resected, fragmented tumor masses. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's postoperative recovery was initially stable. His course was catastrophically complicated on postoperative day 10 by a severe acute ischemic stroke, confirmed by cranial computed tomography, which led to profound neurological deficits. After transfer for continued care, he received maximal supportive therapy; however, he showed no significant recovery and subsequently passed away. Given the pathological finding of tumor involvement at the surgical margin, the stroke was most likely attributable to tumor embolism from residual disease, potentially exacerbated by a paraneoplastic hypercoagulable state. This thromboembolic event was highly consistent with tumor embolism, underscoring the aggressive and relentless biology of intimal sarcoma even after macroscopic complete resection.

Discussion

This case illuminates a critical diagnostic dilemma in cardio-oncology: the potential for high-grade malignancies to masquerade radiologically as common benign mimics.2 This challenge is well recognized, as the objective behind initial evaluation of a cardiac mass is to ascertain its presence, location, and, whenever possible, its benign or malignant nature, a process heavily reliant on multimodality imaging.2,3 The convergence of calcification and avascularity—2 cornerstones of benignity—in our patient's left ventricular mass created a compelling, yet entirely deceptive, illusion of an organized thrombus. While contrast echocardiography is a key tool for assessing mass vascularity, with avascularity being a hallmark of thrombus, and computed tomography is optimal for evaluating calcification, our case demonstrates that these features are not pathognomonic and can be misleading in the context of a high-grade sarcoma.3 This misleading presentation directly led to a preoperative misdiagnosis, exposing a vulnerability in traditional imaging-based paradigms and reinforcing the well-documented challenge of distinguishing benign from malignant masses owing to significant imaging overlap.4,5

The pathophysiological basis for this mimicry likely lies in the tumor's rapid growth outstripping its angiogenic capacity, leading to extensive necrosis and secondary dystrophic calcification and ossification. This process effectively replaces viable, contrast-enhancing tumor tissue with fibrocalcific, avascular material, thereby erasing the typical radiological signature of malignancy. Furthermore, an alternative mechanism whereby a vascular tumor may appear avascular is the formation of surface thrombus, which can encase the mass and prevent contrast uptake—a finding that would be evident on pathological examination. Our experience, contrasted with the case by Alimi and Tajik6 where diagnostic anticoagulation successfully resolved a thrombus, provides a vital “mirror” comparison for clinicians. These 2 cases delineate the opposing ends of a diagnostic spectrum: one validating a conservative approach in the correct context, and the other mandating a paradigm of high suspicion.

Therefore, we propose that the following “red flag” features, even in an otherwise avascular and calcified mass, should prompt an aggressive diagnostic pursuit for intimal sarcoma:1.Atypical calcification pattern: Speckled or amorphous calcification, as opposed to the laminated or rim-like calcification characteristic of a chronic thrombus.2.Rapid clinical or radiological progression: A short, symptomatic history or documented interval growth on serial imaging, which is incongruent with an indolent process. However, it is crucial to note that rapid growth is not pathognomonic for malignancy, as benign tumors such as myxomas can also exhibit significant growth over a short period.73.Suggestive morphology on cardiac MRI: Any feature suggestive of tissue infiltration into the myocardium, rather than a purely intracavitary, “free-floating” mass. However, as this case demonstrates, the absence of clear infiltrative features on imaging does not preclude microscopic invasiveness or residual disease, underscoring the limitation of even advanced imaging.

However, as this case demonstrates, the absence of clear infiltrative features on imaging does not preclude microscopic invasiveness or residual disease, underscoring the limitation of even advanced imaging.

Ultimately, resolving this diagnostic challenge requires a synergistic, multimodal approach that culminates in molecular confirmation. While imaging is indispensable for detection and characterization, it cannot be the final arbiter in cases with atypical features. The definitive diagnosis in our patient was achieved not by microscopy or immunohistochemistry alone, but by demonstrating MDM2 gene amplification—the defining molecular hallmark of intimal sarcoma.8 This aligns with the modern clinicopathologic understanding that intimal sarcoma is the most frequent primary cardiac sarcoma, predominantly occurring in the left heart.8 Consequently, integrating MDM2 testing into the diagnostic workflow for ambiguous left-sided cardiac masses is not a luxury but a necessity. It provides an objective molecular criterion to definitively guide management toward lifesaving radical resection and away from the futile and potentially dangerous path of anticoagulation or watchful waiting.

The devastating outcome in our case serves as a stark reminder of this tumor's aggressive biology. This is consistent with the historically poor prognosis of cardiac sarcomas, which represent the majority of primary malignant cardiac tumors.3,9 Tyebally et al3 reported a dismal 5-year survival rate of only 14%, underscoring the dire consequences of diagnostic delay. We advocate that for any left-sided cardiac mass with atypical or conflicting features, clinicians must maintain a low threshold for pursuing MDM2 testing. This approach epitomizes the modern “imaging-guided, pathology-confirmed, and molecular-defined” diagnostic paradigm, which is essential for improving outcomes in these lethal malignancies.

Conclusions

High vigilance is warranted for left-sided cardiac masses exhibiting classic benign imaging features like calcification and avascularity. Recognition of atypical “red flag” signs should prompt aggressive histopathological and molecular evaluation, including MDM2 testing, to exclude a deceptive intimal sarcoma and guide timely, potentially lifesaving intervention.
