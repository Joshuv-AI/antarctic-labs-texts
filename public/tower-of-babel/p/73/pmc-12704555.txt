Toward Precision Chemotherapy for Pancreatic Cancer Guided by Transcriptomic Signatures
Authors: Brice Chanez, Matthieu Delaye, Nicolas Fraunhoffer, Juan Iovanna, Cindy Neuzillet, Nelson Dusetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the deadliest cancers, with chemotherapy as the mainstay but highly variable efficacy and toxicity. Current regimens, such as FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine‐based combinations, are selected empirically without validated biomarkers to guide choice. Several strategies have been explored to personalize therapy. Patient‐derived organoids and molecular classifiers such as PurIST have improved biological understanding but have limited clinical applicability. More recently, predictive transcriptomic signatures have emerged as practical tools. GemPred identifies patients likely to benefit from adjuvant gemcitabine; GemCore, validated in both resected and metastatic tumors, is compatible with small biopsies; and Pancreas‐View integrates multiple drug‐specific predictors, including for all FOLFIRINOX components and gemcitabine, enhanced by AI. These approaches, retrospectively validated in large cohorts and clinical trials, consistently link predicted sensitivity with improved survival. Beyond regimen selection, signatures enable treatment de‐escalation, optimize first‐line choices, and identify multidrug‐resistant tumors. Ongoing prospective trials will establish their feasibility, supporting transcriptomic profiling as a step toward precision chemotherapy in PDAC.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the leading causes of cancer deaths worldwide, with a 5‐year survival rate of only 11% [1]. Because of its aggressive biology, late‐stage diagnosis, and early metastatic spread, most patients present with unresectable disease, and recurrence is frequent even after surgery, making it the only potentially curative option. Perioperative chemotherapy is therefore essential to improve outcomes and remains the cornerstone of advanced‐stage treatment [2].

Standard regimens include gemcitabine‐based combinations (either alone or with nab‐paclitaxel) [3], FOLFIRINOX (5‐fluorouracil [5FU], leucovorin, irinotecan, oxaliplatin) and its modified form (mFOLFIRINOX) [4], and more recently, as shown in the NAPOLI‐3 trial, NALIRIFOX (5FU, leucovorin, liposomal irinotecan, oxaliplatin) [5]. However, selection of these regimens is solely based on clinical status and expected treatment tolerance, while responses and toxicity vary widely and no validated biomarkers reliably predict efficacy of chemotherapy agents. Tumor biology is not yet used to guide chemotherapy, whereas targeted therapies and immunotherapies only benefit a minority of patients, and most investigational strategies still rely on the backbone of chemotherapy.

Because chemotherapy is indispensable for all stages of disease, prediction of individual treatment response is a critical unmet need. Although several tools have been developed to model drug sensitivity or stratify tumors, few have resulted in actual clinical guidance for chemotherapy. While germline HRD gene mutations (BRCA1/2 and PALB2) remain the only routinely used predictive biomarkers of response to platinum chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors in PDAC, two rare molecular subsets warrant parallel testing because they may open non‐chemotherapy options: (i) MSI‐H/dMMR (∼1–2%), eligible for immune checkpoint inhibitors [6]; and (ii) KRAS‐wild‐type (∼5–10%) tumors, which are enriched for actionable fusions (e.g., NRG1, NTRK, ALK, ROS1) or BRAF alterations [7, 8]. In addition, KRAS‐targeted agents, including pan‐RAS and mutation‐selective inhibitors, are currently in clinical development for patients with KRAS‐mutant PDAC. These markers are not used to predict the cytotoxic response. Complementing transcriptomic chemosensitivity tools, they could enhance the effectiveness of combining chemotherapy with targeted treatments. Thus, robust, rapid, and tumor‐specific predictors are urgently needed to inform real‐time therapeutic decisions in PDAC.

Building on these frameworks, this review traces the development of drug‐specific predictive transcriptomic signatures for personalized chemotherapy in PDAC, from their biological foundations to clinical evaluation and the challenges that remain for routine implementation.

Limitations of Patient‐Derived Organoids and PDAC Subtype Classifiers for Predicting Treatment Sensitivity

In the past decade, various approaches have been developed to personalize chemotherapy for PDAC and to improve the prediction of treatment response. Patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) have been extensively investigated to model drug sensitivity in vitro, while transcriptomic subtype classifiers have helped refine tumor stratification.

Patient‐derived organoids have been shown to successfully model drug response, especially in later lines or for drug repurposing [9]. A landmark pharmacotyping study by Tiriac et al. showed that PDO‐based chemosensitivity signatures predicted clinical responses in PDAC for gemcitabine and oxaliplatin [10]. However, clinical applications are restricted by low culture success rates, long production timelines (6–8 weeks), and limited predictability particularly for first‐line treatments, as organoids are typically established concomitantly with therapy initiation and results become available only after treatment has already started. While emerging technologies such as tumor‐on‐chip platforms and 3D bioprinting are promising, they are not ready for routine applications [11]. Foundational molecular subtyping studies set the stage for precision strategies in PDAC. The seminal study by Collisson et al. [12] was the first to describe transcriptome‐defined subtypes that responded differently to therapy, with the classical subtype displaying a higher response to gemcitabine Later, Moffitt et al. [13] expanded this classification, incorporating the microenvironment and unraveling the normal and activated stromal subtypes. Bailey et al. [14] integrated the genomic landscape to define four subtypes with prognostic/biological relevance. The COMPASS trial‐linked transcriptomic phenotypes with therapeutic outcomes in advanced PDAC: basal‐like tumors had lower response rates and worse survival under contemporary chemotherapy [15]. Recently, Chan‐Seng‐Yue and Kim et al. [16] used a cohort of 317 patients ranging from resectable to metastatic to increase subtype resolution in PDAC. This included the hybrid subtype and demonstrated that transcriptional phenotypes can shift during tumor evolution, thereby modifying the treatment response. Despite their different approaches, these studies consistently converge on the classical and basal‐like subtypes. Rashid and Peng et al. [17] consolidated these findings using the PurIST classifier, which demonstrated that patients with the classical subtype had a higher response rate to mFOLFIRINOX. This finding has recently been confirmed in the PASS‐01 trial [18]. GATA6 immunohistochemistry or in situ hybridization demonstrated similar predictive capabilities in terms of response to mFOLFIRINOX compared with PurIST [17]. Nevertheless, these classifiers were not designed to predict drug response and offer no guidance for treatment‐refractory basal‐like tumors. This shows the need for rapid and applicable predictive tools that directly determine chemotherapy selection from patient tumor samples.

Predictive transcriptomic signatures have emerged as an encouraging option to determine the chemotherapy protocol based on the specific molecular profile of each tumor. Together, these studies underscore that prognostic subtyping informs biology and broad sensitivity trends, while prospective drug‐specific predictors are required to translate transcriptomic profiling into actionable regimen‐level decisions.

Framework for Predictive Signature Development

Various strategies have been explored to develop predictive transcriptomic signatures for PDAC. Some have relied on transcriptomic data from patient cohorts with known clinical outcomes to retrospectively identify response‐associated markers. However, these have not yet been validated in independent cohorts or prospective trials [19]. Another approach combines functional drug response data from patient‐derived preclinical models with baseline transcriptomic profiles. This latter strategy, refined through machine learning algorithms, has yielded some of the most promising and reproducible results to date.

One of these strategies involves a two‐phase framework using patient‐derived preclinical models. First, baseline transcriptomic profiles were generated from untreated tumors including primary cultures, organoids, and xenografts whose sensitivity to standard chemotherapy agents was assessed using functional assays. Multivariate analyses, such as Independent Component Analysis (ICA), were then performed to reveal latent gene expression components associated with drug response and proliferation.

During the second phase, these components were projected onto large‐scale clinical transcriptomic datasets and refined through machine learning algorithms, in particular LASSO and Random Forest. Additional biological features including stromal, immune, and master regulator signatures were then progressively integrated to increase predictive accuracy and robustness. This iterative approach has resulted in the development of multiple clinically relevant transcriptomic signatures supporting their potential integration into personalized chemotherapy decision‐making in PDAC.

The Development of GemPred in the Adjuvant Setting

The first transcriptomic signature developed to predict chemotherapy response in PDAC using this strategy was GemPred, which specifically identifies sensitivity to gemcitabine [20]. This signature was built using RNA sequencing data from preclinical models (patient‐derived primary cultures and xenografts) from patients enrolled in the prospective multicenter PaCaOmics study (NCT01692873). Key transcripts associated with cell proliferation and gemcitabine‐specific cytotoxicity were identified and integrated into a predictive score. This first generation of predictive tools was developed using conventional statistical and bioinformatic methods.

A two‐cohort retrospective validation was performed in 67 and 368 resected PDAC. Patients were stratified into GemPred+ (sensitive, n = 85) and GemPred− (resistant, n = 350) groups. A significant difference in median overall survival (mOS) was observed in GemPred+ patients receiving adjuvant gemcitabine (91.3 vs. 33.0 months; HR = 0.403; p = 0.00216). No difference in survival was observed in untreated patients, confirming GemPred's predictive value for the benefits of gemcitabine but no prognostic value [20].

GemPred's predictive value was confirmed in a 350‐patient RNA‐seq dataset from the pivotal phase III PRODIGE‐24/CCTG PA6 trial, validating adjuvant mFOLFIRINOX versus gemcitabine [4]. In the gemcitabine arm, GemPred+ patients had significantly longer cancer‐specific survival (CSS) than GemPred− patients (68.4 vs. 28.6 months; stratified HR 0.42 [95% CI, 0.27–0.66]; p < 0.001), whereas no difference was observed in the mFOLFIRINOX arm. This confirmed the predictive value of the GemPred signature in a third independent cohort [21].

Expanding Efficacy With GemCore: A Signature for Metastatic PDAC

While GemPred was effective in the adjuvant setting, its predictive value was limited in metastatic disease [22] as it was developed for resected tumors and does not account for smaller, lower‐quality biopsies and the distinct tumor biology of advanced disease. The GemCore signature was developed to address this and optimized for use in advanced PDAC including FNA samples.

Validation studies showed that GemCore reliably identified patients with metastatic PDAC who were likely to respond to gemcitabine. In a retrospective cohort, GemCore+ patients treated with first‐line gemcitabine had a mOS of 13.9 versus 3.1 months in GemCore− patients (HR = 0.19; p < 0.001). This predictive value was confirmed in both primary tumors and metastatic biopsies. This confirmed that GemCore was robust in metastases and was compatible with biopsy sampling procedures [22].

Pancreas‐View: Integrating AI to Advance Precision Oncology for all PDAC Stages

The most recent development in this transcriptomic platform is Pancreas‐View, an AI‐based tool integrating several predictive signatures [23, 24]. In addition to gemcitabine sensitivity, Pancreas‐View was designed to assess response to all three active components of the FOLFIRINOX regimen (5FU, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) by integrating the GemCore, 5FUCore, OxaCore, and IriCore signatures, further enhanced with AI‐based methods incorporating stromal and immune‐related gene signatures from the tumor microenvironment. Pancreas‐View refines transcriptomic features to improve predictive accuracy using machine learning algorithms such as independent component analysis and LASSO–Random Forest models.

The results of the retrospective validation of Pancreas‐View in resectable patients in the PRODIGE‐24 cohort showed a HR of 0.31 [95% CI 0.21–0.44] in patients treated according to their signature sensitivity, with a mDFS of 50 months (33.1–NR) and 33.7 months (25.9–43.0) for matched FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine groups, respectively. Patients treated with the non‐predicted regimen and the 27% identified as multi‐drug resistant had a mDFS of ∼10.5 months.

Based on these results, Pancreas‐View was evaluated to predict outcome in advanced disease and was validated in a retrospective cohort of 167 advanced PDAC patients treated with mFOLFIRINOX [23]. Patients were stratified by predicted sensitivity using the derived signatures (5FUCore, OxaCore, and IriCore). Patients in the mFOLFIRINOX arm with a predicted sensitivity to all three drugs had a median OS of 23.0 months, which decreased progressively as the predicted sensitivity to the number of drugs decreased: 13.6 months for two drugs, 9.4 for one, and 6.6 months for none (p < 0.001). These results confirmed a significant correlation between predicted sensitivity and survival and were consistent in both primary and metastatic diseases.

Clinical Applications of Predictive Signatures: Why Do They Matter

Transcriptomic signatures provide a structured and practical framework to guide treatment selection in clinical practice by predicting treatment sensitivity at all stages of PDAC and with all major chemotherapeutic agents.

De‐Escalation Strategies: Maintaining Efficacy While Reducing Toxicity

mFOLFIRINOX has been found to be more effective in various settings, including adjuvant (PRODIGE 24) [4], locally advanced (NEOPAN) [25], and metastatic disease (PRODIGE 4) [26]. However, it is also associated with significant toxicity. Predictive signatures for gemcitabine‐based regimens identify patients that should benefit from gemcitabine monotherapy. Thus, these patients may have comparable outcomes while avoiding the high toxicity of mFOLFIRINOX, supporting de‐escalation.

Frontline Decision‐Making: Choosing the Correct Initial Regimen

PDAC treatment is generally based on two main types of chemotherapy regimens: gemcitabine‐based and 5‐FU‐based. However, only about 50% of patients with advanced disease receive a second‐line treatment due to rapid clinical deterioration. Thus, optimizing first‐line treatment is critical and may directly affect survival. In resectable or borderline resectable tumors, the efficacy of induction treatment influences surgical eligibility; therefore, choosing the right initial regimen is essential to optimize outcomes. Predictive signatures can help make early informed decisions to maximize the therapeutic benefit during a limited window of opportunity.

Challenges for Clinical Implementation

Several technical and biological challenges must be considered when interpreting results and integrating these tools into clinical practice.

Non‐Evaluable Samples: Quality and Quantity

The reliability of transcriptomic signatures is highly dependent on the quality and quantity of tumor RNA. Core biopsies of liver metastases generally provide adequate tumor cellularity and good‐quality RNA, except for small, highly fibrotic, or necrotic lesions. On the other hand, endoscopic ultrasound FNA from primary pancreatic tumors is the only available tumor sample in these patients, especially when metastases are absent. However, the tumor cellularity and quantities of RNA from FNA specimens are often poor, resulting in a subset of samples that cannot be assessed. This can reduce the clinical applicability, especially when rapid therapeutic decisions must be made based on minimal biopsy samples. Furthermore, the small sample tumor tissue obtained is needed for both routine diagnostic procedures (H&E, and sometimes immunohistochemistry) and molecular characterization (RNA, and DNA for KRAS, MSI, or BRCA status). Although advances in needle design and sampling techniques have improved outcomes, this is still an important issue.

Sample Contamination by Non‐Tumoral Tissues

The predictive accuracy of signatures relies on sufficient tumor content in biopsies. Contamination by inflammatory infiltrates, normal pancreatic epithelium, or adjacent tissue (liver, duodenum, or stomach) can dilute the tumor‐derived signal and impair predictive performance, especially in small or low cellularity biopsies. Systematic histological review, tumor content quantification, and computational deconvolution strategies are essential to minimize this source of variability.

Intratumoral Heterogeneity and Sampling Bias

PDAC exhibits marked spatial and temporal heterogeneity. A single biopsy may not capture the full molecular complexity of the tumor. In multifocal lesions, small spatially restricted resistant subclones may go undetected, leading to treatment failure despite an apparently ‘sensitive’ signature. Moreover, transcriptomic profiles can evolve during chemotherapy, decreasing the predictive value of a single baseline biopsy for multiple lines of treatment. Addressing these issues may require multi‐regional or longitudinal biopsies and integration of single cell analyses to resolve clonal diversity. Liquid biopsies could also be used to monitor treatment response and tumor progression through transcriptomic profiling of circulating tumor RNA or extracellular vesicles (ctRNA/EVs).

Logistical Constraints and Turnaround Time

Several logistical barriers must be overcome for the routine use of transcriptomic signatures, including standardization of processing protocols, rapid and consistent RNA extraction and sequencing, and timely delivery of results. In addition, user‐friendly analysis pipelines, automated decision‐support systems, and clinically validated tools are needed to facilitate integration into clinical workflows. Ensuring cost‐effectiveness in different healthcare systems and similar access to these technologies is also a critical goal to advance precision oncology.

Extending the Role of Transcriptomic Signatures: De‐Escalation, Resistance, and Future Combinations

Tailoring Chemotherapy: Toward à la c arte Regimens

Transcriptomic signatures such as Pancreas‐View can predict the sensitivity to each individual drug in multi‐agent regimens such as mFOLFIRINOX allowing the design of customized combinations. Patients resistant to specific agents (e.g., irinotecan or oxaliplatin) could be treated with simplified regimens such as FOLFOX or FOLFIRI, reducing toxicity but not efficacy. This strategy could be particularly relevant in vulnerable patient populations unfit for triplet chemotherapy, but prospective studies are needed to validate the feasibility and clinical activity of real‐time therapeutic adaptations using minimally invasive samples.

Addressing Multidrug Resistance

One important contribution of Pancreas‐View has been the identification of a multidrug‐resistant subgroup, which represents approximately 27% of patients in the adjuvant setting. These tumors have a predicted resistance to all major cytotoxic agents and are associated with poor clinical outcomes. Early identification of this high‐risk group could help prioritize their enrollment in clinical trials exploring non‐standard or experimental therapies, and support the development of alternative strategies tailored to their specific resistance profile.

Anticipating Next‐Generation Therapeutic Combinations

Current signatures mainly guide cytotoxic chemotherapy, but predictive platforms must be adapted to the use of targeted therapies and immunomodulators in PDAC (KRAS inhibitors, CD40 agonists, CLDN18.2 related agents or the PRMT5‐MTA inhibitors), which are mainly developed in combination with gemcitabine/nab‐paclitaxel or FOLFIRINOX chemotherapy [27].

Toward Clinical Integration of Transcriptomic Signatures in PDAC

Transcriptomic signatures must be prospectively validated for clinical use and to confirm compatibility with routine workflows. Their real‐world utility depends on timely delivery of results from a small amount of biopsy material, particularly in metastatic or borderline resectable cases.

The ongoing PACSign01, GemSign01, and NeoPREDICT trials are prospectively evaluating the feasibility and clinical value of Pancreas‐View. These studies will test whether predictive transcriptomic signatures can enhance treatment efficacy and reduce toxicity as a precision medicine tool to transform conventional chemotherapy into stratified, patient‐specific interventions (Table 1). An important next step will be the prospective evaluation of patient phenotypes defined by predicted sensitivity or resistance, encompassing both treatment response and survival outcomes. These analyses will clarify how transcriptomic signatures translate into clinical benefit across patient subsets and how such effects may vary with the relative contribution of each drug within a regimen. Confirming these correlations in prospective cohorts will be critical to demonstrate the clinical utility of predictive signatures and to guide their integration into therapeutic decision‐making.

Future Directions: Scaling, Longitudinally, and Integration With Targeted Agents

Sampling and Turnaround

To increase real‐world reach, next iterations should standardize EUS‐FNA/FNB acquisition and low‐input FFPE‐compatible RNA‐seq with predefined QC (e.g., DV200/input cut‐offs) and built‐in tumor‐content deconvolution, enabling robust calls from small biopsies and cytological material. Published series suggest that optimized EUS‐FNA/FNB enables high‐adequacy NGS/RNA‐seq, and recent single‐nucleus/scRNA‐seq studies show feasibility even with minimal tissue [28].

Single‐Cell and Spatial Context

sc/snRNA‐seq and spatial transcriptomics are revealing drug‐response programs and stromal/immune niches in PDAC. Insights from these datasets can be distilled into simplified transcriptomic features that improve the predictive value of routine bulk biopsy profiles, especially in basal‐like and mixed phenotypes [29].

Second‐Line Value

Because only ∼50% of advanced PDAC patients receive second‐line therapy, first‐line optimization remains paramount; nonetheless, longitudinal re‐biopsy or liquid biopsy (EV‐RNA, tumor‐educated platelets) could re‐calibrate predictions at progression and prioritize effective second‐line choices. Prospective cohorts embedding repeat sampling and EV/platelet RNA should test this hypothesis. Such longitudinal strategies represent a research priority to extend transcriptomic guidance beyond the first‐line setting [30, 31].

Integration With KRAS‐Pathway Inhibitors

As KRAS‐selective (e.g., G12D) and pan‐RAS inhibitors enter early clinical use, transcriptomic stratification can (i) optimize the chemotherapy backbone for combinations, (ii) flag multidrug‐resistant phenotypes ideal for non‐standard strategies and (iii) capture microenvironmental programs that modulate KRASi response. Early clinical signals with daraxonrasib (RMC‐6236) and zoldonrasib (RMC‐9805), along with strong preclinical synergy of MRTX1133 + chemotherapy, support this path.

Conclusions

The development of transcriptomic signatures illustrates the stepwise progression in the personalization of PDAC treatment from GemPred in the adjuvant setting to GemCore for metastatic disease and a comprehensive AI‐driven platform addressing the full mFOLFIRINOX regimen (Figure 1). Pancreas‐View shows that these tools could personalize standard chemotherapy and complement existing pathological and genomic biomarkers. Future iterations may incorporate genomic vulnerabilities and immune landscape profiling to guide rational combination and sequential strategies, building a framework for the multi‐modal personalization of PDAC. By progressively shifting from empirical choices to biologically informed decisions, transcriptomic signatures pave the way for precision chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer by integrating molecular, genomic, and immune features to guide increasingly tailored therapeutic strategies.
