Genetic Diversity of the Beta‐Casein Gene in Brown Swiss and Creole Cattle From the Peruvian Highlands
Authors: Lucy R. Remigio Lorenzo, Cristina Rivera Romero, Joao De Souza Pacheco, Wilder Bueno Cabrera, Eduardo L. Fuentes Navarro, Rudolf Pichler, Kathiravan Periasamy, Gustavo A. Gutiérrez‐Reynoso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCattle's milk and derivatives are essential for human nutrition. However, up to 90% of people experience gastrointestinal discomfort after consumption. Milk digestibility depends on its protein composition. Casein, about 80% of milk proteins, includes beta‐casein (CSN2) with two variants: A1 and A2. The A1 variant is linked to gastrointestinal issues, while A2 is easier to digest. Therefore, some dairy farms now market A2 milk from cattle homozygous for the A2 allele. This study aimed to evaluate the genetic diversity of the CSN2 gene in Brown Swiss and Creole cattle in the Andes of Peru (Junín, Huánuco, Pasco, Ayacucho and Puno). Blood from 445 Brown Swiss and 190 Creole was collected, DNA extracted with a commercial kit, and genotyped using the Axiom Bovine v3 SNP array. The distribution of A1 and A2 alleles and genotypes showed variation across breeds and regions, likely influenced by local breeding practices and selection programs. In Brown Swiss, the average A2 allele frequency was 0.66 (0.60 to 0.73) and the genotypic frequencies of A2A2 was 0.43 (0.35 to 0.52) and the A1A2 frequency was 0.45 (ranged from 0.41 to 0.50). In Creole, the A2 allele frequency was 0.60 (0.55 to 0.66), and the A1A2 genotypic frequency was 0.53 (0.49 to 0.56), being the most common. In conclusion, the frequency of the A2 allele was high in Creole but intermediate in Brown Swiss. These results suggest that the Brown Swiss and Creole have a favorable genetic potential for A2 milk production in the Highlands of Peru.

Introduction

Milk and dairy products are key in combating hunger and malnutrition due to their high nutrient content (FAO and Organización de las Naciones Unidas para la Alimentación y la Agricultura 2022). Globally, cattle provide 83% of milk production (MINAGRI 2017), while in Peru, per capita consumption reaches 84 kg per year (MIDAGRI 2022).

Milk digestibility is influenced by protein composition, particularly β‐casein, which accounts for 25%–35% of caseins in bovine milk (Tokarchuk et al. 2023). The β‐casein gene presents two main variants, A1 and A2, that differ by a single nucleotide polymorphism. The A1 variant releases β‐casomorphin‐7 during digestion, a peptide linked to gastrointestinal and neurological disorders (Kay et al. 2021; Osman et al. 2021). In contrast, A2 milk is better tolerated and has been associated with digestive and antioxidant benefits. The market for A2 milk has been expanding, in part driven by consumer perceptions of better digestibility, health benefits, and premium quality (Fernández‐Rico et al. 2022). Market reports estimate that the global A2 milk market is valued around USD ~2–3 billion currently, with projections reaching USD ~5–8 billion by the early 2030s.

Given the growing consumer demand for A2 milk and its potential health benefits, numerous studies have explored the frequency of β‐casein variants across different cattle populations to guide breeding and selection programs. However, information from South American herds remains limited, particularly in countries like Peru, where dairy systems operate under diverse ecological conditions and rely heavily on locally adapted breeds.

Breed distribution of these variants varies worldwide: A2 is predominant in Brown Swiss, Jersey, and Guernsey, while A1 is frequent in Ayrshire and Red Danish cattle (Ramakrishnan and Savaiano 2024). In the Peruvian highlands, the Brown Swiss breed is particularly relevant due to its adaptability to harsh environments and its role as a major source of milk for rural communities. Additionally, Creole cattle, which have been traditionally maintained under extensive management systems, offer valuable genetic diversity that could influence β‐casein variant distribution. Understanding the genetic diversity of the β‐casein gene in this population is essential for breeding strategies that promote A2 milk production and improve the nutritional value of dairy products (Mukesh et al. 2022). Therefore, this study evaluated the β‐casein gene variants in Brown Swiss and Creole cattle from Junín, Huánuco, Pasco, Ayacucho, and Puno.

Materials and Methods

Blood samples were collected from 445 Brown Swiss cattle in the Peruvian regions Junín, Huánuco, Pasco, and Ayacucho, and 190 Creole cattle from Ayacucho and Puno with farmer consent. Samples were transported at 4°C to the Genetics and Molecular Biology Laboratory of Universidad Nacional Agraria La Molina (Lima, Peru) for DNA extraction using the Quick‐DNA Miniprep Plus Kit (Zymo Research, USA), following minor modifications to the manufacturer's protocol. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a Take3 plate reader (Agilent, USA). DNA concentrations were adjusted to 40 ng/μL, and purity was evaluated based on 260/280 absorbance ratios of 1.8–2.0 and 260/230 ratios of 2.0–2.2, while integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis stained with GelRed. DNA samples were then stored at −20°C until further analysis.

Genotyping was performed at the Animal Production and Health Laboratory, Joint FAO/IAEA Centre, International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA, Seibersdorf, Austria) using the Axiom Bovine Genotyping v3 Array (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), which comprises approximately 65 000 SNPs. Genotypes at CSN2 variant locus were extracted using Axiom Analysis Suite v 5.1.1.1. The SNP analyzed (AX‐95701009) corresponds to the amino acid substitution at position 67, from histidine (A1) to proline (A2). Then, allele (A1, A2) and genotype (A1A1, A1A2, A2A2) frequencies were calculated in RStudio (V 4.4.1), and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was tested using Chi‐square (p < 0.05).

Results and Discussion

A total of 445 Brown Swiss cattle from four regions in the Highlands of Peru were genotyped for β‐casein variants. The A2 allele showed the highest overall frequency (0.66), and the A2A2 genotype accounted for 43% of cases. Junín had the highest A2 allele frequency (0.73). The A1 allele was less common (frequency of 0.34), with only 12% of the animals exhibiting the A1A1 genotype (Table 1). These results align with previous studies in Brown Swiss. (Şahin and Boztepe 2022; Cartuche‐Macas et al. 2025) and other dairy breeds (Caffaro et al. 2024; Sawicka‐Zugaj et al. 2025) reporting predominance of the A2 allele. The A2 variant has been linked to improved digestibility and reduced milk intolerance symptoms (Jeong et al. 2023).

The β‐casein genotyping of Creole cattle from the Peruvian highlands revealed the presence of both A1 and A2 alleles, with a higher frequency of the A2 allele across the evaluated regions (0.60 overall). In Ayacucho, genotype frequencies were 0.10 (A1A1), 0.49 (A1A2), and 0.41 (A2A2), while in Puno, the respective values were 0.18, 0.56, and 0.27. The higher frequency of the A2 allele observed in Ayacucho (0.66) compared to Puno (0.55) may reflect differences in breeding practices, selection pressures, or gene flow between herds in these regions.

These results indicate that Creole cattle constitute an important genetic reservoir for A2 milk production in the Andean region. Similar A2 allele predominance has been reported in other native or dual‐purpose breeds in Latin America (Duifhuis Rivera et al. 2016), supporting the hypothesis that locally adapted breeds may naturally favor the A2 variant. This genetic potential, combined with their adaptation to harsh high‐altitude environments, positions Creole cattle as strategic resources for sustainable dairy programs aimed at producing A2 milk under low‐input systems (Ward et al. 2024).

The Creole cattle in Peru represent a strategic genetic reservoir, particularly for high‐altitude livestock systems where they have developed unique physiological adaptations over more than five centuries. Modifying their genetic architecture requires a nuanced approach that prioritizes the preservation of these survival traits while seeking productivity gains (Goil et al. 2026).

In this regard, we consider that an appropriate approach is to incorporate the A1 and A2 polymorphism as one component within a broader multi‐trait selection framework. This approach should integrate not only productive traits, but also functional and adaptive attributes, as well as local market conditions and processing practices. In the case of Creole cattle, the conservation of genetic diversity should be a priority, given their role as a reservoir of adaptive alleles that may become increasingly valuable under changing environmental conditions.

Although Brown Swiss cattle have been subjected to artificial selection oriented toward milk production, while Creole cattle have historically been described as triple‐purpose, our results show relatively similar frequencies of the A2 allele (0.66 in Brown Swiss and 0.60 in Creole). This apparent convergence can be explained by a combination of productive, historical, and genetic factors.

First, although Creole cattle have been classified as triple‐purpose, in highland production systems of Peru their current use tends to be primarily oriented toward milk production for self‐consumption and local commercialization. Meanwhile, their use as draft animals has progressively declined due to replacement by equines and, more recently, mechanization; consequently, they are now more appropriately considered dual‐purpose (meat and milk) (Quispe 2021). Additionally, in females, meat production is usually secondary and occurs mainly at the end of the animal's productive life (Moreira et al. 2021). This context favors, in practice, the selection of animals with better dairy traits, even in the absence of formal breeding programs, which could indirectly increase the frequency of variants associated with milk production.

Second, Creole cattle have undergone prolonged natural selection processes in highland environments, which have favored the fixation of genotypes associated with adaptation, hardiness, and biological efficiency. In this regard, it has been reported that in local or less intensively selected cattle populations, the A2 allele tends to remain at relatively high frequencies, possibly due to its ancestral origin and its conservation in adapted populations (Mukesh et al. 2022).

Furthermore, gene flow between populations cannot be ruled out, as in many highland systems of Peru, Creole cattle and breeds such as Brown Swiss coexist and interbreed without strict control. This type of genetic mixing has been widely documented in Latin American Creole cattle, which show evidence of admixture with different bovine populations as a result of historical processes of introduction and crossbreeding (Ward et al. 2024; Armstrong et al. 2022).

The lack of differences in allele frequency can be attributed to their common European origin. Analysis of Peruvian Creole cattle reveals a predominantly Iberian origin, with clear influences from European Y1 and Y2 haplogroups. Brown Swiss is also a Bos taurus breed of European origin; the shared Bos taurus lineage may result in a high degree of conserved genomic regions despite different selection histories (Figueroa et al. 2025).

Another plausible explanation is the relatively recent introduction and continuous gene flow of European breeds into local populations, which may have contributed to genetic homogenization at certain loci. Moreover, the markers analyzed in this study may correspond to genomic regions under stabilizing selection or low differentiation, thus limiting the detection of divergence between breeds.

In Peru, A2 milk represents an emerging high‐value market. The results of this study may support the development of this concept through herd segregation based on genotype and the selective collection of A2A2 milk, aimed at consumers seeking premium dairy products with potential digestive advantages.

Conclusions

The prevalence of the A2 allele in Brown Swiss and Creole cattle from the highlands of Peru indicates a favorable genetic basis for A2 milk production. Using marker‐assisted selection to increase the frequency of the A2A2 genotype could add value to local dairy systems and cater to the growing health‐conscious market.
