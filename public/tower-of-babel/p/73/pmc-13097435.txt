Comparing Teriparatide and Bisphosphonates for Postmenopausal Osteoporosis: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of RCTs
Authors: Marha Zaheer Uddin, Hira Hameed, Anoosha Muhammad Iqbal, Muhamad Irfan, Sanhia Maheshwari, Komal Khan, Shafin Bin Amin, Syed Ali Arsal, Ashish Kumar, Umer Iqbal, Muhammad Maaz, Inibehe Ime Okon

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionOsteoporosis is a bone disorder highly prevalent in post‐menopausal women and is an important predisposing factor towards bone fractures. It is commonly classified based on BMD, which is estimated via DEXA scan. It is an important issue worldwide and requires a multidisciplinary approach to be controlled.MethodsUtilizing online sources including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Google Scholar, a thorough literature search was conducted. To determine the best treatment options with fewer difficulties and adverse events and more good results. There were discovered to be 15 RCTS that qualified. The study was carried out per PRISMA recommendations.ResultsAccording to the outcomes generated from the study, there was a significantly reduced rate of overall vertebral and non‐vertebral fractures with teriparatide use along with data supporting increased BMD observed in the teriparatide group. Importantly, there was a reduction in death rate too observed in the intervention group. The results were also consistent with our various secondary outcomes.ConclusionWe can conclude from the outcomes that a clinically significant result was generated in the group using teriparatide in various aspects. There were still some confounding factors present that need to be addressed to increase the yield of the study, and with increasing research in the field, more comprehensive studies are required in the future to reach a definitive outcome.Keywords: bisphosphonate, meta‐analysis, post‐menopausal osteoporosis, teriparatide

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a disorder of the bones that increases fragility and changes microanatomy, making bones more inclined to fractures [1]. A commonly agreed‐upon criterion classifies individuals as having osteoporosis in case their bone mineral density (BMD) deviates by more than 2.5 standard deviations from the normal BMD of a youthful, healthy population [2]. Osteoporosis has exerted an impact on an estimated 27.5 million individuals around the world, most of whom are women. This results in almost 3.5 million new fractures. 610,000 fractures of the hip, 520,000 fractures of the vertebrae, 560,000 fractures of the forearm, and 1.8 million other sorts of fractures, counting those within the pelvis, ribs, humerus, tibia, fibula, clavicle, scapula, sternum, and other segments of the femur, were among these fractures [3]. Hip fractures are anticipated to climb to 2.6 million a long year worldwide [4]. The following factors are connected to osteoporosis within the elderly population: Osteoarthropathy, age, marital status, history of peptic ulcer and fracture, and female gender [5]. The most excellent standard for deciding BMD is DEXA [6]. Various genes (polygenic traits) have been found to affect BMD, according to genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) [7]. Universally, osteoporosis and fractures have brought approximately undesirable results, both directly and indirectly [8]. Osteoporosis costs the US healthcare framework at the slightest $5–10 billion annually [9]. Osteoporosis increases the chance of breaks, which can lower the quality of life, require hospitalization, result in inability, and lift passing rates [10]. Osteoporosis is a serious worldwide health threat, particularly for postmenopausal women whose estrogen levels drop after menopause [11]. Even though hormone substitution treatment was once commonly utilized, its utilization has diminished over the past 10 years as a result of growing stresses approximately its negative impacts and association with raise the chance of cancer [12]. By controlling and avoiding osteoporosis, however, combining physical activity, a balanced diet, and way of life changes gives an alternative therapeutic approach that can improve well‐being results [13]. Investigating the worldwide predominance of osteoporosis within the common population and the contrasts in age, sexual orientation, geographical regions, and others would give significant data for health professionals on the worldwide disease burden and the benefits of creating worldwide health care planning. Hence, we performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis to assess the worldwide, territorial prevalence and risk factors of osteoporosis.

Methodology

Eligibility Criteria

The meta‐analysis's eligibility criteria encompass randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and a prospective cohort study that examines the impact of teriparatide and bisphosphonates for improving BMD in postmenopausal osteoporosis patients. All bisphosphate medication dosages were included in the studies. In the study, old age post‐menopausal females were examined. Excluded studies included reviews, case reports, and studies that used solutions other than teriparatide and bisphosphonates.

Search Strategy

Searches were conducted using phrases such as “postmenopausal osteoporotic, teriparatide, bisphosphonate” in PubMed, Google Scholar, Cochrane Library, and electronic databases. A manual search was done in addition to the database search. There were no language‐based exclusions in the selection of randomized control studies. This comprehensive search strategy was developed to give us access to pertinent clinical trials on bisphosphonate and teriparatide for improving BMD in postmenopausal osteoporosis patients.

Study Selection

The study choice was conducted as a part of the research selection process; the titles and abstracts of the data obtained were reviewed to identify potentially relevant studies. After that, two researchers examined the complete texts of the selected papers to determine which ones should be included in the meta‐analysis. Disagreements were resolved by dialog with the additional researcher. The PRISMA guidelines [14] were utilised to illustrate the research selection procedure and the reasons for excluding studies, thereby increasing transparency in the review process.

Data Extraction

Through data extraction and assessment of bias risk from each of the chosen RCTs, we gathered baselines, intervention details, patient counts, continuous data, and dichotomous data for our research.

There were several results for us. Our primary outcomes were vertebral fractures, non‐vertebral fractures, mean BMD at lumbar spine, and mean BMD at femoral neck. Additionally, for our study, we made subgroups based on (1) age, (2) duration of follow‐up, and (3) types of bisphosphonates used. Our secondary outcomes were Total body BMD, death, complications, PINP levels, and Musculoskeletal disorders. Standard deviation and mean deviation were applied to continuous data. Also, dichotomous data were utilized.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Bias assessment risk: Two chosen authors formally assessed the quality of the selected studies by calculating the likelihood of bias in randomized trials using the Cochrane Collaboration's methodology. The research was divided into three groups: Low Risk of Bias, Unclear Risk of Bias, and High Risk of Bias. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool's Risk of Bias. The details regarding the risk of bias are mentioned in Figure 1.

Risk of bias summary showing the authors' judgment about each risk of bias item included in the study.

Statistical Analysis

To analyze the outcomes concluded from the meta‐analysis, Rev‐Man 5.2, i.e., Review Manager of version 5.2, was utilized. For every statistical technique in the study, the implementation of a random effects model was done. To represent dichotomous data, relative risk (RR) or risk difference, along with a 95% confidence interval (CI), was used, while mean difference represented continuous data. Statistically significant results were denoted by a p‐value of less than 0.05.

A forest plot, along with the X2 and I2 tests, was assessed in looking for statistical heterogeneity. A p‐value of < 0.05 or 12 > 50% showed high heterogeneity, while to look for the root cause of high heterogeneity, we performed leave‐one‐out analysis.

A total of 838 Studies were found during the literature search. Out of them were excluded based on a similar pattern; 25 studies were remaining, out of them 7 were removed due to non‐availability of full text. Eventually, studies were selected based on full‐text availability. Finally, 14 RCTS and one cohort were included in our meta‐analysis, which met the inclusion criteria properly and had full text available. Risk of Bias Assessment

All of the included RCTS were appraised as having a low risk of bias overall. Three studies had an unclear risk of bias regarding allocation concealment, selection bias and detection bias. A total of six studies had a high risk of bias regarding different domains. Of them, two studies had a high risk of bias regarding performance bias. A summary of the risk of bias assessment and the graph is given in Supporting Information Figure 1.

Primary Outcomes

Occurrence of Vertebral Fractures

This outcome was reported in seven studies which include 4884 patients. After analyzing and pooling up the data, the result was found to be significant and favored the intervention group. The teriparatide was significantly associated with a reduced rate of vertebral fractures than the bisphosphonates with a [p value of < 0.00001, RR = 0.45, 95% CI: {0.35, 0.57} I2 = 0%]. Discussion

Our study examines the effectiveness and safety of teriparatide compared to bisphosphonates in treating post‐menopausal osteoporosis. This meta‐analysis provides an overview of fracture rate reduction, increases in BMD, and various side effects. Our study reported 4 primary and 10 secondary outcomes from 5919 participants in 14 RCTs and 1 cohort in our analysis. Our study favored teriparatide in terms of fracture reduction risk and increasing BMD while most of the outcomes were non‐significant. Moreover, our study was unequivocal about adverse effects associated with teriparatide or bisphosphonate usage, though musculoskeletal and endocrine adverse effects were significantly associated with teriparatide.

The aim of this study was to compile recent evidence regarding two frequently prescribed drugs, teriparatide and bisphosphonates, for fracture prevention in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis [30]. The prior meta‐analysis indicated that teriparatide was linked to a reduced overall incidence of both vertebral and nonvertebral fractures, along with enhanced BMD in the lumbar spine and femoral neck when compared to bisphosphonates [31]. Four new, high‐quality RCTs with shorter follow‐up durations and higher dosages were included in our updated review. Teriparatide significantly reduced the risk of vertebral fractures; however, it did not have a noticeable impact on non‐vertebral fractures or on BMD. While the earlier research aimed to offer a wide examination of the therapy's adverse effects, our investigation delved deeper into the side effects specific to each system. Nonetheless, the association was discovered to be comparable to the non‐significant and inconsistent findings of the earlier studies.

Teriparatide is shown to greatly reduce the risk of vertebral fractures in patients with post‐menopausal osteoporosis [RR = 0.46, 95% {CI: 0.36–0.58} p < 0.00001], aligning with earlier research [32, 33, 34]. The TOWER study demonstrated a notable decrease in new vertebral fractures in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis at elevated fracture risk, confirming the effectiveness of teriparatide in this group [35, 36]. The drug's efficacy was mainly independent of age, baseline BMD, and the prevalence of vertebral fractures [37]. Similar to earlier research, our findings revealed uniform outcomes among all age categories. The diminished effect in the oldest subgroup (≥ 75 years) might indicate age‐related decreases in bone turnover and response to anabolic treatments. There was significant risk reduction compared with alendronate, risedronate, however, no such difference was observed with zoledronic acid. Analysis of subgroups by age showed that teriparatide preserved its ability to prevent fractures in all age categories, with the most significant advantage seen in women between 55 and 75 years old. Nonetheless, the diminished effect in the oldest subgroup (≥ 75 years) might indicate age‐related decreases in bone remodeling and reaction to anabolic treatment [38].

Our research suggests that while teriparatide is useful for vertebral fractures, its influence on non‐vertebral fractures is less robust [RR = 0.81 {95% CI: 0.60–1.10} p‐value = 0.18] and may not reach statistical significance [39]. For example, although teriparatide has shown a reduction in the risk of non‐vertebral fractures by around 37%, this effect is infrequently statistically significant [40]. In contrast, several studies found that teriparatide significantly reduced non‐vertebral fragility fractures (RR 0.47, p = 0.02) only but did not influence traumatic and other fractures [41, 42]. Treatment persistence and adherence are key factors affecting fracture results, as shown by a study in Taiwan where patients with higher persistence (> 12 months) and adherence to teriparatide experienced a considerably reduced incidence of hip and non‐vertebral fractures in comparison to those with lower persistence and adherence [42]. These results emphasize the significance of continuing teriparatide therapy for effective fracture prevention.

Our study found that the increase in BMD at the lumbar spine and femoral neck was clinically and statistically non‐significant; however, the study by Fan et al. reported significant increases in lumbar spine BMD and femoral neck BMD in the long term. A meta‐analysis affirmed that teriparatide 20 mg/day substantially enhanced lumbar spine BMD [43]. Several studies have shown that teriparatide outperforms bisphosphonates, such as risedronate, in boosting BMD at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip [31, 44, 45, 46] [47]. In Indian postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, teriparatide is safe and effective in increasing BMD and bone turnover markers [48]. This finding is consistent with our study, where the marked rise in PINP levels, a marker of the anabolic effect of teriparatide, was found [RR = 2.63, 95% CI: {1.82–3.43} p < 0.00001]. However, more studies are needed to reinforce the effect of teriparatide on increasing BMD.

Importantly, there is a non‐significant increase in the frequency of adverse effects encompassing the gastrointestinal, respiratory, nervous, and urinary systems with teriparatide usage in post‐menopausal women. Previous reports of teriparatide‐related side effects have shown no statistically significant differences from control group events in many studies [49, 50]. Notably, the incidence of cardiac adverse events decreased with teriparatide use, but this difference did not achieve statistical significance. The use of teriparatide was associated with reduced CAD events compared to alendronate and fewer ACA events than zoledronic acid in a direct comparison; however, further studies are necessary to clarify the possible cardioprotective benefits of teriparatide [50, 51]. However, teriparatide use dramatically exacerbated the unfavorable effects on the musculoskeletal and endocrine systems. According to clinical investigations, teriparatide users may experience more painful cramping and spasming muscles [52]. Variations in thyroid metabolism and transient hypercalcemia are possible side effects of teriparatide. This elevation in serum calcium levels can result in symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and confusion. Monitoring serum calcium levels is recommended during teriparatide therapy [45, 53]. Our research additionally indicated that teriparatide led to increased complications and reduced mortality; however, this relationship was not statistically significant, permitting its ongoing application in clinical settings without increased worry over adverse effects [34, 39, 46]. In summary, our research indicates that although teriparatide significantly lowers the risk of vertebral fractures, heightened risks of side effects require thorough patient selection and observation. Future studies need to clarify the long‐term safety aspects of teriparatide and its effects on patient care.

Across most primary analyses in this meta‐analysis, statistical heterogeneity was low, particularly for vertebral fracture outcomes. Subgroup analyses categorized by age, follow‐up duration, and type of bisphosphonate comparator consistently showed I² values ranging from 0% to 21%, reflecting consistent and reliable treatment effects. These results indicate that the positive impact of teriparatide on decreasing vertebral fractures is mostly uniform across different population traits and research methodologies.

Conversely, results concerning BMD particularly lumbar spine BMD exhibited significant heterogeneity (I² reaching 97%), probably indicating differences in initial bone density, treatment protocols, study demographics, and measurement methods. To tackle this, we performed a sensitivity (leave‐one‐out) analysis that showed excluding one influential study (Ikeda et al.) significantly decreased heterogeneity (from 97% to 71%) and altered the overall effect to achieve statistical significance. This implies that the noted inconsistency could arise from methodological variations or distinct baseline traits in that particular study, instead of indicating a real difference in treatment effect.

For outcomes related to non‐vertebral fractures and femoral neck BMD, heterogeneity stayed low (I² usually < 25%), and subgroup analyses indicated that treatment effects were largely consistent across different age groups, follow‐up periods, or types of comparator drugs. This general trend suggests that although teriparatide significantly reduces fractures consistently, its influence on BMD especially in the lumbar spine can be more affected by study‐related variables, highlighting the necessity for standardized reporting, consistent BMD measurement methods, and larger, methodologically harmonized clinical studies.

Limitations

A major constraint of our research is the considerable variability seen among the trials included, which was not thoroughly investigated because our initial method lacked subgroup analyses or meta‐regression approaches. Despite conducting sensitivity analyses and utilizing a random‐effects model to address between‐study variation, these approaches were inadequate in clarifying or diminishing the significant level of variance seen in multiple outcomes. This variability is probably due to several factors, including differences in initial patient traits (such as age, osteoporosis severity, and comorbid conditions), variations in treatment regimens (medication type, dosage, and length), and discrepancies in the assessment and reporting of results among studies. This variability restricts the comparability of the studies included and diminishes the accuracy and trustworthiness of our combined estimates, thus limiting our capacity to make definitive or universally applicable conclusions.

A notable constraint is the inconsistency in methodological quality across the studies included. Multiple trials showed a moderate to high likelihood of bias, especially concerning allocation concealment, blinding, and selective reporting. The variation in study quality might have affected the direction and size of treatment effects, further undermining the reliability of our results. Moreover, negative effects were not uniformly or thoroughly documented in the studies, limiting our ability to effectively evaluate the complete safety profile of the drug being examined. The absence of uniform definitions and reporting structures for adverse events introduces additional uncertainty into our interpretations.

Subsequent studies ought to focus on executing thoroughly planned subgroup analyses and meta‐regression to more effectively pinpoint possible sources of heterogeneity and enhance the clarity of combined findings. Extended randomized studies featuring standardized outcome metrics, uniform reporting of adverse effects, and stringent methodological protections are crucial for delivering a more precise understanding of the effectiveness and safety of these treatments. Additionally, incorporating a wider range of populations like men, younger patients, and people from different geographic and ethnic backgrounds would improve the external validity and generalizability of upcoming results. Tackling publication bias and promoting transparent reporting by strictly following PRISMA and CONSORT guidelines will enhance the evidence base and increase the reliability of future meta‐analyses.

Conclusion

In conclusion, from the outcomes of a clinically significant result was generated in the group using teriparatide in various aspects. There are still some confounding factors present that need to be addressed to increase the yield of the study, and with increasing research in the field, more comprehensive studies are required in the future to reach a definitive outcome.
