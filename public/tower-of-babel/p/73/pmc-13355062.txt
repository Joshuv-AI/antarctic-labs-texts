ShapeRNA: an integrated web server for RNA secondary structure, ensemble, and functional analysis
Authors: Jialu Liang, Minghao Zhou, Mingyi Xie, Qianqian Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA secondary structure plays a critical role in gene regulation, yet existing computational and experimental tools for structure analysis are often fragmented across prediction, ensemble modeling, and functional interpretation workflows. Here, we present ShapeRNA, a user-friendly web server for integrated RNA secondary structure prediction, ensemble inference, and structure-aware regulatory annotation. ShapeRNA supports three complementary analytical workflows, including sequence-based structure prediction, reactivity-guided modeling using SHAPE or DMS data, and sequencing–guided ensemble inference from high-throughput probing experiments. The platform integrates multiple established prediction algorithms and provides standardized data processing, ensemble clustering, and visualization. In addition, ShapeRNA enables mapping of RNA modification sites, microRNA target regions, and RNA-binding protein interaction motifs onto predicted RNA structures and representative ensemble conformations. We demonstrate the utility of ShapeRNA through applications including analysis of mutation-associated structural changes in MAPT exon 10, characterization of conformational heterogeneity in the HIV-1 Rev Response Element, and regulatory annotation of the oncogenic long non-coding RNA HULC. ShapeRNA provides an accessible and extensible platform for investigating RNA structural heterogeneity and regulatory mechanisms. This website is free and open to all users, and there is no login requirement. The server is accessible at https://shaperna.com.

Introduction

RNA secondary structure plays a fundamental role in post-transcriptional gene regulation by modulating RNA stability, localization, translation, and molecular interactions. Structural elements such as hairpins, stem–loops, and pseudoknots regulate key RNA processing events, including splicing, polyadenylation, RNA editing, and degradation, and influence interactions with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and other nucleic acids [1–4]. Disruption of RNA structural elements can contribute to disease pathogenesis [5, 6], as sequence variants may alter functional RNA conformations without affecting protein-coding sequences. Moreover, many RNAs do not adopt a single static secondary structure but instead exist as dynamic ensembles of alternative conformations. Different conformations within an ensemble can differentially regulate the accessibility of functional elements and molecular interaction sites, underscoring the importance of ensemble-level characterization of RNA structure [7, 8]. Consequently, systematic characterization of RNA secondary structure, particularly at the ensemble level, is essential for understanding RNA-mediated gene regulatory mechanisms and their dynamic functional behavior.

Motivated by the central regulatory roles of RNA structure, numerous computational approaches have been developed for structure prediction and analysis, enabling three complementary types of investigation. Sequence-based methods infer RNA secondary structures directly from primary sequences, providing representative structures or sampling alternative conformations (e.g. RNAfold [9], RNAstructure [10]). Other approaches incorporate SHAPE- or DMS-derived probing reactivity profiles to refine structure prediction and characterize experimentally informed conformational variability (e.g. RNAstructure(Rsample) [11], Sfold [12]). More recently, sequencing-based probing technologies enable direct inference of RNA structural ensembles from high-throughput sequencing data, supporting ensemble-level sampling, clustering, and comparative analysis of coexisting RNA conformations (e.g. Seismic [13], DRACO [14]). Despite these advances, most existing tools are optimized for individual analytical tasks or specific data modalities.

Accumulating chemical probing studies have further revealed that many RNA molecules adopt multiple coexisting secondary conformations. Beyond structural heterogeneity, RNA secondary structure is tightly coupled to gene regulatory processes. Therefore, integrative analysis that jointly examines RNA structural ensembles and regulatory features is essential for understanding RNA-mediated gene regulation. However, user-friendly platforms that support unified structure inference, ensemble characterization, and downstream functional annotation remain limited.

To address these challenges, we developed ShapeRNA, a comprehensive and user-friendly web server for RNA secondary structure prediction and analysis. ShapeRNA supports sequence-based structure prediction, reactivity–guided structure modeling, and sequencing–guided ensemble inference within a unified analytical framework. The server provides an end-to-end environment that integrates structure inference, ensemble characterization, regulatory feature mapping, and interactive visualization starting from FASTA sequences or SHAPE/DMS-derived data. By streamlining the analysis of chemical probing–derived data and lowering technical barriers, ShapeRNA enables efficient and reproducible investigation of RNA structural heterogeneity and its regulatory implications.

Materials and methods

The ShapeRNA framework for RNA secondary structure prediction

To build the ShapeRNA server, we integrated representative RNA secondary structure prediction methods spanning major computational paradigms into a unified web-based platform, enabling flexible analysis under both sequence-only and chemical probing–guided settings.

Within the sequence-based structure prediction mode, ShapeRNA performs RNA secondary structure inference using sequence information alone. This mode supports both single-structure prediction and sequence-driven generation of multiple alternative structures, depending on the selected model. For single-structure inference, ShapeRNA integrates established folding implementations such as RNAfold [9], RNAstructure [10], Ufold [18], MXfold2 [19], KnotFold [20], REDfold [21], and SincFold [22], which output a representative secondary structure for a given RNA sequence. In addition, ShapeRNA supports sequence-based sampling of multiple conformations through models such as Sfold [12] and LinearSampling [23], enabling exploration of alternative structural states directly from sequence input.

The chemical probing reactivity–guided structure prediction mode integrates SHAPE- or DMS-derived nucleotide reactivity profiles to improve structure inference and characterize conformational variability. Probing information is integrated into folding or sampling procedures as reactivity-informed constraints or weighting schemes, enabling both single-structure refinement and ensemble-level analysis. For single-structure prediction, ShapeRNA provides models such as RME [24], Foldseq [25], RNAfold [9], and RNAstructure [10], which output one inferred secondary structure for a given RNA sequence. For ensemble-level analysis, ShapeRNA supports sequence-based structural sampling using models including Rsample [11] and LinearSampling [23], enabling generation and exploration of multiple alternative conformations directly from sequence input.

For chemical probing sequencing–guided ensemble inference mode, ShapeRNA enables end-to-end ensemble analysis directly from sequencing-derived probing data. This mode focuses on ensemble-level secondary structure sampling, clustering, and comparative analysis, rather than prediction of a single optimal structure. ShapeRNA supports workflows that process read-level probing signals to infer structural ensembles, with tools such as Seismic [13] and DRACO [14] facilitating integration from raw sequencing data to ensemble-resolved structural states.

All models are implemented through a standardized backend that harmonizes input handling and output formats (dot-bracket notation), ensuring compatibility with downstream annotation and visualization modules. Visualization and comparative structural analysis

For structural rendering and comparative analysis, the ShapeRNA server leverages the R2DT [29] and R4RNA [30] toolkits to deliver high-quality, interpretable visualizations of RNA secondary structures. Predicted structures, initially formatted in dot-bracket notation, are processed by R2DT to generate standardized, publication-ready 2D diagrams that preserve canonical base-pairing and loop arrangements. Server implementation and performance

The ShapeRNA web server is deployed on a Linux-based high-performance computing environment equipped with AMD EPYC 75F3 32-core processors and 64 GB RAM. The server supports parallel execution of multiple user-submitted jobs, with computational resources dynamically allocated under concurrent usage. For the sequence-based structure prediction mode, analyses for typical RNA sequences generally complete within 1–5 min, with runtime primarily scaling with sequence length under thermodynamic and learning-based models. For the chemical probing reactivity–guided mode, runtimes are comparable ( 1–5 min for typical inputs), with additional overhead depending on the size of the input reactivity profiles. For the sequencing-guided ensemble inference mode, workflows require longer runtimes due to read processing and alignment steps, typically 10–60 min depending on input size, read depth, and computational load.

Results

Overview of ShapeRNA platform

ShapeRNA is an integrated and user-friendly web server for RNA secondary structure prediction, ensemble modeling, and structure-aware regulatory analysis. The platform supports sequence-based structure prediction, chemical probing reactivity–guided structure modeling, and chemical probing sequencing–guided ensemble inference, enabling unified analysis of individual RNA secondary structures and multiple coexisting conformations within a single analytical framework.

As illustrated in Fig. 1A–C, ShapeRNA provides three complementary modeling workflows. In the sequence-based prediction mode (Fig. 1A), the server predicts the minimum free energy (MFE) structure and structural ensemble directly from RNA sequences provided in FASTA format. In the chemical probing reactivity–guided prediction mode (Fig. 1B), ShapeRNA integrates SHAPE or DMS reactivity profiles with sequence information to refine structure prediction and ensemble inference. In the chemical probing sequencing–guided ensemble inference mode (Fig. 1 C), ShapeRNA accepts FASTQ files from structure probing sequencing experiments, estimates mutation-derived reactivities using read-level statistical models, and identifies distinct structural clusters representing alternative RNA conformations. These complementary workflows allow ShapeRNA to accommodate diverse data types ranging from sequence-only inputs to high-throughput experimental datasets. Users initiate analysis through the “Tool” page by uploading input data and selecting prediction models such as RNAfold, RNAstructure, or Seismic. Predicted structures and ensembles can then be directly transferred to downstream analysis modules.

Beyond structure prediction, ShapeRNA provides integrated functional annotation and visualization tools to facilitate biological interpretation (Fig. 1D–H). The “Analysis” page accepts RNA secondary structures in standard dot–bracket notation and maps regulatory features, including m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^6$\end{document}A modification sites, miRNA target regions, and RBP motifs onto predicted structures and ensembles (Fig. 1D–F). ShapeRNA further supports interactive visualization of individual RNA structures using two-dimensional structure diagrams (Fig. 1G) and comparative analysis of alternative conformations using arc-based representations (Fig. 1H). By integrating prediction, ensemble characterization, regulatory annotation, and visualization within a single platform, ShapeRNA streamlines RNA structural analysis and enables reproducible, interpretable investigation of RNA structural and regulatory landscapes. Use Case 1: ShapeRNA analyzes RNA secondary structure changes in MAPT exon 10

To demonstrate the functionality of ShapeRNA for investigating RNA secondary structure changes, we examined a well-characterized alternative splicing event involving exon 10 of the MAPT gene. MAPT encodes the microtubule-associated protein tau, and dysregulation of exon 10 splicing alters the balance between three-repeat (3R) and four-repeat (4R) tau isoforms, a process implicated in tauopathies, including Alzheimer’s disease (Fig. 2A) [31–34]. Five experimentally validated intronic splicing silencer (ISS) point mutations upstream of exon 10 (U→G, C→U, A→G, C→U, and C→U), each promoting exon 10 inclusion, were jointly analyzed to assess their combined effects on local RNA secondary structure. The ISS region and mutation sites are highlighted in (Fig. 2B), with mutated nucleotides shown in bold red in the predicted mutant structure. To explore structural mechanisms underlying exon 10 dysregulation, we applied the sequence-based prediction workflow (Fig. 1A) to generate secondary structures for both wild-type and mutant MAPT exon 10 regulatory regions. Wild-type and mutant sequences covering the MAPT exon 10 regulatory region, including 95 nt upstream and 28 nt downstream of the 5′ splice site, were analyzed using RNAfold with default parameters, as implemented in ShapeRNA, to enable comparative structural analysis.

The predicted structures show that both wild-type and mutant sequences preserve a stem–loop architecture associated with the ISS. However, notable differences emerge in their global folding organization and spatial arrangement of regulatory elements. In the mutant structure (Fig. 3A), the ISS and 5′ splice site are separated into two distinct structural segments, each highlighted by independent boxed regions. In contrast, the wild-type structure (Fig. 3B) adopts a more compact configuration in which the ISS and 5′ splice site are integrated within a single structural unit. This structural reorganization is accompanied by a higher predicted MFE for the mutant structure (−20.6 kcal/mol) compared with the wild type (−29.6 kcal/mol), suggesting reduced overall structural stability. Although the local secondary structure within the ISS remains broadly similar between sequences, differences in surrounding pairing interactions and long-range connectivity produce distinct folding topologies within the exon 10 regulatory region. Detailed characterization of the local structural features is provided in Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. S1. Additional validation against experimentally determined structures and base-pairing patterns is provided in Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. S2. The ensemble-based structural variability is further characterized in Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. S3.

Use Case 2: ShapeRNA characterizes structural heterogeneity of the HIV Rev Response Element

To demonstrate the functionality of ShapeRNA to resolve RNA secondary structure ensembles, we analyzed the HIV-1 Rev Response Element (RRE), a well-characterized regulatory RNA that adopts multiple alternative secondary conformations. These conformations are associated with distinct Rev binding and multimerization behaviors that are essential for HIV-1 replication [35–39].

To investigate RRE structural heterogeneity, we applied the chemical probing sequencing–guided ensemble inference workflow (Fig. 1C) implemented in ShapeRNA, to perform ensemble-based structure sampling, clustering, and comparative analysis. We analyzed a 91-nt region (positions 7410–7500) of the HIV-1 genome using raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) [40] processed with the ShapeRNA sequencing-guided ensemble inference module (Seismic workflow [13]) with default parameters in ShapeRNA for clustering and structure inference. The inferred structures recapitulate known alternative conformations, with two dominant states, Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Fig. 4), each representing a thermodynamically and topologically distinct folding state within the structural ensemble. Cluster 1 exhibits an extended branched architecture with multiple helices radiating from a central stem, whereas Cluster 2 adopts a more compact fold characterized by long-range base pairing across distal sequence regions. The predicted MFE structures associated with Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 were −47.5 kcal/mol and -40.1 kcal/mol, respectively. The modest energy difference between the two conformations suggests that both structures are thermodynamically feasible and likely co-exist within the RRE structural ensemble.

Discussion and conclusion

ShapeRNA was designed as an integrated and accessible platform for RNA secondary structure and ensemble analysis, with the goal of reducing fragmentation across structure prediction, experimental data integration, and downstream functional interpretation. By organizing diverse modeling strategies into clearly defined workflows, ShapeRNA enables users to perform sequence-based structure prediction, chemical probing reactivity–guided modeling, and sequencing-based ensemble inference within a single analytical framework. This integration goes beyond simple aggregation of existing tools by enabling coordinated analysis across sequence-based prediction, experimental probing data processing, ensemble inference, and structure-aware regulatory annotation within a unified workflow. Existing platforms typically focus on individual steps of this process and require manual integration across multiple tools. A detailed comparison with representative platforms is provided in Supplementary Table S2, with additional discussion of methodological positioning and integration strategies provided in Supplementary Note 7.

A defining feature of ShapeRNA is its explicit support for ensemble-level analysis. Rather than focusing solely on a single optimal structure, ShapeRNA facilitates systematic exploration of multiple coexisting RNA conformations inferred from sequence data or chemical probing experiments. Beyond structure modeling, ShapeRNA integrates structure-aware functional annotation and visualization, mapping regulatory features, such as RNA modification sites, miRNA target regions, and RBP interaction motifs directly onto predicted structures and representative ensemble conformations. Together with interactive visualization tools, this integration enables users to examine structural features and associated regulatory signals in a unified and intuitive manner.

RNA secondary structure prediction remains inherently limited in accuracy, with thermodynamic approaches typically achieving only moderate base-pair recovery rates [46–48]. In addition, computational prediction of regulatory features is subject to uncertainty, as miRNA, RBP, and m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^6$\end{document}A predictions are influenced by context-dependent factors and may not fully reflect in vivo conditions [49–51]. These limitations highlight that predictions from these approaches should be interpreted with caution, particularly in the absence of experimental validation, as they may be subject to bias and context-dependent variability.

ShapeRNA builds upon established third-party tools for structure prediction, ensemble sampling, and regulatory annotation, ensuring compatibility with widely used methods. The current implementation emphasizes SHAPE- and DMS-based probing data and visualizes representative conformations derived from inferred ensembles. More comprehensive ensemble-level representations, including Boltzmann-weighted structural views and base-pair probability matrices, are not yet fully supported and represent natural directions for future development. Overall, ShapeRNA provides a modular and extensible framework for RNA structural and functional analysis, lowering the barrier to comprehensive RNA structure–function studies. Ongoing and future development will focus on expanding support for additional probing modalities, enhancing ensemble-aware modeling and visualization, and improving computational scalability for large-scale analyses.
