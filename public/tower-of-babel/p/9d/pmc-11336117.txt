Physico-functional properties, structural, and nutritional characterizations of Hodgsonia heteroclita oilseed cakes
Authors: Chanakan Khemthong, Uthaiwan Suttisansanee, Suwilai Chaveanghong, Chaowanee Chupeerach, Sirinapa Thangsiri, Piya Temviriyanukul, Yuraporn Sahasakul, Chalat Santivarangkna, Rungrat Chamchan, Amornrat Aursalung, Nattira On-nom
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The physicochemical and functional properties, structures, and nutritional characterizations of Hodgsonia heteroclita oilseed cake powder (OCP) obtained from oil extraction with no pretreatment (NP), heat pretreatment (HP; drying at 55 °C until reaching 10% moisture content), and the combined heat and enzymatic pretreatment (HEP; 2.98% (w/w) enzyme loading, 48 °C of incubation temperature and 76 min of incubation time) were investigated. HP and HEP caused a decrease in lightness but an increase in the yellow–brown color of OCP. The results showed that HEP-OCP had significantly lower oil and water absorption index, pasting properties and gelatinization enthalpy while higher water solubility index, foaming and emulsifying properties than NP-OCP and HP-OCP. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray diffraction indicated a small change in the protein secondary structure after pretreatment. Moreover, depending on pretreatment method, OCP samples contained a significant difference in nutritional values. However, all OCP samples could be claimed as high protein sources, containing all 9 essential amino acids and 9 non-essential amino acids. Finally, HEP-OCP seemed to have suitable properties to use as a potential ingredient in various food products such as soups, sauces, ice-cream, mousses, chiffon cakes and whipped toppings.

Introduction

Hodgsonia heteroclita subsp. Indochinensis (H. heteroclita) is a wild plant found in north-east India, China, Bangladesh, Nepal, and in hills of upper Malaysia, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Vietnam, Java, Sumatra and Thailand1. H. heteroclita is a climber plant that grows well in hilly terrain with a tropical wet climate. In Thailand, it is locally known as Ma-king2. The seeds are consumed as food and used as traditional medicine by locals. Roasting or baking is needed for seed consumption as snacks or incorporation into various types of food1. This seed contains high contents of fat (32.5–39.16%), followed by protein (26.6–27.6%), and total carbohydrate (19.1–23.0%)3, respectively. While high oil content is extracted3, oilseed extraction produces oilseed cakes as by-products. The industry market analytics, research, and consulting reported that the market value of global vegetable oil is estimated to reach USD 3,700 million by 20264, with the burden of oilseed cake creating an environmental challenge for waste management. Utilizing this oilseed cake may offer economic advantages, in line with the zero-waste concept, in which by-products should be recomposited and used as ingredients for new products5.

Soybeans contain high nutritive values (essential fatty acids, crude fiber, vitamins, and minerals), and their oilseed cake could be used as a functional ingredient of food products as a source of protein6,7. Despite several nutritional and health benefits, soybeans are classified as an allergenic food source that can impact the life quality of soybean-sensitive patients8. Therefore, food industries are looking for alternative ingredients that can replace soybeans without affecting food flavor, color and texture. Several studies reported that cold-pressed oilseed cakes of sunflower, flaxseed, pumpkin, hemp, or sacha inchi contain 19.9–44.9%, 14.4–41.9%, 29.4–53.9%, 23.5–33.6% and 32.62% crude proteins, respectively9–11. These oilseed cakes also possess different compositions and physicochemical characteristics, depending on the initial raw materials, variety, growing condition, and extraction methods.

Our previous study on different oil extraction methods from H. heteroclita seed indicated that extraction with heat and enzymatic pretreatment provided a similar oil yield to that obtained from Soxtec extraction but with better oil quality3. The by-product of this process is oilseed cakes, accounting for 33–53% of oil production. Information on other oilseed cakes suggested that these press cakes might have potential food applications in meat analogs, baking products, and as an encapsulating material in the spray-drying process5,7. However, missing information on the physicochemical, functional, morphological, and structural properties as well as nutritional compositions of H. heteroclita oilseed cake limits its applications in the food industry. Therefore, the aim of this research was to explore the effect of pretreatment methods on physicochemical and functional properties, structures, and nutritional compositions of H. heteroclita oilseed cake powder (OCP) derived from oil extraction with no pretreatment (NP), heat pretreatment (HP), and the combined heat and enzymatic pretreatment (HEP). The results presented in this study provide valuable preliminary information on OCP and its potential applications in the food sector. Moreover, this research aligns with the growing consumer awareness of the zero-food waste concept, contributing to the effective utilization of resources, particularly in the context of H. heteroclita oil extraction, thereby reducing food waste and enhancing sustainability.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

H. heteroclita fruits were collected by Mr. Anan Kaewwongsa in Hui-Nam-Guen village, Weing-Pa-Pao district, Chiang Rai Province, Thailand (19°50′54″N 100°9′12″E), during August–October 2021. The sample was for academic proposes and collected following the guidelines and regulations of the legislation with the consent of Mahidol University and the Plant Varieties Protection Department of Agriculture, Thailand (No. 0551/2567). The sampling complies with the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction12, the Convention on Biological Diversity13 and the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora14. The samples were authenticated and identified by Assist. Prof. Dr. Bhanubong Bongcheewin, Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (Bangkok, Thailand), according to reliable references15. The plants were deposited at Sireeruckhachati Nature Learning Park, Mahidol University, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand with an assigned voucher specimen as PBM 005,646. H. heteroclita oilseeds were collected by removing the hard shell and seed coat before keeping them at -20 °C. The proximate composition of H. heteroclita oilseed consisted of 39.16% fat, 26.58% protein, 13.79% total carbohydrates, 13.42% crude fiber, 18.77% moisture, and 2.41% ash, determined by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) method16 (detailed protocols in Nutritional values analysis section).

Sample preparation

The oilseed cakes were obtained following the procedure described by Piseskul et al.3. The oilseeds were blended using a Philips Daily Collection Blender (450W, 0.7 bowl) (model HR1393, Philips Electronics (Thailand) Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) for 30 s per 100 g sample. For the HP method, the blended oilseeds were dried in a Binder GMBH hot air oven (model FFD115/E2, Binder GmbH, Tuttlingen, Germany) at 55 °C until reaching 10% moisture content. In addition, the HEP method was performed using a mixture of Flavourzyme® and Viscozyme® (1:1, w/w) with extraction condition of 2.98% (w/w) enzyme loading, 48 °C of incubation temperature and 76 min of incubation time. The enzymatic activity was inactivated at 90 °C for 5 min. Then, oil extraction was performed using a rotary screw extractor (model T3, Nature Health and Innovation Co., Ltd., Saraburi, Thailand). The oilseed cakes from three different extraction conditions (mechanical extraction with NP, HP, and HEP methods) were collected and dried at 60 °C for 6 h. The samples were finely ground using a Philips 600 W series grinder (Philips Electronics Co., Ltd., Jakarta, Indonesia) to obtain three types of oilseed cake powder (OCP): These included no pretreatment oilseed cake powder (NP-OCP), heat pretreatment oilseed cake powder (HP-OCP), and a combination of both (HEP-OCP). For subsequent analysis, all samples were stored at − 20 °C.

Pasting properties

Pasting properties were investigated using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA) (Newport Scientific, Warriewood, Australia). A powder sample (2.5 g) was weighed in an RVA canister and added water (25 mL). The resulting slurry in the canister was subjected to a controlled heating process, starting at 50 °C, accompanied by agitation for 10 s to ensure complete dispersion. The slurry was then kept at 50 °C for 1 min, after which the temperature was elevated to 95 °C and held constant for 7.12 min, followed by a subsequent 2-min holding period at 50 °C. A comprehensive analysis of pasting properties was determined, including Peak viscosity (PV, the maximum paste viscosity achieved during the heating phase), breakdown (BD, the difference between peak viscosity and trough viscosity), final viscosity (FV, the paste viscosity upon completion of cooling), setback (SB, the index of the viscosity increase during cooling), trough viscosity (TV, the granule disruption resulting in a decrease in paste viscosity), peak time (PTime, the time taken to reach PV), and pasting temperature (PT, the temperature when the viscosity began to rise).

Thermal properties

The thermal properties were determined by employing a Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) 214 Polyma (Netzsch, Selb, Germany) following the method described by Vidal et al.20 with some modifications. The sample (5 mg) was mixed with distilled water (15 μL) in hermetic aluminium DSC pans. The thermal program initiated with an isothermal phase at 20 °C for 2 min, followed by a controlled heating phase from 20 to 200 °C, progressing at a rate of 10 °C/min. An empty pan served as a reference, and system calibration was carried out using indium. The assessment encompassed the determination of gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) and the investigation of transition temperatures, including the peak temperature (Tpeak) and the onset temperature (Tonset). ΔH was quantified and expressed as joules per gram (J/g) of the dry sample, representing the total area of the main endothermic peak.

Microstructure properties

The sample microstructure was examined via a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (JEOL JSM-IT500, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) according to the previous method21. The samples were placed onto aluminium stubs covered with double-sided adhesive tape and underwent gold coating through a sputter coating technique (2 min, 2 mbar). SEM images were captured under an acceleration voltage of 10 kV at magnifications of 700× and 1500× .

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

All spectra of the OCP samples were measured using the Attenuated Total Reflectance Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectrophotometer (Nicolet™ iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). All spectra were recorded in the transmission mode. The transmission technique was applied to conduct 64 scans for each sample in the 4000–400 cm−1 spectral range. A background was collected for every new sample. Samples to be analyzed are placed directly on the diamond crystal of ATR-FTIR. There is minimal sample preparation since it is ATR-FTIR. OCP samples were kept in aluminum foil bags, which were subsequently placed in a desiccator until the initiation of FTIR analysis to prevent the absorption of moisture from the atmosphere22.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern

The crystalline structures were analyzed using a D2 Phaser XRD analyzer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) with the degree of crystallinity of samples evaluated by DIFFRAC.EVA software follows the procedure of the previous study23 with some modifications. Prior to the analysis, the samples were allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for 24 h. The XRD patterns were recorded under the conditions of 30 kV and 10 mA, employing Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 nm) within an angular range spanning from 4° to 50° signals with a reflection angle of 2θ. The relative crystallinity was quantified as the ratio of the area of the crystalline peak to the area of the entire diffraction pattern.

Nutritional values

All samples were examined for nutritional compositions at the Accredited Laboratory of the Institute of Nutrition, Mahidol University (Nakhon Pathom, Thailand), adhering to ISO/IEC 17,025:2017 standards. The proximate compositions and mineral contents were determined in compliance with the AOAC method16. Briefly, the moisture content was determined using a hot-air drying approach (AOAC 931.04). The total fat content was analyzed using acidic digestion and petroleum ether extraction (AOAC 922.06), while the Kjeldahl method and a conversion factor of 6.25 were utilized for protein content analysis (AOAC 991.20). Incineration of organic matter was used to examine the ash content (AOAC 930.30). The crude fiber content was determined using the Enzymatic Gravimetric method (AOAC 985.29). Total carbohydrate was determined using a differential method involving the subtraction of the sum of moisture, protein, fat, and ash contents from 100. The energy value was calculated by summing the total energy content of total carbohydrate, protein, and fat, taking into account the specific values of 4, 4, and 9 kcal/g for the samples. For mineral content analysis, the ash residue was examined for calcium, sodium, and potassium using a Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS) (Thermo Electron Corporation, Cambridge, UK) following AOAC 985.35. For vitamin content analysis, vitamins B1 (thiamine), C, and E were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The determination of vitamin B1 was carried out following the in-house methods outlined in AOAC 942.23. Vitamin C content was examined according to the methods described by the previous procedure24. For vitamin E analysis, in-house methods similar to those presented by previous procedures were used25.

The results, presented per 100 g fresh weight (FW), can be found in Supplementary Table 1. In order to ensure a precise comparison of the nutritive values among the three pretreated samples, compositions were recalculated per 100 g dry weight (DW).

Fatty acid and amino acid profiles

The fatty acid profile was analyzed using Gas Chromatography (GC) according to an in-house method, TE-CH-208, based on AOAC (2019)16 996.06. Amino acid profiles were determined using an amino acid analyzer (AAA) (Bio 30 + series, Biochrom Ltd, Cambridge, UK) using an in-house method TE-CH-372 based on the Official Journal of the European Journal of Communities, L25726. The fatty acid and amino acid profiles were conducted by the Analytical Service at the Central Laboratory (Thailand) Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed using three sets of independent samples in triplicate (n = 3). The data were statistically analyzed with the SPSS software package version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Mean differences of p < 0.05 were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple comparison test.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties

The physicochemical properties of OCP samples are shown in Table 1. Production yield ranged from 33.37 to 52.74%. HEP-OCP showed a significantly lower yield than NP-OCP and HP-OCP, which could be due to the hydrolysis of plant cell walls by HEP. Moreover, HEP might also cause protein denaturation, leading to more remaining oil in the OCP3. The aw values of all OCP samples ranged from 0.07 to 0.26. The NP-OCP and HP-OCP exhibited significantly higher aw values than HEP-OCP, possibly due to its higher protein and ash contents (acting as humectants), resulting in a reduction in aw (Table 4). It was also previously reported that the decrease in aw might result from the high-water absorption capacity of protein8. A higher aw indicates a higher risk of deterioration in flour quality, which can affect product shelf life27. The pH values of all OCP samples ranged from 5.58 to 6.43. A significantly lower pH in HP-OCP and HEP-OCP than that of NP-OCP was possibly related to fat and protein degradation into fatty acids and amino acids, respectively. The presence of these acids can affect the pH of the sample28. Besides, the oil extraction condition seemed to influence the color of all samples, especially HEP-OCP, which exhibited the lowest lightness (L*) with the highest redness (a*) and yellowness (b*). A decrease in lightness was related to longer heating time, compared to the other pretreatment methods. Heat accelerates a browning reaction, concurring with previous studies of chestnut flour29, which exhibited a decreased L* value after long heating process. The bulk density of all OCP samples were ranged from 0.72 to 0.78 g/L with the HEP-OCP exhibiting the significantly highest. High bulk density was related to moisture content and particle characteristic8. Functional properties

The functional properties of OCP samples were investigated and the results are shown in Table 2. The WAI measures the capacity of proteins to absorb and retain water, while the OAI assesses the ability of fats to bind with the non-polar side chains of proteins. The OAI and WAI of OCP samples varied from 1.62–1.74 g/g and 1.63–2.87 g/g, respectively, similar to kidney bean accessions31. The WAI and OAI values of HEP-OCP were lower than those of NP-OCP and HP-OCP, possibly because both hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups of OCP were destroyed during heat and enzymatic pretreatment. These results suggested that NP-OCP and HP-OCP might be used to process viscous foods such as soups, gravies, sauces, custards, and baked products31. The WSI represents the quantity of soluble solids or degraded molecules within the samples32. As a result, HEP-OCP provided the highest WSI (36.27%), which could be due to the high soluble protein content. The FC, FS, and emulsifier properties (EA and ES) of all OCP samples were varied in the range of 6.54–103.92%, 51.29–98.16%, 9.61–34.52% and 28.41–52.47%, respectively. The HEP-OCP possessed the highest FC and FS, which are significant in food systems for aeration and whipping33. These values were even higher than those previously reported in peas, soybeans and kidney beans31,34. These results were also linked to WSI findings, as soluble proteins can effectively reduce surface tension at the air bubble interface, resulting in a reorientation and form strong cohesive interfacial membranes. Pasting properties

The pasting properties of all OCP samples are shown in Table 3. The PV, TV, BD, FV, and SB were varied in the range of 6.97–169.83 RVU, 3.81–36.47 RVU, 3.17–133.36 RVU, 7.53–42.08 RVU and 2.83–5.61 RVU, respectively. The HEP-OCP exhibited lower PV, TV, BD, FV, and SB than NP-OCP and HP-OCP, possibly due to its lower swelling powder and less release and re-association of solubilized starch35. Moreover, the pasting properties of HEP-OCP were lower than those reported in Chinese yam, Tartary buckwheat, and proso millet (PV of 37.30–326.7 RVU, TV of 33.60–149.2 RVU, BD of 3.70–196.30 RVU, FV of 60.00–269.70 RVU, and SB of 28.3–154.60 RVU)36. Among OCP samples, HEP-OCP depicted the lowest BD, which could be considered a suitable ingredient in food products requiring increased viscosity after cooking, such as soups and sauces. In addition, HEP-OCP and HP-OCP with lower SB might be suitable for food products requiring low retrogradation properties during cooling, such as gels, sauces, and gravies. On the other hand, NP-OCP with the highest FV and SB might be suitable for food products requiring the formation of an organized structure (as a consequence of the reassociation and disassociated starch chains), such as pastas31. The PT and PTime of HEP-OCP were higher than those of NP-OCP and HP-OCP, indicating that HEP-OCP was stable during continued heating and shearing. Thermal properties

DSC is used to determine protein structural and conformational changes and thermal stability. Results in Table 3 show that all OCP samples had only one endothermic peak, with onset Tonset, Tpeak, and ΔH ranging from 95.73–98.00 °C, 115.67–118.30 °C, and 177.60–356.33 J/g, respectively. Our results were in the range of those reported in camellia seed cake protein and pea protein isolate (Tonset of 60.57–98.81 °C, Tpeak of 114.91–119.83 °C, ΔH of 154.90–223.80 °C)34,38. HP and HEP possibly increase Tonset and Tpeak of OCP through destabilization of the crystalline lamella and reduction of the amorphous regions, resulting in the leaching of amylose. The amylopectin chains could interact with the leached amylose, leading to limited molecule movement. The reduction of amylopectin chain mobility and chemical/interactions that occurred during HP and HEP caused an increase in gelatinization temperature39. In addition, the increased Tonset and Tpeak might also be due to the formation of amylose-amylose and amylose–lipid complexes in starch granules during HP and HEP21. HP-OCP and HEP-OCP showed a lower ΔH than NP-OCP. The reduction of ΔH during HP and EHP could be related to a partial disarrangement and gelatinization of the starch granules, re-ordering the disintegrated molecules, and increased associations among starch chains. These changes resulted in a decrease in the content of double-helical order and an elevated idealization of double-helical order, leading to a decrease in ΔH40.

Microstructure properties by SEM

The microstructure of OCP samples was investigated using SEM, as shown in Fig. 1. The OCP granular shape was polyhedral fragments with a rough and non-uniform surface and some pores. Our results concurred with those reported in wild apricot kernel press cake with similar morphology41. However, some structural damages on the granule surface, such as lumps and agglomerates, were shown in HP-OCP and HEP-OCP, possibly due to the deformation of originally dispersed non-starch components that expanded over the surface of the starch granules and lumps. These non-starch components adhered to the starch granules, causing them to clump together. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR is a valuable method for estimating the secondary structure of proteins and provides insights into their structural composition43. In this study, FTIR was employed to investigate the characterization of the secondary structure of OCP, as indicated in Fig. 2A. The protein presented typical amide vibrations at wavelengths ranging from 1100–1235 cm−1 (amide III, C–O and C–N stretching, O=C–N and N–H bending), 1456–1535 cm-1 (amide II, C–N stretching and N–H bending), 1640–1743 cm−1 (amide I, C=O stretching) and 3290 cm-1 (amide A, N–H stretching)43. No difference in the structure and chemical composition of NP–OCP, HP–OCP, and HEP–OCP was observed, as indicated by the similar patterns of FTIR spectra, indicating that no new functional groups were formed in pretreated OCP samples. The protein secondary structures were indicated as the peaks present in wavelength ranges as follows: 1640–1743 cm−1 (amide I), 1656–1674 (β-sheet), 1674–1696 cm−1 (random coil), 1696–1720 cm−1 (α-helix) and 1720–1740 cm−1 (β-turn). The peak shape fitting and area calculation were performed as shown in Supplementary Table 2. Compared with NP-OCP, a decrease in α-helix and β-sheet structures as well as an increase in β-turn and random coil structure in HP-OCP, were possibly explained by protein structural denaturation, resulting in some ordered secondary structures being transformed into unfolded or disordered structures. On the other hand, the HEP-OCP contains more β-sheet structure and lower amounts of α-helix, β-turn and random coil, suggesting that α-helix structure, β-turn structure, and random coil were possibly transformed into β-sheet structures34. Thus, different pretreatments yielded different secondary structural changes in OCP samples. Our results concurred with the previous report on wheat gluten protein44, in which different pretreatments (dry heating, aqueous heating, and ultrasound treatments) induced different secondary structural changes.Figure 2FTIR spectra (A), and XRD patterns (B) of H. heteroclita oilseed cake powder obtained from different pretreated mechanical extraction methods (a) no pretreatment (b) heat pretreatment, and (c) heat and enzymatic pretreatment.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns

Since food materials in solid states can exist in crystalline, semi-crystalline, or amorphous forms, the XRD patterns of OCP samples were investigated and depicted in Fig. 2B. All OCP samples showed a dominant amorphous halo, represented by a broad band with a maximum at 2θ = 20°, corresponding to the characteristic diffraction peaks observed in soy and pea protein isolates34. All OCP samples also demonstrated typical diffraction peaks of low intensity at 9°. The relative crystallinity percentages of NP-OCP, HP-OCP, and HEP-OCP were 14.90%, 14.91%, and 12.8%, respectively. The relative crystallinity of HEP-OCP was slightly lower than NP-OCP and HP-OCP, possibly due to reorientation of the crystallinities during heat and enzymatic pretreatment. Previous research has also reported a reduction in relative crystallinity during heat moisture treatment of proso millet flour and starch36. The diffraction patterns of OCP samples predominantly exhibited amorphous structures with low crystallinity. Therefore, these amorphous powders should be kept in a dry place to prevent caking due to their ability to attract and hold moisture45.

Nutritional properties

The nutritional composition of OCP samples varied depending on the specific pretreated mechanical extraction method, as shown in Table 4. Combining heat and enzymatic pretreatment resulted in the highest energy levels, fat, total carbohydrate, crude fiber, sodium, potassium, and vitamin E. The HEP-OCP displayed elevated energy content (480.40 kcal/100 g DW), primarily due to its remarkable fat content (21.36 g/100 g DW), which was 1.2–2.5 times higher compared to NP-OCP and HP-OCP, respectively. Higher fat levels were retained when using a lower extraction temperature and duration during HEP-OCP preparation. Employing a 2.98% (w/w) enzyme loading with extraction at 48 °C for 76 min, retained higher fat than HP-OCP with extraction at 55 °C for 90 min. Moreover, the total carbohydrate content of HEP-OCP (6.24 g/100 g DW) exceeded that of HP-OCP and NP-OCP by 1.3–1.4 times, with the highest crude fiber detected in HEP-OCP. However, protein content in HP-OCP was higher than in NP-OCP and HEP-OCP by 1.1–1.2 times.

HEP-OCP exhibited the lowest protein content (65.81 g/100 g DW), but its sodium content was 3.5–8.3 times greater than HP-OCP and NP-OCP, while potassium contents in HEP-OCP and HP-OCP were 1.1 times higher than detected in NP-OCP. The absence of pretreatment in NP-OCP yielded the lowest nutritional values across most parameters, except for calcium (99.88 mg/100 g DW), comparable to HP-OCP and 1.2 times higher than HEP-OCP.

Corresponding to its fat content, HEP-OCP contained 1.5–2.6 times higher vitamin E content than detected in NP-OCP and HP-OCP. Vitamin C was undetectable in HEP-OCP samples, while 1.71–2.26 mg vitamin C/100 g DW were detected in NP-OCP and HP-OCP, with vitamin B1 content in HP-OCP 1.1–1.4 times higher than in NP-OCP and HEP-OCP, respectively.

Vidal et al.20 revealed that OCP samples exhibited higher protein content (65.81–79.11%) than cold-pressed sunflower (31.7%), canola (35.2%), and soybean oilseed cakes (51.4%), while OCP samples demonstrated higher protein content than oilseed cakes of safflower (8.38–34.29%), rapeseed (24.96–33.52%), and sunflower (27.87–41.66%) concurring with Bárta et al.46. The differences possibly depended on the type of oilseed and extraction conditions. The OCP samples obtained in this study showed high potential as a functional plant protein source.

By contrast, OCP samples had relatively lower crude fiber content (4.56–5.38%) compared to oilseed cakes from soybean (22.5%), canola (35.8%), and sunflower (41.1%)20. This discrepancy could be due to hull remnants within the oilseed cakes. For ash content, OCP samples (6.66–7.55%) aligned with hemp (4.22–7.65%) and sunflower (5.25–7.42%) oilseed cakes46. Ash content might be related to the mineral composition in the samples. OCP samples showed low calcium (85.32–99.88 mg/100 g DW) and sodium (21.12–175.41 mg/100 g DW) but possessed abundant potassium (1458.50–1602.72 mg/100 g), equivalent to approximately 42–46% of the Thai Recommended Daily Intakes (Thai RDIs)47. Regarding vitamin contents, OCP samples contained low levels of vitamins B1, C, and E. This observation can be attributed to the influence of pretreatment and mechanical extraction conditions, which may trigger the degradation or loss of these sensitive vitamins.

Amino acid profile

The amino acid profile (g/100 g) indicated that all OCP samples contained 18 amino acids, including all 9 essential and 9 non-essential amino acids, as shown in Table 6. Among these, glutamic acid (Glu), arginine (Arg), aspartic acid (Asp), and leucine (Leu) were predominant, accounting for 3.48, 2.83, 1.60, and 1.38 times higher than the other amino acids. Our result concurred with Zhang et al.38, who indicated high amounts of Glu, Gsp, Arg, and Leu in camellia (Camellia oleifera Abel.) seed cake protein. High Glu and Asp contents show potential food applications as flavor enhancers due to their umami taste52. Interestingly, each amino acid detected in HP-OCP was significantly higher than those detected in NP-OCP and HEP-OCP, leading to significantly higher total amino acids (TAA) in HP-OCP (p < 0.05). Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effect of pretreatment methods in oil extraction on the physicochemical and functional properties, structure and nutritional values of OCP. A decrease in lightness but an increase in yellow–brown color were observed in OCP with HP and HEP. HEP seemed to have improved solubility, foaming, emulsifying and pasting properties of OCP. HEP-OCP also showed the lowest ΔH compared to NP-OCP and HP-OCP. The XRD patterns showed slightly lower relative crystallinity in HEP-OCP, and slightly change in the secondary structure was observed by FTIR analysis. In addition, there were significant differences in nutritional values among OCP samples. All OCP samples have a high potential as a functional plant protein source, including all 9 essential and 9 non-essential amino acids. HEP may, therefore, be a suitable method to enhance the physicochemical and functional properties of OCP as a potential ingredient in a variety of food products such as soups, sauces, ice-cream, mousses, chiffon cakes, and whipped toppings.
