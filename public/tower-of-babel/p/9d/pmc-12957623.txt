Results of a phase II open-label, multiple-dose study of vamorolone (VBP15-006) in 7- to < 18-year-old boys with duchenne muscular dystrophy
Authors: Hanns Lochmüller, Hernan Gonorazky, Elisa Nigro, Jean K. Mah, Alberto Alemán, Amanda Yaworski, Maryam Oskoui, Anne Marie Sbrocchi, Kathryn Selby, Ana de Vera, Laura McAdam, Ekaterina Gresko, Aki Linden, Catherine Dutreix, Eric P. Hoffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVamorolone is a dissociative glucocorticoid receptor agonist for treating Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). The VBP15-006 study assessed vamorolone safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics in 2- to < 4 and 7- to < 18-year-old boys with DMD. Results for 7- to < 18-year-old boys, either corticosteroid (CS)-untreated or switching from prior CS treatment, are reported here. Exploratory objectives included efficacy and pharmacodynamic biomarkers related to safety.MethodsIn this phase II, open-label, multiple-dose study, participants received 2 or 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone for 12 weeks, followed by treatment in an expanded access protocol (EAP Canada).Results34 participants (12 CS-untreated, 22 CS-treated) aged 7- to < 18 years were enrolled. Most treatment-emergent adverse events were mild. All 34 participants completed VBP15-006 and entered EAP Canada. During EAP Canada, CS-untreated participants maintained stable linear growth, while CS-treated participants exhibited catch-up growth consistent with serum bone biomarkers (median total vamorolone exposure 1.3 and 1.7 years, respectively). Some weight gain occurred, especially in CS-untreated participants receiving 6 mg/kg/day. Dose-dependent adrenal suppression occurred in CS-untreated and CS-treated participants; 2 individuals who switched from deflazacort to vamorolone 2 mg/kg/day had generalized weakness consistent with adrenal insufficiency. Vamorolone showed dose-dependent pharmacokinetics, rapid clearance, and no accumulation. There were no relevant efficacy changes in CS-treated and CS-untreated groups at either dose.ConclusionsVamorolone demonstrated a consistent safety profile in 7- to < 18-year-old boys with DMD. Switching to 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone appeared to mitigate adrenal insufficiency risk. There was no negative effect on growth, and catch-up growth occurred in previously CS-treated individuals switching to vamorolone.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05185622, NCT03863119. Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked recessive neuromuscular disorder caused by mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes the protein dystrophin [1, 2]. DMD symptoms include progressive muscle weakness eventually leading to loss of ambulation and later complications, such as cardiomyopathy and respiratory weakness [3].

The mainstay of DMD treatment includes classic corticosteroids (CS), such as prednisone/prednisolone and deflazacort. These glucocorticoid receptor (GR) agonists have been shown to initially improve DMD symptoms for 1 to 2 years and then slow the progressive decline seen in all individuals with DMD [4–6].

Long-term classic CS use can result in adrenal suppression and cause iatrogenic adrenal insufficiency, especially during increased physiological stress such as infections or trauma. This necessitates the administration of stress doses of CS such as hydrocortisone (HC) as per clinical guidelines [7, 8]. Prednisone and deflazacort are also associated with significant bone morbidities (particularly vertebral compression fractures), growth stunting, and delay of puberty, and can result in serious mood disturbances [3, 9]. There remains an unmet medical need to maintain the efficacy of CS treatment while minimizing the associated adverse effects [10].

Vamorolone is a dissociative CS used for DMD treatment that retains the efficacy of glucocorticoids while mitigating key side effects via three structure/activity relationships that differ from prednisone, deflazacort, and other CS. First, vamorolone is not a substrate of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases 1 and 2, the metabolic enzymes that carry out pro-drug/drug conversions of all other CS [11, 12]. Second, vamorolone interacts with the cognate glucocorticoid receptor in a unique manner, with a weaker co-activator binding but a slightly stronger co-repressor binding compared to classic CS [13]. Third, as an established antagonist of the mineralocorticoid receptor, vamorolone may provide additional cardio-protective benefits although this remains to be investigated [14, 15].

Vamorolone has been approved in the USA since 2023 for the treatment of individuals with DMD ≥ 2 years old, with a 6 mg/kg/day recommended dose and maximum daily dose of 300 mg/kg/day in individuals > 50 kg [16]. In the European Union (EU), the United Kingdom, Canada, China, and Hong Kong, vamorolone is approved in individuals ≥ 4 years of age with recommended 6 mg/kg/day dosage and a dose cap of 240 mg/day for individuals weighing > 40 kg [17–19].

The clinical trials of vamorolone in DMD to date include two studies in steroid-naïve, 4- to < 7-year-old boys with DMD. The first was an open-label dose ranging study of 48 boys with DMD, where participants were treated with vamorolone in 3 separate sequential protocols (VBP15-002, VBP15-003, VBP15-LTE) for a total of 2.5 years [20–23]. Vamorolone treatment was associated with similar beneficial effects on motor outcomes compared to other CS treatments, without stunting of growth or deleterious changes in serum bone biomarkers. The subsequent pivotal clinical study was a 48-week double blind, placebo- and prednisone-controlled trial of 121 boys with DMD [6, 24]. Vamorolone treatment (6 mg/kg/day) provided similar improvements in motor outcomes to prednisone, with no stunting of growth and less severe mood disturbances.

Until recently, there were no clinical trial data on the use of vamorolone in boys with DMD below 4 years of age, or ≥ 7 years of age who were either CS-untreated or had switched from standard-of-care CS treatment to vamorolone. The VBP15-006 clinical trial enrolled both younger (2- to < 4-year-old) and older (7- to < 18-year-old) DMD participants for a 3-month treatment period, with a following expanded access protocol (EAP) Canada study. Results from the 2- to < 4-year-old cohort are reported separately [Mah et al., 2025; manuscript under review]. This current manuscript describes initiation of vamorolone treatment in the 7- to < 18-years-old cohort, including participants who switched from prednisone or deflazacort to vamorolone on Day 1 of the study. A recent snapshot of limited data from the EAP Canada study for former VBP15-006 participants is also reported to provide more clinical context for vamorolone treatment effects over a longer time period.

Methods

Study design and objectives

The VBP15-006 study was a phase II, non-randomized, multiple-cohort, open-label study at 5 sites in Canada, designed to confirm safe and tolerable doses of vamorolone for 2- to < 4- and 7- to < 18-year-old boys with DMD, based on safety and pharmacokinetic (PK) data over 12 weeks of treatment. A new route of synthesis (ROS2) of the active pharmaceutical ingredient and formulation was used in this study (ROS2 was also used for the marketed formulation). Upon completion of the VBP15-006 study, participants were offered the opportunity to continue treatment with vamorolone in the EAP Canada study.

Participant population

Male individuals with a DMD diagnosis confirmed by genetic testing and/or dystrophin deficiency on muscle biopsy and evidence of immunity to varicella zoster virus were included. Participants were stratified based on age and prior classic CS treatment status and received 2 or 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone over 12 weeks. The first participants recruited were assigned to the 2 mg/kg/day group. Once the 2 mg/kg/day group was fully recruited within each age and previous CS treatment group, subsequent participants were assigned to the 6 mg/kg/day groups. Additional participants aged 12- to < 18 years were recruited following a protocol amendment to increase the proportion of boys above 12 years of age. The amendment was made after discussion with the European Medicines Agency to ensure adequate data were collected for individuals in this age group.

Treatment groups for the 7- to < 18-year-old participants were as follows: (1) CS-untreated (no oral CS or other oral immunosuppressive agents for at least 3 months prior to enrollment) assigned to 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone; (2) CS-untreated, assigned to 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone; (3) previously CS-treated (standard-of-care classic CS for treatment of DMD) with direct switch to vamorolone 2 mg/kg/day on day 1 of study; (4) previously CS-treated, with direct switch to vamorolone 6 mg/kg/day on day 1 of study (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria included any major renal or hepatic impairment, diabetes mellitus, or immunosuppression. Study procedures

Plasma samples for vamorolone PK analysis were collected pre- and post-dose (1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h post-dose) at day 1 and week 2. As the new ROS of vamorolone (ROS2) was used, before enrolling participants into the higher 6 mg/kg/day group, the data on PK and exposure from 2 mg/kg/day were compared with the data from the previous formulation (ROS1) in 4- to < 7-year-old boys with DMD [20]. Participants in the 6 mg/kg/day group weighing ≥ 50 kg received a maximum daily dose of 300 mg vamorolone after a protocol amendment midway during the trial. Upon completion of the 12-week study, participants were given the option of continuing vamorolone treatment as part of the EAP Canada study or reverting to standard-of-care treatment for DMD.

EAP Canada study

The ongoing open-label EAP Canada study (NCT03863119) was available to the participants who completed VBP15-006. The objective was to enable access to vamorolone prior to regulatory approval in Canada while reporting on safety. Individuals in EAP Canada continued their clinical care per standard-of-care for managing DMD with visits approximately every 6 months. The protocol of the EAP Canada study permitted a choice of 2, 4, and 6 mg/kg/day doses, and dose adjustments are at the discretion of the treating physician. Results from the EAP Canada study are therefore presented for the pooled 2–6 mg/kg/day dose group as individual participants may have received various doses (data cut-off: May 2025). This EAP was not designed to assess effectiveness outcomes (Fig. 1). The data on ambulation status of individual participants noted by investigators were therefore not pre-specified or systematically collected, and should not be considered confirmatory.

Objectives and endpoints of the VBP15-006 study

The primary objective was to evaluate vamorolone safety and tolerability at 2 and 6 mg/kg/day over the 12-week treatment period. The secondary objective was to evaluate vamorolone PK at 2 and 6 mg/kg/day and to confirm drug exposure. This study had several exploratory objectives, including efficacy, neuropsychology/behavioral and physical function parameters, study medication acceptability, and pharmacodynamic (PD) safety biomarkers.

Endpoints related to the primary objective included: (1) treatment-emergent adverse effects (TEAEs) and serious adverse effects (SAEs); (2) change from baseline (CBL) to week 12 in height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and vital signs; (3) Cushingoid features; (4) CBL to week 12 in clinical laboratory parameters and 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG); (5) clinically significant eye abnormalities, including cataracts. Adrenal suppression was assessed by measuring morning serum cortisol levels at baseline and at week 12. Changes in height, weight, and BMI were evaluated for the 12-week VBP15-006 study and the total duration of vamorolone treatment, which included VBP15-006 and the EAP Canada study at the time of the last available data snapshot.

Endpoints related to the secondary objective included plasma concentration of vamorolone at several time points (see study procedures), as well as the PK parameters peak concentration (Cmax), area under the curve (AUC), time to peak concentration (Tmax), and terminal elimination half-life (t½).

Endpoints related to exploratory objectives included the following assessments and CBL to week 12: efficacy by Performance of Upper Limb (PUL) test, Personal Adjustment and Role Skills scale, v3 (PARS III)), Pediatric Outcomes Data Collection Instrument (PODCI); study medication acceptability evaluated directly before (smell) and after (taste) dosing on a 5-point hedonic scale; PD biomarkers of safety to assess adrenal suppression (morning serum cortisol levels), measured using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay in a cobas® e602 immunoanalyzer (Roche) with cut-offs of 65 nmol/L and 100 nmol/L defined as indicating adrenal suppression in participants < 12 and ≥ 12 years old, respectively; bone-turnover biomarkers, including serum osteocalcin, C-terminal peptide of type I collagen (CTX), and procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP); insulin-resistance biomarkers (fasting glucose and insulin, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c); gonadal axis (luteinizing hormone [LH], follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH]) and thyroid axis (thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH], free thyroxine [FT4].

Statistical methods

There was no formal sample size calculation for VBP15-006. The sample size was considered sufficient to detect drug safety concerns in a pediatric population and to determine PK parameters, based on clinical judgment and prior study of vamorolone at these doses. No formal statistical hypothesis was tested in this study and consequently no p values are presented. All measurements for both VBP15-006 and EAP Canada were analyzed based upon the type of distribution. Descriptive statistics are presented by treatment group and assessment time point.

During VBP15-006, height was recorded based on ulna length and additionally from standing height for ambulant participants. During EAP Canada, standing height data were collected for ambulant participants, but not for non-ambulant ones. For the longitudinal analysis of growth during VBP15-006 and EAP Canada, only data of participants with standing height were used. Weight was measured directly, and BMI calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) and only if both measurements were taken at the same visit. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS® v9.4 or later.

Results

Participant enrollment

A total of 54 participants were enrolled in VBP15-006, of whom 20 were 2- to < 4-year-olds and 34 were 7- to < 18-year-olds. The first enrolled on 21 March 2022, and the last completed the study on 17 June 2024. All 7- to < 18-year-old participants (N = 34) completed the VBP15-006 study and elected to participate in the EAP Canada study (Fig. 1). Three participants, all previously CS-treated, subsequently discontinued vamorolone during the EAP Canada study (Supplementary results). 31 participants continue to receive vamorolone as part of the ongoing EAP Canada study.

Previously CS-untreated

Both vamorolone dose groups of CS-untreated participants (n = 12) had a similar median age of 8 years. Past CS use was reported for 4 participants, 2 of whom had a medical history of psychiatric spectrum disorders (Supplementary Table S1). The remaining 8 participants were steroid-naïve. At baseline, participants were relatively short but within the expected range for their age and consistent with DMD, with median (Q1; Q3) height z-scores of −0.34 (−1.29; 0.35) for the 2 mg/kg/day group and 0.32 (0.18; 0.74) for the 6 mg/kg/day group (Table 1). BMI at baseline was reduced, with median z-scores of −1.73 and −2.07 for 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone and 6 mg/kg/day, respectively, suggestive of muscle atrophy. All except 2 participants receiving 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone were ambulatory (83.3%, n = 10/12) but with upper limb disability similar to older CS-treated boys (median PUL scores of 34.5 and 37.5 for the 2 mg/kg/day and 6 mg/kg/day subgroups respectively). Baseline comorbidities included one long bone fracture in 1 of 12 participants (8.3%) and endocrine disorders (Cushingoid) in 1 participant (8.3%); no cataracts were recorded.

Previously CS-treated, switched to vamorolone

In participants who switched to vamorolone from standard CS treatment (n = 22), 86% (n = 19/22) had previously received deflazacort (Supplementary Table 2). Boys allocated to the 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone group were older than those receiving 2 mg/kg/day (13.0 years, min/max range 8.3–18.0 vs 10.2 years, range 7.5–17.2) due to the recruitment of 11 additional older participants (12- to < 18 years) following the protocol amendment. The median treatment duration of previous CS treatment was correspondingly higher in the 6 mg/kg/day group – 93.1 months (7.6 years) compared to 54.1 months (5.3 years) in 2 mg/kg/day group. In the subgroup of CS-treated 12- to < 18-year-olds (n = 11), the median duration of previous exposure to CS was approximately 9 years (108.1 months, range 53.6–127.5 months) (Supplementary Table 2). A majority of participants were receiving reduced doses of previous CS at enrollment, ~ 65 to ~ 80% of therapeutic dose. In all, 83.3% (n = 5/6) of participants were ambulatory in the 2 mg/kg/day group compared with 62.5% (n = 10/16) in the 6 mg/kg/day group. The medical history of this cohort was consistent with previous exposure to CS and a history of DMD. Bone fractures (vertebral compression fracture, lower limb fracture) were reported in 37.5% (n = 6/16) of the 6 mg/kg/day dose group, with most (n = 5/6 participants with fractures) in the 12- to < 18-year-old subgroup (Supplementary Table 1). Cataracts were present in 45.5% (n = 10/22) participants, and in 63.6% (n = 7/11) of the 12- to < 18-year-old subgroup.

Consistent with longer previous CS exposure, growth delay was more pronounced in the vamorolone 6 mg/kg/day group than in the 2 mg/kg/day group, with a median (Q1; Q3) z-score of −2.15 (−2.83; −0.97) versus -0.6 (−0.73; −0.35). The 12- to < 18-year-old subgroup had severely stunted growth, with a median (Q1; Q3) z-score of −2.62 (−3.03; −1.89). At the end of the trial, the median duration of vamorolone exposure was similar across all 34 participants (12.0–12.4 weeks), consistent with the 12-week study duration and with all participants completing the study.

Changes in height, weight, and BMI

Previously CS-untreated

There were no relevant changes in height in either the 2 mg/kg/day or the 6 mg/kg/day dose groups at the end of the 12-week treatment period in VBP15-006 (Supplementary Table 3). The median (Q1; Q3) CBL in height z-scores was −0.15 (−0.20; 0.04) for the 2 mg/kg/day group and 0.09 (−0.21; 0.33) for the 6 mg/kg/day group.

At the time of the data snapshot in the ongoing EAP Canada study, the mean/median/(min., max.) age of 11 participants with available standing height was, 10 . 6/9.8(8.2, 18.6) years. The total median (Q1; Q3) duration of 2–6 mg/kg/day vamorolone treatment (during VBP15-006 and EAP Canada) was 1.3 (0.7; 1.7) years (min., max.: 0.7, 2.9 years) and the total exposure to vamorolone was 9.35 (6 mg/kg/day vamorolone), 4.19 (4 mg/kg/day), and 1.15 (2 mg/kg/day) person years.

The height z-scores of CS-untreated participants remained stable, with a median (Q1; Q3) CBL in height z-score of 0.028 (−0.09; 0.31); no growth delay was observed (Fig. 2a). No clinically relevant change in weight or BMI z-scores was observed in the 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone group during the 12-week period of VBP15-006 (Supplementary Table 3). In the CS-untreated group receiving 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone, there was a median increase in weight z-score of 0.54 (median weight increase, 2.25 kg) and a median increase in BMI z-score of 1.01 at the end of 12 weeks (Supplementary Table 3). This group also included 2 individuals who reported TEAEs of increased appetite (one of whom also reported a TEAE of weight increase).

Consistent with this, the median CBL from VBP15-006 baseline to the last available data in EAP Canada in weight z-score (Q1; Q3) was 1.20 (0.03; 1.35) (Supplementary Fig. 1c) and median CBL in the BMI z-score (Q1;Q3) was 1.21 (−0.13; 1.43) (Supplementary Fig. 1d), indicating a weight increase over time on vamorolone. The weight increase occurred in a subset of individuals: 55% (n = 6/11) had excessive weight gain (defined as average CBL in BMI z-score > 0.5), with median/mean values of 1.507/1.418), while 45% (n = 5/11) did not gain weight, with median/mean CBL in BMI z-scores of -0.011/-0.128. At the time of the data snapshot from EAP Canada, half (n = 3/6) of the participants who gained weight were short-distance ambulatory and one (n = 1/6) was part-time in a wheelchair (communication with the study investigators).

Previously CS-treated, switched to vamorolone

There were no relevant changes in height in the two dose groups at the end of the VBP15-006 12-week treatment period (Supplementary Table 3). The median (Q1; Q3) CBL in height z-scores was −0.17 (−0.25; −0.08) for the 2 mg/kg/day group and−0.07 (−0.12; 0.11) for the 6 mg/kg/day group.

At the time of the data snapshot in the ongoing EAP Canada study, the mean/median/(min., max.) age of 14 participants with available standing height data were 13.3/13.3 (10.5, 17.0) years. The total median (Q1; Q3) duration of 2–6 mg/kg/day vamorolone treatment from the start of VBP15-006 until the last available observation in EAP Canada was 1.7 years (1.0; 2.1; range 0.6–2.8). The total exposure to vamorolone was 15.10 (6 mg/kg/day vamorolone), 3.12 (4 mg/kg/day), and 4.36 (2 mg/kg/day) person years.

The height z-scores of previously CS-treated individuals (n = 14, pooled 2–6 mg/kg/day) increased progressively from the baseline of VBP15-006 to the last available data of the EAP Canada, with a median (Q1; Q3) CBL in height z-score of 0.40 (0.03; 0.55) (Fig. 2c). Catch-up growth was observed for 3 boys with available data on standing height in the 12- to < 18-year-old subgroup, with more than 8 cm gained in 12 months (Supplementary Fig. 2a). There did not appear to be an association between the duration of previous CS treatment and catch-up growth (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The absolute heights of participants with available data for the entire treatment period in both studies are presented with DMD-specific (Fig. 2d) and CDC (Supplementary Fig. 1b) growth charts used as references.

No clinically relevant change in weight or BMI z-score was observed in any dose group during the 12-week VBP15-006 period. The median (Q1; Q3) CBL in weight z-scores was 0.11 (−0.03; 0.23) for the 2 mg/kg/day group and 0.26 (0.03; 0.46) for the 6 mg/kg/day group (Supplementary Table 3). The median (Q1; Q3) CBL in weight z-score from the VBP15-006 baseline to the last available data in the EAP Canada study was 0.27 (−0.11; 0.95). Similarly, the median (Q1; Q3) CBL in BMI z-score was 0.13 (-0.07; 0.33) (Supplementary Fig. 1e), indicating consistent weight in this participant group over time on vamorolone. Excessive weight gain (defined as average CBL in BMI z-score > 0.5) was observed in 21% (n = 3/14) participants, with mean/median scores of 1.267/0.805. At the time of the data snapshot, 1 of these individuals had experienced a TEAE of weight gain during the EAP study (and was non-ambulant). Eleven participants did not gain weight, with CBL in BMI z-Score of −0.006/−0.035.

Safety during the VBP15-006 study

Treatment-related TEAEs occurred more frequently in the 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone groups, most TEAEs were mild, and there were no discontinuations and no fatal events across all 7- to < 18-year-old participants at both vamorolone doses.

TEAEs: previously CS-untreated

In the previously CS-untreated group, a total of 6 individuals (100%) in the 2 mg/kg/day dose group and 4 individuals (66.7%) in the 6 mg/kg/day group reported TEAEs.

A TEAE of adrenal suppression (mild) was reported for 1 individual in the 6 mg/kg/day dose group, and a cortisol decrease was reported for 2 individuals in the 2 mg/kg/day dose group (both moderate) (Table 2). All 3 of these TEAEs were based on morning serum cortisol measurements performed at 12 weeks; no symptoms consistent with adrenal insufficiency were reported, and all events were ongoing at study end. TEAEs: previously CS-treated group

In the previously CS-treated group, the most common reported TEAEs were musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, and nervous system disorders (Table 2). Three (50%) participants reported TEAEs in the 2 mg/kg/day dose group (1 related to vamorolone) and 12 (75%) reported TEAEs in the 6 mg/kg/day group (8 related to vamorolone) (Table 2).

In the 2 mg/kg/day dose group, two participants had TEAEs reported as “generalized weakness” (preferred term: asthenia), consistent with symptoms of adrenal insufficiency. Narratives for these participants are in the Supplementary results. All had morning serum cortisol levels ≤ 65 nmol/L at week 12.

At VBP15-006 baseline, there was no clear association between the percentage of the recommended dose of previous CS received and morning serum cortisol (adrenal suppression) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Three (50%) participants in the 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone dose group and 12 (80%) participants in the 6 mg/kg/day group had morning serum cortisol levels at baseline of the VBP15-006 study consistent with adrenal suppression.

Participants who switched to 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone from their previous CS treatment experienced a further reduction in morning serum cortisol levels after 12 weeks of treatment, with the mean (SD) value decreasing to 18.1 (21.2) nmol/L (Fig. 4c,d). Switching to 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone resulted in variable degrees of adrenal suppression (Fig. 4c,d, note that the 2 asthenia cases are not included due to stress steroid HC supplementation). The mean (SD) morning serum cortisol level increased to 91.0 nmol/L (78.5) at 12 weeks from a baseline of 79.0 nmol/L (65.6).

Psychiatric TEAEs consistent with behavior issues were reported in 2/6 of participants with pre-existing medical history receiving 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone and in no participants in the 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone dose group (more details in Supplementary results).

TEAEs in EAP Canada are noted in the Supplementary results.

Exploratory endpoints in VBP15-006

Bone serum biomarkers: previously CS-untreated

In the CS-untreated group, there was no evidence for an effect on the serum bone turnover markers CTX1, osteocalcin, or P1NP at either dose of vamorolone. Serum turnover markers remained within the normal range for the majority of participants (Supplementary Table 5). The only shift occurring in more than 1 participant was an increase in osteocalcin from low at baseline to normal at week 12.

Bone serum biomarkers: previously CS-treated, switched to vamorolone

Bone biomarkers showed progressive recovery in the previously CS-treated groups, independent of vamorolone dose (Supplementary Table 5). Serum levels of bone turnover biomarkers increased in participants in the 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone group (previously CS-treated for a median of 4.5 years) as follows: P1NP levels by 239%, osteocalcin by 168%, and CTX-1 by 179% at the end of 12 weeks. Participants in the 6 mg/kg/day dose group (previously CS-treated for a median of 7.8 years) had P1NP levels increase 252%, osteocalcin 118% and CTX-1 109% by the end of 12 weeks of vamorolone treatment.

Recovery of bone turnover biomarkers to a normal range after 12 weeks was mainly observed in participants with shorter durations of previous CS treatment (i.e., those from the 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone group).

Details on other exploratory endpoints are provided in the Supplementary information (Supplementary results).

Discussion

VBP15-006 was a 12-week, open-label study, which assessed the effects of vamorolone in boys with DMD below 4 and ≥ 7 years old, with the latter age group either previously CS-untreated or switching to vamorolone from previous CS treatment. Results from the 7- to < 18-year-old subgroup are reported here. Upon completion of the VBP15-006 study, all 34 individuals aged 7- to < 18 years (irrespective of previous CS treatment) chose to continue on vamorolone treatment in the EAP Canada study. The inclusion of EAP Canada data provided an opportunity to evaluate the safety of vamorolone over a longer treatment period (median total duration of treatment 1.7 years for CS-treated group and 1.3 years for CS-untreated) than the relatively short 12-week duration of the VBP15-006 study.

Effects of vamorolone on height, weight, and BMI

This study confirmed the absence of a negative impact of vamorolone on the growth of CS-untreated participants, consistent with the results of the pivotal VISION-DMD study [6, 24]. Interestingly, in participants who were growth-stunted due to previous long-term CS treatment, a recovery of height after vamorolone treatment was demonstrated for the first time. This was not previously observed due to the shorter durations of prior CS treatment in trial participants [6, 23]. The height z-scores of CS-treated participants (pooled 2–6 mg/kg/day data) increased progressively from the VBP15-006 baseline to the latest data from EAP Canada, indicating catch-up growth. This was seen even in some 12- to < 18-year-old participants, who had several years of CS exposure.

There was no association between the duration of previous CS treatment and catch-up growth, suggesting that the negative effects of standard CS treatment on growth plates may be at least partially reversible. This observation, based on follow-up data from EAP Canada, is consistent with both the VBP15-006 results on bone turnover biomarkers and previous bone biomarker data from the pivotal VISION DMD study [6]. The results from the bone turnover biomarkers also imply stronger bone and may translate to a decreased risk of vertebral compression fractions. This could contribute to the observed height increases. Higher alkaline phosphatase levels in CS-treated participants were observed during the 12-week duration VBP15-006 without any indication of liver damage upon switching to vamorolone; this also suggests improved bone formation.

Excessive weight gain (defined as CBL in BMI z-score > 0.5) was confirmed in a proportion of participants on vamorolone, both previously CS-treated and CS-untreated, based on preliminary results from the ongoing Canada EAP study. This effect was most pronounced in CS-untreated participants who began on 6 mg/kg/day in VBP15-006. This group had already experienced an increase in weight/BMI during VBP15-006, accompanied by an increase in insulin levels. This was also the only group in which TEAEs of increased appetite and weight were reported. A further weight gain in some of these participants occurred in EAP Canada (when doses could be varied depending on tolerability), but it is difficult to estimate what proportion has been affected due to low participant numbers. In addition, some 7- to < 18-year-old CS-untreated participants of the current study had received CS in the past, which had been stopped at least three months before entering VBP15-006; this might imply a low tolerance to CS treatment and predisposition to weight gain. It would be beneficial to have a biomarker that predicts which individuals may be more prone to weight gain on long-term treatment, to potentially adjust diet and vamorolone dose.

Considering the small participant groups involved, it is difficult to assess whether excessive weight gain had an impact on mobility, or if loss of ambulation contributed to excessive weight gain on vamorolone. Notably, 4/6 CS-untreated participants with excessive weight gain (CBL in BMI z-score of > 0.5) were still ambulatory over short distances at the time of data analysis. In the CS-treated group, excessive weight gain was recorded in only 3 out of 14 participants during the EAP Canada period.

Vamorolone PK profile

The vamorolone PK profile (using the commercial formulation [ROS2]) showed a clear dose-dependent relationship across the evaluated doses of 2 mg/kg/day and 6 mg/kg/day. The findings were also consistent with previously reported PK exposure data using the ROS1 formulation in the 4- to < 7 years age group [20].

Adrenal suppression and adrenal insufficiency

Morning serum cortisol testing is not routinely performed in individuals on long-term CS treatment, but VBP15-006 was an open-label study during which morning serum cortisol measurements (taken at baseline and 12 weeks) were available to study investigators. There was no clear association observed in this study between the level of adrenal suppression (as measured by morning serum cortisol level) and dose of previous CS or treatment duration. However, a dose-dependent reduction in morning serum cortisol was observed in the 2 mg/kg/day and particularly the 6 mg/kg/day treatment groups in CS-untreated participants. Similarly, previous studies demonstrated vamorolone dose-dependent adrenal suppression, with less suppression of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis at 2 mg/kg/day than 6 mg/kg/day [24, 25]. This suggests that individuals receiving vamorolone doses < 6 mg/kg/day may be at risk of symptomatic adrenal insufficiency. In VBP15-006, switching from any previous CS dose to 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone did not cause any symptoms consistent with adrenal insufficiency in any of the 16 participants. Conversely, there were 2 cases of asthenia among those participants who switched to 2 mg/kg/day vamorolone which were consistent with adrenal insufficiency and required prolonged treatment with stress steroid doses of HC.

According to recently released recommendations, switching from any dose of CS to vamorolone should target 6 mg/kg/day to avoid adrenal crises [25, 26]. Since vamorolone is a mineralocorticoid antagonist, it cannot be used for stress dosing as it could increase the risk of hypotension during physiological stress [25].

Other safety assessments

No new safety or tolerability issues were encountered. Behavioral events are of interest in a population of boys initiating DMD treatment with CS and in the pivotal VISION DMD study, the majority of behavioral problem TEAEs were reported during the first 6 months of treatment [6]. The most relevant TEAE finding for vamorolone 6 mg/kg/day was irritability (10.7% of participants) [19]; this did not occur with vamorolone 2 mg/kg/day. Consistent with this, behavioral events during VBP15-006 occurred in 3 of the 7- to < 18-year-old participants overall and only in the 6 mg/kg/day dose group. Importantly, 10/13 of participants with a pre-existing medical history of psychiatric disorders experienced no related TEAEs during VBP15-006.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include the short (12-week) duration of VBP15-006, the limited amount of data from the EAP Canada period, and the relatively small sample size. Open-label study design, lack of randomization, and absence of a control group are other relevant considerations as is the variable dosing in the EAP Canada period. Potential underreporting of TEAEs cannot be excluded in EAP Canada. No testosterone levels were measured for full assessment of the gonadal axis.

Conclusions

The results from VBP15-006 of the individuals with DMD aged 7- to < 18 years stress the importance of following recent recommendations on switching to 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone, irrespective of previous CS dose, to prevent adrenal crisis. No symptoms consistent with adrenal insufficiency were reported in any of the 16 participants who switched from previous CS to 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone, but 33% (n = 2/6) of participants who switched from previous CS to 2 mg/kg/day experienced asthenia. This required regular supplementation with HC until vamorolone was increased to 6 mg/kg/day. A dose-dependent PK profile, including similar exposure, was confirmed at both 2 mg/kg/day and 6 mg/kg/day doses. The data suggest no negative effect of vamorolone on growth (i.e., height) in 7- to < 18-year-old CS-untreated and previously CS-treated boys with DMD, and a potential recovery of bone health parameters related to previous CS treatment. There appears to be a higher proportion of CS-naïve than CS-treated individuals prone to excessive weight gain on 6 mg/kg/day vamorolone, but more data are needed to draw conclusions [26].
