Comparison of Two Variant Analysis Programs for Next-Generation Sequencing Data of Whole Mitochondrial Genome
Authors: Seung Eun Lee, Ga Eun Kim, Hajin Kim, Doo Hyun Chung, Soong Deok Lee, Moon-Young Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith advance of next-generation sequencing (NGS) techniques, the need for mitochondrial DNA analysis is increasing not only in the forensic area, but also in medical fields.MethodsTwo commercial programs, Converge Software (CS) and Torrent Variant Caller for variant calling of NGS data, were compared with a considerable amount of sequence data of 50 samples with a homogeneous ethnicity.ResultsAbout 2,300 variants were identified and the two programs showed about 90% of consistency. CS, a dedicated analysis program for mitochondrial DNA, showed some advantages for forensic use. By additional visual inspection, several causes of discrepancy in variant calling results were identified. Application of different notation rules for mitochondrial sequence and the minor allele frequency close to detection threshold were the two most significant reasons.ConclusionWith prospective improvement of each program, researchers and practitioners should be aware of characteristics of the analysis program they use and prepare their own strategies to determine variants.

INTRODUCTION

For decades, mitochondrial DNA has been noticed as an important source of genetic information for human and ethnic identification in forensic areas. These demands have led to the accumulation of both academic knowledge and practical experiences about mitochondrial DNA. In particular, with the development of next-generation sequencing (NGS) techniques and efforts to apply these techniques in forensic practices, several commercial kits are available, especially multiplex panels covering the entire or control region of the mitochondrial genome.12345 They have been validated in various populations678 using a variety of sample types including blood, bones, hair shafts, frozen tissues, and formalin-fixed tissues.67891011 Additionally, with increasing interest in the role of mitochondria and their DNAs in the development and progression of several medical conditions such as various types of cancers and neuromuscular diseases, a few attempts have been made to apply these techniques to medical research.345121314

In general, an NGS pipeline includes not only experimental kits or equipment, but also a series of bioinformatics tools that aim to report an accurate sequence of the targeted region. Research individuals and groups have developed several useful tools and shared them as a form of open-source codes, some of which are then modified or integrated repeatedly by researchers themselves or others. Many researchers had to search and test numerous analysis tools to set up an NGS pipeline in the past. However, as several biotechnical companies have begun to develop their own bioinformatics tools or provide analysis services for their customers, researchers now have many options for NGS data analysis.

Recently, an integrated form of commercial NGS pipeline for mitochondrial DNA has been launched, including both a targeted multiplex panel and a dedicated analysis program for mitochondrial genomes.1516 This is encouraging in that the exclusive use of a dedicated analysis program for mitochondrial DNA is beneficial for increasing the detection rate of variants with a low frequency, as noted in our previous study.1718 The unique nature of mitochondrial DNA different from nuclear DNA should be considered when developing and applying algorithms for analysis processes such as read mapping and alignment, variant calling, calculation of quality score, setting up thresholds, reporting, and so on. However, as most of the analysis programs for NGS data have been adopting algorithms developed for nuclear DNA, their performances could be altered when they are applied to mitochondrial DNA. As far as the authors know, there have been no studies published so far dealing with this problem in the fashion of comparing the results of certain analysis tools or programs directly, especially for mitochondrial NGS data. This type of study will clear up the advantage of using a dedicated analysis program for mitochondrial DNA and the necessity of follow-up developments.

For these purposes, two analysis programs were compared in this study using identical raw data generated by an Ion Torrent NGS platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with Torrent Variant Caller plugin (TVC; Thermo Fisher Scientific) as a universal program and Converge Software (CS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) as a dedicated program for mitochondrial DNA. They are provided by the same biotechnical company, which enabled several conditions to be controlled, including the environment of program development and maintenance, specifications for hardware and operating system, and the range and scale of potential users. Similarities and differences of these programs emphasize the necessity of target-oriented selection of analysis programs. Guidance of data interpretation is also proposed for forensic and medical researchers.

METHODS

Data collection

A data set of whole mitochondrial genome was collected from a group of patients who were treated for lung cancer at Seoul National University Hospital from 2014 to 2015.18 In the database, 50 cases (#111–#160) were selected randomly without considering clinical, pathologic, or genetic information. These cases were all Koreans. There were 27 males and 23 females with a mean age of 61.32 (ranged 29 to 82) years.

DNA extraction

All samples used in this study were normal lung tissues fixed in neutralized formalin and embedded in paraffin. One unstained section of 10 μm thickness per sample was scraped so that up to 4 mm3 of paraffin particles were collected. For each sample, DNA was extracted using Maxwell RSC DNA FFPE kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) on Maxwell RSC instrument (Promega Corporation). It was quantified with Invitrogen Qubit Flex Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Library preparation and sequencing

The whole mitochondrial genome of each sample was amplified using a total of 2 ng genomic DNA and Precision ID mtDNA Whole Genome Panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific; each 1 ng for primer pools 1 and 2, respectively). Its library was prepared using Precision ID Library kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. IonCode Barcode Adapters (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for barcoding each sample uniquely. Agencourt AMPure XP reagents (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and Ion Library Taqman Quantitation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for purification and quantification of each library, respectively. After samples were diluted to a concentration of 30 pM, they were equally pooled for emulsion PCR on Ion Chef System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then sequenced on Ion S5 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Data analysis

TVC version 5.10.0.3 and CS version 2.1 were used for variant calling in comparison with revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS; NC_012920). For TVC, a default setting of ‘Germline-Low Stringency’ was used for overall parameters (for example, minimal allele frequency for all types of variants was set to 0.1). Default thresholds of CS were different from TVC in a few items (for example, 0.1 for point heteroplasmy, 0.2 for insertion, and 0.3 for deletion).

Both TVC and CS generated a report for each sample about called variants and their details. These reports had a similar format basically, including nucleotide position, allele frequency, type, quality score, total and allele coverage, and strand bias of each variant. However, there were some differences that a researcher should be aware of before analysis.

As a universal variant caller mainly used for the analysis of nuclear DNA, TVC classifies variant calls into ‘homozygous’ and ‘heterozygous’ based on the concept of pairs of homologous chromosomes of the nuclear genome. This approach is not consistent with mitochondrial DNA which has multiple copies of circular DNA and may have more than two alleles. In CS, variant calls are classified into six categories such as ‘true variant’, ‘point heteroplasmy’, ‘length heteroplasmy’, ‘artifact’, ‘numt’, and ‘degradation’, considering characteristics of mitochondrial DNA and possible artifacts frequently observed in forensic practices. Types of the variant were classified similarly such as single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), insertion, and deletion except for multiple nucleotide polymorphisms in TVC which were replaced by sequential SNPs in CS.

While TVC displays only reference and variant codes, CS shows additional ‘sample’ codes. This implies that CS assumes that sample sequences can be frequently different from the reference sequence, which is a common phenomenon in mitochondrial DNA due to numerous haplogroups and haplotypes in general populations. Additionally, CS provides routinely all proportions of A, C, G, and T codes, insertion, and deletion per variant position so that the user can figure out not only the frequency of the second most frequent allele (as minor allele frequency), but also those of other alleles mixed with.

The scale of quality scores is different between TVC and CS. The former ranges from 0 to nearly 100,000 while the latter ranges from 0 to 1. These scores do not correlate in proportion, which indicates that they have adopted different algorithms to calculate quality scores.

In particular, CS provides search results of the EMPOP database for every reported variant, including the frequency and related haplogroup, and suggests the closest haplogroup of each sample.19

Variant calling results were compared and analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). For discrepant variants between TVC and CS, integrative genomics viewer (IGV) version 2.12.3 was used for visual inspection.20 Variants with a coverage depth less than 20 were excluded from the analysis in this study.

Ethics statement

This study followed the recommendations of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (approval No. C-1706-191-865). Informed consent was submitted by all patients when they were enrolled.

RESULTS

Overview of sequencing data quality

Although the two analysis programs used an identical set of raw data, reported coverages and read numbers were slightly different between TVC and CS. In TVC, the average amplicon coverage ranged from 3,508 to 10,381 with a mean value of 6,395.88, whereas it ranged from 3,335 to 9,229 with a mean value of 5,749.60 in CS. The average amplicon coverage of each sample reported by CS was 9.75% less than the value reported by TVC on average ranging from −4.93% to −12.75%. In the case of read numbers, TVC provided only one item named ‘mapped reads’, while CS provided three items named ‘mapped reads’, ‘total reads’, and ‘used reads’. The number of ‘mapped reads’ of CS was 0.95% more than that of TVC on average (range, −0.09% to 1.56%), while numbers of ‘total reads’ and ‘used reads’ were 0.18% (range, −0.49% to −0.10%) and 0.21% (range, −0.55% to −0.12%) less than ‘mapped reads’ of TVC, respectively.

As an analysis program dedicated to mitochondrial DNA, CS reports the range covered by mapped reads in the targeted mitochondrial region. In 43 samples, the whole region of mitochondrial DNA was covered, while seven samples had uncovered regions. The number of uncovered region was only one in five samples, two or six in other two samples. Considering that these two samples showed compatible coverage depth and read numbers, they were not excluded in the following analysis.

Variants reported by CS and TVC

A total of 2,302 variants were reported across 50 samples with a mean number of 46.04 per sample. They showed about 90% of consistency between CS and TVC, whereas 261 variants were reported by CS only and 220 variants by TVC only. Parameters of commonly detected variants between CS and TVC did not show any significant difference. After visualized inspection using IGV, the causes of inconsistency could be classified into four categories for 261 out of 481 variants reported by either CS or TVC, as presented in Table 1. The remaining 220 inconsistent variants, involving length heteroplasmies within homopolymeric regions, were analyzed separately, considering the interpretational ambiguity associated with this type of sequence.

The most common reason for the inconsistency between CS and TVC was ‘different notation of the same variant’. There are differences in the notation for insertions and deletions in mitochondrial sequences between forensic and medical fields. In forensics, insertions and deletions are notated at 3′ relative to the affected position, while medical genetics records them at 5′.21 As CS was developed for forensic use, CS applies notation rules of the forensic community, while TVC adopts traditional medical notation rules. All variants of this category were located at each end of homopolymeric sequence. They were confirmed to be identical by IGV. Table 4 presents details of these variants and related reference sequences. Supplementary Fig. 1A shows an example of IGV analysis at 8271–8289 which was observed in six samples. In the other cases, there was a significant difference in variant frequency among TVC, CS, and IGV for variants reported as insertions. For example, the frequency of insertions reported at 568 by TVC was CCCC (80.3%) or CCCCC (17.5%) while CS defined it as inserted sequence of CCCC (27.1%) at 573. Since these variants were detected in a single sample and IGV showed various length heteroplasmies of poly-C insertion, the frequency detected by TVC was calculated as the sum of both types of insertion in Table 4. In the other example at 594 and 595, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1B, some reads of forward strands seemed to be unselected by TVC or CS when they started near the undetected insertion.

Some heteroplasmies with minor frequencies ‘close to the detection threshold’ of 10% were not reported by either program. The existence of all these variants could be confirmed by IGV as shown in Table 5. For example, sequential polymorphisms at 13057 and 13058 were detected by only TVC in 42 samples. In the case of the SNP at 13786, each program reported it in different samples. The minor allele frequency, or the level of heteroplasmy, is determined by the number of reads with certain variants included in the analysis. If the number of variant reads that passed through a quality-checking process of each program was sufficient to exceed the threshold, the variant would be called and reported. If the number was insufficient, the variant would not be called. Thus, this type of inconsistencies between CS and TVC imply that they are adopting different quality-checking processes or algorithms for read selection.

Inconsistent variants due to ‘alignment errors’ were observed at 249, 16182, 16183, and 16189 by CS and at 248 by TVC. Alignment error indicates an inaccurate alignment of a base within a read and was presumed when a variant appeared across a certain number of reads regardless of the quality of each read. These variants with alignment errors were nearby homopolymeric tracts such as poly-C stretches which could affect the alignment accuracy. There were significant differences between average variant frequencies reported by each program and IGV as shown in Table 6. Some variants were detected by both programs but reported differently. For example, among six samples in which CS reported deletion at 249, TVC reported SNP at 248 in two samples. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A, IGV supported the report of CS. In addition, multiple length variants from 16180 to 16193 were concurrently observed in several samples (Table 7), where reference sequence consisted of poly-A tract followed by poly-C tract as shown in Supplementary Figure 2B. Continuous homopolymeric tracts seem to have caused alignment errors, resulting in confusion of each program’s detection. Further examples of variant calling errors associated with homopolymeric regions are explored in the last paragraph of this section.

Despite thorough inspection using IGV, the reason for inconsistency between CS and TVC remained ‘unknown’ in about 10% of the categorized variants (Table 8). With the exception of one variant, all the variants reported only by CS were SNPs at 6445 in 12 samples, and IGV confirmed their presence with similar frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In the variant report of CS, these variants were noted as ‘likely artifact due to correlated mutations in amplicon’. Additionally, these variants were searched in MITOMASTER,22 a public database of mitochondrial sequences. The frequency values for variants at 6445 and 9950 were determined as 0.034% and 3.819%, respectively. According to the search results, a high frequency of the 9950 SNP (T > C) was found in haplogroup branch B5, which was presented in only one sample in this study. However, it remains unknown why TVC did not report these variants. On the other hand, all the variants reported by only TVC were point mutations of A > C at 10958 in 13 samples. These variants were accompanied by deletions from 10946 to 10948, which were length variants within homopolymeric regions and appeared only in either the forward or reverse strands (Supplementary Fig. 3B). However, their frequency was almost 0% when searched in the MITOMASTER. This implies that they are erroneously detected variants by both TVC and IGV.

Considering their interpretational ambiguity, the length heteroplasmies observed within homopolymeric regions were presented separately in Table 7, distinct from the categorized variants mentioned earlier. Notably, all the insertions and deletions at 309 and 315, which are located within a well-known poly-C tract from 303 to 315, were reported by only CS. It could be inferred that all 50 samples had these variants because they were the most prevalent length variants in Korean haplogroups.23 However, TVC only detected other length variants in this region, rather than the aforementioned common variants. Furthermore, CS reported deletions at 14715 in 20 samples, which are situated within a poly-A tract from 14711 to 14715. Image analysis conducted using IGV indicated that some deletions were overlooked when they were close to the read ends (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B). Additionally, TVC identified sequential deletions from 10946 to 10948 in 13 samples, which were accompanied by the previously mentioned point mutations of A > C at 10958 (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

DISCUSSION

As a dedicated analysis program for mitochondrial DNA, CS is an integrated program providing not only variants, but also their frequencies in population and haplogroups, especially targeting forensic uses. Researchers and practitioners in the forensic field can have an advantage in efficiency by reducing their time and effort in managing several programs and public databases to acquire each part of information. In the case of variant calling and reporting, CS detected major length variants that frequently appear in the Korean population, for example, length variants and heteroplasmies within a well-known poly-C tract between 303 and 315, while TVC did not.

Although CS has several benefits as mentioned above, there are some points its users should be aware of. First, the most common reason for inconsistent variant calling between CS and TVC identified by IGV was the adoption of different notation rules of mitochondrial variants. This could be confusing for researchers who are not familiar with the discrepancy between forensic and medical areas. For their convenience and analysis accuracy, programs should be improved so that users can choose notation rules or at least be informed which notation rules were applied in the variant report, like other parameters. Before that, users themselves should be aware of the notation rules of mitochondrial DNA adopted for the analysis program and confirm variants according to their purposes.

Secondly, a common issue encountered by both CS and TVC is the possibility of excluding heteroplasmies with minor allele frequencies that are in close proximity to the detection threshold. This problem contributes significantly to the substantial number of inconsistent variants reported by both programs. For example, TVC reported the presence of SNPs at 13057 and 13058 in 84% of the samples, whereas CS did not include these variants in its report. To address this, it would be helpful for users to consider setting their detection thresholds slightly lower than their intended levels. By adopting this approach, potential heteroplasmies with minor allele frequencies that are slightly below the threshold could be identified. The detection capability of any variant caller can be unstable if the number or quality of candidate reads, or the overall coverage depth, is suboptimal. Implementing the aforementioned strategy can help prevent any undesired loss of relevant variants.

Moreover, it is worth noting that alignment errors played a significant role in generating considerable discrepancies among CS, TVC, and IGV. These errors were particularly influenced by adjacent variants, especially length variants such as insertions, deletions, and length heteroplasmies. Considering the frequent appearance of homopolymeric regions within the mitochondrial genome, it is recommended to confirm nearby areas of each variant using additional tools such as IGV. Users of CS can utilize a simplified form of IGV integrated within the program. However, it is evident that the alignment algorithm employed by these programs would benefit from further improvement.

As the algorithms or mechanisms of each program were not fully open to the public, reasons for the discrepancy between the two programs remained uncertain in many cases. However, most of the cases are suspected to be related to the adjacent length variants or homopolymeric regions. Within the homopolymeric regions, the length of nucleotide can be sequenced with variability due to the slippage of enzymes during polymerase chain reaction,2425 which complicates the identification of length heteroplasmy and determination of sequence types. Consequently, there is currently no universally accepted guideline for annotating mitochondrial length heteroplasmies, especially in regions outside of the hypervariable regions. To avoid misinterpretation, many researchers and institutions studying mitochondrial DNA tend to exclude length heteroplasmies from their analysis processes.71926

Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the Ion Torrent NGS platform is known to have limitations in accurately detecting length heteroplasmies due to its operating mechanism. Users of this platform should be informed about the potential existence of this type of variant and encouraged to pursue further verification. Additional research and technical advancements focusing of mitochondrial length heteroplasmies are required to address these challenges and improve our understanding of this phenomenon.

There are a few limitations in this study. Only two commercial programs were adopted here considering their comparability, which limited the range of exploration of various analysis tools for mitochondrial DNA. The possibility of the existence of Numts was not investigated using other tools such as UCSC Genome Browser on Human,27 because a vast majority of Numts show much lower frequencies than the detection threshold of 10% applied in this study. This could be confirmed in the following study.

In summary, two commercial programs for variant calling were compared with a considerable amount of sequence data from a homogeneous population. CS and TVC showed consistent results in general, however, a portion of discrepant variants were identified. The application of different notation rules for mitochondrial sequence and the variants close to detection threshold were the two most significant reasons for such discrepancy. Even though CS, a dedicated analysis program for mitochondrial DNA, shows several advantages for forensic users, researchers and practitioners should be aware of characteristics of each analysis program they are using and set up their own strategies to complement against current limitations.
