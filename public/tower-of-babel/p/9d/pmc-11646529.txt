Left Atrial Appendage Opacification on Cardiac Computed Tomography in Acute Ischemic Stroke: The Clinical Implications of Slow‐Flow
Authors: Shan Sui Nio, Leon A Rinkel, Olivia N Cramer, Z Beyda Özata, Chiel F P Beemsterboer, Valeria Guglielmi, Berto J Bouma, S Matthijs Boekholdt, Nick H J Lobé, Ludo F M Beenen, Henk A Marquering, Charles B L M Majoie, Yvo B W E M Roos, Adrienne van Randen, R Nils Planken, Jonathan M Coutinho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft atrial appendage (LAA) slow‐flow may increase the risk of ischemic stroke. We studied LAA attenuation on cardiac computed tomography in patients with acute ischemic stroke.Methods and ResultsWe used data from a prospective cohort of patients with acute ischemic stroke undergoing cardiac computed tomography during the acute stroke imaging protocol. We compared characteristics, functional outcome (modified Rankin scale: higher scores indicating worse outcome), stroke recurrence and major adverse cardiovascular events after 2‐year follow‐up between patients with LAA thrombus (filling defect<100 Hounsfield Unit (HU)), slow‐flow (filling defect ≥100 HU) and normal filling. Of 421 patients, 31 (7%) had LAA thrombus, 69 (16%) slow‐flow, and 321 (76%) normal filling. Patients with thrombus or slow‐flow more often had known atrial fibrillation compared with normal filling (45%, 39%, and 9%, P<0.001). Patients with thrombus had higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale‐scores compared with slow‐flow and normal filling (18 [interquartile range, 9–22], 6 [interquartile range, 3–17], and 5 [interquartile range, 2–11], P<0.001). Compared with normal filling, there was no difference with slow‐flow in functional outcome (median modified Rankin scale, 3 versus 2; acOR 0.8 [95% CI, 0.5–1.4]), stroke recurrence (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.3–1.9]) or major adverse cardiovascular events (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.2 [95% CI, 0.7–2.1]), while patients with thrombus had worse functional outcome (median modified Rankin scale, 6, acOR, 3.3 [95% CI, 1.5–7.4]). In cryptogenic stroke patients (n=156) slow‐flow was associated with stroke recurrence (27% versus 6%, aHR, 4.1 [95% CI, 1.1–15.7]).ConclusionsPatients with slow‐flow had similar characteristics to patients with thrombus, but had less severe strokes. Slow‐flow was not significantly associated with functional outcome or major adverse cardiovascular events, but was associated with recurrent stroke in patients with cryptogenic stroke.Keywords: acute ischemic stroke, computed tomography angiography, filling defect, slow‐flowSubject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Ischemic Stroke, Computerized Tomography (CT), Imaging, Etiology

Approximately one third of ischemic strokes are attributable to cardioembolism and it is believed that cryptogenic strokes often also have a cardioembolic origin. 1 , 2 Slow‐flow in the left atrial appendage (LAA) represents a state of circulatory stasis, which is likely caused by atrial cardiopathy and the lack of organized LAA contractions. 3 In most cases, LAA slow‐flow is attributable to atrial fibrillation (AF), but it has also been described in the absence of AF. 4 Slow‐flow has been associated with an increased risk of cardiac thrombus formation, thromboembolic events, and a high stroke severity. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 As such, slow‐flow might be a prognostic variable for an elevated stroke recurrence risk and worse functional outcome in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS), especially those with cryptogenic stroke. 11

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) as part of the initial stroke imaging protocol is an emerging diagnostic tool in patients with AIS to detect cardioembolic sources of stroke, with studies showing a higher or similar diagnostic yield compared with transthoracic echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 TEE is considered the reference modality with a higher diagnostic yield in detecting high‐risk cardioembolic sources compared with transthoracic echocardiography, but is semi‐invasive and not always readily available. 3 , 16 Therefore, acute cardiac CT may be a promising alternative to echocardiography. Several studies found a similar diagnostic yield between cardiac CT and TEE for the detection of slow‐flow. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Other terminologies for the same phenomenon include early filling defects, circulatory stasis or spontaneous echo contrast. We will use the term slow‐flow within this article.

Few prior studies have investigated LAA slow‐flow on cardiac CT in patients with AIS and to our knowledge, none have been conducted in the acute phase of ischemic stroke. The aim of this study was to compare characteristics, functional outcome and occurrence of new cardiovascular events between patients with AIS with LAA thrombus, slow‐flow and normal filling on cardiac CT.

Methods

This was a post hoc analysis of the Mind the Heart study, a prospective, observational, single‐center cohort study performed in the Amsterdam University Medical Center (location AMC). The study protocol and main results have been reported elsewhere. 14 , 23 Between May 2018 and November 2020, we included consecutive adult AIS patients, who were potentially eligible for reperfusion therapy (intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular thrombectomy). We excluded patients with a final diagnosis other than ischemic stroke and patients with high‐risk cardioaortic sources of embolism other than LAA thrombus, to reliably compare the LAA flow states (Table S1).

Patients presenting with acute neurological deficits at the emergency department underwent a non‐contrast enhanced brain CT, CT perfusion and a non‐gated CT angiography of the brain, cervical arteries, and aortic arch, immediately followed by a prospective electrocardiography‐gated cardiac CT. An ECG was acquired and during hospital admission rhythm monitoring of >24 hours was performed. If stroke etiology was unknown at discharge, patients underwent a Holter registration of 72 hours, a transthoracic echocardiography, and a consult at a cardiologist to systematically search for a cardiac source of stroke. This was performed as part of the routine stroke care protocol in an outpatient setting. Stroke etiology was determined based on the TOAST (Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) classification system. 24 Cardiac CT images were systematically reviewed by experienced cardiac radiologists (R.N.P. or A.v.R.).

We stratified patients according to the LAA filling as either normal filling (complete LAA contrast filling), slow‐flow (any filling defect ≥100 Hounsfield Unit (HU)) or thrombus (filling defect <100 HU) on cardiac CT with a combined first pass and delayed protocol. 14 , 23 A filling defect was defined as a low‐attenuation area within the LAA, displaying less contrast enhancement than the left atrium. We measured HU‐values in the most hypodense area of the filling defect at the discretion of the cardiac radiologist, using the largest possible circular region of interest and by avoiding the inclusion of trabeculae or the atrial wall. Follow‐up was performed after 2 years by trained medical staff, using a structured interview with the patient or their legal representative to assess functional status and the occurrence of new cardiovascular events during follow‐up. Additional information on cardiovascular events was obtained by contacting general practitioners or other treating physicians. Outcomes of interest were functional outcome, measured on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death), recurrent ischemic stroke and occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) during follow‐up. MACE was a composite of acute myocardial infarction, recurrent ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular death.

Statistical Analysis

We compared three groups: patients with LAA thrombus, slow‐flow and normal filling. For baseline characteristics, we used a Kruskal–Wallis non‐parametric test to compare continuous numerous variables. The remaining variables were compared using a Chi‐square test or Fisher exact test as appropriate. To analyze the association between LAA filling and functional outcome, we used ordinal logistic regression to evaluate a shift toward poorer functional outcome on the mRS with higher mRS‐scores indicating worse functional outcome. We corrected for the following potential confounders: age, history of AF, anticoagulant use, history of myocardial infarction, pre‐stroke mRS‐score and intravenous thrombolysis treatment. We used Kaplan–Meier survival analyses to calculate event rates of recurrent ischemic stroke and MACE as a function of time. The association between the LAA filling types and recurrent ischemic stroke and MACE was analyzed using Cox regression models. For the recurrent ischemic stroke analysis, we adjusted for age, history of ischemic stroke and anticoagulation use. For the MACE analysis, we corrected for age, history of myocardial infarction, history of ischemic stroke and anticoagulation use. To analyze the association between slow‐flow and clinical outcomes in patients with cryptogenic stroke, we performed a sensitivity analysis in patients with an unknown stroke etiology. As we included the results of the cardiac CT to determine the stroke etiology, patients with a cardiac thrombus detected on cardiac CT were excluded from this sensitivity analysis. Patients lost to follow‐up were censored at the time of their last available follow‐up and patients who died from another cause were censored at the date of death. We examined both the proportional odds and proportional hazards assumptions to assess the validity of the regression models in this study. We performed a post‐hoc interaction analysis to examine if history of AF might modify the effect of LAA filling on functional outcome, recurrent ischemic stroke and MACE at 2‐year follow up. We added an interaction term to the regression models and performed a subgroup analysis to compare patients with history of AF and patients without history of AF. Analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R foundation for Statistical Computing 2018). For all tests, we used a significance level of 0.05.

All patients or their legal representatives provided written informed consent and the study was approved by the medical ethics committee of Amsterdam UMC, location AMC (2018_017#C2018275). The data may be available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author. S.S.N. had full access to all the study data and takes responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis. This study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 25

Results

Of 452 patients in the Mind the Heart study, we excluded 20 patients with high‐risk cardioaortic sources other than LAA thrombus, and 11 patients with insufficient image quality, resulting in 421 included patients (Figure 1). LAA thrombus was detected in 31 patients (7%), slow‐flow in 69 patients (16%) and normal filling in 321 patients (76%). Figure 2 shows examples of different LAA fillings. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Median age was higher in thrombus and slow‐flow patients compared with patients with normal filling (76 (interquartile range [IQR], 70–88), 77 (IQR, 69–83), 71 (IQR, 60–79) years, P<0.001). More patients with thrombus and slow‐flow had a history of AF compared with patients with normal filling (45%, 39%, 9%, P<0.001). Patients with thrombus had a higher baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, compared with patients with slow‐flow and normal filling (18 [IQR, 9–22], 6 [IQR, 3–17], 5 [IQR, 2–11], P<0.001).

Median follow‐up duration was 16.2 months (IQR, 0.3–27.1) for patients with thrombus, 26.0 months (IQR, 21.5–31.0) for slow‐flow patients and 25.8 months (IQR, 22.5–29.2) for patients with normal filling. After 2‐year follow‐up, 11/31 patients with a thrombus (35%) and 15/69 patients with slow‐flow (22%) were newly diagnosed with AF, compared with 38/321 patients (12%) with normal filling (P<0.001). At 2‐year follow‐up (excluding lost‐to‐follow‐up or deceased patients), a total of 2/13 patients (15%) with LAA thrombus and 20/48 patients (42%) with slow‐flow had no AF, compared with 184/228 patients (81%) with normal filling (Table S2).

Table 2 shows the outcomes at 2‐year follow‐up. All statistical models met the proportional odds and hazards assumptions. With normal filling as the reference category, LAA thrombus was associated with worse functional outcome after 2 years (adjusted common odds ratio [acOR] for a shift toward worse functional outcome: 3.3 [95% CI, 1.5–7.4, P=0.004], Figure 3), but LAA slow‐flow was not (acOR 0.8 [95% CI, 0.5–1.4, P=0.47]). LAA thrombus was associated with an increased risk of MACE (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR), 2.1 [95% CI, 1.0–4.3, P=0.049]), but no such association was found for slow‐flow (aHR, 1.2 [95% CI, 0.7–2.1, P=0.59]) (Figure S1). There was no association between recurrent ischemic stroke and LAA thrombus (aHR, 0.6 [95% CI, 0.1–2.7, P=0.51]) or slow‐flow (aHR, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.3–1.9, P=0.61]) (Figure 4). However, in the subgroup of cryptogenic stroke patients, slow‐flow was associated with a higher recurrent stroke risk (aHR, 4.1 [95% CI, 1.1–15.7, P=0.04], Table 3; Figure 5), but not with an increased risk of MACE (aHR 2.1 [95% CI, 0.6–7.2, P=0.23], Figure S2). The post‐hoc analysis showed that LAA thrombus was associated with a better functional outcome (OR, 0.1 [95% CI, 0.1–0.6, P=0.008]) and a lower risk of MACE in patients with a history of AF compared with patients without history of AF (HR, 0.2 [95% CI, 0.0–1.0, P=0.04]) (Tables S4 and S5). Patients with missing follow‐up data at 2 years (n=23, 5%) did not differ from the retained study population, with the exception of a lower pre‐modified Rankin Score (mRS) and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale at baseline (Table S6).

Discussion

Slow‐flow in the LAA was detected on cardiac CT in approximately 1 in 6 patients with AIS and – similar to LAA thrombus – was strongly associated with AF, both at presentation and during follow‐up. However, unlike LAA thrombi, slow‐flow was not associated with worse functional outcome or the occurrence of MACE. Slow‐flow was also not associated with recurrent ischemic stroke in the full study population. However, the sensitivity analysis suggests that such an association does exist in cryptogenic stroke patients.

Previous studies used different terms to describe slow‐flow on cardiac CT, such as early filling defects or circulatory stasis. 17 , 21 , 22 On TEE, slow‐flow is subsequently defined as spontaneous echo contrast 15 , 17 , 18 , 20 or quantified as decreased blood flow velocity. 10 Previous studies correlated slow‐flow on cardiac CT with spontaneous echo contrast 18 , 26 and a decreased blood flow velocity, 22 , 27 with the latter being a well‐established risk factor for developing thromboembolism. 28 , 29 , 30 The clinical value of slow‐flow on cardiac CT has not been studied extensively yet. One prospective study did not find a significant association between circulatory stasis in the LAA and recurrent stroke risk in patients with AIS. This study included patients with all stroke etiologies that underwent TEE (1 week after stroke) and cardiac CT (mean interval time 5 days after TEE). These results agree with ours, but they did not analyze patients with cryptogenic stroke. 21 Two other studies observed an association between LAA early filling defects on CT and ischemic stroke in patients with AF, but both had a retrospective design and a small sample size. 6 , 9

Patients with LAA slow‐flow had similar characteristics compared with patients with thrombus, but a lower stroke severity at hospital presentation. It can be hypothesized that slow‐flow increases the thrombogenic risk, leading to the formation of small thrombi. Therefore, these “smaller thrombi” are initially not visible on cardiac CT compared with thrombi, and when they migrate to the brain will cause smaller vessel occlusions and therefore less severe strokes.

In our study, approximately 60% of slow‐flow patients were eventually diagnosed with AF after 2‐year follow‐up, compared with 21% of patients with normal filling. A previous study also observed an association between reduced LAA flow velocity on TEE in cryptogenic stroke, and AF occurrence. 31 As such, the identification of LAA slow‐flow should prompt clinicians to search for underlying AF, as this would directly influence patient management. Our main analysis did not show an association between LAA slow‐flow and recurrent stroke, while the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with cryptogenic stroke does suggest that such an association exists. Undetected paroxysmal AF could be an explanation for the increased recurrent stroke risk in the group of cryptogenic stroke patients. These patients were treated with antiplatelet therapy, since no cardioembolic etiology was detected, unlike in the main analysis that included many patients with a history of AF. None of the cryptogenic stroke patients were treated with anticoagulation at baseline or follow up. One could hypothesize that cryptogenic stroke patients with slow‐flow on cardiac CT might benefit from oral anticoagulation, although this needs to be assessed in prospective studies. Additionally, slow‐flow might depict LAA dysfunction, and might therefore be a marker of atrial cardiopathy. 32 The ARCADIA trial did not show a benefit of anticoagulation therapy compared with antiplatelet therapy in reducing recurrent stroke risk in patients with embolic sources of undetermined stroke and atrial cardiopathy. 33 However, they used different markers for atrial cardiopathy, mainly based on ECG lead V1 P‐wave terminal force and serum NT‐proBNP, which raises the question whether other markers of atrial cardiopathy can be used to identify patients who benefit from anticoagulation therapy. Therefore, future research is warranted to better define atrial cardiopathy.

One of the challenges of cardiac CT is to distinguish between a filling defect because of a thrombus or slow‐flow. 20 Implementing a delayed phase cardiac CT improves this differentiation, as thrombi cause a permanent filling defect on delayed imaging, while slow‐flow results in contrast agent opacification. 17 , 18 , 19 , 30 , 34 We mimicked a delayed phase protocol and used a cutoff value of 100 HU for the differentiation between a thrombus and slow‐flow. Earlier studies have shown an adequate prediction of cardiac thrombi with a cutoff value below 100 HU. 35 , 36 Nevertheless, the actual cutoff value remains uncertain. Furthermore, variation in kilovoltage (kV) influences the contrast level in the CT‐image and therefore the LAA HU‐value. 37 As such, dichotomizing the assessment of thrombi versus slow‐flow solely based on a single cut‐off in HU‐values could potentially result in misclassification bias. Previous studies used a LAA/ascending aorta HU‐ratio on cardiac CT to differentiate filling defects. This decreased the sensitivity and specificity of cardiac CT for the detection of thrombi compared with visual analysis of the filling defect. 18 , 19 Our results show that slow‐flow comprises a group of patients that partly has similar characteristics as patients with thrombus, but the differences in stroke severity and functional outcome show that the group of patients with slow‐flow can indeed appear to be a separate entity, suggesting that we are able to adequately differentiate between a thrombus and slow‐flow on cardiac CT. These findings support the use of acute cardiac CT in acute stroke care for the detection of a cardioembolic etiology and the detection of slow‐flow. Currently, TEE is considered the reference modality in diagnosing a cardioembolic source of stroke with the benefit of dynamic features that can measure flow velocities, but it is semi‐invasive, time‐consuming and not always readily available, with a likelihood of causing diagnostic delay. 3 , 14 , 38 Cardiac CT is time efficient, especially when added to the acute stroke imaging protocol, and previous studies have shown a higher or similar diagnostic yield in detecting cardioembolic sources compared with TEE. 12 , 15 , 39 , 40 As technology advances with improved scan protocols and reduced radiation and contrast exposure, cardiac CT may serve as a valuable alternative to TEE in the differentiation between thrombus and slow‐flow and the detection of cardioembolic sources of stroke.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not compare the detection of slow‐flow on cardiac CT with TEE, which is the reference modality for the detection of cardioembolic sources. However, previous research indicates that slow‐flow detected on echocardiography and cardiac CT are similar phenomena. Second, our study had a small sample size and a limited number of outcome events, especially for the sensitivity analysis on patients with cryptogenic stroke. Exploring potential associations in prospective studies with larger sample sizes is warranted, and our results need to be interpreted with caution. Third, this is a single‐center study, which could limit the generalizability.

Conclusions

Patients with LAA slow‐flow were older, more frequently had a history of AF and showed a low stroke severity. They did not have a worse functional outcome, nor did they have a higher number of recurrent ischemic stroke or MACE when assessing the full study population. Patients with cryptogenic stroke did show an association between LAA slow‐flow and recurrent ischemic stroke. Prolonged rhythm monitoring might be indicated, as slow‐flow could potentially be a parameter for paroxysmal AF.
