Predicting the severity of COVID-19 using machine learning methods
Authors: Mehdi Ezati, Bahar Masroor, Zohreh Kahramfar, Roya Najafi-Vosough, Fatemeh Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 represents a wide range of clinical severity. Early identification of patients at high risk of severe disease is critical for appropriate clinical management and resource allocation. This study aims to predict COVID-19 severity using machine learning methods.MethodsIn this retrospective study, laboratory data from 816 hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Hamadan Province, Iran, were analyzed. According to the World Health Organization guideline, patients were classified into two groups: severe and non-severe based on clinical evaluation. Blood parameters including D-dimer, red cell distribution width, mean platelet volume, platelet count, and others were extracted from patient records. The performance of machine learning methods, including support vector machine, least-squares support vector machine, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting, was evaluated in predicting disease severity. Statistical comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney and chi-square tests.ResultsLevels of D-dimer, red cell distribution width, and mean platelet volume were significantly elevated in the severe group (P-value < 0.0001). Random forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting mean sensitivities were 76% and ≥ 74%, respectively. The area under the curve values for these methods were 0.91 and 0.90, respectively. D-dimer was identified as a strong predictor of COVID-19 severity based on random forest analysis.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that random forest outperforms support vector machine and least-squares support vector machine in predicting the severity of COVID-19 using routine blood tests. Applying machine learning methods may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and facilitating timely clinical interventions.Keywords: COVID-19, Machine learning, D-dimer, Severity, Random forest, Support vector machine

Background

Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) has emerged as a global public health crisis and continues to spread worldwide. The clinical signs and symptoms of the disease vary from mild to severe manifestations [1, 2]. The most common symptoms include fever, cough, headache, and myalgia; however, other symptoms such as diarrhea, dyspnea, sore throat, nausea, and olfactory and gustatory disturbances have also been reported [3]. Screening for COVID-19 remains challenging, as many patients are asymptomatic, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) testing may yield false-negative results [4]. Laboratory tests, including complete blood count (CBC), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), D-dimer, prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), and troponin, were ordered to guide physicians in diagnosis [2–5].

Since the onset of the pandemic, numerous studies have sought reliable biomarkers to predict disease progression [5, 6]. Several parameters, including D-dimer, platelet count (Plt), red cell distribution width (RDW), and mean platelet volume (MPV), have been identified as prognostic biomarkers for COVID-19 [3, 4, 6]. Increased levels of D-dimer up to 2000 ng/mL and/or higher are associated with an elevated risk of thrombosis, mortality, and COVID-19 severity [7]. Elevated initial D-dimer levels are associated with disease progression, increased liver enzyme levels, and a hypercoagulable state [8–10].

Due to the vast clinical manifestations and variation in laboratory findings, the diagnosis, treatment, classification, and monitoring of COVID-19 require the development of up-to-date evidence-based guidelines, along with the application of integrative strategies approved by reputable organizations, including the World Health Organization (WHO) [1, 11].

In recent years, machine learning (ML) methods have been increasingly used as novel tools for diagnosis and prediction in various diseases [12, 13]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ML methods can outperform clinicians in analyzing blood indices such as red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), hematocrit (HCT), Plt, and white blood cell (WBC) count [14]. Accurate forecasting of viral infection spread, the development of efficient tools, and proper validation approaches are essential components in healthcare research [15]. This progress has recently been driven by a shift from relying solely on traditional statistical approaches to the development of integrated data–based and hybrid models [16, 17].

Given various health and social concerns, precise diagnosis and accurate prediction of COVID-19 severity are critical for patient management and policymaking. Achieving this requires the use of ML methods, univariate time-series analyses, and hybrid models, as well as the involvement of multidisciplinary specialists [18]. Addressing these challenges necessitates collaboration among clinicians, public health professionals, and experts in mathematics and statistics. ML methods have also been widely utilized in COVID-19–related research to predict disease severity, clinical outcomes, and resource allocation [19]. Accurate prediction of COVID-19 severity may improve quality of care, guide resource use, and ultimately reduce mortality [20]. In light of this, the present study was conducted to predict the severity of COVID-19 using ML methods.

Methods

Data collection and preparation

In this retrospective cohort study, laboratory data from 816 COVID-19 patients admitted to three hospitals in Hamadan Province, Iran, were analyzed. These are the main central and referral hospitals for infectious diseases in Hamadan. The patients were admitted to the hospitals between October 2021 and July 2023. Inclusion criteria were confirmed COVID-19 infection (by CT scan and qRT-PCR and/ or clinical manifestations), hospitalization, consent to participate in the study, and availability of patient data. Exclusion criteria included missing at least one of the study variables and incomplete consent forms. All data were extracted from hospital records using a standardized checklist without any identifying information. The dataset contained demographic variables (age and gender) and laboratory findings. Specifically, the following blood indices and coagulation tests were included: RBC, WBC, Plt, Hb, HCT, MCV, MCH, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), RDW, MPV, PT, PTT, and D-dimer. Due to accurate data collection and observation of the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, there were no missing data. Results of laboratory parameters were collected from the records of the first ordered tests at the time of initial diagnosis and before evaluation of clinical signs/symptoms at the time of admission/hospitalization. Disease severity was determined as severe or non-severe to guide treatment by two clinicians, using clinical features according to WHO guidelines [1] or updated versions, the extent of lung involvement based on CT scan scoring, and laboratory findings at the time of admission. Severe cases were defined as requiring oxygen therapy in the hospital general wards and/or the intensive care unit (ICU) at the time of hospitalization or within the following few days up to one week. ICU admission and related decisions were made according to the decisions of two clinicians and local guidelines, considering available facilities and conditions. To address class imbalance, the dataset was balanced using Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). A key reason for selecting SMOTE was its conceptual simplicity, computational efficiency, and its widespread adoption as a strong baseline [21].

Machine learning methods

Four ML methods were applied to predict COVID-19 severity: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Least-Square Support Vector Machine (LS-SVM), Random Forest (RF), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). SVM is a supervised learning model that identifies the optimal decision boundary (hyperplane) separating data points of different classes. When the data is not linearly separable, SVM uses a kernel function to map the data into a higher-dimensional space in which a linear separation becomes possible. In this study, the radial basis function (RBF) kernel was used due to its strong performance [22]. LS-SVM is a modified version of SVM that simplifies the optimization process. Instead of solving a complex quadratic programming problem, LS-SVM solves a set of linear equations in the dual space, making it more computationally efficient and suitable for certain datasets [23]. RF is an ensemble-based classification method that builds multiple decision trees using bootstrap sampling, where bootstrapped subsets of the training data are used to train each tree. The final prediction is obtained by aggregating the outputs of all trees, which helps improve accuracy and reduce the risk of overfitting. In this study, the importance of input features was assessed using Mean Decrease Accuracy [24]. XGBoost is a powerful ML method used to build predictive tree-based methods. This method was applied to solving classification and regression problems. The structure of XGBoost is based on decision trees and gradient-boosting methods. In this method, decision trees are combined using gradient-boosting. The XGBoost is more accurate than a single decision tree method [25].

The performance of the ML-based classifiers was evaluated using a cross-validation approach, where the data was randomly divided into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. This process was repeated 100 times, and the average value for each criterion was calculated. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software version 4.4.1 with “e1071”, “kernlab”, “CORElearn”, “xgboost”, “randomForest”, “DMwR”, “pROC”, and “fastshap” packages. The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare continuous variables between groups, and chi-square test was used for categorical variables such as gender with SPSS software version 23. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 816 hospitalized patients with COVID-19, 236 (28.9%) were classified as severe and 580 (71.1%) as non-severe. The demographic characteristics of the patients were summarized in Table 1. The severe group was significantly older than the non-severe group, with a mean (SD) age of 61.54 (11.68) years compared to 51.40 (14.12) years (Statistic (U) U = 38190.5, P-Value < 0.0001). A significant association was also found between gender and disease severity (Cramer’s v = 0.17, P-Value < 0.001), with a higher proportion of female patients in the severe group (57.2%) than in the non-severe group (38.6%).

A comparative analysis of laboratory parameters between the two groups revealed significant differences in several biomarkers (Table 2). Specifically, levels of D-dimer, MPV, and RDW were significantly elevated in the severe group compared to the non-severe group (P-Value < 0.0001). Statistically significant differences were also observed in Plt, Hb, HCT, PT, PTT, WBC, and MCHC between the two groups (P-value < 0.0001). Discussion

In this study, we aimed to predict disease severity in COVID-19 patients who had previously been clinically evaluated, using machine learning (ML) methods based on hematological parameters and coagulation test results. Our results indicated a statistically significant relationship between gender and age and disease severity. This pattern may reflect potential physiological or immunological differences, although further investigation is warranted to account for possible confounders. Our results showed that D-dimer, RDW, MPV, and Plt demonstrated the highest discriminatory power between the severe and non-severe groups. The RF model also achieved the highest classification accuracy and showed superior quantitative performance compared with the other models. It is worth noting that these results were obtained in our specific study context, considering the study setting.

Different biomarkers—including hematologic, immunologic, hepatic, and cardiac markers—have been investigated to determine COVID-19 severity, progression, and outcomes. Among them, RBC indices such as RDW, MPV, and Plt, along with D-dimer, are routine, inexpensive, and frequently ordered tests that provide valuable information in this field [5]. On the other hand, the application of well-established ML models and their integration with other tools can improve diagnostic accuracy. These models are also useful for the rapid differentiation between viral infections and comorbid conditions [26–28]. ML models can also analyze clinical and genetic data to guide clinicians in patient management and predict required hospital service needs. This is particularly useful in pandemic settings and during shortages of medical facilities and ICU beds in developing countries [29]. Regarding D-dimer, our findings were consistent with the findings of Asteris et al. (2023), who identified D-dimer as one of the top five variables for predicting ICU admission using an artificial neural network (ANN) model, achieving 95.97% accuracy [30]. Similarly, studies by Yao and Zhang reported that elevated D-dimer levels at admission time were associated with increased mortality in COVID-19 patients [31, 32]. We did not evaluate the relationship between D-dimer and the mortality rate as the final patient outcome. These results suggest D-dimer not only as a possible diagnostic marker but also as a potential input feature in ML-based severity-prediction models. Seyedtabib (2024) predicted COVID-19 outcomes—especially mortality—using various clinical and paraclinical variables. They used decision tree (DT), XGBoost, SVM, Naïve Bayes (NB), and RF models, identifying D-dimer and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) as powerful paraclinical predictors of mortality [3]. In another study, DT, Logistic Model Tree (LMT), and Classification and Regression Tree (CART) models were used to identify effective predictors of COVID-19 mortality. Unlike our findings, PTT was reported as an important predictor [4]. Conversely, in the study by Kukar et al. (2021), which employed the XGBoost algorithm for COVID-19 detection, D-dimer was not ranked among the top features. Instead, MCHC, eosinophil count, albumin, and international normalized ratio (INR) were more influential [33]. Kukar focused on early diagnosis using biochemical and hematological variables. This discrepancy may result from differences in study objectives, populations, settings, using of different guidelines to manage patients, or ML models. We designed the study to classify COVID-19 patients based on clinical evaluation at admission using valid guidelines [1]. We aimed to predict their need for ICU admission and oxygen therapy based on the initial results of general hematological parameters at diagnosis. Patient medical history, underlying diseases, vaccination status, virus variants, and the use of different treatment protocols might affect the applicability and validity of the models. Vaccination reduces illness severity in COVID-19, but the type of vaccine, viral strains, and the phase of the pandemic could alter the reported rate of severe and non-severe cases. These variations can interfere with laboratory values and, consequently, with the capabilities of machine learning models [34, 35]. Moreover, the timing and purpose of laboratory data collection, whether at initial diagnosis or at admission, should be considered as other influential factors. Several mechanisms have been proposed for increased D-dimer levels in COVID-19 patients. Fever, disturbances in coagulation and anticoagulation, and inflammatory cytokine secretion may induce a hypercoagulable state, potentially lead to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pulmonary and other organ injuries, greater need for ICU admission, and prolonged hospitalization [7–9].

One of our findings was the role of RDW in predicting COVID-19 severity. This aligns with the study by Foy et al. (2020), which showed that elevated RDW was associated with increased mortality in hospitalized COVID-19 patients and may serve as a routine marker for risk stratification [36]. Jamshidi (2022) identified RDW as the fourth most important parameter among 15 factors for ICU decision-making using an RF model, achieving 70% sensitivity and 75% specificity for mortality prediction [37]. In the current study, a sensitivity of 76% was achieved using the RF model. In contrast to our results, Suruliandi (2025) reported that RDW performed poorly in diagnosing COVID-19 compared with other clinical and paraclinical datasets, with SVM achieving 94% sensitivity [38]. RDW indicates variation in RBC size and reflects anemia. It associated with greater disease severity and increased mortality in COVID-19 patients [5]. However, other causes of anemia and aspects of patient management could distort RDW results and affect their reproducibility.

MPV and platelet count (Plt) also emerged as significant variables in our study. According to Beceren et al. (2023), MPV levels were significantly higher in deceased COVID-19 patients and demonstrated superior performance in Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis than plateletcrit (PCT) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [39]. Safdari et al. (2025) conducted a multi-country study to validate ML models for COVID-19 prediction scoring using only hematological indices. They reported increased MPV and decreased Plt levels in COVID-19 inpatients across different hospital wards, and XGBoost outperformed the other ML models [40]. Dong (2025) and Panagiotis (2025) used logistic regression (LR) and a dynamic ensemble refinement greedy algorithm (DERGA), respectively [41, 42]. Panagiotis identified NLR as a significant predictor of ICU admission risk, while Dong identified MPV as a moderate predictor of survival in patients with severe COVID-19. The MPV reflects both inflammatory and thrombotic activity, making it a potential indicator of disease severity. Alterations in MPV and Plt might influence immune cell counts, immune system function, and Plt–WBC crosstalk patterns, which may potentially affect disease severity and patient outcomes [39, 40]. However, given the reactive nature of MPV and Plt changes and their dependence on multiple factors, caution is warranted when interpreting these results and applying the models.

From a ML perspective, our findings demonstrated the superior performance of RF in predicting disease severity. The ensemble-based structure of the RF and its ability to integrate complex data enabled it to achieve high classification accuracy, balanced performance, and robustness [24, 37]. Automated ML systems analyzing hematochemical markers can also differentiate between SARS-CoV-2 and influenza infections in children, potentially accelerating the screening process [27].

Based on this approach, we imaging a future where automated laboratory systems and ML methods integrate to enable rapid and intelligent screening for infectious diseases, especially during pandemics when quick decisions and efficient use of resources are critical.

Limitation of the study

Use of a single-province dataset, the retrospective study design, potential biases, low generalizability, and the lack of an external dataset or simulation-based validation were among the noted limitations of the current study. Nevertheless, we collected patient data regardless of vaccination status, virus variants, or initial treatment variations. These factors may confound the results and affect their interpretation.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, integrating routine blood test results with ML methods, particularly RF, can serve as a suitable tool for predicting COVID-19 severity. D-dimer, RDW, MPV, and Plt were able to distinguish severe cases and proved to be reliable biomarkers. These methods can support clinical decision-making, enable care prioritization, and enhance resource allocation in the management of COVID-19.

Future work

Designing an ongoing study with a larger sample size, performing multicenter validation, developing hybrid models (RF + Deep Learning), and enabling real-time deployment in hospitals were suggested to improve validity and reliability.
