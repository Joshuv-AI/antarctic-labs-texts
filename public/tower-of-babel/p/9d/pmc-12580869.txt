Amivantamab Plus Chemotherapy in Japanese Patients With EGFR Exon 20 Insertions NSCLC
Authors: Satoru Kitazono, Akira Ono, Tetsuji Kawamura, Osamu Hataji, Hiroshi Tanaka, Shingo Matsumoto, Naohiro Watanabe, Hiromi Nagashima, Masahide Oki, Michiko Takahashi, Tomoko Anazawa, Takahiro Shirai, Aimi Yamashita, Honeylet Wortman‐Vayn, Archan Bhattacharya, Trishala Agrawal, Mahadi Baig, Roland E Knoblauch, Hidetoshi Hayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exon 20 insertion mutations (EGFR Exon 20ins) are the third most common mutations in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and are associated with a poorer prognosis and resistance to conventional EGFR‐tyrosine kinase inhibitors. This subpopulation analysis of the open‐label phase 3 trial (PAPILLION) evaluates the efficacy and safety of amivantamab–chemotherapy versus chemotherapy among Japanese patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC with EGFR Exon 20ins mutation (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04538664). Patients were randomized 1:1 to either intravenous amivantamab plus carboplatin/pemetrexed chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone. The primary endpoint was progression‐free survival (PFS) by blinded independent central review; the secondary endpoints included objective response rate, duration of response, PFS after first subsequent therapy, overall survival, and safety. The overall population (n = 308) included 34 Japanese patients (amivantamab–chemotherapy, n = 19; chemotherapy, n = 15). Median PFS was 15.5 (95% CI 8.0, NE) months with amivantamab–chemotherapy compared with 5.6 (95% CI 3.0, 7.0) months (HR = 0.22 [0.09, 0.53]) for chemotherapy alone. Improvements in secondary endpoints were also greater in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm than the chemotherapy arm. The predominant adverse events associated with amivantamab–chemotherapy were reversible hematologic and EGFR‐related toxic effects; 2 of 19 patients discontinued all study agents due to treatment‐emergent adverse events. Efficacy and safety results in this Japanese subpopulation were consistent with those in the overall population and support the first‐line use of amivantamab–chemotherapy in this setting. Early identification of patients with EGFR Exon 20ins mutations, preferably with more sensitive next‐generation sequencing‐based methods, is important to ensure appropriate patient access to amivantamab–chemotherapy. Introduction

Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the most common type of lung cancer, is associated with a poor prognosis [1]. In Japan, NSCLC accounts for 80% of the two main types of lung cancer and is associated with a higher 3‐year survival rate in patients who received epidermal growth factor receptor‐tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR‐TKIs) (33%) compared with those who did not (17%) [2].

The EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations (EGFR Exon 20ins) are the third most common EGFR mutations, accounting for up to 12% of all observed EGFR mutations in NSCLC and up to 4% of all NSCLCs [3]. NSCLC with EGFR Exon 20ins mutations is associated with a poorer prognosis and is resistant to conventional EGFR‐TKIs [3]. Platinum‐based chemotherapy remains the standard of care for patients with NSCLC with EGFR Exon 20ins mutations [4, 5, 6]. Therefore, there is a need for a targeted therapy for patients with NSCLC who are resistant to EGFR‐TKIs and several candidates have shown activity in early clinical trials [4].

Amivantamab is a bispecific antibody that targets both the EGFR and cMET pathways as well as possessing immune cell‐directing activity [7, 8, 9]. Blockage of cMET overcomes this alternative pathway that bypasses TKI inhibition and thereby facilitates cancer cell survival [7]. Results from an in vitro study found that, in the presence of human immune cells (PBMCs), amivantamab induced a dose‐dependent decrease in tumor cell viability and increase in apoptosis [8]. It is thought that combining the ability of amivantamab to not only target EGFR/cMET pathways but also to engage immune cells with chemotherapy‐mediated cell death may produce deeper and more durable responses compared with those seen with chemotherapy alone [10].

A recently published international, open‐label phase 3 trial (PAPILLON) confirmed the superior efficacy of amivantamab–chemotherapy in 308 patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC with EGFR Exon 20ins mutation [11]. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was significantly greater among patients treated with amivantamab–chemotherapy (11.4 months) compared with chemotherapy alone (6.7 months) corresponding to a hazard ratio for disease progression or death of 0.395 (p < 0.001) [11].

This subpopulation analysis of the PAPILLON trial evaluates the efficacy and safety of amivantamab–chemotherapy versus chemotherapy among Japanese patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

PAPILLON was an open‐label, randomized, phase 3 study of amivantamab–chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone conducted in 129 clinical sites globally, including 23 sites in Japan (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04538664).

Eligible patients were 18 years and had treatment‐naïve, locally advanced, or metastatic NSCLC with a documented EGFR Exon20 ins mutation and ECOG PS 0 or 1. Brief EGFR‐TKI monotherapy was allowed if there was a documented lack of response. Patients with treated brain metastases were also eligible if they were asymptomatic, clinically stable, and had not received glucocorticoid treatment for ≥ 2 weeks before randomization. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Table S1.

The trial was performed in compliance with Good Clinical Practice, the Declaration of Helsinki, applicable regulatory requirements, and sponsor policies on bioethics and human biologic samples of the trial sponsor, Janssen Research and Development, as detailed in the original publication. All enrolled patients provided written, informed consent.

Randomization

Patients were randomized 1:1 in a nonblinded fashion to amivantamab–chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone based on a computer‐generated randomization schedule.

Randomization was stratified by ECOG Performance Score (PS), history of brain metastases, and prior administration of an EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Subjects from the chemotherapy group had an option to cross over to receive second‐line amivantamab monotherapy after documented disease progression according to RECIST v1.1 and confirmation by blinded independent central review (BICR).

Treatment

Intravenous amivantamab 1400 mg (1750 mg if body weight ≥ 80 kg) was administered weekly for the first 4 weeks, with the first infusion split over 2 days (at a dose of 350 mg on day 1 and the remainder on day 2), then increased to 1750 mg (2100 mg if body weight ≥ 80 kg) in 21‐day cycles from cycle 3 (week 7). The dosing regimen for amivantamab was the same when used in combination with chemotherapy and as monotherapy after crossover. Chemotherapy was administered on the first day of each cycle as carboplatin (AUC of 5 mg/mL·min for up to 4 cycles) and pemetrexed (500 mg/m2 of body surface area) until disease progression.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was progression‐free survival (PFS) according to RECIST v1.1 by BICR. The secondary endpoints included: (i) objective response rate (ORR), defined as the percentage of participants with the best response of complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) by RECIST v1.1, (ii) duration of response (DoR), defined as the time from the date of the first documented response (CR or PR) until the date of documented progression or death, (iii) PFS after first subsequent therapy, defined as the time from randomization to the earliest progression event after that used for the primary endpoint PFS or death after starting the next line of treatment, (iv) overall survival (OS), and (v) safety.

Disease assessments were performed according to RECIST by BICR via imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) within 28 days before randomization, at 6 weeks, and every 6 weeks (±7 days) for the first 18 months, and then every 12 weeks (±7 days) until disease progression. Survival, subsequent treatment, and disease status were assessed approximately every 12 weeks after disease progression.

Safety was assessed by monitoring of (i) AEs, graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI‐CTCAE), Version 5.0; AEs were assessed according to seriousness, causal association with study drug, and severity grade.

Analysis Method and Statistical Methods

Details of the sample size and power calculations for the overall study population have been published previously.

This Japanese subpopulation analysis was preplanned. Tests of statistical significance were conducted at a 2‐sided alpha level of 0.05 for the overall population, whereas no hypothesis testing was performed for the Japanese subpopulation.

Results

Patient and Treatment Characteristics

Between December 2020 and November 2022, a total of 308 patients were randomized (amivantamab–chemotherapy, n = 153; chemotherapy, n = 155), including 34 Japanese patients (amivantamab–chemotherapy, n = 19; chemotherapy, n = 15). Patient disposition for Japanese patients is shown in Figure 1. At the data cutoff for primary analysis (May 3, 2023), 9 patients (47.4%) in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm discontinued all study treatments (disease progression, n = 7, adverse events, n = 2) compared with 14 patients (93.3%) in the chemotherapy arm due to disease progression. Eleven of 14 patients (73%) randomized to the chemotherapy arm subsequently crossed over to receive second‐line amivantamab monotherapy upon disease progression and confirmation.

Patient disposition.

Baseline characteristics of the Japanese patients were generally similar between the amivantamab–chemotherapy and chemotherapy arms, except that a slightly larger proportion of smokers was present in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm (Table 1). Median (range) follow‐up was 15.6 (4.6+, 23.6) months with amivantamab–chemotherapy and 20.1 (8.1, 25.5+) months with chemotherapy (note that + signifies censored observation).

Efficacy

Median PFS was significantly longer with amivantamab–chemotherapy (15.5 [95% CI 8.0‐NE] months) compared with chemotherapy (5.6 [95% CI 3.0–7.0] months) corresponding to an HR of 0.22 (0.09–0.53; Figure 2). At 18 months, the progression‐free event rate was 40% in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm and 0% in the chemotherapy arm.

Primary endpoint of PFS by BICR in Japanese subpopulation.

Regarding secondary efficacy endpoints, best overall response and ORR are shown in Table 2. The ORR assessed by BICR was 72.2% in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm and 66.7% in the chemotherapy arm. The maximum change in tumor size from baseline by best overall response based on blinded independent central review for individual patients in both treatment arms is shown in Figure 3. The mean percent decrease in tumor size was 46.9% with amivantamab–chemotherapy and 42.6% with chemotherapy. Median DOR was 9.8 (95% CI 2.8–NE) months in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm and 5.0 (95% CI 4.1–NE) months in the chemotherapy arm. Median PFS after first subsequent therapy was greater with amivantamab–chemotherapy (18.6 [95% CI 14.6–NE] months) compared with chemotherapy alone (13.9 [95% CI 8.2–NE] months) corresponding to an HR of 0.44 (95% CI 0.15–1.34; Figure 4). A breakdown of first subsequent systemic anticancer therapies is detailed in Table S2. On an interim OS analysis, the median OS was NE (95% CI 18.6–NE) months in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm and 25.5 (95% CI 13.7–NE) months in the chemotherapy arm corresponding to an HR of 0.79 (95% CI 0.17–3.62) (Figure 5).

Safety

Regarding safety, treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs) and treatment‐related TEAEs occurred in all Japanese patients in both the amivantamab–chemotherapy and chemotherapy arms (Table 3). The median duration of treatment in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm was 13.2 months and 5.1 months in the chemotherapy arm. The most common TEAEs (incidence ≥ 15%) are shown in Table 4. The most common TEAEs in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm were neutropenia (73.7%), paronychia (73.7%), leukopenia (68.4%), decreased appetite (68.4%), peripheral edema (57.9%), and constipation (57.9%). The most common TEAEs in the chemotherapy arm were nausea (53.3%), constipation (46.7%), neutropenia (40.0%), leukopenia (40.0%), decreased appetite (40.0%), and increased alanine aminotransferase (40.0%). TEAEs associated with EGFR or cMET inhibition, such as paronychia and hypoalbuminemia, respectively, were predominantly observed in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm. In the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm, pneumonitis was reported in one (5.3%) patient. Serious treatment‐related TEAEs occurred in 9 patients (47.4%), which included 7 patients (36.8%) in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm and 2 (13.3%) patients in the chemotherapy arm. As anticipated, the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm also had a higher proportion of Grade ≥ 3 TEAEs (89.5%) compared with that of the chemotherapy arm (53.3%). However, most TEAEs were manageable through dose interruption and dose reduction, and discontinuation due to TEAE was infrequent. In addition, there were no deaths related to TEAEs in either treatment arm. Six of 19 patients discontinued treatment with any study agent due to a TEAE in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm. The reasons for treatment discontinuation of any agent in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm were alanine aminotransferase increased, aspartate aminotransferase increased, C‐reactive protein increased, dermatitis acneiform, infusion‐related reaction, and pneumonitis reported in one patient each. Overall, 2/19 patients discontinued all study agents due to a TEAE in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm. Twelve of 19 patients had dose reduction of any study agent due to a TEAE in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm. The reasons for dose reduction of any study agent in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm were neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, alanine aminotransferase increased, dermatitis acneiform, and paronychia, each adverse event reported in 3 patients. The dose reduction of amivantamab due to TEAE occurred in 8/19 patients and was due to dermatitis acneiform and paronychia. The incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) was comparable between the two arms, with 2 patients in each arm (one patient each with Grade 2 and Grade 3 in the amivantamab–chemotherapy and 2 patients with Grade 2 in the chemotherapy arm). There were no SAEs or deaths due to VTE, and no patients discontinued due to VTE in either arm.

Discussion

The overall PAPILLON study demonstrated that amivantamab–chemotherapy treatment significantly prolonged PFS compared with chemotherapy alone according to BICR (hazard ratio for disease progression or death, 0.395; 95% CI, 0.30–0.53; p < 0.001) [10]. The results of this subanalysis demonstrated that the reduction in the risk of progression or death in the Japanese subset was consistent with that observed in the overall population. Specifically, median PFS with amivantamab–chemotherapy was 15.5 months among Japanese patients compared with 11.4 months for the overall population. Furthermore, PFS curves showed a clear, early separation indicating rapid disease control that improved with longer follow‐up, as reflected in the 18‐month PFS event‐free rate of 40.0% in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm compared with 0.0% in the chemotherapy arm.

Further, results for ORR, PFS after first subsequent therapy, and OS in the Japanese subpopulation were largely consistent with those of the overall population and showed a favorable trend for amivantamab–chemotherapy versus chemotherapy. In particular, the ORR was 72.2% in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm for the Japanese subpopulation compared with 73.0% for the overall population. On the other hand, the ORR in the chemotherapy arm for the Japanese subpopulation (66.7%) was notably greater than that in the overall population (47.4%); this might have been impacted due to the small size of the patient cohort in chemotherapy (n = 15).

Response to treatment appeared to be relatively rapid among Japanese patients, as observed for the overall population, with a notable separation in the Kaplan–Meier curves from 3 to 6 months after randomization. OS showed a favorable trend in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm, even though 11 of 14 (78.6%) Japanese patients in the chemotherapy arm crossed over to amivantamab monotherapy. This result is consistent with that of the overall population and supports the first‐line use of amivantamab–chemotherapy. In addition, the responses in the amivantamab–chemotherapy appeared to be deeper and more durable compared to chemotherapy alone, with 53% of patients in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm and 7% of patients in the chemotherapy arm ongoing treatment at data cut‐off. Regarding safety, the profile of adverse events observed with amivantamab–chemotherapy in the Japanese subpopulation was broadly consistent with the overall population and no new safety signals were observed. The Grade 3 TEAEs were mainly due to chemotherapy‐associated toxicities and anticipated EGFR‐ and cMET‐related toxicities with amivantamab. All toxicities were manageable with dose interruptions and dose reductions, with rare discontinuation and no TEAEs leading to death. The risk of VTE events in the amivantamab–chemotherapy arm of the PAPILLON Japanese subpopulation reflects the overall risk observed among patients with NSCLC with no major clinical effects. Japanese ethnicity is one of the risk factors for ILD, although only one pneumonitis case was reported in this Japanese subpopulation [12].

As previously noted in the global study report, these results support the value of testing for EGFR Exon20ins at the diagnosis of metastatic disease preferably using next‐generation sequencing (NGS), which is more sensitive and cost‐effective for detecting this mutation than polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests [13]. Indeed, an analysis of real‐world data sets projected that approximately 50% of EGFR Exon 20ins identified by NGS would have been missed by PCR [13]. Biomarker testing in Japan largely follows practices in the United States, and both standalone PCR and multiplex NGS are currently approved in Japan for the detection of EGFR mutations [14]. Such testing can help identify patients who are likely to benefit from targeted therapy such as amivantamab–chemotherapy.

Results of this subpopulation analysis of the PAPILLON study confirm that, as shown in the overall population, Japanese patients with previously untreated, advanced NSCLC with EGFR Exon 20ins who received amivantamab–chemotherapy had significantly longer PFS than those who received chemotherapy alone. Safety results were consistent with adverse event reports of the global overall population. Our results indicate that amivantamab–chemotherapy was an effective first‐line treatment in Japanese patients with advanced NSCLC with EGFR Exon 20ins.
