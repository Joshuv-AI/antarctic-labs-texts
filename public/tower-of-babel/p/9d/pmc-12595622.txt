Association between adequacy of antenatal care and uptake of intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy among women in Ghana
Authors: Francis B Bayuo, Ernest O Antwi, Nixon Adu-Boahen, Esther A Effah, Samuel K Appiah, Halima A Opoku, Mercy B Efiong, Gifty Acheampomaa, Mavis M Senyah, Adusei Bofa, Georgina Afoakwah, Zelalem T Haile

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy (IPTp-SP) is an important strategy for preventing malaria in pregnant women and their unborn children. Ghana has adopted IPTp-SP as part of its National Malaria Control Programme, but its uptake remains suboptimal. This study examined the association between adequacy of antenatal care (ANC) and the uptake of at least three doses of IPTp-SP.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (n = 3692). The adequacy of ANC was assessed based on the receipt of the recommended number of ANC visits, the timing of the first ANC visit, receipt of all components of ANC, and receipt of ANC from a skilled provider. Descriptive statistics and multivariable regression analyses were performed.ResultsOverall, the uptake of at least three doses of IPTp-SP was 61.3%. Approximately 39.2% of participants had at least 8 ANC visits, 63.6% had their first ANC visit within the first trimester, 75.4% reported receiving all components of ANC and 95.5% received ANC from a skilled provider. In the multivariable model, the odds of receiving at least three doses of IPTp-SP were higher among those who had eight or more ANC visits 1.50 (1.18, 1.90; p = 0.001), who had their first ANC visit within the first trimester 1.74 (1.44, 2.09; p < 0.001), and those who received all components of ANC 1.62 (1.29, 2.03; p < 0.001).ConclusionsReceipt of adequate ANC services is positively associated with the uptake of at least three doses of IPTp-SP. This association is independent of potential confounders. Background

Malaria in pregnancy remains a significant public health concern across sub-Saharan Africa, contributing to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including anaemia, low birth weight, and preterm delivery [1, 2]. To mitigate these risks, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the use of intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) [3]. Malaria prevention during pregnancy is not only a national health priority but also a critical component of the global health agenda. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3, call for the reduction of maternal mortality (target 3.1) and the end of epidemics such as malaria (target 3.3) by 2030 [4]. Universal access to comprehensive maternal and child health services is essential to achieving these targets.

Antenatal care (ANC) serves as the primary platform for delivering IPTp, and numerous studies have demonstrated a positive association between ANC utilization and IPTp uptake [5, 6]. In Ghana, for example, IPTp uptake was 79.9% among women who attended four or more ANC visits, compared to 8.3% among those with fewer visits [7]. In another study among Ghanaian women, those who attended 8 or more visits had 16 times higher odds of receiving three or more IPTp doses compared to those with fewer visits. The timing and quality of ANC are equally important in promoting IPTp adherence. Early initiation of ANC particularly in the first trimester has been associated with increased IPTp uptake across several settings [8, 9]. Furthermore, the frequency of ANC visits plays a pivotal role. However, the number of ANC visits and time of first ANC visit alone may not fully capture the adequacy of care received.

Previous research has primarily focused on the number and timing of ANC visits as proxies for ANC adequacy, often using the outdated WHO benchmark of four visits [10–12]. In contrast, the 2016 updated WHO guidelines recommend a minimum of eight ANC contacts, ideally beginning in the first trimester, and emphasize the quality and comprehensiveness of services received [13]. Although recent studies have examined the impact of attending eight instead of four ANC contacts [6, 14, 15], none has specifically investigated the role of ANC service components currently captured in population-based surveys such as the Demographic and Health Surveys. The current study adopts a multidimensional definition of adequate ANC that reflects these updated standards. Specifically, adequate ANC was defined as meeting all three of the following criteria: (1) at least eight ANC visits, (2) initiation of care within the first trimester, (3) receipt of seven essential ANC components and (4) receiving ANC from skilled providers that include doctors, midwives and nurses. These components include blood pressure measurement, urine and blood sample collection, fetal heartbeat monitoring, and counseling on nutrition, breastfeeding, and vaginal bleeding.

Understanding the association between this broader definition of ANC adequacy and IPTp uptake is essential for designing effective interventions. Prior studies have shown that the quality of ANC such as counseling and timely administration of IPTp can enhance adherence [16]. Conversely, systemic barriers like medication stock-outs or inadequate provider training may impede IPTp coverage, even when ANC attendance is high [17, 18]. Socio-demographic factors such as education, income, and marital status further influence both ANC utilization and IPTp adherence [6, 19]. Other studies have also identified the importance of cultural factors influencing both ANC utilization and IPTp adherence [20].

This study examined the association between a comprehensive measure of ANC adequacy and IPTp uptake during pregnancy among women in Ghana. By moving beyond metrics such as visit count or timing alone, the research aimed to identify additional determinants of IPTp adherence. The findings have the potential to inform targeted health system interventions adequate ANC not only facilitates contact with health services but also delivers the full package of care necessary to prevent malaria in pregnancy and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Methods

This cross-sectional study utilized nationally representative data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). The design and methodology of the 2022 Ghana DHS have been described in detail elsewhere [21]. In brief, the survey employed a two-stage, stratified sampling design. In the first stage, enumeration areas (EAs) were selected based on the most recent population and housing census. In the second stage, households were randomly selected within each EA using equal probability sampling. Eligible participants were women aged 15–49 years residing in the selected households.

Of the 15,401 women interviewed in the 2022 Ghana DHS, the analysis was restricted to those who had a live birth in the two years preceding the survey and had complete data on intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy (IPTp) during their most recent pregnancy (n = 3,850). Women who did not receive any antenatal care (ANC) (n = 74) or had missing data on key covariates (n = 89) were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 3,689 participants.

All respondents provided informed consent prior to participation. For participants under the age of 18, parental or guardian consent was obtained in accordance with ethical guidelines. The DHS protocols and survey instruments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of ICF and the Ethical Review Committee of the Ghana Health Service. The DHS data used in this study are de-identified and publicly available to qualified researchers through the DHS Program upon request.

Outcome of interest

Receipt of three or more doses of SP/Fansidar (IPTp3 +)

The primary outcome for this study was the receipt of at least three doses of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP)/Fansidar during pregnancy (IPTp3 +), consistent with WHO recommendations. In the 2022 Ghana DHS, this variable was derived from two survey questions. First, women were asked, “During this pregnancy, did you take SP/Fansidar to keep you from getting malaria?” Those who answered “yes” were then asked, “How many times did you take SP/Fansidar during this pregnancy?” Women who reported taking SP/Fansidar three or more times were classified as having received IPTp3 +.

Independent variables: Antenatal Care Adequacy.

Antenatal care adequacy was assessed using four independent variables.

Number of ANC visits: A binary variable indicating whether a woman attended at least eight ANC visits during pregnancy, in line with updated WHO guidelines.

Timing of ANC initiation: A binary variable indicating whether the first ANC began within the first trimester (i.e., within the first three months of pregnancy).

Components of ANC: A binary variable indicating whether a woman received all seven key components of ANC as captured in the 2022 Ghana DHS. These components included the measurement of blood pressure, collection of urine and blood samples, listening to the baby’s heartbeat, counseling on maternal nutrition and breastfeeding, and being asked about vaginal bleeding.

ANC by skilled provider: A binary variable indicating whether the woman received ANC from a skilled health provider, including a doctor, nurse, or midwife.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous literature examining factors associated with maternal health service utilization and malaria prevention in pregnancy. These included maternal age, marital status, sex of household head, educational attainment, religion, household wealth index, employment status, health insurance coverage, place of residence, perceived distance to health facility, region of residence, pregnancy intention, and birth order of the most recent child.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and proportions were used to describe the characteristics of the study sample. Differences in the proportions of receipt of IPTp3 + by adequacy of ANC and other sociodemographic characteristics were assessed using Rao-Scott chi-square tests. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was then used to determine the independent association between adequacy of antenatal care and receipt of IPTp3 +. All variables were retained in the multivariable regression regardless of statistical significance in the bivariate analyses. To assess potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIF) were computed. All VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 4, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the odds ratios (CI) were reported. As a sensitivity analysis, the multivariable logistic regression model was re-estimated after excluding variables with limited variability (ANC by skilled provider and health insurance coverage) or small subgroup sizes (marital status and religion) to assess the robustness of the main model. Two-sided p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Complex survey design elements including strata, cluster and weighting were incorporated in all analyses as recommended by the DHS program. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Table 1 shows the descriptive characteristics of the study sample. Among the 3,689 women included in the analysis, 61.3% reported receiving three or more doses of intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy (IPTp3 +). Nearly half of the participants were aged 25–34 years (48.1%), the majority were married or living with a partner (82.7%), resided in male-headed households (68.9%), and had attained at least secondary education (64.3%). The sample was predominantly urban (53.2%), employed (80.5%), and insured (95.4%). In terms of antenatal care (ANC), 39.2% of women attended eight or more visits, 63.6% initiated ANC within the first trimester, and 75.4% received all seven essential ANC components. Nearly all women (99.5%) received ANC from a skilled provider.

Table 2 displays the results of bivariate analyses, which identified several sociodemographic and ANC-related factors significantly associated with IPTp3 + uptake. Women aged 25–49 years, those who were married or cohabiting, and those with higher educational attainment were significantly more likely to receive IPTp3 + (p < 0.01 for all). IPTp3 + uptake was also higher among women in richer wealth quintiles, with intended pregnancies, with health insurance, and among those who reported that distance to a health facility was not a major problem. ANC adequacy indicators showed strong associations: women who attended at least eight ANC visits (71.1% vs. 55.0%), initiated ANC within the first trimester (68.0% vs. 49.6%), or received all ANC components (64.9% vs. 50.3%) were significantly more likely to receive IPTp3 + (p < 0.001 for all). IPTp3 + coverage was higher among women who received ANC from a non-skilled provider (81.7%) compared to those who received care from a skilled provider (61.2%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.106). Given the very small number of women who received ANC from a non-skilled provider group (n = 17), these results require cautious interpretation.

Table 3 displays the results of bivariate analyses examining sociodemographic and contextual factors associated with each of the four indicators of ANC adequacy. Women aged 25–49 years were significantly more likely than those aged 15–24 to attend eight or more ANC visits, initiate ANC within the first trimester, and receive all components of ANC (p < 0.01 for all). Education level was significantly associated with ANC visit frequency and timing, with women who had secondary or higher education more likely to initiate ANC early and attend the recommended number of visits (p < 0.001). Religion was also significantly associated with ANC visit frequency and timing; women identifying as Christian or Muslim were more likely than those in the “Other” category to meet these indicators (p < 0.001). Household wealth was strongly associated with ANC adequacy, with women in richer quintiles more likely to attend ≥ 8 visits, initiate ANC early, and receive all components of care (p < 0.001). Employment and health insurance were each significantly associated with attending 8 or more visits (p = 0.003 and p = 0.004, respectively). Rural residence was associated with higher rates of 8 or more visits and receipt of all ANC components, while urban women were more likely to receive ANC from a skilled provider (p < 0.01 for all). A shorter perceived distance to a health facility was significantly associated with all ANC indicators, except for skilled provider attendance. Pregnancy intention and birth order were also associated with multiple ANC adequacy indicators (p < 0.01), with women reporting intended pregnancies or lower parity more likely to receive timely and comprehensive ANC. Despite nearly universal coverage of skilled ANC providers, small but significant differences in provider type were observed by residence and birth order (p < 0.05 for both).

Multivariable logistic regression results (Table 4) confirmed a strong, independent association between ANC adequacy and IPTp3 + uptake. Women who attended at least eight ANC visits had significantly higher odds of receiving IPTp3 + (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.50; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.18–1.90). Similarly, early ANC initiation in the first trimester (aOR = 1.74; 95% CI: 1.44–2.09) and receipt of all ANC components (aOR = 1.62; 95% CI: 1.29–2.03) were significantly associated with greater IPTp3 + uptake. ANC by a skilled provider was not significantly associated with IPTp3 + uptake (aOR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.10–1.10; p = 0.071), likely reflecting the limited variability in this variable. Additional significant predictors included having secondary or higher education and an intended pregnancy, while Muslim women had lower odds of IPTp3 + uptake compared to Christians.

Discussion

In a large nationally representative sample of Ghanaian women, adequacy of ANC is independently associated with increased uptake of intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy (IPTp3 +). Specifically, women who attended at least eight ANC visits, initiated ANC within the first trimester, and received all key components of ANC had significantly higher odds of receiving IPTp3 +. These associations held after adjusting for a range of sociodemographic and contextual factors, reinforcing the importance of both the timing and quality of ANC in improving maternal malaria prevention outcomes.

These findings are consistent with previous studies across sub-Saharan Africa that have highlighted the critical role of ANC attendance in improving IPTp coverage. Studies from Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria have demonstrated that women who attend more ANC visits are more likely to receive the recommended number of IPTp doses [6, 22–24]. Likewise, early ANC initiation has been shown to increase the opportunity for timely SP administration, particularly since IPTp is recommended to begin as early as the second trimester [8–10]. However, the present analysis adds to this literature by incorporating a multidimensional definition of ANC adequacy that reflects updated WHO guidelines, which emphasize not only frequency and timing but also the content and quality of care delivered.

The mechanisms underlying the observed associations are plausible. Frequent ANC visits provide more opportunities for IPTp administration and for providers to reinforce preventive behaviors [15, 25, 26]. Early initiation allows sufficient time for women to receive the full three-dose regimen before delivery. Receipt of comprehensive ANC services including laboratory screening, fetal monitoring, and counseling may also indicate better facility readiness and provider adherence to clinical guidelines [27–29]. Previous research has shown that quality counseling on malaria prevention during ANC is associated with greater IPTp uptake [30–32], highlighting the role of patient-provider interactions in influencing adherence.

While the findings reaffirm the positive association between ANC adequacy and IPTp-SP uptake, they also reveal a significant programmatic gap: two-thirds of women in this study-initiated ANC during the first trimester, a period during which IPTp-SP is not yet recommended. This creates a missed opportunity for malaria prevention during a critical window of placental development. Evidence from multiple studies across sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana, Benin, Malawi, and Burkina Faso, has shown that malaria infection in the first trimester is strongly associated with maternal anaemia, low birth weight, and poor perinatal outcomes due to abnormal placentation and chronic inflammation [33–35]. Given the rising concerns about SP resistance and the limitations of current timing recommendations, future strategies should consider early-intervention approaches, such as test-and-treat protocols during the first ANC visit or alternative chemoprevention therapies such as dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine [36, 37]. A recent study from Ghana also suggests the need to address high rates of anemia and asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women at ANC initiation through targeted treatment strategies [38]. Expanding the malaria prevention toolkit beyond IPTp-SP, particularly for the early gestational period, will be essential for improving pregnancy outcomes and adapting to evolving resistance patterns.

In addition to the main findings on ANC adequacy, this study identified several sociodemographic factors associated with IPTp3 + uptake. Women with secondary or higher education had significantly greater odds of receiving IPTp3 +, likely due to higher health literacy and better navigation of health systems [39–42]. Conversely, Muslim women had lower odds of IPTp3 + uptake compared to Christian women, a pattern previously observed in Uganda [43]. However, other studies in sub-Saharan Africa have reported no association between religion and IPTp3 + uptake [7, 44, 45], suggesting that context-specific sociocultural or geographic factors may be at play. Women with unintended pregnancies were significantly less likely to receive IPTp3 +, a finding consistent with existing literature [9] indicating that unplanned pregnancies are associated with delayed ANC initiation and reduced maternal health care utilization [46–48]. Interestingly, IPTp3 + coverage appeared to be higher among women who received ANC from non-skilled providers, although this finding was not statistically significant. The small sample size in this group (n = 17) and potential misclassification may explain this unexpected result, warranting the need for further qualitative investigation.

This study has several strengths. It utilizes nationally representative data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey, allowing for generalizability to the broader population of women of reproductive age. The analytic framework incorporated a policy-relevant, multidimensional definition of ANC adequacy based on the 2016 WHO recommendations. Additionally, rigorous statistical methods, including adjustment for survey design and control of relevant covariates, strengthen the credibility of the findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the ability to draw causal inference between ANC adequacy and IPTp3 + uptake. Second, DHS data were not collected with the specific intent of assessing the relationship between ANC quality and IPTp3 + uptake; as such, the available variables may not capture the full range of influencing factors. Third, the data are based on maternal recall of ANC services and IPTp doses received during pregnancy, which may introduce recall bias, particularly among women who gave birth within the two years preceding the survey. Third, social desirability bias may also have influenced responses about ANC utilization and preventive behaviors. Another limitation relates to the inclusion of variables with small subgroup sizes or limited variability in the multivariable regression model. For example, nearly all women in the sample received ANC from a skilled provider (99.5%), limiting the utility of this variable in distinguishing differences in IPTp3 + uptake. To address this issue and test the robustness of the results, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding variables with sparse subgroups, including marital status, religion, health insurance, and ANC from skilled providers. The results of this alternate model (presented in Supplementary Table S1) were consistent with the primary model in both direction and magnitude of associations. While this reinforces the reliability of the findings, it also highlights the need for caution when interpreting results involving variables with limited variability. Lastly, the dataset lacked information on important confounding factors such as women’s knowledge of IPTp3 +, provider training, stock availability of IPTp-SP uptake and adherence to clinical guidelines, all of which may play an important role in IPTp service delivery. Future studies incorporating these variables will be necessary to demonstrate further the pathways influencing IPTp3 + coverage.

The observed findings carry meaningful implications for both national and global health policy. Increasing ANC contact alone is not sufficient; efforts must also promote early ANC initiation and ensure that women receive a full package of evidence-based ANC interventions. This calls for improved provider training, consistent SP supply chains, and strengthened monitoring of ANC quality. Community-based interventions should also emphasize the importance of early and comprehensive ANC utilization. These actions are critical not only for achieving malaria prevention goals, but also for reducing broader maternal and neonatal health burdens. Importantly, this study aligns with and supports the achievement of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being. Specifically, target 3.1 aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio, while target 3.3 calls for ending the malaria epidemic by 2030. Adequate ANC is a gateway to achieving both of these targets, as it facilitates access to life-saving interventions like IPTp and improves maternal and newborn outcomes. As Ghana continues to prioritize maternal and child health, investments in the quality and equity of ANC services will be essential for accelerating progress toward the SDGs.

Conclusion

This population-based study demonstrates that adequate ANC, defined not only by frequency of visits but also by early initiation and comprehensive service delivery, is critical for improving the uptake of IPTp3 + in Ghana. As countries strive to eliminate malaria in pregnancy and meet global development goals, scaling up high-quality, timely ANC remains a cornerstone of an effective strategy. Targeted health system improvements that promote equitable access to comprehensive ANC can substantially advance efforts to reduce preventable maternal and neonatal deaths and eliminate malaria as a public health threat.
