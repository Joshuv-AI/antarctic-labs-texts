Improving the explainability of autoencoder factors for commodities through forecast-based Shapley values
Authors: Roy Cerqueti, Antonio Iovanella, Raffaele Mattera, Saverio Storani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoencoders are dimension reduction models in the field of machine learning which can be thought of as a neural network counterpart of principal components analysis (PCA). Due to their flexibility and good performance, autoencoders have been recently used for estimating nonlinear factor models in finance. The main weakness of autoencoders is that the results are less explainable than those obtained with the PCA. In this paper, we propose the adoption of the Shapley value to improve the explainability of autoencoders in the context of nonlinear factor models. In particular, we measure the relevance of nonlinear latent factors using a forecast-based Shapley value approach that measures each latent factor’s contributions in determining the out-of-sample accuracy in factor-augmented models. Considering the interesting empirical instance of the commodity market, we identify the most relevant latent factors for each commodity based on their out-of-sample forecasting ability.Keywords: Explainability, Neural networks, Nonlinear factor models, Shapley value, CommoditiesSubject terms: Statistics, Scientific data

Introduction

Machine learning is a well-developed theoretical framework that is a branch of the wide context of artificial intelligence. It aims to use data and algorithmic procedures to mimic the way humans learn.

Evidently, the versatility of this theoretical structure is the ground of the popularity of machine learning in all the fields of applied science. This paper enters this debate by facing a machine learning-related issue having remarkable applications in the field of finance, with a specific interest in the area of commodities. On this, we are close to the strand of literature dealing with machine learning techniques for commodities1,2.

One of the key instruments used in machine learning is the neural network. Such a tool is a device imitating the human brain when processing flows of information—for us, signals. It is composed of neurons—or, simply, nodes—of different types. The input nodes are the nodes where the signal enters the network; the hidden nodes are devoted to the transformation and elaboration of the starting signal; the output nodes are associated with the final outcome of the transformed signal3.

In this environment, autoencoders are unsupervised learning instruments based on the reduction of the dimensionality of the considered models4,5. On this, they can be seen as a nonlinear counterpart of the principal components analysis (PCA)6 associated with the factor models, that are widely exploited in the context of commodities7–13. Compared to PCA, however, autoencoders capture complex relationships and nonlinearities while estimating latent factors. Therefore, it is not surprising that autoencoders have been used for estimating nonlinear factor models in finance14–16.

Despite this relevance, the employment of autoencoders for commodities is quite neglected. To the best of our knowledge15, is the only paper applying autoencoder factor models to commodities.

This paper fills this gap. We deal with autoencoders when they are applied to factor models in the field of commodities. In this context, one of the major problems is to treat the explainability of autoencoders, which is lower than what one has for PCA17. Indeed, PCA components are easier to interpret because they are linear combinations of the original variables with clearly quantified variance. Autoencoders, on the other hand, use neural networks to learn complex, non-linear representations, making their encoding and decoding processes more opaque18. We here specifically face the issue of explainability by exploring the relevance of the nonlinear latent factors by applying a suitably defined notion of Shapley value, introduced by Lloyd Shapley in19 in game theory. The Shapley value is a well-recognized instrument for determining the importance of a player by measuring how the presence of the considered player affects the payoffs of the coalitions (see20 and the recent handbook21).

The Shapley value is here applied to assess the importance of the latent factors for out-of-sample accuracy when making forecasts through factor-augmented models.

Our approach is in line with the contributions dealing with the application of the Shapley value in the environment of forecasting exercise22,23. Furthermore, our methodological study aims to enhance the explainability of machine learning methods, building on discussions and outlines by24–27. Specifically,24 proposes a framework based on the Shapley-Taylor decomposition to perform parametric inference in machine learning models, offering insights into statistical learning processes and facilitating the generalization of experimental findings. Meanwhile,25 introduces a global explainable artificial intelligence method using Lorenz decomposition, providing a unified variable importance criterion that combines predictive accuracy with explainability, illustrated through Bitcoin price prediction26. Explores the application of Shapley values in random forest and support vector machine models for molecular machine learning, revealing differences in learning characteristics and providing chemically intuitive explanations for accurate predictions. Finally,27 discusses the exploitation of the Shapley value concept for explaining support vector machine models in pharmaceutical research, highlighting the development of a computationally efficient approach by using radial basis function kernels. We take inspiration by the approach of22,23 but, differently from them and from the papers quoted above, we rank the factors identified by the autoencoders in terms of their importance in forecasting according to the Shapley value, thereby highlighting the explanatory power of each factor.

The theoretical proposal is validated through an empirical instance of the daily series of the returns of 22 commodities. The results are quite interesting, and offer room for further developments of the study. Indeed, for each commodity, we identify the key latent factors. Considering five factors, we find that the first latent factor is the most representative of energy-related commodities, whilst the third factor explains most agricultural commodities. Moreover, we find that the last factor mainly relates to metal commodities. Therefore, over five factors, only some of them are important for explaining the commodity market in out-of-sample terms. These considerations not only shed light on the information included in each latent factor, making the autoencoder more explainable, but they also represent a heuristic approach that can guide the selection of the number of relevant factors.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the methodologies advanced in the paper, with a description of the factor models with autoencoders and the forecast-based Shapley value. Section 3 describes the dataset employed for the analysis. Section 4 outlines the results and provides a discussion of them. The last section offers some conclusions and illustrates lines for future research.

Methodology

In what follows we discuss the methodology adopted for the estimation of nonlinear factor models. Moreover, we present the procedure adopted to improve the explainability of factor models with autoencoders.

To provide a better intuition of the empirical instance proposed in the present paper, we will present the methodological framework in the context of commodities.

The proposed approach builds on the forecasting ability of latent factors. Autoregressive models are commonly used in time series modelling, but past returns alone may not capture all relevant information for forecasting commodity returns. Literature suggests using both observable and latent factors for better forecasts, leading to the preference for factor-augmented models28. Observable factors include measurable variables such as interest rates, exchange rates, and others, whilst latent factors are not directly observable and represent underlying economic conditions or market sentiments. Including latent factors helps capture complex interactions that observable factors alone might miss. Therefore, we evaluate the forecasting ability of latent factors within the framework of factor-augmented linear regression models.

Factor models with autoencoders

The standard approach for estimating latent factors is by approximate factor models. Approximate factor models assume that observed time series stored in the matrix X=(xi,t;i=1,⋯,N;t=1,⋯,T) can be decomposed into common factors F and idiosyncratic components Z, that is,

where F=(fk,t;k=1,⋯,K;t=1,⋯,T) with K the number of latent factors, Z=(zi,t;i=1,⋯,N;t=1,⋯,T) and Λ=(λi,k;i=1,⋯,N;k=1,⋯,K). The principal component estimator29, PCE, is commonly adopted to estimate the factors and the loadings from the covariance matrix of the observed time series. In particular, the PCE involves the following steps. First, the data is demeaned and the sample covariance matrix S^ is computed. Then, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of S^ are obtained and ordered in decreasing order. The top K eigenvectors to form the loading matrix Λ are chosen and the common factors F are estimated using the selected eigenvectors. Although highly explainable, the PCE approach assumes linearity between factors and the commodity time series. The linearity assumption is inconvenient in the case of the commodity market, and the introduction of nonlinearities may be preferable. Nonlinear factor models can be estimated using autoencoders14.

An autoencoder is a distinctive type of neural network designed to have its outputs closely match the input variables. In this model, the input data passes through a limited number of neurons within the hidden layer(s), creating a condensed version of the input (encoding). This compact representation is then expanded and translated back into the output layer (decoding). Since the model relies solely on the input data, it operates as an unsupervised learning mechanism4. Figure 1 provides an example of an autoencoder architecture. In this example, we have a single layer and two hidden nodes, which represent the factors. The first part of the figure, where the input data is compressed with the factors, is called the encoder. The second part, where the input is reconstructed from the factors, is called the decoder.

The machine learning literature has long recognized the close connection between autoencoders and PCA4,30 and, by extension, between autoencoders and latent factor asset pricing models14. When the autoencoder has one hidden layer and a linear activation function, it is equivalent to the PCA estimator for linear factor models14.

Autoencoders, like neural networks more broadly, have many advantages relative to traditional linear factor models. In particular, the high capacity of a neural network model enhances its flexibility to construct the most informative features from data. The main weakness of autoencoders is that the results are less explainable than those obtained with the PCA. In this paper, we propose an approach to improve the explainability of autoencoders in the context of nonlinear factor models. In particular, we measure the relevance of nonlinear latent factors using a forecast-based Shapley value approach by measuring each latent factor’s contributions in determining the out-of-sample loss.

Forecast-based Shapley value

Shapley values draw on coalitional game theory and build on the analogy between the predictors in a model and players in a cooperative game earning payoffs. In machine learning the payoff corresponds to the contribution of an individual variable to the model’s prediction. Given its informative content, Shapley value is commonly used in the literature to improve the explainability of machine learning methods25.

Differentials in terms of adjusted-R2 between models are also commonly considered24.

This approach, however, relates to the differences in the in-sample performance of competing models using different subsets of factors, whilst from a time series perspective, it is more relevant to evaluate the differences in the out-of-sample accuracy. Factor-augmented models are indeed adopted in finance with the primary aim of improving out-of-sample forecast accuracy. Therefore, in this setting the goal is to explain the marginal contribution of each factor fk,t in terms of out-of-sample forecasting. The factors contributing most to the out-of-sample performance of a factor-augmented model are the most important. In this regard23, recently developed performance-based Shapley values (PBSVs) which decompose the out-of-sample accuracy of a machine learning model. The PBSVs shed light on how each predictor increases or decreases the out-of-sample accuracy. In what follows, we apply this rationale to the case of nonlinear latent factors obtained with autoencoders.

In this setting, a negative value indicates that adding that k-th factor to the model reduces the overall prediction error in out-of-sample compared to the case where the factor is not included. Thus, using (5) and the definition provided in (7), a negative value of ϕk suggests that the k-th factor positively contributes to the predictive capability of the factor-augmented model when included. In other words, a negative Shapley value indicates that the factor is important and its inclusion improves the overall out-of-sample performance of the model. The more negative the Shapley value, the more important the factor.

We set the neural network to identify a specific number of latent variables, choosing K=5. These latent factors, numbered from 1 to 5, explain the historical series of commodity returns. By setting K=5, we ensure a comprehensive analysis while maintaining a balance between model complexity and interoperability.

Data

For the empirical analysis, we take into consideration the daily returns in dollars of N=22 commodities, comprising 4424 observations, from October 4th, 2005, to November 18th, 2022. particular, we consider log-returns of the i-th commodity, given by xi,t=logpi,t/pi,t-1, with pi,t the price of the i-th commodity at time t. There are several categories into which commodities can be divided. For the empirical study, we select some of the most significant ones in the context of economics and finance32. These include commodities in the energy sector (Brent, Diesel, Gasoline, Natural Gas, and WTI); commodities in the metals sector (Aluminium, Copper, Gold, Nickel, Silver, and Zinc); and commodities in the agricultural sector (Coffee, Corn, Cotton, Lean Hogs, Live Cattle, Sugar, Soybeans, Soybean Meal, Soybean Oil, Sugar, HRW Wheat and Wheat).

As already said above, the autoencoder enables us to reduce the dimensionality of the original data and identify the key factors influencing commodity prices. This approach allows us to better understand the relative importance of each latent factor in the context of commodity time series. It is important to note that the Shapley value represents the average contribution of a latent variable across all possible combinations of N-1 latent variables, where the excluded variable is the one for which the Shapley value is being calculated. This method ensures a comprehensive assessment of each latent variable’s marginal contribution to the explanation and prediction of commodity returns.

Initially, we assess the influence of each latent factor on the selected commodities sectors (energy, metals, and agriculture). Then, we analyze the contribution of each latent factor to individual commodities within these sectors. We consider the forecasting performance of the factors in the last month to assess their relevance using the procedure explained in Section 2. This detailed examination reveals how specific factors drive the returns of individual commodities, providing insights into the underlying dynamics of commodity markets.

Conclusions

This paper explores the explainability in the context of nonlinear factor models, which are grounded on neural networks and represent the machine learning counterpart of the principal component analysis. Due to their flexibility and good performance, autoencoders have been recently used for estimating nonlinear factor models in finance. However, the main weakness of autoencoders is that the results are less explainable than those obtained with the PCA.

In our analysis, we apply the concept of Shapley Value to evaluate the importance of individual k-th factor in a predictive model and, particularly, to improve the explainability of autoencoder factors. In particular, we measure the Shapley value as the difference between the out-of-sample forecasting error of the factor-augmented model with and without the k-th factor included, weighted across all possible combinations of factors.

We set the number of factors K to 5 for our analysis but acknowledged that it could be chosen differently depending on the problem’s needs. We compute the Shapley values by comparing the model’s prediction error when a single factor is included or excluded from the prediction, considering all possible combinations of factors, weighted according to the Shapley Value concept.

The methodological proposal is applied to a relevant set of the daily returns of 22 commodities, suitably divided by sectors. Results illustrate the heterogeneity of the explainability of the latent factors in the different contexts. factors 1, 2 and 3 are predominant in explaining a large part of the commodities. Differently, taking the values of the Shapley values over all the commodities leads to a predominance of factors 3 and 5. When dealing with the individual commodities, we can grasp the relevance of each factor for all the commodities. On this, Sugar and Cotton are the most explainable commodities, whilst Brent is the least explained one. In the analysis at a sector level, we have the predominance of Factor 1 for energy, Factor 5 for metal—and, interestingly, Factor 5 is the unique negative one for this sector—and Factor 3 for agricultural commodities.

In the end, some future research directions can be highlighted. First, we note that the forecast-based Shapely value procedure proposed in the paper assumes that the number of factors K is fixed. Therefore, the researchers can select an arbitrary number of factors (not necessarily K=5) in their empirical studies following the same methodology. However, while the selection of the number of factors in the context of principal component analysis is well-developed33, e.g. see, less is known for autoencoders. The choice of K therefore remains open for further consideration and investigation, as it could affect the results. Future work may involve exploring how the choice of K influences the results and whether K should be adapted based on the specific context.

Another aspect deserving of future research is the impact of the out-of-sample dimension adopted in the computational procedure for the Shapely value calculation. Specifically considering an out-of-sample dimension would modify the results. This applies to any forecast-based methodology since the forecast losses—which are the main ingredient of the Shapely value procedure adopted in the paper—can assume different values. In particular, for our empirical analysis with commodities, we consider one month of daily observations for the out-of-sample testing, which is enough for our purposes.

Indeed, the proposed out-of-sample experiments provides a clear validation of the methodological Shapley value-based proposal. Moreover, we stress that the computational complexity of the problem does not allow to deal with particularly large windows, since in our context the computation of the Shapley value involves the analysis of the partitions of a set for each time window—and the number of the partitions of a discrete set with cardinality n is the huge Bell number B(n). Interestingly, one can deal with the optimal size of such an out-of-sample dimension in our forecasting procedures. This challenging task is left for future research.
