Busulfan resistance in AML is associated with changes in mitochondrial copy number and lipid metabolism
Authors: Vid Mlakar, Simona Jurković Mlakar, Yvonne Gloor, Isabelle Dupanloup, Yoann Sarmiento, Mary Boudal-Khosbheen, Denis Marino, Chakradhara Rao Satyanarayana Uppugunduri, Marc Ansari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Busulfan (BU) is DNA-damaging and also a reactive oxygen species (ROS) generating agent. Both mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNA-CN) and ROS levels have been associated with the sensitivity of solid tumor cells to ROS-generating cisplatin. However, the changes of mtDNA-CN during the development of resistance to other ROS-generating agents or chemotherapeutics remain unclear. In this study, we investigated how the resistance of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell lines to the BU or the DNA synthesis inhibitor cytarabine (Cyt) is associated with alterations in mtDNA-CN and global gene expression. To follow BU resistance acquisition, MOLM13 myeloid leukemia cells, which are sensitive to BU, were subjected to five consecutive BU treatment rounds, generating BU-resistant cells (5TBU). Both parental control cells and the 5TBU cells underwent five consecutive Cyt treatments to generate cells resistant to both Bu and Cyt. We assessed mtDNA-CN using RT-qPCR targeting the mitochondrial gene MTND1 and investigated global gene expression profiles using bulk RNA-sequencing before and after resistance acquisition. We estimated the BU 50% inhibitory concentration (BU-IC50) relative to mtDNA-CN both in MOLM13-derived cells and 28 lymphoblastoid cells (LCLs). MOLM13 cells showed a progressive increase in resistance from 1.86-fold (p < 0.01) to 2.87-fold (p < 0.05) after 3 and 5 cycles of BU treatment, respectively. An additional 5 cycles of Cyt increased cellular resistance to Cyt by 1.90-fold (p < 0.01) and resistance to BU by 1.60-fold (p < 0.01). BU-resistant cells showed a 1.42-fold (p < 0.01) increase in mtDNA-CN after 3 and 5 cycles of BU treatment. In contrast, no significant changes in mtDNA-CN were observed following Cyt treatment. LCLs exhibiting higher BU-IC50 also showed increased mtDNA-CNs. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that BU resistance was associated with dysregulation in cholesterol and fatty acid transport and synthesis pathways. Expression profiling of the BU-resistant AML cell line revealed significant changes in cholesterol transport and fatty acid biosynthesis, along with alterations in mtDNA-CNs. These changes could represent key adaptations resulting from long-term exposure to the electrophilic agent BU. The current findings thus provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying BU resistance and suggest potential targets for preventing or overcoming this resistance in future therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39624-y.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the second most common childhood leukemia1. Frontline treatment typically includes cytarabine, daunorubicin, venetoclax, or azacitidine. Around 30–35% of pediatric AML patients will undergo hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)2, which remains one of the most effective curative options for high-risk AML patients3. BU is a crucial component of conditioning regimens before HSCT, designed to eradicate the disease and to open a niche for engraftment of the transplant4. Busulfan (BU) resistance in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) treatment presents a significant challenge to achieving optimal patient outcomes3,5, increasing treatment failure, relapse rates, and poor overall survival6.

Recent studies have shown that an increase in mtDNA copy number (mtDNA-CN) can promote cancer progression by enhancing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in pancreatic7, brain7, and microsatellite-stable colorectal cancers8. Additionally, mtDNA-CN appears to modulate apoptosis in tumor cell lines such as Hep2, HNE2, and A5499. A recent large-scale genome-wide study conducted by the Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes (PCAWG) Consortium revealed significant variability in mtDNA-CN across different cancer types and its correlation with clinical risk factors, including patient age at diagnosis and tumor stage10. Although the median mtDNA-CN in myeloid cancers reported in this study is relatively low compared to some other cancer types, a substantial intra-group variability suggests that mtDNA-CN may be important for the development of AML10. Moreover, an inverse correlation between mtDNA-CN and overall survival in pediatric AML has been demonstrated11.

Mitochondria are the primary site for intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation through oxidative phosphorylation. Baseline ROS levels are influential in cellular proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis12, particularly when cells are exposed to ROS-generating agents13, such as the electrophilic alkylating agent BU. Previous studies showed that ROS levels and mtDNA-CN correlate with sensitivity to the ROS-producing agent cisplatin in solid tumor cell lines14. This suggests that mitochondrial content and by extension mtDNA-CN could serve as a proxy for cellular ROS levels. Both myeloid and normal cells exhibit sensitivity to the ROS-generating agent BU15; however, the mechanisms involved in resistance and the role of mtDNA-CN in determining sensitivity remain poorly understood.

Mitochondria not only contribute to energy metabolism but also play a pivotal role in cancer cell signaling and immune evasion. In AML, alterations in mitochondrial function have been associated with increased drug resistance and poor prognosis16. Altered mitochondrial metabolism can increase the production of mitochondrial ROS and change cellular redox status, thus influencing transcription factors that regulate gene expression related to tumor proliferation17,18. In AML specifically, therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial function offer promising avenues for treatment. These strategies include inhibiting mitochondrial dynamics to disrupt leukemic stem cell survival, targeting oxidative phosphorylation pathways to exploit metabolic vulnerabilities unique to AML cells, and utilizing agents that induce mitochondrial dysfunction to enhance chemosensitivity17.

To better understand mitochondrial behaviour during the process of acquiring chemotherapy resistance, we generated AML cells with acquired BU and Cyt resistance. We documented the changes in mtDNA-CN and gene expression during the resistance acquisition. Additionally, we investigated the correlation between mitochondrial content in lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) derived from various individuals and their cellular resistance to BU.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Experiments were conducted using the MOLM13 (ACC-554) cell line, obtained from the Leibniz Institute DSMZ‒German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, and 28 immortalized LCL samples sourced from the NIGMS Human Genetic Cell Repository at the Coriell Institute for Medical Research. These LCL samples were previously characterized for their sensitivity to BU based on IC50 measurements15.

Cell cultures

LCL and MOLM13 cell lines were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with L-glutamine and 25 mM HEPES (REF:52400-025, Gibco, Life Technologies Europe B.V.), along with 10–20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (REF:10270-106, Gibco, Life Technologies Europe B.V.) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (REF: P0781, Gibco, Life Technologies Europe B.V.). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2-humidified atmosphere, following the supplier’s recommendations.

Establishment of cell models with acquired resistance to BU and Cyt

MOLM13 cells (denoted as MOLM13-P for parental) were used to generate a BU-resistant MOLM13 cell line (Fig. 1a). Cultures of 2.5 × 106 cells were incubated for 24 h prior to treatment. Cells were exposed in parallel to BU concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 μM (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) or DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich). After a 6-h exposure, BU was washed out, and the cells were maintained in culture for an additional 10 days to evaluate BU´s effects. Cultures exhibiting at least 80% lethality were selected for subsequent treatment rounds after a recovery period lasting until > 80% viability was restored. Recovered cells were cultured at an approximate density of 500,000 cells/mL. This treatment cycle was repeated five times, with recovery periods of 2–3 weeks in the fresh medium after each course. The cells produced were named according to the following nomenclature: [treatment cycle number] T [‘BU’ or ‘dmso’] e.g. 1TBU (Fig. 1a, upper panel). The maximum number of passages was set at 20 for MOLM13-P and 60 for BU-resistant MOLM13 cells. Cell aliquots from each intermediate step were collected and stored in liquid nitrogen. Fresh cells and those thawed after 12 months of storage in liquid nitrogen were used for analyses (Fig. 1b). Cytarabine (Cyt) (Sigma-Aldrich), was used as a non-electrophilic control to assess profiles of BU-resistant cells post-resistance acquisition. Freshly generated 5TBU and 5Tdmso cells were exposed for 72 h to either 400 nM Cyt reconstituted with DMSO or DMSO alone, followed by one week of recovery. The Cyt treatment course was repeated four times with 600 nM Cyt or corresponding DMSO concentrations. Determination of BU- and Cyt-IC50

The IC50 values were assessed by measuring intracellular ATP concentrations using the CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 luminescent cell viability assay (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). Measurements were performed 48 h after treatment with BU concentrations of 7.5, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 750, and 1250 µM reconstituted in DMSO; a control group received only 1% DMSO. For Cyt IC50 determination, cells were treated 72 h with concentrations of 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 2, 10, and 20 µM Cyt reconstituted in DMSO or treated with only 1% DMSO as a control. The IC50 values for both BU and Cyt were calculated through dose–response curve fitting using the Hill equation in Prism version 7 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA., USA). The coefficient of determination (R2) for each plate was used to assess experimental reproducibility, with an acceptability threshold set at R2 ≥ 0.95. All IC50 experiments were repeated a minimum of four times.

Measurement of mtDNA copy number via real-time PCR

DNA was extracted from fresh cells using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Multiplexed real-time relative quantification of mtDNA-CN was performed using MTND1 (Hs02596873_s1 assay with FAM-MGB probes for MTND1) as the target mitochondrial gene and nuclear RNaseP (VIC labeled TaqMan™ copy number reference assay, human, RNaseP) as the endogenous reference. Reactions were conducted on a StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the 2 × TaqMan Genotyping Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). The RT-PCR conditions included an initial activation step at 50 °C for 2 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min. This was followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Relative mtDNA-CN was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with RNAseP serving as an intracellular normalization reference (ΔCT), while MOLM13-P served as an intercellular normalization reference (ΔΔCT). Each experiment was repeated three times with technical triplicates for every sample. Four LCLs exhibiting varying sensitivity to BU15—two with lower IC50 values and two with higher IC50—were used as controls to account for batch effects between experiments (IDs: GM12750, GM11840, GM06985, and GM12057). Two negative controls were included in each RT experiment.

RNA isolation and RNA sequencing

Gene expression analysis using RNA sequencing was performed on the following MOLM13 cells: parental, 5TBU, and 5DMSO. RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA Mini kit (Invitrogen; Thermo Scientific; Wilmington, DE, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Concentration and purity assessments were conducted using an ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies; Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), yielding a mean optical density ratio of OD260/OD280 nm = 1.93 across all mRNA samples (range: 1.8–2.0).

Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the iGE3 Genomics Platform—CMU (University of Geneva, Switzerland) using the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. Sequencing quality was evaluated using FastQC v.0.11.5 software. Reads were mapped to the UCSC hg19 human reference genome using TopHat v.2.0.11 software; alignment summary metrics were obtained through PicardTools v.1.141. A table of gene counts, indicating the number of reads mapping to each gene feature within UCSC hg19, was prepared using HTSeq v0.6p1 (htseq-count). RNA sequencing data from the cell lines is available on Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the GSE246341 code.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis among parental (MOLM13-P), intermediate (1TBU; 3TBU), and resistant (5TBU) cell populations was conducted using DESeq2 software implementing a negative binomial generalized linear model19. The same analysis was performed for corresponding DMSO-treated cells to identify BU-dependent differences in gene expression profiles. Functional characterization of differentially expressed genes was carried out through over-representation analysis in g: Profiler20 utilizing KEGG21, Reactome22, and WikiPathways23 databases.

Statistical analyses

Results from mtDNA-CN measurements are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among mtDNA-CNs from acquired resistance cell models compared to parental sensitive cells (not treated with BU and/or Cyt) were assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. The correlation between BU-IC50 and mtDNA-CNs in LCL cell lines was estimated using a Spearman correlation; statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The Wald test in DESeq2 was employed to identify significantly differentially expressed genes with a false discovery rate threshold set at FDR < 0.05.

Results

Preparation of MOLM13 cells with acquired resistance to BU and Cyt

To investigate mitochondrial and gene expression changes during the acquisition of drug resistance, we first generated MOLM13 cell lines resistant to BU and Cyt (Fig. 1a). The maintenance of this acquired resistance was evaluated after storing the cells for one year in liquid nitrogen (Fig. 1b).

Cells treated with BU (3TBU and 5TBU) showed increased resistance to BU in comparison to respective control cells (3Tdmso and 5Tdmso). In addition, the resistance increased with the number of exposure cycles to BU from 1.86-fold to 2.87-fold after three and five cycles, respectively (Fig. 2a). This change is specific to BU, as control cells (3Tdmso and 5Tdmso) treated with DMSO did not show any increase in BU resistance (Fig. 2a). Next, we investigated the cross-resistance between BU and Cyt. We treated 5TBU and 5Tdmso cells with an additional 5 cycles of Cyt to establish resistance to Cyt and while DMSO was used as a control. In both cases of additional Cyt exposure, the cells demonstrated a significant increase in BU resistance, demonstrating the cross-resistance between BU and Cyt (Fig. 2b). Regarding Cyt resistance, both BU-resistant (5TBU) and control (5Tdmso) MOLM13 cells were exposed to three additional cycles of Cyt treatment (5TBU-3TCyt and 5Tdmso-3TCyt). Only cells receiving previous treatment with BU (5TBU-3TCyt) showed a significant increase in Cyt resistance when compared to their corresponding controls (5TBU-3Tdmso) (Fig. 2c). When subjected to five cycles of Cyt treatment (5TBU-5TCyt and 5Tdmso-5TCyt), both cell lines showed a further increase in Cyt-IC50 compared to DMSO controls (5TBU-5Tdmso and 5Tdmso-5Tdmso) (Fig. 2d).

These results confirm that we successfully generated MOLM13 cells resistant to both BU and Cyt through consecutive exposures to these agents. Notably, exposure to Cyt further contributed to enhanced BU resistance, indicating a potential cross-resistance between the two agents. However, prolonged growth in a medium devoid of BU or Cyt led to a loss of the previously acquired BU resistance (Fig. 2b), suggesting that this resistance is unstable over time. This was further confirmed by the similar loss of acquired BU resistance in 5TBU cells after conservation for 1 year in liquid N2 (Fig. 4a).

mtDNA copy numbers in BU- and/or Cyt-acquired resistant MOLM13 cell models and LCLs

To assess mitochondrial adaptation during the acquisition of BU and Cyt resistance, we measured the evolution of mtDNA-CNs using real-time PCR on the MTND1 gene, which is the most stable region on the mitochondrial genome24. We observed a statistically significant increase in mtDNA-CNs during the first three cycles of BU treatment in MOLM13 cells compared to controls (Fig. 3a). However, after the fourth and fifth rounds of BU treatments, we did not detect any differences in mtDNA-CNs between BU-treated and DMSO control cells as a result of an increase in mtDNA CN within the controls (Fig. 3a). The respective fold changes for the five treatments compared to parental MOLM13 cells were as follows: 1.89 (p1 < 0.01), 1.75 (p2 < 0.01), 1.42 (p3 < 0.01), 1.34 (p4 < 0.01), and 1.42 (p5 < 0.01) (Fig. 3a—fold changes not shown). Discussion

In this study, we found that early-stage acquired cellular resistance to the electrophilic molecule BU was significantly associated with increased mtDNA-CNs and profound alterations in lipid and cholesterol metabolism. Notably, this acquired resistance to BU appeared transient, reverting after prolonged growth in the absence of BU or after a single freeze–thaw cycle. The sensitivity of various LCLs to BU correlated with mtDNA-CNs. However, it should be noted that mtDNA copy numbers were also affected after repeated exposure to DMSO, demonstrating the dynamic nature of mtDNA-CN changes in cells responding not only to BU stress.

Global gene expression profiling revealed an overexpression of lipid and cholesterol metabolism genes, particularly those related to synthesis and transport. One possible explanation is that the alterations in lipid metabolism observed in BU-resistant cells may affect membrane composition, which can influence drug uptake and efflux mechanisms and thereby contribute to drug resistance. These changes could also explain the temporal nature of acquired resistance that was relatively rapidly reversed once the BU was removed or after the freeze/thaw cycle.

Key regulators of cholesterol and fatty acid synthesis pathways, such as SREBPs and FASN, are influenced by various tumor suppressor genes, such as p53, and oncogenes, like AKT, MAPK, and PI3K, which are pivotal in neoplastic transformation25–27. The altered cholesterol synthesis and transport observed in BU-resistant cell lines further support the role of cholesterol in chemotherapeutic resistance in AML cells28–31. Significant activation of cholesterol pathway genes by SREBF (SREBP) and Terpenoid Backbone Biosynthesis supports the hypothesis that increased cholesterol production probably plays a crucial role in BU resistance. Upregulation of the SREBP signaling pathway suggests that this transcription factor is a key regulator in the development of resistance by modulating lipid homeostasis.

This proposal is supported by a previous study, which showed that miR-33, a microRNA that regulates SREBF1 and SREBF2, contributes to the regulation of cholesterol homeostasis32. By inhibiting the expression of SREBF1 and SREBF2, miR-33 influences HDL biogenesis, cellular cholesterol efflux, and fatty acid oxidation, which are critical processes in liver metabolism and function. The involvement of interleukin signaling and cancer-related pathways indicates the complex interactions between metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and cancer progression mechanisms in BU-resistant cells, which are supported by studies documenting the roles of TLR2/Myd88 signaling in cholesterol synthesis in myeloid cells33–35. Previous work indicated an altered expression of TLR2 in BU-resistant AML cells, suggesting a potential mechanistic connection between TLR activation and cholesterol upregulation during the acquisition of BU resistance in AML cells36.

Since previous research indicated altered expression of TLR2 in BU-resistant AML cells, this finding suggests that a hypothetical mechanistic connection may exist between TLR activation and cholesterol upregulation in AML cells’ BU resistance37. One potential explanation could come from the recent report that ABCA1 expression, a cholesterol and other phospholipid transporter, was downregulated in TLR2 knockout mice38,39.

These findings suggest that targeting cholesterol and lipid metabolism could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for overcoming BU resistance. Inhibitors of fatty acid synthesis or cholesterol metabolism, such as statins or fatty acid synthase inhibitors, could potentially enhance the efficacy of BU and other chemotherapeutics in treating resistant AML40. Moreover, given the involvement of immune-related pathways, combining BU with immunomodulatory therapies may enhance treatment efficacy. The observed complex interplay between metabolic reprogramming, immune modulation, and cancer progression mechanisms underscores the need for multi-targeted approaches to overcome chemoresistance in AML. Targeting cholesterol and lipid metabolism could be a strategy to make tumors recognizable or sensitive to chemotherapy41,42.

Mitochondria are known to play a crucial role in regulating cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels43. While ROS are known to induce apoptosis, they can also activate survival pathways that contribute to drug resistance, suggesting their dual role in cancer biology. We previously demonstrated that BU induces oxidative stress in AML cell lines15, suggesting that the correlation between mtDNA-CNs and cellular sensitivity to BU may stem from ROS-stimulating properties rather than direct DNA damage. This proposal aligns with findings showing that increased ROS levels can promote survival pathways that contribute to drug resistance44–46.

One potential limitation of our study is that tumor heterogeneity likely plays a crucial role in developing drug resistance, as subpopulations of cancer cells may harbor distinct genetic alterations that confer different survival advantages under therapeutic pressure47–49. This is why we deliberately chose Epstein-Barr virus-immortalized LCLs for their clean genetic background, minimal confounding oncogenic alterations, and proven utility as a model for DNA-damage response and alkylator sensitivity50. However, our observations, combined with previous research3,15, highlight the need for future studies to explore the relationship between mtDNA-CN levels (and other markers of mitochondrial function) and clinical outcomes in AML patients, including response to treatment and overall survival rates, particularly in the context of the use of new treatment regimens, such as venetoclax, that directly target mitochondrial pathways51. Previous studies have suggested that feedback mechanisms may occur following gene overexpression (e.g., PGC-1 alpha amplification), leading to mtDNA replication and enhanced mitochondrial function52,53. Interestingly, we observed a slight decrease in mtDNA-CNs within the 5TBU cell line after three rounds of BU treatment, contrasting with the initial mtDNA-CN amplification during the acquisition of BU resistance. This finding is consistent with observations that initial stages of carcinogenesis are often accompanied by increased mtDNA-CNs that decline with disease progression54. This supports a previous observation that the initial transient amplification of mtDNA content after exposure to carcinogens is later reduced54. The mechanisms of acquired resistance identified in this study might extend beyond AML, suggesting common pathways involved in drug resistance across various cancer types, as shown in the Pan-Cancer study10. Finally, linking mitochondrial DNA copy number and lipid metabolism to BU resistance in AML suggests new avenues for addressing acquired BU resistance, including combination therapy targeting cholesterol biosynthesis (e.g., statins or fatty acid synthase inhibitors), SREBP pathway inhibitors, and immunotherapies.

Conclusions

Expression profiling of the BU-resistant AML cell line revealed significant changes in cholesterol transport and fatty acid biosynthesis, along with alterations in mtDNA-CNs. These changes could represent key adaptations resulting from long-term exposure to the electrophilic agent BU. The current findings thus provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying BU resistance and suggest potential targets for preventing or overcoming this resistance in future therapeutic strategies.
