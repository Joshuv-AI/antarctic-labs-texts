The influence of physical activity intensity on physical pain and hyper mental activity in undergraduate students
Authors: Rubén Fernández-García, Cristina González-Forte, Eduardo Melguizo-Ibáñez, María Jesús Benzo-Iglesias, María de los Ángeles Valverde-Martínez, Darío Salguero-García
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity has numerous physical and psychological benefits for university students. The present study aims to analyze the influence of physical activity intensity on the variables of physical pain and hyper mental activity.MethodA comparative, descriptive and exploratory design was used in this study. The sample comprises 1900 physical therapy undergraduate students, who were recruited from several universities in the south of Spain using convenience sampling. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form, Chronic Pain Assessment Questionnaire and Mental Hyperactivity Questionnaire were used. A structural equation model has been developed. The proposed model analyzes the relationship between physical activity, bodily pain and hyper mental activity.ResultsThe model shows satisfactory fit across the different indices (Chi2 = 2.853; Df = 5; IFI = 0.948, CFI = 0.903; NFI = 0.900; RMSEA = 0.069). Statistically significant differences were observed in the effect of physical activity intensity on hyper mental activity (p < 0.05). There are also significant differences of hyper mental activity on bodily pain between groups (p < 0.05). A relationship of hyper mental activity on bodily pain was observed for participants performing light-intensity physical activity (β = 0.642). Regarding the effect of physical activity on physical pain, no statistically significant differences between groups were found (p > 0.05). A positive effect between both variables was observed for moderate intensity (β = 0.006).ConclusionsPhysical activity has a very weak effect on mental hyperactivity, regardless of its intensity. On the other hand, mental hyperactivity significantly influences body pain. No significant effect was found between physical activity and body pain. In terms of applicability, strategies should be designed that integrate physical activity and emotional regulation strategies to prevent pain and reduce mental hyperactivity.Keywords: Exercise, Default mode network, Well-being, Higher education

Introduction

The current university student population display a high degree of competitiveness [1]. As a result, they impose considerable demands and pressure on themselves [1]. This can lead to a high level of chronic stress, which has a negative impact on their quality of life [1, 2].

Physical therapy students are under various academic and personal pressures [3]. One of the factors for high levels of stress and anxiety among these students could be the demands of the clinical learning environment [3]. Clinical placements provide opportunities for experiential learning through observation, reflection, application of abstract concepts, and practice of new skills [3]. However, if the results are not as expected, this can lead to increased levels of academic anxiety and stress [3–5]. It has been found that physical therapy students have shown higher levels of stress, anxiety [3] and hyper mental activity than students in other educational areas [4]. Furthemore, recent research has shown that physical therapy students do not meet minimum recommended levels of physical activity [5–7].

The World Health Organization [8] states that it is advisable to perform more than 300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise or 150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise per week. There is evidence that physical activity promotes longevity and supports the prevention, management and treatment of chronic diseases [9, 10]. There are different variables that condition the benefits of physical activity on health [11]. For example, the intensity at which the physical activity is performed has been shown to yield different health benefits [12–15]. Specifically, it plays a key role in improving bone density and physical fitness, as well as in mitigating metabolic syndrome [16, 17].

Both regular physical activity and structured, programmed exercise can promote pain reduction [12–14]. In healthy populations, exercise intensity combined with regular practice increases the experimentally induced pain threshold and prevents the development of chronic pain in certain conditions [18–21]. Regular physical activity helps to improve back pain, fibromyalgia and arthritis [19, 20]. It also induces analgesia and reduces pain levels if performed regularly and at moderate intensity [19, 20]. Moderate physical activity tends to generate less pain than vigorous physical activity [21]. Regardless of the context, engaging in physical activity may help mitigate the adverse effects linked to pain [22]. In addition to the physical benefits, physical activity has been shown to have mental health benefits [1, 2].

In terms of mental health, the Default Mode Network [23] is highly important, as it is vital in the management and organization of the brain [24]. It is a set of brain regions that show high activity when a person is mentally at rest, without focusing on specific external tasks [23, 24]. The Default Mode Network decreases in activity when performing a perceptual or motor task that requires attentional processing, such as meditation or physical activity [25–27]. It has been observed that constantly overthinking about something that generates stress can cause this network to enter a state of hyper mental activity [4]. Based on this, the concept of hyper mental activity is defined as a state of persistent and elevated cognitive activation associated with a dysregulation of the default neural network [4].

A relationship has been found between the activation levels of this network and different pathologies, such as Alzheimer’s disease, schizophrenia, autism, depression and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder [28]. There is also an association with emotional disorders: post-traumatic stress [29], trait anxiety [30] and depression [31]. Likewise, hyper mental activity has been observed to have a positive effect on the perception of bodily pain [4].

There is evidence that engaging in physical activity helps to reduce the effects of hyper mental activity [32]. However, it has not been shown whether the intensity of physical activity conditions hyper mental activity.

The objective of this study is:

O.1. To analyze the relationship between physical activity, physical pain and hyper mental activity in physiotherapy students.

The research hypotheses are:

H1

The intensity with which physical activity is performed will determine the relationship between physical activity and hyper mental activity in physiotherapy students.

H2

High-intensity physical activity will be positively related to bodily pain in physiotherapy students.

H3

Hyper mental activity will be positively associated with bodily pain in physiotherapy students, regardless of the intensity of physical activity.

Materials and methods

Design and participants

The present study used the steps indicated in the STROBE statement [33] as a reference. The study was exploratory, descriptive, cross-sectional and comparative. Non-probability and convenience sampling were used. The sampling error of this study was 0.019, with a confidence interval of 95.0%. The inclusion criteria established were: (a) to be a physiotherapy undergraduate student over 18 years of age, (b) to have done a clinical placement during a physiotherapy undergraduate degree (c) to have passed all the subjects that train and qualify students for clinical placements. Participants were excluded if they (a) were not undergraduate physiotherapy students or were under 18 years of age, (b) had not completed clinical placements as part of their university physiotherapy training, and (c) had not passed all the subjects required to qualify for clinical placements. Sociodemographic information was collected from the students. Specifically, gender (male/female) and age were recorded.

Instruments and variables

International physical activity questionnaire-short form

This questionnaire provides information on frequency and time spent on activities of varying intensity [34]. The version adapted to Spanish for university students was used [34]. The overall range of the intraclass correlation coefficient for the IPAQ-SF items was between 0.560 and 0.886 [34]. This indicates moderate reliability of the instrument. Data related to physical activity carried out during the last 7 days was obtained. It comprises seven questions with validated measurement properties suitable for monitoring physical activity levels across various settings. Three characteristics of physical activity were assessed: intensity (light, moderate, or vigorous), frequency (days per week), and duration (time per day) [35]. This instrument has been used in prior research with university students and proven to be reliable [36, 37]. Cronbach’s alpha value was α = 0.76. The McDonald’s omega value was ω = 0.78.

Chronic pain assessment questionnaire

The Spanish version of this questionnaire [39] was used to assess chronic pain [38]. It uses eight items to evaluate the incidence of pain in the performance of different tasks. These items are scored on a 10-point Likert scale. An overall assessment of bodily pain is obtained using a mean value [39]. The higher the mean value, the higher the incidence of pain when performing different activities [38, 39]. Cronbach’s alpha yielded a value of α = 0.82. The McDonald omega value was ω = 0.86.

Mental hyperactivity questionnaire [4]

This questionnaire evaluates the activation of the default neural network in the three months prior to completing it. The instrument is made up of 10 items that are evaluated through a 5-point Likert scale. The higher the score, the higher the level of hyper mental activity observed [4]. The final score is the sum of the scores of the 10 items, which provides a mean value related to the overall hyper mental activity of the individual. This questionnaire has been validated in university students [4] and only in Spanish. Adequate reliability values were obtained for this instrument (α = 0.89; ω = 0.89).

Procedure

To create the questionnaire for the study, the instruments were entered into the Google Forms platform. Information about the study was disseminated through social networks and university students who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate.

Only two universities sent the questionnaire to their students. The vice-rectorate for students of the two universities that agreed to participate was also contacted. An appointment was made, and the team was informed of the objectives of the study. Once approval was obtained, a mailing list was created and the link to the questionnaire was sent out.

To ensure that the questionnaire was answered correctly, three items were duplicated. In cases in which the answers to these questions did not coincide, the subject was excluded from the study. Twenty-five responses were eliminated. Data were collected between April and November 2024. All participants participated voluntarily after giving informed consent. The ethical criteria established in the Declaration of Helsinki were followed. In addition, the study was continuously monitored by the ethics committee of the University of Almería (EFM 419.25).

Data analysis

The IBM SPSS 25.0 statistical package was used to perform the statistical analysis of the results. The study of the normality was carried out through the values of skewness and kurtosis of each item. The values of skewness and kurtosis should conform to conventional criteria of normality. The value of skewness should be between − 1.5 and 1.5. The kurtosis values should be between − 3 and 3 [40, 41]. Likewise, the reliability of the instruments used was evaluated through the Cronbach’s Alpha and McDonald’s Omega tests. A reliability index of 95% was established.

The IBM AMOS 23.0 statistical package was used to develop the multigroup equation model. To fit the model, the values of the Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Normalized Fit Index (NFI) were consulted. The values of these indices should be greater than 0.90 to show a good fit [42]. The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) was also consulted. The values of this index had to be less than 0.08 [43].

In the multigroup structural equation model, the standardised β weights represent the magnitude and direction of the relationship between variables. The direction is interpreted by the sign of β. If β is positive, it indicates a direct relationship, while if β is negative, it indicates an inverse relationship. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

The value of the coefficient of determination (R2) has been included above each of the endogenous variables. This value indicates the percentage of variance in the variable that is explained by the predictor variables in the model. The higher the value of R2, the greater the proportion of the variability in the endogenous variable that is explained in the model.

Results

The initial sample consisted of 1925 participants, of whom 25 were eliminated for not responding adequately to the items proposed. The final sample consisted of 1900 students (23.06 ± 4.32) who were enrolled at one of two Andalusian universities. In terms of gender distribution, 818 are male and 1082 are female. After proposing and developing the theoretical model of the research, the fit indices were evaluated as follows: Chi2 = 2.853; Df = 5; IFI = 0.948, CFI = 0.903; NFI = 0.900; RMSEA = 0.069.

Figure 1 presents the theoretical model showing the direction of the relationships of the variables, which includes 2 endogenous variables and 1 exogenous variable. Unidirectional relationships were observed. These occur when one-way arrows appear, with the independent variable being the origin of the arrow and the tip being the dependent variable.

Theoretical representation of the structural equation model

The physical activity variable acts as an exogenous variable. It exerts a unidirectional effect on hyper mental activity and bodily pain. Likewise, a unidirectional effect of hyper mental activity on bodily pain was observed. All these variables were continuous and calculated through the mean value of the items of each dimension of the instrument. Figure 1 shows the representation of the theoretical model.

Statistically significant differences were observed in the effect of physical activity on hyper mental activity according to the intensity of the physical activity performed (p < 0.05). It is evident that physical activity performed at both a moderate (Fig. 3) and vigorous degree of intensity (Fig. 4) have a negative effect on hyper mental activity (β= − 0.071; β= −0.013). It was observed that the negative effect is less for vigorous intensity (Fig. 4). On the contrary, light-intensity physical activity had a positive effect on hyper mental activity (Fig. 2) (β = 0.003).

In terms of the relationship of hyper mental activity on bodily pain, statistically significant differences between the groups were found (p < 0.05). A greater relationship of hyper mental activity on body pain was observed in participants performing physical activity at a light intensity (Fig. 2) (β = 0.642). In contrast, the relationship decreased when physical activity was performed at a vigorous (Fig. 4) (β = 0.544) or moderate (Fig. 3) (β = 0.541) intensity.

Regarding the effect of physical activity on bodily pain, no statistically significant differences were found between groups (p > 0.05). A positive effect between both variables was observed for moderate intensity (Fig. 3) (β = 0.006). In contrast, participants performing light (Fig. 2) (β= − 0.074) or vigorous (Fig. 4) (β= − 0.028) physical activity showed a negative relationship between variables.

The results indicate a virtually non-existent relationship between physical activity and mental hyperactivity, particularly when analysed according to the intensity of the physical activity performed. Although the results indicate statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), the values of the standardised regression coefficients are very close to zero, showing a low magnitude of effect. These values indicate that the real and practical impact of physical activity on mental hyperactivity is minimal in quantitative terms. This suggests that the relationship is weak from a practical perspective.

Discussion

The results show that physical activity has a very weak effect on mental hyperactivity, regardless of its intensity. On the other hand, mental hyperactivity clearly influences body pain. No significant effect were found between physical activity and body pain.

Regarding the first research hypothesis, the results show a practically null effect of the relationship of physical activity on hyper mental activity, regardless of the intensity at which it is carried out. Previous studies have shown that hyper mental activity is affected by physical activity and the intensity at which it is performed [44–47]. They have demonstrated a beneficial effect of physical activity on the regulation of hyper mental activity [48]. Previous research has also shown that the type of physical activity conditions the activation of the sensorimotor network [46]. Moreover, interventions with an aerobic component activate the visual-spatial network and subcortical structures [46].

The results obtained in this study point to a negative effect of physical activity on hyper mental activity when performed at a moderate or vigorous intensity. Similarly, previous research found that physical activity at a vigorous intensity increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor and catecholamines (dopamine and epinephrine) [47]. Results from studies in the youth population indicate that vigorous physical activity is the only level of intensity that significantly correlates with cognitive activation and hyper mental activity [48, 49]. Vigorous-intensity physical activity appears to produce this beneficial effect on cognitive functioning and hyper mental activity [48, 49]. Furthermore, physical activities performed at a moderate intensity have been found to show a stronger negative relationship of physical activity on hyper mental activity. Previous research results have shown significant effects of these therapies on executive functions and behavioural self-regulation in clinical and healthy populations [50, 51].

Regarding the second research hypothesis, it has been shown that high-intensity physical activity does not show a positive and significant relationship on bodily pain.

The evidence demonstrates that the intensity at which physical activity is performed does not influence bodily pain. Previous research has shown that physical exercise can be used as a tool to prevent pain in different populations [52]. In addition, interventions have shown that a 15-minute session of high-intensity aerobic interval exercise does not increase pain intensity or pain sensitivity [53].

In terms of the third research hypothesis, a positive and moderate relationship of hyper mental activity on bodily pain was observed, regardless of the intensity of physical activity. The relationship was more pronounced for participants who engaged in light physical activity. Pain is fundamentally a construct that influences both physiological and psychological domains [54]. Indeed, it has been shown that the practice of physical activity at a moderate or vigorous intensity favours a greater secretion of neurotransmitters that promote the feeling of well-being [55].

The findings of this study have several practical applications for both academic and clinical settings. First, given that hyper mental activity strongly predicts bodily pain, interventions for university study should not only encourage physical activity but also integrate psychological and emotional regulation strategies [22, 29–31, 54]. From clinical perspective, university health services should consider screening for hyper mental activity in students reporting recurrent pain, as addressing cognitive and emotional dysregulation may alleviate physical discomfort [4, 19, 20]. Furthermore, since moderate and vigorous physical activity were associated with reductions in hyper mental activity, students’ wellness programs could promote these intensities as part of preventive health strategies, with prior findings on the benefits of exercise for cognitive functioning and stress regulation [47, 49–51]. These programas should ensure monitoring to avoid overtraining or exacerbation of pain, aligning with existing evidence that well-structured exercise can improve well-being, without worsening symptons [52, 53]. Ultimately, combining physical exercise with mental health interventions may provide a more comprehensive approach to improving student well-ebing and reducing the burden of stress-related pain [12, 18, 55].

Limitations and future perspectives

Regarding the limitations of the research, the first is related to the type of sampling. The use of non-probability and convenience sampling implies that the results obtained cannot be extrapolated to the entire population analyzed. Likewise, the cross-sectional design does not allow relationships to be established at that point in time. This should be considered when interpreting the study’s conclusions.

It should also be noted that physical activity and hyper mental activity were measured by means of self-reported questionnaires. It would be advisable to use non-self-reported instruments. Including objective measures such as accelerometers, visual analog scales, or heart rate variability in at least a subsample would have strengthened the findings.

The scale used to measure hyper mental activity has only been validated in Spanish and for university students, which limits its external validity. Furthermore, measuring a neurobiological construct, such as the default mode network, with a self-report tool may not be sufficient. For future research it would be advisable to complement the scale with neuroimaging techniques, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging.

Furthermore, the model proposed only includes three unidirectional variables, excluding possible confounding factors, such as sleep quality, diet and academic stress. Future research models should incorporate latent variables or mediating/moderating factors. The RMSEA value of 0.069 is close to the threshold of acceptable fit. Although the IFI, CFI and NFI are acceptable, the model fit cannot be deemed perfect. For future studies, alternative models should be tested, and comparative analyses could improve interpretability.

In terms of the study’s strengths, it features a large, well-distributed sample and the use of previously validated instruments with good psychometric properties. Ethical and methodological criteria were also applied, such as appropriate adjustment indices when developing the theoretical model. All of this allows for a detailed and reliable data analysis. Despite all of the above, significant weaknesses are identified, such as non-probabilistic sampling. This limits the generalisation of the results. There is also a reliance on self-report questionnaires for measuring physical activity and mental hyperactivity variables. For future research, it would be advisable to use objective instruments for collecting some variables.

Conclusions

Based on the analysis and contextualization of the research findings, the following conclusions were obtained.

In terms of the first research hypothesis, it is concluded that the intensity at which physical activity is performed conditions the relationship of physical activity on hyper mental activity. The results show that the relationship of physical activity on hyper mental activity at a light intensity is positive. On the contrary, a negative relationship of physical activity on hyper mental activity is observed when it is carried out at a moderate or vigorous intensity.

Regarding the second research hypothesis, there is evidence of a non-significant negative relationship of physical activity on bodily pain when performed at a vigorous intensity. A greater negative relationship of physical activity on bodily pain is also observed when it is performed at a light intensity. A positive relationship is only shown when physical activity is performed at a moderate intensity.

In relation to the third research hypothesis, it is concluded that hyper mental activity has a positive relationship on bodily pain regardless of the intensity at which it is performed. A greater relationship is observed when physical activity is carried out at a light intensity.

The findings of the study are applicable to the design of health programmes in university settings, highlighting that moderate or vigorous physical activity can help reduce mental hyperactivity. Although the relationship between physical activity and bodily pain was not significant, trends were observed that suggest benefits of higher-intensity exercise in reducing pain. Furthermore, the relationship shown between mental hyperactivity and bodily pain underscores the importance of addressing psychological factors in pain prevention, promoting comprehensive interventions that combine physical exercise with emotional regulation strategies.
