How are kefir microbial communities and product characteristics maintained in industrial production?
Authors: Ji Qingyang, Li Qiming, Gu Ruixia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The flavor, nutritional, and probiotic properties of kefir are intrinsically linked to its distinctive microbial communities.•Both traditional and modern production methods of kefir exhibit certain inherent limitations.•Synthetic kefir microbial communities and kefir-like starter cultures have been proposed as innovative production approaches for kefir.Keywords: Kefir, Microbiome, Functional property, Fermentation, Advancing kefir manufacturing

Introduction

Within the context of global health-conscious consumer trends and dietary pattern transformation, novel fermented dairy products serving as probiotic delivery systems have emerged as a strategic innovation trajectory for the food industry, with the global fermented milk products market projected to reach $67.35 billion by 2029 (Global Fermented Milk Market Research Report, 2025). In contrast to fermented products containing exclusively specific bacterial strains, kefir—identified by the Global Food Technology Expert Association as one of nine pivotal food trends (Yilmaz-Ersan and Ozcan, 2024)—represents a sophisticated fermented dairy product characterized by complex microbial community fermentation, distinctive sensory attributes, and exceptional functional properties. Capitalizing on the proliferation of probiotic awareness and consumer preference for health-oriented dietary choices, the global kefir products market has demonstrated sustained expansion, growing from $2.49 billion in 2024 to an anticipated $2.62 billion in 2025, reflecting a compound annual growth rate of 5.3 %, with projections indicating the global kefir market will reach $3.25 billion by 2029 (Kefir Market Report, 2025, Forecast and Growth by 2034). As potentially the most extensively researched fermented dairy product worldwide, traditionally produced kefir has been documented across numerous countries and regions, including Russia, Turkey, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Belgium, Czech Republic, Estonia, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Norway, Poland, Romania, Slovakia, Ukraine, China, Malaysia, South Africa, Mexico, Argentina, and Brazil (Bengoa et al., 2019).

Despite the widespread global popularity of kefir, substantial challenges persist in its production, particularly regarding commercial development and scalable manufacturing processes. Traditionally, kefir is produced through fermentation of bovine or caprine milk using kefir grains (Rosa et al., 2017). In local herding families, kefir maturation is facilitated by hanging a cowhide bag containing kefir grains and cow's milk on the door frame and shaking the bag as it is moved back and forth (Pothuraju et al., 2018). Conventionally produced kefir exhibits exceptionally diverse microbial compositions, particularly the viable microbial communities adhering to the surface and interior of kefir grains, which mediate the characteristic flavor profile, nutritional constituents, and functional properties of the final product. However, during commercial development, constraints imposed by limited kefir grain biomass expansion and the heterogeneous nature of grain-based milk fermentation processes preclude large-scale commercial production using the traditional kefir grain fermentation method. Kefir production represents a mixed-culture fermentation paradigm, wherein the geographical origin of kefir grains, substrate diversity, and provenance (including bovine, caprine, and equine milk from different regions), and fermentation processing conditions (traditional artisanal versus industrial-scale fermentation) significantly influence final product quality (Guzel-Seydim et al., 2021). These constraints impose a dual dilemma upon kefir industrialization: traditional production methods fail to achieve the standardization and scalability required for commercial manufacturing while potentially presenting safety concerns, whereas modern commercial kefir contains limited microbial diversity, compromising the distinctive flavor characteristics and probiotic efficacy associated with traditional kefir products. Consequently, this review provides a comprehensive overview of conventional kefir microbial communities, organoleptic properties, nutritional composition, and probiotic functionality, while examining three principal kefir production methods. By integrating current development status and primary challenges confronting the industry, this study proposes innovative production strategies for kefir, intending to guide further industrial development and technological innovation in the kefir sector.

Kefir grains and kefir microbial community

Traditional starter culture for kefir–kefir grains

Kefir grains, serving as the traditional starter culture for kefir, constitute ivory-white or white, elastic, irregular, gel-like granular microbial aggregates resembling miniature cauliflower florets, with diameters ranging from 0.3 to 3.5 cm and exhibiting considerable size heterogeneity (Fiorda et al., 2017). Fig. 1 illustrates two distinct kefir grain morphotypes with varying granule diameters: Fig. 1A depicts kefir grains sourced from Australia, characterized by a smaller particle diameter and firmer texture, while Fig. 1B presents kefir grains obtained from Azerbaijan, exhibiting larger particle diameter, enhanced elasticity, and elevated viscosity. The symbiotic microbial ecosystem of kefir demonstrates considerable complexity, comprising primarily lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, and yeasts, embedded within an extracellular polysaccharide matrix-kefiran and protein framework. This polysaccharide matrix, composed of D-glucose and D-galactose in equimolar proportions (glucogalactan), provides protective functionality for the kefir grain microbial communities against adverse environmental conditions and constitutes approximately 25 % of the dry weight of kefir grains (van Wyk, 2019).

Macrostructural imaging of kefir grains, with A representing small-sized granules obtained from Australia, and B representing large-sized granules obtained from Azerbaijan, preserved by the Jiangsu Provincial Key Laboratory for Probiotics and Dairy Deep Processing, and were subjected to fermentation and activation at 25 °C using pasteurized milk as the culture medium.

Traditional kefir grains demonstrate extensive global distribution, with documented occurrences reported worldwide. Compositional analyses reveal that kefir grains comprise 85 %–90 % moisture, 0.03 %-0.98 % lipids, 0.7 %-1.2 % ash, 3 %-7.26 % protein (Guzel-Seydim et al., 2021; Gökırmaklı and Güzel-Seydim, 2022), while containing essential vitamins B and K, tryptophan, and mineral elements including calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium (Gaware et al., 2011). The microbial architecture of kefir grains exhibits distinctive spatial stratification characteristics: microbial density demonstrates a gradient decline from the outer to inner layers, potentially attributable to reduced nutrient availability and lower pH conditions in the interior regions (Bourrie et al., 2016). Specifically, Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, and Acetobacterium are predominantly distributed on the external surface of kefir grains, while Saccharomyces is distributed across both outer and inner layers (Plessas et al., 2017). Scanning electron microscopy observations revealed that rod-shaped lactic acid bacteria predominate in the inner layers. At the same time, cocci and yeasts dominate the outer layers, thereby influencing the ultimate microbial composition of kefir (Gao and Zhang, 2019).

Kefir grains form spontaneously under natural conditions and exhibit slow growth rates in milk, following an S-shaped growth curve pattern with mass increases of approximately 10 % within 36 h, while maintaining essentially stable microbial composition (Blasche et al., 2021). Researchers have attempted to isolate individual microorganisms from kefir grains and synthesize novel kefir grains through artificial assembly approaches; however, no successful synthetic reports have been documented to date (Wang et al., 2023). The underlying mechanisms governing spontaneous kefir grain formation remain incompletely elucidated, with three principal hypotheses currently proposed (Cheng et al., 2024). 1. Aggregation hypothesis: microorganisms utilize structural components on their outer membranes for recognition and aggregation with other organisms, wherein microbial cell aggregation within kefir grains results from the combination of extracellular polysaccharide adhesive properties and hydrophobic characteristics of microbial cell surfaces; 2. Biofilm hypothesis: kefir grain formation is closely correlated with the biofilm-forming capacity of associated microbial communities; 3. Symbiosis hypothesis: symbiotic interactions among various microorganisms within kefir grains constitute the fundamental basis for kefir grain formation and sustained growth.

Kefir microbial community

Bacterial community

The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) defines probiotics as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host” (Hill et al., 2014). The distinctive flavor characteristics and functional properties of kefir, which differentiate it from conventional yogurt, are primarily attributable to kefir's unique and diverse microbial communities. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of microbial community structure within kefir grains and milk kefir constitutes a fundamental prerequisite for kefir-related research.

Denaturing Gradient Gel Electrophoresis (DGGE) combined with high-throughput sequencing technology and cultivation methods was employed to analyze the microbial community structure in Italian kefir grains, wherein Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens represented the predominant bacterium and Dekkera anomala constituted the dominant yeast (Garofalo et al., 2015). Microbial community structure analyses of kefir grains from Brazilian and Turkish regions revealed that the predominant bacterial genus was uniformly Lactobacillus (Leite et al., 2012; Nalbantoglu et al., 2014; Dertli and Çon, 2017). Bacterial community structure analysis of kefir fermented from 64 kefir grains sourced from 25 countries and regions identified core strains, including Lactobacillus helveticus, Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens subsp. Kefiranofaciens, Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis, Acetobacter cibinongensis, Acetobacter fabarum, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus gallinarum, Lactobacillus helveticus, Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, Lactococcus garvieae, Lactococcus lactis, Lentilactobacillus kefiri, Lentilactobacillus parakefiri, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and Pseudomonas helleri, which were detected at abundances exceeding 1 % in >10 % of samples (Walsh et al., 2023b). Researchers employed 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing to elucidate bacterial species diversity and abundance in artisanal kefir from the United Kingdom, Caucasus region, Ireland, Lithuania, and Korea, revealing high-abundance bacterial species including Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, Lentilactobacillus kefiri, Lactobacillus ultunensis, Lactobacillus apis, Lactobacillus gigeriorum, Gluconobacter morbifer, Acetobacter orleanensis, Acetobacter pasteurianus, Acidocella aluminiidurans, and Lactobacillus helveticus (Sindi et al., 2020).

Microbial communities in kefir grains and milk kefir exhibit distinct compositional differences. High-throughput screening (HTS) analyses revealed that kefir grains typically possess one (lactic acid bacteria) or two (lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria) dominant bacterial genera, with the most prevalent lactic acid bacterial species being Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, Lactobacillus kefiri, and Lactobacillus parakefiri, while other genera typically comprise <10 % of the total community (Marsh et al., 2013; Korsak et al., 2015). Kefir demonstrates substantially greater relative variability in genera compared to kefir grains, with Leuconostoc sp., Lactococcus sp., Lactobacillus sp., and Acetobacter sp. exhibiting the highest relative abundances (Korsak et al., 2015). Researchers analyzing kefir grains collected from diverse geographical locations identified a bacterial core community comprising Lactobacillus sp. (primarily Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens and Lactobacillus kefiri), Leuconostoc sp. (primarily Leuconostoc mesenteroides), Lactococcus lactis, and Acetobacter sp., collectively representing >95 % of all microbial species. Kefir grains differ mainly in their internal yeast populations and low-abundance bacterial species (rare taxa). They propose that the kefir microbiota emerges from an intricate balance of synergistic and antagonistic microbial interactions. At both the solid (kefir grain) and liquid (milk kefir) levels, constituent microorganisms navigate a continuum between competitive exclusion and cooperative facilitation. The differential occupation of ecological niches by specific microbial taxa across the solid-liquid interface underlies the compositional heterogeneity observed between grain-associated and milk-associated microbial communities (Blasche et al., 2021). González-Orozco et al. (2023) employed metagenomic techniques to analyze kefir grains and milk kefir from the United States, identifying predominant species in kefir grains as Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens (98.3 %), Lactobacillus helveticus (0.7 %), and Lactobacillus kefiri (0.16 %), while Lactobacillus helveticus (57.37 %), Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens (13 %), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (9.3 %), and Pseudomonas luteola (5.8 %) dominated in milk kefir. Kefir grains represent a highly resilient microbial ecosystem characterized by conserved core species composition across diverse geographic provenances. The ecological stability of this consortium is potentially underpinned by the predominant role of Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens as the primary architect of the grain matrix, together with the obligate reliance of constituent community members on its metabolic outputs (Nejati et al., 2020; González-Orozco et al., 2023).

Regarding the relative abundance of bacterial species in kefir, a Brazilian kefir grain community structure study demonstrated that the relative abundance of Leuconostoc mesenteroides exceeded 60 % (Rios et al., 2024). These findings demonstrate that microorganisms isolated from kefir grains exhibit regional variations; however, Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens represents the most characteristic species, present in virtually all kefir grains at relatively high abundances and constituting a critical factor in kefir grain formation. The relative abundance of Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens gradually decreases during milk kefir fermentation (Walsh et al., 2016), while other microorganisms, such as Lactococcus lactis, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and Lactobacillus helveticus, progressively become dominant strains during fermentation. This phenomenon may be attributed to the absence of genes involved in tyrosine biosynthesis in Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens (Walsh et al., 2023a). Citrate consumption during early kefir fermentation also promotes the secretion of metalloproteases by Lactococcus lactis and Leuconostoc mesenteroides, facilitating increased abundance of these microorganisms (Blasche et al., 2021).

Fungal community

Witthuhn et al. (2005) demonstrated that viable yeast counts in kefir grains range from 1.5 × 10⁵ to 3.7 × 10⁸ CFU/g, with yeast concentrations reaching 10⁶ CFU/mL in milk kefir after 24 h of fermentation. Metagenomic sequencing analyses of kefir grains and milk kefir revealed that microbial communities are predominantly composed of bacteria, with bacterial relative abundances of 99.4 % and 99.7 % in kefir grains and milk kefir, respectively. In comparison, eukaryotic relative abundances comprise only 0.60 % and 0.30 % (González‐Orozco et al., 2023). Despite their relatively minor proportional representation, fungal presence within the kefir microbiome remains critically important. Bacteria and fungi within the kefir grain ecosystem establish complex symbiotic relationships, performing distinct functional roles during kefir fermentation while simultaneously influencing each other through metabolic interactions, generating metabolic products that can be utilized by complementary organisms (Rosa et al., 2017). For instance, lactic acid bacterial proliferation in milk catalyzes lactose hydrolysis and subsequent lactic acid production, thereby creating favorable conditions for acetic acid bacterial and yeast proliferation. Yeasts contribute through the synthesis of various B-vitamin types and milk protein hydrolysis, generating carbon dioxide and amino acids that provide anaerobic conditions and essential amino acids required for lactic acid bacterial growth (Tingirikari et al., 2024). Yeast species identified in kefir grains and milk kefir from the Tibet region of China predominantly include Kazachstania unispora, Kazachstania turicensis, Kluyveromyces marxianus, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Gao and Zhang, 2019). Kefir grains and milk kefir from the United States yielded Kluyveromyces marxianus, Kazachstania africana, and Naumovozyma dairenensis. Turkish kefir grains contained Geotrichum silvicola and Dekkera bruxellensis (González‐Orozco et al., 2023). German kefir grains harbored Kazachstania exigua and Kluyveromyces marxianus (Blasche et al., 2021). Kazachstania and Kluyveromyces constitute the predominant genera in kefir grains (Sumarmono et al., 2023), with Kluyveromyces marxianus, Kazachstania africana, and Naumovozyma dairenensis representing the dominant fungal species in kefir grains, wherein Naumovozyma dairenensis is particularly prevalent in milk kefir (González‐Orozco et al., 2023). These eukaryotic microorganisms within the kefir microbiome similarly possess probiotic characteristics, including immunomodulatory effects, antioxidant activity, gastrointestinal health benefits, and essential nutrient production (Fakruddin et al., 2017; Hong et al., 2019). Specific yeast strains such as Kluyveromyces lactis, Kluyveromyces marxianus, Pichia kluyveri, Pichia pastoris, and Saccharomyces boulardii have been utilized in probiotic and functional food development (Souza et al., 2022; de Souza et al., 2024). It is noteworthy that the aforementioned investigations of kefir microbial community structure predominantly employed amplicon sequencing and metagenomic sequencing methodologies. Given that methodological variations may influence the absolute values of microbial diversity and abundance, attention should be directed toward the overall trends in natural kefir microbial community structure rather than direct numerical comparisons.

Microbial communities in industrial production

The bacterial and fungal community compositions presented in Fig. 1 and Table 1 are exclusively derived from natural kefir grains or kefir preparations fermented using kefir grains, reflecting the microbial community structure of kefir under natural conditions and influenced by multiple factors including geographical origin, production methodology, fermentation conditions, environmental parameters, and milk substrate characteristics (Sindi et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022). Although substantial variations appear to exist among kefir microbial community structures across different studies, the core microbiome composition or dominant species of kefir may not necessarily exhibit complexity. Given the paramount importance of core microbial community research in industrial kefir production, it becomes imperative to implement specific measures to maintain kefir microbial community stability during industrialized production, particularly ensuring that core microbial communities retain adequate viability throughout the entire production and distribution cycle, thereby guaranteeing kefir's beneficial properties and maintaining fermented beverage quality (de Almeida Brasiel et al., 2021).

The diversity of initial kefir microbial communities represents the primary determinant of quality variations in kefir. Therefore, ensuring that kefir microbial communities employed in industrial production possess rational strain compositions becomes essential, necessitating both mutualistic and competitive symbiotic interactions, which prove crucial for maintaining microbial community stability and diversity (Karkaria et al., 2021). This consideration requires particular attention in kefir production utilizing defined microbial mixtures as starter cultures. For instance, acetic acid bacteria and yeasts experience positive effects from lactic acid bacteria through lactose hydrolysis catalysis and lactic acid production, while lactic acid bacteria benefit from carbon dioxide and amino acid production by yeasts. Simultaneously, acetate salts produced by acetic acid bacteria and excessive alcohol generated through yeast over-fermentation can inhibit lactic acid bacterial growth (Tingirikari et al., 2024). At the strain level, Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens receives beneficial influences from two high-abundance strains, Leuconostoc mesenteroides and Lactobacillus kefiri, while Leuconostoc mesenteroides also benefits from amino acids and small peptides metabolically secreted by Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens (Blasche et al., 2021). Fermentation substrate selection and fermentation conditions significantly influence kefir microbial community structure and stability. Kefir typically utilizes whole, partially skimmed, or fully skimmed milk, with bovine, caprine, or equine milk all proving suitable, provided they comply with requirements for low bacterial counts and absence of pathogenic microorganisms, antibiotic residues, or disinfectant residues that may contain inhibitory substances (Sarkar, 2008). Notably, certain fermentation substrate selections, such as camel milk and soy milk, may impact kefir microbial community stability during fermentation due to the presence of antimicrobial substances or nutritional component differences (Kavas, 2015; Korma et al., 2022).

In industrial production, dairy fermentation typically occurs in large fermentation tanks, representing batch bioreactors that offer structural simplicity, ease of maintenance, and relatively low production costs. Since adding or removing individual substances during fermentation tank operation proves challenging, and influencing fermentation execution becomes difficult once the process commences, fermentation temperature and duration control constitute critical factors affecting kefir microbial communities in industrial production (Ritonja et al., 2024). Higher fermentation temperatures (35–42 °C) generally correlate with accelerated acidification rates, enhanced activity of lactic acid bacteria and thermophilic microorganisms, while yeast activity experiences suppression. Considering fermentation efficiency and microbial community stability throughout the fermentation process, kefir fermentation temperatures are typically maintained at 20–30 °C, with fermentation duration determined by acidity and pH levels, usually spanning 15–24 h (Sarkar, 2008). The two-stage fermentation process (28 °C/5 h and 20 °C/16 h) proposed by Simova et al. (2006) or an adaptive control system that flexibly regulates fermentation temperature to influence fermentation progression, facilitates precise regulation of kefir microbial communities in industrial production, balancing metabolic activity of different microorganisms throughout the entire fermentation processs (Ritonja et al., 2024). Post-acidification and storage-transport temperature control also influence the stability of active microbial populations in kefir products prior to consumer consumption. Following fermentation, kefir should be gradually cooled to 4–6 °C and maintained at low temperatures throughout the entire sales period to ensure biological activity of lactic acid bacteria and yeasts in kefir products (Guzel-Seydim et al., 2000).

Flavor characteristics, nutritional composition, and functional properties of kefir

Flavor characteristics

Volatile flavor compounds constitute the primary determinants of food aroma and serve as the principal sources for human olfactory perception of food fragrance. In contrast to the commercially prevalent lactic acid-fermented dairy products, whose flavor profiles derive exclusively from limited microbial strains such as Lactobacillus delbrueckii and Streptococcus thermophilus, kefir represents a distinctive yeast-lactic acid fermented dairy product characterized by its complex microbial consortium comprising lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, and yeasts that undergo dynamic succession and intricate interactions throughout the fermentation process (Yildiz, 2010). These microorganisms collaboratively orchestrate kefir's rich and distinctive flavor profile through coordinated proteolysis, lipolysis, and glycolysis of milk macronutrients (Farag et al., 2020). Contemporary research has demonstrated that traditional kefir contains volatile compounds spanning multiple chemical categories, including ketones, aldehydes, esters, alcohols, acids, and sulfur compounds. The formation and accumulation of these flavor compounds exhibit profound dependence on the structural composition and dynamic fluctuations of the microbial community during fermentation (Rosa et al., 2017). At the genus level, the metabolic activities of Lactobacillus spp. correlate strongly with the production of carboxylic acids, esters, and ketones, whereas Leuconostoc spp. demonstrate significant associations with acetic acid and 2,3-butanedione biosynthesis. Acetobacter spp. function as the predominant producers of acetic acid. At the species level, specific functional strains exert direct influence on flavor orientation. The incorporation of Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens results in substantial elevation of ester compounds, imparting fruity notes and ketone levels. Conversely, the addition of Leuconostoc mesenteroides enhances acetic acid and 2,3-butanedione concentrations, contributing characteristic buttery aromas. Furthermore, Acetobacter pasteurianus associates with pronounced vinegar-like flavors, while Saccharomyces cerevisiae correlates with ethanol production and the generation of fruit-associated volatile compounds (Walsh et al., 2016).

The abundant and diverse organic acids present in kefir establish the foundational architecture of its characteristic flavor profile. Lactic acid represents the most prevalent organic acid constituent, synthesized through both homofermentative and heterofermentative lactic acid bacterial pathways. This compound not only imparts the distinctive tart flavor but also serves a critical preservation function by reducing pH, thereby effectively inhibiting the proliferation of spoilage microorganisms and ensuring product safety and stability (Magalhães et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2014). Acetic acid constitutes the predominant carboxylic acid component, biosynthesized through the metabolic activities of heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria. This organic acid collaborates synergistically with ethanol and carbon dioxide to generate kefir's characteristic refreshing and palatable flavor signature (Destro et al., 2019). Furthermore, additional organic acids, including citric acid, malic acid, oxalic acid, and succinic acid, have been validated as positive contributors to the overall flavor complexity (Nikolaou et al., 2017; Tu et al., 2019) while potentially exerting synergistic antimicrobial effects (Ji et al., 2023). Ester compounds, including isoamyl acetate and ethyl hexanoate, typically exhibit intense fruity and floral aromatic characteristics and represent one of the principal sources of kefir's distinctive flavor signature. Their biosynthesis demonstrates intimate correlation with the metabolic activities of yeasts and lactic acid bacteria (Gao and Zhang, 2019). Concurrently, microbial degradation of milk fat, particularly by yeasts and specific lactic acid bacterial strains, substantially elevates free fatty acid concentrations to levels five to ten times higher than those observed in raw milk. These fatty acids function not only as essential aroma precursors but also constitute integral flavor components in their own right (Wszolek et al., 2001).

The dynamic equilibrium of the microbial consortium represents the cornerstone of flavor stability. Kefir grains originating from different geographical regions exhibit distinct microbial structural compositions, particularly regarding low-abundance indigenous microorganisms, which mediate the production of regionally characteristic flavor profiles (Dertli and Çon, 2017). However, in industrial production environments, inadequate fermentation control precipitates microbial imbalances, subsequently triggering flavor deterioration. Excessive proliferation of Saccharomyces cerevisiae under anaerobic conditions results in abnormally elevated ethanol concentrations and the generation of undesirable organic solvent-like odors, while overgrowth of Acetobacter spp. dramatically increases acetic acid content, producing harsh, pungent acidity and compromised mouthfeel (Gomes Da Cruz et al., 2025). For industrial-scale production, achieving product flavor consistency and stability necessitates precise regulation and monitoring of microbial community structure throughout the fermentation process. This approach requires comprehensive attention to the dynamic fluctuations of viable cell counts across lactic acid bacteria, yeasts, and acetic acid bacteria, coupled with real-time monitoring of critical physicochemical parameters, including pH, titratable acidity, and alcohol content. Integration of contemporary analytical technologies, such as high-throughput sequencing for microbial community profiling, enables the establishment of precise correlations among process parameters, microbial dynamics, and product characteristics. Through this microbial ecology-based precision control strategy, manufacturers can effectively prevent over-fermentation and ensure kefir products maintain balanced and desirable flavor attributes.

Nutritional composition

Kefir is abundant in diverse nutritional constituents, including vitamins, amino acids, prebiotics, bioactive peptides, fatty acids, and essential micronutrients such as vitamin A, B-complex vitamins, vitamin D, calcium, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, zinc, and iodine, which collectively contribute to its health-promoting and therapeutic properties (Cui et al., 2024). The nutritional composition is determined not only by substrate composition, kefir grain provenance, fermentation methodology, and storage conditions (Rosa et al., 2017), but also by the structure and metabolic activity of its complex microbial community (Prado et al., 2015; Ströher et al., 2025). Chemically, milk kefir typically contains water (80 %-90 %), carbohydrates (2.39 %-6.23 %), lipids (1.10–3.85 %), proteins (2.70 %-4.67 %) and ash (0.61–0.70 %) (Sarkar, 2007; Garofalo et al., 2020; Arapović et al., 2023; Cufaoglu and Erdinc, 2023). The nutrients in kefir during the fermentation process are also influenced by the microbial composition. For example, dominance of Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens and Lactococcus lactis is associated with increased lactic acid production and proteolysis, while the presence of Kluyveromyces marxianus and Saccharomyces cerevisiae contributes to ethanol and volatile compound formation, indirectly influencing nutrient stability and bioavailability (Prado et al., 2015; Moreno and Emata, 2020).

Vitamin content is strongly microbially mediated: Propionibacterium peterssonii and Propionibacterium. pituitosum synthesize vitamin B12, while Propionibacterium Freudenreichii subsp. shermanii produces vitamin B6 (Sarkar, 2007). During fermentation, microbial metabolism enhances pyridoxine, folate, biotin, thiamine, riboflavin, and vitamin B12 concentrations (Guzel-Seydim et al., 2011), improving nutritional value and potential bioavailability. These changes are linked to microbial succession — for instance, increases in Leuconostoc spp. during mid-fermentation correlate with folate enrichment (Ströher et al., 2025). Proteolytic activity from lactic acid bacteria hydrolyzes casein and whey proteins into peptides and free amino acids — including serine, lysine, alanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine, methionine, phenylalanine, and isoleucine — which occur at higher concentrations in kefir than in unfermented milk (Guzel-Seydim et al., 2011). These amino acids contribute to both physiological functions and sensory attributes such as umami and sweetness. Bioactive peptides generated during fermentation exhibit antimicrobial, antioxidant, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitory, immunomodulatory, and antithrombotic activities (Ebner et al., 2015; Amorim et al., 2019).

From an industrial perspective, understanding how microbial composition shifts drive nutrient enrichment or stability is critical for process optimization. Controlled modulation of microbial consortia through starter culture selection, fermentation time-temperature profiles, and functional ingredient supplementation can help standardize vitamin and amino acid content and ensure consistent nutritional quality for large-scale kefir production.

Functional properties

Kefir has garnered considerable scientific attention due to its multifaceted functional properties. Regular consumption of kefir demonstrates diverse health-promoting effects, encompassing Anti-cancer (Fatahi et al., 2021), anti-obesity (Bourrie et al., 2023), antidepressant (Noori et al., 2025), anti-allergy (Baars et al., 2025), hepatoprotective (Cui et al., 2024), gastroprotective (Coco et al., 2023), antidiabetic (Akar et al., 2021), intestinal microbiota modulation (Gupta et al., 2024), anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities (Ekici et al., 2022). These beneficial health outcomes are attributed to the diverse array of bioactive compounds generated by the kefir microbial consortium during fermentation, which includes both fermentation metabolites (exopolysaccharides, organic acids, bacteriocins, bioactive peptides, and vitamins) and the kefir microbiome itself (lactic acid bacteria, yeasts, and acetic acid bacteria), which may influence intestinal microbial communities through independent or synergistic mechanisms to collectively exert probiotic effects. Both animal experimentation and clinical investigations have demonstrated that the probiotic efficacy of kefir correlates intimately with its specific microbial composition. Whole kefir containing viable lactic acid bacteria and yeasts significantly reduced hepatic cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations in hyperlipidemic mice, whereas kefir preparations devoid of either yeasts or lactic acid bacteria failed to produce comparable effects (Bourrie et al., 2022). Studies involving hyperlipidemic patients similarly revealed that commercial kefir exhibited diminished efficacy in reducing inflammatory mediators, including IL-8, CRP, VCAM-1, and TNF-α, compared with traditional kefir grain-derived preparations (Bourrie et al., 2023).

Table 2 provides a comprehensive summary of kefir's probiotic characteristics, revealing that the structural stability of microbial communities directly influences the reliability of kefir's probiotic efficacy. Distinct production methodologies (kefir grain fermentation, back-slopping fermentation, commercial kefir) and product configurations (whole kefir, inactivated preparations, cell-free supernatants) engender variations in kefir microbiome composition, consequently affecting performance in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies, potentially yielding contradictory results. For instance, multiple investigations have demonstrated that kefir exerts beneficial effects on colorectal cancer, including depression alleviation (Sun et al., 2024), enhanced chemotherapeutic drug efficacy (Kim et al., 2021), intestinal microbiota improvement, and tumor development mitigation (Zeng et al., 2021). Conversely, research conducted by Zanardi et al. (2023) revealed that kefir consumption promoted tumor progression. These findings indicate that the health-promoting effects of kefir are not universally consistent, despite the majority of kefir preparations exhibiting similar microbial community structures. Fermentation methodology, kefir grain provenance, compositional categories, and consumption patterns all significantly influence the health-promoting potential of kefir. Furthermore, the efficacy and consistency of kefir's functional properties are highly dependent upon core functional strains present during fermentation, including isolated species such as Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens (Georgalaki et al., 2021), Lactobacillus kefiri (Carasi et al., 2015; Rajoka et al., 2019), Lactobacillus plantarum (Wang et al., 2009), Lactobacillus acidophilus (Zheng et al., 2013), and Lactobacillus pentosus (You et al., 2020), which exert their effects through strain-specific metabolites (exopolysaccharides, bacteriocins, bioactive peptides), cellular components (surface proteins), and intestinal colonization mechanisms. Consequently, to achieve quality-controlled industrial production of kefir and develop kefir products with validated health claims, more comprehensive in vivo experimentation and human clinical trials are required to verify efficacy and elucidate underlying mechanisms. Additionally, establishing quantitative detection standards for key functional strain biomarkers (specific genes, bioactive metabolites) as core indicators for product quality control will ensure consistency and reproducibility of probiotic effects in industrialized kefir products.

Production technology and industrial development of kefir

Fermentation methodologies of kefir

Traditional kefir represents a functional fermented dairy product prepared through inoculation of animal milk with kefir grains. According to the International Food Standards (Codex Stan 243–2003) definition, typical kefir (fermented milk produced from kefir grains) should contain a minimum of 2.7 % protein, 0.6 % lactic acid, and <10 % fat, with alcohol percentage remaining undefined, while the total microbial count in kefir should be at least 107 CFU/mL with yeast populations not <104 CFU/mL. International food standards further stipulate that kefir starter cultures (i.e., kefir grains) used for kefir production must encompass bacterial species of Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Lactococcus, and Acetobacter genera, in addition to lactose and non-lactose-fermenting yeasts.

The production of kefir can be broadly categorized into three principal approaches: Kefir Grain Fermentation (KGF), Back-Slopping Fermentation (BSF) and Direct Vat Set (DVS). Among these, KGF and BSF are considered traditional fermentation techniques. Fig. 3 outlines the process flow for kefir fermentation, while Table 3 provides a comparative analysis of the three major methods, encompassing recommended inoculation conditions, physicochemical properties, organoleptic properties, principal advantages and limitations, and typical applicable scenarios. In contemporary industrial practice, DVS predominates, wherein recombinant starter cultures are formulated to produce commercial kefir. The BSF approach relies on kefir obtained via KGF as the inoculum, and remains in use by certain small-scale dairy producers.

Kefir grain fermentation

KGF refers to the process of inoculating 2 %-10 % (w/v) kefir grains into animal milk, fermenting at room temperature for approximately 24 h, followed by filtration to remove kefir grains and obtain kefir (Ganatsios et al., 2021). In production procedures utilizing kefir grains for fermentation, the provenance of kefir grains is critically important, as microbial compositions of kefir grains from different geographical regions exhibit substantial variation, significantly influencing final product quality. During fermentation processes, kefir grain inoculation ratio (m/v), fermentation temperature (°C), and fermentation duration (h) equally impact kefir product quality. Post-fermentation downstream processing steps are equally crucial, including kefir grain separation from fermented milk, kefir grain washing, and maintaining hygienic and clean environments during refrigerated storage, which contribute to the quality and stability of subsequent production batches. Kefir grains possess a unique microbial architecture, with peripheral regions composed almost entirely of bacteria, predominantly bacilli, while interior regions contain yeasts, and intermediate sections harbor bacteria, yeasts (Lopitz-Otsoa et al., 2006; Palla et al., 2017). Within the kefir grain ecosystem, bacteria and fungi establish complex symbiotic relationships, including spatial structural interactions, each contributing distinct functions during kefir fermentation while mutually influencing one another, thereby conferring traditional kefir with distinctive flavor characteristics and functional properties (Rosa et al., 2017) Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens represents one of the predominant lactic acid bacteria discovered in global kefir grain ecosystems, possessing exopolysaccharide kefiran production capability and robust auto-aggregation capacity, thus participating in spontaneous kefir grain formation processes. Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens exhibits diverse probiotic activities, including antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, metabolic disorder prevention and amelioration, and intestinal microbiota regulation functions (Georgalaki et al., 2021). Kefiran, produced by Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, demonstrates extensive applications in the food industry while possessing therapeutic efficacies, including antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, antihypertensive, antitumor, and antidepressant effects (Sun et al., 2019). Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens proliferation requires stringent anaerobic environments and specialized nutritional growth requirements (Wang et al., 2008). Although Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens dominates within kefir grain interiors, it exhibits poor growth viability in the absence of kefir grains (Blasche et al., 2021). Existing research demonstrates that Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens cannot independently proliferate in milk but achieves milk fermentation objectives through symbiosis with other kefir microorganisms, such as coexistence with Leuconostoc mesenteroides (Zanirati et al., 2015). Lactobacillus kefiri, a high biofilm-forming strain, represents one of the key lactic acid bacterial species in kefir grain ecosystems, similarly participating in spontaneous kefir grain formation processes (Wang et al., 2012). Scanning electron microscopy observations reveal that lactic acid bacteria, including Lactobacillus kefiri, with short-chain arranged lactobacilli occupying kefir grain surfaces, while long-chain arranged lactobacilli, such as Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, aggregate toward the center (Wang et al., 2012). Lactobacillus kefiri has been demonstrated to possess antioxidant (Zanirati et al., 2015), anti-obesity (Ruiz-Tovar et al., 2023), anti-aging (Zhang et al., 2022), antihypertensive (Rubak et al., 2020), anti-inflammatory (Carasi et al., 2015), and anti-tumor effects (Rajoka et al., 2019). These distinctive lactic acid bacteria constitute a portion of shared microorganisms among kefir grains from different sources and represent characteristic strains in kefir produced via kefir grain fermentation, achieving total abundances exceeding 80 % (Carasi et al., 2022). However, extensive microbial diversity within kefir grains poses challenges to maintaining consistent product quality and controlling functional activities required in industrial dairy production. The dynamic nature of kefir microbial composition increases the complexity of kefir production standardization (Zhang et al., 2023). Related studies indicate that in traditional kefir production, kefir grain reuse results in lower proteolytic activity and poor reproducibility of kefir phosphopeptide profiles (O’Brien et al., 2016; Terpou, 2019), quality variations that are unacceptable in industrial production. In actual production processes, large fermentation vessel volumes render KGF non-uniform, collection laborious and impractical, and kefir grain growth relatively slow; therefore, traditional KGF is rarely applied to large-scale industrial production of kefir.

Back-slopping fermentation

BSF employs kefir derived from kefir grain fermentation as a starter culture, inoculated at 2 %-10 % (v/v) into pasteurized milk and fermented at ambient temperature for 15–24 h to yield successive generations of kefir (Nielsen et al., 2014). The kefir starter culture employed in BSF is designated as natural kefir starter, which, owing to its microbial consortium closely resembling that of natural kefir grains, produces kefir with minimal disparities compared to grain-fermented products across multiple parameters: microbiological profiles (encompassing Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, L. acidophilus, Bifidobacterium, and yeasts), chemical composition (including pH, lactic acid, total solids, protein, ethanol, and exopolysaccharide content), rheological properties, and sensory attributes. Moreover, relative to grain fermentation, this approach substantially enhances production yield while promoting proliferation of lactic acid bacteria and concomitant reduction of yeast populations during fermentation (Kim et al., 2018). Nevertheless, the microbial ecosystem within BSF exhibits considerable complexity, wherein each successive fermentation cycle induces compositional alterations in the kefir microbiome, consequently resulting in industrial products that forfeit the majority of traditional kefir's characteristic properties. Multiple studies have revealed that when kefir produced via grain fermentation serves as a subsequent starter inoculum, the dynamic equilibrium of the kefir microbial community becomes compromised. While lactic acid bacteria populations remain elevated, yeast counts progressively decline. However, from a commercial perspective, this yeast reduction confers advantages by extending the shelf life of kefir products during refrigerated storage (Kim et al., 2018; Garofalo et al., 2020).

Direct vat set

DVS utilizes lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, and yeasts isolated from traditional kefir grains or milk kefir, which are processed into freeze-dried bacterial powder and reconstituted to generate kefir starter cultures. In this approach, fermentation can proceed through two modalities: either within fermentation tanks or directly in packaging (Gomes Da Cruz et al., 2025). The implementation of reconstituted kefir starters obviates the necessity for kefir grain recovery, enabling effective control of batch-to-batch consistency in industrial production, thereby rendering large-scale kefir manufacturing more controllable and standardized (Sahoo et al., 2023). Extensive research has been conducted to characterize the microbial consortia of kefir grains and to isolate pure kefir culture inocula, with scientifically developed defined starters being advantageous for enhancing the quality and consistency of kefir. However, it is noteworthy that certain commercial products labeled as "kefir beverages" exhibit diminished microbial diversity compared to traditional kefir (Savastano et al., 2020), potentially containing select strains of Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, and Leuconostoc, while demonstrating a paucity of acetic acid bacteria and yeasts (Plessas et al., 2017; Terpou and Mantzourani, 2019). Since carbon dioxide produced by yeasts during kefir fermentation can cause package deformation during storage, commercial kefir typically contains minimal or no yeast populations (Margareth et al., 2020), facilitating control of gas and alcohol content during the storage period. Traditional kefir possesses greater diversity in its overall microbial ecosystem and, compared to commercial kefir produced using limited bacterial strains, can confer additional health attributes. Therefore, the principal challenge in commercial kefir production lies in manufacturing products that exhibit sensory, physicochemical, and functional properties analogous to those of traditional processing, while simultaneously satisfying the industrial production requirements for automation, standardization, and enhanced safety protocols.

Raw material sources and functional ingredient supplementation in kefir

Multifarious factors influence the product quality of kefir. Beyond the aforementioned predominant fermentation methodologies, parameters including starter inoculum concentration, fermentation conditions, raw material types, ingredient supplementation, and storage conditions exert pivotal influences on the microbial composition, flavor profiles, nutritional constituents, and functional efficacy of kefir (Sindi et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). The fermentation substrates for kefir typically encompass skimmed, semi-skimmed, or whole pasteurized milk, with animal milk varieties including bovine, caprine, ovine, camel, and buffalo milk (Rahimzadeh et al., 2015). Plant-based sources comprise soy milk, walnut milk, coconut milk, rice milk, and peanut milk (Gul et al., 2015), with bovine milk and soy milk representing the most prevalent animal-derived and plant-based substrates, respectively. The incorporation of functional ingredients either preceding or following the fermentation process of kefir contributes to enhanced flavor characteristics and functional properties. The impacts of functional components, including polysaccharides, essential oils, and botanical extracts, on the quality attributes of kefir products have been extensively investigated.

Raw material sources

Investigations have revealed that kefir grains derived from camel, bovine, caprine, or ovine milk exhibit analogous microbial characteristics (Öner et al., 2010). Compared to bovine milk, kefir prepared from buffalo milk demonstrates enhanced viscosity and consistency, while exhibiting diminished elastic modulus values (Gul et al., 2018). Camel milk kefir presents a relatively more acidic profile with intensified aromatic characteristics, displaying reduced consistency and appearance compared to bovine milk kefir (Kavas, 2015). Kefir produced from caprine and ovine milk generates polyunsaturated fatty acids, significantly influencing the aromatic profile of kefir by eliminating whey-associated aromatic compounds (aromatic substances related to whey powder) while augmenting creamy notes (aromatic compounds associated with milk fat; Cais Sokolińska et al., 2015). Furthermore, caprine milk possesses elevated concentrations of protein, fat, solids, vitamins, and minerals (Farag and Wessjohann, 2012). Camel milk kefir exhibits a lower proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids and reduced viable lactic acid bacteria counts, potentially attributable to bacteriocin peptides present in camel milk that inhibit the proliferation of certain microorganisms (Kavas, 2015). The incorporation of multiple animal milk types in kefir production is commonplace, as the co-fermentation of two animal milk varieties can enhance flavor profiles, textural properties, and probiotic characteristics. Ancient inhabitants of Greece and the Middle East traditionally employed blended caprine and bovine milk for fermenting kefir cheese (Kumar et al., 2022).

Kefir prepared using soy milk exhibits pronounced flavor and aromatic compound profiles. A study employing soybean extract to replace partial milk demonstrated that there were no significant differences in sensory acceptability between kefir products containing milk and soybean extract (Norberto et al., 2018). When kefir grains were inoculated into soy milk for adaptive incubation, the reduction in yeast numbers and disappearance of acetic acid bacteria resulted in decreased acetic acid and ethanol content in the fermented soy milk. The primary reason may be attributed to sucrose serving as the predominant carbon source in soy milk rather than lactose as found in bovine milk. Although kefir grain growth in soy milk led to reduced microbial diversity, the grains remained capable of adapting to the soy milk ecological environment, with kefir grain weight growth rates being higher compared to those in bovine milk (Gul et al., 2015). When kefir grains were cultivated in sugarcane molasses substrate, the dominant lactic acid bacteria genera abundance in bovine milk kefir grains shifted toward Bifidobacterium, with average grain weight increasing by approximately 10 %, indicating that interactions with Bifidobacterium or substrate alterations may generate exopolysaccharide-inducing effects (de Oliveira Filho et al., 2023). Plant-based materials, including coconut, walnut, rice, oat, almond, hazelnut, sesame, tiger nut, hemp, peanut, and cacao pulp, can similarly substitute for bovine milk as raw materials for non-dairy kefir beverage production (Aydar et al., 2020). Kefir fermentation using non-animal dairy products necessitates supplementation with additional carbon sources such as glucose, lactose, and sucrose to stimulate lactic acid bacteria and yeast growth. While kefir grains can proliferate in plant-based substrates and yield fermented products with functional properties, this cultivation attenuates grain vitality. Typically, after several fermentation cycles in non-dairy substrates, grains require reactivation in fat-containing bovine milk (Nielsen et al., 2014).

Functional ingredient supplementation

The incorporation of functional ingredients contributes to enhanced stability, flavor quality, and probiotic characteristics of kefir. Existing research demonstrates that the addition of polysaccharides (0.2 % xanthan gum) or pomegranate extract enhances the stability of kefir while conferring optimal rheological and sensory properties (Lagouri et al., 2019). The supplementation of pomegranate juice and honey influences the physicochemical, rheological, and sensory characteristics of kefir (Dimitreli et al., 2019). Thickening agents may enhance the overall stability and shelf life of kefir (Kavas, 2015). The incorporation of thickening agents (such as inulin) during kefir production does not significantly affect its chemical composition, microbial consortium, aroma, or flavor profiles, despite elevated viscosity values (Gul et al., 2018). Phytochemicals present in substances including fruit juices, botanical extracts, and essential oils possess antioxidant, antimicrobial, antihypertensive, and antidiabetic activities (Aydar et al., 2020). Post-fermentation addition of these compounds enhances functional properties while modifying the sensory attributes of the product (Turek and Wszołek, 2021). Using an immobilized starter containing mesophilic and thermophilic lactic acid bacteria along with Kluyveromyces marxianus to produce low-fat kefir, with pomegranate juice and honey added post-fermentation. Pomegranate juice addition resulted in product reddening, pH reduction, and increased acidity. Addition of <5 % pomegranate juice enhanced kefir viscosity, while higher concentrations exhibited contrary effects (Dimitreli et al., 2019). Following the completion of kefir fermentation, the addition of different concentrations of clove essential oil resulted in no significant changes in pH and titratable acidity. Adding 1 % clove essential oil enhanced sensory acceptance of kefir, while higher concentrations yielded diminished effects (Chon et al., 2020). Following the completion of kefir fermentation, the addition of 25 % black carrot juice, black mulberry, pomegranate, and strawberry juice enhanced the antioxidant activity and shelf life of kefir products by 1.8–4.8-fold (Dimitreli et al., 2019). Similarly, a study investigating the effects of radish essential oil on commercial kefir demonstrated that no significant pH differences were attributable to essential oil addition. Pre- and post-fermentation essential oil incorporation yielded differential effects; pre-fermentation addition facilitated uniform distribution and better preservation of sensory and biological characteristics, albeit affecting the fermentation kinetics of kefir bacteria and fungi (Soutelino et al., 2025).

Current status of commercial development of kefir

Existing research indicates that kefir was already widely recognized in Russia, Central Europe, and Eastern European countries by at least 1884. By approximately the 1960s, kefir began gaining widespread popularity in the Western world and gradually expanded globally. In different regions, kefir is designated by various appellations including kephir, kefyr, kiaphur, kefer, kepi, knapon, and kippi (Tamang et al., 2020). The heightened awareness of reducing sugar consumption has further propelled the market growth of kefir in European and American regions. Consumer vigilance toward increasingly sweetened food formulations has created opportunities for sugar-free and tart beverages to capture market share. Kefir attracts consumers through its distinctive and pronounced gustatory experience coupled with organic health and wellness concepts, earning the designation as "the yogurt of the 21st century." Currently, kefir in European and American markets has evolved from a niche ethnic beverage to a mainstream functional dairy product, with market scale and product innovation progressively strengthening. The combined market volume for North America and Europe is projected to increase from $2.49 billion in 2024 to $2.62 billion in 2025, representing a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of approximately 5.3 % (Kefir Market Report 2025- Kefir Market Forecast and Growth by 2034). Industrial production of kefir

The expansion of the kefir market has accelerated its industrialization process, yet this advancement confronts significant challenges encompassing microbial stability control, process scale-up efficiency, and product quality assurance. The fundamental challenge centers on maintaining the stability of kefir's complex microbial ecosystem during large-scale production while ensuring that product safety, sensory characteristics, and functionality meet established standards.

Industrial facilities for kefir fermentation must initially address critical control parameters that directly impact microbial stability, including temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen levels (Nejati et al., 2020). Temperature gradients within large fermentation vessels can create microenvironments that alter species interaction dynamics, potentially leading to abrupt shifts in microbial community structure across consecutive production cycles (Laureys and De Vuyst, 2017). pH regulation and oxygen transfer rates in industrial scale fermentation differ substantially from traditional small-batch processes, profoundly affecting the fermentation kinetics of facultatively anaerobic lactic acid bacteria and aerobic yeasts (Nejati et al., 2020). Consequently, establishing advanced industrial control systems such as Process Analytical Technology (PAT) systems becomes essential for real-time monitoring and flexible regulation of fermentation conditions specifically tailored for industrial kefir production (van den Berg et al., 2013). Modern industrial kefir production necessitates the implementation of multi-tiered microbial monitoring approaches that integrate traditional culture-based methods with advanced molecular technologies including flow cytometry (Bouix and Leveau, 2001), amplicon sequencing, and quantitative PCR techniques (Garofalo et al., 2020) to provide real-time surveillance of microbial community structure and stability throughout the fermentation process. The unique challenges associated with managing active microbial ecosystems in industrial kefir production require specific adaptations to Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) principles. Critical control points must encompass not only conventional food safety parameters but also key factors influencing microbial community stability, such as raw material composition control, starter culture management, and incubation time-temperature profiles (Leite et al., 2012). Furthermore, comprehensive environmental monitoring programs should be implemented across industrial facilities involved in kefir production to prevent the introduction of undesirable microorganisms that could compromise microbial community stability. These programs should include regular sampling of production surfaces, air quality assessment in fermentation areas, and monitoring of water sources utilized in cleaning and production processes (Bokulich and Mills, 2013).

In summary, industrial kefir production exhibits greater complexity and requires more critical control points compared to conventional fermented dairy product manufacturing, particularly regarding microbial community stability control. This necessitates the integration of modern molecular biology techniques, precision fermentation technology, and intelligent monitoring systems, while establishing comprehensive quality control frameworks based on HACCP principles. Such integrated approaches are essential to meet the standardization, stability, and safety requirements demanded for this traditional fermented dairy product in contemporary industrial production environments.

Principal challenges in kefir development

Safety concerns

Quality control and safety issues of kefir have been consistently intertwined with the dairy industry; however, the food safety of kefir has received limited attention. Traditional kefir production necessitates the utilization of kefir grains, with manufacturing processes involving grain separation, washing, and preservation. During these procedures, foodborne pathogens may be introduced due to environmental hygiene issues, thereby generating safety hazards. The complex microbial consortia present in kefir grains also constitute potential safety risks. Hemolysis represents a common virulence factor among pathogens, and bacterial hemolytic activity and antibiotic susceptibility serve as critical indicators for strain safety evaluation (Leite et al., 2015). Strains demonstrating unsafe profiles regarding hemolysis and antibiotic resistance can cause harm to human health. Related investigations have isolated 27 strains of Escherichia coli and other pathogenic bacteria from kefir grains, including Hafnia paralvei, Klebsiella oxytoca, Citrobacter freundii, and Serratia liquefaciens. These pathogenic bacteria exhibit resistance to ampicillin and vancomycin while demonstrating β-hemolytic properties (Çirpici and Çetin, 2023). Ileibacterium valens and Pseudomonas sp. identified in kefir grains have similarly raised contamination concerns, as this pathogenic bacterium has never been previously reported in kefir. Due to its production of deleterious compounds, its presence may be associated with adverse sensory changes in kefir (Liu et al., 2022; Cufaoglu and Erdinc, 2023; González‐Orozco et al., 2023). Given the potential food safety concerns of kefir, evaluation remains necessary regarding whether immunocompromised populations, including cancer, diabetes, AIDS, and immunodeficiency patients, can safely consume kefir to improve health outcomes. Additionally, assessment is required to determine whether high-risk susceptible populations, such as children, elderly individuals, pregnant women, or lactating mothers, can consume kefir without restrictions. While both animal and human clinical trials have demonstrated certain probiotic and therapeutic effects of kefir, cautious consideration should be given to employing kefir as adjunctive dietary therapy in certain disease patients. A clinical trial investigating the effects of kefir on colorectal cancer patients revealed that patients consuming kefir during treatment experienced deterioration in quality of life across multiple domains (Can et al., 2009). Relevant studies have demonstrated that patients consuming kefir during chemotherapy experienced adverse effects (including bloating, constipation, vomiting, dry mouth, nausea, drowsiness, sweating, appetite loss, dysphagia, oral ulcers, weight loss, and hair loss) more frequently compared to patients receiving standard care (Kairey et al., 2023).

Maintenance of probiotic viability and product standardization

The distinctive flavor and health benefits of kefir originate from its diverse symbiotic microbial consortium. Existing research has yet to achieve a comprehensive characterization of traditional kefir microbial communities, wherein the inter-microbial interactions are exceedingly complex, encompassing various modes including symbiosis, mutualism, competition, and antagonism. During the scale-up production and storage-transportation processes of kefir, microbial community imbalances readily occur, resulting in substantial fluctuations in microbial composition and functional viability across different product batches (Farag et al., 2020). Even when employing kefir grains with highly stable microbial communities for production, variations in bacterial and yeast species and abundance arise due to environmental condition changes during subculturing processes, rendering strict product consistency difficult to achieve in industrialized production based on the KGF method (Agarbati et al., 2021).

Currently, kefir-related products are produced and marketed globally, including traditional milk kefir using kefir grains as starter cultures and commercial milk kefir employing reconstituted microbial consortia as starter cultures. The traditional production method for kefir is KGF, and kefir grains from certain regions have been demonstrated to ferment and produce kefir with significant probiotic functional activity. However, due to production limitations of kefir grain fermentation methods, large-scale industrialized production remains challenging. Commercial starter cultures offer advantages in production scale and product standardization. However, due to limitations such as restricted microbial diversity and fermentation method constraints, commercial kefir struggles to achieve equivalent product quality compared to KGF, particularly in terms of flavor and health benefits. Species such as Lactobacillus. kefiri, Lactobacillus. parakefiri, and Lactobacillus. kefiranofaciens are typically associated with kefir grains, demonstrating specific adaptations to kefir grain environments. These strains are ubiquitously present in kefir grain-based kefir (Korsak et al., 2015) but exhibit poor growth vitality in liquid environments without kefir grain presence (Blasche et al., 2021). Non-lactose fermenting yeasts, including Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Torulaspora delbrueckii, Pichia fermentans, and lactose-fermenting yeasts such as Kluyveromyces marxianus, serve as important contributors to the functional properties of traditional kefir (Gil-Rodríguez et al., 2015; Johansen et al., 2019), including immunomodulation, antioxidant effects, gastrointestinal health benefits, and essential nutrient production (Fakruddin et al., 2017). Through co-cultivation with lactic acid bacteria, these yeasts demonstrate enhanced biological activity while imparting refreshing sensory characteristics to kefir. However, in commercial kefir production, yeast incorporation affects product stability during fermentation and storage periods. Therefore, how to rationally control and balance the functional properties and fermentation vitality of kefir-specific lactic acid bacteria and yeasts within the kefir microbial community represents a critical challenge in commercial kefir product development.

Innovative production methods for kefir

Synthetic microbial communities

The concept of synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) originated from microbiome engineering, with its design principles following the "Design-Build-Test-Learn" paradigm (van Leeuwen et al., 2023). Since SynComs employ microbial consortia with defined compositions and functions analogous to natural communities, while maintaining strain interactions present in natural microbial communities to a certain extent, they fulfill the requirements for multi-substrate transformation in traditional fermentation processes (Du et al., 2023). Currently, two primary design and construction strategies exist for SynComs: top-down and bottom-up approaches. The top-down design strategy involves directional domestication of natural microbial communities through environmental condition control and other methods, steering them toward representative microbial consortia with simpler structures yet retaining most functional capabilities. The bottom-up strategy entails investigating the individual characteristics of microorganisms within natural microbial communities, constructing novel artificially synthesized microbial communities based on specific objectives or principles through understanding microbial metabolic characteristics and interactions (Chen et al., 2023).

Top-down assembly strategies are primarily categorized into three approaches: enrichment (Du et al., 2023), artificial selection (Arias-Sánchez et al., 2019), directed evolution, and community fusion (Huet et al., 2023). "Enrichment" refers to spontaneous microbial communities that achieve stable simplification under specific environmental conditions through multiple dilution-growth cycles, thereby obtaining simpler and more stable community structures across multiple subcultures (Estrela et al., 2021). "Artificial selection and directed evolution" involve partitioning a natural microbial community into several identical sub-communities and incubating them in similar or different (directional intervention) environments, with artificial selection and directed evolution of the best-performing functional consortia for subsequent cycles. Artificial selection and directed evolution strategies require robust simulation and precise control of external environments, necessitating the establishment of effective and rapid evolutionary methods. Otherwise, dependence on the original environment may be compromised by environmental microorganisms, leading to prolonged selection cycles (Hu et al., 2022). Community fusion represents a common phenomenon in traditional food fermentation processes, such as microbial consortium fusion occurring when different raw materials and tools converge during spontaneous fermentation, or when microbial communities in starter cultures fuse with indigenous microflora in raw materials during BSF. When employing community fusion strategies to synthesize novel microbial communities, the primary objective is to create enhanced microbial consortia possessing multiple functional characteristics or cumulative functions through the fusion of two microbial communities with distinct functionalities. However, this synthetic strategy exhibits considerable uncertainty, requiring sufficient understanding of the structure and characteristics of foundational microbial communities, and necessitating consideration of fusion timing and proportions based on anticipated functional characteristic enhancement to ensure compatibility between microbial community stability and functional diversity (Diaz-Colunga et al., 2022). Overall, top-down self-assembly strategies can simplify natural microbial community structures while maintaining the functionality and stability of active microbial consortia, minimizing adverse characteristics in practical applications and industrial production (Gao et al., 2022).

The fundamental workflow of bottom-up strategies encompasses strain isolation, screening, and identification from native environments, followed by synthetic microbial community construction through manual combination, directed assembly, or reconstitution approaches. Compared to the challenges of strain isolation or cultivation present in other natural microecosystems, most microorganisms in fermented food systems are cultivable, making bottom-up synthetic strategies more readily applicable to food fermentation fields (Chen et al., 2023). Manual combination involves selecting microorganisms that occupy dominant positions in natural fermented food systems, including strains with similar or complementary functions for simple combinations. Directed assembly matches strains based on metabolic pathways of natural microbial systems or target metabolite types, performing strain combinations based on the concept of metabolic division of labor for target metabolites or target community functions. Reconstitution refers to isolating most strains from natural fermented microbial communities, investigating strain relative abundance, metabolic functions, interrelationships, and cultivation conditions based on culture-dependent or culture-independent (omics) technologies, and replicating the structure and function of original microbial systems through strain combination approaches (Jiang et al., 2024). Kefir SynComs utilize the concepts and methodologies of synthetic microbiology, optimizing and reconstituting microbial communities from traditional kefir grains through top-down or bottom-up approaches, thereby reproducing the community functional characteristics of traditional kefir grains as starter cultures at an industrial scale.

Kefir grain-like starter cultures

Traditional kefir production utilizes kefir grains as starter cultures, wherein the microbial communities within kefir grains undergo only minimal changes during fermentation. Kefir grains control the entire fermentation process through staged release of microorganisms into the milk matrix (Blasche et al., 2021), with products produced through traditional KGF possessing richer microbial community structures and greater consumer acceptance. However, due to difficulties in standardizing microbial communities and the relatively slow growth of kefir grains, this traditional KGF cannot be applied in large-scale industrial production. Constructing reusable and stable kefir grain-like starter cultures that immobilize kefir microbial communities to simulate the fermentation process of natural kefir grains has emerged as a promising solution.

The kefir-like pellet fermenter should contain the main fermenting species in the kefir microbial community. Researchers developed a kefir-like grain starter incorporating yeasts (Kluyveromyces marxianus, Pichia kudriavzevii), lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus kefiri), and acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter lovaniensis). The strains were encapsulated within liquid-core capsules according to their proportional distribution in kefir grains. Experimental results demonstrated that compared to kefir produced via kefir grain fermentation, the flavor compound profiles exhibited remarkable similarity, with no significant differences in alcohol content, protein, and fat levels except for acidity and sugar content, while antimicrobial capacity remained unchanged (Wang et al., 2016). Microbial immobilization typically involves cell adsorption onto material structures through electrostatic or covalent bonding, with certain natural carriers serving as matrices for immobilizing kefir microbial communities, by incorporating kefir cultures onto natural carriers (apple peels, grape skins, and delignified materials) and employing freeze-drying to immobilize the kefir cultures, utilizing these as fermentation starters for producing low-alcohol wine. This methodology of immobilizing kefir cultures on natural carriers through freeze-drying may be applicable to industrial kefir production, potentially achieving equivalent efficacy to kefir grain-based fixed fermentation systems (Nikolaou et al., 2020). Immobilized Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, Lactobacillus kefiri, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and Lactococcus lactis isolated from kefir grains were immobilized onto sugarcane chips via adsorption methods. Scanning electron microscopy revealed that microorganisms became embedded within the porous architecture of sugarcane chips, with the immobilized sugarcane chip starter demonstrating progressively enhanced fermentative capacity during continuous fermentation processes (Ng et al., 2020). Natural substances possessing chemical compositions analogous to kefir grains are equally suitable for producing kefir-like grain starters. Dominant strains isolated from kefir grains can also be employed to simulate kefir grains through encapsulated carriers generated via embedding and cheese carriers produced through conventional manufacturing methods. Cheese carriers exhibited similarities to natural kefir grains in terms of structure, chemical composition, microstructure, and microbial communities, demonstrating potential as kefir grain substitutes for kefir production (Han et al., 2020). Microbial interactions have been demonstrated to constitute strictly spatial interactions; consequently, kefir microbial communities immobilized on kefir-like grain starters should also possess spatial structural distributions comparable to natural kefir grains. Researchers are using encapsulated starters based on yeasts and bacteria isolated from kefir grains to produce kefir, achieving immobilization by encapsulating kefir starter cultures within alginate microspheres. Scanning electron microscopy showed that the short-chain lactobacilli and lactococci occupied the surface of the LAB microspheres, whereas the long-chain lactobacilli were inside the microspheres. The resulting kefir exhibited identical lactic acid bacteria and yeast distributions to KGF, with no significant differences in ethanol and exopolysaccharide content despite varying acidity levels (Chen et al., 2009). 3D bioprinting technology enables the design of intricate spatial architectures, utilizing hydrogels as substrates and employing living cell printing techniques to segregate microbial communities within distinct spatial structures, thereby achieving simulation of the physical spatial distribution of kefir grain microbial communities (He et al., 2022).

Conclusion and prospects

Kefir represents a historically significant fermented dairy product whose distinctive sensory characteristics and probiotic functionality derive from spontaneously formed kefir grains and their diverse, structurally complex microbial consortia. Numerous investigations have employed metagenomic and metabolomic approaches to elucidate the metabolic interactions within kefir microbial communities and decipher the mechanisms underlying kefir grain formation. Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens constitutes the cornerstone species across kefir samples of diverse geographical origins. Additionally, lactic acid bacterial species, including Lactobacillus helveticus, Lentilactobacillus kefiri, Lactococcus lactis, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and Lentilactobacillus parakefiri, acetic acid bacteria such as Acetobacter okinawensis, and yeasts encompassing Kluyveromyces marxianus and Saccharomyces cerevisiae are frequently detected in kefir grains and fermented kefir products. These intricate microbial communities orchestrate proteolysis, lipolysis, glycolysis, and cross-feeding mechanisms, collectively generating kefir's distinctive flavor profile, nutritional attributes, and health-promoting properties.

Three principal fermentation methodologies dominate kefir production: KGF, BSF, and DVS, each presenting distinct advantages and limitations. KGF yields the most authentic kefir products with exceptional flavor and probiotic characteristics, yet remains incompatible with large-scale industrial production. BSF utilizes kefir grain-derived fermented milk as a starter culture, producing kefir with abundant microbial diversity while enabling production scale expansion; however, this approach cannot sustain continuous subculturing and presents product stability challenges. DVS, while commercially prevalent due to its capacity to address stability concerns and scalability requirements, generates products with simplified microbial compositions that frequently exhibit inferior flavor and functional properties. To overcome these constraints, we propose three innovative strategies: Leveraging synthetic microbial community concepts to engineer and regulate kefir microbial consortia, integrating top-down and bottom-up approaches to construct kefir SynComs suitable for commercial development; implementing immobilized fermentation or biomaterial-based kefir grain simulation to replicate natural kefir grain fermentation processes at industrial scale; and utilizing drying technologies to produce powdered kefir products, thereby addressing alcohol and carbon dioxide-related issues during storage and transportation while enhancing product stability and reducing logistical costs.

The fundamental essence of kefir lies in its diverse microbial communities and the resulting distinctive flavor and probiotic characteristics. Consequently, kefir development and optimization should prioritize microbiome regulation as the central focus. Furthermore, current research predominantly concentrates on natural kefir microbial communities, with limited investigation into microbiome regulation under industrial production conditions. Future research endeavors should transition from descriptive surveys toward mechanistic, application-oriented studies that integrate natural kefir microbial community research with novel industrial production technologies. Critical considerations include defining core functional microbial communities in kefir, maintaining sensory and functional properties at an industrial scale, understanding how industrial environments reshape kefir microbial communities, and developing rapid monitoring systems for microbial community stability indicators under industrial production conditions.
