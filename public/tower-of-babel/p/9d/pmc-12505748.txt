Is postpartum preeclampsia a continuation of intrapartum preeclampsia? Maternal risk factors in intrapartum vs. postpartum preeclampsia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hila Shalev-Ram, Talish Razi, Dorit Ravid, Hezzy Shmueli, Ronen Arbel, Tal Biron-Shental
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare maternal characteristics and risk factors associated with intrapartum and postpartum preeclampsia in relation to pregnancies without preeclampsia.Study DesignThis retrospective cohort study used data from 265,068 pregnancies (2013–2024) categorized into three groups: no preeclampsia (96.9%), intrapartum preeclampsia (2.8%), and postpartum preeclampsia (0.2%). Multivariate logistic regression models identified risk factors associated with intrapartum preeclampsia and postpartum preeclampsia, with no preeclampsia as the reference group.ResultsPostpartum preeclampsia shared remarkably similar risk factors with intrapartum preeclampsia after adjusting for confounders. Key associations included maternal age (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.03, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.02–1.03 for intrapartum pre-eclampsia and aOR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.03–1.04 for postpartum preeclampsia); obesity (aOR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.41–1.59 for intrapartum preeclampsia and aOR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.43–1.61 for postpartum preeclampsia), and nulliparity (aOR = 2.20, 95% CI: 2.05–2.35 for intrapartum preeclampsia and aOR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.99–2.27 for postpartum preeclampsia). Additional associations included diabetes (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.36–1.76 for intrapartum preeclampsia; aOR = 1.51 and 95% CI: 1.33–1.71 for postpartum preeclampsia); hypertension (aOR = 4.02, 95% CI: 3.50–4.59 for intrapartum preeclampsia and OR = 4.01, 95% CI: 3.51–4.56 for postpartum preeclampsia), and gestational hypertension (aOR = 2.46, 95% CI: 2.21–2.74 for intrapartum pre-eclampsia and aOR = 2.46, 95% CI: 2.22–2.73 for postpartum preeclampsia.ConclusionPostpartum and intrapartum preeclampsia share similar clinical and demographic risk factors, suggesting postpartum preeclampsia may be a continuation of preeclampsia rather than a distinct clinical entity. Identifying shared risk factors can facilitate early diagnosis and intervention, critical for reducing maternal morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

Hypertension complicates approximately 7% of pregnancies, with 1% attributed to chronic or pre-existing hypertension and 3% to preeclampsia (PE) [1]. PE, a serious hypertensive disorder associated with pregnancy and the puerperium, poses significant short- and long-term risks to both maternal and fetal health[2–7]. Itis broadly categorized into intrapartum PE (IPPE) the most common form, which arises during pregnancy or labor, and postpartum PE (PPPE), which can manifest up to six weeks after delivery and is increasingly recognized as a significant contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality during the postpartum period [8].

Whether PPPE represents a continuation of intrapartum PE or constitutes a distinct clinical entity remains a topic of debate. Traditionally, the delivery of the placenta was believed to “cure” PE; however, the delayed onset of PPPE days or even weeks postpartum challenges this paradigm. This uncertainty raises questions about whether PPPE is an extension of the intrapartum condition or a separate disorder with unique risk factors and pathophysiology.

While IPPE has been extensively studied and is well-documented [9], research on PPPE remains limited [10–13]. Existing studies lack direct comparisons between PPPE and IPPE [14–16], leaving critical gaps in understanding their differences and implications. Delayed recognition and treatment—stemming from a lack of awareness regarding its risk factors and clinical presentation—may exacerbate the condition and lead to worse outcomes [10].

This study aimed to address these gaps by conducting a comparative analysis of IPPE and PPPE while including a control group of women without PE.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study utilized electronic data from Clalit Health Services (CHS) and included all CHS members, ages 18 to 55 years, who delivered in CHS hospitals from 2013 to 2024. CHS includes 9 hospitals and over 1,400 community clinics across Israel, serving more than 50% of the population.

Ethics

The study was approved by the CHS Institutional Ethics Committee and the CHS Data Utilization Committee and was exempted from the requirement to obtain informed consent.

We excluded higher-order multiple pregnancies (triplets and beyond) and births without documented delivery data. The study population was divided into three groups: no preeclampsia, intrapartum preeclampsia (IPPE), and postpartum preeclampsia (PPPE).

Data extraction

The diagnosis of IPPE or PPPE was classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes (642.4, 642.5, 642.6, 642.7). IPPE was defined as preeclampsia occurring during pregnancy and up to 48 h after birth, while PPPE was defined as preeclampsia developing from 48 h to 6 weeks postpartum. This definition was chosen based on recent literature [11] suggesting that PPPE is best characterized as new onset preeclampsia from 48 h to 6 weeks after birth.

PPPE is usually divided into two subtypes: immediate postpartum PE, occurring within the first 48 h after delivery, and delayed onset PE, which develops 48 h to 6 weeks postpartum. Although current guidelines lack precise definitions [4, 8, 9], expert consensus and the latest literature support defining PPPE as preeclampsia first diagnosed 48 h or more after birth [10, 11, 17]. Accordingly, in this study, PPPE was defined as preeclampsia occurring from 48 h to six weeks postpartum. Pregnancies in which preeclampsia was diagnosed within the first 48 h after delivery were categorized under the IPPE group.

Additional study variables included maternal age at birth, smoking status, and socioeconomic status (SES), which were retrieved from electronic records. SES was defined based on the poverty index of the child’s residential area, as determined by the 2011 national census [18]. The poverty index considers multiple factors, including housing type, household income, education level, crowding, material conditions, car ownership, and overall population census data. Obesity (Body Mass Index > 30) and comorbidities, including diabetes, cardiac disease, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and epilepsy, were identified from electronic health records using ICD-9-CM codes. Pregnancy-related variables were retrieved from the CHS electronic databases and included the number of fetuses, presence of gestational diabetes or hypertension, gestational age at delivery, preterm birth, anesthesia during delivery, mode of delivery (cesarean section, vacuum extraction, induction of labor), and fetal birth weight.

Statistical analysis

The three groups (no pre-eclampsia, IPPE, and PPPE) were compared to evaluate differences in characteristics between them. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. Dichotomous variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. To identify factors independently associated with IPPE and PPPE, multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. “Separate models were built for intrapartum and postpartum preeclampsia, with the No Preeclampsia group serving as the reference. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each predictor variable. Variables included in the regression models were selected based on clinical relevance and univariate analyses.

Analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.0.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). All P values were 2-sided, and results were deemed statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Study sample

Among 265,068 pregnancies, 256,917 (96.9%) women did not have preeclampsia, 7,536 (2.8%) had IPPE, and 615 (0.2%) had PPPE.

As shown in Table 1, women with PPPE compared to those with IPPE or no preeclampsia were older (34.0 vs. 31.0 vs. 30.0 years, p < 0.001), had higher socioeconomic scores (6.0 vs. 5.0 vs. 5.0, p < 0.001), and higher rates of smoking (8.0% vs. 4.6% vs. 5.0%, p < 0.001). Obesity prevalence was elevated in both the PPPE and IPPE groups compared to women without PE (25% vs. 23% vs. 12%, p < 0.001). Nulliparity was more frequent in the IPPE group (48%) than in the PPPE (28%) or no-PE (29%) groups (p < 0.001). Assisted reproduction and multiple-fetus pregnancies were also more common in PE groups (ART use: 15.6% and 15.1% vs. 6.6%; multiple gestations: 9.3% vs. 8.3% vs. 2.2%; both p < 0.001).

Comorbid conditions were significantly more prevalent in the PE groups: diabetes (4.7% vs. 4.2% vs. 1.0%, p < 0.001), cardiac disease (1.2% vs. 1.5% vs. 0.7%, p < 0.001), hypertension (4.9% vs. 5.4% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.001), gestational diabetes (12% vs. 5.9% vs. 3.3%, p < 0.001), and gestational hypertension (6.3% vs. 6.1% vs. 1.4%, p < 0.001).

Gestational age at delivery was lower among both PPPE and IPPE patients compared to those without PE (38.0 vs. 38.0 vs. 39.0 weeks, p < 0.001). Preterm labor occurred more frequently in the IPPE group (30% vs. 13% vs. 6.7%, p < 0.001), as did cesarean delivery (43% vs. 41% vs. 18%, p < 0.001) and labor induction (36% vs. 30% vs. 20%, p < 0.001).

Newborns of women with IPPE had lower birthweights (2,885 g vs. 3,140 g vs. 3,232 g, p < 0.001) and longer hospitalization (5.0 vs. 4.0 vs. 3.0 days, p < 0.001) compared to PPPE and non-PE groups.

Comparison between intrapartum and postpartum PE compared to healthy patients

As shown in Fig. 1; Table 2, after adjusting for confounders, both IPPE and PPPE shared similar risk factors after adjusting for confounders. Maternal age (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03 for IPPE and OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.03–1.04 for PPPE), obesity (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.41–1.59 for IPPE and OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.43–1.61 for PPPE), and nulliparity (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 2.05–2.35 for IPPE and OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.99–2.27 for PPPE) were significantly associated with both groups. Diabetes was also strongly associated (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.36–1.76 for IPPE and for PPPE, OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.33–1.71).

Comment

Principal findings

This study found that women with PPPE share similar risk factors with those diagnosed with IPPE, even after controlling for confounding variables.

The classification of IPPE is currently based on gestational age at diagnosis, differentiating early-onset from late-onset preeclampsia. Early-onset preeclampsia is strongly associated with abnormal placentation [19, 20], whereas late-onset preeclampsia is attributed to a mismatch between maternal perfusion capacity and feto-placental demands, compounded by maternal predispositions such as cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome [21]. Consistent with our findings related to PPPE, late-onset preeclampsia is also strongly associated with metabolic risk factors, including diabetes mellitus [22], and elevated BMI [23, 24]. Although this study did not categorize IPPE into early- and late-onset subtypes, we hypothesize that PPPE represents a continuation of late-onset preeclampsia, given the significantly higher prevalence of late-onset cases compared to early-onset pre-eclampsia [22] and their likely predominance within our IPPE cohort.

Results in the context of what is known

Our findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that women with delayed-onset PPPE tend to exhibit distinct clinical characteristics. These women are generally older [12], have higher rates of obesity [12–15], undergo more cesarean deliveries [14, 15] and are more likely to have chronic hypertension [15, 25] and pre-existing diabetes [12, 13]. Notably, these risk factors are also observed in women with IPPE [24, 26, 27], further supporting the hypothesis that PPPE and IPPE may represent overlapping manifestations of a common underlying disease process.

The elevated rate of cesarean delivery in women with PPPE may itself contribute to the development of postpartum hypertension. One proposed mechanism involves increased exposure to intravenous (IV) fluids during labor and delivery [28]. Excessive administration of crystalloids can lead to interstitial fluid accumulation, which may subsequently remobilize into the intravascular compartment after delivery, predisposing to volume overload and elevated blood pressure. This fluid dynamic has been implicated as a potential contributor to the pathophysiology of PPPE.

Other factors, such as the use of assisted reproductive technology [26, 29], multiple gestations [27, 30, 31] and nulliparity [29–31] are established risk factors for IPPE but have not been described in studies on PPPE. This gap highlights the need for further research to explore whether these factors contribute to the development of PPPE, thereby enhancing our understanding of its etiology and overlap with IPPE.

Clinical implications

As our study is among the first to directly compare PPPE and IPPE using a large retrospective cohort, it provides compelling evidence that PPPE likely represents a continuation or variation of pre-eclampsia—specifically late-onset pre-eclampsia—rather than a distinct clinical entity. Supporting this hypothesis, soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) is a well-established pathogenic factor in preeclampsia, with an imbalance between sFlt-1 and the proangiogenic placental growth factor (PlGF) playing a key role in the development of IPPE [32]. A prospective study that measured sFlt-1 and PlGF levels prior to cesarean delivery demonstrated that women who later developed new-onset PPPE exhibited significantly higher sFlt-1 concentrations and an elevated sFlt-1/PlGF ratio compared to those who remained normotensive postpartum [13]. This biomarker profile closely parallels that observed in IPPE, suggesting that PPPE may reflect a subclinical form of preeclampsia that becomes clinically apparent only in the postpartum period.

Research implications

Approximately 60% of patients with delayed-onset postpartum pre-eclampsia (PPPE) remain normotensive throughout pregnancy [13] and have no prior diagnosis of hypertensive disorders [16], underscoring the importance of identifying at-risk populations. Moreover, PPPE occurs in the postpartum period when patients are no longer under the direct care of obstetricians or gynecologists. This transitional phase creates a gap in care, as family physicians and cardiologists, who often take over management, may lack familiarity with the clinical presentation and risks associated with PPPE. The resulting lack of awareness among healthcare providers can lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment, potentially exacerbating the condition and increasing the risk of adverse outcomes.

This study offers valuable insights into identifying women at elevated risk for PPPE, raising awareness of its symptoms, and emphasizing the need for proactive blood pressure monitoring to ensure timely and effective treatment. Remote blood pressure monitoring has proven effective in improving adherence to ACOG recommendations, which advocate for blood pressure assessments within 3 to 10 days postpartum [33]. This approach not only helps to reduce disparities in BP evaluation but also enables early detection of previously unrecognized hypertension, allowing prompt initiation of treatment during this critical period [33–35].

Strengths and limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the retrospective design meant that certain types of data were not available for analysis, which could affect the findings. Second, variability in diagnoses, particularly among women diagnosed outside of hospital settings, may have influenced the results. To address this, our PPPE cohort included only women with no prior diagnosis of hypertension during pregnancy, ensuring that postpartum hypertension represented a new condition. Additionally, we lacked data on the precise timing of pre-eclampsia diagnosis, which could have provided valuable insights to differentiate between the two entities and assess whether their risk factors differ. Finally, the lack of racial data is a notable limitation, as previous research has shown that non-Hispanic Black women are at higher risk for pre-eclampsia [12, 15, 36]. Additionally, the absence of data on aspirin use represents a limitation, as aspirin is known to reduce the risk of intrapartum preeclampsia [37, 38].

Despite these limitations, the study’s large sample size increased its statistical power and generalizability. Furthermore, the inclusion of important variables such as maternal age, and smoking and socioeconomic status strengthens the robustness of the analysis, providing a comprehensive understanding of pre-eclampsia risk factors.

Conclusions

The results of this study underscore the need for improved awareness and monitoring of postpartum preeclampsia. Recognizing shared risk factors between IPPE and PPPE can aid in early identification and intervention, potentially reducing maternal morbidity and mortality. Moreover, the findings support the systemic vascular dysfunction theory as the primary pathophysiological mechanism in PPPE, suggesting a need for further research into targeted prevention and treatment strategies for women at risk for this condition.
