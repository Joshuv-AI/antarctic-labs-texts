Bionic sleep therapy for refractory insomnia: a case report and literature review
Authors: Qiu-Xia Xiao, Shuai-Yu Zhu, Yan-Yan Ma, Peng-Cheng Zhao, Hong-Xia Chen, Yi-Jun Bao, Jiang-Ming Lei, Lin Wang, Huan Zhou, Run-Xing Ma, Liu-Lin Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe management of refractory insomnia remains a significant clinical challenge. This case report describes a novel intervention termed “Bionic Sleep Therapy,” involving the combined intravenous administration of propofol and dexmedetomidine, in a patient with severe, long-standing insomnia that had proven resistant to conventional multimodal treatments.Case DescriptionA 49-year-old woman with a 21-year history of refractory insomnia, characterized by severe sleep initiation and maintenance difficulties accompanied by symptoms of anxiety and depression. Despite prior treatments including Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), various sedative-hypnotics, and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), her symptoms persisted. She underwent “Bionic Sleep Therapy”—a target-controlled infusion of propofol and dexmedetomidine—over three sessions. Polysomnography (PSG) during therapy revealed a pharmacologically induced sleep-like state predominantly composed of N2 sleep architecture. Following the intervention, her objective sleep notably improved from a baseline of 0% to a peak of 51%. Concurrently, notable improvements were observed in her subjective sleep quality and daytime alertness scores, alongside a reduction in anxiety and depressive symptoms.ConclusionBionic Sleep Therapy, utilizing propofol and dexmedetomidine, may serve as a promising short-term bridging intervention for patients with refractory insomnia who are unresponsive to standard therapies. It appears to facilitate the breaking of the hyperarousal cycle and improve subjective sleep quality. However, given the pharmacological nature of the sleep induced, further large-scale, controlled studies are required to validate its long-term efficacy and safety profile.

Introduction

Sleep, a fundamental physiological process occupying approximately 20–40% of the daily life cycle in most adults, serves as a cornerstone for cognitive function, emotional regulation, and long-term health [1, 2]. Conversely, insomnia-characterized by persistent difficulties with sleep initiation, maintenance, or early morning awakenings-is a prevalent disorder that impairs daytime functioning and diminishes quality of life [3]. In China, the prevalence of insomnia is notably high, with reports indicating nearly half of adults experience sleep disturbances, a figure seemingly exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic [4]. First-line treatments typically include Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) and pharmacotherapy. However, these approaches face significant limitations, including limited access to specialized CBT-I providers, risks of medication dependency, suboptimal long-term efficacy, and side effects [5, 6]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to explore safe and effective alternative therapeutic strategies for patients who respond poorly to conventional treatments.

Bionic Sleep Therapy is an emerging sleep treatment strategy with preliminary reports in Chinese clinical practice. Its theoretical foundation lies in the observation that certain anesthetic and sedative agents can induce a physiological state closely resembling specific stages of natural sleep. For instance, the sedative state produced by the α2-adrenergic receptor agonist dexmedetomidine exhibits electroencephalographic (EEG) features strikingly similar to those of stage N2 non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep [7, 8]. Similarly, the GABAA receptor agonist propofol can induce rapid loss of consciousness accompanied by EEG signatures sharing properties with NREM sleep [8]. Building on this, Bionic Sleep Therapy involves the controlled and monitored administration of such agents to deliberately induce a sustained sleep-like state. The proposed mechanism involves reducing cortical and subcortical hyperarousal-a core pathophysiological feature of chronic insomnia-thereby promoting stabilization of the sleep-wake system [8–10]. The synergistic combination of propofol and dexmedetomidine is particularly noteworthy; propofol offers rapid and reliable hypnosis induction, while dexmedetomidine contributes a stable N2-like sleep architecture with favorable hemodynamic and respiratory profiles [11]. This strategy thus aims to rapidly, yet in a physiologically mimetic manner, induce a sleep state to address the hyperarousal in insomnia and potentially improve sleep quality in a safe, controllable fashion. Guided by this theory, Bionic Sleep Therapy entails inducing a sleep state with NREM-like EEG features through precise titration of anesthetic agents under close monitoring, aiming to restore sleep rhythms and thereby facilitate short-term symptomatic relief and subsequent consolidation therapy.

This case report details the successful application of this novel Bionic Sleep Therapy in a patient with severe, chronic insomnia who had undergone extensive prior interventions, highlighting its potential as a targeted treatment option.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old woman presented with a chief complaint of refractory insomnia persisting for over 21 years. Her core symptoms included severe difficulties with both sleep initiation and maintenance, resulting in near-total sleeplessness on most nights. Associated symptoms encompassed bilateral temporal and orbital distending pain, anxiety, impaired attention and memory, irritability, and anhedonia unrelated to her occupation. Despite extensive prior pharmacotherapy at other institutions—including trials of lorazepam, alprazolam, citalopram, clonazepam, valproic acid sodium, fluvoxamine, eszopiclone, duloxetine, and olanzapine—her sleep disturbance remained refractory. A comprehensive baseline assessment was conducted upon her admission to our sleep therapy clinical center. The evaluation included a sleep diary (recording 0% sleep efficiency on the admission night), the Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS; score 50, indicating mild anxiety), the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS; score 58, indicating mild depression), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; score 17, indicating poor sleep quality), the Somatic Self-Rating Scale (SSS; score 45, indicating moderate severity), and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; score 27, indicating severe clinical insomnia) (Fig. 1). Furthermore, baseline polysomnography (PSG) monitoring revealed an absence of N3 (slow-wave) sleep and a reduction in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (Fig. 2A, D). The final diagnosis was chronic insomnia disorder, comorbid with mild depressive and anxiety states, as well as moderate somatization.

Given the chronicity, severity, and psychiatric comorbidities, a comprehensive conventional treatment regimen was initiated. This included psychotherapy (e.g., Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia [CBT-I], suggestive therapy, and relaxation training), physical therapies (e.g., light therapy, biofeedback, transcranial magnetic stimulation [TMS], transcranial electrical stimulation, medium-frequency pulse electrotherapy), stellate ganglion block, and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) modalities (e.g., acupoint selection based on the midnight-noon ebb-flow theory, therapeutic massage). For pharmacotherapy, a combination of eszopiclone 2 mg and trazodone hydrochloride 25 mg was administered nightly at bedtime to target both sleep initiation and mood improvement (Fig. 1). To standardize the assessment of sleep and daytime function, the patient maintained a structured sleep diary throughout her hospitalization, completed each morning. Parameters recorded included caffeine intake, daytime naps, exercise, bedtime, sleep onset latency (SOL), number and total duration of nocturnal awakenings (WASO), final awakening time, final rise time, total sleep time (TST), time in bed (TIB), and calculated sleep efficiency (SE = [TST/TIB] × 100%). Subjective ratings for sleep quality and daytime alertness were also recorded using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = worst, 5 = best). Nights subjectively reported as having “no sleep” were documented as TST = 0 min and SE = 0% [12]。However, following this multimodal intervention, the patient reported no subjective improvement in sleep, describing persistent nights with perceived absence of sleep. Her sleep diary consistently showed SE = 0%. Consequently, to effectively address the significant “sleep debt” accumulated from chronic sleep deprivation and to rapidly restore therapeutic confidence, a multidisciplinary team comprising specialists in anesthesiology, neurology, psychiatry, and nursing evaluated the case and recommended proceeding with Bionic Sleep Therapy.

The patient was transferred to the sleep monitoring suite in the morning after fasting for at least 6 h for solid foods and 2 h for clear fluids. Standard monitoring (electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry) was established, a peripheral intravenous line was secured, and PSG monitoring was initiated synchronously. Under supplemental oxygen, propofol (1 mg/kg) was administered as an intravenous bolus over 30 s, followed by a continuous intravenous infusion of dexmedetomidine at 0.2–0.4 µg/kg/h to maintain a sleep-like state for approximately 1 h. The PSG recorded during the therapy session was scored predominantly as stage N2 sleep (Fig. 2B, C). Hemodynamic and respiratory parameters remained stable throughout the procedure, with no adverse events such as hypotension, bradycardia, or respiratory depression.

Prior to the first treatment session (Day 1), her sleep diary indicated an SE of 0% (TIB = 450 min), with subjective ratings for sleep quality and daytime alertness at 1/5 and 2/5, respectively. On the night following the first Bionic Sleep Therapy session, however, the patient again reported insomnia and anxiety. Consequently, alprazolam 0.2 mg was added at night, and the dose of trazodone hydrochloride was increased from a baseline of 25 mg to 50 mg. Despite this, she reported only a vague sensation of having slept. Her sleep diary showed an SE of 0% (TIB = 420 min) with subjective sleep quality and daytime alertness scores remaining at 1/5 and 2/5, respectively. To consolidate therapeutic effects, a second Bionic Sleep Therapy session was performed on Day 3. On the night following this second session, SE improved to 29% (TST = 120 min; TIB = 410 min), with subjective sleep quality and daytime alertness scores improving to 3/5 and 4/5, respectively (Fig. 2E, F). Following the third Bionic Sleep Therapy session on Day 4, SE was maintained at 29%, with subjective ratings at 3/5 for sleep quality and 4/5 for daytime alertness. Notably, on the night after the third session (Day 5), SE further increased to 51% (TST = 210 min; TIB = 410 min) despite prolonged wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO = 120 min), while the subjective ratings remained stable. Following the three Bionic Sleep Therapy sessions, a marked reduction in sleep onset latency was observed. The patient continued her conventional multimodal treatment regimen for an additional 4 days before discharge. Re-assessment prior to discharge showed significant improvements across rating scales: SAS score 45 (within normal range), SDS score 52 (mild), PSQI score 13 (indicating generally poor sleep quality), SSS score 38 (mild), and ISI score 20 (moderate) (Fig. 2D). The patient was scheduled for long-term outpatient follow-up upon discharge but was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Discussion

This case report describes a 49-year-old female patient with a 21-year history of severe, refractory insomnia accompanied by significant anxiety, depressive symptoms, and somatic complaints. Her condition remained unimproved despite extensive prior pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. The combined administration of propofol and dexmedetomidine, termed Bionic Sleep Therapy, successfully induced a sustained, monitored, sleep-like state characterized predominantly by N2-stage EEG architecture, without any adverse events. Following three Bionic Sleep Therapy sessions, objective sleep efficiency improved from 0% to a peak of 51%. This improvement was paralleled by significant enhancements in subjective sleep quality, daytime alertness, and affective symptoms. This case suggests that Bionic Sleep Therapy may represent a novel short-term intervention capable of disrupting the vicious cycle of chronic insomnia, particularly in treatment-refractory cases. It could serve as a "therapeutic bridge," creating a window of improved physiological and psychological state during which patients might become more responsive to other non-pharmacological therapies, potentially reducing long-term reliance on hypnotic medications. Therefore, consideration should be given to integrating this approach earlier into the treatment algorithm for patients who have failed first- and second-line therapies (Fig. 3).

Literature review

Clinical research on bionic sleep therapy for insomnia

In recent years, Bionic Sleep Therapy, often exemplified by pharmacologically-induced "sleep modulation," has garnered significant attention in clinical research as an interventional strategy for refractory chronic insomnia. Its core premise involves using short-term pharmacological sedation to aim at "resetting" the dysregulated sleep-wake rhythm. Existing clinical evidence primarily centers on two agents: Dexmedetomidine and Propofol.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, demonstrates multifaceted therapeutic potential within the context of Bionic Sleep Therapy. Multiple clinical studies report that dexmedetomidine-induced sleep modulation effectively improves sleep quality in patients with chronic refractory primary insomnia, manifested as shortened sleep latency, prolonged total sleep time, and enhanced sleep efficiency. Its mechanism of action is likely associated with reducing cortical hyperarousal and modulating the activity of neurotransmitters such as serotonin and norepinephrine [13–16]. Research also suggests potential benefits for concomitant cognitive impairment [17]. Within combination regimens, dexmedetomidine has been reported to yield further improvements in sleep architecture and emotional state when co-administered with interventions such as stellate ganglion block [18], esketamine [19, 20], or repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) [21]in insomnia patients comorbid with pain, depression, or anxiety symptoms. Regarding dosage exploration, studies have attempted to determine its 95% effective dose (ED95) for achieving adequate sedation in chronic insomnia patients [22]. In terms of safety profile, most studies report a low incidence of adverse events with minimal respiratory and cardiovascular depression, although its long-term safety profile warrants further evaluation [23].

Propofol, a classic intravenous anesthetic agent, has also been utilized to induce sleep modulation. Clinical studies indicate that propofol-induced sleep therapy can effectively and rapidly improve sleep quality in patients with chronic insomnia, and has been described as an efficient method for short-term correction of “sleep debt” [6, 24]. Its mechanism likely involves the modulation of neurotransmitter systems such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and norepinephrine (NE) [25]. Compared to dexmedetomidine, some studies note that propofol exhibits less pronounced suppression of sympathetic nervous function and may not significantly suppress periodic limb movements during sleep [26, 27]. Regarding combination therapy, propofol co-administered with Acanthopanax senticosus injection has been reported to produce synergistic effects [28]. Overall, existing literature suggests that propofol-based sleep modulation acts rapidly and demonstrates a favorable safety profile under strict monitoring.

Pharmacologically-induced sleep modulation, primarily based on dexmedetomidine and propofol, offers a potential therapeutic alternative for refractory insomnia. The Bionic Sleep Therapy explored in this case report represents a practical application and investigation conducted within this clinical research context. The improvement observed after three treatment sessions in our case aligns with the findings reported in this literature. The induced sleep-like state may contribute not only to immediate sleep restoration but also to disrupting the “insomnia-anxiety” cycle, reducing hyperarousal, and thereby promoting sleep stabilization. While monotherapy with either dexmedetomidine or propofol is commonly reported, our case utilized a combined regimen to leverage their complementary properties: propofol for rapid and reliable induction, and dexmedetomidine for maintaining a stable, physiologically more analogous sedative state with analgesic/anxiolytic properties and minimal respiratory depression, potentially enhancing safety [9, 29, 30]. This strategy may allow for lower doses of each agent, helping to preserve cardiopulmonary stability and mitigate dose-related adverse effects [30].

Role of propofol and dexmedetomidine in sleep architecture

Propofol is a rapidly acting GABAA receptor agonist that induces a state of deep sedation characterized on EEG by slow waves and spindle-like activity. These EEG features bear a striking resemblance to those of NREM sleep, particularly stage N2, leading to its more accurate description as a “pharmacologically-induced sleep-like state” [31, 32]. Mechanistically, propofol enhances GABAergic inhibition, directly suppressing multiple key wake-promoting nuclei. This includes cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain, noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus, and reduces the release of wake-promoting neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine and glutamate within the hypothalamic arousal circuitry [33–36]. By broadly inhibiting these arousal pathways, propofol effectively counteracts the core pathophysiology of insomnia—central hyperarousal—thereby facilitating rapid sleep onset and promoting a sleep architecture predominated by stage N2 [32]. Studies indicate that short-term propofol infusion not only immediately increases NREM sleep but may also exert regulatory effects lasting for several months, suggesting the drug might aid in the long-term consolidation of sleep stability by inhibiting the initiation of wakefulness [6]. Consequently, in the context of Bionic Sleep Therapy, propofol plays a crucial role in rapidly suppressing hyperarousal and reestablishing sleep continuity.

Dexmedetomidine is a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist whose hypnotic effects are primarily mediated by activating α2 receptors within the locus coeruleus. This action inhibits norepinephrine release, thereby reducing the level of cortical hyperarousal. The sedative state induced by this agent exhibits EEG features similar to those of stage N2 sleep, such as spindle activity, and is thus considered a pharmacologically-induced state closely approximating physiological NREM sleep [10, 37]. Mechanistically, dexmedetomidine stabilizes and prolongs NREM sleep by inhibiting the locus coeruleus-noradrenergic system. This inhibition disinhibits the sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO). The activated VLPO, in turn, releases GABA to suppress wake-promoting nuclei, such as the tuberomammillary nucleus [38–40]. Clinical studies confirm that dexmedetomidine significantly increases the duration and proportion of stage N2 sleep, reduces the number of awakenings, improves sleep efficiency, and enhances overall sleep architecture [41, 42]. Its effects extend beyond the improvement of macroscopic sleep parameters to include neuromodulation of the sleep-wake pathways, making it particularly suitable for chronic insomnia characterized by hyperarousal.

Potential mechanisms underlying the alleviation of insomnia with concomitant anxiety and depression symptoms by propofol and dexmedetomidine

This study observed that the combined regimen of propofol and dexmedetomidine not only improved insomnia but also alleviated the accompanying symptoms of anxiety and depression. This synergistic effect suggests that the underlying mechanisms may involve multifaceted interactions between pharmacological actions and sleep-mediated pathways. First, chronic insomnia is increasingly viewed as a multidimensional disorder of hyperarousal encompassing cortical, autonomic, and cognitive-emotional systems. This persistent, systemic state of heightened arousal shares overlapping neural circuits and neurochemical substrates with anxiety and depressive disorders, such as hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis hyperactivity and enhanced amygdala reactivity [43, 44]. Therefore, effectively mitigating this hyperarousal can, in itself, confer secondary emotional benefits [45, 46]. The present combined regimen, by reinforcing sedation and sleep promotion, likely contributes directly to reducing central and peripheral arousal levels, thereby establishing a necessary physiological foundation for subsequent emotional improvement. Second, the restoration of sleep continuity and architecture holds direct and independent value for emotional health beyond mere sedation. Neuroimaging and physiological studies confirm that sleep fragmentation specifically impairs the “top-down” regulatory function of the prefrontal cortex over limbic system emotions, such as those processed by the amygdala. This leads to increased emotional reactivity, diminished emotion regulation capacity, and impaired fear memory extinction [47, 48]. The regimen employed here significantly enhanced sleep efficiency and reduced nocturnal awakenings. This consolidation of sleep aids in restoring normal sleep-wake rhythms and related neuroendocrine fluctuations (e.g., cortisol rhythm), thereby strengthening daytime emotional stability and cognitive coping abilities. This process holds the potential to disrupt the vicious cycle wherein insomnia exacerbates emotional dysregulation, which in turn further disrupts sleep. On a pharmacological level, propofol and dexmedetomidine promote emotional stability through complementary pathways, either directly or indirectly. Dexmedetomidine, as a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, exerts a key action at the locus coeruleus. By inhibiting the excessive firing of noradrenergic neurons in this nucleus, it effectively reduces central sympathetic tone and overall arousal drive, thereby producing distinct anxiolytic and calming effects [49]. Propofol primarily exerts its rapid sedative-hypnotic action by potentiating GABAA receptor-mediated central inhibitory neurotransmission [50]. Notably, extending beyond their traditional sedative roles, emerging evidence indicates that both agents possess certain neuroprotective and immunomodulatory properties. For instance, they can mitigate oxidative stress damage (e.g., by increasing superoxide dismutase activity and reducing lipid peroxidation) and suppress the release of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) within the central nervous system [51, 52]. Given that neuroinflammation and oxidative stress have been widely recognized as core, shared pathological mechanisms linking chronic insomnia, anxiety, and depression—mechanisms that can impair neuroplasticity and affect monoaminergic neurotransmitter function [53, 54]—this modulatory effect of the drugs may provide a crucial molecular-level explanation for the improvement of emotional symptoms [53, 54]. In summary, the combined application of propofol and dexmedetomidine may constitute a multi-target interventional strategy: it rapidly reduces hyperarousal via pharmacological actions, repairs the neural substrates of emotional regulation by promoting physiological sleep, and may directly modulate the shared pathophysiological background of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. This provides an integrated mechanistic framework to explain the synchronous improvement in sleep and emotional metrics observed in this study. Future translational research, particularly investigations targeting specific neural circuits and molecular pathways, is needed to further elucidate the interrelationships and relative contributions of these mechanisms.

Limitations and future directions

This case report is subject to several inherent limitations. First, as a single-case study of a 49-year-old female patient, the findings may not be readily generalizable to broader or more diverse populations. The lack of a control group precludes definitive attribution of the observed clinical improvements to Bionic Sleep Therapy itself. Furthermore, the concurrent use of conventional therapies and adjustments to medication types and dosages during the treatment period represent potential confounding factors. Second, the current body of evidence for this therapy is primarily based on Chinese clinical studies. Validation through international multicenter collaborations involving more diverse ethnic populations is therefore necessary. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short, limiting the ability to assess the long-term sustainability of the therapeutic effects. Fourth, the optimal parameters for Bionic Sleep Therapy—such as dosage, duration, and frequency—remain undefined. How adjustments to these parameters influence outcomes warrants exploration. Finally, treatment efficacy was predominantly evaluated based on patient-reported sleep diaries. Incorporating more objective metrics and monitoring tools in the future would allow for a more comprehensive assessment. Consequently, future research should prioritize conducting larger-scale, controlled clinical trials with extended follow-up periods to address these limitations and further elucidate the role of Bionic Sleep Therapy in the management of refractory insomnia.

Conclusion

This case provides preliminary evidence suggesting that Bionic Sleep Therapy, utilizing a combined propofol-dexmedetomidine regimen, may represent a promising short-term bridging intervention for refractory chronic insomnia, with potential benefits extending to both sleep continuity and emotional well-being. Further controlled studies are warranted to validate its efficacy and safety.
