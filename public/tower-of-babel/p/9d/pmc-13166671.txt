Massive jejunal hemorrhage secondary to Dieulafoy’s lesion in a young male with congenital urogenital anomalies: a rare case report
Authors: Shaheen Abshar, Azizullah Mohammadi, Abdulwaly Jalal, Aida Bidar, Mikdolina Seddiqi, Mansoor Ahmad Faizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Dieulafoy’s lesion (DL) is a rare but potentially life-threatening vascular anomaly that can cause severe gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding. While it most commonly occurs in the stomach, jejunal involvement is exceedingly rare and poses significant diagnostic challenges.Presentation of case:We report a 25-year-old male with a history of congenital urogenital anomalies who presented with melena and hematochezia for 7 days. Initial endoscopic and imaging studies failed to identify the bleeding source. Owing to hemodynamic instability and diagnostic limitations, the patient underwent emergency exploratory laparotomy, which revealed a massively hemorrhagic jejunal DL. Segmental jejunal resection with primary anastomosis resulted in complete recovery.Clinical discussion:DL accounts for 1%–2% of GI hemorrhages and is often associated with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs use and comorbidities. Diagnosis of jejunal DL is challenging due to its rarity and inaccessibility via routine endoscopy. While endoscopic and angiographic interventions are preferred, surgical management remains definitive in unstable patients or when noninvasive techniques fail.Conclusion:Jejunal DL should be considered in unexplained GI bleeding when conventional diagnostics are inconclusive. Early multidisciplinary management and timely surgical intervention can significantly improve outcomes.

Introduction:

Dieulafoy’s lesion (DL) represents a dilated and tortuous submucosal artery that erodes the overlying mucosa in the absence of any significant inflammatory or ulcerative process[1]. This aberrant artery, which unusually extends close to the mucosal surface, typically measures 1–3 mm in diameter – approximately 10 times larger than a normal mucosal capillary[2]. The small size and concealed nature of DL often render its endoscopic identification challenging, posing a notable diagnostic difficulty. Patients with DL frequently have underlying comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, or a history of alcohol use[3]. Moreover, the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) has been associated with DL-related bleeding, as these agents may induce mucosal atrophy and ischemic damage[1]. Similarly, in our case, the bleeding episode was temporally related to the use of diclofenac tablets.

Gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding represents a condition associated with considerable morbidity and mortality if not promptly and appropriately managed. The most frequent etiologies include peptic ulcer disease and erosive lesions of the esophagus or duodenum[4]. DL, first described in 1898 by the French surgeon Paul Georges Dieulafoy, accounts for approximately 1%–2% of all cases of GI hemorrhage[5]. Despite being recognized for more than a century, DL remains a relatively uncommon and underreported cause of GI bleeding. Although it can occur throughout the GI tract, the stomach constitutes the most prevalent site, representing about 72% of cases, followed by the duodenum (15%), esophagus (8%), colon (2%), rectum (2%), and, most rarely, the jejunum or ileum, comprising only about 1% of cases[4]. When DL arises in atypical locations, its diagnosis poses substantial challenges and often necessitates a multidisciplinary diagnostic and therapeutic approach.

DL may present without preceding symptoms and is most commonly diagnosed through endoscopic evaluation. In patients presenting with active GI bleeding, hemostatic intervention is frequently required. Such interventions are typically achieved through minimally invasive modalities, including endoscopic or angiographic techniques, while surgical management is rarely necessary. The present case report describes a massively hemorrhagic jejunal DL (JDL) identified intraoperatively in a 25-year-old male who initially presented with melena and clinical signs of hypovolemia. Following diagnostic assessment, the patient underwent emergency laparotomy with partial jejunal resection and primary anastomosis.

This case report has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria[6].

Case presentation

A 25-year-old male presented to the Emergency Department with a chief complaint of melena and bloody stools for 7 days, accompanied by abdominal pain and nausea. He reported recent use of NSAIDs, specifically 75 mg diclofenac tablets three times daily for 4 days. On physical examination, the patient exhibited signs of hypovolemic shock, including mucocutaneous pallor, weak peripheral pulses, delayed capillary refill, and mild drowsiness. Abdominal examination revealed no signs of peritoneal irritation.

The patient had a known history of multiple congenital urogenital anomalies, including bladder exstrophy, scrotal hypospadias, and severe symphyseal diastasis (Fig. 1), for which he had undergone several corrective surgeries. He also had a comorbid seizure disorder. Prior to referral, he had been hospitalized elsewhere due to hematochezia, where both upper and lower GI endoscopies were performed to identify the source of bleeding. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed pangastritis and duodenitis (Fig. 2), whereas colonoscopy demonstrated maroon-colored blood throughout the colon extending to the terminal ileum (Fig. 2), but no definitive bleeding source was detected. Capsule endoscopy was advised to further localize the lesion; however, it could not be performed due to unavailability of the device and financial constraints. To exclude Meckel’s Diverticulum and arteriovenous malformation, contrast-enhanced abdominal CT angiogram was subsequently performed but failed to reveal any active bleeding source. Upon admission, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 90/65 mmHg, pulse rate 130 beats per minute, respiratory rate 20 breaths per minute, and temperature 36.8°C. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated a hemoglobin level of 5.9 g/dL despite having received 13 units of fresh whole blood under a massive transfusion protocol over the previous 7 days. Platelet count was 92 000/µL, blood urea 41.2 mg/dL, and serum creatinine 1.12 mg/dL. Coagulation profile revealed an international normalized ratio of 1.0, prothrombin time of 13 seconds, and activated partial thromboplastin time of 38 seconds. Liver function tests and serum electrolytes were within normal limits except for mild hypokalemia (3.19 mmol/L).

Given the unavailability of selective arterial embolization and enteroscopy, and after multidisciplinary consultation with internal medicine and hematology departments, the patient was managed with intravenous fluid resuscitation and transfusion of two additional units of fresh whole blood. He was then urgently transferred to the operating room for emergency exploratory laparotomy.

After induction of general anesthesia, the abdomen was cleansed with povidone-iodine and draped under aseptic conditions. A laparotomy was then performed with the patient on supine position. Intraoperatively, no intraperitoneal effusion or free blood was identified. On exploration, multiple markedly tortuous, large-caliber vessels were observed approximately 20 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, entering the jejunal wall from the mesenteric border (Fig. 3). The involved segment measured approximately 10 cm in length. Following enterotomy, active bleeding was localized to this region. Approximately 15 cm of the affected jejunum, including the pathological area, was resected followed by a primary end-to-end Gambee hand-sewn anastomosis. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient demonstrated prompt hemodynamic stabilization with complete cessation of bleeding and hematochezia. He received one additional unit of fresh whole blood postoperatively, bringing the total to 16 units transfused since the onset of illness, and achieved a hemoglobin level of 9.8 g/dL. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 with stable vital signs and satisfactory oral intake. At follow-up visits conducted at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively, he remained asymptomatic and well-nourished, with no recurrence of GI bleeding.

Discussion

DLs were first described by Paul Georges Dieulafoy in 1898 as “exulceratio simplex” – a large-caliber submucosal artery lacking the typical characteristics of a gastric ulcer, identified in three of his patients[7]. The term “Dieulafoy’s lesion” was later adopted to describe this vascular anomaly. DLs are reported to occur more frequently in males, with an estimated male-to-female ratio of 2:1[4]. The use of NSAIDs and anticoagulants has also been associated with an increased risk of DL-related bleeding[1].

The precise pathogenesis underlying the rupture of DLs remains incompletely understood. Anatomically, DL consists of an abnormally large submucosal artery that fails to branch normally into the mucosa, maintaining a diameter of 1–3 mm – approximately 10 times larger than a typical mucosal capillary[2]. This vessel may protrude through a small mucosal defect, predisposing it to rupture from minor mechanical trauma such as passage of the food bolus in the stomach or solid stool in colon. Eventually, such erosions can erode into the lumen to cause severe acute GI bleeding[8].

The pathophysiological mechanisms proposed include mucosal ischemia and subsequent erosion secondary to vascular abnormalities. Some authors suggest that oxidative and ischemic stress – triggered by factors such as NSAID use, alcohol consumption, chronic gastritis, or prior surgeries – may contribute to vascular fragility and rupture. A congenital predisposition has also been proposed. Alternatively, arterial thrombosis may lead to localized ischemia and subsequent bleeding. Regardless of the exact mechanism, DLs are more commonly observed in older patients and in those with comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular or renal disease[9].

Endoscopy remains the diagnostic modality of choice for DLs and serves both diagnostic and therapeutic roles. Repeated endoscopic evaluations may be required in cases of intermittent or minimal bleeding. The characteristic endoscopic finding is a single protruding vessel surrounded by otherwise normal mucosa, without ulceration or associated mass[10]. However, JDLs are particularly challenging to diagnose, as standard upper and lower endoscopy rarely visualize the jejunum. In the present case, both esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy failed to localize the bleeding source, demonstrating only pangastritis, duodenitis, and the presence of blood throughout the colon up to the terminal ileum.

Enteroscopy, particularly with single- or double-balloon systems, offers the highest diagnostic yield for distal small bowel lesions (96%–98%)[10]. Nonetheless, its use is limited by the patient’s hemodynamic stability, equipment availability, and institutional expertise. Among imaging techniques, radionuclide scintigraphy is considered the most sensitive for detecting active GI bleeding, capable of identifying bleeding rates as low as 0.1 mL/min[11].

The management of DLs depends on the lesion’s location, the severity of bleeding, and the available expertise. Given the potential for massive hemorrhage and high mortality, prompt hemostatic therapy is crucial. Several therapeutic options exist, including endoscopic hemostasis, angiographic embolization, and surgical resection. Although no universal treatment consensus exists, endoscopic intervention remains the preferred initial approach due to its safety and effectiveness[1]. Surgery is generally reserved for cases where endoscopic and angiographic methods fail or are unavailable. Approximately 3%–16% of DLs ultimately require surgical management[1]. Surgical treatment involves segmental resection of the affected bowel followed by anastomosis, effectively controlling the bleeding and minimizing recurrence risk[12]. Post-resection rebleeding has been reported very low to negligible. However, standardized long-term surveillance protocols have not yet been established.

In previously reported cases, patients with JDLs typically presented with a mean hemoglobin level of 8.4 g/dL, requiring transfusion of (2–9 units of packed red blood cells)[5]. In comparison, our patient had a hemoglobin level of 5.9 g/dL after 7 days of ongoing bleeding despite receiving 13 units of fresh whole blood prior to surgical intervention.

DLs are generally reported among middle-aged to elderly men with comorbid cardiovascular, renal, or metabolic diseases and a history of alcohol consumption[1]. The current case is unusual, involving a young male patient with multiple congenital urogenital anomalies who developed a JDL. To our knowledge, there is no well-established or widely proven direct correlation between JDL and congenital urogenital anomalies in current literature.

The prognosis for patients with JDL largely depends on early diagnosis and the effectiveness of the chosen management strategy. A multidisciplinary approach – integrating gastroenterology, radiology, and surgical expertise – is essential to ensure timely intervention and optimize clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

DLs should be considered in the differential diagnosis of GI bleeding, particularly when conventional endoscopy fails to identify the bleeding source. Diagnosis remains challenging, especially when the lesion is located at atypical sites such as the small intestine or colon. In emergency settings where advanced diagnostic modalities are unavailable due to time or resource limitations, prompt surgical intervention – either open or laparoscopic – may be necessary to achieve definitive hemostasis and complete remission.

Given the high mortality associated with delayed or missed diagnose, DLs must be managed urgently and in specialized centers with a multidisciplinary approach. In hemodynamically unstable patients or when noninvasive methods fail, exploratory laparotomy serves as both a diagnostic and therapeutic strategy. Surgical management carries a low risk of rebleeding and remains a life-saving option.

This case of 25-year-old male with a JDL underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by this rare entity and highlights the importance of timely, team-based management in improving patient outcome.
