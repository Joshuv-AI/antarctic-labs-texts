Identification of ferroptosis-related therapeutic targets and causal risk factors in aortic dissection via multi-omics integration
Authors: Shaoyi Peng, Peng Liu, Kaiyuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of aortic dissection (AD) has not been fully clarified. Data from the gene expression omnibus database were analyzed to identify differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) in AD. Key FRGs were selected using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression and validated with receiver operating characteristic analysis. Single-cell RNA sequencing was applied to assess expression patterns in specific cell types. Mendelian randomization was further performed using genome-wide association study summary statistics for 211 gut microbial taxa and 1400 serum metabolites to explore causal associations with AD. A total of 14 differentially expressed FRGs were identified. Among them, 7 genes (CHAC1, CDO1, HILPDA, GSK3B, DPEP1, BRD4, and NUPR1) were recognized as key candidates. GSK3B and BRD4 were significantly downregulated in endothelial cells, while DPEP1 showed reduced expression in fibroblasts. Immune cell analysis indicated an increase in neutrophils and a decrease in NK and dendritic cells in AD tissues. Regulatory network prediction identified 194 microRNAs, 218 long noncoding RNAs, and 61 potential therapeutic drugs targeting the key FRGs. Mendelian randomization analysis suggested causal associations between AD risk and 8 gut microbial taxa as well as 64 serum metabolites. This study identified critical FRGs and their regulatory networks in AD. Potential causal links with microbial and metabolic factors were also uncovered. These findings provide new insights into ferroptosis-mediated mechanisms of AD and suggest promising targets for therapeutic intervention.

1. Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening vascular emergency characterized by an intimal tear that permits blood entry into the medial layer of the aortic wall, forming a false lumen and leading to rapid hemodynamic deterioration and high mortality rates.[1] Despite advances in surgical and endovascular interventions, the prognosis of AD remains poor, and its underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood.[2,3]

Ferroptosis, a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and metabolic imbalance, has recently emerged as a novel pathological process in cardiovascular diseases.[4] Experimental studies suggest that ferroptosis contributes to vascular injury, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation; however, its specific role in the initiation and progression of AD has not been systematically elucidated.[5,6] A key question that remains unresolved is whether ferroptosis acts as a primary driver of AD pathogenesis or represents a secondary consequence of vascular damage. Moreover, the cell type-specific vulnerability to ferroptosis within the aortic wall – particularly vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and immune cells – remains poorly defined.[7]

Intercellular communication mediated by noncoding RNAs adds another layer of complexity. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) regulate gene expression posttranscriptionally and have been shown to participate in cardiovascular pathology.[8] However, how these regulatory networks intersect with ferroptosis-related pathways in AD is largely unexplored. Similarly, the translational potential of ferroptosis-targeted interventions – such as iron chelators, lipid metabolism modulators, and antioxidant strategies – has yet to be rigorously evaluated in the context of AD. Technological advances now offer powerful tools to address these knowledge gaps. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our ability to dissect cellular heterogeneity, enabling the identification of distinct cell populations and their molecular states in diseased tissues.[9,10]

Meanwhile, Mendelian randomization (MR), which leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables, provides a robust framework for inferring causal relationships between modifiable exposures (e.g., metabolites, ferroptosis regulators, or gut microbiota) and disease outcomes.[11–13] Despite their potential, no study has comprehensively applied these approaches to ferroptosis in AD.

Therefore, this study seeks to systematically identify and validate ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) implicated in AD by integrating bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data, regulatory network predictions, and MR analyses (Fig. 1A). By doing so, we aim not only to clarify the mechanistic role of ferroptosis in AD but also to resolve several critical issues: determine whether ferroptosis is a causal driver versus a bystander in AD progression; identify the vascular and immune cell types most susceptible to ferroptotic injury; delineate how noncoding RNAs orchestrate ferroptosis-related regulatory networks in AD; and evaluate whether MR approaches can establish causal links between ferroptosis-related metabolites and AD risk. Addressing these aims will advance our mechanistic understanding and open new avenues for therapeutic targeting in AD (Fig. 1B).

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Identification and correlation analysis of key genes in AD

This study retrieved gene expression datasets related to AD from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, accessed on May 18, 2024) using the keyword “aortic dissection.” Datasets were included if they met the following criteria: whole-genome mRNA microarray data available; samples derived from human aortic tissue of patients with AD and healthy controls; and species limited to Homo sapiens. Two datasets (GSE84274 and GSE190635) were selected. Probe annotation was conducted using corresponding platform files. scRNA-seq data (GSE224559) were also downloaded.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “limma” package in R (version 4.2.1), with thresholds of P < .05 and |log₂ FC| > 0.5. FRGs were obtained from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/). DEGs overlapping with FRGs were considered candidate FRGs. Heatmaps and correlation plots were generated using the “heatmap” and “corrplot” R packages.

2.2. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of genes

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package. Based on the hypergeometric distribution, enrichment was performed for 3 GO categories: molecular function, cellular component, and biological process.[14,15]

2.3. Screening of key FRGs using LASSO regression

To identify key FRGs, the “glmnet” R package was employed to construct a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression model. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to select the optimal penalty parameter (λ) that minimized the mean squared error. This regularization approach penalizes less relevant variables by shrinking their coefficients toward zero, thus reducing model complexity and improving interpretability.

2.4. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis

The “pROC” R package was used to generate ROC curves and calculate the area under the curve to assess the diagnostic accuracy and stability of the selected FRGs.

2.5. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of genes

GSEA was performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package to evaluate the biological pathways associated with the expression of key FRGs. The “ggplot2” and “enrichplot” packages were used to visualize the enrichment results.[14]

2.6. Prediction of potential therapeutic drugs using drug–gene interaction database (DGIdb)

Potential drug–gene interactions were identified using the DGIdb. Cytoscape software was used to construct a drug–target interaction network based on the predicted results.

2.7. Validation of results

An independent dataset (GSE190635) was analyzed to validate the expression patterns of the 7 key FRGs using the same screening criteria as applied to GSE84274.

2.8. Prediction of gene-miRNA interactions and lncRNAs

miRNA–mRNA interactions were predicted using TargetScan, miRDB, and miRanda. Only miRNAs identified by all 3 tools were considered for downstream analysis.[16,17] These miRNAs were then submitted to the StarBase 2.0 database to predict interacting lncRNAs, applying a filtering threshold of >5. A competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network involving lncRNAs, miRNAs, and FRGs was constructed in cytoscape.

2.9. ScRNA-seq quality control and analyses

The “Seurat” R package was used for quality control and clustering analysis of the single-cell dataset (GSE224559). Low-quality cells were filtered based on gene count and sequencing depth. Highly variable genes (n = 1500) were selected, and dimensionality reduction was conducted via principal component analysis. Louvain clustering was performed, and marker genes were identified for each cluster. Cell types were annotated based on literature, the CellMarker database, and the “SingleR” package.

2.10. MR study

MR analysis was conducted to assess causal relationships between exposures (gut microbiota and serum metabolites) and AD. Three core assumptions were satisfied: the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are significantly associated with the exposure; SNPs are not associated with confounders; and SNPs influence the outcome solely through the exposure.

2.11. Data sources of AD

The dataset for AD (finn-b-I9_AORTDIS, 470 cases and 2,06,541 controls) was obtained from the Finnish database (Table 1). The data included in this study were obtained from publicly available data sources and therefore did not require ethical approval.

2.12. Data sources of 211 gut microbiota and genetic instrumental variables

Gut microbiota data were sourced from the MiBioGen consortium, including 211 traits across multiple taxonomic levels. Due to limited SNPs with P < 1 × 10⁻⁸, SNPs with P < 1 × 10⁻⁵ were used as instrumental variables for MR.[18] This screening strategy is designed to ensure the accuracy of the analysis and the scientific validity of the results (Table 1).

2.13. Data sources of serum metabolites and genetic instrumental variables

Data on 1091 metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios were obtained from genome-wide association study catalog, numbers ECST90199621-ECST90201020, including 8299 individuals from the Canadian Longitudinal Study of Aging cohort.[19] Undertaking a genome-wide association study of metabolite levels identified 1509 associations for 647 metabolites that passed a stringent Bonferroni correction, adjusted for the total number of tested metabolites (P < 5 × 10−8/1091 = 4.58 × 10−11; Table 1).

2.14. MR analysis and sensitivity analysis

The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method is utilized as the primary estimation technique to evaluate the causal relationship between exposure and outcome, with the results presented as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) to estimate the causal effect of exposure on the outcome. Heterogeneity was assessed using the P-value of the Cochran Q-test. Pleiotropy test was used to verify the reliability of the MR analysis results, which was expressed using the intercept term of the MR-Egger method, with an intercept term of P > .05 indicating the absence of horizontal pleiotropy.

3. Results

3.1. Identification of differentially expressed FRGs

After merging and organizing the GSE84274 dataset, a total of 401 FRGs were screened (Table S1, Supplemental digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q805). Among these, 14 FRGs exhibited differential expression. Specifically, CDO1, HILPDA, GSK3B, SMG9, DPEP1, NUPR1, and KDM4A were downregulated compared to the control group, while CHAC1, NOX1, TP53, ANO6, MTCH1, SRC, and BRD4 were upregulated (Fig. 1C). Correlation analysis was performed on these 14 FRGs, resulting in a correlation matrix diagram. As shown in Figure 1D, each square in the correlation matrix represents the correlation between the expressions of 2 FRGs. Red and blue indicate positive and negative correlations, respectively. The correlation coefficient is shown in the lower-left area of the matrix, while its significance is shown in the upper-right area.

3.2. Functions and pathways of FRGs

To further explore the primary molecular functions and signaling pathways of these 14 FRGs, this study conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. GO enrichment analysis revealed that FRGs were enriched in various GO terms, including response to P53 binding, cellular response to NADPH oxidase complex, and others (Fig. 1E). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that FRGs were primarily enriched in pathways such as lipid and atherosclerosis, ErbB signaling pathway, chemokine signaling pathway and others (Fig. 1F).

3.3. Identification of 7 key FRGs and signaling pathways identified by GSEA

Through LASSO regression 7 key FRGs were screened out: CHAC1, CDO1, HILPDA, GSK3B, DPEP1, BRD4, and NUPR1 (Fig. 2A, B; Table 2). Subsequently, the ROC curve of the model was plotted, and the area under the curve values of all 7 FRGs were 1, indicating high diagnostic accuracy and reliability of these genes (Fig. 2C). GSEA was performed based on the data of the 7 key FRGs (Fig. 2D–J).

3.5. Targeted drug prediction

Potential drugs targeting the key genes were predicted through the DGIdb database. These agents may influence AD by regulating the identified FRGs. In total, 61 candidates were identified, including 46 for GSK3B, 3 for DPEP1, and 12 for BRD4 (Fig. 3B).

3.6. Construct ceRNAs for 7 key FRGs

A total of 194 miRNAs predicted to regulate the 7 key FRGs were identified through TargetScan, miRDB, and miRanda. Among them, 27 miRNAs were associated with BRD4, 10 with CDO1, 66 with CHAC1, 3 with DPEP1, 118 with GSK3B, and 6 with NUPR1. In addition, 218 lncRNAs targeting these miRNAs were predicted, and a ceRNA network was subsequently constructed (Fig. 3C).

3.7. Expression of characteristic genes in single-cell sequencing

To improve cell-type identification, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding and uniform manifold approximation and projection analyses categorized AD cells into 14 clusters. DEGs for each cluster were obtained using the “FindAllMarkers” function (log FC = 0.25). Based on 10 subtype-specific DEGs per cluster, 9 major cell types were identified, including neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells, dendritic cells, monocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. The proportions of these cell types were then compared between AD and control tissues. Neutrophils were increased in AD, whereas NK cells and dendritic cells were decreased (Fig. 4A). In the single-cell datasets, expression of the previously identified FRGs was further examined. GSK3B and BRD4 were downregulated in endothelial cells, and DPEP1 was reduced in fibroblasts compared with controls (Fig. 4B).

3.8. Causal relationship between gut microbiota and AD

The IVW analysis indicated that 4 gut microbiota, including genus Anaerostipes.id.1991 (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16–0.79, P = .01), were negatively associated with AD risk. In contrast, class Melainabacteria.id.1589 (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.02–2.37, P = .03) showed a positive association with AD risk (Fig. 5A). Multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.

Results of Mendelian randomization. (A) Causal relationship between 211 gut microbiota and aortic dissection. (B) Causal relationship between 1400 serum metabolites and aortic dissection.

3.9. Causal relationship between serum metabolites and AD

The IVW analysis identified 36 serum metabolites, including carnitine C5:1 (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.37–0.73, P = .001), that were negatively associated with AD risk (Fig. 5B). In contrast, 36 metabolites, including sebacate (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.32, P = .04), showed positive associations with AD risk. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these results.

4. Discussion

For the first time, a potential link between ferroptosis and AD was revealed through bioinformatics analysis. This finding may provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms of AD. Seven FRGs – CHAC1, CDO1, HILPDA, GSK3B, DPEP1, BRD4, and NUPR1 – were identified with significantly altered expression in AD, suggesting crucial roles in disease progression. Notably, GSK3B and BRD4 were downregulated in AD, indicating that these genes may contribute to pathogenesis by regulating oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. These observations are consistent with recent studies demonstrating that ferroptosis is critical in cardiovascular pathology, particularly through the regulation of endothelial and smooth muscle cell stability.[20–23]

The single-cell transcriptome analysis revealed significant alterations in immune cell populations, suggesting that cellular immune responses play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of AD.[24,25] Neutrophils, one of the first responders to inflammation, were markedly increased, reflecting an acute inflammatory reaction in AD.[26,27] Previous studies have demonstrated that neutrophil activation and aggregation are associated with arterial intimal injury and thrombosis.[28] Thus, the increase in neutrophils may represent a response to the intimal tear, further exacerbating tissue damage and inflammation through the release of enzymes and reactive oxygen species. In contrast, reductions in NK cells and dendritic cells may impair immune regulation, decreasing the ability to control inflammation and tissue damage. Such dysfunction has been linked to persistent inflammation and the progression of atherosclerosis.[29,30] Therefore, their reduction in AD may contribute to decreased immune surveillance and insufficient clearance of abnormal cells, aggravating lesion development.

Analysis of FRGs in single-cell datasets revealed that GSK3B and BRD4 were downregulated in endothelial cells, while DPEP1 expression was decreased in fibroblasts. Given the role of fibroblasts in maintaining arterial wall integrity, these changes may reflect cell-type-specific mechanisms by which ferroptosis contributes to AD.

In addition, the prediction of miRNA, lncRNA, and drug targets provided potential avenues for therapeutic development.[31] A total of 61 candidate drugs, together with predicted regulatory noncoding RNAs, may offer novel strategies for targeting ferroptosis-related pathways. For example, drugs targeting GSK3B and BRD4 may not only modulate ferroptosis but also improve cellular stress responses and inflammatory states, thereby slowing AD progression.

Furthermore, MR analysis indicated potential causal associations between gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and AD risk, underscoring the importance of environmental and metabolic factors beyond genetic predisposition. Alterations in these pathways may influence vascular structure and function, thereby contributing to AD development.[32,33] Notably, protective gut microbiota negatively associated with AD risk may regulate immune and metabolic processes, while detrimental alterations could promote cardiovascular pathology.

5. Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms of AD through the identification of FRGs and their functional roles. Seven key FRGs (CHAC1, CDO1, HILPDA, GSK3B, DPEP1, BRD4, and NUPR1) with significantly altered expression in AD were identified, suggesting their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Single-cell transcriptome analysis revealed immune cell heterogeneity in AD tissues, highlighting the involvement of neutrophils, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells in disease pathogenesis. The cell type-specific expression of FRGs further supports their contribution to vascular dysfunction. In addition, MR analysis indicated potential causal relationships between gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and AD risk, underscoring the importance of metabolic and environmental factors alongside genetic influences. Together, these findings propose novel therapeutic strategies targeting the ferroptosis pathway and establish a foundation for future studies aimed at validating these targets and developing effective treatments for AD.
