Evaluation of ChatGPT’s ability to detect intraosseous pathologies with panoramic radiographs
Authors: Gamze Şirin Sarıbal, Yusuf Nuri Kaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of GPT-4 V and GPT-5 in detecting intraosseous jaw lesions on panoramic radiographs and to assess the readability of their responses.Methods119 biopsy-confirmed panoramic radiographs were retrospectively analyzed. Each image was presented to both models with four standardized diagnostic questions. Unguided and region-guided prompting were compared. Information accuracy, relevance, actionability, quality, and readability (Flesch Reading Ease Score and Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level) were recorded.ResultsUnguided lesion detection sensitivity was low. With location guidance, lesion identification increased to 73.9% for GPT-4 V and 78.1% for GPT-5; however, diagnostic sensitivity remained limited (31.1% and 36.1%). Information accuracy, relevance, actionability, and quality scores were mostly concentrated in the lower-to-middle range (scores 2–3). No statistically significant differences were found between the models (p > 0.05). Readability analysis showed high textual complexity (mean FKGL 12.8 and 12.7).ConclusionGPT-4 V and GPT-5 did not achieve clinically acceptable diagnostic sensitivity on panoramic radiographs. Further model refinement is required before diagnostic use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07913-7.Keywords: Vision-language model, Jaw radiolucency, Differential diagnosis, Clinical decision support safety, Patient-facing readability

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI)-powered chatbots are applications that can communicate with humans via natural language through text, voice, and visual modalities. These systems have the capacity to provide real-time solutions, including creative processes and problem solving [1, 2]. Introduced in 2022 by OpenAI as a chatbot-based AI model, the Chatbot Generative Pretrained Transformer (ChatGPT) has become widely used in various applications. The visual input version ChatGPT-4 Vision (GPT-4 V) was officially announced by OpenAI in September 2023 [3], and ChatGPT-5 (GPT-5) was publicly released on August 7, 2025 [4]. The ability of these systems to process visual data also has potential for the combined assessment of textual clinical information and visual data in clinical radiology applications [5]. The high volume, structural diversity, and analytical complexity of data encountered in radiology make the use of such tools a growing trend. Systems such as ChatGPT can provide opportunities for radiologists in developing decision-support mechanisms, standardizing and optimizing radiological reports, supporting student training, and facilitating patient-centered communication. Patients may also serve as easily accessible sources of information [6, 7].

The use of AI systems by patients and institutions highlights the need for strong control and management to guide this transformation in medical processes. Therefore, maintaining human oversight in academic and clinical applications is of critical importance [8]. As the use of these tools has expanded, attention has shifted toward the accuracy, interpretability, and readability of the information they provide, as many individuals now use them directly to obtain health information. Most patients stated that they are not afraid to use AI to obtain health information [9]. These systems can be used in various critical fields, such as cancer, radiology, and preventive medicine. Therefore, the accuracy of the information presented to the public is highly important. This can be determined by clinicians evaluating these responses [10]. Generally, optimistic comments have been made in research conducted in various fields on this topic. However, it has been emphasized that this optimism is cautious in complex situations such as diagnosis. In several areas, such as endocrinology and metabolic diseases, the results contradict this optimistic interpretation [9]. In studies evaluating the radiological image interpretation ability of the ChatGPT, it was predicted that the model was not yet capable of reliably interpreting such images [11]. However, in a study evaluating histologically, high detection rates were achieved [12].

Just as important as the sensitivity of the responses obtained from AI applications is that they are understandable by patients of all backgrounds. It has been reported that the response of ChatGPT’s in the field of dentistry show higher empathy and understanding [13]. However, there are conflicting results in terms of readability and quality. While some studies report high information quality, inconsistencies in readability and comprehensibility levels have been reported, and no consensus has been reached [14, 15].

This study primarily aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of GPT-4 V and GPT-5 in detecting intraosseous jaw pathologies on panoramic radiographs. Additionally, the study aimed to assess the readability, relevance, and informative quality of the responses generated by these models.

Methods

This study is a retrospective investigation, conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Pamukkale University Ethics Committee. All the data were anonymized, and the personal information of the individuals was protected. All steps described in the methodology were performed jointly by a maxillofacial radiologist and a maxillofacial surgeon, both with at least 4 years of experience, actively working as academics and clinicians.

Data collection

Panoramic radiographs were retrospectively selected from the digital archive of the Faculty of Dentistry Bursa Uludağ University for the period of 2018–2025. The study group consisted of 119 panoramic radiographs obtained from 62 female and 57 male patients, with an age range of 19–68 years. All radiographs contained intraosseous pathologies that had also been detected histopathologically and were obtained via a Veraview IC5 HD panoramic device (J.Morita Mfg.Corp.) at 60–70 kVp and 17.5 mA. Images obtained prior to surgical or endodontic intervention were used. Each panoramic image was exported in TIFF format at a maximum resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. This resolution was chosen to ensure consistency across all inputs while remaining within the processing limits of the models’ vision capabilities.

ChatGPT interpretation process

All radiographic evaluations were performed between September 1and September 30, 2025. All analyses were performed via the GPT-4 V and GPT-5 (preview/stable) versions available via the web interface. To ensure a standardized testing environment and prevent bias from previous interactions, evaluations were conducted through a newly created account. To ensure the highest level of deterministic response and minimize variability, the temperature parameter was kept at its default (0.0 equivalent in interface), and no system-level persona instructions were provided prior to the prompts.

A new chat interface was created for each prompt to minimize the influence of previous responses, and responses were documented for later analysis. Each panoramic image was uploaded to the models, and the following questions were used. The models did not refuse to respond to any queries due to diagnostic questions, which generated responses for all the images. The prompt was designed by considering the conceptual themes reported in previously published studies [11, 16, 17]. These themes were structured according to routine clinical evaluation steps, including the presence and location of the lesion, a preliminary radiographic diagnosis, and potential malignancy risk. The diagnostic interaction followed a standardized, conditional four-step sequential protocol for each radiograph. First, the model was asked to detect the lesion (Prompt 1). If the model failed to detect the lesion, it was then provided with the specific region (region-guided) and asked to evaluate the area (Prompt 2). Regardless of the detection outcome in the first two steps, the interaction proceeded to Prompt 3 for the characterization of the lesion’s features, followed by Prompt 4 for the differential diagnosis. This sequential approach ensured that the model’s ability to characterize and diagnose was tested even in cases where initial autonomous detection failed. One week after the initial analysis, the prompt was repeated for each radiograph.

A Likert-type scale [20] ranging from 1 to 5 was used for the evaluation. To ensure objectivity and reproducibility in expert scoring, specific operational criteria were established for the 5-point Likert scales. These criteria distinguish between scores based on the depth of radiological description and clinical guidance provided. The complete scoring rubric is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Very low/not at all appropriate (1): The response is completely incorrect or irrelevant to the question. It does not demonstrate sufficient understanding or knowledge of the subject.

Low (2): The response contains some correct information about the topic but is largely inaccurate and/or includes substantial omissions.

Moderate/partially appropriate (3): The response is approximately half correct, but the other half contains major errors and/or significant omissions.

High (4): The response contains largely accurate information but also includes errors and/or omissions. While not entirely incorrect, the answer is not sufficiently accurate or complete to be considered reliable or appropriate.

Very high/fully appropriate (5): The response is completely correct and demonstrates a thorough and accurate understanding of the subject. All key elements are addressed accurately and comprehensively.

Quality: Quality was assessed via the Global Quality Scale (GQS) [21], which is a widely used tool for classifying health information into five categories on the basis of quality, flow, and usefulness for patients. A score of 1 indicates that the response is of low quality, poorly organized, contains incomplete information, and is not useful at all, whereas a score of 5 indicates that the response is of excellent quality and flow and is highly useful for patients

This is a commonly used test to indicate how difficult an English text is to understand. The score ranges 0–100, with higher scores indicating that the text is easier to read and understand

Each evaluator scored according to a 5-point Likert scale for information accuracy, relevance, actionability, and quality for all cases. All disagreements were resolved by consensus. Expert assessments were used as the reference standard. According to the scoring system, a score of 5 on the five-point Likert scale represented a completely correct, relevant, and clinically appropriate response as determined by the expert evaluators.

The minimum required sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University) for a two-tailed McNemar test with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.95. The analysis was designed to compare paired outcomes obtained from the same panoramic radiographs before and after providing lesion location guidance. Under these assumptions, approximately 100 paired observations were estimated to be sufficient to achieve the desired statistical power. However, to further increase the statistical power and reliability of the results, all eligible cases available in the archive (n = 119) were included.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 20.0(IBM Corp.). Interobserver agreement was evaluated using weighted kappa for ordinal variables and Cohen’s kappa for binary assessments.

In the statistical analysis, primary evaluation metrics (diagnostic accuracy and localization) were formally compared between models, while secondary metrics such as relevance and actionability were treated descriptively to avoid potential issues related to multiple comparisons. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality and, it was determined that it did not show a normal distribution. The diagnostic performance of the models was evaluated based on lesion detection sensitivity. The McNemar test was used to compare binary categorical data between the two models and to evaluate the impact of region-guided vs. unguided prompts within each model. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which is appropriate for matched data, was used to compare the models’ Likert scale scores (information accuracy, relevance, actionability, and quality). A paired t-test was used to compare the readability scores (FKGL and FRES) of the models. As the dataset consists exclusively of lesions confirmed by biopsy and does not include a negative control group (healthy cases), specificity and false positive rates cannot be estimated. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Regarding the evaluation of interobserver agreement, weighted kappa values for ordinal variables were 0.991 for information accuracy, 0.995 for relevance, 0.997 for actionability, and 0.994 for GQS. For the binary assessments, Cohen’s kappa was 0.982. Initially, the two observers evaluated the images independently. Disagreements occurred in 11 cases (approximately 9%), primarily regarding Likert scores. These cases were resolved through a consensus meeting where the experts reviewed the images together, leading to the final reported Kappa values (> 0.99). Intra-observer consistency, assessed by repeating the measurements one week later, showed weighted kappa values ranging from 0.985 to 0.993. According to the Landis and Koch classification [23], these values indicate almost perfect agreement.

A total of 119 panoramic radiographs from 62 female and 57 male patients aged 19–68 years were included in the study. The mean age was 42.6 ± 11.3 years. 101 lesions (84.9%) were in the mandible, and 18 (15.1%) were in the maxilla. Lesions were distributed as 24.1% in the anterior region and 75.9% in the posterior region. Histopathological diagnoses are as follows: 38 (31.9%) radicular cysts, 21 (17.6%) residual cysts, 26 (21.8%) dentigerous cysts, 19 (16.0%) ameloblastomas and 15 (12.6%) odontogenic keratocyst.

The sensitivity for lesion detection using unguided prompts was low for both GPT-4 V (24.4%) and GPT-5 (28.6%), with no significant difference observed between the models (p = 0.542). However, the implementation of region-guided prompting led to a significant increase in detection sensitivity for both models, reaching 73.9% for GPT-4 V and 78.2% for GPT-5 (p < 0.00 for both). There was no statistically significant difference in detection performance between ChatGPT-4 V and ChatGPT-5 in the guided condition (p = 0.542). (Table 1)

Discussion

Systematic reviews on the role of ChatGPT in radiology highlight its potential in workflow and report standardization while emphasizing the necessity of clinical validation and field-specific training [7]. This study evaluated the ability of ChatGPT vision models to interpret two-dimensional dental panoramic radiographs containing pathologies in the mandible and maxilla. The aim was to assess whether the model could serve as a reliable decision-support tool for clinicians and provide accurate, comprehensible information for patients. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the models, GPT-5 yielded higher values across most parameters. In the unguided lesion detection task, both models demonstrated low sensitivity. This query type was planned under the assumption that lesions are generally unable to be described in clinical terms, unlike healthcare professionals do. With guided prompts referring to specific regions such as the mandibular ramus and maxillary anterior areas, lesion detection rates increased.

However, it is remarkable that the success rate of lesion localisation in our study is significantly higher than diagnostic accuracy. This situation demonstrates that, although the models can detect density changes and anomalies in radiographs, they cannot synthesise the radiological details (border characteristics, internal structure, and cortical bone changes) that are critical in the differential diagnosis of complex lesions such as ameloblastoma or odontogenic keratocyst. Current models do not yet possess the radiological depth of insight required to distinguish these specific pathological signs in panoramic films at an expert level.

Studies have investigated the diagnostic potential of large language models in dental radiology using various imaging modalities and prompting strategies. Kahalian et al. [24] evaluated ChatGPT-4.0 in interpreting 52 oral and maxillofacial radiographs and reported a true prediagnosis rate of 30.7% without guidance, which increased to 56.9% when guided prompts were provided. These results are consistent with our findings, where both models showed higher detection performance when guided questions were used. Silva et al. [16] assessed a ChatGPT-based system for describing radiolucent jaw lesions on panoramic radiographs and ranking differential diagnoses. Their model achieved the highest performance for margin and bone involvement parameters but lower scores for internal structure and shape. Similarly, in this study, both models demonstrated limited diagnostic sensitivity despite satisfactory lesion detection. Kim and Kim [17] compared ChatGPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro using multimodal inputs from panoramic, CT, and histopathological images and reported diagnostic sensitivity of 82% and 63%, respectively. Although their study used more complex image data, the diagnostic trend resembled our results, with model performance varying according to input type and prompt specificity. Finally, Aşar et al. [25] compared a customized ChatGPT-4 V, standard ChatGPT-4 V, and ChatGPT-4o for detecting supernumerary teeth on periapical radiographs and reported the highest accuracy (91%) for the customized version. The greater performance in that study may be related to the use of periapical radiographs, which provide clearer, localized visualization than panoramic images do.

ChatGPT models have been reported to achieve high accuracy rates in text-based queries [26, 27]. However, our findings indicate that this success does not extend to visual diagnostic analyses. Hassona et al. [28] assessed ChatGPT-3.5 for oral cancer–related patient questions and reported moderate quality but limited actionability, indicating that responses were understandable yet lacked clinical clarity. Helvacioglu-Yigit et al. [29] compared ChatGPT-4 and Gemini in implant-surgery contexts and reported good accuracy and relevance but frequent contextual errors. Özcivelek and Özcan [30] reported similar patterns when comparing multiple chatbots for prosthesis-related inquiries, with the highest scores for understandability and the lowest for actionability. Halboub et al. [31] noted that ChatGPT-4 provided the most understandable answers among other models but remained weak in clinical applicability. Collectively, these studies highlight a consistent pattern: AI models can generate coherent and contextually appropriate text, yet they often fail to provide reliable, actionable, and diagnostically precise information. Also, it is noteworthy that a common false positive response obtained from the models is the reporting of impacted third molars on radiographs. This tendency limits the reliability of the chatbot in those contexts. In many cases, these teeth were either absent or not impacted. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the detection of impacted third molars was genuine or coincidental.

In our study, for relevance, responses captured the intent of the prompt but often missed specific lesion characteristics. It is thought that current models cannot synthesise pathological findings in radiological images (margin irregularities, radiopacity levels, etc.) with clinical protocols and cannot provide an explainable argument. Superpositions and technical variations in panoramic radiographs may also negatively affect the diagnostic reliability of the models. In terms of actionability, some statements were clinically oriented but lacked adequate guidance or showed misleading certainty. Despite their low diagnostic accuracy, AI models that provide persuasive clinical recommendations create serious safety risks in clinical use. Therefore, formal safety mechanisms and “ethical firewall” are required to control the decisions of these models and seek expert opinion when necessary [32].

In terms of quality, responses were structured and clear but uneven in terms of completeness and accuracy. Our findings are consistent with the general literature. Russe et al. [33] evaluated the GPT-4 model with guideline-based contextual prompts and achieved 100% accuracy and high explanation quality without any additional training. Compared with our results, its higher performance is likely due to the use of structured, standardized guideline content, whereas our evaluation involved open-ended and case-specific radiographic scenarios. The QOS scale used is considered to provide a standard measure of information quality and has been used in similar studies [34]. However, as it was developed for the purpose of evaluating patient-centred web resources, its ability to fully reflect the technical depth of expert-level radiological interpretations may be limited.

Recent research in dental and maxillofacial radiology has shown that AI-generated texts remain challenging to read for nonexpert audiences. Hassona et al. [28] and Helvacioglu-Yigit et al. [29] evaluated ChatGPT models for oral cancer information and reported low readability (FKGL ≈ 12–14; FRES ≈ 30–38). Halboub et al. [31] similarly ChatGPT-4 responses to oral cancer questions were understandable yet linguistically complex and above the typical patient comprehension level. Stephan et al. [35] examined radiology report summaries produced by ChatGPT and observed that while sentence organization improved, readability remained demanding (FKGL ≃11). In a later study, Stephan et al. [36] achieved better readability after iterative text refinement (FKGL ≈ 9; FRES ≈ 46). In our study, models produced mean FKGL scores of 12.8 and FRES values of approximately 31–32, consistent with previous findings showing that although structurally coherent, AI-generated radiological content remains above the optimal readability range for public use. The readability scores obtained in this study (FKGL ~ 12.7) reflect the English text outputs provided by the models. However, a high readability level does not imply that the information is clinically accurate. Considering the low accuracy rates of 31–36%, the models may produce information that is easy to read but incorrect. This situation carries the risk of misleading users.

Ultimately, the fundamental criterion for clinical acceptability is that an AI system must be statistically comparable to the diagnostic success of a specialist radiologist (the gold standard). It must also minimise the risk of missing pathologies (false negatives). Current findings indicate that the models are not yet suitable for use as an independent diagnostic tool. However, they could serve as interactive educational material for dental students to recognise radiographic anatomy and identify pathological variations. In clinical practice, they could be preferred as a pre-screening aid to reduce the workload of radiologists. However, this would only be possible if low false negative rates were guaranteed.

There are some limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. First of all, is the exclusion of malignant cases and a healthy control group from the dataset. Since all evaluated radiographs consisted of biopsy-confirmed benign lesions, standard diagnostic specificity analyses for differentiate healthy-pathological states were not performed. Instead, the models’ performance was assessed as benign-set malignancy suspicion behavior. This approach analyzed the models’ tendency to incorrectly flag benign pathologies as suspicious for malignancy (false positive suspicion), providing a specialized measure of model cautiousness within a confirmed pathological dataset. Future studies are required to completely evaluate the ability of models to accurately distinguish healthy anatomical structures from jaw pathologies. Radiographs without pathology and containing malignant lesions should be preferred in these studies. Also, the models occasionally showed inconsistencies in some anatomical localisations. Although the general distribution of lesions (maxilla and mandible) was recorded, the models sometimes struggled to clearly distinguish lesion boundaries in the maxilla due to complex anatomical superpositions specific to panoramic radiographs (e.g., maxillary sinus and zygomatic arch). In contrast, the mandible’s clearer cortical boundaries provided more consistent localisation.

In addition, a significant limitation of this study was the inability to perform a robust statistical comparison between specific lesion types during the detection and diagnosis phases. This limitation stems from the fact that our sample size, while sufficient for overall performance metrics, became fragmented when divided into clinical subgroups (e.g., specific cysts vs. tumors). The relatively small number of cases in certain subgroups reduced the statistical power, making it difficult to detect subtle performance differences between GPT-4 V and GPT-5 for all pathologies. Consequently, while descriptive trends were observed across different lesion types, these findings should be interpreted with caution. It is thought that visual assessments with different AI models and a larger amount of data will be necessary in the future.

Conclusions

GPT-4 V and GPT-5 have shown limited sensitivity in detecting intraosseous lesions on panoramic radiographs. Although lesion localization performance improved when regional guidance was provided, diagnostic sensitivity remained low. Although GPT-5 scores are slightly higher in most parameters, this difference is not statistically significant. While the texts produced by both models are structurally understandable, their readability levels are still high for patient populations. These findings indicate that further optimization and field-specific developments are necessary for reliable diagnostic use in clinical practice.
