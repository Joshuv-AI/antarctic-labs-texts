Intracellular amyloidosis in peripheral nerve and skeletal muscle
Authors: Grace K Grafham, Gloria Mak, Sandra Grant, Alison Murphy, Steven K Baker, Mark Tarnopolsky, Jian-Qiang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyloidosis is an etiologically heterogeneous group of disorders pathologically characterized by the extracellular deposition of amyloid fibrils, leading to tissue damage. It commonly affects the peripheral nervous system and occasionally involves skeletal muscle. The pathogenic mechanisms driving cellular injury in amyloidosis remain elusive. In this study, we examined 3 sural nerve and 5 skeletal muscle biopsies from patients with amyloidosis to localize the deposition of amyloid fibrils. Histologically, the nerve biopsies showed axonal degeneration and Schwann cell (SC) changes; muscle biopsies demonstrated variable myopathic and neurogenic features with multifocal amyloid deposition, including within intramuscular nerves. Electron microscopy identified both intracellular and extracellular amyloid deposition in all 8 biopsies. The ultrastructural localization of amyloid fibrils included the nerve and muscle extracellular matrix, as well as the SC processes, peri-/sub-sarcolemmal region of non-necrotic myofibers, and endoneurial and endomysial/perimysial blood vessel cells. Notably, SC processes, particularly bands of Büngner, formed amyloid-related complex inclusions in all 3 nerve cases. These findings suggest that intracellular amyloid deposition is common in peripheral nerve and skeletal muscle and may play a significant role in cellular injury and degeneration, and ultimately the progression of neuropathy and myopathy.Keywords: amyloid fibril, amyloidosis, electron microscopy, intracellular pathology, myopathy, neuropathy, tissue biopsy

INTRODUCTION

Amyloidosis represents a heterogenous group of disorders pathologically characterized by the extracellular deposition of misfolded amyloid proteins. These can accumulate either locally, at the site of protein production or systemically affecting distant tissues and organs.1 The commonly affected structures/organs include the blood vessels, peripheral nerves, heart, gastrointestinal tract, kidney, eyes, and meninges.2 Skeletal muscle, including its microvasculature, is less frequently involved by amyloidosis.3,4 The clinical manifestations of systemic amyloidosis are largely determined by the biochemical nature of the amyloid precursor protein and specific organs involved.1

Within the neuromuscular system, amyloid involvement is most closely associated with immunoglobulin light chain (AL) amyloidosis and hereditary transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis.2,4 Amyloid neuropathy typically presents as a progressive, length-dependent polyneuropathy with small myelinated and non-myelinated fiber involvement.2,5 Pathological findings include axonal degeneration with nerve fiber loss and Schwann cell (SC) changes.6–8 While skeletal muscle involvement by systemic amyloidosis is uncommon, about 25% of amyloid myopathies are isolated, and around 30% are associated with peripheral neuropathy.3,4 Clinically, amyloid myopathy is characterized by proximal muscle weakness, muscle pseudohypertrophy, macroglossia, and in some cases elevated muscle enzyme levels.5 Myopathological features include infrequent necrotic and regenerating myofibers, denervation atrophy, and intramuscular vasculopathy.3,9,10 Amyloid deposition is also observed in the nerve endoneurial vessels.7,11 While amyloid deposition in tissue is the common cause of progressive damage in amyloid myopathy and neuropathy, the underlying pathogenic cellular mechanisms remain elusive and their links to clinical symptoms is still poorly understood.

Interestingly, the formation of intracellular inclusions with amyloid fibrils, a phenomenon well-documented in neurodegenerative diseases, has been rarely reported in systemic amyloidosis involving peripheral nerve and skeletal muscle.1,12 We recently described a case of intracellular amyloidosis with SC inclusions and associated axonal degeneration in a patient with ATTR amyloidosis.12 The present study aimed to further explore intracellular amyloidosis in the peripheral nerve and skeletal muscle to provide new insights into the pathogenic mechanisms underlying amyloid-associated neuromuscular dysfunction.

METHODS

Patient selection

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board. Surgical pathology reports from the Hamilton Regional Laboratory Medicine Program (Hamilton, Ontario, Canada) were retrospectively reviewed between January 2000 and October 2024 for cases of amyloid deposition in peripheral nerve and skeletal muscle biopsies. Among the total cases evaluated (n = 237), amyloidosis was reported in 3 sural nerve and 5 skeletal muscle biopsies. These 8 cases with nerve or muscle amyloidosis were included regardless of etiology or comorbidity given the scope of the study. Electron microscopy (EM) was routinely performed in the muscle and nerve biopsies in our institution. The exclusion criteria for this study were the absence of nerve fascicles, skeletal muscle, or EM examination. We reviewed all microscopic slides and EM images of these 8 cases. Intracellular amyloidosis was identified in all 3 peripheral nerve and 5 skeletal muscle biopsies. Amyloidosis was defined pathologically by the multifocal presence of positive Congo red (CR) staining and/or EM identification of amyloid fibrils within multiple cell types and extracellular matrix.

Clinical data of the patients were extracted from electronic medical records, including patient demographics (age and sex), presenting symptoms, comorbid conditions, relevant genetic testing results, and evidence of systemic amyloid involvement in other organs (Table 1). Additionally, findings from nerve conduction study and needle electromyography were recorded when available. Amyloidosis was classified based on clinical and pathological criteria.13

Histopathological analysis

All biopsy specimens were collected and evaluated following the standard diagnostic protocol of peripheral nerve or skeletal muscle examination for identification of pathological changes.8,14 Sural nerve biopsies were examined in multiple semithin sections with toluidine blue staining, and 7-μm-thick formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections with various histological and immunohistochemical stains.12,15,16 These sections contained nerve fascicles in both transverse and longitudinal orientation. Skeletal muscle biopsies were snap frozen in isopentane pre-cooled with liquid nitrogen to −160°C and stored at −80°C until further processing. Serial 7-μm-thick transverse sections were routinely stained with various histological, histochemical, and immunohistochemical stains. The muscle toluidine blue-stained semithin sections in both transverse and longitudinal orientation were also examined. CR staining was routinely performed in all biopsy specimens. Examination of the nerve or muscle biopsies led to a diagnosis of neuropathy or myopathy with amyloid deposition.

Electron microscopy

EM was performed on all studied cases. For EM, a small portion of the nerve or muscle tissue was fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde, postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate and examined using a JEOL 1230 transmission electron microscope. EM was used to identify ultrastructural changes including amyloid deposition. The exact intracellular or extracellular localization of amyloid deposition was identified only by EM and was specified in 4 regions: extracellular matrix, intra-blood vessel (BV) cell, intra-myofiber, or intra-SC (Table 1).

RESULTS

Table 1 summarizes the clinical and pathological characteristics of all 8 included patients with amyloid deposition in peripheral nerve or skeletal muscle biopsies.

Clinical features

We identified 8 patients (6 males and 2 females) with pathologically confirmed amyloid deposition in the sural nerve (3 patients) or skeletal muscle (5 patients). The patient ages at the time of tissue biopsy ranged from 59 − 79 years. Four patients had hereditary forms of amyloidosis: patient 1 with iatrogenic ATTR following domino liver transplant, patient 2 and 3 with familial ATTR, and patient 6 with AGel amyloidosis. Two patients were diagnosed with light-chain amyloidosis (AL) secondary to Lambda (patient 4) and Kappa (patient 5) light-chain multiple myeloma. The remaining 2 patients (patients 7 and 8) had skeletal muscle deposits of unclassified amyloid type.

Clinical presentations varied with the common symptoms being lower extremity weakness (7/7), paresthesia (7/7), and autonomic disturbances (3/7). Coexisting cardiac amyloidosis was confirmed, or suspected, in 3 patients (2, 4, and 7 in Table 1), 2 of whom were symptomatic at initial presentation. Patient 6 had biopsy-confirmed amyloid deposition in the transverse colon. The remaining 3 patients had isolated amyloid deposition in the nerve (patient 1; Figure 1D-G) or skeletal muscle (patients 5 and 8) without identified amyloidosis in other tissue/organs. In addition, patient 6 was initially diagnosed and treated as Sjögren’s disease due to symptoms of xerostomia, bifacial weakness, and positive rheumatologic serology. Patient 7 had an uncommon presentation with primarily ophthalmologic symptoms suggestive of chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO). Patient 8 was clinically suspected to have sporadic inclusion body myositis (sIBM). Electrodiagnostic studies in 7 patients revealed varying degrees of axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, with occasional myopathic changes (Table 1).

Amyloid detection

Amyloid deposition was detected by positive CR staining of amorphous material deposits with yellow/green birefringence under polarized light in 6 biopsies (2 nerve and 4 muscle) including the microvasculature, and identified by EM in all biopsies (3 nerve and 5 muscle). Two biopsies (1 nerve and 1 muscle) were CR negative, which may be due to small amounts of amyloid deposition (detected on EM in both cases) and/or technical issues of the tissue processing or CR staining.17,18 The amyloid fibrils identified on EM measured 5 − 15 nm (mostly around 10 nm) in diameter. Amyloid fibrils were deposited to varying degrees in all cases (8/8), extracellularly in vascular/perivascular regions, and endoneurial or endomysial/perimysial areas; intracellularly within BV cells (8/8), myofibers in all muscle cases (5/5), and Schwann cells in all nerve cases (3/3) (Table 1; Figures 1 and 2).

Nerve pathology

The sural nerve biopsies in all patients (1-3 in Table 1) revealed histological features of axonal degeneration with marked loss of myelinated/unmyelinated fibers, SC proliferation characterized by cytoplasmic process expansion, and amyloid deposition within the endoneurial tissue including BVs (Figure 1A-C). In patient 2, there were also mixed features of demyelination-remyelination attributed partially to comorbid diabetes.15 EM confirmed the histological features and further specified the location of amyloid deposition, particularly intra-BV cells and SCs (Figure 1D-G). Amyloid fibrils were prominent within the SC processes (Figure 1G). The SC processes frequently intertwined with amyloid fibrils, sometimes disrupting the basal lamina and extending into the extracellular matrix (Figure 1H and I). Additionally, the SC processes, specifically bands of Büngner, contained complex inclusions that were focally fibrillar and connected to amyloid fibrils (Figure 1G-L).

Muscle pathology

The thigh muscle biopsies in all patients (4-8 in Table 1) demonstrated variable myopathic changes including myofiber atrophy in addition to the aforementioned amyloid deposition. These patients (except patient 4) also had other concomitant myopathic processes, including inflammatory myopathy in patient 5,19 hydroxychloroquine-induced myopathy in patient 6,16 mitochondrial myopathy with a muscle mDNA deletion in patient 7, and a severe myopathy in patient 8 with clinically suspected sIBM. The histologically identified amyloid deposits were relatively limited to the endomysial/perimysial BVs, necrotic myofibers, some peri-sarcolemmal areas of non-necrotic myofibers, and/or intra-muscular nerve twigs (Figure 2A-F). EM revealed amyloid fibrils primarily localized to the vascular/perivascular areas including intra-BV cells, as well as peri-/sub-sarcolemmal areas with associated lobulation and multifocal loss of the muscle plasma membrane and basal lamina (Figure 2G-L). Other EM findings included abnormal mitochondria with paracrystalline inclusions (patients 6 and 7) and curvilinear bodies (patient 6).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrates intracellular amyloidosis in all examined nerve and skeletal muscle biopsies, suggesting that intracellular amyloidosis is common in amyloid-affected tissues, despite being rarely described in previous case reports.12,19–22 In muscle, intracellular deposition of amyloid fibrils is limited to the endomysial/perimysial BV cells, necrotic myofibers, and peri-/sub-sarcolemmal areas with focal lobulation, and is presumably of limited contribution to the myopathic process (except amyloid vasculopathy) and clinical manifestation.4 In nerves, intracellular amyloid is prominent in endoneurial BV cells and SC processes. Another novel observation of our present study is that all 3 examined nerve biopsies showed complex amyloid-associated inclusions within SC processes particularly in the bands of Büngner. These amyloid-related features were associated with SC proliferation and marked axonal degeneration, indicating a role in cellular injury and neuropathy progression.

While amyloidosis is traditionally characterized by the extracellular deposition of amyloid fibrils leading to tissue damage, there is ongoing evidence of intracellular amyloid deposition in peripheral nerve and skeletal muscle.12,19–22 Intracellular amyloidosis in nerves and muscles is likely underreported, as only a few case reports exist, and its detection relies almost exclusively on EM at ultrastructural levels, a technique rarely performed in neuromuscular amyloidosis studies in recent decades.3,4,11,19,22 Our present study reveals that while intracellular amyloid is commonly identifiable at the EM/ultrastructural levels, it may be invisible at light microscopic levels, in particular the subsarcolemmal areas of non-necrotic myofibers and SC processes. It has been observed that intracellular amyloid deposition is associated with cell membrane destruction and organelle degeneration, leading to cell dysfunction and degeneration.6,8,11,23 The observation of amyloid intracellular distribution and its associated cell injury may contribute to modulating cellular pathways, developing pathological markers, and/or finding therapy targets.24

The presence of intracellular amyloid fibrils in SCs has been described in limited case reports although their distribution was commonly restricted to the cytoplasmic region abutting the membrane and usually linked with extracellular amyloid deposits.6,12,20,21,25 The extracellular amyloid fibrils commonly penetrated the SC membrane and distorted SC processes.6,8 This direct toxicity of amyloid deposits, along with endoneurial amyloid vasculopathy, has been implicated in small-fiber axon loss.7,11,25 In ATTR polyneuropathy, axonal loss and SC atrophy are evident, with distal nerve regions exhibiting prominent axonal degeneration, while proximal regions show segmental demyelination followed by remyelination.6,7,26 In our study, axonal degeneration observed in the sural nerve biopsies was likely biopsy site-related, with 1 of 3 cases also exhibiting demyelination-remyelination, potentially due to the patient’s comorbid diabetes. We also reported complex inclusions within SC processes particularly in the bands of Büngner. These SC inclusions were likely derived from amyloid fibrils given their focal fibrillar appearance and connection to amyloid fibrils. While the presence of SC inclusions was described in our recent case report,12 the present study reveals its prevalence in 2 additional sural nerve biopsies from patients with ATTR. Whether these inclusions are specific to ATTR remains unknown and warrants further investigation in non-ATTR amyloidosis cases. As the formation of intracellular amyloid inclusions is well-documented in neurodegenerative diseases, the formation of amyloid-related inclusions in SC processes is presumably similarly associated with axonal degeneration and SC degeneration compensated by proliferation, and the progression of neuropathy.1

Skeletal muscle involvement in systemic amyloidosis is rare, occurring in only 1.5% of AL amyloidosis cases and even less frequently in ATTR and AGel amyloidosis.3–5,13,14 However, muscle amyloid deposition is commonly seen in various disorders, including AL amyloidosis, familial amyloidosis (including ATTR and AGel), malignant neoplasms14, some primary myopathies such as anoctaminopathy, dysferlinopathy,3 and sIBM.27,28 Typically, amyloid myopathy occurs independently of systemic amyloidosis. The common association of amyloid deposition with other myopathies is also seen the present study, including associated inflammatory myopathy (2 cases), hydroxychloroquine-induced myopathy (1 case), and chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO) (1 case). Regardless of the etiology, intracellular amyloid deposition was detected on EM in all our present cases but limited to areas adjacent to extracellular amyloid, suggesting an extension of the extracellular deposits into cells. While amyloid deposition may be seen in necrotic fibers, necrotic fibers are typically rare or few in amyloid myopathy,3,4 unless there is another coexisting myopathy such as inflammatory myopathy.3,14,19 ATTR amyloid myopathy may have more necrotic fibers compared to non-ATTR amyloid myopathy.9

A common pathological feature of amyloid myopathy is muscle denervation atrophy with or without fiber type grouping suggestive of reinnervation. This pattern, seen in 80%-100% of cases, suggests concurrent amyloid involvement of the muscle innervating nerve or peripheral nerve.3,9,14 In this study, neurogenic features of amyloid myopathy were evident, with amyloid deposition seen in intramuscular nerve fibers (Figure 2A-F). Notably, muscle weakness does not always correlate with the degree of amyloid deposition, as skeletal muscle involvement in systemic amyloidosis is often clinically silent.4 These clinical findings are compatible with our observation of limited intracellular and extracellular amyloidosis in the examined muscle biopsies. Nevertheless, the intra-muscle amyloid vasculopathy, similar to intra-nerve amyloid vasculopathy,7,11 may cause destruction of the microvasculature and adjacent tissue with nerve edema.7,26 Further research is needed to clarify the role of amyloid myopathy and microvasculopathy in clinical manifestations.

CONCLUSIONS

Intracellular amyloid deposition is commonly observed in the neuromuscular BV cells, SCs of peripheral nerves, and/or peri-/sub-sarcolemmal areas of skeletal muscle fibers. The localization of intracellular amyloidosis is best identified by EM at ultrastructural levels. In muscle, intracellular amyloid deposition is relatively limited, apart from its involvement in amyloid vasculopathy, suggesting a minimal contribution to the myopathic process and clinical symptoms. In contrast, nerve intracellular amyloid deposition is more pronounced, particularly in the endoneurial BV cells and SCs with amyloid-associated complex inclusions formed within SC processes. These inclusions likely contribute to progressive axonal degeneration and impaired nerve fiber regeneration, driving the progression of peripheral neuropathy. Our present findings advance understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms involved in amyloid-related neuromuscular disorders.
