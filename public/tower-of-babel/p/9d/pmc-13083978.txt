Huperzine A improves neurological function in mice with intracerebral hemorrhage by alleviating neuroinflammation and ferroptosis
Authors: Shanjun Wang, Shuangyan Li, Xinguo Sun, Yunzhao Chen, Ruiyun Yan, Yuheng Liu, Zengguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a devastating neurological condition that is characterized by high morbidity and long-term disability, which frequently results in severe neurological deficits. Given the limited therapeutic options, pharmacological neuroprotection has emerged as a crucial area of research. This study explored the neuroprotective potential of huperzine A (HupA) in a murine model of collagenase-induced ICH in the caudate nucleus. Three experimental groups—Sham, ICH, and ICH + HupA—were assessed at multiple timepoints (1, 3, 7, and 14 days post-ICH) for neurological function, neuroinflammatory responses, and ferroptosis-related markers. Our findings reveal that HupA exerts significant neuroprotection through dual mechanisms: (1) attenuating neuroinflammation via the suppression of IL-1β/IL-6 release and inhibition of glial activation, and (2) mitigating ferroptosis by reducing iron accumulation and upregulating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression, and thereby preserving neuronal viability. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of HupA in alleviating ICH-induced brain injury, and thereby offers a promising multi-target strategy for ICH treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38642-0.Keywords: Ferroptosis, Huperzine A, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Neuroinflammation, Neurological functionSubject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a serious neurological disorder characterized by high rates of mortality and disability. ICH accounts for approximately 15–25% of stroke cases1. Despite its high prevalence, there are limited therapeutic options for ICH. Further, post-ICH neuronal death and its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Following ICH, a series of primary and secondary reactions occur, with degradation products of hemoglobin triggering various pathological processes, including inflammation and ferroptosis, which contribute to neuronal damage2,3. Neuronal death is the main cause of irreversible damage to neurological function after ICH4. One of the main types of neuronal death following ICH is ferroptosis 5. Therefore, suppressing inflammation and ferroptosis after ICH is a promising strategy for treating ICH and improving prognosis6. However, despite numerous drug intervention studies targeting this pathological process, there remains no clinically effective drug7.

Huperzine A (HupA), an extract from Huperzia, is a reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor8,9. HupA can easily cross the blood–brain barrier and is widely used clinically as a treatment for Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia and other diseases10,11. HupA can enhance cholinergic nerve function, alleviate abnormal neuroinflammation, and promote brain function recovery12,13. However, the impact of HupA on post-ICH neurological impairment is unclear. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the efficacy and mechanism of action of HupA in improving neurological dysfunction after ICH. Specifically, we evaluated HupA’s neuroprotective potential in a collagenase-induced ICH mice model and assessed its effects on neuroinflammation and ferroptosis.

Materials and methods

Animals

We used male C57BL/6 mice (7–9 weeks, 20–22 g) as experimental animals. Animals were purchased from Charles River Co. (Beijing, China), and were kept in the Animal Center of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. The conditions were controlled at 20 ± 2 ℃, 45% ± 5% humidity, and light and dark for 12 h. The procedure for euthanasia before brain tissue harvesting was performed as follows: Mice were first anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (90 mg/kg + 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, IP). Following confirmation of deep anesthesia (as evidenced by absence of a toe pinch reflex), euthanasia was humanely performed via cervical dislocation. Death was verified by assessing the absence of both heartbeat and corneal reflexes. Subsequently, the brain was carefully dissected after transcardial perfusion for further analysis. This study was carried out in full adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal research.

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (Tianjin, China; Approval No. IRB2023-DWFL-104). All experiments were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (2011). At the end of the study, euthanasia was performed in accordance with the AVMA 2020 Guidelines. All possible steps were taken to alleviate animal suffering during the experiment.

Anesthesia and ICH model

During animal modeling, Mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (90 mg/kg + 10 mg/kg, IP). The depth of anesthesia was assessed by confirming the absence of a toe pinch reflex, indicating adequate surgical anesthesia. Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C with a heating pad. After anesthesia, the hair on the top of the mouse’s head was clipped. ICH modeling was performed as previously described 14. Mice were fixed on a stereotaxic apparatus, and the scalp was incised longitudinally to expose the skull and fontanel. Subsequently, the fontanel was located 0.5 mm in front, with a hole (diameter: approximately 0.3 mm) being drilled through the skull (approximately 2.2) mm to the right. Subsequently, a microinjection needle (33-G) 3.5 mm was inserted vertically through the hole. Then, 0.60 L of collagenase (0.25 U/L; Sigma C5138) was injected at an average rate of 0.5 L/min using a microinjection pump. The needle was left in the brain tissue for 10 min before removing it. The skin incision was then closed. Finally, mice were resuscitated from anesthesia on a warm blanket.

To ensure the reliability and consistency of the ICH model, stringent exclusion criteria were applied both prior to and following group assignment. An initial assessment was conducted 12 h after model induction. Animals that exhibited no neurological deficits—such as a lack of left-sided weakness in limb-placing tests—were excluded, as this indicated unsuccessful ICH induction. Mice that died within the first 12 h after surgery were also excluded. A second set of criteria was applied after enrollment: any animal that died during the experimental period, as well as those with a hematoma diameter of less than 1.0 mm upon macroscopic inspection during brain tissue collection, were excluded due to insufficient hemorrhage.

Drug administration and grouping

Of the 295 mice that underwent ICH induction, 32 were excluded prior to enrollment (21 due to the absence of neurological deficits and 11 due to death). The remaining 263 C57BL/6 mice were randomly allocated to three groups: Sham, ICH, and ICH + HupA. The ICH and ICH + HupA groups were used to establish the collagenase-induced ICH model as described in Sect. 3.2. Contrastingly, Sham mice were injected with saline rather than collagenase. HupA was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg, starting 24 h after ICH induction and followed by daily injections. This dosage was selected based on a preliminary 14-day toxicological assessment (doses of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 mg/kg; n = 6 per group), which established 0.3 mg/kg as the highest well-tolerated dose without overt adverse effects. The chosen dose is well below the reported murine LD₅₀ of 1.8 mg/kg (i.p.)15. After model establishment, mice in the ICH + HupA group received HupA (0.3 mg/kg, once daily) for 14 days (IP), while mice in the other groups received a similar amount of saline (Fig. 1A, B).

Experimental Procedure. (A) On the first day after ICH, medication therapy was started and continued for 14 days; further, measurements were performed at the designated time points. (B) Flow chart showing the experimental protocol with the number of animals that were used, died, and included in the study.

Among the 263 enrolled mice, 17 died during the experiment, and 8 were excluded upon post-mortem examination due to a hematoma diameter of < 1 mm. The final cohort therefore consisted of 238 mice, corresponding to a model success rate of 80.7% (238/295) and an overall mortality rate of 9.5% (28/295).

Morris water maze (MWM) and modified neurological severity score (mNSS)

MWM assessment training began on day 7 after modeling, and the mice were trained with MWM for 6 consecutive days. The final Morris water maze assessment was performed at the 14-day post-ICH (day 7 after the start of training). We used the MWM to assess the cognitive function of mice.16. Briefly, the mice were trained four times a day at intervals of ≥ 45 min, starting at a random position that changed daily. If the mice found a platform, they stayed on the platform for 5–6 s; alternatively, if they did not find a platform, they were manually placed on it to adapt for 20 s. On the 7th day after the start of the assessment, we removed the platform and placed the mice in the quadrant opposite from the target quadrant for 90 s and allowed them to explore freely. During this period, we kept track of the number of times they passed through the platform, platform latency, and the overall travel distance. To reduce experimental bias, the operators were blinded to the experimental grouping. After each test, we dried the mice with a hair dryer and put them in a cage to keep them warm.

We referred to the method of Yuan17 for the assessment of mNSS. The mNSS was scored by testing for motor, sensory, beam balance, reflex deficits, and abnormal movements. Scores range from 0 to 18, with a higher score indicating more severe neurological damage. We scored mice on days 1, 3, 7, and 14 after ICH using the mNSS, which was performed by a researcher blinded to the experimental grouping.

Nissl staining

We applied 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for fixation of mouse brain tissue. Subsequently, the brain tissue was cut into 8-μm sections for Nissl (Solarbio Science & Technology [Beijing, China] G1432) staining. We referred to the method by Yang et al. for Nissl staining18. We observed brain tissue sections under a light microscope (BX53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and counted the number of positive cells using Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence

Neural expression was assessed through immunofluorescence staining of extracted brain tissue as follows. After euthanasia, the heart was injected with PBS and 4% PFA, and then the entire brain was removed, and then it was fixed in 4% PFA at 4° C for 24 h. Then, the cerebral tissue was dried in 15–30% sucrose, placed in an optimum OCT solution, and sliced into 8 µm coronal cryosections (CM 1950, Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA) by means of a microtome. The OCT solution was flushed with PBS after reheating, and the tissue was labeled with a water-repellent pen. Then, the brain slices were incubated in PBS containing using 2% BSA, 2% sheep serum, 0.2% Triton X-100, and 0.05% Tween 20 at ambient temperature for 1.5 h. The tissue was then washed and incubated at 4 °C with a dilution of 50 µg of primary antibody (Neu N, Abcam [Cambridge, UK], ab104224, 1: 1000; Iba-1,CST, E4O4W, 1: 100; GFAP, Abcam, ab68428, 1: 500). After reheating, the tissue was washed with PBS and incubated for 1 h in the dark with fluorescently-labelled secondary antibodies. The slices were then sealed with a 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole-containing seal and examined under an inverted fluorescent microscope (Olympus). The number of positive cells was calculated with ImageJ software.

Perls’ staining

Perls’ staining Kit (Solarbio G1428) was used for the detection of intracellular iron. The specific procedure followed the kit instructions. Briefly, the slides were stained, dehydrated, and sealed with neutral gum, followed by examination under a microscope. The mean intensity was computed with the Image J software.

Western blotting

After ICH, the mice were euthanized on days 1, 3, 7, and 14, and brain tissues were injected with cold PBS. Extraction of the whole protein using the Radio-Immune Precipitation Assay Protein Dissociation Buffer and 1% PMF were performed. A 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was performed for 90 min at 120 V and transferred onto the polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against IL 1β (Cell Signaling Technology [Danvers, MA, USA], D3U3E 12,703; 1:1000), IL 6 (Proteintech [Danvers, MA, USA], 21,865–1-AP; 1:1000), Acsl-4 (Proteintech, 22,401–1-1-AP; 1:2000), Gpx4 (Abcam, ab41787; 1:1000), FPN (Novus [Chesterfield, MO, USA], NBP1-21,502 SLC40A1; 1:500), TfR1 (Abcam, ab84036; 1:1000), Neu N (Abcam, ab104224; 1:1000), Gapdh (Proteintech, 10,494–1-AP; 1:5000), and β-actin (Proteintech, 66,009 -1-Ig; 1:5000) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the membrane was washed with a solution of 0.1% Tween ® 20 with tris-buffered saline solution and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with anti-mouse or anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000, Zhongshan Jinqiao [Zhongshan, China]). Exposure was measured by ECL chemiluminescence using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). Image J was used to quantify the protein band density and to compare it with β-actin or GAPDH.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Immediately after induction of deep anesthesia, perihematoma tissues (1 cm3) were removed and fixed in 2.5% gluta solution (Solarbio Science & Technology P1126). The tissue sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 2% Pb citrate. Images were taken with the Tecnai G20 Twin transmission electron microscope (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA).

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean and were analyzed with one- or two-way variance. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The data was analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

HupA can improves neurological score and learning ability after ICH in mice

Compared with the other two groups, the post-ICH mNSS score was lower. The mNSS score was significantly lower in the ICH + HupA group than in the ICH group at 7 and 14 days post-ICH (p = 0.027 and p = 0.045, respectively) (Fig. 2A). However, at 1 and 3 days post-ICH, mNSS scores did not differ significantly between the ICH and ICH + HupA groups. Regarding the MWM experiment, on days 6 and 7 of the experiment, the latency time for mice in the ICH + HupA group to reach the platform was significantly lower than that in the ICH group (p = 0.039 and p = 0.003, respectively) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, mice in the ICH + HupA group stayed in the S1 region significantly longer than those in the ICH group (p = 0.031) (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the total distance traveled by mice in the ICH + HupA group during the experiment was significantly shorter than that traveled by mice in the ICH group (p = 0.024) (Fig. 2D, E).

Western blotting revealed significantly higher NeuN expression in the ICH + HupA group than in the ICH group on the 14th day post-ICH (p = 0.034) (Fig. 3B, D).

HupA can alleviates post-ICH neuroinflammation in mice

The number of Iba-1 and GFAP-positive cells in the ICH + HupA group was significantly reduced compared to that of the ICH group at the 3- and 7-day time points (Iba-1, p = 0.002 and p = 0.041; GFAP, p = 0.010 and p = 0.019 respectively) (Fig. 4A, B, D, E). In addition, western blotting showed that the expression of IL-1β and IL-6 was significantly lower in the ICH + HupA group than in the ICH group at both 3- and 7-day time points (IL-1β, p = 0.001 and p = 0.002; IL-6, p = 0.000 and p = 0.000 respectively) (Fig. 4C, F, G). These findings suggest that HupA treatment effectively attenuates ICH-induced neuroinflammation.

HupA can alleviate iron metabolism disorder and ferroptosis after ICH in mice

Iron deposition was evaluated using Perls’ Staining, and western blotting was used to assess the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins, such as TFR1, FPN, ACSL4, and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). A post-ICH increase in the expression of TFR1 was observed. At day 3 following ICH, the ICH + HupA group had a significantly lower expression of TFR1 compared with the ICH group (P = 0.043). The expression of FPN decreased following ICH. The ICH + HupA group demonstrated a significant increase in FPN expression at day 7 and 14 following ICH compared with the ICH group (p = 0.019 and p = 0.004, respectively) (Fig. 5A–D). After ICH, there was increased ACSL4 expression; however, there were no significant differences between the ICH and ICH + HupA groups. GPX4 expression was decreased after ICH. GPX4 expression was significantly higher in the ICH + HupA group than in the ICH group (p = 0.000 and p = 0.032, respectively) (Fig. 5E–H).

Discussion

The occurrence of ICH is followed by both primary and secondary brain injuries. Primary brain injury often occurs within hours of ICH, during which initial hematoma compression leads to localized brain tissue destruction19. Secondary brain injury is more closely associated with poor prognosis. The main potential targets for ICH treatment include blood–brain barrier disruption after hemorrhage, oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and brain edema20.

Neurons are the basis of all neural activity; accordingly, neuronal protection serves to protect neural function. After ICH, hemoglobin around hematomas leads to a variety of pathologic processes that contribute to neuron injury, such as inflammatory and iron-overload2,3.

HupA is known for its cognitive-enhancing properties21–23. It is mainly used in China for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease. HupA works by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, an enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine, and, therefore, increases acetylcholine levels in the brain. This action helps improve memory and cognitive function. Accordingly, HupA is a valuable therapeutic agent for neurodegenerative diseases. Additionally, HupA has demonstrated neuroprotective effects, which further support its use in preserving cognitive health. In our study, HupA treatment was initiated 24 h post-ICH to mirror the typical clinical delay in hospital presentation and diagnosis, thereby enhancing the translational relevance of our model. The 0.3 mg/kg dose was selected based on empirical safety-efficacy profiling. Although neuroprotective efficacy has been reported across a range of 0.1–0.5 mg/kg24,25, toxicological assessment revealed that higher doses (e.g., 0.5 mg/kg) induced cholinergic adverse effects. The 0.3 mg/kg regimen optimally balances therapeutic benefit with minimal toxicity, consistent with observed functional and histological improvements. Only male C57BL/6 mice were used in this study. This decision was made to minimize potential confounding effects arising from hormonal fluctuations associated with the estrous cycle in female animals, a common strategy in initial mechanistic investigations of ICH aimed at enhancing internal validity and reducing biological variability. Our findings demonstrate that HupA enhances neurological function, as assessed by mNSS, and improves cognitive and learning capabilities in ICH mouse models, as evidenced by MWM performance.

Although HupA has been shown to reduce neuroinflammation26, its efficacy in treating post-ICH neuroinflammation remains unclear. GFAP and Iba-1 are markers for astrocytes and microglia. Activated microglia induce the release of proinflammatory cytokines and mediators, such as IL-1 and IL-18. Further, GFAP and Iba-1 are involved in the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation27. Elevated levels of IL-1β and IL-6 may exacerbate neuroinflammation, leading to secondary brain damage28. In our study, the number of Iba-1 and GFAP-positive cells in the ICH + HupA group was significantly lower than in the ICH group. Meanwhile, Il-1β and IL-6 expression in the ICH + HupA group was also significantly lower than that in the ICH group. Therefore, HupA may reduce neuroinflammation after ICH.

HupA inhibited unstable iron in neurons mediated by ferric citrate. At the same time, HupA attenuated iron deposition in the brain tissue of APP/PS1 mice29,30. Therefore, HupA may be used to treat diseases associated with abnormal iron metabolism by reducing iron deposition in brain tissue.

Iron overload is generally considered to be closely related to ferroptosis. Ferroptosis has been recognized as an important form of death in stroke31. Recent studies have shown that ferroptosis occurs in ICH32,33. Thus, ferroptosis is increasingly recognized as an important target for the treatment of CNS diseases. Glutathione depletion and subsequent GPX4 inactivation, abnormal iron metabolism, and aberrant ACSL4 expression have been shown to cause ferroptosis3. Although there are currently numerous drugs with different mechanisms of action, their treatment effect is often poor. HupA is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that is clinically used to treat neurological diseases related to cognitive impairment34. HupA has a significant dose-dependent and time-dependent effect on cognitive function in patients with Alzheimer’s (AD)23. We speculate that HupA exhibits neuroprotective properties by mitigating post-ICH ferroptosis. Therefore, the present study investigated its mechanism of action. Previous studies have elucidated the pathological mechanisms of ferroptosis, particularly the roles of TFR1, FPN, ROS, GSH, GPX4, and ACSL46,35–37.. To determine whether HupA modulates ferroptosis following ICH, we analyzed key pathway markers—TFR1, FPN, GPX4, and ACSL4—to uncover its potential mechanism.

Previous research has shown that GPX4, which acts as a catalyst for reducing lipid peroxidation, may play an important role in the regulation of ferroptosis38,39. GPX4 is the only factor known to be active against lipid peroxidation. The effect of GPX4 is closely related to the amount of GSH40. Ferroptosis can be independently inhibited by GPX441. Our findings demonstrated that HupA can significantly increase GPX4 levels after ICH.

ACSL4 expression is a hallmark of ferroptosis42. Thus, we studied the influence of HupA on the expression of ACSL4 in mice after ICH. Our findings demonstrated that HupA does not significantly influence ACSL4 expression. Potential explanations for this observation include HupA’s selective (rather than broad) inhibition of the ferroptosis pathway and the temporal expression pattern of ACSL4. We propose that the primary mechanism involves HupA’s selective ferroptosis inhibition, which aligns with previous findings demonstrating that GPX4 overexpression alone can effectively suppress ferroptosis43. Another possible explanation for our negative results could be that the time point of the assay was not in the time window of ACSL4 expression; however, we do not believe this to be valid, for several reasons: although the between-group differences in ACSL4 expression were not statistically significant, we observed a consistent temporal pattern—a gradual increase at 1d and 3d, with a peak at 7d, followed by a decline at 14d after ICH. Importantly, our experimental time points cover this peak expression.

Iron homeostasis is essential for maintaining brain health. Circulating iron needs to pass through iron transporters on blood–brain barrier cells to enter the brain; in addition, ferrous and trivalent iron need to enter the cytoplasm through TFR144. Iron in the cytoplasm participates in redox reactions as a cofactor or is stored in ferritin. Translocation via FPN is the only channel for the release of iron from the cell. Iron metabolism can be regulated through FPN44,45. Overexpression of TFR-1 or underexpression of FPN indirectly leads to increased reactive oxygen species production, which results in cell death46. TFR1 expression regulates iron uptake47, while iron export is controlled by the unique iron exporter FPN48. Therefore, we investigated the expression levels of TFR1 and FPN after ICH. Our findings demonstrated that HupA can reduce iron content in brain tissue after ICH, reduce TFR1 protein expression, and increase FPN1 protein expression. Disturbed iron metabolism and low GPX4 expression are strongly associated with ferroptosis. Ferroptosis has been shown to induce neuronal damage in aged mice with ICH49. This finding is further validated by our study. Ferroptosis is typically characterized by mitochondrial atrophy, and we confirmed this finding by TEM observations of mitochondria in brain tissue 7 days after ICH. Taken together, HupA attenuated ferroptosis after ICH, and this protective effect was achieved by promoting the expression of FPN and GPX4 and inhibiting the expression of TFR1.

Our findings showed that HupA significantly reduced neuronal loss after ICH. We speculate that neuronal loss and cognitive impairment after ICH are related to neuroinflammation and ferroptosis (Fig. 7). Additionally, HupA may ameliorate the intracranial inflammatory response and reduce ferroptosis in mice with ICH. Taken together, our findings provide a theoretical basis for the clinical application of HupA in neurologic dysfunction after ICH. The clinical use of HupA may provide a treatment alternative for patients with ICH.

HupA improves the prognosis of mice with ICH by regulating inflammatory responses and ferroptosis.

Although our results suggest that HupA may attenuate neuroinflammation and ferroptosis following ICH, it is important to note that the precise causal relationship remains to be fully elucidated. The present study does not include interventional experiments—such as co-administration with ferroptosis inducers or inhibitors, or the use of genetically modified animals (e.g., GPX4-deficient mice)—that would be necessary to definitively establish HupA’s mechanism of action. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as revealing a correlative rather than a causative link. Future studies employing these mechanistic approaches will be essential to validate whether modulation of ferroptosis and inflammatory signaling indeed constitutes the primary pathway through which HupA confers neuroprotection.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that HupA confers protection against ICH-mediated neurological deficits, and the mechanisms underlying this neuroprotective effect may involve the reduction of neuroinflammation and ferroptosis.
