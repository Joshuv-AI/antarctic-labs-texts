Experimental Study on Sustainability Involving the Pugh Matrix on Emission Values of High-Temperature Air in the Premixed Charged Compression Ignition Engine
Authors: Paul James Thadhani Joshua, Annamalai Kandasamy, Elumalai Perumal Venkatesan, Chanduveetil Ahamed Saleel
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main aim of the study was to reduce carbon emissions in the atmosphere using a novel Andropogon narudus (AN) biofuel using higher air temperatures and reducing the consumption of conventional fossil fuel (diesel). The use of a heat exchange chamber within the air intake manifold is a popular method to reduce hydrocarbon (HC) and carbon monoxide (CO) emissions during cold starts. A premixed charged compression ignition engine in the dual-fuel mode was used in this study with raw diesel, raw AN oil, AN70+D30, AN80+D20, AN80+D20 (35 °C), AN80+D20 (40 °C), and AN80+D20 (45 °C). A chamber was designed and analyzed to measure the exit temperature and density change and to determine the reduction in volumetric efficiency of the engine, using Ansys Fluent software. A sustainability assessment study was performed to understand the feasibility of the fuel and the design using the Pugh Matrix. The fuel AN80+D20 with an air temperature of 45 °C was found to be superior to all other fuels in terms of brake thermal efficiency, reaching at 32.1%. D100 used the least amount of energy, whereas AN80+D20 used the most. Engine HC emission was at the lowest (45.01 ppm) for AN80+D20 fuel at 45 °C air input and reached the highest (50 ppm) for AN100 fuel. With an air temperature of 45 °C, CO emission was at its lowest for AN80+D20 gasoline (0.018%) and was at its highest for AN100 (0.072%). Nitrogen oxide emissions were the highest for AN80+D20 fuel with an air temperature of 45 °C, with an air concentration of 1254 ppm, whereas they were the lowest for AN100 (900 ppm). CO2 values were reduced, with D100 showing the lowest levels and AN100 showing the highest. The smoke emission was minimum for AN80+D20 fuel at 45 °C, with a smoke number of 15 compared to 33 for D100 fuel. As per the Pugh Matrix assessment, AN80+D20 with 35 °C air temperature had higher scores compared to all of the other fuel mixtures.

Introduction

Emissions resulting from fossil fuel use in the automotive sector have forced researchers to explore unconventional engine operations and alternative fuels. These untraditional approaches to engine technology and fuel use lead to the promotion of sustainable practices. Sustainability in the engine and fuel research can be obtained by (a) introducing low viscous biofuels, (b) lowering the consumption of fuel, (c) reducing the formation of pollutants, (d) reducing the exposure of carbon in the atmosphere, and (e) lessening the environmental impact.

Sustainability is built upon three foremost benchmarks—all-encompassing economical equilibrium, ecological steadiness, and societal influence—as shown in Figure 1. An aberration in any of these criteria leads to the instability of the system. Therefore, the assessment of any novel fuel or engine modification relies on sustainability. The detailed stimulus of sustainability configurations will be complemented by an assessment of the cost-effectiveness within the automotive industry.1

Pillars of sustainability.

The current reserves of fossil fuel remain uncertain, and usage in automotive and other industrial applications poses a threat to the environment. Strict emission norms have been imposed by regulatory bodies to reduce the level of greenhouse gases. The norms act as a catalyst for research in engine and fuel to meet the demands of the automobile and other industrial sectors.2−4 The need for the replacement of fossil fuels has led to a prioritization of biodiesel, biofuel, and bioethanol in research trends. Many methods have been used in the production of biofuel and biodiesel, but the major role has been played by countries such as Brazil and the USA.3,5 These countries use first- and second-generation fuels in many applications including power, marine transportation, and many other commercial applications. Biodiesel and biofuel are the most commonly used fuels.6,7 In 1893, Rudolph Diesel used peanut oil in the development of diesel engines. As fuel technology evolved, biodiesel underwent secondary processing to derive its methyl ester for addressing problems arising when used in the compression ignition (CI) engine. The methyl esters of the biodiesel are nonaromatic, sulfur less, and advantageous compared to the conventional fuel. Biodiesel has drawbacks such as high viscosity, gumming, and injector clogging when used in a CI engine. A secondary process for reducing the viscosity of the fuel, such as pyrolysis, transesterification, preheating, dilution, or blending, is required.8,9 However, biofuels have significantly lower viscosities, which avoid most of the problems caused by biodiesel use. The production methodologies for these two processes influence the viscosity of biodiesel and biofuel. Biodiesel is derived by grinding plant seeds, whereas biofuel is derived by heating the leaves or any other part of a plant with alcohol, condensing the steam into water and oil, and then separating the oil from the water. Biofuel has reduced viscosity, can be directly injected into the conventional engine, and has fewer drawbacks than biodiesel. Some of the commonly used biofuels are pine oil, lemongrass oil, and lemon peel oil, which are processed and produced by using the steam-distillation method. Many techniques were used in past decades to reduce emissions without compromising performance.10 Strategy for combustion modes involves homogeneous charge compression ignition (HCCI) and premixed charged compression ignition (PCCI) engines. However, a difficulty with the HCCI engine is the creation of a perfect mixture of the fuel and air due to the reduced fuel volatility than petrol. In addition, a high cetane number of the fuel produces an increased pressure rise, creating difficulty in controlling the combustion phase. These challenges led the researcher to consider the PCCI engine, which offers a middle way in choosing a conventional diesel engine and an HCCI engine. In PCCI engines, part of the fuel is used for preparing a homogeneous mixture using an early injection strategy while the remaining undergoes conventional combustion at top dead center (TDC), eliminating the need of HCCI engines.3,4 Compared to HCCI engines, PCCI engines pass premixed and diffusion combustion phases, with autoignition producing a relatively low combustion temperature and lower nitrogen oxides (NOx) due to the lean homogeneous mixture. However, the early injection technique in the PCCI engine reduces the combustion temperature because of the fuel spray formation, wall wetting, slow fuel vaporization rate, and increased liquid penetration, which increase the formation of hydrocarbons (HCs) and carbon monoxide (CO). This delay in ignition in a PCCI engine by 5° crank angle (CA) poses challenges in monitoring of the start of combustion, resulting in reduced NOx and increased HC and CO emissions.11 Premixing of fuels in a PCCI engine results in an upsurge in carbon-based emissions and reduced nitrogen-based emissions.12 This is due to incomplete combustion in a PCCI engine. Controlling the air inlet temperature in PCCI engines shows a drastic change in emissions, including NOx, HC, CO, and CO2.13 A premixed stratification charge involving early injection followed by charge introduction at TDC is recommended for reducing carbon-based emission.14 A solution is the use of biofuels containing oxygen, which isolates fuel-rich regions, enhances the combustion process, and reduces the formation of HC, CO, and soot.15,16 Biofuels have the advantage of reduced viscosity and can be directly used in internal combustion (IC) engines without any amendment. However, in the presence of natural oxygen, biofuels have a higher combustion efficiency than diesel fuel, resulting in increased nitrogen oxide emissions. Also, the wall-wetting issue can be resolved by increasing the temperature of air intake, ranging from 40 to 70 °C.17,18 However, these techniques rapidly increase the combustion chamber temperature, which leads to the formation of NOx due to high temperature and availability of oxygen. The formation of NOx can be balanced by an early injection strategy, which is considered to reduce the combustion temperatures.

Many researchers have analyzed the strategies developed in the past decades. Some of them have demonstrated the effectiveness of using low viscous biofuel and engine adaptation along with an early injection strategy. On the basis of the discussion and inference from the literature, it was found that only a small number of researchers have verified the concurrent decline of nitrogen- and carbon-based emissions. A PCCI engine outperforms an HCCI engine in terms of NOx emissions; however, due to incomplete combustion, the PCCI engine also generates higher HC and CO. To reduce the atmospheric concentration of carbon-based emissions, an early injection strategy can be helpful. A spike in carbon-based emissions might also be addressed by increasing the air intake temperature.13 Instead of adding oxygen, which would likewise increase the engine’s thermal efficiency, a biofuel containing oxygen would be a more reliable way to increase the thermal efficiency of the PCCI engine. To address the issue of the increase in carbon-based emissions, an additional air intake setup was developed for our investigation.

Additionally, a novel biofuel, Andropogon narudus (AN), was used as the oxygen source of the engine to improve combustion. Initially, various fuel blends such as D100, AN100, AN80+D20, and AN70+D30 were used in this study. To address cold start conditions, a novel heat supply chamber was designed, analyzed, and fabricated. The chamber used AN80+D20 as a fuel source and temperature of output air was adjustable within the range of 35, 40, and 45 °C. Subsequently, a sustainability study based on the emission of fuels using a statistical tool (the Pugh matrix) was conducted.

Materials and Methods

Heat Supply Chamber Design

The heat supply chamber was designed to deliver a precise temperature without reducing the density of air into the air intake manifold of the PCCI engine. The reduction in the density of air due to the thermal expansion of air molecules may lead to a reduction in the volumetric efficiency of air. For identifying a suitable design to deliver air at a certain temperature without compromising density, initially, four chambers were modeled and simulated using Ansys Fluent.26,27 The results were studied at the end of the simulation, and a single model of the chamber was selected for manufacturing and mounted on the PCCI engine for testing with fuel samples using elevated temperature air. The initial modeling of the chamber is shown in Figure 2.

Engine Arrangement

A four-stroke single-cylinder direct injection (DI) diesel PCCI engine was used to perform all of the experimental tests. The speed of the engine was constantly maintained at 1500 rpm. The results were obtained at four loading conditions: quarter, quasi, three-fourths, and full load.16,28 The air intake manifold was equipped with a Chamber 1 (Ch-1), which allowed temperature control as per requirements.

Each test was run under ambient and enhanced conditions. The inaccuracies of the values obtained were ensured by making the engine run under ambient air conditions before each load was applied using conventional fuel. Emission values were measured using the AVL gas analyzer and performance output was noted.2,3,10,29 The results were obtained for conventional fuel, AN biofuel, along with AN (70 + 30) and AN (80 + 30) fuels, including temperature increase, after the best fuel combination was obtained. The entire setup was monitored by using a computer. The setup and arrangement are shown in Figure 3. The uncertainties of the values are shown in Table 2.

In our experimentation, the performance of the engine was determined using parameters such as brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and brake-specific energy consumption (BSEC), with brake power (BP) being produced by the engine. Emission Characteristics

Engine performance is significantly influenced by the production of pollutants, and engine emissions consist of CO, NOx, HC, CO2, and smoke. The emission characteristics of the test fuel have been discussed in the following sections.

HC vs BP

The combustion quality of an engine can be summarized based on the formation of HC in the engine exhaust gas. The emission of HC depends on the homogeneity of the air–fuel mixture and the chemical kinetics of the fuel.6 Under cold start conditions, the HC emissions significantly increase compared to the ambient atmosphere, with temperature playing a vital role.17Figure 9 depicts the formation of HC under different load conditions with D100, AN100, AN80+D20, and AN70+D30 as well as air temperatures of 35, 40, and 45 °C for AN80+D20.

BP vs HC discharge.

Under the initial loading conditions, conventional fuel usage, represented by D100, AN100, AN80+D20, and AN70+D30, showed higher formation of HC experimentally compared to AN80+D20 at 35, 40, and 45 °C air temperatures. However, HC formation was significantly decreased when the AN80+D20 fuel was used with 45 °C air induction. This was because of the elevated air temperature during intake. The fuel–air mixture gets heated, enhancing the temperature of the combustion air–fuel mixture. As the ignition point of D20 is 60 °C when atomized, quickly combusts, reducing the possibility of the cold air–fuel mixture that could quench the combustion process.5 The formation of HC across all the loading conditions, including the half-, three-fourth-, and full-load conditions, was consistent (Figure 9). While conclusively observing all the formations of HC, it can be said that the increase in air temperature contributes to decreased formation of HC.

CO vs BP

The emission mechanics of CO rely on dynamics such as incomplete combustion, insufficiency of oxygen, and a cooled air–fuel mixture. Under cold loading conditions, the in-cylinder air–fuel charge produces a cooling effect, which, in turn, induces CO formation. Figure 10 shows the values of CO emitted under various loading conditions for the fuel mixture along with the increase in air temperature for AN80+D20 at temperatures of 35, 40, and 45 °C. From the observations on the emission of CO during the initial loading condition, it is found that the formation of CO is higher for all the fuel mixtures, including elevated temperature air–fuel mixtures. However, the formation of CO for the 45 °C air intake conditions was reduced compared to other fuels under initial loading conditions. The same trends of emission were observed during the half load, three-fourth load, and full-loading conditions. The reduced formation of CO can be attributed to the elevated temperature inducted by air within the combustion process. This prevents the cooling effect of the air–fuel mixture, which in turn leads to a reduction in the quenchable effect and an increase in combustibility. The variation in CO emission33 is summarized in Figure 10. It could be summarized that increasing the air intake temperature reduces the formation of CO by decreasing the incombustibility of the air–fuel charge.28

NOx vs BP

When the combustion chamber reaches an extremely high temperature, the molecules of nitrogen, which are balanced as diatomic, become monatomic. In brief, the outer electron orbit in the nitrogen molecule becomes a valence electron, which swiftly reacts with oxygen to produce NO, NO2′ and various oxides of nitrogen, denoted as NOx, where x indicates the number of oxygenated molecules. The chemical mechanisms responsible for NOx formation are categorized as prompt, thermal, and fuel-related. The rapid reaction of HC, nitrogen, and oxygen at excessively elevated temperatures forms prompt NOx. On the other hand, thermal NOx is formed by the reaction of oxygen at excessively high combustion chamber temperature with nitrogen present in the fuel–air mixture.4Figure 11 shows the emission of NOx for different fuel mixtures, including AN80+D20 fuel, at 35, 40, and 45 °C. The observation indicates that the emissions of NOx for all the fuels are high, including AN100. This is because of the increased intake air temperature inducted in the combustion chamber, which in turn elevates the combustion chamber temperature and promotes the formation of thermal NOx. However, the formation of NOx decreased for AN80+D20 fuel at 35 °C under full-load conditions compared to diesel. This was attributed to the lack of oxygen molecules due to increased air intake temperature, which expands the air and makes the charge nonreactive with monatomic nitrogen despite the rise in the combustion chamber temperature. The variation in the NOx emission is shown in Figure 11. In conclusion, an increase in the air temperature can induce NOxformation, but the controlled air temperature may mitigate the reactivity of monatomic nitrogen without oxygen.2

Smoke vs BP

Smoke is formed during incomplete combustion of the fuel–air mixture due to the lack of oxygen and the presence of a fuel-rich zone inside the combustion chamber. Figure 12 shows the amount of smoke emitted for different fuel mixture variants as well as AN80+D20 under elevated air temperatures of 35, 40, and 45 °C. During the initial loading condition, the emission of smoke is higher for fuel mixtures D100, AN100, AN80+D20, and AN70+D30 compared to elevated air temperature levels of AN80+D20 fuel with air temperatures of 35, 40, and 45 °C. This is due to the nonavailability of the fuel–air mixture, which stagnates within the combustion chamber and emits smoke during the next exhaust cycle. However, the emission of smoke during the final loading condition gradually increases with an increase in the engine load. When the engine load is high, the mechanical propulsion of the engine is reduced, stagnating the combusted air–fuel mixture inside the combustion chamber. As a result, a rich fuel mixture is formed, which, in turn, increases the smoke emission. The variations in smoke emission are summarized in Figure 12. However, in this observation, AN80+D20 with 35 °C air shows reduced smoke emission when compared to the remaining fuels tested. This is due to the ignition timing of the air–fuel mixture, which perfectly combusts the air–fuel mixture, leaving significantly fewer fuel-rich zones inside the combustion chamber compared to the other fuel combinations.35

CO2 vs BP

CO2 emissions in a diesel engine are due to the reactivity and availability of carbon molecules within the fuel. When an HC fuel is completely combusted, the remaining carbon reacts with the oxygen present in the fuel–air mixture, leading to the production of CO2. Also, hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water (H2O). When the amount of fuel combusted increases, CO2 emission also increases. Figure 13 shows the amount of CO2 emitted for fuel mixtures, including AN80+D20, under elevated air temperature conditions. Under the initial loading conditions, the fuel–air mixture with increased oxygen levels emits more CO2 when compared to diesel. This suggests that the presence of oxygen in AN100, AN80, and AN70 generates more CO2 compared to D100, which lacks an oxygen molecule. However, when the engine is loaded during half load, three-quarter load, or full-load conditions, the CO2 emission increases gradually for all fuels tested. This is because of the higher availability of air inside the combustion chamber.16 Under full-load conditions, the availability of oxygen in the air, besides the oxygen in biofuel, leads to higher CO2 emission compared to D100. In conclusion, the observations of CO2 emission show that the increase in air temperature input for AN80+B20 fuel at 35, 40, and 45 °C leads to elevated CO2 emission. However, at an air input temperature of 35 °C, AN80+B20 fuel shows reduced CO2 emission when compared to the remaining settings (40 and 45 °C). Sustainability Assessment Study

In modern automotive engineering, the use of alternative fuels and the impact of engine modification play a vital role in research. Standardization of sustainability is used as a tool to categorize automotive research as a case study to identify the possible parameters influencing emission and engine modification. Exposure of carbon into the atmosphere is a very important aspect in many cosmopolitan cities of developed countries. Extraction and availability of alternative fuels influence the entire lifecycle of vehicles, and any engine modification provides integrity to any research work.

An engine operating under ideal conditions will show improved performance and reduced emissions. However, operating an engine with increased performance and reduced emission is almost unachievable, depending on its operating conditions, fuel usage, engine modification, and environmental factors, including the temperature and amount of moisture in the air. The optimization of automotive research focuses on identifying possible operating conditions that impact emission values but with no or reduced consideration toward cost and ecological impact of the fuel.1 The development of a characterization matrix based on cost and ecological impact is very much needed when considering a fuel for research.

The Pugh Matrix is a decision-making tool used to select parameters and identify viable solutions among various parameters used by using a characterization matrix.36 In this technique, a numerical weightage is provided for all of the considered variables, ranging from −2 to +2, based on their importance. The selected variables are ranked from exceptional to outshining scores.37 A step-by-step procedure to allocate and identify the weightage of the score using this technique is shown in Figure 14. The highest score signifies the most critical variable, assigned a score of +2, while the least significant variable is designated with −2. Finally, the total score is calculated by summing up the values of all of the sustainable variables and is depicted in Figure 15 using a kiviat plot. The highest totaled value is considered the best trategy. Table 6 summarizes the Pugh Matrix with all the sustainable variables and their scores.

Environmental Impact

Engines operating under various conditions pollute the environment. Although minor amounts of pollutants are released in the atmosphere, it must be considered when studying the environmental impact. Therefore, a weightage score of 2W was given; based on the experimental values and exposure of carbon in the atmosphere, a score of −2 was provided for conventional D100 fuel. When the AN100 fuel was used in the experimental engine, a score of −1 was assigned because of the presence of oxygen in the fuel, which increased the combustion and reduced the amount of carbon exposure in the atmosphere. Scores of 1 and −1 are assigned based on the experimental values of carbon emission for AN80D20 and AN70D30. However, scores of 2, 1, and 1 are assigned to AN80D20 fuels at 35, 40, and 45 °C, respectively. This is because these blends reduce the carbon exposure values. However, at 40 and 45 °C, nitrogen oxide emissions are higher compared to the remaining fuels, excluding diesel.

Performance Responses

Brake Thermal Efficiency

The BTE of an engine reflects an engine’s ability to use fuel efficiently and is based on the chemical kinetics of the engine. Higher BTE indicates better utilization of the fuel and less carbon emission into the atmosphere. Fuel properties, such as the cetane number and calorific value, impact the fuel’s chemical properties, influencing the engine’s BTE. Hence, the 2W was assigned. Based on the experimental data that show variations in the values of the BTE of all the fuels, the scores assigned were −2, −1, 1, −2, 2, 1, and 1 were assigned for D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C, respectively.

Brake-Specific Fuel Consumptions

Brake-specific fuel consumption in the engine under various load conditions is determined by the amount of fuel consumed by the engine based on the fuel calorific value and cetane number. The chemical properties of the fuel provide energy for propelling the engine. the engine governor supports the fuel injection system by injecting the required amount of energy from the fuel to propel the engine. The energy required for the fuel to propel the engine is regulated by a governor-controlled injection system. Hence, a weight of 2W is assigned. Based on the experimental observation of the BSFC values, scores of 2, −1, −2, −2, 1, 1, and −1 are assigned for D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, and AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C, respectively.

Emission Responses

Emission from an engine determines the combustion as well as the performance of the engine. Higher HC values in an engine indicate a reduced combustion. Similarly, CO values also indicate incomplete combustion, similar to HC. Also, nitrogen oxide emission is higher when the combustion chamber temperature exceeds 1500 °C. This occurs when more fuel is combusted within the combustion chamber, leading to a higher temperature. Hence, excess oxygen in the emitted gases reacts, oxygenates with nitrogen, and forms nitrogen oxide emissions. All of the emissions are significant because of their harmfulness to the atmosphere. Hence, an equal weightage of 2W was assigned to all of the values.

HC Emission

HC emission is one of the most important emissions produced by IC engines. They release carbon into the atmosphere and increase carbon footprints in the environment. Based on the experimental observations, the scores for HC provided are −2, −2, 1, −2, −1, 1, and 2 for D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, and AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C, respectively.

CO Emission

Similar to HCs, CO emissions are also important as they can have severe health effects, including brain death, when exposed to high concentrations. Primary health effects of CO emission include irritation to the eyes and lung function disorders. The scores of the decision matrix, based on the experimental values of CO, were −1, −2, −1, 1, −1, −2, and −2 for D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, and AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C, respectively.

Oxides of Nitrogen Emission

The reaction of oxygen with nitrogen at a very high temperature produces NOx. This phenomenon is observed in the combustion chamber when fuel has a high calorific value, and the cetane number of the fuel is higher. The scores assigned in the decision matrix are based on experimental observation. Specifically, scores of 2, 1, −1, 1, −1, 1, −1, −2, and −2 are allocated to D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, and AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C, respectively.

Smoke Emission

Smoke emitted from an engine is based on the air and fuel ratios within the combustion chamber. Any imbalance in the air-to-fuel ratio influences the emission of smoke from the tailpipe. A lean fuel ratio increases the emission of HC and CO while a rich mixture can lead to increased smoke emission. The scores assigned in the decision matrix are based on the experimental values of smoke obtained from the engine. Sequentially, scores of 1, −2, −1, −2, 1, 2, and 2 were allocated to D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C.

CO2 Emission

CO2 emission in an engine is based on the combustion completion of the air–fuel mixture. When fuel is completely combusted, the tailpipe emits more CO2, but when the combustion is incomplete, it emits less CO2. CO2 is one of the greenhouse gases. When higher amounts of CO2 are emitted, it depletes the ozone layer and results in global warming. When compared to all of the emissions, CO2 emission is less harmful, so the emission of CO2 in an engine is advantageous. The scores assigned for CO2 emission are based on the experimental values obtained. A sequential score of 1, −2, −1, −2, 1, 2, and 2 is provided for D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, and AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C.

Cost of Fuel

When the cost of the fuel is considered, it becomes clear that the use of conventional diesel fuel is significantly cost-effective when compared to the biofuel used in the experimentation.38−40 Therefore, based on the conventional fuel cost, the scores were provided. The scores were sequentially allocated and are as follows: 1, −1, −1, −1, −1, −1, −1, and −1 for D100, AN100, AN80D20, AN70D30, and AN80D20 at 35 °C, AN80D20 at 40 °C, and AN80D20 at 45 °C.

When consolidating all the scores obtained for sustainable assessment parameters, a maximum score of 6 was obtained for AN80D20 fuel with preheated air up to 35 °C. This indicates that this fuel is more advantageous than any other fuel combination at different temperatures. The conclusive values of sustainability parameters are shown in Figure 16. So, the experimental and decision matrix case study proves that AN80D20 fuel with preheated air up to 35 °C is preferable to any other fuel combination and temperatures.

Conclusions

The examination validates the contribution of biofuel research to sustainability through the use of a PCCI engine operating in dual-fuel mode, a novel high-temperature combustion setup, the use of a novel AN fuel, and a novel approach to sustainability evaluation.

In this study, the effect of a novel higher-temperature air intake setup is studied in a PCCI engine using a novel AN fuel in combination with AN70+D30, AN80+D20, AN100, and D100. We evaluated the fuels individually, with increased air temperature intake settings at 35, 40, and 45 °C while focusing on the AN80+D20 fuel combination.
