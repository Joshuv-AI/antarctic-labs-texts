Overlapping risk factors and pathogenic mechanisms in lung cancer and cardiovascular disease
Authors: Yan Liu, Yifei Li, Haiyang Ning, Yujia Xi, Yifan Li, Jinfeng Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are leading global causes of mortality, with growing evidence suggesting a complex interplay between the two. While traditionally viewed as distinct conditions, shared risk factors and intrinsic mechanisms, particularly inflammation, indicate potential commonalities. This article provides a comprehensive analysis of the roles of endothelial cell activation, the mononuclear phagocyte system, and cytokines in the pathogenesis of both diseases, highlighting the central role of inflammatory pathways. Additionally, we explore the contributions of non-inflammatory factors, such as metabolic dysregulation and coagulation abnormalities, to disease progression. By identifying shared mechanisms, we propose novel preventive and therapeutic targets that could simultaneously address both lung cancer and CVDs. This review aims to deepen the understanding of the intricate relationship between these diseases and provide a foundation for future research and clinical strategies aimed at improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Lung cancer, Cardiovascular disease, Inflammation, Glucose reprogramming, Clonal Hematopoiesis

Introduction

Lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are leading causes of global mortality, accounting for a significant proportion of premature deaths worldwide. According to the 2022 global disease statistics, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) accounted for the highest proportion of disease-related deaths (40%), followed by cancer (30%) [1]. Regarding cancer-specific data, lung cancer ranked first in both incidence and mortality: it represented 12.4% of total global cancer cases (2,480,301 new cases) and contributed to 18.7% of all cancer-related deaths (1,817,172 deaths) [2, 3]. While traditionally considered distinct entities, emerging evidence suggests a complex interplay between these two diseases, driven by shared risk factors such as smoking, air pollution, and unhealthy dietary habits. These factors contribute to disease pathogenesis through common biological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [4–6]. Among these, inflammation has been identified as a central player, linking the progression of both lung cancer and CVDs [7, 8].

Recent studies have highlighted a bidirectional association between lung cancer and CVDs, particularly in smokers. For instance, Mendelian randomization analyses have demonstrated an increased risk of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) among individuals with cardiovascular disease, with this association being most pronounced in current and former smokers [9]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this relationship remain poorly understood, particularly regarding the roles of non-inflammatory factors such as lipid metabolism, coagulation dysfunction, and hormonal changes. While inflammation has been extensively studied, the contributions of these non-inflammatory pathways to the co-occurrence of lung cancer and CVDs have yet to be fully elucidated.

Lung cancer and CVDs share overlapping risk factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, unhealthy diet, and air pollution, which contribute to disease pathogenesis through interrelated biological mechanisms such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Among these, inflammation plays a central role, driving both atherosclerosis and lung cancer progression by promoting endothelial damage, cholesterol deposition, and plaque formation, ultimately increasing the risk of cardiovascular events. Similarly, within the tumor microenvironment, inflammatory responses facilitate cancer development and may serve as potential diagnostic markers. Beyond inflammation, other shared pathways—such as coagulation dysfunction, dysregulated tumor metabolism, and clonal hematopoiesis—further link these diseases by enhancing tumor proliferation and metastasis while concurrently elevating cardiovascular risk. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms could unveil novel therapeutic targets, offering dual benefits in the prevention and treatment of both lung cancer and CVDs. Our findings may offer a scientific foundation for understanding the intricate relationship between lung cancer and CVDs, while providing theoretical support for future prevention and treatment strategies.

Risk factors

Smoking

Smoking is a major risk factor for both lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). The toxic components (nicotine et al.) of tobacco smoke induce DNA damage and chronic inflammation, leading to tissue destruction in the lungs [10]. Long-term smoking promotes the infiltration of inflammatory cells into lung tissues, resulting in matrix destruction, inadequate blood supply, and epithelial cell death, which may progress to pneumonitis and even malignancy [11]. Moreover, smoking significantly disrupts systemic and intrarenal hemodynamic homeostasis. Nicotine-induced elevation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels contributes to vascular endothelial dysfunction, promoting atherosclerotic plaque formation, platelet activation, and intrarenal hemodynamic disturbances [12]. These mechanisms collectively elevate the risk of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction [13, 14].

Continuing to smoke after a lung cancer diagnosis is associated with poor prognosis, reduced survival rates, and an increased risk of cancer recurrence. Smoking also exacerbates the quality of life for patients and increases the incidence of adverse drug reactions during treatment [15]. Importantly, smoking cessation has been shown to significantly reduce these risks. A Korean study involving over 300,000 cancer patients who had never experienced a heart attack or stroke evaluated the risk of cardiovascular events in four groups: never smokers, quitters, current smokers, and relapsers, as well as those who started smoking. Compared to never smokers, continuous smokers, starters or relapsers, and quitters had 86%, 51%, and 20% higher risk of cardiovascular events, respectively. Quitting smoking showed significant benefits compared to continuing smoking, with 57% of individuals quitting smoking after a cancer diagnosis experiencing a 36% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular events [16]. Furthermore, smoking cessation increases levels of indirect bilirubin, an endogenous antioxidant that may further protect against both lung cancer and CVDs [17].

These findings highlight the dual role of smoking in promoting both lung cancer and CVDs through shared mechanisms such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Smoking cessation interventions are therefore critical not only for reducing the risk of these diseases but also for improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

Alcohol consumption

Alcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for both cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). As a human carcinogen, alcohol promotes the production of reactive oxygen species, leading to DNA damage and impaired nutrient metabolism [18]. Even low levels of alcohol intake have been shown to increase cancer risk, with a clear dose–response relationship observed in epidemiological studies [19]. In an attribution analysis of 30 types of cancer, researchers examined a variety of modifiable risk factors, including smoking, exposure to secondhand smoke, overweight and obesity, alcohol consumption, red and processed meat intake, insufficient fruit and vegetable consumption, inadequate dietary fiber and calcium intake, physical inactivity, ultraviolet radiation, and seven carcinogenic infections. The analysis revealed that smoking is the leading risk factor for cancer cases and cancer-related deaths, followed closely by overweight and alcohol consumption. Notably, lung cancer has the highest number of cases and deaths attributed to these assessable risk factors among all types of cancer [20].

The impact of alcohol consumption on the cardiovascular system is intricate and multifaceted, presenting a paradoxical nature that contrasts with the direct association with cancer. Research indicates that, in moderation, wine is associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular mortality compared to beer or spirits [21]. However, this association is influenced by a variety of confounding factors, including lifestyle, genetic background, and socioeconomic status. When considering the optimization of lifestyle and the improvement of healthy habits, the potential benefits of moderate alcohol consumption on the cardiovascular system may be diminished. Genetic epidemiological studies further reveal that any amount of alcohol consumption is associated with an increased cardiovascular risk. Nevertheless, there are significant risk differences between various levels of alcohol intake, indicating that the relationship between alcohol and cardiovascular health is not a simple linear one [22, 23]. The scientific statement from the American Heart Association is: “If you do not drink alcohol, do not start; if you choose to drink alcohol, limit intake.” [24] Therefore, while moderate alcohol consumption may be correlated with a lower risk of cardiovascular diseases in certain contexts, this relationship is not stable and is subject to multiple influences. When assessing the potential impact of alcohol on cardiovascular health, it is essential to take into account an individual’s lifestyle, genetic predispositions, and socioeconomic background. These complex interactions suggest that a more nuanced and personalized approach is necessary when discussing the relationship between alcohol consumption and cardiovascular health.

Unhealthy diet

The association between unhealthy diet and the increased risk of lung cancer is well-supported by extensive scientific research. WHO/IARC has classified processed meat as a Group 1 carcinogen. Diets high in processed meats, red meats, and salt, combined with low intake of fruits, vegetables, and dietary fiber, promote chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, which are key mechanisms in the development of lung cancer [25]. Low physical activity, often associated with poor dietary habits, further exacerbates these risks. Notably, processed and red meats contain carcinogenic compounds ((such as N-nitroso compounds, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, etc.) that directly contribute to lung cancer progression, while the lack of antioxidants from fruits and vegetables reduces the body's ability to counteract oxidative stress [25, 26].

The impact of unhealthy diets on CVDs is equally profound. The Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study (https://www2.phri.ca/pure/), involving 147,642 participants from 21 countries, found that diets high in salt, saturated fats, and sugar significantly increase the risk of CVDs by promoting metabolic syndromes such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. These dietary patterns also trigger chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are shared mechanisms in both CVDs and lung cancer. Interestingly, the PURE study highlighted regional disparities, with low-income countries experiencing a higher burden of diet-related diseases due to limited access to healthy foods.

These findings underscore the global impact of unhealthy diets on both lung cancer and CVDs. Adopting healthier dietary patterns—such as increasing the intake of fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, and fish while reducing processed and high-sugar foods—could significantly reduce the risk of these diseases. Public health interventions should prioritize improving diet quality, particularly in low-income regions, to mitigate the dual burden of cancer and cardiovascular diseases.

Air pollution

Zhu et al. conducted an analysis of 33,903 mortality cases over a 14-year follow-up period, which included 17,083 cancer-related deaths (with 2,835 attributed to lung cancer and 14,248 to non-lung cancer), 6,970 deaths due to cardiovascular diseases (CVD), 2,429 due to respiratory diseases, and 1,287 due to digestive system diseases. The study found that for each interquartile range (IQR) increase in ambient concentrations of PM2.5, NO2, and NOx, the risk of all-cause mortality significantly rose by 7%, 6%, and 5%, respectively [27]. Short-term exposure to particulate matter PM2.5, PM10, and NOx is consistently associated with an increased risk of hypertension and of triggering myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke (both fatal and non-fatal). Long-term exposure to PM2.5 is primarily linked to an elevated risk of atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, hypertension, as well as an increased risk of stroke and stroke mortality [28]. The impacts may be partly attributed to oxidative stress, inflammation, and the activation of the autonomic nervous system [28]. These findings hignlight the dose–response relationship between air pollution and mortality, emphasizing the need for stricter air quality regulations. Reducing exposure to air pollutants could significantly lower the burden of both lung cancer and CVDs, particularly in urban areas with high pollution levels.

Inflammation

Vascular endothelial cell

Endothelial cells play a critical role in maintaining vascular homeostasis by regulating vasodilation, preventing clot formation, and balancing coagulation and fibrinolysis [29]. However, when activated by risk factors such as hypertension, high cholesterol, and smoking, endothelial cells promote immune imbalance, excessive inflammation, and fibrosis [30], contributing to the development of both CVDs and cancer [31]. In CVDs, endothelial dysfunction accelerates atherosclerosis by increasing platelet aggregation and coagulation, leading to thrombotic events such as myocardial infarction [30]. In cancer, activated endothelial cells facilitate tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion through mechanisms such as angiogenesis and the secretion of pro-tumor factors [32].

Angiogenesis, a key process in both CVDs and cancer, is regulated by factors such as placental growth factor (PlGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor A-D (VEGF-A-D) (Fig. 1). While PlGF inhibition has shown potential in reducing tumor growth [33], it may also increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular events [34], highlighting the complex interplay between these diseases. Additionally, the endothelial activation and stress index (EASIX) has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for poor survival in small cell lung cancer (SCLC), further underscoring the role of endothelial dysfunction in cancer progression [35].

Targeting endothelial cells to simultaneously address lung cancer and CVDs presents both opportunities and challenges. Modulating endothelial cell activity through drug interventions or gene regulation could influence vascular function, inflammation, and angiogenesis, potentially slowing disease progression in both conditions. Currently, targeting tumor angiogenesis is mainly accomplished through two principal strategies: monoclonal antibodies designed to prevent the interaction between VEGF and its receptor (VEGFR), and small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that suppress the downstream signaling pathways activated by VEGFR. These therapeutic approaches have contributed to enhanced overall survival rates among individuals with lung cancer [36]. However, the dual role of endothelial cells in promoting and suppressing disease processes necessitates further research to develop precise and effective therapeutic strategies.

Lipid molecules contribute to angiogenesis by promoting the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells. The VEGF family synergistically enhances angiogenesis through both intravascular and intratumoral mechanisms, while simultaneously inducing increased expression of inflammatory factors. These inflammatory factors further recruit monocyte-macrophages, which, in turn, amplify the release of inflammatory factors through a positive feedback loop, ultimately establishing a pro-angiogenic inflammatory microenvironment.

Circulating monocyte-macrophages in the peripheral blood circulation

Monocytes, key components of the innate immune system, are a heterogeneous population with distinct subsets that play critical roles in immune regulation. Based on surface markers CD14 and CD16, monocytes can be classified into classical, intermediate, and non-classical subsets. In lung cancer, classical and intermediate monocytes are more abundant than in healthy individuals, with classical monocytes promoting tumor progression through angiogenesis, immune suppression, and extracellular matrix remodeling [37]. Intermediate monocytes, when activated by tumor cells, enhance the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-12 while reducing anti-inflammatory IL-10, contributing to a pro-tumor microenvironment [38, 39]. Non-classical monocytes, on the other hand, inhibit tumor metastasis by activating NK cells through FOXO1 and IL-27 [40]. CCR2 is a chemokine receptor. Histone deacetylase (HDAC1-3) inhibitors downregulate CCR2 expression, which mediates the migration of monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) into tumor tissues. Concurrently, these inhibitors attenuate the immunosuppressive activity of M-MDSCs, thereby reversing immune evasion in the tumor microenvironment [41]. In cardiovascular diseases, intermediate monocytes are associated with increased risk of events such as stroke, particularly under inflammatory conditions [38, 39].

Macrophages, present in all tissues, are primarily established prenatally but can also be replenished by adult monocytes. In lung adenocarcinoma, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) derived from monocytes promote tumor growth and are linked to poor prognosis (Table 1) [42]. These macrophages, regulated by the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) receptor, contribute to tumor progression by remodeling the tumor microenvironment [43]. Zubair Hussain et al. developed a novel approach utilizing dual-targeting ZrMOF/C@P nanoparticles that activate the STING pathway in M2-like macrophages and reprogram them into an M1-like phenotype. This intervention resulted in decreased M2-like macrophage populations with concomitant increase in M1-like macrophages, enhanced dendritic cell (DC) maturation, and increased tumor infiltration of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells [44]. The treatment not only suppressed primary tumor growth but also stimulated a systemic immune response. Similarly, in cardiovascular diseases, macrophages play a dual role. In atherosclerosis, they respond to inflammatory stimuli, leading to plaque formation [45], while in acute myocardial infarction (AMI), they contribute to ventricular remodeling [46]. Shifting macrophage phenotypes from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 has shown promise in protecting against adverse cardiac remodeling post-AMI [47]. Chitosan oligosaccharides (COS) attenuate atherosclerotic foam cell formation through dual modulation of cholesterol homeostasis: suppressing cholesterol influx via downregulation of scavenger receptor class A1 (SR-A1) and cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) expression, and promoting cholesterol efflux via upregulation of ATP-binding cassette transporters ABCA-1 and ABCG-1 [48].

Regulating intermediate monocytes and macrophages may open up new therapeutic avenues, potentially preventing and treating these diseases by intervening with intermediate monocytes to modulate inflammatory responses, inhibit angiogenesis, or enhance immune surveillance. Oncologists are attempting to develop a drug that enhances the "appetite" of macrophages. This medication is not only expected to combat cancer but may also be effective against cardiovascular diseases.

Inflammatory cell

The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), proposed by Hu et al., integrates neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts to assess systemic inflammation and immune status [67]. Elevated neutrophil counts are strongly associated with increased mortality in cardiovascular diseases [68], particularly in conditions like atherosclerosis, where neutrophils promote plaque formation through the secretion of myeloperoxidase (MPO) and oxidation of low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL). However, neutrophils can also play a protective role by eliminating plaques through endothelial cell shedding, a process known as plaque erosion. Additionally, a specific subset of neutrophils expressing CXCR4highVEGFR + CD49d + has been shown to promote vascular repair, highlighting their dual role in cardiovascular health [69].

In cancer, neutrophils exhibit a similarly complex role. Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) secrete cytokines and proteases such as MMP-9, creating a microenvironment that facilitates tumor metastasis [70]. Conversely, anti-tumor neutrophils can enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy by expressing interferon genes (e.g., IRF1) and interacting with dendritic cells and cytokines like IL-12 and IFNγ. For example, in lung adenocarcinoma models, successful immunotherapy is often accompanied by a significant accumulation of neutrophils in tumor tissues, suggesting their critical role in anti-tumor immunity [71]. Furthermore, T cell immunotherapy has been shown to activate neutrophils, leading to secondary tumor-killing effects even in the absence of target antigen expression [72].

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has emerged as a valuable biomarker for assessing the risk and severity of acute coronary syndromes (ACS) and coronary artery disease (CAD) [73, 74]. In clinical practice, lymphocyte subset analysis is widely used to monitor immune function and guide treatment responses in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [75, 76]. These findings highlight the importance of immune cell dynamics in cardiovascular diseases.

T cells and B cells play complex and dual roles in both lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases. In lung cancer, T and B lymphocytes are enriched in tumor tissues compared to normal lung tissues, indicating the activation of adaptive immune responses [77]. However, the Th1 immune response, while protective against tumors, can exacerbate cardiovascular inflammation and promote atherosclerosis [78]. In contrast, regulatory T cells (Treg) exert anti-atherosclerotic effects by secreting IL-10 and TGFβ, which suppress pro-inflammatory T cell activity [79]. B cells also exhibit dual roles in vascular diseases. While they contribute to the pathogenesis of conditions such as vasculitis, arterial injury, hypertension, and atherosclerosis, certain B cell subsets may have protective effects [80–83]. For example, B cells can promote inflammation through antibody production and cytokine secretion, but they also play a role in immune regulation and tissue repair (Fig. 2) [84]. These insights into the roles of T cells and B cells in lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases underscore the need for targeted therapies that modulate immune responses. Future research should focus on understanding the context-dependent functions of these cells to develop more effective treatment strategies for both diseases.

Disordered lipid metabolism

Lipids are essential components of cells and tissues, and abnormalities in lipid metabolism can lead to pathological changes in the body. Regulating lipid metabolism may be the starting point for improving or curing lipid metabolism-related diseases. Wang et al. conducted single-cell RNA sequencing on different early-stage lung cancers and found widespread dysregulation of lipid metabolism in different cell types, with glycerophospholipid metabolism being the most significantly altered pathway [85]. Analysis of lipid metabolism-related genes in early-stage LUAD identified 12 predictive gene features associated with recurrence and survival, revealing potential mechanisms involving lipid metabolism, inflammation, and immune pathways for their prognostic value [86]. Salvador et al. also demonstrated that alterations in lipid metabolism are independent molecular variables for the prognosis of lung cancer patients, providing additional information for the prognosis of lung cancer patients [87]. Additionally, calorie restriction and ketogenic diets have been shown to inhibit tumor growth by reducing lipid availability and altering metabolic pathways [88].

In cardiovascular diseases, lipid metabolism plays a central role in the development of atherosclerosis. High levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) promote plaque formation by inducing inflammatory responses in vascular cells, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and macrophages. This inflammatory process not only accelerates atherosclerosis but also increases the risk of thrombotic events, such as myocardial infarction and stroke [89].

Adipokines are a class of biologically active polypeptides secreted by adipocytes that play crucial regulatory roles in various physiological processes. At the systemic level, adipokines modulate the biological functions of organs such as the heart and liver through complex signaling networks, while also participating in immune responses, inflammatory signaling, and myocardial contractility [90]. Based on their functional properties, adipokines can be broadly categorized into pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory subtypes: pro-inflammatory adipokines (e.g., TNF-α, leptin, and visfatin) are closely associated with inflammatory responses, whereas anti-inflammatory adipokines (e.g., adiponectin, CTRP family proteins, and omentin-1) exhibit anti-inflammatory and protective effects [91]. Visfatin, primarily derived from macrophages in visceral adipose tissue, promotes the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and upregulates the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), matrix metalloproteinases, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), thereby activating key signaling pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K [91]. Clinical studies have demonstrated elevated serum visfatin levels in patients with atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease (CAD) [92], suggesting its potential role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases. The CTRP (C1q/TNF-related protein) family represents another group of adipocyte-derived peptide adipokines predominantly secreted by adipose tissue. Research indicates that CTRP proteins possess significant anti-inflammatory and vasoprotective properties. Their mechanisms of action include attenuating Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated inflammatory signaling, suppressing lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced pro-inflammatory responses [93], and enhancing endothelium-dependent vasodilation in aortic rings [94]. These biological characteristics highlight the critical role of CTRP family proteins in modulating inflammatory responses and maintaining vascular homeostasis. leptin and adiponectin, further link lipid metabolism to both lung cancer and CVDs. Leptin promotes tumor growth and metastasis by activating the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) program, while adiponectin exerts anti-tumor effects by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways [95]. In cardiovascular health, leptin contributes to obesity-related hypertension, whereas adiponectin protects against atherosclerosis by reducing endothelial inflammation [96]. However, the "adiponectin paradox," where both low and high levels of adiponectin can have adverse effects, highlights the complexity of adipokine signaling in disease progression [97].

When people mention lipid metabolism, obesity often comes to mind, as obesity has been proven to be a risk factor for various diseases. Physical exercise and healthy diet naturally become focal points of attention. However, by focusing on lipid metabolism pathways and further examining key regulatory factors (such as leptin, adiponectin, resistin) and biological processes within these pathways, studying key enzymes, signaling pathways, and regulatory factors, novel drug targets and treatment strategies may be discovered, providing new insights and approaches for the prevention and treatment of diseases. Current advances in pharmacotherapy targeting the adipokine network have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential. Statins exert beneficial effects primarily through downregulating pro-inflammatory factors including IL-6, adipocyte-derived leptin, and TNF-α [98]. Fibrates exhibit multifaceted therapeutic actions by reducing visceral adipose tissue mass, decreasing serum TNF-α concentrations, promoting fatty acid β-oxidation, and elevating serum adiponectin levels [99]. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors have been shown to significantly improve adipokine profiles, while mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) antagonists demonstrate dual mechanisms by both stimulating adiponectin secretion and suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory adipokines [100]. These targeted therapeutic strategies provide novel options for the clinical management of metabolic disorders, although their precise mechanisms of action and long-term efficacy require further investigation and validation.

Cytokines

Cytokines are critical signaling molecules that regulate immune responses and inflammatory processes, playing a central role in both lung cancer and CVDs. Among these, IL-6 is particularly significant. Elevated serum IL-6 levels are associated with an increased risk of lung cancer and poor prognosis [101], while also contributing to immunotherapy resistance [102]. In cardiovascular health, IL-6 accelerates atherosclerosis by promoting inflammation and plaque formation [103].

IL-1β, another key cytokine, is released in response to environmental triggers such as PM2.5 exposure, promoting tumorigenesis in lung cells carrying EGFR mutations. In CVDs, IL-1β is linked to hypertension and endothelial dysfunction, with monocytes from hypertensive patients producing high levels of IL-1β under inflammatory conditions. Similarly, elevated levels of IL-8 and IL-17 are associated with both cancer progression and cardiovascular events, highlighting their dual role in disease pathogenesis [104–106].

C-reactive protein (CRP), a marker of low-grade inflammation, is a risk factor for both CVDs and lung cancer. Elevated CRP levels predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes and are associated with increased cancer risk, particularly in lung cancer subtypes such as adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma [107]. High-sensitivity CRP is also an independent predictor of heart failure in patients with cardiovascular disease [108, 109].

TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, promotes tumor growth by creating an inflammatory tumor microenvironment [110], while in the heart, it induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis and cardiac remodeling, potentially leading to heart failure [111]. Despite its detrimental effects, clinical trials of TNF-α inhibitors have shown mixed results, underscoring the complexity of targeting this cytokine in cardiovascular diseases [112, 113].

IFN-γ, another important cytokine, balances pro-tumor and anti-tumor immunity in the tumor microenvironment [114]. However, it can also contribute to hypertension by inducing angiotensinogen expression in renal cells, highlighting its dual role in cancer and cardiovascular health [115].

In 2017, "Canakinumab" emerged as a novel type of "anti-inflammatory drug." Originally developed to treat cardiovascular diseases, Canakinumab underwent a Phase III clinical trial involving 10,061 patients to verify its efficacy in managing cardiovascular conditions. The trial's conclusive results demonstrated that Canakinumab significantly reduced the risk of cardiovascular mortality. Remarkably, among patients treated with Canakinumab, the mortality rate from lung cancer plummeted by a staggering 77% [116]. Canakinumab is a targeted drug against IL-1β, capable of blocking the activity of IL-1β, thereby inhibiting the occurrence of inflammation [117]. The emergence of Canakinumab has opened up new avenues for research. Anti-inflammatory therapeutic interventions may simultaneously alter the outcomes of CVD events and lung cancer. Given that it can target and regulate IL-1β, it is plausible that other inflammatory factors can also be targeted to intervene in the progression of cardiovascular diseases and lung cancer.

Hypercoagulability

Hypercoagulability, characterized by enhanced coagulation, reduced anticoagulation, and impaired fibrinolysis, is a common feature in lung cancer patients and contributes to both disease progression and cardiovascular complications [118]. Tumor cells directly activate the coagulation cascade by producing procoagulants, inhibiting anticoagulants, and increasing platelet and monocyte activity [119, 120]. Elevated plasma fibrinogen levels, often observed in lung cancer patients, are associated with poor prognosis and reduced overall survival, although their exact role remains debated [121, 122]. Additionally, increased platelet counts have been identified as a predictive factor for high-risk progression in lung cancer, highlighting the critical role of platelets in tumor growth and metastasis [123, 124].

Pulmonary embolism (PE), a major cause of cardiovascular-related deaths, is particularly prevalent in lung cancer patients due to their hypercoagulable state. Approximately 50% of PE cases in these patients result from deep vein thrombosis (DVT), where thrombi dislodge and travel to the pulmonary arteries [125]. Tumor cells further exacerbate hypercoagulability by upregulating the coagulation cascade, secreting cytokines that activate endothelial cells and monocytes, and releasing fibrinolysis inhibitors [126]. These mechanisms not only promote thrombosis but also facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis by disrupting vascular integrity [127]. Targeting coagulation pathways, such as through anticoagulant therapies, may offer new therapeutic opportunities to reduce both thrombotic events and tumor progression. However, the complex interplay between coagulation and cancer biology necessitates further research to develop safe and effective treatment strategies.

Glucose reprogramming

In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) enables cancer cells to rely on glycolysis for rapid energy production even under oxygen-rich conditions, while suppressing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [128]. Similarly, macrophages and smooth muscle cells in atherosclerotic lesions exhibit "metabolic inflexibility"—characterized by heightened glucose uptake but impaired mitochondrial function, exacerbating inflammation and plaque instability [129]. This shared metabolic phenotype may involve the sustained activation of HIF-1α, which is upregulated in both the hypoxic tumor microenvironment (TME) [130] and ischemic vascular walls [131], further promoting the expression of glycolytic enzymes (e.g., HK2, PKM2) [132].

Beyond glycolysis, both diseases demonstrate common dysregulation in the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), the polyol pathway, and the pathological effects of lactate accumulation. The rate-limiting enzyme GFAT1 in the HBP mediates O-GlcNAcylation, which promotes cell survival and inflammation in both NSCLC metastasis [133] and atherosclerosis [134]. Under hyperglycemic conditions, aldose reductase (AR) activation leads to NADPH depletion and oxidative stress, aggravating tumor drug resistance and endothelial dysfunction [135, 136]. Additionally, both cancer cells and ischemic myocardium secrete excessive lactate, activating the GPR81 receptor to drive immunosuppression (e.g., T-cell exhaustion) and fibrosis (e.g., myocardial remodeling) [137, 138].

Competitive glucose consumption by NSCLC cells suppresses the function of tumor-infiltrating T cells, while hyperglycemia in atherosclerosis similarly impairs the immunomodulatory capacity of vascular endothelial cells. Single-cell metabolomics further reveals that lung cancer stem cells and atherosclerotic progenitor cells both depend on glutamine metabolism to maintain stemness [139, 140], suggesting that targeting metabolic dependencies may simultaneously eliminate treatment-resistant subpopulations in both diseases.

Therapeutic strategies targeting glucose metabolism (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors, HK2 inhibitors) show promise in both oncology and cardiovascular protection [141, 142]. For instance, metformin, by inhibiting mitochondrial complex I, can suppress NSCLC growth while ameliorating atherosclerosis. Future efforts should focus on developing tissue-selective delivery systems (e.g., pH-responsive nanoparticles) to minimize off-target effects of systemic metabolic modulation. However, current research faces limitations due to the imbalance in available data—rich single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) databases for tumors versus fragmented spatial metabolomics data for cardiovascular diseases—as well as the lack of technologies capable of capturing real-time metabolic interactions between the two diseases in the same patient. Moving forward, greater emphasis should be placed on constructing comprehensive spatial metabolomics databases for cardiovascular diseases, and the development of whole-body metabolic tracers (e.g., 13C-glucose PET-MRI) may offer a viable solution.

Hormonal changes

At the molecular pathological level, lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) exhibit striking similarities in hormonal metabolic regulation. Sex hormones demonstrate bidirectional regulatory effects in both disease entities: while estrogen promotes lung adenocarcinoma development via the ERβ/EGFR signaling axis [143], it simultaneously induces vascular endothelial dysfunction [144]. Conversely, testosterone suppresses lung cancer progression by enhancing CD8 + T cell-mediated antitumor immunity while improving lipid metabolism through LDL receptor modulation [145].

The thyroid hormone system similarly displays disease-specific regulatory patterns. Thyroid hormone receptor α1 (TRα1) drives tumor progression in lung squamous cell carcinoma by activating the HIF-1α-glycolysis pathway [146], whereas in myocardial tissue, it promotes pathological remodeling [147]. Notably, hypothyroidism, though potentially protective against lung cancer by sustaining tumor-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., let-7) [148], impairs cardiovascular function—a "metabolic trade-off" phenomenon underscoring the importance of tissue-specific hormonal responses.

Key intersections between the two diseases in hormonal metabolism include: Shared nuclear receptor pathways (e.g., ERα/TRα1); Common metabolic regulatory nodes (e.g., LDLR/HIF-1α); Analogous hormone-immune microenvironment crosstalk. Elucidating these shared mechanisms not only provides novel insights into disease pathogenesis but also establishes a foundation for developing dual-protective strategies targeting hormonal metabolic pathways (e.g., selective ER modulators or TRα1 antagonists). Future research should prioritize the development of tissue-specific hormone receptor animal models and explore circulating hormone levels as clinically actionable biomarkers for risk stratification.

Clonal hematopoiesis

The non-malignant clonal expansion of somatic mutations in the hematopoietic system is referred to as clonal hematopoiesis (CH). CH is a common age-related condition that has recently been characterized as a novel driver of cancer and CVD. The identification of CH in normally aging individuals has led to the concept of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), which is typically defined as CH occurring with somatic driver mutations associated with hematological malignancies (such as DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, JAK2), but does not meet the standard clinical pathological criteria for hematological malignancies. Carriers of CHIP have only a 0.5%-1% annual risk of transitioning to acute leukemia, yet it can increase the risk of CVD by 40% [149]. In addition, CH appears to have a relative risk similar to traditional risk factors for CVD. Experimental studies suggest that the link between cardiovascular diseases and CH is causal, with inflammation being one of the underlying mechanisms. Mutations associated with CHIP, such as DNMT3A, TET2, and JAK2, can activate inflammatory signaling pathways, such as NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes. This activation enhances the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators like IL-1β and IL-6, inducing local inflammatory responses and promoting the formation of atherosclerotic plaques [149, 150].

Researchers analyzed 415 NSCLC patients who underwent surgery and received adjuvant therapy from 2011 to 2017. Targeted deep sequencing of blood samples using an 89-gene panel was employed to assess the status of CH. A total of 86 patients (20.7%) were found to have clonal hematopoiesis (CH). The most frequently mutated gene was DNMT3A (33.0%), followed by ASXL1 (13.2%), TET2 (11.3%), and PPM1D (7.5%). These four genes accounted for 65.1% of all detected mutations. Patients with CH mutations had a worse overall survival (OS) compared to those without CH (hazard ratio [95% CI] = 1.56 [1.07–2.28], p = 0.020), and this significance persisted after multivariate analysis (1.58 [1.08–2.32], p = 0.019). Genes involved in DNA repair, epigenetics, and cell survival were affected by cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiation therapy, thereby promoting the clonal expansion advantage. From this perspective, it is speculated that preoperative CH could amplify a series of processes that induce adverse outcomes related to CH through cancer-related treatments, leading to a deterioration in the patient's survival condition [151].

Although CHIP is not listed as a routine test, it is an effective assessment method for healthy individuals with risk factors who have not yet developed a disease, as well as for those who have a disease but have not yet identified the specific cause. We recommend simultaneous testing of tumor tissue and peripheral blood DNA to distinguish whether the mutations originate from the tumor tissue or the peripheral blood, which can help determine the treatment strategy. We believe that genomic testing will play an important role in the screening and prevention of cancer and cardiovascular diseases in the future.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the shared mechanisms between lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), highlighting the critical role of inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation in the pathogenesis of both conditions. Our findings underscore the importance of understanding the interplay between these diseases, as they share common risk factors and biological pathways, which may offer novel therapeutic and preventive strategies.

Smoking, alcohol consumption, unhealthy diet, and air pollution are significant risk factors for both lung cancer and CVDs. These factors contribute to chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, which are central to the progression of both diseases. Our analysis confirms that smoking cessation, dietary modifications, and pollution control could significantly reduce the risk of both conditions.

Inflammation, particularly mediated by cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, plays a pivotal role in the progression of lung cancer and CVDs. Targeting these inflammatory pathways, as demonstrated by the success of canakinumab in reducing cardiovascular events and lung cancer mortality, offers a promising therapeutic avenue. Monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils exhibit complex roles in both diseases. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and intermediate monocytes promote tumor progression and cardiovascular inflammation, while certain subsets of immune cells, such as non-classical monocytes, may have protective effects. Modulating immune cell phenotypes, particularly shifting from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory states, could offer new therapeutic opportunities. Endothelial cell activation is a common feature in both lung cancer and CVDs, contributing to atherosclerosis in CVDs and tumor angiogenesis in cancer. The dual role of endothelial cells in promoting and suppressing disease processes presents both challenges and opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Drugs targeting angiogenesis, such as VEGF inhibitors, have shown efficacy in lung cancer but may also increase cardiovascular risks, highlighting the need for precise therapeutic strategies.

Dysregulated lipid and glucose metabolism are implicated in both lung cancer and CVDs. Lipid metabolism abnormalities, particularly involving adipokines like leptin and adiponectin, link obesity to both conditions. Similarly, the Warburg effect in cancer cells and glycolytic pathways in cardiovascular tissues suggest that targeting metabolic pathways could simultaneously address tumor growth and cardiovascular complications. Hypercoagulability, a common feature in lung cancer patients, contributes to both tumor progression and cardiovascular events such as pulmonary embolism. Hormonal changes, particularly involving sex hormones and thyroid hormones, also influence the risk and progression of both diseases, suggesting that hormonal therapies could be explored as part of a comprehensive treatment strategy.

Targeting shared signaling pathways in cardiovascular diseases and lung cancer (e.g., VEGF, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, JAK/STAT) holds therapeutic potential but presents significant clinical challenges. Foremost, cross-toxicity remains a critical concern, as exemplified by EGFR inhibitors suppressing lung cancer while potentially inducing myocardial fibrosis or arrhythmias [152], and anti-inflammatory JAK/STAT inhibitors potentially compromising anti-tumor immunity [153]. Off-target effects also lead to severe adverse events: VEGF inhibitors disrupt normal vascular function during tumor angiogenesis blockade, causing hypertension and thrombosis [154, 155], while PI3K inhibitors may interfere with cardiomyocyte survival signals, exacerbating heart failure [156]. Furthermore, these pathways may exert divergent or even opposing effects in the two disease contexts, creating therapeutic dilemmas. These limitations underscore the need to develop more selective targeted agents and establish personalized combination regimens to optimize antitumor efficacy while minimizing cardiovascular toxicity risks.

The bidirectional relationship between lung cancer and CVDs has significant clinical implications. Patients with lung cancer are at increased risk of cardiovascular complications, and vice versa. This necessitates a multidisciplinary approach to patient care. Currently, cardiologists and oncologists unanimously agree that baseline cardiovascular risk assessment is crucial for patients considering cancer treatments that may induce cardiotoxicity. Researchers have proposed a baseline cardiovascular risk assessment tool for oncology and hematology services, designed to assist in planning for patients receiving one of the seven common cancer treatments associated with cardiotoxicity. Utilizing this assessment tool can help healthcare teams provide personalized care, identify the risk of cardiovascular complications in a timely manner, and take necessary measures to reduce the patient's cardiovascular risk, ensuring their safe passage through the treatment process. Future research should focus on several aspects: (1) Preventive Strategies -Implementing public health interventions aimed at reducing modifiable risk factors, such as smoking cessation programs, dietary improvements, and air pollution control, to mitigate the dual burden of lung cancer and CVDs. (2) Identifying Biomarkers—Further exploration of biomarkers that can predict the risk of developing both lung cancer and CVDs, such as inflammatory cytokines, lipid metabolism markers, and immune cell profiles. (3) Developing Targeted Therapies—Investigating drugs that can simultaneously target shared pathways in lung cancer and CVDs, such as anti-inflammatory agents, angiogenesis inhibitors, and metabolic modulators. (4) Personalized Medicine—Understanding the genetic and molecular underpinnings of these diseases to develop personalized treatment strategies that account for individual risk factors and disease mechanisms.
