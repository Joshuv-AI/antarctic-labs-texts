Multicenter retrospective analysis of success and failure patterns in groin flap reconstruction for upper extremity soft tissue defects
Authors: Saleh Abualhaj, Mosleh M Abualhaj, Shadi Hamouri, Anas As’ad, Omar S Mansour, Waleed Haddad, Obadah Obeidat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aims to analyze the success and failure patterns of groin flap procedures performed across multiple healthcare centers in Jordan.Patients & MethodsA multicenter retrospective case series was conducted involving patients who underwent groin flap reconstruction for upper extremity soft tissue defects between 2015 and 2024 in Jordan. All cases where the groin flap was used as the primary reconstructive method with at least six months of follow-up were included. Variables analyzed included patient demographics, defect characteristics, surgical details, and postoperative outcomes. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to assess flap viability over time.ResultsA total of 89 patients were included, with a flap survival rate of 94.6%. Partial flap necrosis occurred in 5.4% of cases. The 12-month flap survival was 98.9% (95% CI: 96.7%-100%), while 36- and 60-month survival rates were both 94.4% (95% CI: 89.1%-99.9%). Failed flaps were associated with factors such as smoking, diabetes, and prolonged defect ischemia.ConclusionsGroin flaps remain a reliable reconstructive option in upper limb injuries, particularly in settings with limited microsurgical resources. This study highlights key predictors of failure and supports the continued role of the groin flap in selected patient populations.Keywords: Groin flap, upper limb reconstruction, flap survival, reconstructive surgery, soft tissue defect, flap failure predictors, flap viability

Introduction

Reconstruction of soft tissue defects in the upper limb remains a complex and challenging endeavor [1–3]. A variety of reconstructive strategies are available, ranging from local flaps to microsurgical free tissue transfers [4,5]. In such contexts, the groin flap, has long been a reliable option for upper limb reconstruction due to its consistent vascular anatomy, ease of harvest, and minimal donor-site morbidity [6].

Originally introduced by McGregor and Jackson in 1972, the groin flap is based on the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA). Its thin, pliable characteristics make it especially suitable for upper limb reconstruction, and its use avoids threatening the vascular integrity of a compromised limb [7]. These attributes make the groin flap a viable alternative in patients with poor vascular status or in settings where microsurgical capabilities are limited [8,9].

Despite a decline in routine use due to drawbacks such as prolonged immobilization and the need for a secondary flap division procedure, the groin flap remains clinically relevant [10]. However, the literature lacks comprehensive, large-scale analyses that explore the success and failure patterns of this technique across varied healthcare settings [11].

While previous reports on groin flap reconstruction for upper extremity injuries have primarily been limited to single-center case series with relatively small sample sizes, our study contributes a broader perspective by incorporating data from multiple institutions across Jordan, including university, military, public, and private hospitals. Jordan represents a particularly relevant setting due to its mix of civilian and military healthcare systems, variable access to microsurgical resources, and diverse patient population. By examining cases performed over a nine-year period, we aim to evaluate the outcomes of groin flap reconstruction, focusing on success rates, patterns of flap failure, and associated patient and surgical factors.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter retrospective case series was conducted to assess the outcomes of groin flap procedures utilized in upper extremity reconstruction. The study covered diverse healthcare settings across Jordan—including university-affiliated, military, public, and private hospitals—offering a representative overview of surgical practices within the country. All relevant surgical cases performed between January 2015 and June 2024 were included in the review.

Study population and sampling

All patients who underwent groin flap reconstruction for upper extremity soft tissue defects during the study period were eligible for inclusion. A census sampling approach was employed, ensuring that every case meeting the inclusion criteria was analyzed. Inclusion required that the groin flap serve as the primary reconstructive method and that postoperative follow-up be available for a minimum of six months. Patients were excluded if groin flaps were not the primary technique or if essential postoperative documentation, including follow-up visits, clinical assessments, or outcome data, was incomplete. Cases with missing follow-up data were not analyzed, ensuring the accuracy and completeness of the dataset.

Surgical technique

Groin flap reconstruction was performed using a pedicled flap based on the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA) axis. The flap was designed over the groin, with the lateral border at the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and the medial edge extending toward the femoral vessels. An incision was made along the inguinal crease, and subfascial dissection was carried out to elevate the flap, ensuring preservation of the vascular pedicle throughout.

Flaps were classified according to anatomical considerations, including the presence and course of SCIA perforators, rather than the traditional “classical” versus “tubular” classification, to account for vascular and anatomical heterogeneity. When feasible, superficial circumflex iliac artery perforators (SCIPs) were identified and preserved to enhance flap perfusion.

After inset, the affected limb was immobilized with a temporary stitch connecting the hand to the abdominal wall, combined with a support sling to prevent tension on the pedicle. Flap perfusion was monitored regularly using clinical parameters such as color, temperature, capillary refill, and turgor. Venous congestion, if present intraoperatively or postoperatively, was managed conservatively with limb elevation, gentle massage, and warming; in selected cases, measures such as releasing tight dressings or temporary use of medicinal leeches were applied. No surgical re-explorations were necessary in this cohort.

Pedicle division was performed under regional or general anesthesia between 14 and 24 days postoperatively (mean 16 ± 3 days), after clinical confirmation of adequate neovascularization and flap integration. Division was carried out with meticulous dissection and hemostasis to minimize complications.

Data collection procedures

Patient data were retrieved retrospectively from electronic health records, paper-based charts, operative reports, surgical logbooks, and follow-up clinic notes from all participating institutions. A standardized data extraction form was used to collect key variables, including demographic information (age, gender, comorbid conditions, smoking status), characteristics of the defect (location, size, cause), and intraoperative details (flap type, pedicle length, flap dimensions and design, and complications such as venous congestion).

Venous congestion was identified intraoperatively and postoperatively by clinical signs such as a dusky or purplish hue, increased turgor, and delayed or absent capillary refill. It was managed conservatively postoperatively through limb elevation, gentle flap massage, and warming to improve venous outflow. No patients required immediate surgical re-exploration for venous congestion. This conservative approach was effective in most cases, and patients were closely monitored during the postoperative period to ensure flap viability.

Defect and flap sizes were determined intraoperatively by measuring their length and width with a sterile ruler. The area (cm2) was calculated by multiplying the two dimensions.

Flaps were classified as either classic or tubular based on their design and rotation. Classic flaps were designed with a wide base and rotated up to 90° to cover the defect, whereas tubular flaps were fashioned as narrow, cylindrical flaps that could be rotated up to 180° for coverage of circumferential or complex defects. The angle of rotation and flap geometry were recorded intraoperatively to ensure reproducibility and facilitate outcome analysis.

Additional surgical interventions, such as flap debulking, pseudo-syndactyly division, tendon repair, or tendon grafting, were also recorded.

Primary outcome measures included flap viability, which was categorized into two groups: complete survival, defined as full flap take without necrosis, and total failure, defined as complete flap loss necessitating alternative reconstruction.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.5.4. Descriptive statistics—means, medians, standard deviations, and proportions—were used to summarize patient characteristics and surgical outcomes. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was employed to assess flap viability over time, with the log-rank test used for subgroup comparisons. Associations between clinical variables (such as age, comorbidities, defect site, and flap type) and flap outcomes were explored using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data and independent t-tests for continuous variables. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Al-Balqa Applied University, along with necessary clearances from the participating hospitals. As this was a retrospective study utilizing anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Confidentiality was strictly upheld through data de-identification and secure handling procedures.

Results

Demographic, clinical, and surgical characteristics

A total of 89 patients underwent groin flap reconstruction for upper extremity defects. The mean age of the cohort was 27.7 years (SD: 8.9, range: 14.0–60.0). The majority of patients were male (81, 91.0%), while only 8 patients (9.0%) were female. Comorbidities were present in 4 patients (4.5%), whereas 85 (95.5%) had no known comorbid conditions. Smoking was reported in 67 patients (75.3%), while 22 (24.7%) were nonsmokers. The mean BMI was 24.7 kg/m2 (SD: 4.2, range: 17.0–33.1).

The most common defect site was the finger (46, 51.7%), followed by the hand (33, 37.1%) and forearm (10, 11.2%). The defects resulted from various injury mechanisms, with crush injuries being the most frequent (28, 31.5%), followed by degloving injuries (23, 25.8%), postoperative skin loss (16, 18.0%), burns (16, 18.0%), and infection (6, 6.7%). Most cases (67, 75.3%) had secondary defects, whereas 22 cases (24.7%) had primary defects. The mean defect length was 7.6 cm (SD: 2.0, range: 4.0–14.0), and the mean defect width was 4.7 cm (SD: 1.7, range: 2.0–8.0). The overall mean defect size was 37.9 cm2 (SD: 21.2, range: 8.0–112.0).

The mean flap length was 13.1 cm (SD: 2.6, range: 8.0–20.0), and the mean flap width was 5.9 cm (SD: 1.7, range: 3.0–10.0), resulting in a mean flap size of 80.3 cm2 (SD: 36.2, range: 24.0–180.0). The mean operation time for the first stage was 55.6 minutes (SD: 13.6, range: 32.0–120.0). The flap rotation technique was categorized as classic in 31 patients (34.8%) and tubular in 58 patients (65.2%). Additional procedures were required in 36 patients (40.4%), while 53 patients (59.6%) did not require any additional intervention. The mean wound healing time was 32.7 days (SD: 8.1, range: 23.0–60.0). Venous congestion occurred in 36 cases (40.4%), with a mean congestion duration of 12.5 days (SD: 3.6, range: 7.0–17.0). The overall flap success rate was 95.5% (85 cases), while flap failure was observed in 4 patients (4.5%) (Table 1).

Comparison between failed and successful flaps

Table 2 presents the comparison of continuous variables between failed and successful flaps. Although there was no significant difference in age (p = 0.51) or BMI (p = 0.13), patients with flap failure had significantly smaller defect widths (p = 0.03), flap widths (p = 0.006), and flap sizes (p = 0.019) compared to those with successful outcomes. Additionally, the operation time was significantly shorter in failed cases (mean: 39.25 minutes) compared to successful cases (mean: 56.38 minutes, p = 0.007). Other parameters, including defect length, defect size, and wound healing time, did not show significant differences between the two groups.

Flap viability results

The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the long-term viability of flaps. The overall survival probability of the entire cohort demonstrated excellent outcomes, with a median follow-up period of 24 months (range: 12–108 months). The 12-month flap survival rate was 98.9% (95% CI: 96.7%–100%), which slightly declined to 94.4% at both 36 and 60 months (95% CI: 89.1%–99.9%) (Figure 2).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates a high long-term survival rate of flaps used in upper extremity reconstruction, with excellent viability outcomes across all time intervals. The overall flap survival rate was 98.9% at 12 months, declining slightly to 94.4% at both 36 and 60 months. These results reflect the durable success of reconstructive flaps in this cohort, even in the presence of challenging defect characteristics such as traumatic injuries and postoperative skin loss. The median follow-up duration of 24 months (range: 12–108 months) provides a robust window for evaluating long-term outcomes. The minor decrease in flap survival over time, with only 3 documented events at 36 months and none at 60 months, underscores the sustained viability of these flaps beyond the early postoperative period. These findings are consistent with prior literature, where flap survival typically exceeds 90%, particularly when microsurgical techniques are used with meticulous patient selection and intraoperative assessment [12,13].

These findings align with those of the study by Kang et al. (2020), who found a 94.3% flap survival rate among 35 patients undergoing subacute flap reconstruction for complex upper extremity trauma. Our investigation represents an extension of follow-up time to 60 months, confirming the durability and long-term viability of these reconstructions beyond the short- and mid-term timeframe. While Kang et al. showed events of partial flap necrosis and infection, our cohort displayed three flap loss events, all within the first 36 months. In addition, our Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed no statistically significant difference in flap outcomes by defect site (hand, finger, or forearm), a finding that counters the work of Kang et al., who successfully applied various flap type to a wide range of anatomical sites. Both studies demonstrate that flap transfer is a relatively straightforward and delicate procedure despite challenging clinical scenarios, emphasizing that prompt reconstruction both in the early and subacute period can yield excellent viability outcomes and manageable mid- and long-term morbidity [14].

Among the small subsets of patients who experienced flap failure in our cohort, several notable patterns emerged that may offer insights into potential risk factors. Smoking appeared prominently among the failed cases, aligning with existing literature that identifies tobacco use as a critical risk factor for compromised vascular perfusion and delayed wound healing [15–18]. Additionally, failed flaps were more frequently associated with smaller defect sites, particularly in the fingers, where limited vascular territory and technical complexity may contribute to higher failure rates [19,20]. Most failures also occurred in cases involving tube flaps rather than classic flap techniques, possibly indicating that the added complexity of flap rotation and vascular tension in tubular configurations may increase the risk of ischemia [21].

While our study confirms the high reliability and success rate of groin flaps for upper extremity reconstruction, these findings also have important clinical implications. Groin flaps may still be preferred in cases where microsurgical expertise or equipment for SCIP flaps is limited, or when larger or more complex defects require robust tissue coverage [22]. Additionally, groin flaps may be advantageous in patient subgroups where preservation of lymphatic structures is less critical, or when minimizing operative time and surgical complexity is prioritized [23]. In contrast, SCIP flaps may be preferred when reduced donor-site morbidity, particularly to lymphatic structures, is desired [24]. By clarifying these scenarios, surgeons can make more informed decisions tailored to patient characteristics and institutional resources, optimizing both functional and esthetic outcomes.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection and reporting biases, limiting the ability to establish causal relationships. The relatively small number of flap failures, while clinically favorable, restricts the statistical power for subgroup analysis and may limit the generalizability of predictive findings. Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths, including a relatively large sample size for a multiple-center groin flap series, comprehensive documentation of perioperative and postoperative outcomes, and the use of survival analysis to evaluate long-term flap viability.

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies to validate predictors of flap success and failure, as well as incorporating standardized functional and quality-of-life metrics. Exploration of adjunctive interventions such as preoperative vascular imaging, intraoperative perfusion monitoring, or enhanced recovery protocols could also refine patient selection and improve reconstructive outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the groin flap remains a reliable and effective reconstructive option for complex upper extremity defects, offering high flap survival rates and satisfactory clinical outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that flap width, defect dimensions, and operative time are potential factors associated with flap success, emphasizing the importance of detailed surgical planning.
