Enantioselective effects of imazalil on banana postharvest ripening aroma revealed by integrated volatilomics, lipidomics and metabolomics
Authors: Xianliu Chen, Yanling Zhang, Qiong Wu, Lvyun Zhuang, Zhonghua Yang, Qun Zhang, Lehe Tan, Weijie Lan, Mingyue Wang, Chen Ma, Zhi Xu, Daizhu Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImazalil (IMZ) is the most widely used postharvest fungicide during banana storage and transport, yet its potential impact on banana quality remains largely unknown. In this study, integrated volatilomics, targeted lipidomics, and untargeted metabolomics were applied to investigate how different enantiomers of IMZ affect aroma in the postharvest banana yellow ripening stage. Results showed that Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ disrupted fatty acid and branched-chain amino acid metabolism, significantly lowering precursor pools of linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, leucine, and valine required for ester formation via the lipoxygenase and amino acid pathways. S-(+)-IMZ exhibited minimal interference with these pathways, thereby maintaining aroma quality comparable to untreated fruit. These findings highlight an impact associated with the use of racemic imazalil in banana postharvest preservation and provide multi-omics evidence that replacing the racemate with the single S-(+)-enantiomer can achieve effective postharvest disease control while largely preserving the characteristic aroma of banana.Keywords: Imazalil, Enantioselective effects, Banana, Lipidomics, Metabolomics, Volatilomics

Introduction

Banana is popular among consumers for its strong, distinctive aroma and taste, as well as its high nutritional value, making it the most traded fresh fruit worldwide (Bantayehu & Alemayehu, 2020; Moreno et al., 2020). Banana is a typical climacteric fruit that undergoes pronounced postharvest ripening, but it is also highly susceptible to pathogens. Consequently, pesticides are commonly used to enhance banana production. However, the use of pesticides poses safety risks to agricultural products. Imazalil (IMZ) is a commonly used chiral triazole fungicide in agriculture, which is typically applied as a racemate for commercial fungicidal treatment of postharvest bananas and provides preservative effects during subsequent storage and transportation (Balkan & Karaaal, 2023). Ma et al. analyzed 344 bananas sold in the Chinese market and detected pesticides in 56.10% of whole fruit samples; 13 fungicides were detected, and the detection rate of IMZ reached 12.5% (Ma et al., 2022). Previous studies have revealed significant differences in enantioselective activity between R-(−)-IMZ and S-(+)-IMZ against multiple pathogens, including Glomerella cingulata and Penicillium digitatum (Li et al., 2019). In our laboratory studies, the antibacterial activity of S-(+)-IMZ against banana anthracnose was 4.3-fold that of R-(−)-IMZ and 2.5-fold that of the racemate. Thus, the highly efficient enantiomer S-(+)-IMZ shows strong potential for banana preservation.

While the use of a highly efficient enantiomer can reduce disease-related losses, pesticide stress can also induce the production of substantial reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plant tissues. ROS accumulation damages cells, promotes lipid peroxidation and protein degradation, and can alter the taste, flavor, and nutrient distribution in agricultural products (Dubey et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2023). Flavor is a core quality attribute that affects consumer preference and market competitiveness (Chen et al., 2025; Dou et al., 2020). However, most research on chiral pesticides has focused on their selective biological activity and environmental behavior, and relatively few studies have examined their effects on post-application flavor quality. Liu et al. reported that chiral pyraclostrobin has a selective effect on tomato flavor; the S-enantiomer increases the content of soluble sugars and decreases the content of organic acids, enhancing taste, whereas the R-enantiomer negatively affects flavor (Liu et al., 2022). Using metabolomics, Zhao et al. showed that tebuconazole enantiomers have distinct stereoselective effects in lettuce: only (−)-R-tebuconazole reduced sucrose, isoleucine, and leucine, while only (+)-S-tebuconazole up-regulated quercetin, quercetin-3-galactoside, and amino acids related to glycine/serine/threonine metabolism. Tebuconazole enantiomers show significant stereoselective effects in lettuce (Zhao et al., 2022). Wang et al. found that chiral epoxiconazole reduces the overall content of nutrients in leeks, and the effect of (+)-epoxyconazole was stronger than that of (−)-epoxyconazole (Wang et al., 2023). As consumer demand for high-quality fruit rises, there is an urgent need for effective and low-toxicity fungicides with minimal effect on sensory quality. Flavor has always been an important factor affecting consumer preferences for bananas because of its characteristic aroma and taste. Given that S-(+)-IMZ exhibits high efficacy against banana anthracnose, replacing racemic commercial IMZ with optically pure S-(+)-IMZ is a promising strategy for cleaner banana production by enabling reduced application while maintaining antifungal effectiveness. However, whether the racemate or enantiomer has a greater effect on banana quality remains unclear. Therefore, clarifying the enantioselective effects of IMZ enantiomers on banana flavor is of significance and provides theoretical guidance for the coordinated promotion of efficient postharvest disease control and fruit quality maintenance in bananas. To address this, the effect of different types of IMZ on banana flavor quality was evaluated by determining how IMZ racemates and each enantiomer, particularly the highly efficient S-(+)-IMZ, affect the aroma and metabolites of banana fruit. The potential mechanisms underlying changes in aroma components were also examined, focusing on fatty acid and amino acid metabolism. The findings of this study not only fill the research gap regarding the enantioselective effects of chiral fungicides on aroma quality at the yellow ripening stage of bananas, but also provide experimental data and new insights for the development of S-(+)-IMZ-based products and their applications in postharvest preservation of bananas.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Banana fruits (cv. ‘Nantianhuang’) were harvested from the Chengmai Banana Industrial Park (Hainan, China). After rinsing with clean water, fruits were fully immersed in 500 mg/L IMZ racemate or single-enantiomer solutions, with continuous stirring to ensure uniform mixing. After 1 min of treatment, bananas were removed and air-dried naturally. The fruits were then stored at room temperature (20–22 °C) and ripened under 85% relative humidity until the yellow ripening stage. At this stage, the peel and pulp were separated, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Chemicals and reagents

Racemic IMZ (98%) and single-enantiomer IMZ (purity ≥98.0%) were purchased from Shanghai Minjie Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HPLC-grade methanol, acetonitrile, formic acid, methylene chloride, and isopropanol were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Ammonia and ammonium acetate were supplied by Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water was produced with a Milli-Q Integral 3 system (Millipore, USA). A divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber (50/30 μm film thickness) was purchased from Supelco (Bellefonte, PA, USA).

Volatile compounds

At the yellow ripening stage, banana pulp was homogenized in liquid nitrogen. Five g of homogenate was transferred to a 40 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 40 °C for 10 min in a thermostatically controlled water bath. A SPME fiber was then exposed to the headspace of each vial at 40 °C for 30 min. The adsorbed volatiles were desorbed in the splitless injector of the GC–MS system at 250 °C for 3 min.

Volatiles were analyzed by GC–MS (Agilent 5977 A GC system 7890B GC/MS triple Quad) equipped with HP-MS Ultra Inert 5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent, USA). The oven program was as follows: 40 °C for 1 min; ramp at 3.5 °C/min to 135 °C; ramp at 10 °C/min to 200 °C; and hold 3 min at 200 °C. Helium was the carrier gas at a constant flow of 0.8 mL/min. Mass spectra were acquired in electron-impact (EI) mode at 70 eV. Chromatograms were recorded in scan mode with a scanning m/z range of 40–450.

Sensory evaluation

Samples were prepared on the evaluation day following a standardized procedure. The apical and basal 2 cm sections of banana fruits were discarded; each fruit was split longitudinally into two halves, with one half used for subsequent chemical analysis and the other half was used to collect pulp, which was cut into 1.5–2 cm3 pulp cubes. The Cubes were placed in 50 mL odor-free containers with 3-digit random codes, sealed, and equilibrated at 22 ± 1 °C before evaluation. Quantitative descriptive analysis of the samples was performed by 12 panelists (all from our laboratory, 8 males and 4 females, aged 25–55 years) with tropical fruit sensory experience. All the panelists provided written informed consent and were required to refrain from consuming spicy foods, using strongly scented products, smoking, and eating for at least 1 h before evaluation. Four attributes were evaluated: overall aroma intensity, fruity aroma intensity, grassy aroma intensity, and overall flavor acceptability, using a 100 linear scale (0 = no perception, 100 = extremely strong intensity). Samples were presented in a completely randomized order. After evaluating each sample, panelists rinsed their mouths with purified water and rested for at least 90 s to eliminate sensory carry-over effects. Each group included three biological replicates and three replicate scores were collected for each sensory attribute, with the mean value used as the final statistical result. Evaluation was performed in the following order: overall aroma intensity upon container opening, fruity aroma intensity, grassy aroma intensity, and overall pulp flavor acceptability. All sensory evaluations were conducted in the Laboratory of Nutrition and Quality Evaluation, Analysis and Testing Center, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences. The laboratory was equipped with 12 individual sensory compartments, with ambient temperature steadily controlled at 22 °C and illuminated by LED daylight equivalent light sources, complying with standard requirements for sensory evaluation environments.

Targeted lipidomics and amino acid analysis

Lipid sample extraction was performed following Su (Su et al., 2024). Briefly, each freeze-dried banana pulp sample (100 mg) was weighed into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube, to which 800 μL of MTBE and 560 μL of water (400 μL + 160 μL) were added. The mixture was vortexed and sonicated in an ice bath for 15 min; the mixture was then held at −20 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen and redissolved in 200 μL of dichloromethane: methanol solution (1:1). The supernatant was used for lipidomic analysis.

A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling 20 μL of supernatant from each sample to monitor data stability. During instrumental analysis, one QC injection was run after every six samples.

Mass analysis was performed on an AB Sciex UHPLC system coupled to an AB Sciex 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA). Electrospray ionization (ESI) parameters were as follows: ion spray voltage, +5200 V (positive mode) and − 4500 V (negative mode); source temperature, 350 °C; nebulizer gas (GS1), 55 psi; heater gas (GS2), 55 psi; and curtain gas (CUR), 40 psi. For positive-ion mode, the mobile phases were solvent A (water:methanol:acetonitrile with 7 mM ammonium acetate, 1:1:1, v/v/v) and solvent B (isopropanol with 7 mM ammonium acetate). Chromatographic separation was performed using a CSH C18 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Kinetex C18) maintained at 45 °C with a 1 μL injection volume and a 0.3 mL/min flow rate. The gradient elution conditions were as follows: 0–1.5 min, 20% B; 1.5–3 min, increased to 40% B; 3–13 min, increased to 60% B; 13–13.1 min, increased to 98% B; and 13.1–17 min, decreased to 20% B. For negative-ion mode, mobile phase A was isopropanol:acetonitrile (7:93, v/v) with 5 mM ammonium acetate, and mobile phase B comprised water:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) with 2 mM ammonium acetate. Separation was performed using a Luna NH2 (Amino) column (3 μm, 2.0 mm × 100 mm; Phenomenex, USA) at 35 °C with a 1 μL injection volume. The gradient elution conditions were as follows: 0–2 min, 0% B; 2.01–7 min, increased to 50% B; 11–11.5 min, increased to 70% B; 11.5–12.5 min, increased to 100% B and held for 15 min; 15–15.1 min, decreased to 0% B and held 17 min. The flow rates were 0.2 mL/min (0–2 min), 0.7 mL/min (2.01–15 min), and 0.2 mL/min (15.01–17 min).

Amino acid extraction and analysis followed. Banana pulp was homogenized in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle to obtain powder; 200 mg was transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube, 1 mL of 50% methanol aqueous solution containing 0.2% vitamin C was added, and the tube was vortexed for 30 s. After extraction on a multi-tube vortexer for 10 min, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (300 μL) was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and transferred to a vial for UPLC–MS/MS analysis.

For amino acid analysis, separations were performed on an HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA) using an AB Sciex UHPLC system coupled with an AB Sciex 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA). Mobile phase A was water, and mobile phase B was methanol, both of which contained 0.02% formic acid. The gradient program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 1% B; 0.5–2.5 min, increased to 8% B; 2.5–15 min, increased to 50% B; 15–18 min, increased to 95% B; 18–18.1 min, decreased to 1% B; and 18.1–20 min, held at 1% B. The injection volume was 1 μL, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the flow rate was 0.2 mL/min. ESI settings were as follows: ion spray voltage, +5500 V (positive mode); source temperature, 550 °C; GS1, 50 psi; GS2, 40 psi; and CUR, 40 psi (Zhao et al., 2021).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Metabolite extraction and analysis were carried out following Liu (Liu et al., 2022) with minor modifications. Approximately 100 mg of banana was ground in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in 500 μL of prechilled 80% methanol containing 0.1% formic acid. The mixtures were incubated on ice for 5 min and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Next, 133 μL of water was added to 400 μL of the supernatant, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling 20 μL of the supernatant from each sample.

The UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) was used for LC–MS/MS analysis. Samples were injected onto a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the autosampler temperature was held at 10 °C. Mass spectra were acquired on the Q Exactive mass spectrometer in full-scan, data-dependent MS2 (ddMS2) mode using Xcalibur software (Thermo). Elution was performed under the following conditions. For positive-ion mode, mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was methanol. The gradient program was as follows: 0–1 min, 5% B; 1–21 min, increased to 95% B; 21–24 min, held at 95% B; and 24.1–28 min, decreased to 5% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, and the column temperature was 40 °C. For negative-ion mode, mobile phase A was 0.1% ammonia in water, and mobile phase B was methanol. The gradient program was as follows: 0–2 min, 2% B; 2–19 min, increased to 98% B; 19–22 min, held at 98% B; and 22.1–25 min, decreased to 2% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, and the column temperature was 40 °C. MS parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 30 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 25 Arb; capillary temperature, 350 °C; full MS resolution, 70,000; MS/MS resolution, 17,500; spray voltage, +3.5 kV (positive) or − 3.2 kV (negative); and scan range, m/z 60–900.

Data processing and analysis

The relative odor activity value (ROAV) was defined as the ratio of a compound's relative content in banana to its odor threshold and was calculated following. One-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test was used to compare aroma components between treatment and control groups. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson correlation. Untargeted metabolomics raw datasets were processed in Compound Discoverer 3.0 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Peak extraction, identification, alignment, and integration were performed; metabolites were searched against the mzCloud and ChemSpider databases, and identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database. Features with a coefficient of variation (CV) >20% were excluded. Data were acquired in three biological replicates. Targeted lipid and amino acid data were acquired as follows: Sciex OS (Sciex Inc., Foster City, CA, USA) was used for peak-area extraction and identification. Peaks with missing values exceeding 80% in the samples were removed and imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Principal component analysis (PCA), KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and fold change (FC) and p-value (two-sided t-test) calculations for each compound comparing the treatment with the control group were performed in MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). KEGG pathway enrichment was performed for differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) and differentially accumulated lipids (DALs). DAMs were identified using the criteria FC ≥1.20 with p < 0.05, whereas DALs were identified using the criteria FC ≥1.5 and p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

The influence of IMZ racemate and enantiomer on the volatile aroma components of bananas

The aroma components of bananas treated with the IMZ racemate and its enantiomers at the yellow ripening stage were analyzed. A total of 40 volatile substances were identified; esters were the predominant volatile compounds, followed by aldehydes, phenols, and alkanes (Fig. 1A). The IMZ racemate and enantiomers influenced the production of ester volatile aroma compounds (VACs) during the yellow ripening stage of bananas, with significant differences among the treatment groups. In the control group, 23 esters were detected; 20 esters were identified in the S-(+)-IMZ treatment group, whereas only 17 esters were detected in the Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ groups (Fig. 1A). All the IMZ-treated groups lacked certain ester volatiles relative to the control group, including isobutyl hexanoate, isoamyl hexanoate, ethyl dodecanoate, isobutyl isobutyrate, 3-methylbutyl pentadecanoate, and valeric acid 2-tridecyl ester (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, IMZ treatments reduced the levels of several characteristic banana esters: Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ significantly decreased the content of isoamyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, isobutyl isovalerate, isoamyl isovalerate, and hexyl isovalerate at the yellow ripening stage (Fig. 1D). Isoamyl acetate is a primary aroma component at the yellow ripening stage of bananas (Dou et al., 2020; Golding et al., 1999; Zhu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2019). The reduction in isoamyl acetate decreased the banana aroma, thereby compromising quality. In contrast, S-(+)-IMZ only significantly reduced isoamyl isovalerate and increased the content of esters such as isoamyl isobutyrate, isoamyl n-pentanoate, and valeric acid 2-pentyl ester. The total ester content of the different treatment groups was calculated from the peak area of the esters (Fig. 1 C) and did not differ significantly between the S-(+)-IMZ treatment group and the control group; however, the total ester content was significantly lower in the Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ treatment groups than in the control group (Fig. 1C). Thus, Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ substantially reduced both the diversity and abundance of ester VACs at the yellow ripening stage, indicating that these two IMZ treatments adversely affected aroma quality, whereas S-(+)-IMZ had a much weaker effect on aroma components. Notably, after IMZ treatments, more aldehyde VACs were produced, such as (E)-2-hexenal and hexenal, which are typical volatile compounds in unripe bananas. This reflects alterations in the physiological and metabolic processes during the ripening stage of bananas under IMZ exposure.

Because VACs differ in concentration and in the minimum level at which they can be perceived, their effects on aroma vary. The odor activity value (OAV) method is the most widely used approach for identifying the primary contributors to fruit aroma (Xie et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2023). This method was used to evaluate the effects of the IMZ racemate and its enantiomers on key aroma components in bananas. Among volatile components with ROVA >1, isoamyl acetate, isoamyl isovalerate, and isoamyl butyrate were the most dominant contributors in the control group, with isoamyl acetate exhibiting the highest OAV and thus making the greatest contribution to aroma. In the S-(+)-IMZ treatment group, (E)-2-hexenal, isoamyl acetate, and isoamyl butyrate were the primary aroma contributors. (E)-2-hexenal imparts a green/grassy flavor to banana aroma, and the reduction of ester gases reduces the fruity flavor.

In the Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ groups, only (E)-2-hexenal and isopentyl acetate had OAV > 10, indicating that these aroma components had a higher grassy flavor and a lower fruity flavor. Overall, although all IMZ treatments increased the content of (E)-2-hexenal during the yellow ripening period, the aroma characteristics of the S-(+)-IMZ treatment were more similar to those of the control group, suggesting that compared with the other two treatments (Table 1), the effect of S-(+)-IMZ treatment on aroma composition was weaker. The aroma variations detected by volatilomics were further verified by sensory evaluation (Table S1). Compared with the control, samples treated with Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ showed significant decreases in overall aroma intensity and fruity aroma intensity (P < 0.05), accompanied by a distinct increase in grassy aroma. In contrast, no significant differences in overall aroma intensity, fruity aroma intensity, and flavor acceptability were observed between the S-(+)-IMZ group and the control, except for a slight increase in grassy aroma. These results were highly consistent with the changes in ester aroma compounds, confirming that Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ significantly impair the typical aroma quality of bananas, whereas S-(+)-IMZ exerts the mildest effect on aroma characteristics.

IMZ affects the generation of aroma by altering lipid and amino acid metabolism

The main metabolic pathways for the synthesis of banana ester aroma include fatty acid metabolic pathways and amino acid metabolic pathways, which jointly determine the composition and intensity of the characteristic aroma of the fruit. Pesticides are important abiotic factors that can directly affect the synthesis and dynamics of plant lipids (Dubey et al., 2015; Liu & Zhu, 2020). To further clarify the mechanisms by which the IMZ racemate and its enantiomers influence banana VACs, this study integrated volatilomics, lipidomics, and metabolomics data to systematically explain the intrinsic mechanism underlying the enantioselective effects of IMZ on banana aroma quality from the perspective of metabolic pathway - substrate supply - product synthesis.

Analysis of the lipidomic data indicated that the total ion current (TIC) of the quality control (QC) samples in both positive and negative ion modes exhibited substantial overlap with consistent peak intensities (Fig. S1), demonstrating high repeatability of the lipidomic methodology. Moreover, the RSD values of more than 90% of the detected metabolites were within ±15%, which met the general quality control criteria for metabolomics analysis. A total of 315 lipids were detected in banana pulp, encompassing 16 subclasses across four major categories: glycerolipids (GL), glycerophospholipids (GP), fatty acyls (FA), and sphingolipids (SP) (Fig. 2A), which provides a relatively comprehensive data foundation for the subsequent lipid metabolism analysis. The PCA plot (Fig. 2B) showed clear separation among treatment groups, indicating significant differences in pulp lipid composition under IMZ racemate and enantiomer stress. Class-level trends and volcano plots of DALs were analyzed at the yellow ripening stage after IMZ treatment (Fig. 2C, D). Relative to the control, 84 DALs were upregulated and 47 DALs were downregulated in the Rac-IMZ group. Rac-IMZ also decreased the total DAG content in the pulp while increasing PE, PG, PI, LPA, LPC, and LPG. In the S-(+)-IMZ group, 149 DALs were upregulated, and 23 were downregulated; TAG showed the most pronounced increase, which was significantly higher in the S-(+)-IMZ group than in the other three groups (p < 0.0001). In the (−)-IMZ group, 115 DALs were upregulated, and 51 DALs were downregulated; MAG, PC, PE, PI, LPC, LPE, and LPI were upregulated, whereas DAG and FFA were downregulated. These results indicate that IMZ enantiomers had distinct effects on lipid synthesis and turnover in plants. These results indicate that IMZ enantiomers can affect lipid metabolism and lipid molecular composition in banana pulp by differentially regulating glycerolipid, glycerophospholipid, and free fatty acid metabolism, with significant differences in lipid disturbance induced by different chiral configurations. To identify major pathways of these DALs, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DALs indicated that IMZ treatments affected distinct metabolic pathways (Fig. 3A–D). The results showed that IMZ treatments mainly disturbed glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways, with significant differences in enrichment patterns among different enantiomers: Rac-IMZ treatment mainly affected glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, glycerol metabolism, and α-linolenic acid metabolism; S-(+)-IMZ treatment was mainly enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism; R-(−)-IMZ treatment significantly affected glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, α-linolenic acid metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism. Notably, linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid are key precursors in the fatty acid branch of banana ester aroma synthesis, which can be ultimately converted into characteristic aroma compounds included isoamyl acetate, isobutyl butyrate, and isovalerate via β-oxidation, lipoxygenase (LOX)-catalyzed reactions and acyl transfer reactions. (Espino-Díaz et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2019). Accordingly, Pearson correlation analysis of α-linolenic acid, linoleic acid, and seven ester aroma components was conducted at the yellow ripening stage. As shown in Fig. S2, linoleic and α-linolenic acids were positively correlated with isobutyl acetate, isoamyl acetate, isobutyl butyrate, isoamyl butyrate, isobutyl isovalerate, and isoamyl isovalerate. Quantitative analysis of these two compounds showed that both Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ treatments decreased the contents of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid, whereas S-(+)-IMZ only lowered linoleic acid level. Meanwhile, Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ significantly reduced the total content of volatile esters (p < 0.05). In contrast, S-(+)-IMZ treatment only caused a significant decrease in isoamyl isovalerate (p < 0.05), with no significant changes detected in other key volatile esters relative to the control. These findings provide direct evidence for a strong metabolic coupling between lipid metabolism and aroma ester biosynthesis. Integrating lipidomic and volatilomic datasets, we infer that IMZ treatments restrict the precursor supply for ester aroma formation by suppressing linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid accumulation, thereby reducing the production of characteristic aroma compounds. Such enantioselective metabolic differences accounts for the distinct effects of different IMZ formulations on banana aroma profile. This result is also consistent with previous reports in horticultural crops such as rose, pear, and carambola, where the abundance of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid directly determines the biosynthesis intensity of volatile aldehydes and esters (Chen et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2019). Collectively, the integrated lipidomic and volatilomic analyses clearly demonstrate that chiral IMZ enantiomers differentially regulate glycerophospholipid and unsaturated fatty acid metabolism to alter the aroma precursor pool, ultimately leading to significant changes in banana ester aroma synthesis capacity and resulting in enantioselective differences in aroma quality.

To clarify the effects of racemic IMZ and its enantiomers on banana metabolism, metabolic profiling of treated and control bananas was performed using an untargeted metabolomic approach. Subsequently, systematic analysis was then conducted based on DAMs. Positive and negative ion TIC plots of the QC samples were overlaid (Fig. S3), with retention times and peak intensities showing high consistency, indicating stable MS performance. Moreover, the RSD values of more than 90% of the detected metabolites were within ±15%, which met the general quality control criteria for metabolomics analysis. DAMs between each IMZ treatment and the control were screened, and DAMs were subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis to explore metabolic pathways affected by different IMZs. The PCA plot (Fig. 4A) showed clear separation among treatment groups, indicating that the IMZ racemate and enantiomers significantly affected banana pulp metabolism; the metabolite clustering heatmap (Fig. 4B) further confirmed the distinct metabolic profiles among different groups. Relative to the control, 50 DAMs were upregulated and 115 DAMs were down-regulated in the Rac-IMZ treatment group; 31 DAMs were up-regulated and 114 DAMs were down-regulated in the S-(+)-IMZ treatment group; and 27 DAMs were up-regulated and 145 DAMs were down-regulated in the R-(−)-IMZ treatment group (Fig. 4C). These DAMs were primarily composed of amino acids and their derivatives, as well as organic acids and their derivatives. KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 5A–C) indicated that, compared with the control, DAMs in the Rac-IMZ treatment group were enriched in valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis, β-alanine metabolism, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism pathways, among which the branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis pathway had an impact value of 0.40 and was identified as the core regulatory target. DAMs in the S-(+)-IMZ treatment group were enriched in cyanoamino acid metabolism, lysine biosynthesis, and valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis pathways. DAMs in the R-(−)-IMZ group were enriched in valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, and lysine biosynthesis pathways. Among these pathways, valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis exhibited the highest impact value (0.35–0.45) across all the treatments, indicating that this pathway is the most critical node for IMZ to interfere with banana amino acid metabolism and characteristic ester aroma formation. Notably, the impact values of this pathway in the Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ groups (0.40 and 0.45, respectively) were significantly higher than that in the S-(+)-IMZ group (0.35), which explains from the perspective of metabolic network topology why the Rac and R configurations exert stronger inhibitory effects on banana aroma quality, whereas the S configuration causes milder interference. To further reveal the association between amino acid metabolism and ester aroma, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between 13 amino acids at the yellow ripening stage of bananas and seven major ester aroma compounds. As shown in Fig. 4D, six amino acids, arginine, leucine, lysine, valine, isoleucine, and serine, were positively correlated with all seven esters. Previous studies have confirmed that branched-chain amino acids such as valine and leucine provide key precursors for ester biosynthesis, whereas the present study further reveals that arginine, lysine, and serine are also significantly positively correlated with banana ester aroma. In this study, levels of valine and leucine were closely associated with the production of major aroma compounds such as isoamyl butyrate and isoamyl acetate. Therefore, leucine and valine were further quantified in subsequent experiments. As shown in Fig. 5D, Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ significantly reduced the leucine content; however, there was no significant difference in the content of leucine in the S-(+)-IMZ treatment and the control group. As shown in Fig. 5D, Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ significantly reduced the leucine content, whereas leucine levels in the S-(+)-IMZ treatment group and the control group did not significantly differ. In this study, reduced leucine under Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ likely led to a decrease in isoamyl acetate synthesis via pathways catalyzed by branched-chain amino acid aminotransferase (BCAT) and branched-chain α-keto acid decarboxylase (BCKDC); leucine reduction also led to a decrease in the formation of isobutyl isovalerate, isopentyl isovalerate, and hexyl isovalerate via alcohol acyltransferase (AAT). Similarly, decreased valine in the Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ treatments resulted in lower levels of 2-methylpropyl esters (Fig. 5D). In summary, Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ treatments altered amino acid metabolism in bananas, and reductions in the precursor amino acids leucine and valine led to decreased levels of isoamyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, isoamyl isovalerate, and isobutyl isovalerate—characteristic aroma compounds of bananas at the yellow ripening stage. In contrast, S-(+)-IMZ had a weaker effect on leucine and valine and thus on the ester aroma components derived from these precursors.

Conclusion

Integrated metabolomic, lipidomic, and volatilomic analyses revealed that different enantiomers of IMZ exhibited significant enantioselective effects on banana aroma. Rac-IMZ and R-(−)-IMZ interfered with banana ester aroma biosynthesis through a dual inhibitory mechanism. On the one hand, they disturbed glycerophospholipid and unsaturated fatty acid metabolism, significantly decreasing key precursors in the fatty acid pathway for ester aroma synthesis, including linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, and leucine. This consequently reduced the contents of key aroma compounds such as isoamyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, isoamyl isovalerate, and isobutyl isovalerate, among which isoamyl acetate represents the typical characteristic aroma compound of banana. On the other hand, they suppressed the biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids and lowered the levels of leucine and valine, which serve as precursors in the amino acid pathway for banana ester formation. These changes ultimately severely impaired the aroma quality of bananas. In contrast, S-(+)-IMZ only decreased linoleic acid content, leading to a reduction in isoamyl isovalerate, which exerted the weakest negative effect on overall aroma. Moreover, sensory evaluation further verified that S-(+)-IMZ maximally preserved the aroma quality of banana samples. This study systematically elucidated the metabolic regulatory mechanism underlying the enantioselective effects of IMZ on banana aroma quality at the multiomics level, which can provide a theoretical foundation for the safe application of postharvest fungicides and the maintenance of fruit aroma quality during storage.
