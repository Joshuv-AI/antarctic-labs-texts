Current Orthopaedic Sports Medicine Fellowships Have a Limited Presence on Social Media
Authors: Ashley Treanor, Michelle Shimizu, Shreya M Saraf, Mary K Mulcahey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo determine the level of involvement of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships on social media, the type of content shared, and the overall level of engagement based on the type of content posted.MethodsA web-based systematic approach identifying and analyzing the social media presence of the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education–accredited orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs across 3 major platforms—Instagram, Facebook, and X—from the inception of each Instagram and X account to January 30, 2025, and from January 1, 2020, to January 30, 2025, for each Facebook account. Data on each program’s profile and posts were collected. The χ2 tests evaluated the variability of content posted between programs. Kruskal-Wallis tests of the mean likes/post/follower evaluated variability in engagement between program profiles.ResultsOf the 95 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education–accredited orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs identified, 20 (21.1%) posted about their fellowship on Instagram, 13 (13.7%) on X, 9 (9.5%) on Facebook, and 5 (5.3%) on all 3 platforms. On average, Instagram profiles had 148.7 posts (range, 8-985), X profiles had 2,807.0 posts (range, 102-6,747), and Facebook profiles had 527.4 posts (range, 23-1,185). Non-fellowship-related content (national holidays, patient information) was the most common category across all platforms (mean of 103 posts on Instagram, 2,260 on X, and 489 on Facebook), with little variability between programs. Level of engagement did not statistically differ across any platform (Instagram, P = .457; X, P = .446; Facebook, P = .433).ConclusionsAlthough Instagram was identified as the most favored platform, the overall social media presence of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships across all platforms was limited. Content analysis revealed a predominant focus on posts unrelated to the program’s orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship.Clinical RelevanceThis study highlights the underutilized potential of social media in enhancing visibility, engagement, and communication for orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships.

Social media has become increasingly prominent within the health care field, with over 70% of adults seeking health-related information online.1,2 Two-thirds of orthopaedic surgeons in the Arthroscopy Association of North America use at least 1 social media platform, creating a unique space for communication, marketing, and education between sports medicine surgeons and the public.3 During the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, there was substantial growth in the social media presence of orthopaedic surgery residency programs as applicants could not visit programs in person.4 Residency programs were able to reach more prospective applicants and showcase unique program attributes that may have otherwise gone unnoticed on websites, while applicants could get a sense of the program’s culture through the various posts. Wang et al.5 found that orthopaedic surgery residency programs with a social media presence were more likely to receive a higher number of applications and have more diversity in race and gender among residents. Another study by Butler et al.6 found that 69.2% of orthopaedic surgery residency applicants utilized Instagram to learn more about residency programs, and 34% of rank lists were influenced by social media. While previous studies have focused on the use of social media by orthopaedic surgery residency programs, social media use among orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs has not been similarly examined. The purpose of this study was to determine the level of involvement of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships on social media, the type of content shared, and the overall level of engagement based on the type of content posted. We hypothesize a high prevalence of orthopaedic surgery sports medicine fellowship programs across all 3 social media types.

Methods

Orthopaedic Sports Fellowship Program Selection

The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine website, and the sports fellowship directory from the Arthroscopy Association of North America were queried on January 20, 2025, yielding a list of 95 ACGME-accredited orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs.7, 8, 9 All 95 programs were included in the search for social media profiles. ACGME-accredited primary care sports medicine fellowships were excluded from our study.

Social Media Presence

Social media profiles for orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs were identified by searching each program’s website for profile links, as well as searching for the name of the program directly on Instagram, X, and Facebook and through Google. Profiles directly sponsored by the fellowship or linked to the official program’s website were screened for inclusion. Social media pages were excluded if they had zero posts specific to their orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs. Resident-specific accounts were excluded.

Data Collection

Following the confirmation of a social media presence and posts regarding the orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship, the social media pages were reviewed for both quantitative and qualitative data. An initial assessment of the date of creation, follower count, following count, and post number was extracted to establish a baseline of a program’s timeline and existing presence. Detailed analysis, including likes per post, comments per post, most common hashtags, and nature of posts, was calculated for further analysis into how orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs chose to interact with their platform (Appendix 1). Although not a comprehensive list, posts clearly about the fellowship program were categorized by content based on the following descriptions: educational, surgical, social, program information, visiting lecturers, and research. All posts not specifically related to the fellowship were categorized as non-fellowship related (Appendix 2). Posts did not always neatly fit one of the above categories; therefore, labels were used for the overall theme of each post. All data were collected manually from the inception of each Instagram and X account to January 30, 2025, and from January 1, 2020, to January 30, 2025, for each Facebook account. A χ2 test was used to evaluate the variability of content themes between programs. Engagement with social media profiles was calculated using mean likes/post/follower. A Kruskal-Wallis calculation was then used to evaluate the variability in engagement between program profiles.

Results

Of the 95 ACGME-accredited orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs, 20 (21.1%) posted about their fellowship on Instagram, 13 (13.7%) on X, and 9 (9.5%) on Facebook. Five programs (5.3%) posted about their fellowship on all 3 platforms. Of the accounts included, 90% of Instagram accounts (n = 18), 15% of X accounts (n = 2), and 22% of Facebook (n = 2) were created after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020. The level of engagement with program profiles did not statistically differ across any social media platform (Instagram, P = .457; X, P = .446; and Facebook, P = .433).

Discussion

Of the 95 ACGME-accredited orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs identified, 20 posted about their fellowship on Instagram, 13 on X, 9 on Facebook, and 5 programs across all 3 social media platforms, indicating that a limited number of fellowships are present on social media. There was not high variability in the type of content posted on Instagram, X, and Facebook, as most posts were non-fellowship related. The level of engagement with fellowship profiles did not statistically differ across any social media platform.

We found that 44.2% of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships had at least 1 social media profile that posted about the program, with some profiles being more active than others. Only 21.1% of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs had an affiliated Instagram account that posted about the fellowship. A study by Wilson et al.10 examined orthopaedic hand surgery fellowships with active Instagram accounts and found that 24% of fellowships had accounts. The frequency of posting ranged from 0.29 to 24.68 posts per month. The majority of content posted was case examples, whereas content related to research was posted the least.10 In our study, Instagram was the most common social media platform used to promote a program’s orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship, and Facebook was the least common. Limniou et al.11 found that 82% of all social media users 18 to 30 years of age prefer Instagram. In contrast, 74% of social media users 31 years and older prefer Facebook. Fewer Facebook profiles could be a reflection of fellowship programs transitioning away from Facebook and toward Instagram accounts to target their average applicant’s age group.

Specifically looking at the Instagram platform, all but 2 orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs created their profile in 2020 following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings regarding Instagram accounts correspond with a similar trend observed by previous studies. Wilson et al.10 investigated social media use among orthopaedic hand surgery fellowships and found that 81% of Instagram accounts used by orthopaedic hand surgery fellowships were created after the onset of COVID-19 in 2020. Geller et al.3 analyzed the impact COVID-19 had on the use of social media by orthopaedic surgery residency programs and found that 77.4% of Instagram accounts were made after the start of COVID-19 in March 2020 through August 2021. Moving to a virtual format for interviews and relying on online information through program websites may have stimulated the increased use of Instagram by orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs following the COVID-19 pandemic.

There was no statistical variability in the types of posts between orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs and social media platforms. Most posts made on Instagram, X, and Facebook were unrelated to the orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship program, indicating a lack of focus on highlighting the program itself. Of the posts focusing on orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships, most posts made across all 3 platforms were used to provide more information about the respective sports medicine fellowship program, which included highlighting faculty, facilities, and unique program opportunities. Posts illustrating program culture, such as social events and group photos, were the second most common type of fellowship-related posts. Programs may tend to focus their social media content on specific fellowship details in order to more successfully recruit applicants. Butler et al.6 evaluated the perspective of program directors and orthopaedic surgery residency applicants on social media usage and its impact on the residency application process. The authors found that many prospective orthopaedic surgery applicants prioritize work-life balance within residency. Furthermore, the results of their study show that the posts related to departmental social outings were among the most influential for an applicant’s perception of a program.6 However, our study showed that most orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship social media posts do not focus on the program itself. Therefore, orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships may benefit by using their social media platform to highlight the program’s culture to help prospective applicants determine their compatibility with the fellowship program. Programs may be able to enhance the recruitment process by changing their posting strategy to focus more on content that highlights the culture and social activities within the fellowship.

Level of engagement did not statistically differ across social media platforms, indicating that no platform was more interesting to potential applicants. This contrasts with previous studies, including a study by Wilson et al.,10 who found significant variability in the level of engagement depending on the content of posts of hand fellowship social media accounts. Higher levels of engagement were associated with posts displaying their fellows’ surgical experience such as skills labs, case examples, and team dynamics. Prospective applicants were less engaged with posts of content easily found on the program’s website, such as facility pictures and program logistics.7 This suggests that there is potential for orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs to enhance their engagement through social media platforms by strategically highlighting their fellows’ surgical experience. Overall, orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships should frequently post a variety of content to catch the attention of prospective applicants as they scroll through their social media feed.

Fellowship programs can use the information from this study to increase their social media presence. Instagram is likely the social media platform where potential applicants are most active. Fellowship programs should focus their posts on fellowship-specific content rather than general posts. Lastly, programs should prioritize showcasing surgical experiences, program culture, and social events as these types of content have been shown to engage applicants.

Given the low amount of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship programs on social media, analysis of posting trends to determine recruitment efficacy was limited. Future studies should look at other orthopaedic surgery fellowships with a higher program presence on social media to analyze any posting strategies that enhance recruitment of fellowship applicants. As social media continues to grow and fellowship recruitment remains mostly virtual, this study should be repeated to include any potential new orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship accounts and posts.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. We acknowledge that we may not have been able to identify all social media accounts for the sports fellowships due to social media account names that were not clearly tied directly to the fellowship. To decrease the potential for nonresponder bias, we cross-referenced each program’s official website for any linked social media accounts. Content themes were subjective, and thus categorizing posts was not a standardized process, which increased the potential for sampling bias. Engagement with programs’ social media profiles was used to determine an applicant’s interest in the programs, but we were unable to distinguish if these interactions came solely from prospective applicants. Due to this, we cannot definitively conclude which content (educational, surgical, social, program information, visiting lecture, research, or non-fellowship-related posts) is most desired by prospective applicants and the success rate of recruitment by each post. Additionally, we did not take into consideration the timeline of posts. Some programs recently began posting more frequently, while other programs have not posted for years. Outliers in posting frequency could have impacted the accuracy of the mean posts per week as a representation of account activity. Inconsistent posting may have also impacted the analysis of engagement and not accurately reflected current prospective applicant interests in a program’s social media. Due to the number of posts by each Facebook profile, manual data collection only went back 5 years. Although we did not collect data from the inception of Facebook accounts similar to Instagram and X, the data collected should still be representative of each Facebook profile’s activity. Lastly, Instagram has a feature where the number of likes on some posts is visible only to the posting account. Instagram also offers additional postings that disappear after 24 hours, known as stories. Because stories are temporary, we are unable to include posts on stories in our data, which may impact analysis.

Conclusions

Although Instagram was identified as the most favored platform, the overall social media presence of orthopaedic sports medicine fellowships across all platforms was limited. Content analysis revealed a predominant focus on posts unrelated to the program’s orthopaedic sports medicine fellowship.
