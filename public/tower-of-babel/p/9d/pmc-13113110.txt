Parabacteroides goldsteinii-derived outer membrane vesicles alleviate acute lung injury via modulation of bile acid metabolism
Authors: Wensi Zhu, Linxiao Han, Ludan He, Shuoyan Wei, Juan Li, Liangcao Xin, Haiyun Zhang, Jie Shen, Yuanlin Song, Jiebai Zhou, Chih-Jung Chang, Jian Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe clinical syndrome with limited therapeutic options. Acute lung injury (ALI) is widely used as an experimental animal model that recapitulates the key pathological features of human ARDS. Parabacteroides goldsteinii, a newly identified Gram-negative probiotic, exhibits anti-inflammatory effects in certain disease models. Gram-negative bacteria release nanoscale structures called outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), which show varying composition across species. The role of P. goldsteinii-derived OMVs (Pg-OMVs) in ALI or ARDS remains to be elucidated.ResultIn this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of Pg-OMVs in a bleomycin (BLM)-induced ALI mouse model and explored their effects on pulmonary inflammation and gut microbiota composition. Compared to mice receiving BLM alone, Pg-OMV-treated mice exhibited significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Notably, Pg-OMV treatment significantly altered the gut microbiota composition, characterized by an increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila and a decreased abundance of Clostridia_bacterium. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments confirmed that the protective effects of Pg-OMVs were mediated via gut-lung axis. Further analysis revealed elevated cholic acid (CA) levels in the peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid following Pg-OMV treatment. CA was shown to suppress BLM-induced macrophage pyroptosis in the lung. Pharmacological inhibition of CA reversed the protective effects of Pg-OMVs, further confirming its pivotal role.ConclusionsIn summary, Pg-OMVs increased the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila while decreasing the abundance of Clostridia_bacterium in the gut, elevated systemic CA levels, and suppressed macrophage pyroptosis via inhibition of the NF-κB pathway, thereby attenuating pulmonary inflammation and ultimately alleviating ALI. These findings highlight a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of ALI or ARDS by targeting the gut-lung axis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04288-3.Keywords: Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Parabacteroides goldsteinii, outer membrane vesicles, cholic acid, pyroptosis

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), a severe respiratory condition with a high mortality rate, is characterized by progressive hypoxemia and diffuse pulmonary inflammation [1, 2]. According to the 2012 Berlin Definition, ARDS is classified as mild, moderate, and severe based on the degree of hypoxemia. In experimental studies, acute lung injury (ALI) is commonly used to describe animal models that recapitulate key pathological features of human ARDS [3]. ARDS or ALI is caused by various factors, including severe infection, trauma, shock, and massive blood transfusion, all of which promote the recruitment and activation of neutrophils and macrophages [4], triggering excessive inflammatory responses. This inflammatory response damages alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial cells, leading to diffuse pulmonary interstitial and alveolar edema, which impairs oxygen exchange and diffusion [5].

Macrophage pyroptosis, a lytic form of programmed cell death [6], is initiated by inflammasome activation [7], which in turn activates caspase-1 [8]. Activated caspase-1 cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), leading to pore formation in the plasma membrane and the release of mature interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18 [9, 10]. The subsequent secretion of these pro-inflammatory cytokines amplifies the inflammatory response and contributes to tissue damage.

Despite substantial advances in understanding the pathogenesis of ALI or ARDS, current primary interventions remain largely nonpharmacological, including mechanical ventilation, fluid management, nutritional support [11] and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) [12]. However, these treatments show limited effectiveness, with mortality rates still ranging from 40% to 60% [11]. Thus, the identification of novel and effective therapeutic strategies remains a critical clinical need.

Recent studies on the gut-lung axis have highlighted the critical role of the gut microbiota in the progression of ARDS and ALI. Patients with ARDS exhibit a marked reduction in beneficial commensal bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium, alongside an increased abundance of pathogenic or opportunistic bacteria, including Escherichia coli and Streptococcus [13]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from healthy donors into individuals with disrupted gut microbial communities has been shown to confer therapeutic benefits. Beyond microbial composition, metabolites derived from the gut microbiota-particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and bile acids-play pivotal roles in regulating the gut microenvironment and host physiological functions [4]. Consequently, modulation of the gut-lung axis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for ARDS.

However, the clinical application of FMT or live bacterial therapies is limited by safety, regulatory, and ethical concerns [14], highlighting the need for non-viable, microbiota-derived strategies with improved controllability. Recently, gut microbiota-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as key mediators of host-microbiota crosstalk, coordinating immune and metabolic responses across multiple organs [15]. In gram-negative bacteria, these vesicles originate primarily from the outer membrane and are referred to as outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) [16]. OMVs contain lipopolysaccharides (LPS), proteins, nucleic acids, and, in the case of pathogens, toxins, and virulence factors [17–19].

Studies have demonstrated that OMVs derived from engineered bacteria can activate the intestinal mucosal immune response, promote CD8+ T cell generation, and inhibit the growth of metastatic melanoma in the lungs [20]. Similarly, Lactobacillus-derived OMVs have been reported to exert antidepressant-like effects in murine models, illustrating the functional significance of bacterial gut-lung and gut-brain axes [21]. Beyond these direct effects, accumulating evidence suggests that OMVs can indirectly influence distal organ function by reshaping gut microbiota composition and metabolic activity. For example, Akkermansia muciniphila-derived OMVs increase the relative abundance of beneficial commensal bacterial and attenuate colitis [22], whereas Faecalibacterium prausnitzii-derived OMVs alleviate porcine epidemic diarrhea virus infection by modulating gut microbiota metabolism [23].

The genus Parabacteroides comprises commensal gram-negative bacteria found in the gut [24], the abundance of which is associated with the pathogenesis of several human diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease [25] and psoriasis [26]. Recently, Parabacteroides goldsteinii has attracted increasing scientific attention owing to its anti-inflammatory effects demonstrated in obese and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease mouse models, with its abundance negatively correlating with pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and IL-1β [27, 28]. However, whether OMVs secreted by P. goldsteinii exert anti-inflammatory effects in ARDS or ALI remains unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively analyze the role of P. goldsteinii-derived OMVs (Pg-OMVs) in a murine model of ALI and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Methods

Extraction and identification of Pg-OMVs

Pg-OMVs were extracted from cultured P. goldsteinii (JCM 13446) using ultracentrifugation (Hitachi, CP100MX, Tokyo, Japan). The procedure was as follows. (1) P. goldsteinii solution was coarsely filtered through a filter bag (500 mesh), followed by filtration using a ceramic membrane (0.2 MPa) (GuoChu Technology, Model GCM-C-03, Xiamen, China) to obtain the filtrate. (2) The filtrate was then concentrated by tangential flow filtration using a 100 kDa membrane (GuoChu Technology, Model GCM-S-02). (3) P. goldsteinii filtrate was centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. (4) The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C to remove larger vesicles. (5) The resultant supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. (6) The filtrate was then centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C. (7) The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of pre-chilled 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). (8) Ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000 × g for 70 min, at 4 °C. (9) After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of 1× PBS, and the Pg-OMVs were collected and stored at -80 °C.

Animals

C57BL/6J male mice (8 weeks old, approximately 25 g) were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and randomly assigned to three experimental groups: (1) Control, (2) bleomycin (BLM), and (3) BLM_OMVs. Based on our previous research protocol, mice in the BLM and BLM_OMVs groups were administered BLM (3.5 mg/kg, Abmole, Houston, TX, USA) via intratracheal instillation on day 0 to induce a murine model of ALI, while the Control group received an equivalent volume of PBS [29]. Mice in the BLM_OMVs group were administered Pg-OMVs (300 µg) via gavage at 2 h, 1 d, 3 d, and 5 d following BLM administration. All animals were housed at Zhongshan Hospital in a controlled environment, with 24 °C temperature, 40%-60% relative humidity, and 12 h light/dark cycle. The mice had ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (approval no.: 2023.093).

Generation of germ-free mice

To obtain germ-free mice, the gut microbiota was depleted using oral antibiotic administration via intragastric gavage for 10 d. The antibiotics used were: ampicillin sodium (120 mg/kg; HY-B0522A; MedChemExpress [MCE], Shanghai, China), vancomycin hydrochloride (60 mg/kg; HY17362; MCE), neomycin sulfate (120 mg/kg; HY-B-0470; MCE), and metronidazole (60 mg/kg; HY-B0318; MCE).

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments

Fecal pellets were collected from the Control, BLM, and BLM_OMVs groups. According to a previously described method [28], fecal pellets were suspended in PBS at a concentration of 100 mg/mL. After complete dissolution, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 250 × g for 5 min. Fecal suspensions were sequentially passed through 100 μm, 70 μm, and 40 μm filters to remove large debris, undigested food particles, and residual solids. The supernatant was collected and stored at -80 °C. Germ-free mice were randomly assigned to three experimental groups: (1) Con_Control_FMT, (2) BLM_BLM_FMT, and (3) OMVs_BLM_FMT. Fecal microbiota suspensions from the Control, BLM, and BLM_OMVs groups were gavaged daily to the corresponding germ-free mice in the Con_Control_FMT, BLM_BLM_FMT, and OMVs_BLM_FMT groups for 14 d consecutively. On day 7, mice in the BLM_BLM_FMT, and OMVs_BLM_FMT groups received BLM instillation via the airway, whereas those in the Con_Control_FMT, received PBS. All mice were euthanized 7 days after BLM treatment, and tissues were collected.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

The upper lobe of the right lung was infiltrated with 4% formalin for 24 h and subsequently embedded in paraffin. HE staining was performed on 3 μm tissue sections according to a previously described protocol [30]. Lung injury was assessed in a blinded manner as previously described [31]. The histological scoring criteria included the following features: (1) alveolar congestion; (2) hemorrhage; (3) thickening of the alveolar membrane; and (4) infiltration of neutrophils into the airspace or vessel wall. Each parameter was graded on a scale of 0 to 4 as follows: 0 = no injury; 1 = injury involving < 25% of the observed area; 2 = 25%-50% involvement; 3 = 50%-75% involvement; and 4 = > 75% involvement.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin Sections (3 μm) were used for IF. Following the tissue retrieval procedure outlined in our previous study [32], the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GSDMD-N (1:100; ab215203; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and F4/80 (1:100; 11-4801-81; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The following day, Alexa Fluor® 647 goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (H + L) (A 21244; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for staining, and the samples were analyzed under a confocal microscopy (FV3000RS, Olympus, Japan). The positively stained cells were assessed using Fiji (an ImageJ distribution, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

For cells, after stimulation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 20 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA, ST2249, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with anti-GSDMD-N primary antibody (1:100; Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. The following day, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with AF488-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (A0423, Beyotime Biotechnology). Nuclei were counterstained using DAPI (P0131; Beyotime Biotechnology), and images were captured by confocal microscopy.

Quantitative real-time-PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA was extracted from mouse lung tissue according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RN001; Shanghai Yishan Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China), and cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription (RR036A; Takara Biotechnology, Osaka, Japan). The target genes were amplified by TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR420A, Takara Biotechnology). Primer sequences for the target genes are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting

Lung tissue (20 mg) was homogenized in 200 µL of RIPA lysis buffer (PC01; Epizyme, Shanghai, China). The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Next, 5× loading buffer (LT101, Epizyme) was added to the protein supernatant, followed by incubation in a metal bath at 95 °C for 10 min to achieve complete protein denaturation. All protein samples, at appropriate concentrations, were separated using SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) (8242T; Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, MA, USA), phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-NF-κB p65) (3033T; Cell Signaling Technology), NLRP3 (ab263899; Abcam), cleaved caspase-1 (89332T; CST), GSDMD (ab209845; Abcam), IL-1β (ab254360; Abcam), and β-actin (AF7018; Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA). The following day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST (PS103; Epizyme) for 10 min each and incubated with an anti-rabbit secondary antibody (7074T; Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein expression was visualized using a Tanon imaging system (Shanghai, China) [32].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of IL-6 and IL-1β in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were quantified using IL-6 DuoSet (DY406; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and IL-1β DuoSet (DY401; R&D Systems) ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted from lung tissues using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The expression levels of each transcript were calculated as transcripts per million reads. RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization was used to quantify the gene abundance. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with |log2FC| ≥1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed [33]. Data analysis was performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com).

Metagenomic sequencing

Fecal samples were collected from the mice at the time of euthanasia for analysis. DNA was extracted using the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (Magnetic Bead) (MJYH, Shanghai, China). After assessing the concentration, purity, and integrity of the DNA, 350 bp fragments were selected for paired-end library construction. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 Plus platform (Illumina Inc.) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. according to the manufacturer’s instructions (www.illumina.com). Data analysis was conducted using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com).

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to assess the infiltration of inflammatory cells into the lungs. First, BALF was collected, and cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 5 min. Leukocytes were identified using PE anti-mouse CD45 (157603; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), while neutrophils were identified using FITC anti-mouse Ly-6G (127605; BioLegend) and PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse/human CD11b (101215; BioLegend) antibodies.

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from mouse lung tissues and stained with Zombie Aqua™ Fixable Viability Kit (423101, BioLegend) to exclude dead cells. Cells were then labeled with APC-Cy7 anti-mouse CD45 to identify hematopoietic cells. After gating on live CD45+ cells, Ly6G+ (PE/Cyanine7, 127618, BioLegend) neutrophils were excluded. Pulmonary macrophages, including alveolar macrophages (AMs) and interstitial macrophages (IMs), were identified as CD64+ cells using APC anti-mouse CD64 (17-0641-80, Thermo), based on canonical macrophage markers, such as CD64 and MerTK [34]. For assessment of pyroptosis, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained intracellularly with PE-conjugated anti-GSDMD antibody (ab246713, Abcam). Pulmonary macrophage pyroptosis was defined as the proportion of GSDMD+ cells within the CD45+Ly6G−CD64+ population. Data were acquired on a BD flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Serum samples were collected, and metabolites were extracted using a methanol: water (1:1, v/v) solution. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Data processing and metabolite identification were conducted using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com).

Cholic acid inhibition

Mice were randomly divided into four groups. A4250 (10 mg/kg, HY-109120, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China), a selective inhibitor of apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT) that disrupts enterohepatic bile acid reabsorption, was administered via oral gavage once daily, starting 1 day prior to BLM instillation for ALI model induction and continued until day 7. All mice were euthanized on day 7 for further analysis.

Cholic acid detection

Serum and BALF samples were collected for CA quantification using a Mouse Cholic Acid ELISA Kit (MET5007, Cell Biolabs, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, CA-conjugated antigen was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, 50 µL of standards and samples were added to the wells and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Plates were then washed three times, followed by the addition of the secondary antibody-HRP conjugate and a 1 h incubation. After adding the substrate solution, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell culture and stimulation

The immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophage (iBMDM) cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; SH30243.01, HyClone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% GlutaMAX (35050061, Gibco), and 0.04% β-mercaptoethanol (M6250, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. iBMDMs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. After incubation, cells were pretreated with CA (25 µM, HY-N0324, MCE) for 2 h, followed by stimulation with LPS (1 µg/mL, Sigma) for 5 h. Then, nigericin was added directly to the medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 1 h [35].

Targeted bile acid profiling

Targeted bile acid profiling was performed using LC-ESI-MS/MS (UHPLC-Qtrap) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Calibration solutions were prepared by mixing bile acid standards, and isotope-labeled internal standards were prepared at concentrations of 2000 ng/mL and 200 ng/mL. Calibration curves were generated by combining bile acid standards with internal standard solution (200 ng/mL, 1:1, v/v). Fecal samples (25 mg) were spiked with internal standard (2000 ng/mL) and extracted with organic solvent. The mixture was homogenized, sonicated, incubated at low-temperature, and centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant was analyzed. Targeted bile acid detection was conducted on an AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 + operating in negative electrospray ionization mode, with data acquisition and quantification performed using AB Sciex OS software.

Acute lung injury induced by LPS

C57BL/6 mice were orally administered Pg-OMVs or PBS daily via gavage for 7 consecutive days. On day 7, mice were intratracheally injected with LPS at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg to induce ALI model. Mice were sacrificed on day 10 (i.e., 3 days post-LPS administration) for sample collection.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. A t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and a one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis (for non-normally distributed data) test was applied for comparing differences involving more than two groups. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test.

Results

Preparation and identification of Pg-OMVs

Figure 1A illustrates the Pg-OMV isolation process. Cultured P. goldsteinii was harvested and subjected to ultracentrifugation to isolate OMVs originating from P. goldsteinii (Fig. 1A). To confirm the identity of the isolated material as EVs, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to examine the morphology, and nanoFCM was used to determine the diameter. The extracted particles exhibited a cup-shaped structure typical of OMVs (Fig. 1B), with a mean diameter of 74.8 ± 11.8 nm (Fig. 1C). These results demonstrate that the particles obtained through ultracentrifugation represent membrane vesicles derived from P. goldsteinii (Pg-OMVs).

We further performed proteomic analysis of Pg-OMVs. In total, 30,743 peptides corresponding to 2,258 protein groups were identified (Fig. S1A). To characterize the protein composition of the isolated OMVs, subcellular localization analysis was conducted after excluding proteins with ambiguous localization. The results showed that a substantial proportion of the identified proteins were associated with membrane-related and extracellular compartments. Specifically, outer membrane proteins accounted for 29% of the total proteins, followed by cytoplasmic membrane proteins (6.6%), periplasmic proteins (3.6%), and extracellular proteins (1.5%). The pronounced enrichment of outer membrane proteins, together with proteins from periplasmic and membrane compartments, is consistent with the known biogenesis and heterogeneous composition of bacterial OMVs, providing strong proteomic evidence for the successful isolation of OMVs (Fig. S1B and Supplementary Table e1). Gene Ontology (GO) cellular component enrichment analysis further confirmed significant representation of “membrane” and “membrane part”, supporting the membrane-derived nature of the isolated vesicles (Fig. 1D). Consistently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in metabolic pathways and O-antigen nucleotide sugar biosynthesis (Fig. 1E). Notably, the latter pathway is closely linked to lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis and outer membrane assembly in Gram-negative bacteria, further supporting the outer membrane origin of Pg-OMVs. Collectively, these proteomic data demonstrate that Pg-OMVs are characterized by a distinct enrichment of outer membrane-associated proteins and LPS-related biosynthetic pathways, providing molecular evidence for the successful isolation and characterization of Pg-derived outer membrane vesicles.

We further mapped the identified miRNAs to small RNA families and quantified the number of miRNA families shared with Pg-OMVs across species. The top three species were Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, and Gallus gallus (Fig. S1C). GO analysis of the top20 miRNA target genes indicated enrichment in biological processes related to lipid localization and negative regulation of immune system processes (Fig. S1D).

Pg-OMVs alleviated BLM-induced ALI and leukocyte infiltration

ALI models are commonly established via airway instillation of BLM, with the inflammatory response typically peaking on day 7 [36]. To investigate the effect of Pg-OMVs on lung injury, ALI was induced in mice and Pg-OMVs were administered at different doses in preliminary experiments. Treatment with 300 µg Pg-OMVs was identified as the minimal effective dose that significantly alleviated BLM-induced lung injury (Fig. S2A-B). Notably, administration of Pg-OMVs alone did not cause detectable pathological changes in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, or ileum, compared with control mice, indicating a favorable safety profile (Fig. S2C). Based on these results, 300 µg was selected for all subsequent experiments. Figure 2A shows the experimental design for oral Pg-OMVs administration. In the BLM group, mortality occurred on days 4, 5, and 7, indicating a 50% survival rate. However, Pg-OMVs administration significantly improved survival, with no fatalities by day 7, indicating a 100% survival rate (Fig. 2B). Histopathological evaluation of lung tissues using HE staining revealed severe alveolar destruction following BLM administration, along with significant infiltration of erythrocytes, neutrophils, and other immune cells into the alveolar spaces (Fig. 2C-D). Treatment with Pg-OMVs alleviated alveolar damage and reduced the infiltration of erythrocytes and immune cells, including neutrophils, into the alveolar space (Fig. 2E-G). Furthermore, analysis of inflammatory factor concentrations in BALF demonstrated that BLM increased the secretion of IL-6 and IL-1β, whereas Pg-OMV treatment significantly reduced IL-6 and IL-1β secretion (Fig. 2H-I).

Pg-OMVs mitigated macrophage pyroptosis via regulation of the NF-κB-NLRP3 signaling pathway

RNA-Seq analysis was performed on the lung tissue. As shown in the Venn diagram (Fig. S3A), 14,430 genes were expressed in all three groups. Compared to the Control group, 1,966 genes exhibited increased expression, whereas 2,409 genes showed decreased expression following BLM instillation (Fig. 3A). After Pg-OMV administration, 56 genes were upregulated and 105 genes were downregulated compared with those in the BLM group (Fig. 3B). These DEGs were further analyzed to identify potential regulatory molecules affected by Pg-OMVs, revealing 88 genes with increased expression after BLM instillation but decreased expression after Pg-OMV treatment (Fig. 3C). In contrast, an additional 42 genes showed a decrease in expression upon Pg-OMV treatment (Fig. S3B-C). A heat map of 88 differential expression genes is shown in Fig. 3D. KEGG analysis predominantly identified such as the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and chemokine signaling pathways (Fig. 3E). Reactome enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and pyroptosis, suggesting that Pg-OMVs exert anti-inflammatory effects via the NLRP3 inflammasome (Fig. 3F).

Western blot analysis revealed increased expression of phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-NF-κB p65), NLRP3, and cleaved caspase-1. Moreover, the release of the N-terminal domain of GSDMD and IL-1β (IL-1β p17) was enhanced. Notably, Pg-OMVs mitigated this process through the suppression of NF-κB phosphorylation, thus attenuating BLM-induced lung injury (Fig. 4B-C).

As previously reported, pyroptosis predominantly occurs in macrophages and plays a critical role in ALI development [37–39]. Immunofluorescence analysis using F4/80 to identify macrophages and GSDMD-N as a pyroptosis marker revealed that BLM administration markedly enhanced macrophage pyroptosis compared with the Control group, whereas this effect was alleviated by Pg-OMV treatment (Fig. 4D-E). To further validate these findings, pulmonary macrophage pyroptosis was assessed by flow cytometry. Pulmonary macrophages were gated as CD45+Ly6G−CD64+ cells, and pyroptotic cells were identified via intracellular GSDMD staining (Fig. S4). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of GSDMD+ macrophages in the BLM group, which was substantially reduced following Pg-OMV treatment (Fig. 4F-G).

Oral administration of Pg-OMVs modulates the gut microbiota composition

Numerous studies have highlighted the existence of the gut-lung axis, which connects the lungs and gut, wherein alterations in the gut microbiota can exert profound effects on lung phenotypes [40]. Metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples collected from the three groups of mice (Fig. 5A) revealed 5,199 bacterial species across the three groups (Fig. S5A). Alpha diversity indices were calculated, and the Sobs and Ace indices indicated community richness and diversity, whereas no significant differences in bacterial species composition were observed among the three groups (Fig. S5B).

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) results demonstrated significant differences in microbiota composition at the species level across the three groups (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.74, p = 0.004) (Fig. 5B). Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) further revealed that inter-group differences were significantly greater than intra-group variations (R = 0.893, p = 0.001) (Fig. S5C), highlighting the impact of Pg-OMVs on the gut microbiota composition.

At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the dominant components of the gut microbiota (Fig. 5C). Compared with the Control group, the BLM group exhibited an increased relative abundance of Firmicutes and a concomitant reduction in Bacteroidota, leading to an elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio, indicative of global microbial dysbiosis. Notably, Pg-OMVs administration partially reduced the F/B ratio, suggesting a restoration of gut microbial homeostasis (Fig. 5D).

Next, species-level composition was analyzed (Fig. 5E). Among the detected species, Akkermansia muciniphila (A.muciniphila) and Clostridia_bacterium showed significant differences in relative abundance among the three groups (Fig. S5D). To identify microbiota with significant differences among the three experimental conditions, we performed Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA threshold = 3, p < 0.05). At the species level, Clostridia_bacterium (LDA = 4.3239, p = 0.03899) was significantly enriched in the BLM group compared to that in the Control group. In contrast, A.muciniphila (LDA = 5.04382, p = 0.02732) showed the highest relative abundance in the BLM-OMVs group (Fig. 5F). Cladogram analysis further revealed a restructuring of the fecal microbiota at multiple taxonomic levels among the three groups (Fig. 5G). Notably, the relative abundance of A.muciniphila was negatively correlated with the concentrations of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β. While Clostridia_bacterium and Muribaculum_sp._NM65_B17 showed positive correlations with IL-6 and IL-1β levels (Fig. 5H).

Pg-OMVs alleviate BLM-induced ALI via the gut-lung axis

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) was performed to investigate the functional role of the gut microbiota, as illustrated in Fig. 6A. Compared with the Con_Control_FMT group, mice in the BLM_BLM_FMT group, which received fecal microbiota from BLM-treated donors, exhibited significant weight loss (Fig. 6B). Although the difference in survival was not statistically significant, the OMVs_BLM_FMT group tended to show improved survival on day 7 compared with the BLM_BLM_FMT group (Fig. 6C). Histopathological analysis revealed severe destruction of alveolar cavities in the lungs of the BLM_BLM_FMT group, with extensive infiltration of immune and erythrocyte cells (Fig. 6D-E). Flow cytometry revealed a significant increase in leukocyte and neutrophil counts in the BLM_BLM_FMT group (Fig. 6F-G). Moreover, IL-6 and IL-1β concentrations were elevated in the BLM_BLM_FMT group (Fig. 6H). However, FMT from mice in the BLM_OMVs group mitigated these phenotypic changes, leading to increased body weight and reduced lung damage, immune cell infiltration, and secretion of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-1β) induced by BLM.

To verify the role of CA, A4250, a potent and selective inhibitor of the ileal apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT), was administered to block CA reabsorption and reduce its systemic levels (Fig. 8F). A4250 administration attenuated the protective effects of Pg-OMVs against BLM-induced ALI, as indicated by greater weight loss and higher mortality in the BLM_OMVs_A4250 group compared to that in the BLM_OMVs group (Fig. 8G-H). HE staining further revealed more severe inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar destruction in the BLM_OMVs_A4250 group than in the BLM_OMVs group (Fig. 8I-J). Analysis of CA levels in plasma and BALF showed that Pg-OMV treatment increased CA concentrations compared with BLM group. Notably, A4250 significantly reduced CA levels in both plasma and BALF compared to the levels in the BLM_OMVs group (Fig. 8K-L). In addition, the levels of IL-6 and IL-1β in BALF were elevated following A4250 treatment (Fig. 8M-N).

Western blotting analysis was further conducted to assess pyroptosis in lung tissue. The results showed that A4250 administration increased pyroptosis in the BLM_OMVs_A4250 group compared to that in the BLM_OMVs group, as evidenced by elevated expression of p-NF-κB p65, cleaved caspase-1, NLRP3, GSDMD-N, and IL-1β p17 (Fig. 8O-P).

We next investigated whether the elevation of systemic CA was mediated by alterations in the Pg-OMVs-modulated gut microbiota. To evaluate the contribution of baseline gut microbiota to BLM-induced lung injury, a Control_BLM_FMT group was included, in which fecal microbiota from control mice were transplanted into recipient mice followed by BLM administration. Compared with mice receiving microbiota from BLM-treated donors, Control_BLM_FMT mice exhibited partial attenuation of lung injury and reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-1β (Fig. S7A-C). However, this intervention did not significantly suppress lung macrophage pyroptosis, as assessed by flow cytometry (Fig. S7D-E).

In contrast, mice receiving fecal microbiota from Pg-OMVs-treated donors exhibited significantly higher CA concentrations in both plasma and BALF compared with mice transplanted with microbiota from either BLM-treated or control donors under BLM challenge (Fig. S7F). Notably, administration of A4250 abrogated the protective effects conferred by transplantation of fecal microbiota from the Pg-OMVs group (Fig. S7). Collectively, these results indicate that transplantation of control microbiota provides limited protection against BLM-induced inflammation, whereas Pg-OMVs-modified microbiota uniquely enhances systemic CA availability, which is associated with suppression of macrophage pyroptosis. To further characterize intestinal bile acid remodeling following Pg-OMVs treatment, targeted bile acid metabolomics was performed on fecal samples from Control, BLM and BLM_OMVs_groups. The analysis revealed a significant increase in the secondary bile acid deoxycholic acid (DCA) in the BLM group, whereas CA levels in feces did not differ significantly among groups (Fig. S7G-H). These findings suggest that Pg-OMVs treatment modulates bile acid transformation and distribution.

To identify potential microbial contributors to this bile acid remodeling and its systemic consequences, correlation analyses were conducted between the top 15 abundant bacterial species, ALI-related phenotypes, and CA/DCA levels. The relative abundance of A.muciniphila positively correlated with CA concentrations in plasma and BALF, and negatively correlated with fecal DCA levels and multiple BLM-induced ALI-associated inflammatory indices. Conversely, the relative abundance of Clostridia_bacterium was positively correlated with fecal DCA levels and BLM-induced ALI-associated phenotypes, but negatively correlated with plasma and BALF CA levels (Fig. S7I).

To further validate these findings in vitro, a macrophage pyroptosis model was established using iBMDMs (Fig. 9A) and treated with varying concentrations of CA. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that CA treatment significantly reduced Il6 and Il1b mRNA expression, with the strongest inhibitory effect observed at 20 µM (Fig. 9B). Consistently, measurement of cytokines in cell culture supernatants revealed that 20 µM CA markedly suppressed the secretion of IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. 9C). Western blot analysis showed that CA treatment inhibited macrophage pyroptosis by suppressing NF-κB phosphorylation and downregulating NLRP3 expression, thereby preventing cleavage of caspase-1 and GSDMD, as well as maturation of IL-1β (IL-1β p17) in iBMDMs (Fig. 9D). Confocal immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that CA markedly attenuated macrophage pyroptosis in vitro (Fig. 9E-F).

Discussion

P.goldsteinii is an intestinal commensal bacterium that has been reported to exert therapeutic effects in acute kidney injury [41], obesity [42], and related metabolic disorders by maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and suppressing inflammatory responses [27]. However, most existing studies have focused on live P.goldsteinii, whereas the biological functions and therapeutic potential of P.goldsteinii-derived outer membrane vesicles in ALI remain largely unexplored. In the present study, we demonstrate for the first time that Pg-OMVs ameliorate ALI through a gut microbiota-dependent mechanism.

Previous studies investigating the role of OMVs in ALI have predominantly focused on vesicles derived from pathogenic bacteria, such as Acinetobacter nosocomialis and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia. These pathogenic OMVs are enriched in pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), particularly lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [43], which can be recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on immune and non-immune cells, thereby triggering robust inflammatory responses and exacerbating lung injury [44]. In contrast, evidence regarding the role of commensal- or probiotic-derived OMVs in ALI is limited. Notably, unlike the hexa-acylated lipid A of Escherichia_coli LPS, which is highly pro-inflammatory, P.goldsteinii-derived LPS contains a penta-acylated lipid A structure and lacks a direct homolog of the acyltransferase gene LpxM, resulting in markedly attenuated inflammatory signaling [28]. Furthermore, both P.goldsteinii and its derived LPS have been proved to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production and alleviate lung inflammation in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [28]. These properties likely explain why Pg-OMVs did not induce overt inflammatory responses or tissue damage in multiple organs in our study. Importantly, our data further indicate that Pg-OMVs attenuate ALI by limiting macrophage pyroptosis which plays a crucial role in the pulmonary inflammatory cascade [45] and reducing the release of key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β.

In contrast to previous studies in which OMVs exerted direct regulatory effects on host tissues, accumulating evidence suggests that the biological functions of OMVs may depend on the presence of gut microbiota. For instance, A.muciniphila-derived OMVs have been shown to restore intestinal homeostasis by promoting the abundance of beneficial commensal bacteria [22], while Faecalibacterium prausnitzii-derived OMVs can selectively interact with specific gut microbiota and reprogram microbial metabolic activity to alleviate porcine epidemic diarrhea virus infection [23]. Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that the therapeutic effects of Pg-OMVs in BLM-induced ALI model are largely microbiota-dependent. Pg-OMV treatment partially restored gut microbial homeostasis disrupted by bleomycin, as evidenced by a reduction in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio. An elevated F/B ratio has frequently been reported in patients with ARDS and is considered a hallmark of intestinal dysbiosis associated with systemic inflammation [46, 47]. Importantly, A.muciniphila emerged as a dominant bacterial species following Pg-OMV-treatment. Notably, as a representative member of the phylum Verrucomicrobiota, A.muciniphila, is a well-recognized beneficial commensal that adheres to the intestinal mucus layer [48] and exerts protective effects in respiratory diseases. Previous studies have shown that A.muciniphila attenuates nanoplastic-induced lung inflammation and fibrosis [49], and that oral administration of A.muciniphila suppresses NF-κB signaling in lung tissue, thereby alleviating ALI [50]. The successful transfer of Pg-OMVs-mediated protection through FMT further supports a gut microbiota-dependent mechanism.

The gut microbiota constitutes a complex ecosystem that plays a pivotal role in host metabolic and immune homeostasis. In the present study, metabolomic profiling identified cholic acid (CA), a primary bile acid, as a key metabolite associated with Pg-OMV treatment. Bile acids are a major class of microbiota-modulated metabolites that function not only as digestive detergents but also as endogenous signaling molecules linking intestinal microbial activity to distal organ immunity, including the gut-heart [51] and gut-joint [52] axes. Our findings support CA as a central metabolic mediator through which Pg-OMVs exert immunoregulatory effects during ALI.

Mechanistically, CA has been reported to suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation [53], a critical driver of macrophage pyroptosis. Consistent with these reports, the protective effects of Pg-OMVs in our study were accompanied by increased systemic CA levels, supporting a role for CA in limiting inflammasome-mediated macrophage pyroptosis and pulmonary inflammation.

Notably, alterations in CA levels appear to result from coordinated regulation at both the synthesis and conversion stages of bile acid metabolism. A.muciniphila, which was enriched following Pg-OMV treatment, has been reported to promote hepatic expression of CYP8B1, the key enzyme required for CA synthesis. Increased CYP8B1 expression expands the CA pool and elevated systemic CA levels, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine release in peripheral tissue. In contrast, Clostridia_bacterium possess strong 7α-dehydroxylation activity, facilitating the conversion of primary bile acids into secondary bile acids [54, 55]. Consistently, targeted fecal bile acid profiling revealed increased levels of the secondary bile acid deoxycholic acid (DCA) in the BLM group. Correlation analysis further demonstrated that CA levels in plasma and BALF were positively associated with the abundance of A.muciniphila and negatively correlated with Clostridia bacterium. Collectively, these findings suggest that Pg-OMVs restore a microbial configuration that preserves the primary bile acid pool and enhances systemic CA availability.

The first seven days following intratracheal instillation of BLM represent the acute inflammatory phase commonly used to model ALI [29]. In addition to BLM-induced lung injury, LPS is also widely employed to establish experimental ALI model [3]. Accordingly, LPS was instilled intratracheally to induce ALI, followed by Pg-OMVs treatment. Based on previously established gut microbiota modulation strategies in LPS-induced ALI models [56], Pg-OMVs were administered one week prior to LPS challenge. Pg-OMVs treatment significantly reduced LPS-induced leukocyte infiltration, further supporting its therapeutic potential in inflammatory lung injury.

Given the safety concerns associated with clinical FMT, as well as the susceptibility of live bacterial therapies to environmental factors such as gastric acid, bile salts, and intestinal mucus, outer membrane vesicles represent a more stable and potentially safer therapeutic alternative [57]. Collectively, our findings highlight the therapeutic potential of Pg-OMVs in experimental ALI and propose a novel microbiota-based strategy for the treatment of ARDS.

Conclusions

In this study, Pg-OMVs derived from P. goldsteinii were successfully isolated, and their primary components were characterized through proteomic and small RNA-seq analyses, revealing close associations with inflammatory pathways. Our results demonstrated that the oral administration of Pg-OMVs modulated the gut microbiota in BLM-induced ALI mice, notably increasing the abundance of A.muciniphila and decreased the abundance of Clostridia_bacterium, which in turn reshape the host bile acid pool, and elevated circulating CA levels. The elevated CA suppressed NF-κB phosphorylation in the lung tissue, reduced inflammasome activation, and inhibited macrophage pyroptosis. These effects led to decreased secretion of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6, thereby attenuating ALI progression and improving survival rates in ALI mice. Unlike conventional therapies, Pg-OMVs exert their therapeutic effects via the gut-lung axis, offering advantages such as reduced systemic side effects and improved potential for long-term use. This study provides novel insights into the development of gut microbiota-derived vesicle therapies for ALI. Future research should aim to enhance Pg-OMVs production and evaluate their clinical applicability.
