Weber Classification Does Not Correlate with PROMIS Outcome Measures
Authors: Steven M. Hadley, John J. Peabody, Rachel Bergman, Sarah J. Westvold, Eric H. Durudogan, Frederic Washburn, Shaun Chang, Muhammad Y. Mutawakkil, Milap Patel, Anish R. Kadakia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:It remains unclear whether Weber subtype correlates with outcomes measured by validated Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) following ankle fracture surgery. We examine whether Weber subtype correlates with PROMIS.Methods:This was a retrospective review of 527 patients who underwent ankle fracture surgery at a single institution (2016-2021). Two fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons independently reviewed all radiographs. Of the 527 who were included in final analysis evaluating complications, 182 with Weber B and Weber C fractures completed PROMIS physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) measures. All 182 had anatomically reduced ankles and were included for analysis of PROMIS. Primary outcome was PROMIS.Results:Among survey responders (n = 182), no significant difference existed between Weber B and Weber C anatomically reduced in mean PF (52.3 ± 9.9 vs 53.5 ± 9.2, P = .47) and PI (48.4 ± 8.8 vs 47.0 ± 7.7, P = .34). In adjusted-regression, Weber B and Weber C were not significantly different for mean PF (53.8 vs 53.9, P = .92) or PI (48.0 vs 46.4, P = .27). Body mass indexes (BMIs) of 30 or greater had worse mean PF (P = .04) and PI (P < .01). Compared with males, females had worse mean PF (P < .001) and PI (P < .01). Older patients had lower PF compared to younger (P = .01). In overall cohort (n = 527), Weber C radiographic complication rate was 32.3% (41/127) vs 11.5% (46/400) Weber B (P < .001), with higher syndesmotic loss of reduction and early-onset degenerative joint disease (DJD) rates (9.4% vs 1%, P < .001; 11.8% vs 2.5%, P < .001, respectively). Weber C was 3.52 times (95% CI: 2.08-5.98) as likely to have radiographic complication as Weber B.Conclusion:Patients with anatomically reduced ankles may achieve population mean PROMIS regardless of Weber subtype. Although at early follow-up Weber C fractures have higher radiographic complication rates, including early-onset DJD, proper reduction and stabilization may be especially important to help patients achieve equivalent functional outcomes to patients with Weber B fractures. Higher BMI, female sex, and older age may negatively affect outcomes.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.

Introduction

The Danis-Weber, or Weber, classification of ankle fractures is a reliable system of categorizing fracture patterns based on the level of the primary fibular fracture with respect to the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis.1,2 Weber A fractures (WA) describe fractures distal to the syndesmosis, Weber B fractures (WB) at the level of the syndesmosis, and Weber C fractures (WC) proximal to the syndesmosis. Glen et al 3 demonstrated that the Weber classification provides a greater degree of interobserver and intraobserver reliability compared with other ankle fracture classification systems such as the AO/OTA and Lauge-Hansen systems.

However, mixed data exist regarding the utility of the Weber classification in correlating patient outcomes. Testa et al 4 demonstrated that WC fractures are a risk factor for poor outcomes of trimalleolar fractures. Conversely, Kennedy et al 5 reported that the Weber classification was only helpful in unimalleolar fractures. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the Weber subtype is associated with functional outcomes, especially when comparing operatively fixed WB and WC fractures.

The Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS), composed of physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) computerized adaptive tests (CATs), is an effective measure of clinical outcomes, especially with regard to ankle fractures.6-9 PROMIS exhibits high generalizability and demonstrates an effective ability to detect clinically important differences in patient function throughout various post-operative time points along with decreased floor and ceiling effects.6-9 Multiple studies support its reliability over other metrics such as Foot and Function Index, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure, and American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society scores.6-12

Therefore, this study investigates whether Weber fracture subtype is associated with clinical outcomes as measured by PROMIS to determine whether operative anatomic fracture reduction results in satisfactory outcomes regardless of severity of fracture pattern (WB vs WC). We hypothesize that that among fractures anatomically reduced operatively, outcomes would be similar regardless of Weber subtype. To our knowledge, no other study has compared PROMIS between Weber subtypes.

Methods

Population

This investigation is an institutional review board–approved retrospective review of 782 patients who underwent surgical repair of an ankle fracture at a single urban, Level 1 trauma center, academic institution (2016-2021). Two foot and ankle fellowship–trained orthopaedic surgeons (greater than 10 years of experience) independently assessed all radiographs and stratified them by Weber subtype, fixation technique, and reduction quality at the time of most recent radiographs obtained. Postoperative radiographs were compared to intraoperative fluoroscopic images. Reduction quality was determined by standard radiographic criteria evaluating radiographs of the ankle in anteroposterior (AP), lateral, and mortise views with the patient weight bearing. Anatomic reduction was determined via the following: medial clear space (MCS) <4 mm, superior clear space (SCS) <4 mm (ideally equal to MCS), tibiotalar congruity (defined as parallelism of the tibial plafond and talar dome articular surfaces), talocrural angle within normal range (~78°-83°), intact coin sign (unbroken arc between the lateral talar articular surface and the distal fibular recess), appropriate fibular rotation on the mortise view (assessed by symmetric fibular contour profile and lateral fossa cortex shadow compared with the contralateral side), appropriate syndesmotic reduction (defined as tibiofibular clear space on AP and mortise views of <6 mm, tibiofibular overlap on AP of >6 mm, tibiofibular overlap on mortise of >1 mm), posterior malleolar displacement <2 mm, medial malleolar displacement <2 mm, and no residual ankle displacement (defined as coronal plane distance ≤2 mm, talar tilt ≤3°, and sagittal plane distance ≤5 mm). Malreduction was defined as ankles not meeting these criteria. Loss of reduction was defined as change in alignment from previously reduced ankle to malreduced.13-20 Syndesmotic instability, as determined by standard radiographic criteria, was identified via intraoperative fluoroscopic and direct visual examination.13,21 Patients with multiple injuries at time of presentation, pilon fractures (defined as tibial plafond fractures secondary to axial load with intraarticular extension), Maisonneuve fractures, open fractures, neuropathic patients, and intraoperative malreduction were excluded. 22 WA fractures were excluded because of the low number of patients who had WA fractures operatively fixed, as the aim of this study was to compare outcomes between surgically treated WB and WC fractures. Finally, those without minimum one-year radiographic follow-up were excluded, thereby leaving 527 patients after exclusion criteria. These 527 fractures were evaluated for final analysis of radiographic complications.

For PROMIS analysis (ultimately n = 182), these 527 patients were contacted by email and phone for up to 5 attempts before ceasing contact attempts and asked to complete a post-operative PROMIS physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) CATs via REDCap. 23 From the initial 527 contacted, 218 patients responded. Eighteen had incomplete responses and 18 had non-anatomic reduction of the ankle at final follow-up. These 36 patients were therefore excluded, leaving 182 with anatomically reduced ankles who completed the questionnaire. These 182 with closed WB (n = 140) and WC (n = 42) fractures were included for final analysis of PROMIS. All surveys were voluntarily completed after obtaining written informed consent.

Therefore, 527 patients were evaluated for complications, and 182 with only anatomically reduced ankles who responded to the survey were analyzed for PROMIS as a subset from the 527 (study schema; Figure 1).

Study flowchart.

All surgeries were performed by fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons in either foot and ankle (n = 4) or trauma (n = 3) with minimum 5 years’ experience. By convention at our institution, those with trauma fellowship utilize syndesmotic screws and do not repair the deltoid ligament, whereas those with foot and ankle fellowship employ dynamic syndesmotic fixation techniques and repair the deltoid ligament.

Outcomes

Please see Figure 1, study schema, for illustration of which subgroup was evaluated for each analysis.

Primary Outcomes (n = 182)

The primary outcomes included mean PROMIS PF and PI among the 182 patients with anatomic reduction of the ankle at final follow-up. Both measures were developed for US general population mean score of 50 and used age-matched norms.7-9 Higher PF indicates better physical function; higher PI designates more pain.7-9 The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is a change of 3-30 for PF and 3-25 for PI.8,24

Secondary Outcomes (n = 527)

Secondary outcomes evaluated radiographically evident and surgical complications between WB and WC among the entire cohort (n = 527). Radiographic complications included syndesmotic loss of reduction, posterior malleolus loss of reduction, malleolar malunion, early-onset degenerative joint disease (DJD), and early-onset end-stage arthritis. Loss of reduction was defined as postoperative change in alignment/loss of reduction when the intraoperative reduction was considered anatomic. Posttraumatic osteoarthritis was defined using the Kellgren-Lawrence scale and as asymmetric narrowing and articular wear relative to the intraoperative reduction. 25 Surgical complications included deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), superficial wound complications, deep wound complications, and reoperation within 365 days.

Statistical Analysis

For the comparison of PROMIS among WB (n = 140) vs WC (n = 42), we determined a priori an MCID of 5 in PROMIS. To calculate power to detect an MCID of 5, we divided it by the overall sample SD. For PF (SD = 9.77), which had a larger overall SD than PI, the standardized effect size was 0.51 (5 / 9.77). Assuming 2-sided 2-sample t test with unequal group sizes, the power to detect a 5-point difference in PROMIS PF was 82% at α = 0.05. After applying a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.025, power was 74%. To account for multiple primary outcomes and reduce the risk of Type I error, statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value <.025 using Bonferroni correction. Parametric tests were used for comparisons between groups. We also conducted multivariable linear regression to estimate adjusted mean PROMIS for WB and WC. These means were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI because of their statistically significant (P < .05) association with PROMIS in unadjusted analysis and evidence suggesting that they may impact outcomes.26-29 Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate associations with radiographic complications, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and posterior malleolus involvement because of evidence suggesting these factors may impact complications.26-35 We did not perform a subgroup analysis evaluating deltoid repair because a previous study of the same cohort of patients comparing deltoid repair to no repair has already been published detailing these results. 36 We did not have the statistical power to compare PROMIS between those with an anatomic reduction (n = 182) to those without an anatomic reduction (n = 18), as comparing such a large number of patients to such a small number would result in unreliable estimates. R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used.

Surgical Technique

All fractures were operatively fixed according to AO principles with fluoroscopic guidance. When clinically indicated, syndesmotic reduction and stabilization were performed with either the Syndesmosis TightRope XP (TightRope, Arthrex), InternalBrace (Arthrex), or syndesmotic screws, and the deltoid ligament complex was repaired with DX FiberTak all-suture anchor (Arthrex).21,36 Syndesmotic fixation was determined by intraoperative syndesmotic instability as measured by intraoperative fluoroscopic stress examination and direct visual examination. 21 Deltoid ligament rupture was radiographically identified by injury films, defined as a ≥2-mm difference between the superior clear space and the medial clear space, or stress positive with same criteria. 36 Anatomic reduction and adequate fixation were confirmed intraoperatively prior to wound closure. Postoperatively, the ankle was placed in a short leg plaster splint in neutral position. Patients were instructed to remain non-weightbearing until postoperative evaluation in clinic. After transitioning out of the splint, patients began weightbearing under physical therapy supervision regardless of presence of syndesmotic injury. Per institutional protocol, all patients remain non-weight-bearing for 6 weeks postoperatively. Post-operative protocol was standardized regardless of surgeon at this institution.

Results

PROMIS (n = 182)

For PROMIS analysis, 182 (140 WB and 42 WC; Table 1) patients were included. The mean clinical (time of surgery to survey completion) follow-up was 40.0 ± 11.2 months. No significant differences in PF (P = .67) or PI (P = .47) were found by length of clinical follow-up beyond 1 year. Posterior malleolus fractures (n = 56) did not result in statistically different mean PF (P = .68) and PI (P = .55) compared with fractures without posterior malleolus involvement (Figures 2 and 3). The presence of a syndesmosis injury (n = 90) similarly did not result in significantly different mean PF (P = .83) and PI (P = .65) compared with no syndesmotic injury. Comparing WB with syndesmotic disruption to WC with syndesmotic disruption revealed no difference in mean PF and PI (P = .18 and P = .13, respectively).

Fracture subtype classified by both Weber and number of malleoli involved did not result in significant differences in PROMIS (Supplementary Table S2).

Compared to patients with lower preoperative BMI, those with BMI ≥30 had lower mean PF (P = .04) and higher PI (P < .01) than those with BMI <30 (Table 2). Compared with males, females had worse mean PF (49.8 ± 7.8 vs 58.4 ± 11.0, P < .001) and worse mean PI (49.3 ± 8.5 vs 45.5 ± 8.1, P = .01). Age resulted in a significant difference for PF (P = .01) with younger patients reporting higher PF, although no significant differences in PI (P = .70).

Complications (n = 527)

For complications analysis, 527 (400 WB and 127 WC, Table 1) with minimum 1-year radiographic follow-up were included. Mean radiographic follow-up in this cohort was 14.1 ± 1.3 months.

In the overall cohort (n = 527), WC radiographic complication rate was 32.3% (41/127) vs 11.5% (46/400) among WB (P < .001), with higher syndesmotic loss of reduction and early-onset DJD rates (9.4% vs 1.0%, P < .001, and 11.8% vs 2.5%, P < .001, respectively). When restricting to just fractures with syndesmotic injuries (n = 255), WC had higher complication rates compared to WB (33.6%, 41/122 vs 10.5%, 14/133 P < .001) with higher rates of syndesmotic loss of reduction (9.8% vs 3.0%, P = .05) and early-onset DJD (12.3% vs 1.5%, P < .001, Table 3).

The majority (88%, 22/25) had loss of reduction. The 3 patients with early-onset DJD who did not have loss of reduction (and thus were still included in the survey group of 182) all had stage 1 DJD. Therefore, 1.6% (3/182) of patients had early-onset DJD in the survey response group vs 4.7% overall (25/527). Categorized by Weber subtype, the incidence of early-onset DJD in those who responded to the survey vs those who did not, respectively, were as follows: WB 2/140 (1.4% ) vs 10/400 (2.5%) and WC 1/42 (2.4%) vs 15/127 (11.8%).

Discussion

The results of this study contribute to the body of literature that demonstrates that surgical repair of unstable ankle fractures via open reduction and internal fixation is associated with satisfactory clinical outcomes.21,36-42 Mean PROMIS scores suggest that an association between proper surgical fixation of ankle fractures may result in most patients achieving population mean functional outcomes.7-9 Fracture patterns classified as WB and WC are not associated with significantly different clinical outcomes in terms of pain and PF when fractures are anatomically reduced intraoperatively (n = 182). Proper fixation of both fracture patterns therefore may enable patients to achieve PF and pain levels similar to that of the general population.

WC most commonly involves syndesmotic disruption, which is critical for ankle joint stabilization.43-46 In contrast, only 40% of WB results in syndesmotic disruption. 47 This study’s cohort demonstrated that 33% (n = 133/400) of WB had a syndesmosis injury compared with 100% (n = 127/127) of WC. Therefore, WC may be considered higher level of injuries because of the higher incidence of soft tissue trauma.5,39,48 Our study’s results demonstrating WC has significantly increased radiographic complication rate (n = 527), including early-onset DJD, may bolster this notion of WC as a more severe fracture pattern. Because of higher radiographic complication rates, aggressive ligamentous stabilization should be prioritized in WC to ensure patients can achieve similar functional outcomes to those with WB. The focus on deltoid and syndesmotic stability in both the rotational and sagittal planes and not isolated to simple coronal stability therefore may be important. Although WC with known syndesmotic injury results in greater degrees of soft tissue injury and was found in our study to be more than 3 times as likely to result in a radiographic complication as WB, anatomic reduction and appropriate ligamentous stabilization may result in patients achieving equivalent functional outcomes to patients with lower level of injury WB fracture patterns (n = 182). Positive functional outcomes may be achieved regardless of fracture subtype when appropriate reduction and stabilization are achieved intraoperatively.21,36,49

A previous study evaluating a subset of the same cohort of patients with deltoid ligament disruption compared outcomes between those who underwent deltoid repair to those who did not. 36 In this study, 265 patients with minimum 1-year follow-up were analyzed for complications. The 50 of 265 who underwent deltoid repair had zero radiographic complications compared with an overall complication rate of 14% among the 215 of 265 who underwent no repair, including a 3.26% early-onset DJD rate and 1.86% syndesmotic loss of reduction incidence. As for a comparison of those who completed PROMIS questionnaires (n = 112), deltoid repair (n = 21) was associated with non-significant improvements in functional outcomes compared with no repair (n = 91). 36 This subgroup analysis therefore suggests that appropriate ligamentous stabilization of ankle fractures may improve outcomes, and in particular for deltoid repair, reduced radiographically evident complications.

Although some studies suggest that syndesmosis injury is an independent risk factor for poor functional outcomes,48-51 our results demonstrate that syndesmotic injury is not associated with poor PROMIS score (n = 182). Prior literature corroborates our study’s findings.21,26,52,53 Interestingly, all patients in the total cohort (n = 527) who had loss of syndesmotic reduction had either fixation with screw or no fixation at all. Seventy-five percent (12/16) of patients who had loss of syndesmotic reduction had no fixation at all, which may emphasize the importance of syndesmotic stabilization in all planes. Fluoroscopic stress examination is limited in the ability to assess syndesmotic malreduction and instability.54-58 Therefore, visualization with either arthroscopic examination or direct visualization may be recommended to assess syndesmotic instability accurately.54-58 In addition, 25% (4/16) of those who had a loss of reduction in the total cohort (n = 527) had fixation with screw compared to 0% syndesmotic loss of reduction with either TightRope or InternalBrace, which suggests that dynamic syndesmotic fixation techniques may be associated with lower complications and improve syndesmotic functioning by more effectively mimicking the physiologic motion of the syndesmosis. 21 Additionally, most cases (75%) of syndesmotic loss of reduction occurred in WC (n = 527 cohort). Therefore, syndesmotic fixation may be of particular importance for WC with greater soft tissue trauma. The lack of appropriate ligamentous stabilization in many of these fractures that lost reduction may explain the higher-than-expected loss of reduction rates. Notably, 0% of patients with deltoid repair and 1.0% (1/97) of patients with dynamic syndesmotic fixation techniques developed early-onset DJD (of note, the 1 patient who developed DJD who had dynamic fixation had stage 1 DJD).21,36 As further evidence that appropriate ligamentous stabilization may be especially important, all patients with early-onset end-stage arthritis (2.1%, 11/527) had loss of reduction and the majority of patients who developed early-onset DJD in the entire cohort of 527 (88%, 22/25) had loss of reduction. The 3 patients with early-onset DJD who did not have loss of reduction all had stage 1 DJD. Two previous cadaveric studies demonstrated that merely 1 mm of lateral talar shift—representative of malreduction—results in significant decrease in tibiotalar contact area (42% in Ramsey and Hamilton 59 and 40% in Lloyd et al 14 ), thereby concentrating joint reactive forces over a smaller articular surface and predisposing to accelerated cartilage degeneration and posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Therefore, although achieving an anatomic reduction may not guarantee that patients do not develop posttraumatic osteoarthritis, anatomic reduction through appropriate ligamentous and bony stabilization may be associated with decreased incidence of early-onset arthritis. 15

Posterior malleolus fracture did not correlate with PROMIS among those who responded to the survey (n = 182), which underscores that more severe fracture patterns may not indicate worse outcomes. Among anatomically reduced fractures, those with posterior malleolus did not have significantly different PROMIS compared to those without posterior malleolus involvement. When evaluating complications among the total cohort of 527 patients, most patients (57%) with loss of posterior malleolus reduction did not have posterior malleolus fixation, which corroborates prior literature indicating the importance of posterior malleolar fixation.30-35

As supported by previous literature, higher BMI, female sex, and older age may negatively affect outcomes (n = 182) following ankle fracture surgery and may help surgeons guide patient expectations during recovery.26-29 Future studies should evaluate the etiology of this discrepancy between outcomes among men and women in order to optimize function and minimize pain for females.

This study is not without limitations, most notably the retrospective design. Future studies should include prospective design to compare preoperative and postoperative PROMIS, as the retrospective nature of this study limited the ability to evaluate pre-injury function and pain. PROMIS nevertheless provides meaningful interpretive value even in the absence of internal control groups because its T scores are calibrated to a population mean of 50. PROMIS therefore allows comparison against a validated external reference standard, which enables clinically relevant interpretation of outcomes. Patients may have interpreted survey questions differently, which may lead to measurement bias. Patients were also necessarily at different follow-up time periods in their recovery. Not everyone completed surveys at 1 year post-operatively, for example, because this study was performed retrospectively. However, beyond 1 year, PROMIS was not associated with differences in follow-up (Table 2). Future studies should also evaluate psychosocial factors that influence functional outcomes.60-64 The low rate of survey follow-up (35%, 182/527) further serves as a limitation and may introduce selection bias. However, this reported rate of participation meets the acceptable survey response rate reported in the literature for orthopaedic trauma and aligns with response rates of prior studies on ankle fractures published in both Foot & Ankle Orthopaedics and Foot & Ankle International.24,29,36,65 There is also potential for nonresponse bias given the low response rate. However, aside from a larger proportion of 26-50-year-olds in the responder group relative to the non-responder group, baseline characteristics were balanced between the responders and non-responders. Supplementary Table S3 showing complications among the 182 demonstrates that the lack of difference in PROMIS between groups is likely not explained by any differences in complications relative to the overall cohort of 527. Moreover, baseline characteristics were not balanced between WB and WC in the overall cohort evaluating complications (they were balanced in the survey response groups evaluating PROMIS, though). Although age was statistically different between groups, an age difference of 5 years is not clinically significant. As evidence, although those aged ≥50 years had worse PROMIS PF than those aged <50 years, the age groups 18-25 and 26-50 had similar scores that did not reach MCID. Similarly, age groups 51-75 and 76-100 had similar scores that did not reach MCID. Therefore, smaller age differences were not found to be clinically meaningful in our study. Furthermore, multiple studies across a variety of orthopaedic conditions—especially in foot and ankle populations specifically—indicate that an age differences of 5 years is not clinically meaningful.66-74 BMI was significantly higher in WC, and therefore it is possible that higher BMI contributed to the higher complication rate. A greater proportion of WC had posterior malleolus fracture, which may also contribute to the higher complication rates in WC compared to WB. Regardless, even with higher proportion of posterior malleolus fracture involvement, WC patients were able to achieve population mean outcomes like WB patients. Moreover, DJD may take longer than 1 year to develop. As a result, early-onset DJD incidence only reflects short-term development associated with loss of reduction, and the study was unable to compare long-term DJD rates between fracture subtypes. Further, we did not have postoperative CT scans for evaluation of reduction quality post-operatively, which is reported to be more sensitive in the detection of syndesmotic malreduction, because routine postoperative CT scans are not standard of care and this study was performed retrospectively.75-77 Lastly, the absence of preoperative baseline PROMIS scores prevents calculation of the proportion of patients meeting the PROMIS MCID threshold in each group. Future prospective work should include baseline PROMIS collection to allow this analysis.

Conclusions

Anatomic reduction during operative fixation of Weber B and Weber C ankle fractures was associated with equivalent functional outcomes among patients with anatomically reduced ankles in this cohort.
