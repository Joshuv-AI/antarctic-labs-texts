Three‐Cusp View Orientation in TAVI: Evaluating LOA Versus Non‐LOA Impact on Outcomes With Contemporary Valve Prostheses
Authors: Rim Kasem Ali Sliman, Amnon Eitan, Keren Zisman, Yuval Avidan, Amir Aker, Hussein Sliman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOptimal projection views during transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures are critical for procedural success and patient outcomes.AimsThis study investigated the influence of 3‐cusp view orientation—line of alignment (LOA) versus non‐LOA—on procedural outcomes in patients undergoing TAVI with contemporary transcatheter heart valves.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included 421 consecutive patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis who underwent transfemoral TAVI at a single center between March 2019 and December 2023. Patients received either SAPIEN 3 (n = 140) or CoreValve Evolut R/PRO (n = 281) prostheses and were classified as having either LOA (n = 312, 74.1%) or non‐LOA (n = 109, 25.9%) projection based on pre‐procedural computed tomography. Primary endpoints included device success according to VARC‐2 criteria, post‐procedural aortic regurgitation, and 30‐day clinical outcomes. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of device success, post‐procedural hemodynamics, and the need for permanent pacemaker implantation.ResultsNon‐LOA patients had larger annulus dimensions (461.37 ± 83.52 vs. 441.16 ± 80.83 mm², p = 0.03), higher calcium burden (3072.26 ± 1524.36 vs. 2646.0 ± 1189.11 AU, p = 0.01), and required more contrast (179.99 ± 67.12 vs. 161.94 ± 64.7 ml, p = 0.01). Multivariate analysis revealed that aortic valve calcium score was the only independent predictor of device success (p = 0.018), while LOA orientation showed no significant association (p = 0.971). Valve type was the primary determinant of post‐procedural hemodynamics (SAPIEN 3 superior, p = 0.025), and prior bundle branch block was the strongest pacemaker predictor (p = 0.005). Device success (94.81% vs. 95.88%, p = 0.86) and safety outcomes were comparable between groups.ConclusionsWhile LOA orientation creates anatomical differences, it doesn't change fundamental treatment decisions—calcium burden and valve type remain the primary determinants of outcomes. LOA status should inform procedural planning through enhanced contrast management and risk‐stratified surveillance, without compromising fundamental treatment decisions. Contemporary TAVI procedures demonstrate excellent safety and efficacy across both types of projections.Keywords: 3‐cusp view; aortic root anatomy; aortic stenosis; computed tomography; transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI); valve deployment, anatomical variation

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has revolutionized the treatment of symptomatic severe aortic stenosis (AS). It is currently the treatment of choice for most patients with intermediate or high surgical risk [1, 2] and is increasingly used in patients with lower surgical risk [3, 4]. Consequently, the number of TAVR procedures performed annually continues to rise [5].

The widespread integration of pre‐procedural cardiac computed tomography (CT) scans in transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) protocols has markedly enhanced patient selection, procedural planning, and clinical outcomes [6]. The 3‐cusp view has emerged as a critical component in TAVI procedures among the various imaging modalities employed. This view offers a perpendicular perspective of the aortic valve (AV), facilitating precise delineation of the left coronary cusp (LCC), right coronary cusp (RCC), and non‐coronary cusp (NCC) [7].

Proper projection views are crucial during transcatheter heart valve deployment to avoid parallax with the annulus and delivery catheter. This precise alignment is crucial for accurately gauging the valve's implantation depth and minimizing potential complications, including paravalvular leaks (PVL) and conduction disturbances [8]. The Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (SCCT) has issued expert guidelines strongly advocating for personalized optimal projection predictions derived from pre‐procedure multi‐slice computed tomography (MSCT) scans, which should be tailored for each patient to enhance procedural accuracy and outcomes [9].

Understanding specific anatomical relationships is essential to implement these guidelines effectively in clinical practice. In the coronal plane, the orientation of the perpendicular line relative to the vertebral column determines the classification of the 3‐cusp view; it can project either to the right or left. A projection to the left of the vertebral column results in classification as a line of alignment (LOA) view, while a projection to the right leads to classification as a non‐LOA view [10].

The choice between these views may have implications for procedural outcomes. Previous studies demonstrated that specific anatomical characteristics, notably increased aortic angulation (often referred to as a “horizontal aorta”), can complicate TAVI procedures and affect their success rates [11, 12] However, with the advent of newer‐generation devices, the specific impact of the 3‐cusp view orientation on TAVI outcomes remains an open question, warranting further investigation.

This study aims to assess the influence of 3‐cusp view orientation (LOA vs. non‐LOA) on procedural success and short‐term clinical outcomes in patients undergoing TAVI with contemporary balloon‐expandable (BE) and non‐balloon‐expandable (NBE) valves.

Comprehensive baseline clinical, echocardiographic, and procedural data were collected, with follow‐up evaluations conducted 30 days after the procedure. Pre‐procedural evaluation encompassed a comprehensive analysis of 3‐cusp views using CT scans for each patient. AV function was assessed via transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), while concomitant coronary artery disease (CAD) was evaluated using pre‐procedural coronary CT scans and/or coronary angiography. A CT expert cardiologist and an interventional cardiologist evaluated each case.

TAVI Device

In this study, TAVR was performed using the SAPIEN 3 (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) and CoreValve Evolut R and PRO [Medtronic] prostheses. The selection of prosthetic valve type, size, and vascular access route was at the discretion of the operating physician, based on individual patient characteristics and procedural requirements.

All procedures were performed under either conscious sedation or general anesthesia, based on patient characteristics and the operator's preference. Vascular access was obtained via percutaneous femoral approach using the pre‐closure technique with two Perclose ProGlide devices (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Following arterial access, systemic anticoagulation was achieved with unfractionated heparin (70−100 IU/kg), targeting an activated clotting time of greater than 250 s. A temporary pacing lead was positioned in the right ventricular apex. Pre‐dilatation of the AV was performed at the operator's discretion using appropriately sized balloons under rapid ventricular pacing (160–180 bpm). Valve sizing was determined based on pre‐procedural CT measurements using manufacturer guidelines. For SAPIEN 3 valve deployment, the valve was positioned using fluoroscopic landmarks and deployed during rapid ventricular pacing with controlled balloon inflation. For Evolut R/PRO valves, commissural alignment was achieved by rotating the delivery system to align valve commissures with native commissures, followed by controlled self‐expansion under fluoroscopic guidance, with the option for recapture and repositioning if needed. Fluoroscopic projections were optimized based on pre‐procedural CT‐derived 3‐cusp view orientation: standard orthogonal projections (LAO 10−20°, cranial 10−15°) for LOA cases and modified angulation (RAO 5−15°, cranial 15−25°) for non‐LOA cases. Additional orthogonal views were obtained when initial projections were suboptimal. Post‐deployment valve function was assessed using aortography and echocardiography. It was also performed when residual AS or significant paravalvular regurgitation was identified. Anticoagulation was reversed with protamine sulfate, and vascular closure was completed using the pre‐deployed closure devices.

All patients received single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel) or anticoagulation alone, as indicated, post‐procedure, unless contraindicated.

Outcome Analysis

The study evaluated primary and secondary endpoints related to the outcomes of TAVR. Primary endpoints included device success defined according to Valve Academic Research Consortium‐2 (VARC‐2) criteria, post‐procedural AR degree, and mortality rates during hospitalization and at 30‐day follow‐up.

Secondary endpoints included procedural and in‐hospital complications, including valve embolization, need for a second valve, aortic rupture or dissection, myocardial infarction (MI), cardiac tamponade, emergency surgical intervention, stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), major vascular complications, permanent pacemaker (PM) implantation, acute kidney injury (AKI), and sepsis.

The 30‐day follow‐up assessment evaluated adverse events, including cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations, heart failure, endocarditis, valve thrombosis, stroke or TIA, MI, angina, and AR. All endpoints were defined and evaluated according to VARC‐2 criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons between patients with and without (LOA) were performed using t‐tests for continuous variables and Pearson's chi‐square test for categorical variables. Results are presented as means ± standard deviations and counts (percentages). A subgroup analysis stratified by valve type (Sapien vs. Evolut) was conducted to assess potential associations between valve types and LOA occurrence, employing the same statistical methods for each valve group.

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of three primary endpoints: device success, post‐procedural AR (defined as an AV mean gradient > 5 mmHg), and PM implantation. Variables for inclusion were selected based on clinical relevance and the results of univariate analysis (p < 0.20). The device success model included LOA status, valve type, age, gender, and AV calcium score. The hemodynamic model included LOA status, valve type, gender, annulus area, annulus perimeter, and AV calcium score. The pacemaker model included LOA status, valve type, post‐dilatation use, prior bundle branch block, and LVOT diameter. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer−Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

From March 2019 to December 2023, 429 TAVI procedures using second‐generation devices were performed at our center. Exclusions comprised four non‐transfemoral access cases and four cases of pure AR. This study included 421 patients who underwent transfemoral TAVI procedures, 140 patients who received SAPIEN 3 valves, and 281 who received CoreValve Evolut R/PRO devices. The patients were divided into two groups based on the LOA, with 109 patients (25.9%) in the non‐LOA group and 312 (74.1%) in the LOA group. The groups shared similar baseline characteristics, including age (79.56 ± 6.76 vs. 79.2 ± 7.7 years, p = 0.66), BMI (27.77 ± 4.13 vs. 28.36 ± 6.42, p = 0.27), and STS mortality score (3.13 ± 1.71 vs. 3.49 ± 2.14, p = 0.49). Only the gender distribution showed a significant difference, with a higher proportion of males in the LOA group (54.98% vs. 42.2%, p = 0.0288). Post‐Procedure Outcomes

No significant difference was observed in residual aortic insufficiency (AI) between the non‐LOA and LOA groups (15.49% vs. 11.68%, p = 0.402). However, valvular gradients remained comparable between groups with no significant differences, with similar post‐procedure AV maximum gradient (18.83 ± 8.53 vs. 17.71 ± 7.61 mmHg, p = 0.3) and AV mean gradients (10.6 ± 5.22 vs. 10.44 ± 4.99 mmHg, p = 0.79). Lower ejection fraction (EF) was observed in the non‐LOA group (57.72 ± 7.34% vs. 59.63 ± 7.08%, p = 0.02).

Complications and Safety Outcomes

Procedural safety and success metrics were comparable between groups. The non‐LOA and LOA groups have similar rates of periprocedural complications (0.34 ± 1.32 vs. 0.27 ± 1.05, p = 0.6), a measure of device success (94.81% vs. 95.88%, p = 0.8581), and early safety composite endpoints, a critical indicator of overall procedural safety (89.23% vs. 88.78%, p = 1.0).

Subanalysis: Comparison of Sapien and Evolut Valves in LOA and Non‐LOA Groups

Subgroup analysis revealed distinct patterns between valve types. Among Sapien valve recipients, significant differences were found in multiple anatomical parameters between LOA and non‐LOA orientations, including LVOT average diameter, area‐derived diameter, perimeter‐derived diameter, annulus area, annulus perimeter, and fluoroscopy time. In contrast, the Evolut cohort showed different distinguishing features, with significant variations in vlve calcium score, pre‐dilatation size, and post‐procedural AI between LOA and non‐LOA orientations (Table 4).

Multivariate Analysis

Independent predictors of device success were assessed using logistic regression (overall model χ² = 6.241, p = 0.283). After adjusting for confounders, AV calcium score was the only independent predictor of device success (OR = 1.000 per 100 AU, 95% CI: 0.999−1.000, p = 0.018), while LOA orientation showed no significant association (OR = 1.028, 95% CI: 0.234−4.523, p = 0.971). For post‐procedural hemodynamics (overall model χ² = 9.232, p = 0.161), valve type was the primary independent predictor (SAPIEN 3 vs. Evolut: OR = 0.486, 95% CI: 0.258−0.913, p = 0.025), while LOA orientation was not significant (OR = 1.588, 95% CI: 0.816−3.091, p = 0.173).

For PM implantation (overall model χ² = 12.023, p = 0.061), prior bundle branch block was the strongest predictor (OR = 9.463, 95% CI: 1.971−45.423, p = 0.005), with non‐LOA orientation showing a trend toward increased risk (OR = 4.723, 95% CI: 0.942−23.675, p = 0.059) (Table 5).

Enhanced Valve‐Specific Analysis

Among SAPIEN 3 recipients (n = 140), non‐LOA cases required significantly longer fluoroscopy time (30.07 ± 15.5 vs. 22.05 ± 7.49 min, p = 0.004) and demonstrated larger anatomical dimensions, including annulus area (472.97 ± 79.2 vs. 441.63 ± 74.78 mm², p = 0.032).

Among CoreValve Evolut recipients (n = 281), non‐LOA cases exhibited higher AV calcium scores (3213.03 ± 1656.78 vs. 2674.42 ± 1206.17 AU, p = 0.021) and significantly higher post‐procedural AI grades (0.79 ± 0.81 vs. 0.58 ± 0.74, p = 0.043).

Discussion

Our findings provide comprehensive insights into the relationship between 3‐cusp implantation views and TAVI procedural outcomes. While previous research has focused on general TAVI outcomes, our analysis specifically investigated the impact of LOA versus non‐LOA views on procedural success and short‐term outcomes in second‐generation devices.

The most notable finding was the difference in aortic root anatomy between the non‐LOA and LOA groups. Non‐LOA patients demonstrated significantly larger annulus areas, average annulus diameters, LVOT diameters, and higher AV calcium scores. These anatomical differences suggest that the 3‐cusp view orientation may be associated with distinct structural characteristics of the aortic root. These findings align with the current literature, which emphasizes the crucial role of detailed anatomical assessment in TAVI planning. An expert consensus document by Blanke et al highlighted the critical role of CT imaging in TAVI planning, particularly in assessing aortic root geometry [13]. The implications of these anatomical variations are particularly significant for valve selection and sizing strategies. Previous research by Jilaihawi et al. has demonstrated that precise CT‐based sizing can substantially reduce paravalvular AR post‐TAVR [14].

However, despite anatomical variations, most key procedural metrics remained comparable between groups, including valve sizing, fluoroscopy duration. This suggests a degree of adaptability in current TAVI techniques to different aortic root anatomies, aligning with recent advancements in TAVR technology [15]. However, our findings diverged from previous research regarding procedure duration and complications. Piayda et al. reported that patients with extra‐large aortic annuli typically experienced prolonged procedure durations and faced higher rates of procedural complications, including vascular complications and PM implantation [16]. Our cohort did not demonstrate these associations; Interestingly, the non‐LOA group required significantly higher contrast volumes during procedures, likely due to the geometric complexity of non‐LOA anatomy requiring multiple orthogonal projections to achieve optimal valve positioning, as standard fluoroscopic views may not provide adequate visualization of the asymmetric aortic root dimensions characteristic of this anatomical variant. This finding has important clinical implications, particularly for patients at risk of contrast‐induced nephropathy.

Furthermore, post‐procedural analysis outcomes revealed two key findings regarding left ventricular function and valve competency. Notably, patients in the non‐LOA group exhibited a significantly lower post‐procedural EF compared to the LOA group (57.72 ± 7.34% vs. 59.63 ± 7.08%, p = 0.021), although both groups maintained LVEF within the normal range ( > 50%). While statistically significant, this modest difference in LVEF suggests a potential association between non‐LOA anatomy and early post‐procedural ventricular function, warranting further investigation to establish clinical relevance.

Post‐procedural valve function assessment demonstrated comparable significant AI rates between groups (11.68% in LOA vs. 15.49% in non‐LOA, p = 0.402). This finding suggests that modern TAVI devices can achieve effective valve sealing regardless of cusp orientation. These outcomes indicate that while non‐LOA anatomy may subtly influence immediate post‐procedural left ventricular function, it does not significantly impact valve competency, supporting the efficacy and safety of TAVI procedures in patients presenting with non‐LOA anatomical variants.

The hemodynamic evaluation demonstrated comparable maximum and mean AV gradients between groups, demonstrating equivalent valve performance regardless of coronary cusp orientation. These findings are consistent with established clinical evidence supporting TAVI's efficacy across diverse anatomical variants, as shown in multiple landmark trials [17]. Furthermore, analysis of clinical outcomes revealed comparable results between LOA and non‐LOA cohorts across key metrics, including periprocedural complications, device success rates, and early safety endpoints. This observation suggests that cusp orientation does not adversely affect the safety or procedural success of TAVI, reflecting the adaptability of modern TAVI techniques and devices to various anatomical configurations. These results align with evidence from pivotal large‐scale trials, including PARTNER 3 and Evolut Low‐Risk, which have established TAVI's safety and efficacy across diverse anatomical presentations [17, 18].

Subgroup analyses revealed distinct valve‐specific patterns in anatomical and procedural characteristics between LOA and non‐LOA orientations. Anatomical dimensions and procedural complexity were the predominant differentiating factors in the Sapien cohort. Non‐LOA cases exhibited significantly larger aortic root dimensions, with greater annulus area (472.97 ± 79.2 vs. 441.63 ± 74.78 mm², p = 0.032) and perimeter (79.48 ± 6.61 vs. 75.17 ± 6.18 mm, p = 0.006), correlating with extended fluoroscopy duration (30.07 ± 15.5 vs. 22.05 ± 7.49 min, p = 0.004). In contrast, the Evolut cohort demonstrated divergent patterns, with differences primarily manifesting in technical aspects and clinical outcomes. Non‐LOA cases required larger pre‐dilatation balloon sizes (21.78 ± 1.59 vs. 19.19 ± 6.81 mm, p = 0.009), presented higher AV calcium scores (3213.03 ± 1656.78 vs. 2674.42 ± 1206.17 HU, p = 0.021), and exhibited increased post‐procedural AI (0.79 ± 0.81 vs. 0.58 ± 0.74, p = 0.043). These findings suggest that the impact of LOA versus non‐LOA orientation manifests differently between valve types, with Sapien valves demonstrating greater sensitivity to anatomical variations while Evolut valves show heightened susceptibility to procedural complexity and outcome variations.

The multivariate logistic regression analysis provides the most clinically relevant findings of this study. For device success, AV calcium score emerged as the only independent predictor (OR = 1.000 per 100 AU, 95% CI: 0.999−1.000, p = 0.018), while LOA orientation showed no significant association (OR = 1.028, 95% CI: 0.234−4.523, p = 0.971). This finding reinforces the established importance of calcium burden in TAVI outcomes, suggesting that anatomical orientation alone should not influence fundamental treatment decisions. This finding aligns with established literature demonstrating calcium burden as a primary determinant of TAVI outcomes across multiple device generations [19, 20, 21].

For post‐procedural hemodynamics, valve type was the primary independent predictor, with SAPIEN 3 demonstrating superior performance (OR = 0.486, 95% CI: 0.258−0.913, p = 0.025). This finding aligns with previous studies that have shown differences in hemodynamic performance between BE and self‐expandable valves, independent of anatomical considerations [22, 23].

Perhaps most intriguingly, for PM implantation, prior bundle branch block was the strongest predictor (OR = 9.463, 95% CI: 1.971−45.423, p = 0.005), while non‐LOA orientation showed only a trend toward increased risk (OR = 4.723, 95% CI: 0.942−23.675, p = 0.059). This suggests that while anatomical orientation may influence conduction system outcomes, pre‐existing conduction abnormalities remain the dominant risk factor.

This valve‐specific response to anatomical orientation highlights the importance of considering valve selection and cusp orientation during TAVI procedural planning and execution to optimize patient outcomes. The relationship between anatomy, procedural success, and patient outcomes underscores the need for detailed pre‐procedural planning, including careful consideration of cusp orientation and the use of appropriate valve selection strategies.

Future research could explore whether specific valve designs or deployment techniques are optimized explicitly for non‐LOA orientations to reduce PVL risk. Additionally, longer‐term follow‐up studies are needed to determine whether differences in residual AI impact survival outcomes, particularly given the known association between even mild PVL and increased mortality.

Clinical Implications

Pre‐procedural planning: While LOA orientation should not influence patient selection or valve choice, it should inform procedural planning and execution. Non‐LOA cases require enhanced contrast management protocols and consideration of renal protection strategies, particularly in patients with baseline renal dysfunction.

Procedural approach: Operators should anticipate increased contrast requirements in non‐LOA cases and consider alternative imaging strategies, such as intracardiac echocardiography or fusion imaging, to minimize contrast exposure while maintaining procedural precision.

Risk stratification: Traditional risk factors, particularly AV calcium burden and pre‐existing conduction abnormalities, should remain the primary focus of risk assessment. LOA orientation may inform surveillance strategies, but should not alter fundamental treatment decisions.

Valve selection: The superior hemodynamic performance of SAPIEN 3 valves in our cohort, independent of anatomical orientation, supports evidence‐based valve selection based on hemodynamic requirements rather than anatomical considerations alone.

In summary, while LOA orientation creates anatomical differences, it doesn't change fundamental treatment decisions—calcium burden and valve type remain the primary determinants of outcomes. This evidence‐based approach ensures optimal patient care while acknowledging anatomical variations that require procedural adaptation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The single‐center, retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and may limit generalizability to all populations. The follow‐up period was limited to 30 days, precluding the assessment of long‐term outcomes. Additionally, the impact of different valve types (BE vs. NBE) within anatomical subgroups warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

This comprehensive multivariate analysis demonstrates that while 3‐cusp view orientation creates distinct anatomical and procedural patterns, traditional risk factors—particularly AV calcium burden and valve type—remain more predictive of clinical outcomes than anatomical orientation alone. LOA status should inform procedural planning through enhanced contrast management, modified imaging protocols, and risk‐stratified surveillance, without compromising the fundamental safety and efficacy of contemporary TAVI procedures.

Key clinical messages include: (1) Calcium burden remains the primary determinant of procedural success across anatomical variants, (2) valve‐specific response patterns suggest tailored approaches for complex anatomy, (3) cumulative risk models improve pacemaker risk prediction, and (4) contemporary TAVI techniques successfully adapt to anatomical variations while maintaining excellent safety profiles. These findings provide evidence‐based guidance for personalized procedural planning while reassuring clinicians that anatomical variations do not alter fundamental treatment decisions in the contemporary TAVI era.
