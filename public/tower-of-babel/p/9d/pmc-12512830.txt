Interval between diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer in Brazil: comparative analysis of cases with and without diagnosis prior to arrival at the hospital unit
Authors: Letícia Gabriella Souza da Silva, Paulo Vitor de Souza Silva, Maria Fernanda Dantas Chaves, Ana Camilly da Costa Cruz, Jonas Eduardo Monteiro dos Santos, Nayara Priscila Dantas de Oliveira, Marianna de Camargo Cancela, J. Smith Torres-Roman, Dyego Leandro Bezerra de Souza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTimely initiation of cancer treatment is essential to improving outcomes and reducing mortality. In Brazil, Federal Law No. 12.732/12 mandates that patients with confirmed cancer diagnoses begin treatment within 60 days. This study aimed to assess compliance with this law among women diagnosed with cervical cancer, comparing cases with and without a prior diagnosis at the time of hospital admission.MethodsEcological study of temporary series, based on data from the Integrator of Hospital Cancer Records (IRHC). The cases were divided into two groups (cases diagnosed during hospital care and those with a previous diagnosis) and analyzed in the periods before (2006 to 2012) and after the enactment of the law (2013 to 2019). The percentage trend of adherence was analyzed by the Joinpoint Regression Program.ResultsAmong 120,311 eligible cases, 31.7% (n = 38,143) were diagnosed at the hospital, and 68.3% (n = 82,168) had a prior diagnosis. Overall, 43.2% of women initiated treatment within 60 days. After the law was enacted, the proportion of timely treatment decreased to 66.7% in the no prior diagnosis group and 28.7% in the prior diagnosis group. Significant regional and interstate disparities were observed. Joinpoint analysis revealed both increasing and decreasing trends across states, with notable declines in timely treatment among women with a prior diagnosis.ConclusionCompliance with the 60-day treatment law remains suboptimal, particularly among women with a prior cervical cancer diagnosis. These disparities suggest persistent barriers in patient navigation and systemic inefficiencies within Brazil’s public healthcare network. Our findings underscore the need for strengthening integrated care pathways and implementing national patient navigation programs to ensure timely cancer treatment.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most incident cancer and the fourth most frequent cancer-related cause of death among women worldwide [1]. The main etiological factor to its onset is the continuous infection by oncogenic types of the Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) [2]. Genetic and lifestyle factors such as smoking, early sexual initiation and multiple sexual partners are also risk factors [3]. This cancer is the only one that can be eliminated through primary and secondary prevention strategies, as well as having a high chance of cure when detected and treated early [4].

In Brazil, cervical cancer is the third most incident cancer (Age Standardized Rate - ASR: 13.25/100,000) among women - excluding non-melanoma skin tumors [5], behind breast and colorectal cancers [6]. The age-adjusted (World) mortality rate for cervical cancer in Brazil was 4.79 deaths/100,000 women in 2022.

The main strategies for controlling cervical cancer are prevention through HPV vaccination and early detection through early diagnosis and screening. In Brazil, the cytopathological test is the most widely used method of choice for cervical cancer screening, indicated for women between 25 and 64 years of age, once every three years, after two consecutive normal annual exams [7]. In 2024, the Ministry of Health introduced molecular testing for HPV, aiming to accelerate early detection and identify pre-cancerous lesions. This measure is part of strategies aimed at eliminating cervical cancer as a public health problem by 2030 [8]. Despite this, the country still does not have a population-based screening program in place standardized. In countries where control of this cancer is satisfactory, broad population-based or organized screening coverage has been implemented, which includes the active search for women and longitudinal follow-up after a positive screening test [9].

However, although there has been an increase in the coverage of cytopathological exams in Brazil, from 78.7% in 2013 to 81.3% in 2019, there is great inequality in access to the exam [10]. In addition, the diagnosis of cancers at advanced stages showed an increase from 2013 to 2017, demonstrating flaws in the public policies employed to enable early diagnosis [11].

With the aim of reducing delay between diagnosis and treatment in Brazil, Law 12.732/12 (the 60-day law) was published in November 2012, which guarantees cancer patients in Brazil the right to start treatment in the Unified Health System (SUS) within 60 days of being diagnosed with the disease [12].

However, studies show a long delay between diagnosis and initiation of treatment for patients with cervical cancer [13]. One study showed that the average time to start treatment from confirmation of the diagnosis of cervical cancer in women living in the state of Pernambuco in 2017 and 2018 was 92.07 days [14]. This data, combined with the lack of resources and access to health services, exposes the challenges experienced by women affected by this pathology [15].

Issues related to inequalities in access to treatment seem to influence the waiting time. It is worth highlighting cases that start treatment by referring the health system after diagnosis and those that are diagnosed and treated in the same hospital unit [16, 17].

From this perspective, it is worth highlighting the publication of Ordinance GM/MS No. 6,592 of February 2025, which establishes the Navigation Program for People with a Cancer Diagnosis within the SUS. This initiative aims to improve comprehensive care for cancer patients, with one of its objectives being to ensure early diagnosis and timely treatment [18].

The aim of this study was to analyze the compliance with the Federal Law 12.732/12 (the 60-day law), evaluating the delay between cervical cancer diagnosis and treatment in Brazil for cases with and without prior diagnosis.

Methodology

This is an ecological time series study including analytical cases of malignant neoplasia of the cervix (C53) [19] in women aged between 20 and 99 years, diagnosed between 2006 and 2019, admitted to the High Complexity Oncology Units (UNACON) and High Complexity Oncology Centers (CACON) in Brazil. The analysis period was established considering the relevance of the analysis before and after the Law, with 2019 being the year with the greatest data completeness. Analytical cases are defined as those who had their treatment planned and/or carried out in the hospital unit [20].

Individual data on the time between diagnosis and treatment was collected from the Integrator of Hospital Cancer Registries (IHCR) [21]. The information from the HCR comes from health providers in the 27 Brazilian states, which are considered to be cancer care hospital centers or units [22]. The Brazilian RHC system gathers information on cancer cases treated in the country’s healthcare facilities, the majority of which (90%) are accredited by the public healthcare system [23]. The databases used were extracted from the IHCR made available at the National Cancer Institute (INCA) website, and cases selected according to the eligibility criteria. In addition to the databases for the 14 years analyzed, we used the shapefiles of the Brazilian states provided by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE).

Cases of carcinoma in situ, women under 20 years of age, i.e. cases of childhood cancer [24], cases with missing data on age at diagnosis and cases who had arrived at the hospital with previous cancer treatments were excluded from the study. Cases with missing information on diagnosis and cancer treatment (dates of first diagnosis and start of treatment and type of first cancer treatment) were also excluded from the study.

The selected cases were categorized into two groups, without previous diagnosis, and cases with previous diagnosis, both without treatment prior to arrival at the hospital. The cases without diagnosis refer to those in which the patient arrives at the treatment unit without a history of diagnostic investigation, performing both the diagnosis and the treatment in the unit itself. On the other hand, the cases with previous diagnosis correspond to situations in which the patient already has a confirmed diagnosis and is referred to the oncological unit only to start treatment.

The dependent variable was the time in days between the date of first diagnosis and the date cancer treatment, for both groups. The time in days was dichotomized into “up to 60 days” and “more than 60 days”, in accordance with Federal Law 12.732/12 [12]. The outcome was the proportion of cases treated within 60 days of the date of first diagnosis.

For statistical analysis, the proportion of cases treated within 60 days was calculated. Descriptive maps were plotted for two periods: before (2006–2012) and after the law (2013–2019), using GeoDa version 1.14 [25]. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to check for statistically significant differences in the time taken to start treatment in the periods before and after the 60-day law was passed, considering the variance for a 95% confidence level, using SPSS software [26].

The prevalence of cases with treatment starting up to 60 days between 2006 and 2019 was analyzed using the Joinpoint regression software [27]. Analysis using Joinpoint identifies the moment when changes in the trend are observed and calculates the Annual Percentage Change (APC) in each segment. The method applied identifies Joinpoints based on the model with a maximum of three change points, the analysis contrasts whether one or more of these are significant for adding to the model. The final model selected was the best-fit model, with the Annual Percentage Change (APC) based on the trend of each segment, estimating whether these values are statistically significant at a 95% confidence level [28]. The data analyzed in the Joinpoint were imported to the construction of the graphs through the Stata version 14 [29].

States with repeated zero cases in more than one year were excluded from the Joinpoint analysis. States with zero cases in isolated years were averaged between the previous year and the following year to establish the percentage. This study was carried out using unidentified data from secondary sources, extracted from official websites open to public consultation,, which does not require submission to a Research Ethics Committee, in accordance to the Resolution 580/2018 [30].

Results

Between 2006 and 2019, 187,389 cases of cervical cancer were recorded in hospital cancer registries in Brazil. According to the eligibility criteria listed in the study, 120,311 cases were included in the analysis, with an exclusion rate of 35.79%.

Of the included cases, 31.70% (n = 38,143) of the women arrived at the hospital without a diagnosis, while 68.29% (n = 82,168) arrived with a previous diagnosis. With regard to the group of women without a previous diagnosis of cervical cancer, in the period before the law, Amazonas (48%), Bahia (44.9%) and Sergipe (46.7%) were the states with the greatest inadequacy in starting treatment in less than 60 days. After the law, Roraima (46.7%), Pernambuco (48%) and Rio de Janeiro (56.3%) became the states with the highest percentage of non-compliance with the law. In the group of women with a previous diagnosis of neoplasia, before the law was enacted Pernambuco (17.9%), Roraima (18.4%) and Amapá (15.3%) were the states with the worst percentages for starting treatment on time. After the law, Amazonas (9.6%), Roraima (22.1%) and Rio de Janeiro (11.3%) had the lowest percentages of compliance with the 60-day law.

Discussion

The present study analyzed compliance with Federal Law 12.732/12 regarding the initiation of treatment within 60 days for cases of cervical cancer treated in oncology centers, both with and without a prior diagnosis. This study found that the percentage of cases that began treatment within 60 days was higher among women who arrived at the oncology unit without a prior diagnosis compared to those who already had one. This difference may be attributed to the fact that cases with a prior diagnosis likely navigate a longer path within the healthcare network, as efforts to improve patient navigation processes are a relatively recent reality [31, 32].

It was observed that cases of cervical cancer without a prior diagnosis, treated in oncology units, demonstrated a greater propensity to begin treatment within 60 days when compared to cases with a prior diagnosis. This pattern was identified both before and after the enactment of the 60-day law.

Delays in cancer treatment initiation are associated with poorer prognosis regarding disease progression and increased mortality [33]. A study conducted by Carvalho et al. (2018) in Rio de Janeiro with women diagnosed with cervical cancer reported an average time to treatment initiation of 115.4 days after arrival at the oncology care center. When comparing waiting times by type of treatment, the study found an average of 142.6 days for radiotherapy, 116.8 days for combined radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and 104.8 days for surgery [34].

Studies indicate that delays in the initiation of cervical cancer treatment are a reality in Brazil’s Unified Health System (SUS) due to the fragility of the oncology patient care network, which is associated with increased lethality [31, 35]. Within the oncological care network integrated into the healthcare system, it is essential to connect the three levels of care to organize the care pathway in a timely manner: primary care, which serves as the system’s entry point; secondary care (or medium complexity); and tertiary care (or high complexity), which includes Oncology Centers (CACONs), Oncology Units (UNACONs), and hospital complexes [36]. The Brazilian healthcare system operates on a referral and counter-referral system, where lower-complexity units perform diagnoses and refer patients for high-complexity treatment. However, some high-complexity centers also accept open demand from patients without a prior diagnosis, which explains the differences in waiting times between the two groups analyzed.

State Oncology Plans play a fundamental role in organizing and directing the flow of the oncology network. A national study evaluating the 27 federation units from 2013 to 2023 found that 17 states developed and published specific State Plans for Oncological Care. However, the states of Rondônia, Acre, Amazonas, Amapá, Tocantins, Alagoas, Goiás, and Mato Grosso do Sul lack formulated and publicly available plans, highlighting a significant gap in the implementation of public health policies for oncology care in these regions [37].

Although a considerable number of Brazilian states have a specific State Plan for Oncological Care, the absence of an effective and efficient care pathway for the diagnosis-to-treatment process is still observed, particularly in cases of women who already arrive at treatment units with a cervical cancer diagnosis, as evidenced in this study [38]. These plans now require revision to improve patient navigation within the system. To enhance the flow of cancer patients through the healthcare system, ensure early diagnosis, timely treatment, and reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with the disease, Ordinance GM/MS No. 6.592 was published on February 7, 2025, establishing the Patient Navigation Program for Individuals Diagnosed with Cancer within the SUS framework [39].

These findings highlight the weaknesses in the integration process among different levels of healthcare and the limited implementation of patient navigation, which results in delays in timely treatment access. Despite the National Regulation Policy of the SUS being established in 2008 through Ordinance No. 1.559/GM, which aims to organize the healthcare network and care pathways, obstacles remain in the regulation process for oncology patients within the system, contributing to accumulated demand and increased waiting times for treatment [40, 41].

From this perspective, the establishment of a Navigation Program, which aims to guide the oncology patient’s journey within the healthcare system to ensure comprehensive care, could be an effective strategy for filling gaps such as those observed in this study [18].

Beyond the organizational weaknesses of the healthcare system, the longer waiting times for treatment among women with a prior diagnosis may also be associated with socioeconomic factors, access to healthcare services, territorial and housing issues, which can influence the integration of the oncology care network. The individual and contextual factors associated with longer waiting times need further investigation [17, 42].

One of the strengths of this study is the use of the IRHC, a comprehensive secondary data source available in Brazil, which enables a detailed analysis of diagnostic and treatment patterns across different regions of the country. However, the lack of more recent data in the IRHC prevented analysis beyond 2019, along with the potential existence of incomplete or outdated records from oncology care units. Related to the completeness of IRHC data for cervical cancer, from 2006 to 2015 the proportion of cases with missing data for clinical tumor staging was 32.39%, demonstrating a high completeness of information [40].

The exclusion of states with years zered from the analysis is a limitation of this study due to the possibility of bias in the interpretation of regional trends. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that this study excluded cases with missing information to prevent inconclusive records from interfering with the final analysis, which is also a limitation.

The findings of this study provide insights into the delays in oncological treatment for cervical cancer cases in Brazil, demonstrating that despite the legal right to initiate treatment within 60 days, this deadline is frequently not met. The fragility in meeting legal deadlines highlights the urgent need to reorganize the healthcare network and implement the patient navigation program within the SUS. To ensure treatment initiation within the legally established timeframe, it is necessary to develop strategies that increase the number of cases treated in a timely manner within the healthcare system through patient navigation [18].

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight that the waiting time for treatment initiation continues to exceed 60 days, even after the enactment of Law 12.732/12. Cases arriving at the hospital unit with a prior diagnosis exhibited longer waiting times than those without a diagnosis, which may be attributed to difficulties in navigating the healthcare system.
