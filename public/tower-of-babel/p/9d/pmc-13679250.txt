Demographic and socioeconomic determinants influencing households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana
Authors: Anthony Edward Boakye, Addae Boateng Adu-Gyamfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence indicates that foods consumed outside the home are often more energy-dense and less nutrient-rich than home-prepared meals, contributing to poorer dietary quality and increased risks of overweight, obesity, and other diet-related non-communicable diseases.ObjectiveTherefore, the study sought to examine the extent to which demographic and socioeconomic determinants influence households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana.MethodsThe study employed a cross-sectional descriptive design and applied a four multistage sampling technique to select 1,200 household heads from eight communities across four districts in the Western region. The study was conducted from 5 June 2025 to 4 June 2026. Data were gathered using a structured questionnaire developed to measure the variables of interest. The data were analyzed using frequency distributions, Pearson’s chi-square test, and binary logistic regression in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27, with the level of statistical significance set at 0.05.ResultsThe study found that the majority of households (54.5%) had engaged in out-of-home food consumption, while 45.5% did not. The study found that households with a tertiary school education (AOR = 1.543; 95% CI: 1.014–2.347; p=.043), and females (AOR = 1.461; 95% CI: 1.047–2.037; p=.026) had greater odds of engaging in out-of-home food consumption. The study found that households with a secondary school education (AOR=0.442; 95% CI: 0.295-0.663; p<.001), those with a vocational training school education (AOR=0.135; 95% CI: 0.079-0.231; p<.001), and rural dwellers (AOR=0.140; 95% CI: 0.308-0.547; p<.001) had lower odds of engaging in out-of-home food consumption.ConclusionThe study findings demonstrate that demographic and socioeconomic factors significantly influence households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption. Introduction

Globally, the consumption of food outside the home has increased substantially over the past few decades, driven by rapid urbanization, changing lifestyles, rising incomes, and increased participation of women in the workforce [1, 2]. Similar trends are evident in Ghana, where out-of-home food consumption has become an important component of household food consumption [3, 4]. Evidence indicates that approximately 80% of Ghanaian households spend part of their food budget on food prepared outside the home, with such expenditures accounting for about 17% of total household food expenditure [4]. At the same time, Ghana is experiencing a nutrition transition characterized by shifts in dietary patterns, particularly in urban areas [3]. Traditional home-prepared meals such as fufu, banku, kenkey, and ampesi increasingly coexist with commercially prepared foods, including fried foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, instant noodles, and other processed products [3]. Studies have documented that in urban Ghana, there has been a gradual shift away from home cooking and communal eating practices toward greater reliance on out-of-home meals and convenience foods [3, 5]. Evidence from low-income urban communities in Accra further shows frequent consumption of deep-fried foods and sugar-sweetened beverages, reflecting changing food preferences and lifestyles [6]. These dietary changes have important public health implications [7]. Research suggests that foods consumed outside the home are often more energy-dense and less nutrient-rich compared to home-prepared meals, contributing to poorer dietary quality and increased risks of overweight, obesity, and other diet-related non-communicable diseases [8]. The growing availability of processed and commercially prepared foods, combined with increasing consumption of food outside the home, has raised concerns about the rising burden of obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [9].

Several demographic and socioeconomic factors influence the increasing out-of-home food consumption [10]. Key demographic characteristics include the respondent’s sex, age, marital status, household headship, household size, and place of residence, while important socioeconomic factors include the educational attainment of the household head, employment status, and household income [4, 10]. Rising urbanization and the growth of dual-income households have significantly altered traditional eating patterns, making out-of-home food consumption a convenient and time-saving option for many families [4]. Higher income levels and greater workforce participation, particularly among women, have further increased the demand for meals prepared outside the home [11, 12]. In addition, the aspirational appeal of Western-style dining experiences, often promoted by international fast-food chains, has contributed to the perception of out-of-home food consumption as a symbol of modernity and social status, thereby shaping consumer preferences and dietary choices [13]. However, the growing accessibility and convenience of out-of-home food consumption are frequently associated with increased consumption of energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods, raising important public health concerns [8]. Foods consumed out-of-home are often high in salt, saturated and trans fats, and added sugars, factors that contribute to the increasing prevalence of non-communicable diseases such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases [14, 15]. Furthermore, limited nutritional information and inadequate food labelling in many food service establishments restrict consumers’ ability to make informed dietary decisions, thereby increasing the health risks associated with frequent out-of-home food consumption [16].

Most previous studies, including Suleman and Suleman [4], Agyekum et al. [17], Agyepong et al. [18], Ecker and Fang [19], Galbete et al. [20], Gersten et al. [21], Kushitor [22], Ting et al. [23], and Addai [24], have examined the ongoing nutrition transition and the factors shaping household food demand in Ghana. Among these, some studies [17, 20] have focused on national-level food consumption patterns and dietary transitions, while others [19, 22] have explored the broader relationship between nutrition transition and economic development. In addition, several studies [18, 21, 23, 24] have investigated household food expenditure and consumption behavior, and Suleman and Suleman [4] specifically examined out-of-home food consumption in Ghana. Although these studies provide important national-level insights, most adopt aggregated perspectives that may conceal substantial regional variations in household food consumption behavior. Ghana is characterized by marked socio-economic, demographic, and spatial heterogeneity, which significantly influences food acquisition and consumption decisions at the household level. Consequently, evidence generated from national analyses may not adequately capture context-specific consumption patterns and determinants across different regions. Notably, there remains limited empirical evidence on the demographic and socioeconomic determinants of out-of-home food consumption at the regional level, particularly within the Western region of Ghana, despite its distinct livelihood structure and rapid socio-economic transformation. This region presents unique characteristics shaped by agriculture, fishing, mining, and expanding urbanization, all of which are likely to influence household food consumption behaviors differently from national averages. This study, therefore, addresses this gap by examining the demographic and socioeconomic determinants influencing households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana. By providing region-specific empirical evidence, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of household food consumption behavior and helps to fill the existing gap in localized evidence. The findings are expected to support the design of targeted nutrition, public health, and food policy interventions that are responsive to regional realities, while also complementing existing national-level studies. The objectives of the study are to: (1) analyze if demographic determinants influence households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana, and (2) assess whether socioeconomic determinants influence households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana.

The study further hypothesized that there is no statistically significant relationship between demographic as well as socioeconomic determinants and households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana.

Theoretical framework

This study is anchored in Household Production Theory, originally developed by Becker in 1965 [25], which posits that households derive utility not directly from market goods and services but from commodities that they produce by combining purchased goods, household resources, and time. The theory asserts that households allocate their limited resources, including income and time, to maximize utility subject to budget and time constraints [25, 26]. Food consumption decisions are therefore influenced not only by the monetary cost of food but also by the time required for food preparation and consumption [27]. Within this framework, the decision to consume food outside the home can be viewed as a household strategy for minimizing the time and effort associated with meal preparation while satisfying dietary needs and preferences. As households experience changes in income, employment, education, and other socioeconomic conditions, they may adjust their food consumption patterns by substituting home-prepared meals with commercially prepared foods.

The theory is relevant to the present study because it provides a basis for understanding how demographic and socioeconomic characteristics shape household engagement in out-of-home food consumption. Household income, for instance, influences the ability to purchase meals outside the home, while employment status affects the opportunity cost of time devoted to food preparation. Households with employed members, especially those engaged in full-time work, may be more likely to consume food outside the home due to time constraints. Similarly, educational attainment may influence food choices through its effect on income levels, nutritional knowledge, and lifestyle preferences [4].

Demographic factors are also consistent with the propositions of Household Production Theory [28]. Household size may affect economies of scale in food preparation, thereby influencing the relative attractiveness of home-prepared versus commercially prepared meals. Age and marital status may shape consumption preferences, time allocation, and household responsibilities, while household headship may influence decision-making authority regarding food expenditure. Place of residence is likewise important, as urban households generally have greater access to food vendors, restaurants, and other food service establishments than their rural counterparts. The theory suggests that households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption is the outcome of rational decisions made in response to resource constraints, time availability, and socioeconomic circumstances [25]. The theory therefore provides a suitable framework for examining the influence of demographic and socioeconomic factors on households’ participation in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the Western region of Ghana, with Sekondi-Takoradi serving as its administrative capital (Fig. 1). The region is one of Ghana’s sixteen administrative regions and comprises fourteen districts. It covers an area of approximately 23,921 square kilometers, representing about 10% of the country’s total land area. Approximately 75% of the region’s vegetation falls within Ghana’s high forest zone, and it lies within the equatorial climatic zone, which is characterized by moderate temperatures throughout the year [29, 30]. The Western region is the wettest part of Ghana, receiving an average annual rainfall of about 1,600 mm. It is bordered by the Central region to the east, Côte d’Ivoire to the west, the Ashanti, Bono, and Ahafo regions to the north, and the Gulf of Guinea to the south [29, 31].

Map of Western region showing study locations. Source: Geographic information system; remote sensing and cartography laboratory, University of Cape Coast

The Western region was selected for this study because it records one of the highest levels of food insecurity among the southern administrative regions of Ghana. The prevalence of food insecurity in the region is estimated at 14.2%, exceeding the national average of 11.7% [32, 33]. The region also faces significant nutritional challenges across different population groups. Among children, the prevalence of underweight, stunting, and wasting has been reported at 64.3%, 57.0%, and 56.4%, respectively [32]. Among adolescents aged 15–19 years, thinness affects 21.3% of females and 50.2% of males. Among adults aged 20–49 years, thinness is reported among 4.8% of women and 4.6% of men [34]. Furthermore, only 60.8% of women achieve minimum dietary diversity, while 40.7% consume sentinel unhealthy foods and 51.9% consume sweetened beverages. Anaemia remains a major public health concern, affecting 45.9% of women in the region [34]. These indicators underscore the nutritional vulnerabilities that justify the selection of the Western region as the study area.

Demographically, children of household heads constitute approximately 40% or more of household members across the region. Male-headed households outnumber female-headed households in all districts. Additionally, more than 90% of residents in all districts, with the exception of Juaboso-Bia, are Ghanaians by birth. The Akan ethnic group, comprising the indigenous Wassa and Sefwi people as well as migrant Fantes, accounts for more than two-thirds of the Ghanaian-born population in every district [31].

The Western region is richly endowed with natural resources, making it one of the country’s most economically significant regions. It is Ghana’s largest producer of cocoa, rubber, and coconut and is also a major producer of oil palm. Its extensive tropical forests support the production of raw timber, sawn timber, and processed wood products. The region also possesses abundant mineral resources, including gold, bauxite, iron ore, diamonds, and manganese, many of which are either currently exploited or have considerable economic potential [31, 35].

The economy of the region is predominantly agriculture-based. Agriculture, including crop farming, fishing, animal husbandry, and hunting, employs approximately 58.1% of the working population. Other major economic activities include production and transport (14.5%), trading (10.2%), and professional and technical occupations (5.4%). These diverse livelihood activities influence household income, food production, food access, and nutritional outcomes, making the Western region an appropriate setting for investigating issues related to food insecurity and nutrition.

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive study design [36] was employed to examine how demographic and socioeconomic factors influence households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana, with a particular focus on individual household members. A descriptive cross-sectional design was considered the most appropriate because the study sought to assess household characteristics and examine the prevalence and relationships among the study variables at a single point in time without manipulating the study environment [36]. This design enabled the collection of data from a relatively large and geographically dispersed sample within a limited period and with available resources, while providing a snapshot of the determinants influencing households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the selected districts [37]. Furthermore, the cross-sectional approach is well suited for estimating the prevalence of key outcomes and identifying associations between variables, thereby addressing the study objectives efficiently.

Sampling technique

A sample size of 1,200 households was determined for the study, representing approximately 0.06% of the total population of 2,022,816 households in the Western region. The selection of this sample size was guided by the principle that the sample must be adequate and representative of the target population to ensure the reliability and validity of the study findings. As noted by Corgi [38], determining an appropriate sample size is a critical step in research design, as it influences the accuracy and generalizability of the results. Cochran’s [39] sample size determination formula was employed to calculate the required sample size. This formula was selected because it is widely recognized as one of the most appropriate methods for determining sample sizes in large populations. As indicated by Cochran, the application of this formula requires knowledge of the population size and the desired level of precision [39]. Based on these considerations, a sample size of 1,200 households was deemed sufficient for the study.

A multistage sampling technique was employed to select the respondents for the study. The sample frame of the study included all districts in the Western region. The region is divided into 14 MMDAs (made up of 1 Metropolitan, 8 Municipals and 5 Ordinary Assemblies) [29, 31].

The first stage

The districts in the region are not the same in terms of population, social, economic and geographic factors [41]. Therefore, through cluster sampling technique, the region was grouped into two strata, urban (districts with adequate social amenities) and rural (districts with inadequate social amenities [42]. The urban stratum constitutes Ahanta West Municipal, Jomoro Municipal, Nzema East Municipal, Prestea-Huni Valley Municipal, Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis, Tarkwa-Nsuaem Municipal, Wassa Amenfi East Municipal, Effia-Kwesimintsim Municipal, and Wassa Amenfi West Municipal, while the rural stratum also involves Ellembelle, Mpohor, Shama, Wassa Amenfi Central, and Wassa East Districts.

The second stage

A simple random sampling technique was employed to select two districts from each stratum, resulting in four study districts. Under this approach, the names of all districts within each stratum were written on separate pieces of paper, folded, and placed in separate containers. The containers were thoroughly shaken to ensure adequate mixing of the papers. A volunteer was then invited to randomly select two folded papers from each container. The districts whose names were drawn constituted the selected study districts. This procedure ensured that every district within a stratum had an equal probability of being selected Table 1.

Following the selection of the study districts, two communities were randomly selected from each district using the same simple random sampling (lottery) method. A list of all communities within each selected district was compiled, and the names of the communities were written on separate pieces of paper, folded, and placed in a container. After thorough mixing, two communities were randomly drawn from each district. The selected communities were as follows: Apremdo, and Ahenta Abasa from Effia-Kwesimintsim Municipality; Nkroful, and Effiankwanta from Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis; Nyame Tiase, and Mpohor from Mpohor District; and Ateiku, and Nsadweso from Wassa East District. These eight communities constituted the study clusters from which households were subsequently selected for data collection.

The third stage

The third stage of the sampling procedure was a proportionate sampling, thus, assigning the number of respondents to each stratum. For representativeness, the strata were given equal proportion of 25% of the total sample size which translates into 300 respondents. Equal proportions were assigned to the strata because on average, each stratum was made up of 1.6 households per house. Again, the number of persons per household was 4.7 [43].

The fourth stage

Systematic sampling was employed to select households from the study communities, with a predetermined sampling fraction applied. Respondents were drawn from Apremdo and Ahenta Abasa in the Effia-Kwesimintsim Municipality; Nkroful and Effia-Nkwanta in the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis; Nyame Tiase and Mpohor in the Mpohor District; and Ateiku and Nsadweso in the Wassa East District. So, based on the sample fraction, the first house from Apremdo was selected. The sample fraction was determined by dividing the total number of houses within each study site by the number of respondents to be selected at each community from each district, (i.e., 150). Per the 2021 Population and Housing Census, Apremdo has a total of 1,664 housing units. Based on this, a sampling fraction was computed as 1,664 ÷ 150 = 11.09, which was approximately rounded to 11. A random number between 1 and 11 was first generated, from which 2 was selected. Beginning at a major landmark (such as a lorry station or bus stop), households were then selected in a serpentine pattern starting from the second house. Subsequent selections followed a fixed interval of 11, resulting in the 2nd, 13th (2 + 11), 24th (13 + 11) houses, and so on. In each selected household, one available and willing household head was randomly chosen to participate in the study. Where a selected household had no eligible respondent, the procedure was restarted from a different landmark and the selection process repeated until the required sample size was achieved. This same systematic procedure was applied uniformly across all eight study communities, namely Apremdo and Ahenta Abasa in the Effia-Kwesimintsim Municipality; Nkroful and Effia-Nkwanta in the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis; Nyame Tiase and Mpohor in the Mpohor District; and Ateiku and Nsadweso in the Wassa East District.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this study, eligible participants were households residing in the selected study communities, represented by the household head or principal breadwinner who was at least 18 years of age, knowledgeable about the household’s food acquisition, preparation, and consumption practices, and willing to provide informed consent to participate in the study. Households were excluded if they were unoccupied during the survey period, if no eligible respondent was available after repeated visits, or if the selected respondent declined to participate.

Measures

The primary independent variables (IVs) in this study were demographic and socioeconomic factors. Demographic factors included the respondent’s sex, age, marital status, household headship, household size, and place of residence. Socioeconomic factors encompassed the highest level of education completed by the household head, the household head’s employment status, and household income. The dependent variable (DV) was out-of-home food consumption, measured dichotomously as “Yes” (coded 1) or “No” (coded 0). A value of 1 indicated that the respondent consumed food outside the home, whereas a value of 0 indicated that they did not. Out-of-home food consumption was assessed using self-reported responses. Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire developed by the first author as part of his PhD thesis entitled “Factors Influencing Food Security, Hunger, and Dietary Quality among Households in the Western Region of Ghana.” The questionnaire collected data on household demographic (sex, age, marital status, household headship, household size, and place of residence) and socioeconomic (highest education level of the household head, employment status, and household income) characteristics, out-of-home food consumption, food security, hunger, dietary quality, and food availability and accessibility dimensions. An English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data collection procedure

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the University of Cape Coast Institutional Review Board (UCCIRB) (UCCIRB/CES/2024/203). The study was conducted from 5 June 2025 to 4 June 2026. To enhance inclusivity, the questionnaire was translated into Fante, a widely spoken language in the study area, to accommodate participants who were not fluent in English. Data collection was carried out over a two-week period, from 10 June to 29 June 2025, across the selected eight communities in the four districts (Effia-Kwesimintsim Municipal, Sekondi Takoradi Metropolitan, Mpohor District, and Wassa East District) in the Western region of Ghana. The process was supported by five field assistants who were recruited and adequately trained prior to fieldwork. They assisted respondents in completing the questionnaires where necessary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation. Household heads were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to withdraw at any time without penalty, and the confidentiality of their responses. Participant anonymity was strictly maintained, and no identifying information was collected. Questionnaires were administered through face-to-face interviews, and no financial or material incentives were provided to participants.

Data processing and analysis

Data were coded, entered, and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. The analysis was based on completed questionnaires, yielding a response rate of 95% (N = 1,140). Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize participants’ responses on out-of-home food consumption into proportions. Pearson’s chi-square test of independence was employed to examine bivariate associations among these variables in order to determine whether significant relationships existed between them. Subsequently, a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the extent to which the independent variables (demographics, and socioeconomic factors) predicted the dependent variable (out-of-home food consumption). Statistical significance was determined at p < .05.

Discussion

This study examined factors associated with household engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana. Overall, the findings indicate that a slightly higher proportion of households (54.5%) engaged in out-of-home food consumption compared to those who did not (45.5%). The multivariable analysis further revealed that education level, sex of respondent, and place of residence were significant predictors of out-of-home food consumption. The results suggest that socioeconomic and demographic factors play a key role in shaping household food consumption behaviors. Specifically, household members with a tertiary school education were found to have higher odds of engaging in out-of-home food consumption compared to household members with no formal education. This finding suggests that out-of-home food consumption is not solely driven by lack of education or nutritional awareness but is also shaped by socioeconomic advancement, employment patterns, and changing lifestyles associated with higher educational attainment. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that higher educational attainment is often associated with increased consumption of foods prepared outside the home [44, 45]. Individuals with tertiary education are more likely to be employed in formal sectors characterized by demanding work schedules, urban lifestyles, and limited time for home meal preparation, thereby increasing reliance on restaurants, fast-food outlets, and other food vendors. In addition, higher educational attainment is frequently linked with increased income and purchasing power, which may enhance the affordability and accessibility of out-of-home foods. This finding can also be interpreted through the household production theory, which posits that households allocate both time and income in the production of commodities such as meals [25]. Individuals with tertiary education often face higher opportunity costs of time due to employment and professional commitments [25]. Consequently, purchasing meals prepared outside the home may represent a rational substitution for time-intensive home meal preparation, thereby increasing engagement in out-of-home food consumption [25]. Studies conducted in both developed and developing countries have similarly reported positive associations between higher education and eating out practices [11, 46]. For example, Landais et al. [11] found that individuals with higher socioeconomic and educational status consumed more meals outside the home due to convenience and lifestyle preferences. Likewise, Oliveira and Raposo [46] observed that highly educated individuals were more likely to consume restaurant and takeaway foods, partly because of urbanization and occupational commitments. Although higher education may improve nutrition knowledge, the convenience, social appeal, and time-saving nature of out-of-home foods may outweigh health considerations among highly educated households. The finding highlights the need to address out of home food environments in strategies aimed at improving population diet quality and reducing non-communicable disease risk.

The study found that household members with a secondary school education and those with vocational training school education had lower odds of engaging in out-of-home food consumption compared to household members with no formal education. This means that having these levels of education is associated with a reduced tendency to consume meals in restaurants or other outside-food settings. It suggests that, in this context, people with secondary or vocational education may prefer home-prepared meals or may have limited financial or lifestyle factors that reduce their likelihood of eating out compared to those without formal education. This finding aligns with existing literature suggesting that educational attainment influences dietary behaviors and food choices [47]. Individuals with higher levels of education are generally more aware of the health implications of frequent consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods and are more likely to prioritize home-prepared meals. Education may also enhance food literacy and cooking skills, particularly in the case of vocational training, thereby increasing confidence and competence in meal preparation at home. From the perspective of the household production theory, household members with greater food preparation skills may produce meals more efficiently within the home, reducing the need to substitute home-produced meals with commercially prepared foods [25]. Improved cooking competence lowers the time and resource costs associated with meal preparation and may therefore encourage reliance on home-cooked meals [25]. Moreover, higher educational attainment can shape economic decision-making and lifestyle patterns, fostering a preference for home-cooked meals despite the availability of out-of-home food options. Previous studies have demonstrated that less educated populations are more likely to consume fast foods or street foods due to convenience and limited nutritional knowledge [48, 49]. In contrast, educated households may allocate resources more strategically, balancing cost, health, and dietary quality [47]. Similar patterns have been observed in diverse contexts, including Germany [49] and Saudi Arabia [50], respectively, where higher education was associated with reduced reliance on ready-made meals. This finding highlights the role of education as a protective factor against frequent out-of-home food consumption and highlight the potential of nutrition education and skill-based interventions to promote healthier eating behaviors, mainly among populations with lower levels of formal education.

The study found that female household members had higher likelihood of engaging in out-of-home food consumption compared to their male household members. This finding may reflect changing social and economic roles of women, mostly in urban settings where increased participation in education, employment, and income-generating activities reduces the time available for home meal preparation. As a result, women may rely more on foods prepared outside the home because of convenience and time constraints [51]. This observation mirrored the household production theory, which suggests that household members make consumption decisions based on trade-offs between time and market resources [25]. As women’s participation in employment and income-generating activities increases, the opportunity cost of time spent preparing meals at home also rises, making out-of-home food consumption a practical alternative [25]. In many developing countries, the growing involvement of women in the workforce has been associated with increased demand for ready-to-eat and commercially prepared foods [52]. Additionally, women are often more socially engaged in activities that involve eating outside the home, such as workplace meals, social gatherings, and shopping-related food purchases [5]. Previous studies have similarly reported higher frequencies of out-of-home food consumption among females, primarily among working-age women and urban residents [53, 54]. In contrast, the finding refuted a study by Seguin et al. [55], which found that the frequency of out-of-home food consumption was higher among males than females. The availability and accessibility of fast foods, street foods, and restaurant meals may further contribute to this pattern. This finding therefore, calls for interventions that promote healthy and convenient food choices for working women and female-headed households.

The study found that rural dwellers had lower odds of engaging in out-of-home food consumption compared to urban dwellers. This suggests that residence location influences eating habits, where urban dwellers are more inclined to consume out-of-home foods—possibly because restaurants and food vendors are more available and accessible in cities, while rural residents may rely more on home-prepared meals due to fewer food outlets or different lifestyle patterns. This finding is consistent with existing literature indicating that urbanization is a major driver of changing dietary behaviors and increased consumption of foods prepared outside the home [52]. Urban residents are more frequently exposed to restaurants, fast-food outlets, street food vendors, and convenience food services, which increase the accessibility and availability of out-of-home meals [56]. In contrast, rural households often rely more on home-produced or home-prepared foods due to limited commercial food outlets and stronger adherence to traditional dietary practices [3]. Additionally, urban lifestyles are commonly associated with demanding work schedules, longer commuting times, and reduced time for meal preparation, thereby encouraging dependence on convenient ready-to-eat foods [52]. The household production theory provides further support for this finding by emphasizing the role of time constraints in household decision-making [25]. Urban households may face greater time pressures and therefore choose commercially prepared foods as substitutes for home-produced meals, whereas rural households may have relatively greater flexibility for meal preparation and access to home-produced food resources [25]. Rural dwellers, on the other hand, may have greater access to fresh agricultural produce and more opportunities for home cooking, which can reduce the need for eating outside the home. Similar findings have been reported in several studies, where urban residence was positively associated with eating out frequency and fast-food consumption [57, 58]. Economic and environmental factors may also contribute to this disparity. Urban households generally have higher disposable incomes and greater exposure to modern food environments, including aggressive marketing of processed and convenience foods [56]. Conversely, rural households may face limited access to commercial food establishments and may prioritize cost-saving behaviors through home meal preparation [59]. This finding emphasizes the influence of urbanization and the food environment on dietary behaviors and suggest the need for targeted nutrition interventions in rapidly urbanizing settings.

The study found statistically significant relationships between demographic and socioeconomic factors and households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption in the Western region of Ghana. Variables such as sex, age, educational attainment, marital status, family size, employment status, household income, family headship, and place of residence were all significantly associated with out-of-home food consumption. These findings suggest that households’ decisions to consume food outside the home are influenced by a complex interplay of individual, household, and environmental characteristics. The findings also align with the household production theory, which argues that household consumption choices are shaped by the allocation of limited resources, particularly time and income. Factors such as education, employment status, income, household size, and place of residence may influence the relative costs of preparing meals at home versus purchasing prepared foods, thereby affecting engagement in out-of-home food consumption [25]. This observation is consistent with previous studies that identified demographic and socioeconomic factors as important determinants of eating-out behaviors [60, 61]. For instance, Yalvac et al. [60] reported that factors such as income, education, urban residence, and employment significantly influence the frequency of out-of-home food consumption. Similarly, Rahbarinejad et al. [61] noted that socioeconomic advancement, urbanization, and lifestyle transitions in developing countries have contributed to increased reliance on commercially prepared foods. Higher income and employment levels may increase purchasing power and exposure to convenience foods, while demographic characteristics such as age and sex may shape food preferences, social behaviors, and time available for meal preparation. The significant association between place of residence and out-of-home food consumption further highlights the role of the food environment and urbanization in shaping dietary practices. Urban residents are often exposed to a wider variety of restaurants, fast-food outlets, and street food vendors compared to rural residents, thereby increasing opportunities for eating outside the home [52]. This finding emphasizes the multidimensional nature of out-of-home food consumption and suggest that interventions aimed at promoting healthy dietary behaviors should consider both demographic and socioeconomic contexts.

The study found that 54.5% of households in the Western region of Ghana reported engaging in out-of-home food consumption. This finding indicates that more than half of the surveyed households regularly consume meals outside the home, highlighting the growing prevalence of this dietary behavior. The observed level of engagement may reflect shifts in lifestyle, including increased urbanization, busier work schedules, and greater access to commercially prepared foods, which collectively reduce the reliance on home-cooked meals. This finding contradicts earlier studies, which reported that approximately 80%, 27%, and 95.9% of household members incurred some expenditure on food prepared outside the home [4, 62, 63], respectively. From the perspective of the household production theory, these changes may increase the opportunity cost of time devoted to meal preparation, leading households to substitute home-produced meals with foods obtained from restaurants, street vendors, and other commercial sources [25]. This result highlights the need for targeted nutrition education and policies that promote healthy, convenient alternatives to out-of-home meals in urbanized and rapidly changing food environments.

The study found that 45.5% of households in the Western region of Ghana did not engage in out-of-home food consumption. This finding indicates that, although a slight majority of households consume meals outside the home, a substantial proportion still relies on home-prepared foods. The continued prevalence of home-cooked meals among nearly half of the household members may reflect traditional dietary practices, limited access to commercial food outlets, or economic considerations, generally among rural or lower-income households [52, 59, 64]. The household production theory may also explain this behavior, as households with lower incomes or greater access to household labor may find it more economical to produce meals at home rather than purchase prepared foods [25]. In such settings, home meal preparation remains an efficient strategy for maximizing household welfare while minimizing food expenditure [25]. A previous study reported that only 4.1% of participants never ate food outside the home, a finding that contrasts with the results of the present study [63]. The persistence of home meal preparation suggests the potential for promoting healthy dietary behaviors through interventions that build on existing home-cooking practices. This finding calls for nutritional policies and programs that address the growing risk of diet-related non-communicable diseases while respecting cultural and socioeconomic contexts.

Implications

Since all independent variables showed statistically significant relationships with out-of-home food consumption, interventions should adopt a multidimensional approach that considers demographic, socioeconomic, and residential characteristics. Government agencies and public health stakeholders may need to regulate food vendors, improve food hygiene standards, and encourage healthy menu options in food establishments. Future studies employing mixed-methods or longitudinal designs may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants and dynamics of out-of-home food consumption among households in Ghana. Additionally, studies should incorporate detailed dietary assessments to evaluate the nutritional implications of such consumption patterns. From a policy perspective, there is a need to strengthen urban food environment regulations and promote access to affordable, healthy food options, particularly for working populations and urban households.

Limitations

Although the findings of this study provide important insights into households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption, they should be interpreted in light of several limitations and within the context of the Western region of Ghana. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the independent variables and households’ engagement in out-of-home food consumption. Consequently, the associations observed cannot be interpreted as evidence of causation. Second, the study relied on self-reported information from respondents, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Respondents may have inaccurately reported their food consumption practices, thereby affecting the validity of the findings. Third, the measurement of out-of-home food consumption did not capture the frequency, quantity, or nutritional quality of foods consumed outside the home. This limits a more comprehensive understanding of households’ consumption patterns and their nutritional implications. Fourth, the quantitative design of the study did not provide insights into the underlying motivations and contextual factors influencing households’ decisions to consume food outside the home. Finally, the absence of clustering information in the dataset limited the ability to adjust for potential community-level effects, which may have influenced standard error estimates.

Conclusion

Out-of-home food consumption was reported by 54.5% of households in the Western region of Ghana. The study found that education, sex, age, income, household size, occupation, marital status, and place of residence, were significantly associated with this behavior. Overall, the findings answered the research question by demonstrating that out-of-home food consumption is shaped by key socio-economic and demographic characteristics. Tertiary education and female respondents increased the likelihood of engaging in out-of-home food consumption, while secondary and vocational education levels and rural residence were associated with lower odds. These findings contribute to understanding dietary behavior by highlighting the influence of education, gender, and place of residence on food choices. The study suggests that nutrition and food environment policies should account for socio-economic disparities, particularly urban–rural differences and access to food outlets. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and future research should adopt longitudinal and qualitative approaches to further explore underlying motivations and behavioral drivers.
