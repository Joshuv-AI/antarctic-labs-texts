The Epidemiology and Infection Control of Candida Auris in Shanghai
Authors: Mingjing Wei, Jian Guo, Li Li, Junhao Zhu, Yuyuan Xue, Guo Chen, Fan Yang, Wenjuan Wu, Min Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Candida auris is a globally emerging multidrug‐resistant fungus. Its dissemination is driven by patient movement and asymptomatic carriers. However, molecular epidemiological studies linking imported cases to local strains in large metropolitan areas remain limited.ObjectivesTo analyse the molecular epidemiology and resistance mechanisms of 33 clinical isolates in Shanghai and propose an integrated infection control strategy tailored to metropolitan healthcare settings.MethodsIsolates from 19 patients were identified via MALDI‐TOF MS and sequencing. Antifungal susceptibility and biofilm formation were assessed. Environmental contamination was evaluated using PCR. Whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) was employed to identify resistance mutations and perform phylogenetic analysis.ResultsAll 33 isolates were fluconazole‐resistant. Resistance to amphotericin B and echinocandins was observed in 9 and 4 isolates, respectively. All echinocandin‐resistant strains harboured the Fks1‐S639F mutation. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Clade III (South African clade) was the dominant lineage. Colonisation was concentrated in the urinary tract and inguinal regions, while bedside tables were identified as key environmental reservoirs. All strains exhibited strong biofilm‐forming capacity.ConclusionsICU patients serve as primary reservoirs, with transmission driven by interregional movement. The distinct colonisation patterns and environmental hotspots provide specific targets for screening. Integrating PCR‐based environmental monitoring with culture methods constitutes an effective strategy. These findings underscore the need for a standardised protocol integrating molecular diagnostics to optimise infection control in urban hospitals.

Introduction

The first case of C. auris in China was reported in Beijing in 2018, followed by sporadic cases in various regions [1]. By the end of 2024, infections in China had shown a rapid upward trend, with 1391 new cases reported between 2023 and 2024, representing an increase of more than 300% compared to previous years [2]. Significant geographic clustering and interprovincial transmission were observed, particularly in Hong Kong, Guangdong, Liaoning, and Beijing [2, 3, 4]. Epidemiological data indicate a predominance among male patients, with individuals over 50 and critically ill patients most affected [5, 6]. Colonisation was most frequently observed in the urinary and respiratory tracts [5, 7, 8]. Molecular typing has confirmed that the current epidemic strains are primarily derived from the South African (Clade III) and South Asian (Clade I) clades [6]. Resistance surveillance revealed a fluconazole resistance rate of 98.4%, with resistance rates to amphotericin B and echinocandins reaching 39.1% and 4.1%, respectively [5, 6, 9]. C. auris is capable of spreading through hospital outbreaks, interregional transmission, and international importation [6, 10].

C. auris is capable of colonising human skin as well as inanimate surfaces, which may result in a variety of clinical manifestations, including bloodstream infections and central nervous system infections [11, 12, 13, 14]. Globally, isolates are classified into six major geographic clades: South Asian (Clade I), East Asian (Clade II), South African (Clade III), South American (Clade IV), Iranian (Clade V), and Bangladeshi (Clade VI) [15, 16]. Despite the clear genetic distinctions among these clades, their evolutionary characteristics and epidemiological implications require further investigation.

The complicated resistance mechanisms involve multiple molecular‐level alterations. Azole resistance is primarily linked to mutations in the ERG11 gene, particularly at critical amino acid residues, such as Y132F, K143R, and F126L [17, 18, 19, 20]. Overexpression of efflux pump genes, including CDR1, CDR2, and MDR1, also significantly contributes to azole resistance [21, 22]. Resistance to echinocandins is strongly related to mutations in the FKS1 gene, especially S639P and S639F, which affect the function of 1,3‐β‐D‐glucan synthase, the primary target of this drug class [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Mutations in genes involved in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, such as ERG2, ERG6, and ERG13, are associated with amphotericin B resistance [28, 29]. Flucytosine resistance is mainly attributed to the F211I mutation in the FUR1 gene [30]. These resistance‐associated mutations show clade‐specific patterns: Y132F and K143R mainly occur in Clades I and IV, while F126L is unique to Clade III [31].

In this study, we employed whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) and antifungal susceptibility testing to analyse 33 clinical isolates from the Shanghai region [32, 33]. The antifungal susceptibility of the isolates to nine commonly used agents was assessed, confirming the emergence of multidrug‐resistant strains. This research represents the first epidemiological investigation in Shanghai and examines the molecular epidemiological relationships between imported cases and locally prevalent strains. These findings underscore the critical importance of admission screening. A standardised screening and surveillance protocol was then developed, integrating conventional culture methods with molecular diagnostics.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Data and Isolates

This study collected 33 clinical isolates from 19 patients with C. auris infection or colonisation in Shanghai between February 2021 and February 2025. The specimens included sputum, ear discharge, urine, and skin colonisation samples, along with associated clinical data. All sampling procedures followed CDC guidelines for C. auris screening (https://www.cdc.gov/fungal/candida‐auris/c‐auris‐screening.html). This study was approved by the Ethics Board of Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, China (Approval No: KY2025‐791). Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because the samples used were residual clinical specimens and the study did not involve any intervention. These strains were used exclusively for scientific research.

Identification

All 33 clinical isolates were identified using matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionisation time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS; Zybio Corporation, China). To evaluate colony morphology and phenotypic characteristics, isolates were inoculated onto CHROMagar Candida medium (CHROMagar Microbiology, China) and incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Thermal tolerance was assessed by culturing the isolates on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) plates at 30°C (suboptimal), 37°C (physiological), and 42°C (febrile temperature) for 48 h. For molecular identification, genomic DNA was extracted using a nucleic acid extraction kit (Shanghai Suchuang Diagnostic Products Co. Ltd.) following the manufacturer's instructions [34]. Briefly, cells were suspended in 0.2 mL of extraction buffer containing the extraction matrix, vortexed vigorously for 5 min, heated at 100°C for 5 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min. The ITS region was amplified and sequenced using primers ITS1 (5′‐TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG‐3′) and ITS4 (5′‐TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC‐3′). Sequencing was conducted by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd., and species identity was confirmed via BLASTN comparison. Sequences for each isolate were deposited separately in GenBank under accession numbers PX114516–PX114549.

Whole‐Genome Sequencing and Phylogenetic Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted as described in Section 2.2. The supernatant containing genomic DNA was collected for library preparation and sequencing on the DNBseq T7 platform (MGI Tech, China). The total fold coverage was approximately 206×. Strict filtering criteria were applied, including a minimum variant allele frequency of 10%. Raw sequencing data underwent quality control using FastQC v0.12.0 and adapter trimming with Trim Galore v0.6.10. For assembly‐based analysis, de novo assembly was performed using SPAdes v3.15.5, retaining contigs ≥ 1000 bp, and assembly quality was assessed with QUAST v5.2.0. For phylogenetic analysis, clean reads were aligned to the C. auris reference genome B8441 (GenBank accession: GCA_002759435.3) using BWA‐MEM v0.7.17. Subsequently, single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling was performed using GATK HaplotypeCaller v4.4.0.0. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using IQ‐TREE v2.2.0 under the TVMe+ASC + R3 substitution model with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) in the National Genomics Data Centre, China National Centre for Bioinformation (Accession No. CRA15534244) and are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

In Vitro Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

To ensure the robustness of the susceptibility profiles, testing was performed using two independent methods. First, Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs) were determined by the broth microdilution method in accordance with CLSI M27‐A3 guidelines, using nine antifungal agents purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd [35, 36]. Second, the Sensititre YeastOne YO10 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed following the manufacturer's instructions. The results from both methods were cross‐referenced to verify accuracy. In the absence of established MIC breakpoints for C. auris , susceptibility interpretation was based on the tentative breakpoints published by the U.S. Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22,019 and Candida krusei ATCC 6258 were used as quality control strains for both assays.

Environmental Sampling and PCR Detection

To evaluate the extent of environmental contamination, we conducted systematic sampling of the hospital bed and surrounding high‐touch surfaces (including the bedside table, ventilator, and bed rails) of Patient Pt10. A total of 48 swab samples were collected using sterile cotton swabs moistened with sterile saline. To enable simultaneous comparison between culture and molecular methods, each swab was immersed in a tube containing 2 mL of sterile saline and vortexed vigorously for 1 min to release adherent microorganisms. The resulting suspension was divided into two aliquots: One aliquot was inoculated onto CHROMagar for culture, while the other was subjected to genomic DNA extraction. Real‐time PCR was subsequently performed using a commercial PCR detection kit (Dynamiker Biotechnology (Tianjin) Co. Ltd.) [37]. The specific PCR reaction conditions are shown in Table 1.

Biofilm Formation

All isolates were initially cultured on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) plates at 30°C for 24 h to obtain single colonies. A single colony was inoculated into 4 mL of YPD broth and incubated at 30°C with shaking at 220 rpm overnight (16–18 h) to reach the stationary phase. Cells were harvested, washed, and standardised to 1 × 106 cells/mL in RPMI‐1640 medium using ahaemocytometer. A volume of 200 μL was dispensed into each well of a flat‐bottom 96‐well microtiter plate and incubated at 37°C for 24 h to allow biofilm formation. After incubation, wells were carefully washed three times with sterile PBS to remove planktonic cells. The adherent biofilm was fixed with methanol for 15 min, stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min, and gently rinsed with distilled water to remove excess dye. The plates were air‐dried, and the retained dye was solubilised in 33% glacial acetic acid. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Six technical replicates were included for each sample, and the assay was performed in three independent biological replicates. C. albicans (ATCC 14,053) and C. parapsilosis (ATCC 22,019) served as quality controls, while RPMI‐1640 medium alone was used as a negative control [38].

Statistical Analyses

An analysis of variance test was used for statistical analysis of biofilm‐forming capacity.

No generative AI tools were used in the creation or manipulation of the figures.

Results

Clinical Characteristics

This study analysed the characteristics of 19 patients with C. auris infection or colonisation in Shanghai. The epidemic timeline was initiated by an imported index case from Beijing (Patient 1), who was admitted in January 2021. This highlighted the critical role of inter‐regional patient transfer in the initial introduction of the pathogen. Following this introduction, two sporadic cases were identified between 2021 and 2023. Subsequently, a rapid expansion phase commenced after May 2024, during which 17 additional cases were detected. A male predominance was observed (15 cases, 78.9%), with a mean age of 62.8 ± 14.0 years. Cases were primarily identified in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) (10/19, 52.6%). The mean duration from hospital admission to first C. auris isolation was 8.7 days. Underlying comorbidities were prevalent, particularly diabetes and hypertension. Regarding clinical outcomes, one in‐hospital death occurred (5.3%). Among the surviving patients, 12 (63.2%) showed clinical improvement, while 6 (31.6%) were discharged without improvement, indicating the persistence of colonisation in a significant proportion of cases. Strain Identification and Collection Results

The mass spectrometry identification results were all confirmed as C. auris , with a confidence level of 99.9%. After conducting first‐generation sequencing on 33 strains of C. auris , the gene sequencing results were compared against the BLASTN database, showing 98%–100% similarity to C. auris , consistent with the mass spectrometry identification results.

Sampling distribution analysis (Figure 2) showed C. auris colonisation was primarily in urethral and groin areas, with detection rates of 75% and 50%, respectively. Additionally, the strain could also be detected on high‐contact environmental surfaces, particularly bedside tables.

Phenotypically, colonies cultured on CHROMagar Candida at 37°C appeared creamy white initially, transitioning to pale purple after 48 h (Figure 3A,B). Phylogenetic Analysis

To characterise the genomic landscape, WGS was performed on all 33 isolates using the DNBseq T7 platform. Phylogenetic analysis, incorporating reference sequences from all six major clades, delineated the Shanghai isolates into three distinct lineages (Figure 5). Clade III (South African) emerged as the dominant lineage, comprising the majority of the isolates (28/33). Notably, strain SH0001, imported from Beijing, clustered uniquely within Clade II (East Asian). Furthermore, four isolates (SH0002, SH0024, SH0027, and SH0033) clustered firmly with Clade I (South Asian) reference strains.

Phylogenetic tree of isolates in Shanghai based on WGS.

Phylogenomic reconstruction of Clade III identified ICU as the primary nexus for local transmission, characterised by a high‐confidence cluster comprising isolates SH0005, SH0006, and SH0018 (Figure 6). These strains, recovered from Patients 4, 5, and 10 who shared overlapping EICU residencies between August and October 2024, exhibited minimal genetic distances despite originating from diverse anatomical sites (urine, sputum, and skin swabs), confirming rapid cross‐site dissemination within the unit. Crucially, the integration of specimen source data revealed a complex pattern of compartmentalised polyclonal colonisation (Figure 6). For instance, Patient 11 exhibited significant genetic discordance between invasive and superficial isolates; strains SH0019 and SH0020, recovered from deep abscesses, were phylogenetically distinct from the skin isolate SH0021, suggesting multiple independent acquisition events rather than a single infection source. Similarly, Patient 10 harboured the cluster‐associated strain SH0018 on the skin, while simultaneously carrying genetically distant lineages (SH0015 and SH0029) in the respiratory tract.

Pairwise SNP analysis provided further corroboration of the phylogenetic structure. As illustrated in the heatmap (Figure S1), distinct high‐SNP barriers separated the three identified lineages. Strain SH0001 displayed extensive genetic divergence from all other Shanghai isolates, consistent with its classification into Clade II. Notably, a highly clonal sub‐cluster comprising isolates SH0002, SH0024, SH0027, and SH0033 was evident; these strains exhibited near‐zero pairwise SNP distances among themselves but high divergence from the dominant outbreak lineage. The remaining isolates formed a large, related cluster corresponding to Clade III, indicative of a local clonal expansion.

To elucidate resistance mechanisms, resistance‐associated gene sequences were aligned against the reference strain CBS 10,913 T. All Clade III isolates harboured the ERG11 F126L amino acid substitution, whereas all Clade I and II isolates carried ERG11 Y132F. Notably, the FKS1 S639F mutation was identified exclusively in the echinocandin‐resistant isolates SH0010–SH0013 (Pt08), showing complete concordance with the phenotypic profiles. Broth microdilution assays yielded MIC values consistent with those obtained from YeastOne panel testing.

PCR

We conducted systematic monitoring of Patient Pt10's hospital bed and surrounding environment (including the bedside table, ventilator, and other surfaces near the bed), collecting a total of 48 swab samples. These samples were then subjected to both CHROMagar culture and PCR testing. The results showed that only the sample from Pt10's bedside table tested positive by both PCR and culture, yielding an overall positivity rate of 2.08% (1/48). This indicates that colonisation was limited to the patient's direct contact area, with no detectable spread to other zones. These findings suggest that the current disinfection measures are effective in non‐close‐contact areas.

Biofilm‐Forming Capacity

Each isolate exhibited strong biofilm‐forming capacity through the crystal violet assay. The biofilm formation of 66.7% (22/33) of C. auris isolates was comparable to that of C. albicans (p > 0.05). In comparison to C. parapsilosis , 78.8% (26/33) of the isolates showed higher biofilm‐forming capacities (p < 0.05). Strong biofilm production was universally observed across all three identified lineages (Clades I, II, and III), highlighting the inherent virulence potential of these strains.

Discussion

Studies have shown that C. auris adapts well to hospital environments, persistently colonising on patient skin and spreading via medical devices and surfaces, despite routine disinfection. This poses a significant risk of infection, particularly among immunocompromised patients [39, 40]. Since the first case of infection was reported in China in 2018, sporadic cases have been observed across various regions. However, to date, no cases have been reported in Shanghai, an international metropolis with a high concentration of medical resources.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of 33 isolates from Shanghai and classified them into three genetic clades (Clade I–III), with Clade III being the predominant lineage. The first case, imported from Beijing, belonged to Clade II, indicating a risk of interregional transmission. Antifungal susceptibility testing revealed universal resistance to fluconazole, with partial resistance to amphotericin B and echinocandins. WGS identified mutations in resistance genes, including mutations in ERG11 in fluconazole‐resistant strains and the FKS1 S639F mutation in echinocandin‐resistant strains.

High colonisation rates in the urinary tract and groin areas, combined with environmental detection and strong biofilm‐forming ability, underscore the role of C. auris in nosocomial transmission and the urgent need for improved infection control.

The resistance of isolates to fluconazole observed in this study is consistent with previous reports [41, 42], with all strains demonstrating high‐level resistance. Notably, the majority of the 19 patients had a history of hospitalisation in other healthcare facilities. Isolates recovered from these patients exhibited fluconazole resistance even in the absence of fluconazole treatment during the current admission, suggesting the presence of healthcare‐associated infections acquired outside the current institution.

Previous studies have shown that resistance‐related mutations in the ERG11 gene exhibit distinct clade‐specific distributions, with Y132F and K143R substitutions predominantly found in Clades I, IV, and V, while the F126L mutation is restricted to Clade III [31, 43]. Our findings are in agreement with these observations. All four Clade I isolates in this study were resistant to amphotericin B and exhibited higher MICs compared to isolates from Clades II and III, indicating that different genetic clades may harbour distinct antifungal resistance profiles [31]. Studies have shown that isolates from Clades I and III are often associated with outbreaks of invasive infections and exhibit multidrug resistance, particularly with fluconazole resistance rates reaching 94% and 96%, respectively [44]. Clade I isolates demonstrate significantly higher resistance rates to echinocandins and amphotericin B compared to other clades, predisposing them to the development of pan‐drug‐resistant infections [45]. Clade II isolates are most frequently recovered from patients with ear infections and generally exhibit lower levels of resistance relative to other clades [46].

One isolate exhibiting high‐level resistance to 5‐flucytosine was identified, warranting ongoing surveillance. Additionally, four echinocandin‐resistant isolates were recovered from the urine of patient Pt08. According to medication records, the detection of these resistant strains preceded the administration of echinocandins. This suggests the acquisition of a primarily resistant strain rather than resistance induced by therapeutic pressure. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring antifungal treatments based on local molecular epidemiology and the specific susceptibility profiles of isolates.

Shanghai, as a major referral centre, receives a large number of asymptomatic carriers transferred from other provinces and abroad. Due to the subtle clinical manifestations ofcolonisation, these cases are prone to being missed under current screening protocols. Such patients may disseminate the pathogen to high‐risk areas, such as ICUs, via inter‐facility transfer and intra‐hospital department changes.

Molecular epidemiological analysis further confirmed the presence of clonally related strains among the isolates from Shanghai, which were highly homologous to both domestic and international epidemic strains. This provides direct evidence of inter‐regional and cross‐border transmission. These findings highlight the critical need for comprehensive infection control in large tertiary care centres, including admission screening, contact isolation, and environmental decontamination.

Phylogenetic and SNP analyses revealed a complex, polyclonal mixed colonisation pattern in patient Pt08. Four longitudinal urine isolates, collected at two‐day intervals, were separated by substantial genetic distances and failed to cluster into a single terminal clade. Since accumulating hundreds of SNPs within days is biologically implausible for a single lineage, this topology likely captures the dynamic shedding of co‐existing, divergent clones. Consistent with recent reports of multi‐clonal C. auris infections [3, 31, 47], these findings suggest that relying on a single clinical isolate may underestimate the true within‐host genomic diversity and resistance potential in patients.

Environmental surveillance in this study indicated that C. auris contamination was primarily localised to the immediate patient zone, particularly high‐touch surfaces. Consequently, enhanced disinfection of these areas must be prioritised as a cornerstone of infection prevention and control (IPC) measures. In response to the challenges faced by international medical hubs like Shanghai, we propose a three‐tiered containment strategy. First, a rapid identification protocol should be established to optimise admission screening, specifically targeting high‐yield anatomical sites such as the urinary tract and inguinal region. Second, strict patient management protocols must be enforced; patients colonised or infected with C. auris should be placed in single‐room isolation or cohorted with rigorous contact precautions. Third, environmental hygiene and monitoring must be reinforced. This includes standardised terminal cleaning, regular environmental sampling, and strict adherence to hand hygiene protocols by healthcare workers to minimise the risk of nosocomial transmission.

Regular multi‐site colonisation screening should be conducted among high‐risk patients to enable early detection and intervention. A dual‐track detection strategy combining culture and PCR is recommended to balance sensitivity and turnaround time. Special disinfection protocols should be applied to high‐touch surfaces and medical equipment.

Notably, persistent colonisation was observed in patients Pt03 and Pt10 across two separate hospitalisations several months apart, confirming the organism's ability to survive long‐term in both the human host and hospital environment. We therefore recommend the establishment of long‐term follow‐up mechanisms for previously infected patients to facilitate ongoing surveillance.

This comprehensive infection control model offers a scalable framework for large metropolitan healthcare systems to mitigate transmission risks. Future studies should focus on delineating the transmission dynamics among different clades and evaluating the efficacy of environmental decontamination strategies in eradicating biofilms.

Conclusion

ICU patients serve as primary reservoirs, with transmission driven by interregional movement. The distinct colonisation patterns and environmental hotspots provide specific targets for screening. Integrating PCR‐based environmental monitoring with culture methods constitutes an effective strategy. Consequently, a standardised protocol integrating molecular diagnostics was developed to optimise infection control in urban hospitals.
