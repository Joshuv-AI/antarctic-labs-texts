Association between positive preadmission screening and COVID-19 community transmission: A retrospective observational study during an emergent novel infectious disease pandemic
Authors: Shingo Mitsushima, Yasushi Ohkusa, Kiyosu Taniguchi, Yasuharu Tokuda, Kenji Shibuya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter COVID-19 emerged, hospitals adopted preadmission screening to detect asymptomatic cases and avert nosocomial outbreaks; whether such clinical screening should be routine policy remains debated and has not been quantified in Japan.ObjectiveTo evaluate preadmission clinical screening as a component of pandemic preparedness.MethodsThe primary outcome was positivity on preadmission screening. We modeled COVID-19 positivity among asymptomatic preadmission patients using regression and a Tobit specification to accommodate excess zeros, then developed a predictive model. Analyses used hospital data linked to prefecture-level indicators of community transmission, including newly confirmed cases and influenza-like-illness (ILI) sentinel activity.ResultsIn this single-center retrospective study, 5897 newly confirmed COVID-19 cases were analyzed at the hospital. During the same period, Mie Prefecture reported 355,879 cases overall, including 45,551 visits to ILI sentinel sites. In 2022, screening positivity among asymptomatic preadmission patients correlated strongly with the number of newly confirmed cases in the prefecture. The model predicted non-zero screening positivity when newly confirmed prefectural cases exceeded ∼10,000, regardless of assumed ILI positivity. When prefectural cases were <2,000, predicted screening positivity was ∼0 even if assumed ILI positivity was ∼40 %.Discussion and conclusionsFindings suggest conditions for conducting preadmission clinical screening during the COVID-19 pandemic, which can support healthcare infrastructure preparedness against pandemics of novel infectious diseases.Keywords: COVID-19, Influenza-like illness, Positive rate, Preadmission screening, Predictive model

Introduction

After the emergence of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), which is caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), many hospitals implemented preadmission screening procedures for outpatients to prevent outbreaks on their premises. For detection of COVID-19, earlier studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of screening tests administered to asymptomatic patients before hospital admission [1,2]. By contrast, other reports have indicated low positive rates and high costs associated with screening of asymptomatic patients. Furthermore, lower transmissibility of COVID-19 has been observed among asymptomatic patients [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Evidence of discontinuous or selective screening is mixed: some studies have found no change in nosocomial outbreaks, whereas another identified increases; several did not adequately adjust for local epidemiology [[7], [8], [9]]. Decisions about implementing, continuing, or discontinuing routine preadmission screening of asymptomatic patients might depend on community transmission [10,11]. Therefore, caution is warranted when considering discontinuation.

For estimating COVID-19 prevalence, random population sampling that includes asymptomatic patients is ideal, but it is rarely feasible [12]. Prevalence estimated from random sampling simplifies estimation of the number of individuals who have acquired immunity within society. Moreover, it facilitates prediction of epidemic trends. However, implementing such sampling methods can be challenging in practice. For example, in Japan, monitoring surveys aimed at identifying asymptomatic individuals with COVID-19 among the general population were conducted, but the results were strongly biased because of the voluntary characteristics of the screening tests. It is likely that only individuals who were strongly concerned about infection prevention participated in the tests, leading to a very low positivity rate. Preadmission or outpatient screening before medical examinations of individuals not suspected of having COVID-19 can be regarded as a random sampling.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommended evaluation of the extent of infection in the community to develop prevention strategies for COVID-19 because it is related to SARS-CoV-2 community transmission [13]. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) also advised against testing asymptomatic hospitalized patients when the prevalence in the community was less than 2 % [14]. However, IDSA recommends testing when the prevalence of COVID-19 in the community is greater than 10 %. Nevertheless, the IDSA did not provide any rationale justifying that recommendation.

Starting March 19, 2021, The National Hospital Organization Mie Hospital (Mie Hospital) in Mie Prefecture implemented preadmission screening for COVID-19 for all newly hospitalized patients and caregivers, irrespective of the presence or absence of COVID-19 symptoms. Additionally, all outpatients were screened and triaged according to their symptoms. All healthcare workers and their family members underwent symptom checks every morning. For those who had symptoms, COVID-19 testing was performed. It is noteworthy that, until May 2023, no nosocomial outbreak of COVID-19 had occurred at Mie Hospital.

By analyzing data from these screenings, the study was conducted to ascertain whether correlation existed, or not, between the prevalence of COVID-19 cases in the community and the rate of positive cases among asymptomatic individuals. This analysis will facilitate evaluation of the effectiveness and necessity of preadmission screening tests for COVID-19 for identifying asymptomatic patients and for preventing outbreaks within the hospital setting. Therefore, the objective of this study is consideration of preadmission clinical screening testing during a pandemic of novel infectious disease based on the experiences of one hospital and community during the COVID-19 pandemic, which will yield lessons and relevant knowledge about the preparedness of healthcare infrastructure for pandemics of novel infectious diseases. This report is the first of a study quantitatively evaluating preadmission screening testing in Japan.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted as described hereinafter. First, the Mie Hospital patient characteristics are presented, followed by explanation of trends in the numbers of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in Mie Prefecture, the COVID-19 positive rate of screening in Mie Hospital, and the positive rate among influenza-like illness (ILI) patients in Mie Prefecture. Subsequently, a relation between the number of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in Mie Prefecture and the positive rate found among ILI patients is demonstrated. Screening testing was conducted using the HISCL™ SARS-CoV-2 antigen assay (Sysmex Corp., Hyogo, Japan) based on chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (CLEIA) technology. The test was useful for detection and for quantitative estimation of the presence of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (NP) in nasopharyngeal swabs. Finally, assuming a constant number of ILI cases in Mie Prefecture, the positive rate among people screened at Mie Hospital and the number of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in Mie Prefecture were derived.

This study used data of the number of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in Mie Prefecture, and data of the number of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients at Mie Hospital. Additionally, data of the positive rate for COVID-19 among individuals with ILI in Mie Prefecture were obtained from reports published by the Mie prefectural government [15,16].

For our study, we conducted regression analysis of the COVID-19 positive rate among asymptomatic cases in the screening group (outpatients on admission) at Mie Hospital. The regression model included the logarithmically transformed number of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in the whole community in Mie Prefecture, which encompassed both asymptomatic and symptomatic cases. Additionally, we included the COVID-19 positive rate among patients with ILI in Mie Prefecture, as well as their polynomial terms.

To ascertain the appropriate specifications of the regression function, we followed an incremental approach, adding higher-order terms if lower-order terms were found to be significant. We analyzed the data on a weekly basis because the ILI data were reported weekly. The study period extended from the 10th week of 2021 to the 50th week of 2022, providing a long timeframe to assess trends and patterns in the variables under investigation. For our study, we used the Tobit estimation procedure, which was weighted by the number of asymptomatic patients who underwent a COVID-19 test. This approach was chosen because of the existence of numerous zero positive rates observed from screening of asymptomatic patients.

Therefore, to understand the estimation results if a higher order was selected to polynomial function of log(Nt) or St, we present a contour plot for the estimated Pt > 0 in the domain of Nt and St. Additionally, association among Pt and Nt was found, assuming St as constant.

We adopted 5 % as the significance level and conducted all statistical analyses using software (Stata SE 17.0; Stata Corp.). The datasets supporting the results of this study are available in the figshare repository: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.24160341.v1.

Results

During the study period, there were 355,879 newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in Mie Prefecture, 5897 at Mie Hospital, 112,276 patients with ILI in Mie Prefecture, and 45,551 COVID-19 patients among the ILI patients in Mie Prefecture. Table 1 presents an outline of the subject characteristics, with 71 % being female, and respondents in their 30s as the most common age group. Fig. 1 presents data of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients, screening positivity rates, and rates among ILI patients, all showing peak values in 2022. Until 2021, no significant correlation was found between screening positivity rates and newly confirmed COVID-19 cases, perhaps because the number of patients had been insufficient to show any association among them. However, in 2022, and particularly later in the year, notably high correlation was found between these variables.

Fig. 2 shows that the gray area located above the line represents a greater than zero prediction for the positive rate among preadmission screening. This line is presented as a convex decreasing line on the plane defined by the number of newly confirmed patients and the positive rate among ILI patients. The white area below the line in Fig. 2 represents the zero prediction of the positive rate among screenings before admission. This finding indicates that screening might be unnecessary under such circumstances because the likelihood of detecting positive cases through screening is minimal. It is noteworthy that Fig. 2 presents point estimations of the results. Although the point estimation near or within the white area in Fig. 2 is indeed zero, that does not account for the uncertainty associated with the estimation. The probability that the positive rate in screening is higher than zero might not be negligible. Therefore, when judging the necessity of screening, considering the upper limit of the 95 % confidence interval is crucially important. By doing so, the borderline should be adjusted downwards. The region below the borderline, considering the upper limit of the 95 % confidence interval, might be more appropriate and might suggest that screening is unnecessary. This possibility highlights the importance of incorporating confidence intervals for better elucidation of the range of uncertainty in estimations and for making better informed decisions about screening requirements.

Discussion

An association has been demonstrated between the positive rate of COVID-19 in a community and the positive rate of preadmission screening for patients who were not suspected of having COVID-19 [11,17]. One earlier study was conducted until May 2020 in the Netherlands, during the very early stage of the pandemic. The PCR positive rate among asymptomatic patients in the region increased linearly when the mean daily COVID-19 hospital admission rate was greater than 1.5 patients per 100,000 residents [11]. By applying this condition, because the Mie Prefecture population was approximately 1.7 million, the number of hospital admissions related to COVID-19 was associated with the positive rate of COVID-19 in Mie Prefecture when the daily hospital admission with COVID-19 was greater than 26 in our study. Because our estimation specification was a cubic polynomial, it increased rapidly in the early phase, with fewer than 20 newly infected individuals up to 18 % of the points in the positive rate, assuming that the positive rate among ILIs was constant. Then, the estimation results were implied to be almost flat until reaching 1500 newly infected individuals, as shown in Fig. 3. If the number of newly infected individuals was greater than 1500, then it increased monotonically up to the 25 % point, when the number of newly infected individuals was 20,000, which corresponded with the maximum number presented in Fig. 1. Therefore, the early stage until reaching 20 newly infected individuals might show results similar to those found in the earlier study, which suggested a 5 percentage point increase in the positive rate when daily hospital admissions per 100,000 residents increased by one. However, this earlier study specifically examined the original strain. By contrast, we were limited to mutated strains such as Alpha, Delta, and Omicron variants, which had prevailed respectively throughout Japan during March–June 2021, during July–December 2021, and from January 2022. There was also difference between this earlier study and ours in terms of the testing strategy and outbreak situation, aside from differences in the outbreak measurement, which was the admission rate in the earlier study and the number of newly confirmed infected persons in the present study.

Another study conducted in Germany demonstrated a linear association between the number of COVID-19 outbreaks in hospitals and COVID-19 cases in the general population [17]. During a period overlapping with our study, from the 15th week of 2021 to the 52 nd week of 2021, an increase of 11 outbreak cases per 100,000 COVID-19 cases was observed in the general population. This finding seems to be consistent with our study findings indicating that the number of COVID-19 outbreaks in hospitals increased when the number of COVID-19 cases in the general population increased. However, healthcare-associated infection outbreaks were caused by infections among COVID-19 patients with false-negative results in preadmission screening or healthcare workers with COVID-19. Therefore, recognizing that the concept of healthcare-associated infection outbreaks might be different from the positive rate of preadmission screening is important.

The IDSA recommendation described above might be comparable with Fig. 2 [14]. Because Mie Prefecture has a population of 1.7 million, “10,000” on the vertical axis in Fig. 2 presents approximately 0.6 % of the daily incidence for the general population. As the figure shows, even for 0.6 % incidence, a gray area with a greater than zero positive rate among screening was expected, irrespective of the positive rate among ILI patients. Therefore, a screening test for COVID-19 would be expected to be necessary even if the daily incidence for the general population were 0.6 %. Alternatively, if the daily incidence per general population were below 0.06 %, then the positive rate in screening would be expected to be zero unless the positive rate among ILI patients were higher than 50 %. Therefore, based on the obtained results, 0.06 % was the criterion to cease the screening test in Japan if the positive rate among ILI patients were less than 50 %. The recommended values of 2 % by IDSA and 0.06 % are markedly different, but these two figures cannot be compared directly. The US and Japan experienced extremely different COVID-19 outbreak situations. For example, during the study period, the number of confirmed COVID-19 cases in the United States was approximately 90 million, whereas in Japan, it was approximately 1.1 million [18]. Furthermore, Japan has universal health coverage, whereas the United States does not. These differences in the epidemiological situation and healthcare system highlight the distinct contexts of the two countries.

Similarly to our study, several earlier studies have also examined the utility of preadmission screening [7,10,[19], [20], [21]]. These include comparisons of the incidences of COVID-19 patients or outbreaks in hospitals before and after discontinuing preadmission screening. However, several studies have neglected investigation of the relation between regional epidemic situations and the number of SARS-CoV-2-positive cases, although they incorporated consideration of the situation prevailing in the area [7,19,20]. Other studies have examined the association between regional epidemic situations and the number of SARS-CoV-2 positive cases. For instance, a study conducted in Pakistan demonstrated that when the regional SARS-CoV-2 positivity rate exceeded 6.6 %, the positive rate of PCR as preprocedural testing among cancer patients also exceeded 1 % [10]. Another study conducted in Austria found that the preadmission screening positivity rate during a low-incidence period (7-day incidence of <50 patients per 100,000 population) was 0.2 %, whereas it increased to 2.3 % during a high-incidence period (7-day incidence of ≥50 patients per 100,000 population) [21]. However, these studies did not specifically investigate the use of regional epidemic situations as indicators for implementing preadmission screening. For our study, we quantified the COVID-19 positive rate using influenza-like illness surveillance and determined the specific number of patients at which the probability of preadmission screening became zero.

It is noteworthy that the variables used for our estimation (the number of newly confirmed patients and the positive rate among ILI cases) might exhibit strong correlation. In scenarios with a considerable rise in confirmed cases, one would expect an increase in the positive rate among ILI cases as well. Although this correlation might introduce multicollinearity issues, our study found significant coefficients across all variables, indicating that, despite the possibility of multicollinearity, it did not strongly affect our estimation. The significant coefficients suggest that our model captured and addressed the relations among variables effectively, even accounting for potential correlation.

At the time of this study, more than three years had already passed since the pandemic emerged. Many residents had been infected naturally and/or with vaccine-derived immunity [[22], [23], [24]]. Consequently, patients with mild symptoms as well as asymptomatic cases will not consult doctors and will never be counted as cases. However, as indicated by sewage water surveillance [25], a high viral load persists in communities and constitutes a hazard to high-risk groups. Considering these circumstances, it might be ideal to show the nosocomial infection risk using a positive rate in acute respiratory infection surveillance so that institutional clusters in hospitals and long-term care facilities can be prevented.

Our study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the subjects were skewed by age group. Because Mie Hospital has numerous pediatric patients, most of the age groups are those of children and their parents. In other words, the selection of study subjects might not represent a random sample of the general population.

Second, the obtained results might not be directly applicable to other hospitals or prefectures because we analyzed data only from Mie Prefecture. Therefore, confirming the validity and generalizability of the findings through further research is crucially important.

Third, in Japan, COVID-19 was classified initially as a disease requiring official notification until May 8, 2023, entailing mandatory reporting to health officials. However, starting from May 9, 2023, COVID-19 was reclassified as a sentinel reported disease: similar to seasonal influenza. As a result, the precise number of confirmed COVID-19 cases has been reported more widely, although it can still be estimated at any time. This change in classification, according to the infection control law, presents implications for the accuracy and certainty surrounding the reported number of COVID-19 cases. Therefore, this change is considered to be extremely important when applying the results of our study to any decision-making related to screening or other measures after May 8, 2023.

Finally, as Fig. 1 shows, no association was found between the positive rate for COVID-19 in screening and the number of newly confirmed COVID-19 patients in 2020. However, it is noteworthy that this finding might not extend to situations in which there are only a few COVID-19 patients. From an earlier study, it was reported that when the prevalence in a region was high, a higher COVID-19 positive rate in preadmission screening was observed. However, low prevalence in a region does not necessarily indicate a lower COVID-19 positive rate; instead, a constant COVID-19 positive rate was observed. Additionally, the availability of data of the positive rate among ILI started from the 40th week of 2021. Consequently, the estimation model is inapplicable to the period which includes 2020 and most of 2021. Therefore, it is crucially important to recognize that the estimation procedure fits well for the Omicron variant. However, the obtained results might not be applicable to the original Wuhan, Alpha, or Delta variants.

Conclusions

Results obtained from this study indicate that consideration of the COVID-19 epidemic situation in a given area can provide valuable insights suggesting the necessity of a policy for conducting screening for COVID-19 before admission. The obtained results and discussion of them the case of COVID-19 pandemic are expected to provide fruitful lessons and knowledge for preadmission clinical screening policy, supporting preparedness of healthcare infrastructure against pandemics of novel infectious diseases.
