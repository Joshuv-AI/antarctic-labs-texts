Genome-wide identification and gene expression analysis of AhNHXs in peanut
Authors: Yishuang Zhou, Qing Xie, Zhanpeng Liang, Tingting Chen, Enyou Feng, Qiaoling Lin, Hanqiao Hu, Yingbin Xue, Xingyu Jiang, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter (NHX) family plays a crucial role in regulating intracellular Na and H homeostasis in plants. This study conducted a genome-wide analysis and expression assessment of peanut NHXs under different stress conditions.ResultsFourteen AhNHXs were identified based on the Na⁺/H⁺ exchange domain, and all contained the amiloride-binding site that inhibits Na+ transport in plants. Phylogenetic analysis grouped these genes into three subfamilies. Cis-regulatory element analysis identified elements associated with hormone signaling, stress responses, and tissue-specific expression. qRT-PCR results indicated their roles in salt stress, selenium stress, and tolerance to metal ion toxicity such as cadmium, manganese, and aluminum. For instance, AhNHX4, AhNHX5, and AhNHX7 were upregulated in roots under salt, cadmium, and aluminum treatments, whereas AhNHX7 was downregulated in roots under selenium and manganese stress and in leaves under all stress conditions.ConclusionsThis study examined the evolutionary, structural, and functional characteristics of the AhNHX family and highlighted their roles in multiple stress responses in peanut. These findings provide insight into potential applications for improving crop tolerance to abiotic stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08590-y.

Introduction

The Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter protein (NHX) belongs to the CPA1 type of the CPA (cation/proton antiporter) superfamily [1]. It was first identified in eukaryotes in humans and Arabidopsis thaliana [2]. These proteins participate in maintaining cellular ion homeostasis, vesicle transport, plant growth and development, salt tolerance, and cellular pH regulation [3]. NHX proteins typically contain 10–12 transmembrane helices (TMHs) [4] and localize to different compartments such as vacuoles, endosomes, and plasma membranes [5]. In Arabidopsis, eight NHX genes are categorized into plasma membrane, vacuole, and endosome types based on subcellular localization [6], which reflects their different roles in preserving ion homeostasis under specific environmental conditions [3]. NHX proteins share conserved structural features, including the Na⁺/H⁺ exchange domain (PF00999) and a characteristic amiloride-binding site at the N-terminus ((L/F)FF(I/L)(Y/F)LLPPI) [2, 7]. This site is sensitive to amiloride and inhibits sodium transport [8]. It is also a typical hallmark of NHX proteins in various plants such as A. thaliana, Gossypium hirsutum, and Populus alba [9].

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea) is a major oil and food crop, with a global planting area of 26.64 million hectares [10]. It is cultivated mainly in Asia, Africa, and the Americas [11]. China is the largest producer and consumer of peanut worldwide. Peanut provides an important source of edible oil and is rich in protein, vitamins, and minerals, which contribute to high nutritional and economic value [12]. Legume crops, due to broad environmental adaptability, nitrogen-fixing capacity, and nutritional value, contribute significantly to sustainable development goals [13].

However, peanut production is restricted by abiotic stresses such as salt stress [14]. Soil salinization interferes with seed germination, inhibits seedling growth, and affects physiological processes such as photosynthesis [15]. More than one billion hectares of farmland are affected by salinization, and 20% of irrigated land experiences salt damage [16], causing annual economic losses exceeding 27 billion US dollars [17]. Salt stress leads to excessive Na⁺ accumulation, depletion of essential elements such as K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺, and disruption of chloroplast ultrastructure, which includes deformation of thylakoid membranes and disintegration of grana stacks [18]. It also induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, causing oxidative damage, reduced seed germination, stunted growth, and lower yields [19]. Plants have evolved ion-regulation strategies to cope with salt stress [20]. Ion transport proteins such as HKT1, NHX, and SOS1 contribute to maintaining ion homeostasis [21]. Plants maintain low cellular sodium through two main mechanisms: sodium exclusion and sodium compartmentalization. The membrane-localized NHX protein (such as SOS1/AtNHX7) is activated through the SOS signaling pathway and exports Na⁺ out of cells [22]. The vacuole-localized NHX protein, such as AtNHX1, compartmentalizes Na⁺ into vacuoles and reduces cytoplasmic Na⁺ concentration [23]. Numerous gene sequences encoding salt-tolerance proteins that confer salt tolerance have been identified, and functional studies of the NHX family are extensive [24]. In Solanum lycopersicum, NHX overexpression enhances salt tolerance by reducing Na⁺ accumulation and increasing potassium absorption [25]. In A. thaliana, the membrane-localized NHX1 is regulated by the salt-inducible transcription factor WRKY25 and improves salt tolerance [26]. NHX5 contributes to Na⁺ compartmentalization in vacuoles and reduces sodium toxicity [27]. Overexpression of NHX genes enhances salt tolerance in various species. TaNHX2 from Triticum aestivum improves salt tolerance in Medicago sativa by maintaining potassium homeostasis [28], whereas the nhx5/nhx6 double knockout mutant of A. thaliana shows salt-sensitive phenotypes due to defects in vacuolar transport [29]. These studies indicate that the NHX family regulates salt tolerance by modulating ion homeostasis and enhancing Na⁺ compartmentalization, providing valuable genetic resources for generating salt tolerant crops [9].

Apart from salt stress, elements such as selenium, cadmium, manganese, and aluminum significantly impact plant growth. Selenium exhibits dose-dependent effects [30]. Low doses enhance antioxidant capacity and induce defense mechanisms [31], while high doses disrupt photosynthetic pigment formation and inhibit nutrient absorption [32], leading to leaf chlorosis and inhibited root growth [33]. Cadmium, a highly toxic industrial pollutant [34], inhibits enzyme activity, disrupts photosynthesis and respiration [35], and suppresses root growth [36]. Manganese is an essential trace element [37], but excessive manganese reduces growth by ion antagonism, lowering Ca and Fe content [38]. Aluminum toxicity is a major limiting factor in acidic soils [39], significantly inhibiting root development and reducing crop yield [40]. Studies have shown that NHX genes play regulatory roles under cadmium and aluminum stress. For example, overexpression of SsNHX1 and SeNHX1 enhances cadmium tolerance and maintains ion balance [41]. MdNHX2 regulates H⁺ transport to support vacuolar sequestration of aluminum ions [42]. However, the functional mechanisms of NHX under manganese and selenium stress remain under-researched [43].

Although NHX antiporters play important roles in plants, no systematic analysis of the NHX family in peanut has been reported. In this study, fourteen members of the NHX family were identified in peanut. Their structures, phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs, chromosome localization, collinearity, cis-regulatory elements, predicted 3D structures, and interaction networks were examined. Expression profiles under stress conditions were assessed using real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). These findings provide resources for future studies on the roles of AhNHX proteins in regulating stress tolerance in peanut.

Materials and methods

Identification of NHX proteins in peanuts

The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) were performed in TBtools II (v2.351) [44] (accessed on 15 November 2025) using the configuration file downloaded from Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) (accessed on 15 November 2025) with an E-value cutoff of ≤ 0.01 to search the peanut proteome for Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger family proteins (Pfam ID: PF00999). For further refinement, protein sequences of NHX from A. thaliana, protein sequences of NHX from A. thaliana, Oryza sativa, and Medicago truncatula were used as query sequences. These sequences were searched against the peanut genome available in the Ensembl Genomes Release 61 (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/ensemblgenomes/pub/release-61/plants/fasta/arachis_hypogaea/pep/) (accessed on 15 November 2025) using BLASTP. All NHX candidate homologous protein sequences were selected when they met the search criteria, which included an e-value threshold of 1e-10 and a bit score higher than 100. Redundant sequences were removed based on gene IDs and sequence similarity. Sequences with identical chromosomal locations or 100% identical amino acid sequences were considered redundant and discarded. In addition, proteins with amino acid lengths outside the range of 450–600 residues or lacking the Na⁺/H⁺exchanger domain were also excluded.

Characteristics of NHX proteins in peanuts

Chromosomal localization was analyzed using Tbtools-II. Coding sequence (CDS) length and protein length were obtained from Ensembl Plants Release 61 (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html/) (accessed on 16 November 2025). The isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), and average hydrophobicity (GRAVY) of each AhNHX protein were predicted using ExPASy ProtParam 3.0 (http://expasy.org/protparam/) (accessed on 18 November 2025). Transmembrane helices were predicted using Protter 1.0 (http://wlab.ethz.ch/protter/start/) (accessed on 18 November 2025). Multiple sequence alignments were produced using DNAMAN 10.0 (accessed on 18 November 2025) [45]. Conserved acidic residues were examined using Jalview 2.11 (accessed on 18 November 2025) [46]. Secondary structure was predicted using SOPMA (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) (v3.4.0) (accessed on 19 November 2025) [47]. Subcellular localization was predicted using Plant-mPLoc 2.0 (accessed on 18 November 2025) (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/) [48].

Structural analysis of peanut NHX

GFF file of the peanut genome from Ensembl Genomes Release 61 (Index of /ensemblgenomes/pub/release-61/plants/gff3/arachis_hypogaea) (accessed on 19 November 2025), gene structure analysis of the AhNHX family was conducted using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) 2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) (accessed on 19 November 2025) [49]. Tbtools-II was used to visualize the predicted structures.

Construction of the NHX phylogenetic tree

IQ-TREE (v3.0.1) (accessed on 20 November 2025) was used with default parameters to predict the best substitution models and construct maximum-likelihood gene trees with 1000 bootstraps [50]. Peanut NHX members were classified into subfamilies based on homologous sequences from A. thaliana [51], O. sativa [1], M. truncatula [52], and T. aestivum [3]. All NHX protein sequences used in the analysis are listed in Supplementary File S1. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using Evolview (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/#/login) v3 (accessed on 21 November 2025) [53].

Analysis of conserved motifs in AhNHXs

Conserved motifs were identified using the MEME Suite 5.5.9 (http://meme-suite.org/) (accessed on 22 November 2025) with default settings [54]. Parameters included 2–20 motif sites, extraction of up to 10 motifs, and motif width between 6 and 50. Structural prediction was conducted using the NCBI CDD Batch tool (v3.21) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) (accessed on 22 November 2025) [55]. Tbtools-II was used to visualize motif compositions of peanut Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters.

Chromosome localization and co-linearity analysis of the peanut gene family

Based on genomic annotations from Ensembl Plants, the chromosomal positions of the 14 AhNHX genes were determined. Tbtools-II was used for chromosome mapping.

To gain insight into the evolution of AhNHXs, peanut genome sequences were compared with those of A. thaliana and M. truncatula. Co-linearity relationships were identified, and Tbtools-II was used to generate the co-linearity map. Synonymous substitution rates (Ks) and non-synonymous substitution rates (Ka) were calculated for each duplicated gene pair. Genomic and annotation data were obtained from Ensembl Plants.

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements

The 2 kb upstream promoter regions of AhNHXs were submitted to PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) (accessed on 25 November 2025) for cis-regulatory element prediction [56]. Tbtools-II was used to visualize and simplify the results.

Protein interaction network and protein 3D structure analysis

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for peanut proteins was inferred based on Arabidopsis homologs. Homologous relationships were identified using the STRING database (v12.0) (https://string-db.org/) (accessed on 26 November 2025) [57], with a screening criterion of sequence identity greater than 65% and an E-value cutoff less than 1e− 5. Predicted interactions were further filtered based on STRING confidence scores, with high confidence threshold of 0.700 applied. Three-dimensional (3D) structures of the 14 AhNHX proteins were estimated using I-TASSER (https://zhanglab.ccmb.med.umich.edu/I-TASSER/) (accessed on 26 November 2025) [58]. Modeling was performed using PyMOL (v3.1.6.1) (accessed on 28 November 2025).

Expression analysis of the AhNHX genes in different tissues

Transcriptome data for a total of 22 peanut tissues, including vegetative organs such as roots and leaves and reproductive organs such as flowers, gynophores, fruits, and seeds, were obtained from the PeanutBase database (accessed on 27 November 2025) (https://dev.peanutbase.org/expression/expr_tissue_Hyp.html) to evaluate AhNHX expression patterns [59]. These data were then used to generate a heatmap using TBtools-II for visualization.

Sample collection and processing

The peanut variety used was Zhanyou 75, developed by the Agricultural Science Research Institute of Zhanjiang City, Guangdong Province. Plants were grown in the experimental field of Guangdong Ocean University (longitude 110.30311, latitude 21.15005). Seeds of similar size and fullness were selected and germinated in white quartz sand for 10 days. Seedlings with uniform growth were transferred to 15-liter plastic boxes for hydroponic culture with nutrient solution [60]. Nutrient components are listed in Table S2. All chemical reagents were of analytical grade (Kermel, Tianjin, China). The concentrations of stress treatments used in this study were selected based on previous literature and were adjusted to effectively induce physiological and molecular responses in the plants while minimizing lethal effects. These concentrations have been shown to cause measurable stress responses in plants without causing excessive damage, those stress treatments included 50 mM NaCl as described by Khatri and Rathore [61], 0.015 mM selenious acid as described by Cunha et al. [62], 0.02 mM cadmium chloride as described by Wang et al. [63], 0.01 mM and 0.3 mM manganese sulfate as described by Liu et al. [64], and 4 mM aluminum sulfate octahydrate as described by Shi et al. [65]. Three replicates were included for each concentration. Cultivation conditions were: 25–30 °C during daytime, 18–22 °C at night, a 12 h light cycle, 2000 lx light intensity, and nutrient solution replacement every 5 days. After 15 days of treatment, peanut leaves (fourth leaf from the base) and roots were collected.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Leaves and roots subjected to salt, selenium, cadmium, manganese, and aluminum treatments were used for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the MolPure Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Yisheng, China). cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair II First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yisheng, China). qRT-PCR was performed using Hifair UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Premix (Yisheng, China) and the ABI7500 system (Applied Biosystems, USA) [66]. The 10 µL reaction system contained 0.25 µL forward primer, 0.25 µL reverse primer, 4 µL nuclease-free water, 0.5 µL cDNA template, and 5 µL Hifair UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Premix (Yisheng, China). The qRT-PCR cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 10 min; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min [67]. AhUbiquitin (DQ887087.1) was used as the reference gene. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [68]. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel 2016 and IBM SPSS Statistics 27. The values were the means ± standard deviations (SD) of three independent biological replicates. Independent sample t-test was used to assess the difference between control groups and treatment groups (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 *** p < 0.001).

Results

Identification and molecular characteristics of AhNHX proteins

Based on chromosomal localization, all 14 AhNHX proteins were named AhNHX1 to AhNHX14 (Table 1). The amiloride-binding site was conserved in the TMH3 region of AhNHX1–AhNHX14 (Fig. 1), which is a typical feature of NHX transport proteins. The putative cation-binding domain was conserved in the TMH5 and TMH6 regions of AhNHX1–AhNHX14 (Fig. 1). Sequence alignments showed that all AhNHXs contained conserved acidic residues essential for Na⁺/H⁺ transport. These included D153, E176, and D181 in AhNHX1 and AhNHX8; D155, E178, and D183 in AhNHX2; D172, E196, and D201 in AhNHX3 and AhNHX9; D163, E186, and D191 in AhNHX4; D166, E190, and D195 in AhNHX5; D190, E213, and D218 in AhNHX6; D160, E183, and D188 in AhNHX7, AhNHX10, and AhNHX14; D162, E185, and D190 in AhNHX11; D166, E190, and D195 in AhNHX12; and D161, E184, and D189 in AhNHX13. The number of predicted transmembrane helices (TMHs) ranged from 10 to 13 (Table 1, Figure S1). AhNHX3, AhNHX8, AhNHX10, AhNHX11, and AhNHX14 contained 13 TMHs. AhNHX2, AhNHX4, AhNHX6, AhNHX9, and AhNHX12 contained 12 TMHs. AhNHX1, AhNHX5, and AhNHX7 contained 11 TMHs, while AhNHX13 contained 10 TMHs.

A total of 14 AhNHXs were identified as transmembrane transport proteins (Table 1). Three genes were located on chromosomes 1 and 11, and the remaining genes were distributed across chromosomes 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 14, 16, and 19. The average CDS length was approximately 1630 bp. AhNHX6 had the longest CDS at 1746 bp and encoded a protein of 581 amino acids. The molecular weight of the AhNHX proteins ranged from 58.14 to 64.42 kDa. The isoelectric point ranged from 5.41 to 9.22, and the hydrophobicity index ranged from 0.409 to 0.603. TMH predictions confirmed the presence of multiple transmembrane regions (Table 1), consistent with the properties of membrane-localized ion transport proteins.

Secondary structure prediction indicated that all AhNHX proteins contained α-helices, β-turns, extended strands, and random coils. Subcellular localization prediction results showed that all 14 AhNHX proteins localized to the vacuole (Table S5).

Structural analysis of AhNHXs

Gene structure analysis showed that the AhNHX family genes ranged from 4 to 7 kb in length and all contained more than twelve exons (Fig. 2A). Domain prediction confirmed that all genes possessed the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger domain (Fig. 2B).

Evolutionary tree analysis of NHXs

A phylogenetic tree was constructed for 65 NHX proteins from A. thaliana, O. sativa, M. truncatula, Triticum aestivum, and A. hypogaea. Based on multiple sequence alignment and established classification in A. thaliana, NHX proteins were grouped into three subfamilies: Vac, Endo, and PM. The PM subfamily contained 13 proteins, the Endo subfamily contained 18 proteins, and the Vac subfamily contained 34 proteins. Peanut NHX proteins were distributed only in the Vac and Endo subfamilies (Fig. 3).

Chromosome localization and collinearity analysis of AhNHXs

Chromosomal mapping revealed that several AhNHX genes were located in gene-rich regions (red and yellow), whereas AhNHX7, AhNHX8, AhNHX9, AhNHX10, AhNHX12, and AhNHX14 were located in gene-dense regions (blue) (Figure S2). Three genes were located on chromosomes 1 and 11, and one gene was located on each of chromosomes 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 14, 16, and 19 (Figure S2, Fig. 5B). Six genes mapped to the 90–150 Mb region, six mapped to the 0–30 Mb region (AhNHX1, AhNHX5, AhNHX6, AhNHX8, AhNHX12, AhNHX13), and two were located between 30 and 60 Mb (AhNHX7 and AhNHX14) (Figure S3).

Six collinear gene pairs were identified between peanut and A. thaliana, and 15 pairs between peanut and M. truncatula (Fig. 5A). Fifteen collinear regions were identified within the AhNHX family (Fig. 5A). Fourteen AhNHXs were distributed across ten peanut chromosomes, whereas no AhNHXs were detected on the other ten chromosomes (Fig. 5B).

Fifteen segmental duplication events were identified (Fig. 5B, Table S6). The Ka/Ks ratios ranged from 0.0520 to 0.2471 (Table S6). The divergence time of AhNHX gene pairs was calculated using the equation T = Ks / (2r). The r represents the neutral substitution rate (r = 8.12 × 10− 9 Ks yr− 1) (Table S6) [69].

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements in the promoter region of AhNHXs

Cis-regulatory element prediction identified regulatory elements associated with light responses, hormone responses, stress responses, and growth and development (Fig. 6). All AhNHXs contained hormone-responsive elements, although the composition varied among genes. The promoter regions contained 46 light-responsive elements, 42 methyl jasmonate-responsive elements, 30 abscisic acid-responsive elements, and 20 anaerobic induction elements. Additional elements included 12 MYB-binding sites, 9 MYBHv1 transcription factor binding sites, and smaller sets of salicylic acid-responsive elements, low-temperature-responsive elements, defense and stress response elements, auxin-responsive elements, gibberellin-responsive elements, maize albumin regulatory elements, meristem expression elements, endosperm expression elements, and palisade mesophyll cell differentiation elements (Fig. 6).

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) prediction

AhNHX2–AhNHX7 and AhNHX9–AhNHX14 were predicted to share several putative interacting partners, including Salt Overly Responsive 1 (SOS1), SOS2, SOS3, High-Affinity Potassium Transporter 1 (HKT1;1), and High-Affinity Potassium Transporter 5 (HAK5) (Fig. 7). AhNHX2–AhNHX7 and AhNHX9–AhNHX14 were predicted to share several putative interacting partners, including SOS1, SOS2, SOS3, HKT1;1 and HAK5 (Fig. 7). In addition, NHX2 (AhNHX2, AhNHX4, AhNHX10, AhNHX11) was predicted to interact with NHX6 (AhNHX3, AhNHX5, AhNHX9, AhNHX12), CBL9, and AKT1; NHX1 (AhNHX6, AhNHX7, AhNHX13, AhNHX14) was predicted to interact with NHX6, AKT1, CBL9, and CBL1; and NHX6 was predicted to interact with NHX1 and NHX2.

Prediction of protein 3D structure

Modeling predicted that AhNHX11 had the highest C-score at 0.48, whereas AhNHX6 had the lowest score at − 0.63 (Table S7). The length of the NHX domain ranged from 441 amino acids in AhNHX6 to 400 amino acids in AhNHX5 and AhNHX12 (Table S6).

As shown in Fig. 8, the predicted spatial structure of AhNHX1–AhNHX14 generally exhibited a typical funnel-shaped conformation (Fig. 8A). Conserved acidic residues of AhNHX1–AhNHX14 were predicted to be located within the protein core (Fig. 8B).

Expression patterns of peanut NHXs in different tissues and under different abiotic stresses

As shown in Figure S4, the Endo subfamily members AhNHX3 and AhNHX9 were expressed in all tested tissues. The homologous pair AhNHX5 and AhNHX12 showed very low expression levels under the examined conditions (Figure S4). Within the Vac subfamily, AhNHX2 and AhNHX10 were predominantly expressed in seeds (Figure S4). AhNHX7 and AhNHX14 exhibited low expression across tissues (Figure S4).

Based on their phylogenetic relationships and predicted predicted protein–protein interactions, 12 AhNHXs were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. Under salt stress, three genes (AhNHX4, AhNHX10, AhNHX13) were significantly upregulated, whereas four genes (AhNHX5, AhNHX7, AhNHX12, AhNHX14) were significantly downregulated in leaves. In roots, six genes (AhNHX2, AhNHX4, AhNHX5, AhNHX7, AhNHX10, AhNHX13) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 9A). Under selenium stress, four genes (AhNHX3, AhNHX10, AhNHX13, AhNHX14) were significantly upregulated, and five genes (AhNHX2, AhNHX4, AhNHX5, AhNHX7, AhNHX9) were significantly downregulated in leaves. Except for AhNHX6 and AhNHX11, which did not change significantly, the remaining ten genes were significantly downregulated in roots (Fig. 9B). Under cadmium toxicity, nine genes were significantly downregulated in leaves, except AhNHX5, AhNHX11, and AhNHX12, which showed no significant changes. Seven genes (AhNHX4, AhNHX5, AhNHX6, AhNHX7, AhNHX11, AhNHX12, AhNHX13) were significantly upregulated in roots, whereas AhNHX2, AhNHX3, and AhNHX14 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 9C). Under manganese toxicity, three genes (AhNHX11, AhNHX12, AhNHX13) were significantly upregulated, while five genes (AhNHX2, AhNHX5, AhNHX7, AhNHX10, AhNHX14) were significantly downregulated in leaves. In roots, only AhNHX9 was significantly upregulated. Five genes (AhNHX4, AhNHX5, AhNHX7, AhNHX10, AhNHX14) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 9D). Under aluminum stress, two genes (AhNHX5, AhNHX14) were significantly upregulated in leaves, and six genes (AhNHX2, AhNHX3, AhNHX6, AhNHX7, AhNHX11, AhNHX12) were significantly downregulated. In roots, seven genes (AhNHX2, AhNHX4, AhNHX5, AhNHX7, AhNHX10, AhNHX11, AhNHX12) were significantly upregulated, whereas three genes (AhNHX9, AhNHX13, AhNHX14) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 9E).

Discussion

The Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter protein belongs to the NHX gene family and is widely present in many organisms. Candidate NHX genes have been reported in several plant species [3]. Triticum aestivum contains 30 members [3], while Gossypium hirsutum, G. arboreum, and G. raimondii have 23, 12, and 12 respectively [70]. Zea mays contains 11 [71], Punica granatum has 10 [4], Glycine max and Avicennia marina each have 9 [72, 73], A. thaliana has 8 [51], O. sativa and Solanum lycopersicum each have 7 [1, 24], and M. truncatula has 6 [52]. NHX proteins are likely to be central regulators of ion balance in plant cells [74]. The NHX gene family may play an important role in plant tolerance to abiotic stresses [74–76]. Vacuolar NHX genes, such as AtNHX1 in Arabidopsis [74], OsNHX1 in rice [75], and TaNHX1 in wheat [76], likely enhance plant tolerance to salinity and other stresses by transporting Na⁺ into vacuoles and thereby reducing cytoplasmic Na⁺ concentrations. In contrast, endosomal NHX genes, such as AtNHX5 in Arabidopsis [77] and OsNHX5 in rice [78], are mainly localized to endosomes and other endomembrane systems, where they may play key roles in regulating ion balance within endosomes and mediating intracellular stress responses.

Evolutionary conservatism describes the persistence of specific characteristics with minimal modification over long evolutionary periods. All AhNHXs contained a conserved sodium–hydrogen exchanger domain (Fig. 2B). Physicochemical analysis showed that each AhNHX was hydrophobic because all GRAVY values were above zero (Table 1). This result is consistent with the reported results [79]. To place the peanut NHX family in a broader evolutionary context, gene numbers, conserved motifs, and duplication patterns across legumes and related crops were compared. Peanut contains fourteen AhNHX genes, which is more than the seven NHX genes in common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris) [19] but fewer than the thirty-five NHX genes in alfalfa (Medicago sativa) [80] and the thirty NHX genes in wheat [3]. These differences in gene number likely reflect variations in genome size and the history of polyploidization in each species. Despite differences in gene copy number, all four species shared a highly conserved motif architecture, with ten core motifs identified in their NHX proteins (Fig. 4A), indicating that the structural framework and essential transport functions of NHX proteins might be evolutionarily constrained. Segmental duplication appeared to have been the main driver of NHX family expansion in peanut, with fifteen duplication events identified (Fig. 5B), whereas only three such events were observed in common bean. In contrast, alfalfa and wheat showed lineage-specific expansion patterns, with twenty-five and nine duplication events, respectively. These findings suggest that gene duplication dynamics may have shaped the diversification of NHX members in a species-specific manner. To investigate the evolutionary relationships of NHX proteins, 14 peanut NHXs and 51 NHXs from four plant species were included in a phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 3). The dataset comprised three dicotyledonous and two monocotyledonous plants. NHX proteins formed three groups: Vac, Endo, and PM. The Vac subfamily contained the most AhNHX members, which was similar to observations in sugar beet (Beta vulgaris) [81] and pumpkin (Cucurbita) [82]. Gene structure analysis supported these groupings (Fig. 3A). Members within the same subgroup had similar exon–intron compositions, independent of intron length, whereas different subgroups showed clear structural variation. In peanut, the Endo subfamily contained 20 or 21 exons, which was higher than the Vac subfamily and consistent with sugar beet [81]. These findings indicate structural variation across subfamilies and suggest functional diversity. Comparison of Vac genes showed that the fourth exon of AhNHX6 could be divided into two shorter exons, which increased exon number in AhNHX13 (Fig. 2A). A similar pattern was reported in Actinidia chinensis [2]. Additionally, Endo genes had more introns and a fixed number of two exons, while Vac genes exhibit variable exon numbers (0–3) (Fig. 2A). Compared to Vac genes, Endo genes showed lower Ka/Ks ratios and earlier divergence times. These findings further highlight that Endo genes may be more evolutionarily conserved, whereas Vac genes are likely to reflect a more complex regulatory mechanism and functional diversification.

This study identified ten conserved motifs in AhNHXs (Fig. 4A). The conserved transmembrane pore and cation-binding domain, recognized as the amiloride-binding site, is a central structural feature of membrane-bound NHX transporters [9]. This site inhibits cation/H⁺ exchange in the presence of amiloride [81]. As a characteristic feature of NHX proteins, the amiloride binding site was completely conserved in all AhNHX proteins, shedding light on the evolutionary preservation of specific functional amino acids within the NHX protein family. This result is consistent with that observed in M. truncatula [80] and common bean [83]. Motifs 1–7 and 9–10 were universal and present in all AhNHX genes (Fig. 4A). The results indicate that only the vacuolar membrane-associated AhNHX proteins contain motif 8, suggesting that this specific motif may play a crucial role in their function. These patterns were consistent with findings in Populus trichocarpa PtNHX [84] and P. granatum PgNHX [4], indicating evolutionary conservation. These observations enhance our understanding of conserved features in AhNHXs and support the evolutionary relationships inferred from gene structure analysis.

Gene duplication at the levels of individual genes, chromosomes, or whole genomes is a major driver of plant genome evolution [85]. Variation in NHX gene number among species reflects such duplication and loss events. Co-linearity analysis can reveal evolutionary relationships based on conserved gene arrangements [82]. In this study, AhNHXs displayed symmetrical co-linearity (Fig. 5B), which suggested that peanut NHXs experienced a whole-genome duplication event. Whole-genome duplication (WGD) provided the initial genome-wide gene copies after polyploidization, while segmental duplications might have further facilitated the expansion and retention of specific NHX genes[86]. Therefore, the observed segmental duplications likely reflect the combined influence of ancient whole-genome duplication events and subsequent local duplication events, which have shaped the current structure of the peanut NHX gene family. Comparative co-linearity between peanut and two other species showed that the most closely related homologs were in M. truncatula (Fig. 5A). These extensive co-linearity relationships support a close evolutionary association among these species [86]. The Ka/Ks ratios for all paralogous pairs were below one, indicating strong purifying selection on the AhNHX family (Table S6) [87].

Cis-acting regulatory elements function as transcriptional regulators and participate in hormone responses, abiotic stress adaptation, and development [88]. Previous studies have shown that NHX genes are regulated by plant hormones and can be induced by abiotic stress [4]. The promoter regions of AhNHX genes contained ABRE (abscisic acid response) elements and MeJA (methyl jasmonate) -responsive elements (Fig. 6), indicating their participation in hormone signaling during peanut development and stress responses [89]. Further analysis revealed that genes with stress-related cis-elements, such as ABRE and MeJA motifs (CGTCA, TGACG), were upregulated under salt stress, pointing to ABA and MeJA-mediated pathways in salt adaptation. Genes containing TC-rich repeats and TCA-elements were upregulated in roots under cadmium and aluminum stress, suggesting SA and defense-related signaling might be involved. MBS elements in certain promoters correlated with stress-responsive transcriptional changes, likely regulated via MYB pathways. Similar cis-elements in Cicer arietinum [25] and Actinidia deliciosa [2] NHX genes suggest conserved regulatory mechanisms across species. These findings may offer insights into the regulatory networks linking hormone signaling, stress responses, and development in peanuts.

In the protein interaction network, most AhNHXs shared the same putative interacting proteins AVP1, HKT1, SOS1, SOS2, and SOS3 (Fig. 7), indicating that they might cooperatively regulate Na⁺ efflux and K⁺ uptake. NHX1 also interacted with CBL9 and AKT1, suggesting that it might be involved in regulating the calcium signaling pathway and worked together with potassium channels to maintain cellular ion homeostasis. NHX1 and NHX2 were also predicted to interact with CBL9 and AKT1, further supporting their potential role in coordinating calcium signaling and potassium channel-mediated ion balance. Both NHX1 and NHX2 belonged to the vacuolar membrane subfamily, and their interaction might suggest potential functional redundancy and cooperation in regulating Na⁺ transport. Members of the endosomal subfamily, such as NHX6, were mainly involved in Na⁺ sequestration and transport and, through cooperation with vacuolar membrane subfamily members, likely enhanced Na⁺ efflux efficiency, thereby enhancing the plant’s tolerance to stress. These predicted interacting proteins have important roles in abiotic stress responses. In order to investigate the functional mechanism of AhNHX proteins, a structural analysis of their 3D models was performed. Using the most suitable structural templates and crystal structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), the possible predicted models for the eight AhNHX proteins were generated. The confidence of these models was assessed using the C-score, which typically ranges from − 5 to 2, with higher values reflecting greater confidence in the accuracy of the model. The predicted AhNHX models in the present study showed high reliability, with C-scores between − 0.63 (AhNHX6) and 0.48 (AhNHX11) (Table S7). The 3D structure prediction suggested that AhNHX1–AhNHX14 might share similar ion exchange mechanisms (Fig. 8). As tandem repeats, the Endo subfamily members AhNHX3 and AhNHX9 showed consistent tissue expression profiles (Figure S4), indicating their involvement in endosomal ion transport. In contrast, the Vac subfamily members AhNHX7 and AhNHX14 exhibited consistently low expression across different tissues, which might point to their limited constitutive activity and potential regulation under specific conditions. Moreover, several AhNHX genes displayed different transcriptional levels among tissues, indicating tissue specificity and suggesting that these genes might have different functional roles. This pattern was similar to findings in Brassica napus, B. rapa, and B. oleracea [90]. The variation in physical characteristics among peanut NHXs further highlights the need to understand genetic diversity within this family to clarify their biological functions.

The qRT-PCR results showed significant expression differences in AhNHXs under multiple stress conditions, indicating that these genes were likely involved in stress responses. Among them, AhNHX7 as a general stress-responsive gene, was induced under all stress conditions, suggesting a broad stress-responsive role. However, there was no single gene as a stress-specific responder found to respond specifically to individual treatment. AhNHX4, AhNHX5, and AhNHX7 were upregulated in roots under salt, cadmium, and aluminum stress, whereas AhNHX7 was downregulated in roots under selenium and manganese stress and in leaves under all stress treatments (Fig. 9). For the same gene in the same tissue, such as AhNHX14 in leaves, expression was downregulated under salt, cadmium, and manganese stress and upregulated under selenium and aluminum stress. This tendency was similar to observations in Solanum tuberosum and Sorghum bicolor [5, 91] (Fig. 9). Several genes also exhibited opposite transcriptional responses in roots and leaves under the same stress. These included AhNHX5 and AhNHX7 under salt stress; AhNHX3, AhNHX10, AhNHX13, and AhNHX14 under selenium stress; AhNHX4, AhNHX6, AhNHX7, and AhNHX13 under cadmium stress; and AhNHX2, AhNHX7, AhNHX11, AhNHX12, and AhNHX14 under aluminum stress (Fig. 9). A similar pattern was reported in Brassica oleracea [92]. AhNHX3, AhNHX6, AhNHX9, and AhNHX11 showed no significant change under salt stress (Fig. 9A), suggesting that ATPase activity may provide energy for Na⁺/H⁺ antiport function to maintain Na⁺ balance [93].

In salt-tolerant plants such as Leptochloa fusca and Suaeda salsa, cadmium stress can regulate NHX1 expression [94, 95]. One possible explanation is that cadmium interferes with ion homeostasis, particularly Na⁺ balance, which may trigger compensatory mechanisms mediated by NHXs. Introduction of HtNHX1 or HtNHX2 from Helianthus tuberosus into rice improved tolerance to aluminum toxicity and soil acidity [], supporting the functional importance of NHX genes in aluminum stress[96, 97]. Although NHX proteins participate in cadmium and aluminum responses, studies addressing their role in manganese and selenium stress remain limited. This study showed that many AhNHX genes displayed significant transcriptional changes under both stresses, suggesting that NHXs contribute to manganese and selenium responses.

To date, no systematic study has characterized the qRT-PCR expression of the NHX family under selenium (Se), cadmium (Cd), manganese (Mn), and aluminum (Al) stress. Our findings demonstrate that AhNHXs exhibit different expression patterns across these five stresses, and many genes were strongly regulated. Based on the results of this study, a hypothetical model was constructed to describe the potential role of AhNHXs in regulating multiple stress responses in peanut (Figure S5). The results suggested that AhNHX genes might play roles in responses to multiple abiotic stresses. However, because qRT-PCR was conducted at a single late time point, dynamic transcriptional changes might not have been fully captured. In addition, independent t-tests were applied as an initial exploratory analysis to identify potential differences. Therefore, future studies incorporating time-course experiments and statistical approaches such as multiple comparison corrections will be necessary to validate these findings. These genes represent candidates for promising further functional research. Overall, this study provides new insights into the diversity and evolution of the NHX family in peanut and offers preliminary theoretical support for advancing the development of crop varieties with tolerance to multiple different abiotic stresses.

Conclusion

This study employed multiple analytical approaches to identify 14 AhNHX genes in the peanut genome. The physicochemical characteristics, gene structures, phylogenetic relationships, and expression patterns of these genes were confirmed. Phylogenetic analysis grouped the AhNHXs into three categories, and AhNHX members were present only in the first and second evolutionary groups. This pattern suggests that peanut NHXs may have differentiated biological functions compared with NHXs in other species. The conserved motifs and gene structures of AhNHXs showed strong conservation, indicating evolutionary stability in their functional domains. Protein interaction network analysis and 3D structure prediction further supported the important roles of AhNHXs in ion balance and transmembrane transport. The qRT-PCR results demonstrated that AhNHXs responded actively to multiple abiotic stresses, providing a basis for future studies on their molecular mechanisms.
