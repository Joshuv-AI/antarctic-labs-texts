Foliar spraying with amino acids and their chitosan nanocomposites as promising way to alleviate abiotic stress in iceberg lettuce grown at different temperatures
Authors: Andrzej Kalisz, Andrzej Kornaś, Joanna Gil, Elżbieta Rudolphi-Szydło, Katarzyna Gawrońska, Apolonia Sieprawska, Hessam Jafari, Gholam Reza Mahdavinia, Muhittin Kulak, Gholamreza Gohari, Vasileios Fotopoulos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe analyzed the effects of foliar spraying with amino acids, chitosan (CHS) and nanocomposites (NCs) of chitosan with the amino acids proline, l-cysteine and glycine betaine (CHS-Pro NCs; CHS-Cys NCs, CHS-GB NCs, respectively) on the changes in the physiological and biochemical parameters of iceberg lettuce grown at the control temperature (20 °C) and under chilling conditions (4 °C). The physicochemical parameters of the phospholipid monolayers (PLs) extracted from plants showed the effects of the treatments on the properties of the monolayers, namely, the packing density and flexibility. We observed increased accumulation of proline at 4 °C, and differences in the concentrations of sugars in most of the analyzed variants were a consequence of the lowered temperature and/or the use of organic compounds. A temperature of 4 °C caused a significant increase in the l-ascorbic acid level compared with that at 20 °C. Differences were also found in glutathione (GSH) content depending on the temperature and treatment with the tested organic compounds. CHS NCs loaded with Pro and GB were effective at increasing the amount of phenols under stress temperature conditions. We noted that a significant increase in the antioxidant activity of plants at 4 °C occurred after priming with Cys, CHS-Cys NCs, Pro and CHS-Pro NCs, and the CHS nanocomposites were more effective in this respect. Both low-temperature stress and foliar spraying of lettuce with various organic compounds caused changes in the activity of antioxidant enzymes. Two forms of dismutase (SOD), iron superoxide dismutase (FeSOD) and copper/zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu/ZnSOD), were identified in extracts from the leaves of iceberg lettuce seedlings. The application of the tested organic compounds, alone or in combination with CHS, increased the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA) in plants grown under controlled temperature conditions. Chilling caused an increase in the content of MDA, but some organic compounds mitigated the impact of low temperature. Compared with that of plants subjected to 20 °C, the fresh weight of plants exposed to chilling decreased. However, the tested compounds caused a decrease in fresh weight at 4 °C compared with the corresponding control samples. An interesting exception was the use of Cys, for which the difference in the fresh weight of plants grown at 20 °C and 4 °C was not statistically significant. After Cys application, the dry weight of the chilled plants was greater than that of the chilled control plants but was also greater than that of the other treated plants in this group. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that engineered chitosan–amino acid nanocomposites could be applied as innovative protective agents to mitigate the effects of chilling stress in crop plants.Keywords: Antioxidants, Catalase, Low-molecular weight compounds, Plasma membrane, Polyphenols, Superoxide dismutasesSubject terms: Plant physiology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Plants are constantly challenged by a range of biotic and abiotic stresses. These stresses critically affect plant growth, development and productivity and are also major constraints on the spatial distribution of crops1. Among abiotic stresses, cold stress includes chilling (< 20 °C) and freezing (< 0 °C) temperatures, which prevent the manifestation of the full genetic potential of plants due to impaired metabolic processes resulting from low-temperature-induced osmotic and oxidative stress1,2.

The plasma membrane (PM) is the main target of environmental stresses that affect its composition, structure and properties, thereby disrupting cellular homeostasis3. Receptor proteins on the PM sense various environmental stimuli and transmit them downstream to intracellular and intercellular signaling networks4. At low temperature, the PM shifts from a liquid crystalline phase to a rigid gel phase, resulting in reduced membrane fluidity mediated by changes in unsaturated fatty acid content; other changes include increased membrane permeability, ion leakage, inactivation of transmembrane proteins and disruption of ion homeostasis5. Thus, maintaining the fluidity of the membrane and the integrity of the PM is essential for conferring abiotic stress tolerance in plants6.

As with other stress factors, low temperature causes the generation of excessive amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress, which disrupts redox homeostasis and causes oxidative damage in plants2,7–9. An imbalance between ROS production and removal interferes with normal cell function, damages biomolecules such as carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and DNA; and ultimately causes cell death10. Plants try to maintain ROS at nondamaging levels in their cells; this can be achieved by upregulating enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidant systems. This defense system involves several enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), ascorbate/guaiacol/glutathione peroxidase (APX/GPOX/GPX), glutathione S-transferase (GST) and catalase (CAT)7,8,11.

The nonenzymatic response relies on the regulation of the production of polyamines12, as well as low-molecular-weight compounds such as reduced glutathione (GSH), vitamins (such as ascorbate and tocopherol), osmolytes (such as proline and glycine betaine), and secondary metabolites, particularly polyphenols and carotenoids13.

A great deal of research is currently being carried out to develop sustainable solutions that increase the tolerance of crop plants to harsh environmental conditions. Generally, amino acids play a variety of important roles in plants, including regulating osmolyte function, ion transport and stomatal opening; influencing the synthesis and activity of specific enzymes; serving as precursors for the synthesis of secondary metabolites and signaling molecules; and regulating gene expression and redox homeostasis14. For this reason, we investigated the impact of externally applied amino acids or their derivatives on plants while comparing the responses of plants to low-temperature stress and nonstressed controls. The exogenous application of amino acids is known to have positive effects on plant metabolism under stressful conditions15,16. We chose cysteine, a semiessential proteinogenic amino acid, glycine betaine, a trimethylated derivative of the amino acid glycine, one of the most widely used compatible solutes in nature, and proline, which is biosynthetically derived from the amino acid l-glutamate. The importance of cysteine (Cys) in plants is defined not only by its role as an amino acid in proteins but also by its ability to fix inorganic sulfur absorbed from the environment and provide metabolic sulfide donors for the generation of methionine, phytochelatins, iron-sulfur clusters, vitamin cofactors, and multiple secondary metabolites17,18. Cysteine is involved in the synthesis of reduced glutathione (GSH), which is regarded as an important determinant of cellular redox homeostasis19. Proline (Pro) accumulation is an evolutionarily conserved cell defense mechanism against stressful environments: the quenching of hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) protects cells from ROS oxidation, acts as an osmolyte, maintains osmotic balance, prevents high salinity-mediated cell shrinkage, stabilizes the structures of key protein enzymes and membranes, protects photosynthetic products and counteracts the formation of ice crystals, protecting against cell disruption by freezing20,21. Glycine betaine (GB) is a major cellular osmolyte that elevates photosynthetic capacity and protects thylakoid membrane proteins and lipids, maintaining membrane integrity against the damaging effects of stress stimuli. Additionally, GB increases the activity and concentration of key Calvin cycle enzymes (such as Rubisco, Rubisco activase, fructose biphosphatase, fructose biphosphatase aldolase, and phosphoribulose kinase), by stabilizing their structures under stress conditions, and antioxidant enzymes, including POX, CAT, and SOD22–24. Thus, we chose the amine compounds described above because they play significant roles in increasing plant tolerance to abiotic stress, hence, we tested their effectiveness under conditions of plant exposure to cold. An additional and, in our opinion, very interesting aspect of this research was the use of analogous compounds produced at the nanoscale and using chitosan as a nanocarrier. Al-Shaheen et al.25 applied nanoproline to Stevia rebaudiana and observed promising results, including increases in the number of branches, the stem diameter and the chlorophyll content. Chitosan (CHS) can stimulate the photosynthetic rate and stomatal closure, increase the activity of antioxidant enzymes, and induce the production of organic acids, sugars, amino acids and other metabolites required for osmotic adjustment, stress signaling and energy metabolism under stress26. CHS nanoparticles functionalized with various organic compounds are likely to have greater potential for improving plant tolerance to abiotic stress27–29. Given the recent advances in chitosan-related nanocarriers or nanocapsules, such as CHSs loaded with thiamine30 or phenylalanine31, the use of this approach is becoming increasingly important in various areas of plant science. This experimental design enables simultaneous comparisons of the response of plants to the application of conventional and nanoparticle materials under nonstressed and stressed conditions. Compared with their larger counterparts, nanoparticles (NPs) have specific physicochemical properties; thus, they may affect plants differently than larger particles32. The ability of NPs to shield crop plants from various abiotic challenges has been described in many reports33–35.

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa L.) is a widely grown leafy vegetable and is popular for its health-promoting properties due to its antioxidant polyphenols, including chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid derivatives36. Its leaves are edible and significant sources of vitamins A and C and lactupicrin37. As with most agricultural crops, the productivity of lettuce is hampered/limited by cold stress38,39. For this reason, the present study focused on the potential effects of foliar spraying of amino acids, chitosan and nanocomposites of chitosan with amino acids on the physiological and biochemical traits of iceberg lettuce grown under nonstressed temperature (20 °C) and chilling conditions (4 °C). We tested several hypotheses based on whether foliar application of amino acids, CHS and amino acid-functionalized nanocomposites could (a) alleviate the response of plants to chilling stress; (b) affect bioactive compounds under specific temperature conditions; and (c) affect the properties of the plasmalemma of plant cells.

Results and discussion

Membranes

Any change in the environment affects the state of cell membranes, and the plasmalemma is at the center of changes when chemical compounds are supplied from the environment. The reactions that take place at this level are the initial stages of mechanisms that determine the directions of metabolic pathways, leading to cell acclimatization or damage and death in later stages. Proteins that make up the plasmalemma are mainly responsible for these processes, but their proper functioning is strictly dependent on the mechanical properties (i.e., fluidity, flexibility) of the lipid part of the membrane, which is the environment in which these proteins operate. Even subtle changes in the cell membrane can prevent cells from responding appropriately to stimuli and sending the correct signals to neighboring cells. Membrane lipids were extracted from plant material treated with the tested organic compounds and then separated into phospholipid (PL) and galactolipid fractions. The PL fraction was selected for further research because it is a model of the plasmalemma of native cells. The isotherms obtained, as well as the profile of the compression modulus, were used to determine the physicochemical parameters characterizing the monolayers tested. Alim, a parameter defining the surface area per single lipid molecule in the maximally packed layer, and Cs−1max, a parameter determining the degree of flexibility/stiffness of the monolayer, were determined. The physicochemical parameters of PL monolayers extracted from plants grown in the presence of CHS, Pro, GB, Cys and CHS nanocomposites containing these amino acids indicated that these factors significantly affected the properties of the monolayers, namely, their packing density and flexibility. For plants grown at 20 °C, the average surface area per lipid molecule in the layer was greater for each treatment than for the control. For CHS alone, this increase was approximately 22%, while for amino acids, the increases were approximately 27%, 44% and 32% for Pro, GB and Cys, respectively. In comparison, the presence of nanocomposites containing the same amino acids increased the Alim content for CHS-Pro NPs (approximately 42%), while for those containing GB and Cys (CHS-GB NCs and CHS-Cys NCs, respectively), the Alim were only approximately 4% and 6% greater than that of the control.

The second parameter analyzed was Cs−1, which increased for each treatment tested in plants grown at 20 °C. The presence of CHS alone caused a change of approximately 27%, while the presence of amino acids and nanocomposites caused much greater changes (over 50% compared with the control). In the case of Pro, the amino acid alone had a greater effect than the nanocomposite with Pro (CHS-Pro NCs), while the reverse was true for Cys.

Compared with those grown at 20 °C, plants grown at 4 °C showed fewer significant changes, especially in the case of Alim. All treatments reduced the value of Alim; in particular, amino acids applied alone caused changes in this parameter in the range of 10%, while for the chitosan and CHS nanocomposites, these changes were in the range of 4–8%. A much more sensitive parameter at this temperature was Cs−1, for which increases of up to approximately 90% were recorded for CHS-GB NCs and CHS-Cys NCs NPs, while for amino acids alone, these changes were lower by approximately 58% for Pro and GB and not statistically significant for Cys.

Based on the obtained PL monolayers extracted from the plants grown in the presence of CHS and Pro, Gly, and Cys amino acids and chitosan nanocomposites containing these amino acids, we can conclude that these factors significantly affected the properties of the plasmalemma of plant cells. At both 20 °C and 4 °C, they caused significant increases in membrane stiffness (Cs−1). The tested nanocomposites had the greatest effects on this parameter, while among them, those containing Cys were the most effective, followed by those containing GB and those containing Pro. The analysis of changes in the Alim parameter showed that at 4 °C, the nanocomposites also had greater effects than other treatments, while at 20 °C, for CHS-GB NCs and CHS-Cys, the effects on Alim were less than those for the amino acids alone.

Other directions of change in membrane properties at different temperatures result from the fact that temperature itself significantly affects the fluidity of membranes40. Low temperature reduces the possibility of movement of membrane-forming molecules, as a result, lipids with a higher percentage of unsaturated fatty acids are incorporated into cells treated at such a temperature. The interaction of chitosan with membranes has primarily been investigated in terms of the potential use of these nanocomposites as drug carriers. However, in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, researchers have demonstrated that the chitosan layer adheres to the membrane surface. The interaction between DPPC bilayers and chitosan chains has also revealed changes in membrane properties41.

Considering that the studied nanocomposites contained amino acids, the impact of individual amino acids on lipid membrane models was also explored. In a study by Tu et al.﻿42, detailed molecular interactions between 20 amino acids and DOPC membranes were investigated using MD simulations that included all atoms. The diversity of interactions, synergistic effects of hydrophilic and hydrophobic group interactions, and the structural flexibility of amino acids play crucial roles in determining membrane insertion capabilities. Taking these findings into account, the interaction of chitosan nanocomposites containing different amino acids with cellular membranes significantly depends on the type of attached amino acid.

The experimental setup employed in the study, which included experiments with pure amino acids and nanocomposites containing the same amino acids, unequivocally indicated that both the nanocomposite and the amino acid influenced this interaction. The direction and magnitude of change depended on the specific amino acid. These findings offer hope that by carefully selecting the appropriate amino acid, the desired effects can be achieved.

In this way, by remodeling their membranes, cells ‘adapt’ their membranes so that the processes they carry out (e.g., signaling or transport) can continue to function properly. Therefore, in the present research, the state of the cell membranes was influenced by both the temperature change and the presence of the organic compounds tested, and their effect was the sum of the processes by which the plants adapted to the environment so that they could continue to function properly.

Analysis of soluble carbohydrates, starch, and proline

Stress causes plants to accumulate amino acids and sugars, which play important roles in metabolic processes and plant growth and development. The levels of sugars in plant cells, as well as their distribution, use and storage, are carefully controlled and conditioned by the physiological activity of cells, the type of tissue, the environmental conditions and the stage of plant development during ontogeny43.

No statistically significant differences in the concentration of soluble sugars were observed under control conditions or after the use of Pro with or without chitosan (Table 1). In the remaining treatments analyzed, the differences in sugar concentration were a consequence of the lower temperature and/or the use of additional organic compounds.

The presence of proline, which acts primarily as an osmoprotector, supports acclimatization to stress conditions. An increase in proline concentration under stress is one of the first plant responses to stress. Zhu et al.45 postulated that proline may act as a scavenger of hydroxyl radicals, an energy absorber, and a regulator of redox potential, thereby mitigating oxidative stress in maize. The increased accumulation of proline at 4 °C may be related to the activation of processes involved in the synthesis of this metabolite and is an important mechanism to counteract the osmotic changes induced in plant cells by chilling stress (Fig. 4). Only after the use of GB and Cys were decreases observed, in relation to both the control and the culture conditions at 20 °C.

Analysis of L-ascorbic acid, glutathione and total phenolics

Under nonstressed conditions, the L-ascorbic acid (AsA) content peaked in the CHS-Pro NC treatment group. However, the application of GB and Cys alone resulted in higher AsA levels than the application of their conjugated forms with CHS. No significant differences in AsA content were detected between control plants and plants treated with CHS. Compared with those in the control plants, the AsA concentrations in the plants treated with GB and Cys increased (Table 2). Considering the stress conditions, the t test results showed that compared with 20 °C, low temperature significantly increased the AsA content in all the treatments. In particular, all treatments increased the AsA content under low-temperature conditions, with the highest values observed for CHS-Pro NC- and Cys-treated plants (Table 2).

The application of most of the compounds tested increased glutathione (GSH) levels in plants at 20 °C, with the exception of CHS-Cys NCs and GB (Table 2). As in the case of the low-temperature conditions, the responses of glutathione were very different according to the treatments. Among the treatments, CHS, Pro and Cys significantly increased the GSH concentrations compared to that in the control, and the values peaked at Cys. Glutathione levels decreased in plants exposed to 4 °C and primed with CHS-Pro NCs. In all other cases, GSH levels were greater in plants grown under stress.

Low-temperature stress can lead to the overproduction of ROS, which can disrupt redox homeostasis and cause extensive damage to plant cells and cellular organelles. Plants activate an antioxidant defense mechanism to detoxify ROS, which includes molecules such as ascorbate and glutathione46. Plants exposed to abiotic stress show greater accumulation of AsA and GSH under stress conditions47,48, which is consistent with our results.

Chitosan induces many reactions in plants, leading to an increase in their tolerance to adverse environmental conditions by improving their performance under stress, stimulating water uptake and photosynthetic activity, and acting as a ROS scavenger because its structure consists of hydroxyl and amino groups available to react with ROS26,49. Based on our results, CHS application induced AsA biosynthesis under stress conditions, similar to all other organic compounds applied to the plants. The greatest increases in l-ascorbic acid content were observed after the application of the CHS-Pro NCs compared with those in the control plants at both temperatures.

Proline has diverse roles under stress conditions, including osmoprotection, the stabilization of proteins, membranes, and subcellular structures, and scavenging of reactive oxygen species50–52. In this context, Lopez-Delacalle et al.53 showed that the proline and ascorbate pathways act synchronously and in concert to maintain cellular redox homeostasis. Although the detailed underlying mechanisms are still under discussion, this may explain the increases in AsA contents with CHS-Pro NC treatment at both temperatures and with Pro treatment alone at 4 °C. At both temperatures, Cys, CHS-Cys NCs, GB and CHS-GB NCs increased the concentration of L-ascorbic acid. Picchi et al.54 observed increased AsA levels in durum wheat when chitosan nanoparticles loaded with N-acetylcysteine were applied, probably related to the increased cysteine availability. Cys has been shown to function as a precursor for a variety of biomolecules, such as vitamins55.

According to Rady et al.56, the foliar application of glycine betaine significantly increased the ascorbic acid content in salt-stressed onions. Hasanuzzaman et al.50 suggested that GB added to stressed plants improved the levels of monodehydroascorbate reductase (MDHAR) and dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR), enzymes of the AsA–GSH cycle, thereby increasing AsA levels. Glutathione synthesis depends on γ-ECS (γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase) and GSHS (glutathione synthase) activity and the availability of glutamate, cysteine and glycine57,58. Marked effects of pure Cys on the increase in glutathione content in plants were observed at both temperatures, but CHS-Cys NCs decreased the GSH content at 4 °C. Such decreases were also observed for GB at stress and nonstress temperatures. This finding is not consistent with the results obtained by Rady et al.56 for GB-sprayed onions, which showed an increase in the amount of GSH under stress conditions compared with the control. Conversely, CHS-GB NCs applied to plants at 20 °C increased GSH levels, but not at chilling temperatures. As mentioned above, chitosan has been shown to enhance the physiological response of plants and to mitigate the adverse effects of abiotic stress. The increases in glutathione levels under the influence of CHS at both temperatures demonstrated the role of this compound in the plant defense system as an elicitor of GSH biosynthesis. However, chitosan nanocomposites may have different effects due to the modification of their physicochemical properties. In our experiment, priming with CHS-Pro NC increased glutathione levels in plants exposed to 20 °C but not to chilling stress. These results suggest that this nanocomposite may not be suitable as a regulator of GSH biosynthesis under stress conditions but is excellent for increasing l-ascorbic acid levels.

Treatment with Pro and CHS-Pro NCs significantly decreased the total phenolic content in iceberg lettuce seedlings at 20 °C; conversely, CHS, GB and especially CHS-GB NCs increased the phenolic contents compared with that in the control (Fig. 5). Chitosan nanocomposites loaded with proline and glycine betaine proved to be effective at increasing the amount of phenolics under stress temperature conditions. However, no such situation was observed for cysteine-containing nanocomposites, although the application of Cys alone increased the level of total phenolics compared with that in control plants.

The levels of total phenolics in the control and CHS-treated plants at 4 °C were not significantly different. Interestingly, the total phenolic contents in the control plants were similar at both temperatures, and in the case of CHS, the contents were even lower at 4 °C. Chilling temperature resulted in significant increases in total phenolic content in lettuce treated with Pro and CHS-Pro NCs and in Cys-treated seedlings but not in CHS-Cys NCs.

Phenolics are secondary metabolites involved in the scavenging and inactivation of reactive oxygen species, and there is evidence that stress accelerates phenolic content in plants59. However, in our previous study60, most of the basil genotypes tested did not show significant changes in the levels of these compounds, similar to the results of the present study for control plants exposed to 20 °C and 4 °C. Chandra et al.61 showed for Camelia sinensis that the total phenolic accumulation levels after exposure to chitosan and chitosan nanoparticles increased by approximately 20% and 24%, respectively, in comparison with that of the control. Such increases were observed in our study under nonstress conditions. However, no increase in phenolic content at 4 °C was observed in the chitosan-treated plants. Gao et al.107 showed that proline-treated celery plants exhibited significant increases in several phenolic compounds under control and salt stress conditions.

However, these findings were inconsistent with our results for Pro- and CHS-Pro NC-treated lettuce at the control temperature and for Pro-treated plants at 4 °C, where a decrease in total phenolic content (20 °C) or no changes (4 °C) were observed, but loading proline into CHS nanocomposites altered the effects on plants, causing an increase in phenolic content at stress. Shams et al.62 demonstrated that exogenous GB treatments could increase the total phenolic contents of lettuce under salt stress and control conditions, consistent with our results for plants grown at 20 °C (GB and CHS-GB NCs); however, at 4 °C, only the CHS-GB NCs were effective. CHS-GB NCs showed greater stimulation of phenolic biosynthesis at both temperatures. The increased levels of total phenolics in plants maintained at stress temperatures and treated with Cys can be explained by changes in the phenylpropanoid pathway.

A relatively high phenolic content is associated with increased phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) activity and suppressed polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity63. As demonstrated by these authors, the activity of PPO may decrease after cysteine application. In our study, CHS-Cys NCs were not effective in the biosynthesis of phenolics under stress conditions.

Antioxidant activity of lettuce leaves

Under nonstress conditions, increases in total antioxidant capacity (TAC), based on the DPPH assay, were observed for plants treated with Pro, GB and CHS-Cys NCs (Fig. 6). A significant decrease in TAC was associated with the use of CHS-GB NCs. CHS had no effect on the plants in this respect. CHS had no significant effect on plant TAC in plants exposed to 4 °C. Significant increases in the antioxidant activity of plants at 4 °C occurred after priming with Cys, CHS-Cys NCs, Pro and CHS-Pro NCs, with CHS nanocomposites containing the organic compounds tested being more effective. Differences in the scavenging efficacy of DPPH radicals due to temperature conditions were found for CHS-Pro NCs and Cys (an increase in TAC), while the use of GB significantly reduced the TAC at stress temperatures compared with 20 °C.

Exogenously applied CHS-Pro NCs were the most efficient at increasing the total antioxidant activity under stress conditions, probably due to the increased levels of AsA and phenolics in plants treated with this nanocomposite (Table 2, Fig. 5). A likely similar relationship should be considered to explain the positive effect of CHS-Cys NCs on the total antioxidant capacity of plants stored at 4 °C. Although plants treated with GB at 20 °C showed a greater ability to scavenge DPPH radicals, such a response was not observed in plants exposed to 4 °C, which is inconsistent with the results of Shallan et al.64, who showed that the total antioxidant capacity of cotton plants under drought conditions was increased by spraying with GB. In our study, the TAC levels of untreated plants and plants after CHS application were similar at both temperatures, which was somewhat surprising because stress conditions65 and treatment of plants with chitosan66 usually increase the antioxidant capacity of plants.

Activities of antioxidant enzymes

CAT and SOD play important roles in the oxidative stress tolerance of plants. As shown in Fig. 7, not only low-temperature stress but also foliar spraying of lettuce leaves with different organic compounds and chitosan nanocomposites of these compounds caused changes in antioxidant enzymatic activity (Fig. 7). Under nonstress conditions, all the CHS nanocomposites, pure CHS and GB decreased the CAT activity. Only an increase in CAT activity was observed in plants treated with Cys compared with the control. Cys (pure and in the form of nanocomposites with chitosan) as well as the CHS, GB and CHS-Pro NCs increased the CAT activity at 4 °C. A significant decrease in catalase activity was found in the Pro-treated plants compared with that in the control plants at the stress temperature. The CAT activity decreased at 4 °C compared with 20 °C for control plants and plants treated with Pro or CHS-GB NCs but increased when plants were primed with CHS, GB or CHS-Cys NCs. In almost all cases, the activity of SOD increased at 20 °C after the application of CHS, CHS nanocomposites loaded with amino acids and their derivatives and the latter compounds, with the exception of Cys and CHS-Cys NCs.

In plants under stress, the greatest increase in SOD activity was detected in GB-treated plants, but lettuce sprayed with CHS, CHS-Pro NCs, Cys and CHS-Cys NCs also exhibited increased activity levels of this antioxidant enzyme. Only a decrease in SOD activity was observed in the Pro-treated plants compared with the control plants. Comparing the response of the plants to the temperature level, we observed lower SOD activity in the control plants and in the plants treated with Pro, CHS-Pro NCs, CHS-GB NCs and Cys. Conversely, treatments with CHS, GB and CHS-Cys NCs caused increases in SOD activity.

The stimulatory effect of chitosan on CAT activity was observed only in plants exposed to 4 °C, and indeed, there is evidence that chitosan increases catalase activity under stress conditions67. In our study, the application of Cys at both temperatures and of CHS-Cys NCs at 4 °C significantly increased CAT activity. Aysin et al.19 also reported increased CAT activity in maize seedlings after exogenous Cys application under nonstress and stress conditions. There are reports describing higher CAT activity in plants after proline application106. However, this effect was not detected in our study under nonstress conditions, with the exception of the presence of CHS-Pro NCs in plants subjected to 4 °C. Although the application of GB and CHS-GB NCs did not affect the CAT activity under nonstress conditions, which was even lower than that in the control, GB significantly increased the CAT activity under chilling conditions, which is partially consistent with the findings of Min et al.68, who reported that GB had an ameliorative effect on increasing the activity of CAT under low-temperature conditions. Plants grown at 20 °C showed a significant increase in SOD activity due to the application of chitosan, Pro, CHS-Pro NCs and CHS-GB NCs. Guo et al.69 reported the induction of SOD by oligochitosan treatment in leaves, which is consistent with our results but was not observed in CHS-treated plants exposed to 4 °C. Kartashov et al.105 reported a negative correlation between proline content and SOD activity in the wild species tested, which is consistent with our data showing higher levels of SOD activity in the plants treated with Pro and CHS-Pro NCs, which were negatively correlated with proline content in these plants (Fig. 4), for Pro only under stressed conditions, for CHS-Pro NCs at both temperatures, indicating that the nanocomposite was more effective at increasing SOD activity.

Cysteine applied to the plants decreased SOD activity at chilling temperature or had no effect at the higher temperature of 20 °C. This finding is not consistent with the results published by Sadak et al.70 for soybeans exposed to irrigation with tap water and saline water, cysteine treatments caused significant increases in SOD activity in both nonstressed and stressed plants compared with the control. In our study, no significant effects of GB application on SOD activity were detected; higher SOD activity was detected in plants grown under control conditions after CHS-GB NC application, but this was not confirmed for chilling temperature, which was associated with increased SOD activity in chilling-treated control plants.

Two forms of SOD, iron superoxide dismutase (FeSOD) and copper/zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu/ZnSOD), were identified in extracts from the leaves of iceberg lettuce seedlings (Fig. 8). The activity levels of all forms of SOD are shown in Fig. 7.

CHS had no effect on APX activity at 20 °C, but chilling decreased APX activity in plants sprayed with chitosan. Chitosan had no effect on GPOX activity in plants grown at nonstress or stress temperatures (Fig. 9B). At 20 °C, the activity of GPOX significantly increased in plants sprayed with GB, Cys and CHS-Cys NCs. Compared with the control, all the chitosan nanocomposites increased the activity of GPOX at 4 °C, as did GB and Cys. Moreover, GPOX exhibited significantly greater levels of activity at stress temperatures for all the CHS-NCs, but the application of CHS and GB decreased the activity of this enzyme compared with that at 20 °C. Plants treated with Pro (at both temperatures) and CHS-Pro NCs (at 4 °C) showed increases in APX activity (Fig. 9A), which are considered to be related to the ascorbate–glutathione pathway71. The lower l-ascorbic acid levels and higher APX activity observed for Pro-treated plants at 20 °C than in the control suggest some consumption of the ascorbate pool, whereas the much higher l-ascorbic acid levels and lower APX activity for CHS-Pro NC-treated plants at 20 °C showed that this process was not yet very advanced.

It is likely that the explanation underlying the changes in APX activity in the plants at nonstressed temperatures may be similar. The lower APX activity in the GB-treated plants at 4 °C compared with that in the CHS-GB NC-treated plants corresponded to the higher l-ascorbic acid content in the GB-treated plants and lower l-ascorbic acid content in the GB-loaded chitosan nanocomposite; thus, the relationships for these organic compounds were quite similar. The lack of effects of Cys and CHS-GB NCs at either temperature on APX activity was probably related to the incorporation of cysteine into GSH metabolism.

Exogenous application of GB caused the most marked increase in GPOX activity in plants grown at 20 °C. Patade et al.72 also reported that the application of GB improved GPX activity in sugarcane; in our study, CHS-GB NCs were more effective under stress conditions. In contrast to APX, we observed stimulating effects of Cys and CHS-GB NCs on GPOX activity. Conversely, Arabaci73 tested different concentrations of cysteine on dill and reported its inhibitory effect on GPOX activity.

Analysis of malondialdehyde

Under control conditions, the application of the organic compounds tested, either alone or in combination with chitosan, increased the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA) in the plants, acting as a stress stimulus (Fig. 10). At 4 °C, the application of Pro, GB and Cys as well as the CHS and CHS-GB NCs ensured similar or even lower MDA levels (CHS-Pro NCs, GB, CHS-Cys NCs) compared with those of the control. In almost all cases, chilling caused an increase in MDA levels compared with those in plants at 20 °C. MDA is a known end product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation in cells that occurs in response to oxidative stress74. As observed by Farooq et al.75, the exogenous application of GB to cotton plants reduced oxidative stress, as evidenced by decreased MDA levels in the leaves and roots of plants grown under Cd stress conditions. Chitosan nanocomposites with Pro and Cys were found to be effective in this respect, while Pro and Cys alone were not.

Fresh and dry weights of the plants

The production of plant biomass is dependent upon the temperature surrounding the plant. Low temperature affects cell survival and cell division and impairs photosynthesis and water transport, slowing plant growth and eventually constraining crop productivity104. A temperature below the optimal level for that particular plant species reduces leaf expansion and internode length; decreases photosynthesis due to partial stomatal closure; slows electron transport; inhibits carbohydrate metabolism (not only carbohydrates) and respiration; and interferes with phloem loading76. The increase in the fresh weight of plants exposed to chilling temperatures is much slower than that under optimal thermal conditions, which is a well-known effect widely described in the literature77,78. Climate change and frequent extreme weather conditions, including the occurrence of low temperatures, have seriously affected crop growth, especially at the early stages of ontogeny, and disturbed plant productivity79. Finding a method to increase the tolerance of plants to different abiotic stresses, including low temperatures (by priming), would ensure the harmonious growth of plants and higher yields80. Perhaps the amino acids and their nanocomposites used in this experiment are such priming agents. Under nonstressed conditions, CHS-Pro NC and Cys application decreased, whereas GB treatment increased the fresh weight of the iceberg lettuce seedlings (Table 3). Most of the treatments partially buffered the decreases in fresh weight at 4 °C and did not completely alleviate the adverse effects of low temperature. The only exception was the application of Cys, where the difference in fresh weight between plants grown at 20 °C and those grown at 4 °C was not significant, suggesting that this compound is a promising stress tolerance agent. Some positive effects of foliar application of Cys on Avena sativa growth parameters were observed by Perveen et al.81. The results for dry weight content were more varied. At 20 °C, the dry weight of the plants increased after the application of CHS, CHS-GB NCs, CHS-Cys NCs, Pro and GB, whereas at 4 °C such a result was observed for Cys and CHS-Cys NCs. The use of CHS and GB did not prevent the reduction in dry weight of chilled seedlings compared to plants from the temperature of 20 °C. After application of Cys, the dry weight of the chilling plants was higher than that of the chilling control plants, but also higher than that of the other treated plants in this group. Cys application increased the dry weight of plants in 4 °C compared with 20 °C. Thus, Cys application proved to be the best option for chilled plants, and seemed to be potential priming agent for an increase in dry weight productivity.

Materials and methods

Chemical materials and their characteristics

Proline, l-cysteine and glycine betaine were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (sales representative: Merck Life Science Sp. z.o.o., Poznań, Poland). According to Sigma Aldrich, proline (C5H9NO2) is a white powder soluble in water with a molecular weight of 115.13 g/mol (CAS 147-85-3); l-cysteine (C3H7NO2S) is a white crystal soluble in water with a molecular weight of 121.16 g/mol (CAS 52-90-4); and glycine betaine (C5H11NO2) is a white crystal soluble in water with a molecular weight of 117.15 g/mol (CAS 107-43-7). Chitosan with a low molecular weight of 100 kDa and a degree of acetylation of 85% was purchased from Sabz Gostaresh Azin Turkan Co., Maragheh, Iran. Proline, acetic acid, and sodium tripolyphosphate were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich Co., USA. Double-distilled water was used in all stages of this work, and the other chemicals used were of analytical grade and used without any purification.

Preparation of nanocomposites

The preparation of CHS-Pro NCs and CHS-GB NCs was performed according to previous works82,83. To prepare the CHS-Cys NCs, a CHS solution was prepared by dissolving 200 mg of CHS powder in 50 mL of a 1% acetic acid solution. After stirring for 2 h at 70 °C, a clear CHS solution was obtained. A cysteine solution was prepared by dissolving 200 mg of Cys powder in 30 mL of distilled water and adding it to the CHS solution. The CHS-Cys NC mixture was stirred for 1 h. To crosslink the CHS, 80 mg of sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) was dissolved in 20 mL of distilled water and added to the CHS-Cys mixture while stirring vigorously. The stirring of the CHS-Cys NCs continued for 24 h83.

Characterization of synthesized nanocomposites

TEM images, XRD patterns, FT-IR spectra, and EDS analyses of the CHS-Pro NCs and CHS-GB NCs were confirmed in our previous works82,83. The surface morphology of the CHS-Cys NCs was imaged by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, MIRA3 TESCAN, Czech Republic). Figure 11a shows the surfaces of the quasispherical nanoparticles. The dynamic light scattering (DLS) results were also analyzed with a MALVERN Zen3600 instrument, and the mean size of the CHS-Cys NCs was estimated to be approximately 134 nm (Fig. 11b).

(a) SEM image and (b) DLS result of the chitosan-cysteine nanocomposite (CHS-Cys NCs).

Plant material and culture conditions

The experiment was carried out on iceberg lettuce (Lactuca sativa L.) cv. ‘Rumors’ (Bejo Zaden). The plants were purchased from a certified supplier, Krasoń—A Group of Vegetable Seedling Producers (Piaski, Poland). Lettuce seeds were sown in cubic peat pots (individual pot dimensions 4 cm × 4 cm × 4 cm, total pot volume 64 mL) placed in plastic boxes (150 pots per box). The propagation substrate Potgrond 007 from Kronen (Grądy, Poland) was used. Three weeks after sowing, the seedlings were transferred to the greenhouse of the University of Agriculture in Krakow, Poland. The lettuce seedlings were placed on a rolling table. Irrigation was carried out without wilting the shoots by flooding the bottoms of the trays in which the plants were placed. The plants were not fertilized.

In the greenhouse, the plants were exposed to natural light, the duration of the day was approximately 16 h, the average air temperature was 23/19 °C (day/night), and the relative humidity of the air was approximately 75%. At the age of 25 days, plants with 5–6 leaves were foliar sprayed with amino acids, chitosan and chitosan nanocomposites. The plants were then transferred to the phytotron room and placed on foil with raised edges to facilitate watering from the bottom of the boxes. In separate rooms of the phytotron, the temperature was kept constant for 24 h at 20 °C or 4 °C. The photoperiod was set to 16/8 h (day/night), the light source was two OSRAM Plantstar high-pressure sodium lamps (400 W, 56,500 lm, 2000 °K) per room, and the relative humidity was approximately 75%. The plants were kept in the phytotron for 7 days.

Experimental design

The treatments involved foliar application of different organic compounds, which included chitosan (0.1%), proline (50 mM), l-cysteine (50 mM), glycine betaine (50 mM) and chitosan nanocomposites (CHS NCs) with proline, l-cysteine and glycine betaine (50 mM); control plants were simultaneously sprayed with deionized water. The dose was 150 mL per seedling box for all experimental combinations, approximately 1 mL per plant. The suspensions were ultrasonicated for 15 min prior to application. After spraying, half of the plants in each treatment group were transferred to a phytotron at 20 °C, while the other half were transferred to a phytotron at 4 °C. The other microclimatic conditions described above were identical in both phytotron species. Necessary care, such as watering, was given regularly. The experimental design and the abbreviated names of the treatments are presented below (Fig. 12).

Experimental design of the study.

After 1 week, all plants from each treatment were sampled and transferred to the laboratory. Fresh material was used to determine seedling fresh weight, dry weight, l-ascorbic acid content and glutathione content. The remaining plant samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 40 °C for future assessments of other biochemical parameters.

Membranes

The lipid components were isolated using a mixture of chloroform and isopropanol and later with chloroform alone, based on an adjusted version of the Bligh and Dyer method103. Then, the proteins were separated into phospholipid (PL) and galactolipid fractions via column chromatography on silica gel under nitrogen, followed by purification through thin-layer chromatography as described by Block et al.84. The PL fraction was selected for further research because it is a model of the plasmalemma of native cells. The experiments were carried out using the Langmuir technique (Minitrough, KSV, Finland). The monolayers were prepared by spreading the chloroform solutions of PL on the water surface and were compressed at a rate of 3.5–4.6 Å2/molecule × min. The dependence of the surface pressure (π) on the area per lipid molecule (A) was the basis for obtaining the parameters characterizing the lipid monolayer structure, such as Alim, the smallest area occupied by a single molecule in a layer, πcoll, the pressure at which a layer collapsed; and Cs−1, the static compressive modulus, which shows the mechanical resistance against layer compression. The surface pressure was measured with an accuracy of ± 0.1 mN/m using a Platinum Wilhelmy plate connected to an electrobalance. All of the experiments were carried out at 25 °C and were repeated three or four times to ensure high reproducibility of the isotherms obtained to ± 0.1–0.3 Å2.

Proline determination

The proline content was quantified using the method described by Bates et al.85 and modified by Grzesiak et al.86. Briefly, 1 g of leaves was incubated in a water bath at 96 °C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was mixed with ninhydrin reagent (1% in 60% acetic acid). The samples were then incubated, and toluene was added. After mixing, quantitative analysis was performed using a spectrophotometric technique (Evolution 201, Thermo Scientific).

Analysis of soluble carbohydrates and starch

The total sugar concentration (both starch and soluble sugars) was analyzed spectrophotometrically (Evolution 201, Thermo Scientific) at 620 nm according to the anthrone method described in detail by McCready et al.87 as modified by Janeczko et al.88.

Analysis of l-ascorbic acid, glutathione and total phenolics

To determine the content of l-ascorbic acid, a modified iodometric method described by Ikewuchi and Ikewuchi89 was used in this study. First, 2.5 g of plant material was collected and ground in a mortar using 10 mL of 1% oxalic acid. The prepared samples were then transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at room temperature for 10 min. Then, 5 mL of the supernatant was removed, and 1 mL of 1% starch solution was added. The resulting solution was titrated with an iodine solution in potassium iodide. After all the ascorbic acid was oxidized, the iodine quickly formed a dark blue complex with the starch. This process indicated the end of the titration. The AsA content of the sample was calculated by measuring the amount of iodine solution in the potassium iodide solution used for the titration. Pure ascorbic acid at a concentration of 0.1% was used to determine the titer of the iodine solution in potassium iodide. The content of L-ascorbic acid was expressed as mg/100 g FW.

The method described by Guri90 was used to determine the reduced form of glutathione. Two grams of fresh material was placed in a mortar and homogenized with 10 mL of cold 0.0005 M EDTA with the addition of 3% TCA. The mortar was kept on ice throughout. The prepared samples were transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 13,968×g for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, 2 mL of the supernatant was removed, and 5 mL of 0.2 M K-phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) was added. From this mixture, 2 mL was taken for the reaction, and the same buffer was added again in a volume of 1 mL. The addition of 0.1 mL of 5,5′-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid to the sample caused a color reaction. In parallel to each test sample, blank tests were carried out in which 5,5′-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid was replaced by K-phosphate buffer (pH 7). The intensity of the resulting yellow color was measured after 45 min using a Helios UV‒VIS spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 412 nm. The glutathione content is expressed as µg/g FW.

The total phenolic content was determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu method described by Djeridane et al.91. For this purpose, 2 g of plant material was extracted with 80% methanol in a volume of 10 mL. The homogenate obtained was then centrifuged in centrifuge tubes at 3492×g for 10 min at 18 °C. After centrifugation, 0.1 mL of the supernatant was taken for analysis and mixed with 2 mL of 2% Na2CO3, followed by the addition of 0.1 mL of a solution containing Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and distilled water at a 1:1 ratio for five minutes. The samples were then incubated in the dark for 45 min, after which the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 750 nm. The content of phenolic compounds was calculated using a calibration curve established for gallic acid at known concentrations. The results are expressed as mg GAE/g FW.

Antioxidant activity of the lettuce leaves

Antioxidant activity was determined using the DPPH radical method proposed by Molyneux92. The reaction mixture consisted of 0.1 mL of supernatant and 4.9 mL of an 80% methanol solution of 0.1 mM DPPH. The control solution consisted of 0.1 mL of 80% methanol and 4.9 mL of DPPH radical. All samples were mixed thoroughly and incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. The absorbance of all the samples was then measured at a wavelength of 517 nm. The antioxidant activity of DPPH was determined using the formula DPPH [%] = [(A0 − A1)/A0] × 100, where A0 is the absorbance of the control solution and A1 is the absorbance of the sample solution after incubation with DPPH.

Antioxidant enzymatic activities

To assay the activity of SOD and CAT, 1 g of plant material was first homogenized in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in cold 50 mM phosphate buffer (KH2PO4/Na2HPO4, pH 7.5) containing 3 mM EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) at a ratio of 2.5:1 v/FW. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for measuring antioxidant enzyme activity. The protein concentration was determined according to Bradford93. The SOD activity was estimated according to the procedure described by Beauchamp and Fridovich94, while the CAT activity was estimated according to the method of Aebi95. One unit of CAT activity was defined as 1 μmol of H2O2 decomposed per minute, and the absorption coefficient ε240 = 43 (μmol/L)/cm was used for calculation.

The determination of APX and GPOX activities in plant material was carried out using two methods. Both analyses were performed using supernatants prepared by the same procedure.

Two grams of plant material were ground in the presence of 10 ml of 50 mM K-phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) supplemented with 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% soluble polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) at a low temperature (4 °C) using a mortar placed on ice. The prepared samples were then centrifuged at 13,968×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The method of Nakano and Asada96 was used to determine the activity of APX. The test sample consisted of 1.85 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.5 mL of 0.5 mM ascorbate (AsA), and 0.15 mL of supernatant. The addition of 0.5 mL of 0.1 mM hydrogen peroxide, which was the last component of the reaction mixture, initiated the oxidation of AsA, which was monitored by measuring the absorbance six times at one-minute intervals at a wavelength of 290 nm (Helios Beta spectrophotometer), assuming an absorption coefficient of 2.8 mM/cm. The activity of APX was expressed as µmol AsA/min/g FW. The activity of GPOX was determined using the method of Zhang et al.97 with guaiacol as the substrate. The reaction tubes were filled with 1.4 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.2 mL of guaiacol (4%) and 1 mL of H2O2 (1%), and the enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding 0.4 mL of the supernatant to the prepared mixture. The solution was then measured three times at one-minute intervals at a wavelength of 470 nm using a molar extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM/cm. The activity of GPOX was expressed as μmol tetraguaiacol/min/g FW.

Analysis of SOD by native PAGE

The separation of the soluble protein fractions was carried out according to the procedure described by Laemmli98 using native (without sodium dodecyl sulfate) PAGE at 4 °C and 180 V. Samples containing 8 μg of protein were loaded onto the gel. Visualization of the SOD bands was performed on 12% discontinuous polyacrylamide gels according to the methods of Beauchamp and Fridovich94. The gels were incubated in staining buffer for 20 min in the dark at room temperature and then exposed to white light until the SOD activity bands became visible. Identification of the different forms of SOD was based on their sensitivity to 3 mM KCN and 5 mM H2O2 added during activity staining according to Fridovich99. The forms of the enzymes were confirmed by inhibition by KCN and H2O2 (see Supplementary Material, Fig. S1). Cu/ZnSOD is inhibited by potassium cyanide, and H2O2 inhibits both FeSOD and Cu/ZnSOD100,101. Densitometric analysis of SOD bands was performed using ImageJ 2 (GPL license).

Analysis of malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The amount of malondialdehyde in the sample was determined by the method described by Dhindsa and Matowe102. For this purpose, 2 g of plant material was weighed and homogenized in a mortar with 10 mL of 1% trichloroacetic acid on ice. The resulting samples were then centrifuged at 13,968×g for 15 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and 0.5 mL was transferred to a new tube. To this tube, 0.5 ml of 0.1 M K-phosphate buffer (pH 7.6) and 1 mL of 0.5% thiobarbituric acid dissolved in 20% trichloroacetic acid were added. A blank control was prepared using 0.5 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid, K-phosphate buffer (pH 7.6) and TBA in the same volumes as the reaction samples. All tubes were covered tightly with aluminum foil and incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were rapidly cooled on ice and centrifuged again for 5 min. The absorbance of the samples was measured using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer at wavelengths of 532 and 600 nm. The MDA content in each sample was calculated from the molar absorption coefficient and is expressed as µmol/g FW.

Fresh and dry weights of the plants

Plants were sampled from each treatment (15 plants, 5 plants per treatment) and weighed with an Ohaus PA214CM/1 balance (OHAUS Europe GmbH, Nänikon, Switzerland). Next, the plants were placed in an oven at 65 °C until a constant weight was achieved, after which the dry weight was measured. The fresh and dry weights are expressed in grams per plant.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using Statistica version 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the significance of the temperature treatments and the organic compounds applied, with a threshold of p ≤ 0.05. When ANOVA indicated a difference between means, Duncan’s multiple range post hoc test analyses were performed to determine which specific means were different. One-way ANOVA was used to determine the effects of the organic compounds applied to the plants at a given temperature. Differences between temperatures for specific treatments (i.e., organic compounds) were evaluated using a t test. Homogeneous groups were created at p ≤ 0.05. Standard deviations (SDs) were calculated for all the datasets.

Conclusions

Foliar spraying with CHS and the amino acids Pro, Gly, Cys and chitosan nanocomposites containing these amino acids significantly affected the properties of the plasmalemma of iceberg lettuce cells. The tested nanocomposites had the greatest effect on this parameter, while nanocomposites containing Cys were the most effective, and those containing Pro were the least effective. Significant increases in the total antioxidant activity and total phenolic content in the leaves of iceberg lettuce plants grown at 4 °C occurred after priming with CHS-Pro NCs and Cys in comparison to those of the corresponding plants grown at 20 °C. All tested substances increased the l-ascorbic acid and glutathione contents in chilled plants, with the exception of the CHS-Pro NCs in the case of glutathione. The activities of CAT and SOD were lower at 4 °C, but compared with 20 °C, the CHS-Pro NCs increased the activities of SOD, APX and GPOX by 46%, 187%, and 240%, respectively. Compared with those in plants subjected to 20 °C, the APX and GPOX activities in plants subjected to CHS-GB NCs increased. Standard-sized amino acids and their chitosan nanocomposites therefore represent promising priming tools that can be effectively used to alleviate the responses of plants to cold stress.
