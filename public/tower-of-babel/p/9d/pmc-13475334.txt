Clinical and Histopathologic Spectrum of Primary Nonfunction in Renal Allografts
Authors: Tiffany N. Caza, Hamza Ijaz, Jason J. Paris, Christopher P. Larsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPrimary nonfunction (PNF) is a serious complication of renal allografts, occurring in a subset of patients with delayed graft function (DGF) who never obtain freedom from dialysis. Prior studies have examined donor and recipient variables implicated in DGF and PNF; however, data on the underlying kidney pathology are largely lacking.MethodsData from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) was merged with biopsy records, and patients with a history of DGF or PNF were identified. We compared all patients with PNF (n = 61) and DGF with a biopsy within 30 days posttransplantation (n = 714) to identify donor, recipient, and histologic variables associated with PNF. Student t tests and Fisher exact tests were used for evaluation of continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Binary logistic regression was performed for more stringent assessment of PNF predictors.ResultsPNF was significantly associated with increased donor age (P = 0.002) and terminal creatinine > 1.5 mg/dl (P = 0.005). There was no impact of recipient age, sex, race, or disease comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, or obesity). Kidney pathology of allograft biopsies showed an increase in diagnoses of cortical necrosis (P < 0.0001), arterionephrosclerosis (P = 0.0002), oxalate nephropathy (P = 0.03), pyelonephritis (P = 0.009), and antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) (P = 0.0008) compared with patients with DGF who did not progress to PNF.ConclusionIn patients with DGF progressing to PNF compared with DGF with recovery, cortical necrosis, arterionephrosclerosis, oxalate nephropathy, and ABMR were more common. Identification of these entities on biopsy may identify patients at highest risk of graft failure.

PNF is a serious complication of renal allografts, occurring in a subset of patients with DGF, where patients never obtain freedom from dialysis. PNF complicates between 1% to 8% of allografts, compared with 20% to 40% who develop DGF.1, 2, 3 Prior studies have examined donor and recipient variables implicated in DGF and PNF; however, data on the underlying pathology are largely lacking.

The SRTR contains detailed data on donor and recipient variables at the time of transplantation; and allograft outcomes, including DGF, PNF, and days of graft survival.4 In this study, integration of SRTR data with kidney pathology data from biopsies with the indication of DGF was used to elucidate the donor variables, recipient variables, and histopathology underlying progression from DGF to PNF to aid in identification of at-risk patients.

Methods

Cohort Design

Following the Solutions Institutional Review Board approval, transplant data from the SRTR were merged with data from all allograft biopsies diagnosed at Arkana Laboratories from 2001 to 2023 (N = 60,186 biopsies from 33,409 unique patients). Biopsies in patients with a history of DGF (defined as dialysis within the first week of transplantation) or PNF (defined as failure of the transplanted kidney to ever initiate function) were identified. Recipients of living donors were excluded from analysis, because several analyzed donor variables would not apply (such as terminal serum creatinine, cause of death, among other factors).

We compared all patients with PNF (n = 61 patients, 82 biopsies) and 714 patients with DGF (all of those with DGF who were biopsied in the first month posttransplantation with adequate tissue for diagnosis). Biopsies from patients with DGF were limited to those performed within the first 30 days of transplantation to reflect those most likely to be biopsied for DGF rather than other indications (although the indication for biopsy was not known for all patients).

Donor and Recipient Variables Examined

Donor and recipient factors were extracted from merged SRTR and biopsy data to identify factors associated with PNF compared with DGF with >1 year of allograft function. Donor variables examined included type of death for deceased donors (anoxia, cerebrovascular accident, head trauma, and other), expanded criteria donor (ECD) status, high risk status, number of human leukocyte antigen mismatches, race, age, history of hypertension, and terminal serum creatinine. ECD status is defined as a donor aged ≥ 60 years or 50 to59 years of age with 2 of the following: a history of hypertension, cerebrovascular accident as the cause of death, and/or a terminal serum creatinine ≥ 1.5 mg/dl.5 High risk status was synonymous with an increased risk donor, or an increased risk of infectious disease transmission.6

Recipient variables extracted included age, sex, race, body mass index and obesity status, history of diabetes (type 1 or type 2), cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and prior transplantation. These data represented total patients rather than total biopsies, because some patients underwent >1 biopsies, particularly in the PNF arm. In cases with >1 biopsies diagnoses for both biopsies were included as the same patient entry, so that donor and recipient variables were not duplicated in analysis.

Histopathologic Review

All specimens included needle core biopsies. There were no nephrectomy specimens available for evaluation and donor or preimplantation biopsies were excluded. Kidney pathology data examined included kidney biopsy diagnosis and chronicity indices (including interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and arteriosclerosis). The 2019 Banff classification was used for the evaluation of allograft rejection.7 Mixed rejection was defined as a combination of ABMR and T-cell–mediated rejection (TCMR). Vascular rejection may represent either ABMR or TCMR and represents all cases with ‘v’ lesions because we are often unable to distinguish the origin based on histopathology alone. The category of “ABMR” included cases with or without C4d positivity along the peritubular capillaries. Donor-specific antibody status was not known for all patients, and therefore, was not stratified in the analysis.

In addition to examination of individual diagnoses, diagnoses were grouped into larger categories for further comparisons. These included rejection (encompassing borderline changes for TCMR, TCMR, ABMR, and vascular rejection), tubular injury (acute tubular injury [ATI], acute tubular necrosis, protracted tubular injury, with myoglobin casts, with hemoglobin casts, with calcium oxalate crystals, with calcium phosphate crystals, with isometric vacuolization suspicious for calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity, and with DHA crystals), interstitial nephritis (granulomatous and nongranulomatous, glomerular diseases (immune complex deposition without activity, C3 glomerulonephritis, collapsing glomerulopathy, diabetic nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, lupus nephritis, IgA nephropathy, minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, infection-associated glomerulonephritis, or thin glomerular basement membranes/Alport syndrome), vascular (thrombotic microangiopathy, cortical infarcts, arterionephrosclerosis, and fibrin thrombi), or infection (pyelonephritis, polyomavirus nephritis, or presence of yeast forms).

Statistical Analyses

Between PNF and DGF groups, categorical variables were compared using Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were assessed using unpaired 2-tailed t tests. For all analyses, α = 0.05.

Binary Logistic Regression

To identify transplant recipient and donor factors independently associated with PNF, 2 separate binary logistic regression models were constructed with PNF versus DGF as the dependent variable. The donor model included donor age, race (categorized as White, Black, or Other), hypertension status, terminal serum creatinine, ECD status, and cause of death (categorized as anoxia, head trauma, cerebrovascular accident, or other). The recipient model included the following independent variables: recipient age, sex, race (categorized as White, Black, or Other), body mass index, and diabetes status. Model fit was assessed using the Cox and Snell R2 statistic.

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Logistic Regression

To assess pathologic predictors of PNF, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator logistic regression was performed with PNF versus DGF as the dependent variable. The following biopsy-derived variables were entered as candidate predictors: individual diagnosis flags for ATI, acute tubular necrosis, protracted tubular injury, myoglobin casts, oxalate crystals, calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity, borderline changes for TCMR, TCMR, ABMR, vascular rejection, thrombotic microangiopathy, cortical infarct, arteriosclerosis, pyelonephritis, IgA nephropathy, minimal change disease, and acute interstitial nephritis; ordinal chronicity scores for interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and arteriosclerosis (each scored 0–3 corresponding to none, mild, moderate, and severe); composite flags for any rejection and negative biopsy; and total number of diagnoses.

To address the class imbalance between PNF (n = 61) and DGF (n = 714), inverse class frequency weights were applied during model fitting. Regularization strength was selected using 5-fold stratified cross-validation optimizing the area under the curve with the final model fit at the most conservative regularization parameter within 1 standard error of the cross-validation optimum (lambda.1se = 47.15). Variables surviving least absolute shrinkage and selection operator selection were subsequently entered into an unpenalized binary logistic regression to obtain conventional odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

Cohorts

Of 33,409 transplant patients who underwent an allograft biopsy, 3315 (9.9%) had DGF as defined by requirement for dialysis within the first week of transplantation. Patients with DGF were censored to those with ≥1 year graft function. Of these patients, 714 had a biopsy ≤30 days posttransplantation with adequate tissue for diagnosis and Banff classification (≥ 7 glomeruli). DGF progressed to PNF in 61 patients with DGF (1.8%). The mean time from transplant to biopsy of patients with DGF was 13.5 ± 8.0 and 34.2 ± 40.6 days for patients with PNF (Table 1). Donor Variables Associated With PNF

PNF was associated with several donor factors, including increased donor age (46.0 ± 14.9 vs. 39.7 ± 13.4 years, P = 0.002), elevated terminal creatinine > 1.5 mg/dl (39.1% vs. 21.4%, P = 0.005), a history of hypertension (45.9% vs. 26.5%, P = 0.001), and cerebrovascular causes of death (42.6% vs. 24.9%, P = 0.003; Table 2). The mean terminal creatinine was higher (1.6 ± 1.3 mg/dl) in PNF compared with 1.3 ± 1.1 mg/dl for DGF with recovery (P = 0.03; Table 2). ECD status was more common in PNF donors (31.2% vs. 11.8%, P < 0.0001), whereas high-risk donor status was less frequent (3.3% vs. 13.8%, P = 0.02; Table 2). There was a nonsignificant trend for human leukocyte antigen mismatches to be greater in the PNF group, compared with DGF with recovery; however, both groups had poorly matched allografts (4.5 ± 1.3 vs. 4.1 ± 1.5, P = 0.09, n.s.; Table 2). Donor race, human leukocyte antigen mismatch, and death from anoxia or head trauma did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2). The donor logistic regression model demonstrated a strong fit (Cox and Snell R2 = 0.429). Donor age (OR = 1.054, 95% CI: 1.005–1.105, P = 0.03) and donor terminal creatinine (OR = 1.571, 95% CI: 1.144–2.158, P = 0.005) were independently associated with PNF. Specifically, each additional year of donor age increased the odds of PNF by 5.4%, and each 1 mg/dl increase in terminal creatinine increased the odds of PNF by 57.1%. Donor race, hypertension, ECD status, and cause of death did not independently predict PNF after adjustment.

Recipient Factors Were not Associated With PNF

There was no impact of recipient age, sex, race, diabetes mellitus, or hypetension status on DGF progression to PNF, and prior transplantation or number of prior allografts was not associated with PNF (Table 3). Comparisons of the Cause of ESKD on PNF

Lupus nephritis as a cause of ESKD was more prevalent in PNF recipients (8.2% vs. 2.5%, P = 0.01), as were rheumatoid arthritis (3.3% vs. 0.0%, P < 0.0001) and sarcoidosis (1.6% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.0006). Acute interstitial nephritis as a cause of ESKD was identified only in PNF recipients (1.6% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.03). All other causes of ESKD did not significantly differ between the groups (Supplementary Table S1).

Histopathology Underlying PNF

Kidney pathology on allograft biopsies showed an increase in cortical infarcts (21.3% vs. 3.1%, P < 0.0001), ABMR (14.8% vs. 4.6%, P = 0.0008), arterionephrosclerosis (27.9% vs. 11.3%, P = 0.0002), and polyomavirus nephritis (3.3% vs. 0.0%, P < 0.0001) compared with patients with DGF who did not progress to PNF (Table 4) and Figure 1. Oxalate crystals (8.2% vs. 2.9%, P = 0.03), protracted tubular injury (4.9% vs. 0.8%, P = 0.004), pyelonephritis (4.9% vs. 1.0%, P = 0.009), and hemoglobin casts (1.6% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.0006) were significantly more prevalent in PNF (Table 4). Because “protracted tubular injury” lacks a standard definition, we it defined as the presence of tubular injury with a background of disproportionately greater interstitial fibrosis to the degree of tubular atrophy and global glomerulosclerosis, consistent with a protracted insult. It is uncertain whether cases of oxalate nephropathy were donor-derived, or represent recurrent or de novo disease, because donor biopsies were not available for evaluation. The same is true for IgA nephropathy and thrombotic microangiopathy cases, although neither of these diagnoses reached statistical significance.

Donor-derived vascular disease (59.0% vs. 19.8%, P < 0.0001) and infectious disease (8.2% vs. 1.1%, P < 0.0001) were more common in patients with PNF (Table 4). Notably, patients with PNF were more likely to have ≥1 diagnoses on biopsy (P < 0.0001; Table 4). All remaining individual diagnoses did not differ significantly between groups.

Allograft Rejection

Of patients with PNF with rejection (n = 15), 9 had mixed rejection (60.0%), with 9 having ABMR (60.0%), 6 (40.0%) with borderline changes for TCMR, 6 (40.0%) had vascular rejection, and 3 (20.0% had TCMR. Four of the 9 patients with ABMR were C4d-positive (C4d ≥ 2 by immunofluorescence). For patients with PNF with TCMR, all had Banff Class 1A. In comparison, patients with DGF who developed rejection were less likely to have ABMR (27.3%, compared with 60.0% with PNF). Frequencies of vascular rejection were identical between groups (40.0% vs. 39.7%, P = 1.0).

Impact of Chronicity

PNF biopsies were more likely to show increased chronicity compared with those with DGF without progression to PNF (Table 5). Moderate and severe interstitial fibrosis were more prevalent in PNF than in DGF (10.5% vs. 2.1%, P = 0.0002 and 8.8% vs. 1.1%, P < 0.0001, respectively), whereas mild interstitial fibrosis was less frequent in PNF (35.1% vs. 50.8%, P = 0.03). Tubular atrophy was absent more often in DGF (71.1% vs. 42.1%, P < 0.0001), and moderate and severe tubular atrophy were both significantly more prevalent in PNF (14.0% vs. 2.6% and 7.0% vs. 0.6%, both P < 0.0001). Severe arteriosclerosis was markedly more frequent in PNF (35.7% vs. 11.1%, P < 0.0001), whereas absent arteriosclerosis was more common in DGF (51.1% vs. 28.6%, P = 0.001). Logistic Regression Modeling

The contribution of histopathologic features on progression to PNF was assessed via a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression model. Twenty-three candidate pathologic predictors were assessed (area under the curve = 0.759 at lambda.1se = 47.15). Three variables were revealed as significant independent predictors of PNF: cortical infarct was the strongest predictor (OR = 11.16, 95% CI: 4.76–26.16, P < 0.0001), followed by ABMR (OR = 3.28, 95% CI: 1.31–8.22, P = 0.011), and arteriosclerosis severity score (OR = 1.36 per grade, 95% CI: 1.03–1.79, P = 0.030; Supplementary Figure S1).

Repeat Biopsies

Sixteen patients with PNF underwent repeat biopsy, with the most common diagnoses being ATI, vascular insults (thrombotic microangiopathy or cortical necrosis), and rejection. Repeat biopsies with ATI often demonstrated a protracted pattern of tubular injury within the second biopsy, showing disproportionate interstitial fibrosis to the degree of global glomerulosclerosis and vascular scarring (Figure 2).

Discussion

PNF is a catastrophic complication of kidney transplantation, associated with dialysis dependence, low retransplantation rates, and increased mortality.3,8, 9, 10 The pathology underlying PNF is poorly understood, with the majority of data from case reports and transplant nephrectomy series.2,11 Histopathology of transplant nephrectomies is not reflective of most PNF cases, because the vast majority do not require nephrectomy and would disproportionately reflect patients with vascular complications or severe rejection.12 Biopsy series of patients with DGF are small or do not include PNF as an end point. In the largest DGF biopsy cohort, the most common diagnosis was ATI, as may be expected in this setting; however, a different or concurrent diagnosis was observed in 58%, demonstrating a need to biopsy patients with DGF.13

Other studies have examined factors influencing allograft loss, including surgical causes, allograft rejection, recurrent disease, and deaths with a functioning graft; however, these studies lack detailed kidney pathology data.14 Similar to our study, ECD donor status, impaired renal function at the time of death, and donor age have been previously associated with an increase in allograft loss.5,12,15 Oxalate deposition in allografts has been reported as a poor prognostic indicator, although not specifically in the context of PNF. Calcium oxalate deposition is relatively common in the early posttransplant period, with nearly 20% of allografts at 3 months.16 Patients with ESKD can have high serum concentrations of oxalate that can deposit within a new allograft, or rarely, oxalate deposition in an allograft may be the first recognition of primary hyperoxaluria in patients with an unknown etiology of ESKD.17 This was not a factor observed in this study, however, because none of the patients had a history of primary hyperoxaluria.

Because of the relatively small number of patients with PNF available for analysis, there are identified trends which may merit further investigation. IgA nephropathy showed a trend toward association with PNF that did not reach statistical significance (OR = 2.92, 95% CI: 0.87–9.79, P = 0.08) and may warrant evaluation in larger cohorts. Tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and the total number of diagnoses were selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator but did not reach significance in the refit model. Taken together, these data indicate cortical infarction and ABMR, superimposed on a background of advanced donor arteriosclerosis, and were the most prominent pathologic hallmarks of vascular- and immune-mediated injury associated with PNF.

A limitation of this study is its retrospective nature with clinical data limited to that available in the SRTR database. Referral and selection bias may occur in that all cases were evaluated at a single reference laboratory, although it includes patients from 48 states, in both rural and urban communities. The data may underrepresent vascular complications, because these patients would be less likely to undergo kidney biopsy. The cause of ESKD was not biopsy-proven in all patients. In addition, donor biopsies were not available for histopathologic comparisons. Although the sample size is small for PNF, we included all patients that we had available for analysis, with the unique aspect of the study being inclusion of kidney biopsy data to determine diagnoses associated with PNF.

Conclusion

Multiple clinical factors are associated with PNF, including increased donor age and donor terminal creatinine. Patients with DGF progressing to PNF more commonly showed cortical necrosis, donor-derived vascular disease, oxalate nephropathy, and had a higher frequency of ABMR. Therefore, identification of these entities on biopsy may identify patients at highest risk of graft failure.
