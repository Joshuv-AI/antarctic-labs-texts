Role of artificial intelligence in diagnosing pericoronal radiolucency
Authors: M. Madhumitha, Devika S. Pillai, Pradeep Kumar Yadalam, Prasanthi Sitaraman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial Intelligence (AI) significantly enhances the diagnosis of pericoronal radiolucency by accurately interpreting dental radiographs. Through advanced algorithms, AI can identify early signs of abnormalities near unerupted teeth. This helps clinicians differentiate between benign and malignant conditions, leading to more informed decisions; improved treatment plans, ultimately benefiting patient care and outcomes.Methodology: A total of 2500 radiographs were screened of which 1070 radiographs were used in the study. 315 images of pericoronal radiolucency in mandibular third molars and 755 images of the normal mandibular third molars were included. The AI algorithms employed in the study were Logistic regression and Naive Bayes. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, F1, AUC-ROC curve were used for performance evaluation.ResultsThis study found that Logistic regression model showed slightly higher accuracy than Naive Bayes model in predicting peri coronal radiolucency. In performance prediction for logistic regression model in predicting pericoronal radiolucency in third molars in 315 images, showed a slightly higher rate of prediction of 58.3 %, whereas, Naive Bayes model showed a comparatively lower prediction of pericoronal radiolucency, 52.2 %. During performance evaluation, Logistic regression performed better in CA, F1, and Recall, and Naive Bayes performed better in AUC and Precision model.ConclusionThe current study demonstrated that Logistic regression have slightly highest accuracy in detecting pericoronal radiolucency in digital orthopantomogram images, which is consistent with the normal radiographic evaluation. Also, the Naive Bayes algorithm showed a fairly considerable performance in the classification of pericoronal radiolucencies.

Introduction

The crowns of unerupted teeth are normally surrounded by the dental follicle, a remnant of the enamel forming organ that is lined by reduced enamel epithelium.1 The pericoronal radiolucency is a radiolucent area surrounding the crown structure of an impacted or erupting tooth, which is seen sometimes as a normal feature or because of a pathological condition.2 Pathological conditions such as cysts and odontogenic tumors present with pericoronal radiolucency.3 A normal dental follicle, which radiographically appears as a uniformly homogenous radiolucent area around the crown of a developing tooth with thin radiopaque border.4 However, the term is more commonly applied to describe larger radiolucent areas that may be linked to pathological conditions. During a routine radiographic examination, pericoronal radiolucencies are the most common pathologies to be identified. They can be found in unerupted, impacted, or embedded teeth.5

Any pathological process associated with the dental follicle can be pre-diagnosed with disturbances in the pericoronal radiolucency, which can appear as a widening or enlargement in the pericoronal space.6 Although other pathologies present as radiographic findings, dentigerous cysts are the most common type of pathology associated with pericoronal enlargement. Guidelines to differentiate between a normal and an abnormal dental follicle space include: pericoronal space exceeding 2.5 mm for teeth other than maxillary canines on periapical radiographs or 3 mm in panoramic radiographs or follicular radiolucency exceeding 2.5 cm.7 The most common pathology with pericoronal radiolucency is dentigerous cyst. Among young adults, the common condition associated with pericoronal radiolucency is impacted tooth.8 According to research, one in every five third molars of maxillary and mandibular teeth are impacted. The decision to extract an impacted tooth becomes easier when there are clear signs and symptoms of disease, but it is more difficult when the patient shows no symptoms.9

In recent years, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into healthcare has revolutionized diagnostic processes, particularly in radiology. Artificial Intelligence (AI) algorithms, AI algorithms, especially machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models, have shown immense potential in automating image analysis, reducing diagnostic errors, and enhancing the accuracy of disease detection.10 Improved early detection of cystic lesions using artificial intelligence (AI) can significantly enhance patient outcomes by differentiating between benign and potentially malignant cystic lesions with slightly higher accuracy, enabling clinicians to choose appropriate interventions (e.g., monitoring vs. biopsy or surgery) earlier and more confidently. Early and accurate identification allows for timely treatment before lesions enlarge, rupture, or become infected, thereby reducing risks such as bleeding, infection, or malignancy progression. AI-driven imaging analysis provides detailed insights into lesion size, location, and relation to nearby structures, helping surgeons plan less invasive, more precise procedures, and potentially avoid unnecessary surgeries. These are highly accurate in analyzing dental radiographs, and detecting subtle radiolucent areas around tooth crowns. The incorporation of AI and machine learning (ML) technologies into medical imaging represents a breakthrough in healthcare. These tools have the potential to transform how clinicians diagnose and treat a wide range of medical conditions, including uncommon yet significant disorders.11 In dentistry, General dentists frequently encounter pericoronal radiolucencies in routine Orthopantamograms(OPGs) hence this technology improves diagnostic precision, minimizes human error, and helps dental professionals identify early signs of disease that might otherwise be missed. The use of artificial intelligence in radiographic interpretation can help streamline the diagnostic workflow by decreasing both the radiologist's workload and the time required for image analysis.12 AI's capability to handle large amounts of data and deliver consistent outcomes also supports treatment planning, making it an essential tool in contemporary dental diagnostics.13 Despite growing applications of AI in dental diagnostics, limited studies have compared simple classification models like logistic regression and Naive Bayes for detecting pericoronal radiolucency in routine OPGs. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the performance of two interpretable machine learning models in the automated classification of pericoronal radiolucencies in routine dental radiographs and it aimed to compare the diagnostic accuracy of logistic regression and Naive Bayes algorithms in identifying pericoronal radiolucency in mandibular third molars using digital orthopantomograms. These simple models were chosen in this study for their interpretability, lower computational cost, or to assess baseline AI performance before applying deep learning.

Materials and methods

The present study utilized digital orthopantomograms (OPG) that were obtained from the archives of Department of Oral Medicine and Radiology at Saveetha Dental College & Hospitals, Chennai. The OPGs showing pericoronal pathologies were included and those showing artifacts and distortions were excluded. The OPGs were taken using Carestream CS 8100 unit with exposure parameters of 120 kVp, 8 mA and 4 s. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional ethical committee with reference number; IHEC/SDC/OMED-2204/24/299.

A total of 1070 digital OPGs were screened by convenient sampling and dataset including 315 images of pericoronal radiolucency in mandibular third molars and 755 images of the normal mandibular third molar were included. The images were independently reviewed and labeled as either “pericoronal radiolucency” or “normal third molars” by two board-certified oral and maxillofacial radiologists. Both reviewers were blinded to patient information and to each other's assessments. In cases of disagreement, a third experienced radiologist acted as an adjudicator to resolve discrepancies and reach consensus. The classification was based on established radiographic diagnostic criteria as outlined in White & Pharoah's Oral Radiology: Principles and Interpretation, 8th Edition, including factors like the size of the radiolucency (>3 mm), corticated borders, and the relationship to the crown of impacted or unerupted teeth. For image size standardization, the radiographs were cropped using the Snipping Tool integrated within the Microsoft Windows 11 operating system (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). The collected images were further processed by image preparation, image embedding, and a ML approach.

The study utilized a machine learning (ML) approach to analyze the annotated images, employing Orange Software, Version 3.35.0 (released in 2023 by the University of Ljubljana, Slovenia), a cross-platform, open-source AI tool for data visualization. This software supports a variety of tasks, including data loading, transformation, interactive visualization, model inference, and model visualization. Orange is a component-based visual programming package designed for data visualization, machine learning, data mining, and data analysis.

Orange is a data mining framework that offers tools for clustering, classification, and interactive visualization of both data and models. It also includes an image analytics add-on with image-specific extensions. Cross-validation of the dataset was conducted using three machine learning models—logistic regression, neural networks, and Naïve Bayes—to achieve reliable results.14

Algorithms used

Logistic regression: This classification algorithm is designed for categorical response variables and seeks to determine the relationship between features and the likelihood of a specific binary outcome.

Neural Network: In this step, a neural network model is constructed to classify the images. Neural networks are machine learning models capable of learning intricate relationships between predictors and outcomes.

Naïve Bayes: In this step, a Naïve Bayes model is developed to classify the images. Naïve Bayes is a probabilistic classifier based on the assumption that the predictors are independent of each other.

Confusion matrix: A confusion matrix is a table that summarizes a classifier's performance by displaying the counts of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). This matrix is useful for calculating metrics such as precision, recall, and F1 score, and it helps visualize how well the classifier is performing.

Accuracy: Accuracy measures an algorithm's capability to distinguish between diseased and healthy cases. It is computed using the formula (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN), where TP stands for true positives, TN for true negatives, FP for false positives, and FN for false negatives.

Precision: Precision represents the proportion of true positives (TP) among all predicted positives (TP + FP). It is calculated using the formula TP/(TP + FP).

Recall: Recall measures the proportion of true positives (TP) out of all actual positives (TP + FN) and is calculated using the formula TP/(TP + FN). A good classifier should exhibit high precision and recall. Ideally, both precision and recall would be 1, indicating no false positives (FP) or false negatives (FN). Since achieving this is often impractical, a metric that combines both precision and recall is necessary.

F1 Score: The F1 score is a metric that takes into account both precision and recall. It is calculated as 2 ∗ (precision ∗ recall)/(precision + recall). The F1 score ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 representing the best possible performance. A score of 0.5 indicates that the classifier's performance is equivalent to random guessing.

Performance evaluation: The primary performance metric for assessing the classification models was the AUC-ROC curve (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), which is used to evaluate binary classification models. This metric measures the model's ability to differentiate between two classes: the positive class (cases of interest) and the negative class (non-cases). The ROC curve provides a graphical depiction of a model's performance across various discrimination thresholds, plotting the true positive rate (TPR) or sensitivity against the false positive rate (FPR).

TPR or Sensitivity: This metric assesses the percentage of actual positive cases that the model correctly identifies as positive. It is the ratio of true positives to the total number of actual positives. The true positive rate (TPR) is computed using the formula TP/(TP + FN).

FPR: This metric measures the proportion of actual negative cases that the model incorrectly classifies as positive. It is the ratio of false positives to the total number of actual negatives.

Specificity: Specificity refers to the ML model's ability to correctly identify true negative cases, also known as the true negative rate. It is calculated using the formula TN/(TN + FP).

Area under the curve (AUC) calculation: The AUC-ROC metric measures the area under the ROC curve, reflecting the model's ability to distinguish between classes. Its range is from 0 to 1, with the following interpretations: AUC = 0.5 indicates that the model performs no better than random guessing; AUC > 0.5 means the model has some discriminatory power, with higher AUC values showing better performance; and AUC = 1 signifies perfect discrimination, where the model accurately identifies all positive cases without misclassifying any negative cases. A high AUC-ROC score, such as in medical diagnostics, suggests that the model effectively identifies conditions like elongated styloid processes (high sensitivity) while minimizing false positives (high specificity), which is important for avoiding unnecessary treatments.

Classification Accuracy(CA): It is the percentage of correct predictions made by the model out of the total number of predictions. Accuracy = TP + TN/TP + TN + FP + FN.

Discussion

Summary of findings of the current study

This study investigated the application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques for the detection of pericoronal radiolucencies on panoramic radiographs. Among the three models tested, Logistic Regression demonstrated superior predictive capability when compared to Naïve Bayes. While evaluating 315 radiographic images, Logistic Regression achieved a slightly higher prediction rate, correctly identifying more cases of pericoronal radiolucency than Naïve Bayes. Logistic Regression also showed higher prediction than Naïve Bayes in terms of classification accuracy (CA), area under the curve (AUC), F1 score, and recall, highlighting its strength in correctly identifying true positive cases. In contrast, Naïve Bayes showed slightly higher precision, indicating a lower incidence of false positives. These findings emphasize the broader diagnostic strength of Logistic Regression while acknowledging the specific advantage of Naïve Bayes in minimizing over-diagnosis.

Comparison with previous studies

The strong performance of the Logistic Regression model can be attributed to its capacity for handling binary classification tasks effectively, especially in datasets with structured variables like radiographic features. Its transparency and ease of interpretation also make it favorable in medical environments, where understanding model decisions is vital.15 Similar success with Logistic Regression has been reported in other healthcare applications, such as cancer diagnostics, reinforcing its utility in structured clinical datasets.16 Its ability to capture underlying data relationships likely contributed to better identification of pericoronal radiolucencies in this study. The model's higher recall is particularly significant in a clinical context, as it aids in minimizing missed diagnoses.17

The role of AI in dental imaging has expanded substantially in recent years. Pericoronal radiolucency, often associated with impacted teeth or cystic lesions, presents diagnostic challenges that benefit from enhanced interpretation accuracy.18,19 Traditionally, diagnosis relies heavily on clinicians’ experience and subjective interpretation, which may lead to variability. AI-driven tools can address these shortcomings by offering consistent and objective analysis.20

Previous literature also supports the integration of AI in dental diagnostics. For example, Lee et al. demonstrated the efficacy of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in identifying periapical lesions in dental radiographs, showing improved accuracy compared to traditional diagnostic methods.21 Similarly, Liu et al. utilized deep learning techniques to detect dental caries and other abnormalities, indicating the potential of AI to identify subtle radiographic signs, including those indicative of pericoronal radiolucency.22

Clinical implications

This study underscores the practicality of incorporating interpretable ML models such as Logistic Regression in clinical dental workflows. Given its relatively simple architecture and transparent decision-making, Logistic Regression is well-suited for clinical use, especially in scenarios where trust and explainability are essential. Its higher recall is advantageous in ensuring more cases are detected, ultimately reducing the likelihood of missed pathology. Furthermore, the implementation of a no-code ML platform like Orange facilitates the engagement of dental professionals who may not have formal training in programming. The use of actual clinical orthopantomographs enhances ecological validity, demonstrating the feasibility of deploying such tools in everyday dental practice. By assessing performance across multiple metrics rather than relying solely on accuracy, this study provides a more nuanced understanding of model capabilities. The structured and reproducible ML workflow developed here can be a valuable resource for further research and clinical translation.

Limitations

Despite promising results, this study has several notable limitations. All radiographic images were sourced from a single institution, which limits the generalizability of the findings due to potential differences in imaging protocols, equipment, and population characteristics. The absence of external validation using datasets from multiple institutions further restricts the applicability of the models to broader clinical settings. Although the models demonstrated diagnostic potential, their performance may not yet be sufficient for routine clinical use, particularly in the absence of predefined thresholds for identifying high-risk cases. Moreover, the study did not conduct statistical significance testing to compare the models, which weakens the robustness of the comparative analysis. The retrospective cross-sectional design limits conclusions regarding the predictive value of the models over time and does not provide insights into lesion progression or treatment outcomes, emphasizing the need for prospective longitudinal studies.23 Additionally, the labeling process may be subject to inter-observer variability or diagnostic bias, especially in borderline cases, and could be improved with a blinded multi-rater or consensus-based approach. The specific features used for model training (such as pixel intensity or texture descriptors) were not explicitly described, affecting reproducibility. Furthermore, the exclusion of patient demographic and clinical variables may reduce the model's diagnostic performance and limit its applicability in real-world scenarios.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of artificial intelligence in improving the detection of pericoronal radiolucency. Logistic regression achieved better accuracy and recall, suggesting its suitability for settings where early recognition of pathology is essential. Naïve Bayes, on the other hand, showed higher precision, making it useful in situations where avoiding false positives is a priority. These findings indicate that both models bring complementary advantages to dental diagnostics. However, clinical application will require larger datasets, standardized diagnostic thresholds, and external validation. With further refinement, AI-based models could streamline radiographic interpretation, enhance diagnostic consistency, and support more confident clinical decision-making.
