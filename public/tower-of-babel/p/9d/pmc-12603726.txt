Effect of Anatomic Joint-Line Obliquity Prostheses on Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee Alignment in a Chinese Population
Authors: Poon Jerome Chi Wang, Qunn-jid Lee, Kenneth Wing-kin Law, Daniel Wai-yip Wong, Cho Pong Lo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification describes knee phenotypes based on limb alignment and joint-line obliquity (JLO). This study evaluates CPAK distribution in the Hong Kong population and assesses how prosthesis design affects CPAK reproducibility and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients who underwent navigated total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with adjusted mechanical alignment in a single institution in 2021. First, we analyzed the epidemiology of CPAK phenotypes in our population. Second, patients were divided into 2 propensity score-matched groups based on implant design: Journey II BCS TKA (JLO prothesis) vs Persona PS TKA (joint line neutral prothesis). Radiological outcomes (mechanical hip–knee–ankle angle, lateral distal femoral angle, medial proximal tibial angle) and clinical outcomes (range of motion, Knee Society Knee Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index score, Forgotten Joint Score) assessed preoperatively and at 6 and 12 months postoperatively.ResultsOur CPAK distribution was comparable to that of other East Asian populations. The Journey II BCS TKA more effectively restored JLO and CPAK phenotypes compared to the Persona PS TKA. However, there was no statistically significant clinical correlation between the restoration of JLO, arithmetic HKA, and CPAK.ConclusionsAlthough radiological restoration improves with JLO prostheses, short-term clinical outcomes remain unaffected.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an established and effective treatment for knee osteoarthritis, with reported implant survivorship rates of 95% at 20 years [1]. Over the past decade, there has been increasing interest in optimizing implant positioning to further improve prosthesis longevity and functional outcomes. Mechanical alignment has long been the gold standard in TKA, demonstrating reliable long-term survivorship [2,3]. Anatomic alignment, introduced by Hungerford and Krackow to replicate native joint-line obliquity (JLO), produced inconsistent results when applied universally [4]. More recently, kinematic alignment has gained popularity, aiming to restore constitutional limb and joint-line alignment. Emerging evidence suggests kinematic alignment offers superior short-term function and PROMs without compromising implant survivorship [5].

To better understand coronal alignment variability, MacDessi et al. [6] proposed the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification, which incorporates native limb alignment and JLO to provide a practical framework for categorizing knee phenotypes and guiding alignment strategies through soft tissue balance optimization. Despite its increasing adoption, limited research has examined whether restoring native CPAK phenotypes improves clinical outcomes, and studies on the reproducibility of CPAK in relation to outcomes remain scarce. Recent work by Valtanen et al. [7] showed that a dynamic, force-controlled, gap-balancing technique in posterior-stabilized TKA improved short-term outcomes, suggesting that respecting individual knee anatomy may yield functional benefits. These findings support growing interest in alignment strategies beyond traditional mechanical alignment. Based on this, we hypothesize that restoring the native CPAK phenotype may similarly enhance clinical outcomes, particularly when paired with implant designs accommodating anatomic JLO. Furthermore, if CPAK restoration is shown to correlate with improved clinical outcomes, CPAK classification could serve as a practical tool to guide alignment strategies and implant selection in future clinical practice.

Modifications of the design of prosthesis have been introduced to accommodate these different concepts of coronal alignment. One specific modification is the prosthesis with built-in anatomic JLO. The femoral implant has asymmetric distal condyle thickness, and the tibial insert also has a reciprocal thickness asymmetry; a 3′ degree of anatomic JLO is produced by this combination [8].

The first objective of this study is to compare the distribution of CPAK types in our population with that reported in a Chinese cohort by Gao et al. [9], given the shared ethnic background. We hypothesize that our cohort will exhibit a similar CPAK phenotype distribution to the Chinese population but may differ from the patterns described in MacDessi’s original study. Notably, Tang et al. [10] reported that the Chinese population in Hong Kong demonstrates a higher prevalence of constitutional genu varus compared with Caucasian cohorts. This highlights the importance of considering population-specific anatomy when evaluating implant design and alignment strategies. If regional phenotypes are found to be comparable, this could support the cross-application of analytic models and clinical strategies developed in 1 population to the other.

The second objective is to evaluate whether prosthetic designs incorporating anatomic JLO more effectively restore native CPAK alignment than conventional designs. We hypothesize that such implants will more accurately replicate preoperative CPAK phenotypes. As an extension of this, we will assess whether restoration of native CPAK alignment is associated with improved short-term clinical outcomes.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective case–control study compared the clinical and radiological outcomes of 2 TKA implant designs, namely Journey II BCS (Smith & Nephew, London, UK) and Persona PS (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, Indiana), performed at our institution. The Journey II BCS implant features a built-in JLO (JLO prothesis), with approximately 3° of coronal JLO (Fig. 1), whereas the Persona PS implant employs a neutral joint line design (joint line neutral [JLN] prothesis).Figure 1Illustration of joint line obliquity design and MPTA calculation in Journey II.

Illustration of joint line obliquity design and MPTA calculation in Journey II.

A total of 403 TKAs were conducted by 5 experienced joint surgeons, each with a minimum of 10 years of practice in joint replacement, between January and December 2021. All cases performed within this timeframe were included in the analysis. Surgeon implant preferences were evaluated and showed no statistically significant difference between groups (P = .83).

During this study period, prior to the adoption of robotic TKA at our local high-volume joint center, mechanical alignment was the standard approach, and each implant system was routinely paired with a distinct navigation platform (Journey II BCS with OrthoAlign and Persona PS with I-Assist), a factor that may have influenced alignment and clinical outcomes.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this retrospective cohort study was determined using prevalence data reported by MacDessi et al. [6] and Gao et al. [9]. In the Chinese population, the predominant CPAK phenotype is type 1, with a prevalence of 43.6% [9], compared with 19.4% in the cohort reported by MacDessi et al. [6]. Based on these figures, assuming a 1:1 enrollment ratio, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 57 cases per group [11]. To further increase the statistical power and account for potential data loss, we expanded the sample to 105 subjects per group.

Participants and variables

Patients who underwent TKA with a minimum follow-up of 1 year were included. Bilateral TKAs were allowed, with each knee considered an independent unit of analysis and analyzed separately in the statistical models. Only bicruciate-stabilized and posterior-stabilized implant designs were included, while cases requiring higher-constraint prostheses were excluded. TKAs performed using navigation systems other than OrthoAlign or I-Assist (eg, Stryker Articular Surface Mounted Navigation System) were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were a history of lower limb surgery, previous fractures, or bone loss exceeding 3 mm. Patients lost to follow-up within 1 year or without a postoperative lower limb scanogram within 1 year were also excluded.

The JLO and JLN prothesis groups were matched using 1:1 nearest neighbor propensity score matching (based on age, body mass index, and gender) with good covariate balance (standardized mean differences <0.1). The final matched cohort included 210 knees from 188 patients, with 105 knees in each design group. (Fig. 2).Figure 2Cohort flowchart.

Measurements

Two implant designs were evaluated in this study: the Journey II BCS TKA (JLO prothesis) and the Persona PS TKA (JLN prothesis). Preoperative weight-bearing, anterior–posterior scanograms of the lower limbs were obtained approximately 1 month before surgery for operative planning. Each preoperative radiograph was classified into 1 of the 9 CPAK categories.

As described by MacDessi et al. [6], the mechanical hip–knee–ankle (mHKA) angle was defined as the angle subtended by the mechanical axes of the femur and tibia. The mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA) was the lateral angle between the femoral mechanical axis and the distal femoral joint line, whereas the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) was the medial angle between the tibial mechanical axis and the proximal tibial joint line. The arithmetic HKA (aHKA) was calculated as: aHKA = MPTA – LDFA. A varus aHKA was defined as < −2°, valgus aHKA as > +2°, and neutral aHKA as 0° ± 2°. JLO was defined as the obliquity of the knee relative to the floor in double-leg stance: JLO = MPTA + LDFA. A sum of 180° ± 3° indicated a neutral joint line. Values >183° represented an apex-proximal joint line, whereas values <177° indicated an apex-distal joint line (Fig. 3).Figure 3Preoperative coronal film measurements.

Preoperative coronal film measurements.

Postoperative weight-bearing lower-limb scanograms were obtained at 6 months. The mHKA, LDFA, MPTA, aHKA, and JLO were measured using the same definitions as preoperatively. For postoperative LDFA, the distal reference was the inferior surface of the femoral component. For MPTA, the reference was the superior surface of the tibial component. According to the manufacturer’s design rationale (Journey II TKR Design Rationale, pp. 8 and 10) [8], the Journey II BCS implant incorporates a built-in 3° varus JLO. Because the polyethylene insert is radiolucent, the MPTA was measured at the tibial metal baseplate level, and a correction of 3° was subtracted to obtain the final MPTA. In contrast, for the Persona implant featuring a neutral insert with equal medial and lateral thickness, the measured MPTA directly corresponded to the superior surface of the tibial baseplate. The aHKA and JLO were then derived from the measured LDFA and corrected MPTA (Figs. 4 and 5).Figure 4Postoperative coronal film measurements of JLO prothesis.Figure 5Postoperative coronal film measurements of JLN prothesis.

Postoperative coronal film measurements of JLO prothesis.

Postoperative coronal film measurements of JLN prothesis.

Primary outcome was the percentage of our population falling into each of the CPAK types in the whole cohort.

Secondary outcome was the percentage of the total knee replacement patients that could restore the native coronal alignment according to the CPAK classification in each of the 2 implants. The clinical outcomes of patients of each implant in relation with the restoration of the native coronal alignment will also be assessed. Individual limb alignment was measured preoperatively and postoperatively, with detailed analyses conducted on the aHKA, JLO, and CPAK classification. Reproducibility was defined as the postoperative alignment falling within the same classification category as the corresponding preoperative aHKA, JLO, and CPAK.

Clinical outcomes, including Knee Society Knee Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index score, Forgotten Joint Score and range of motion, were recorded at approximately 1 month preoperatively and 1, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Assessments were conducted by joint specialty nurses in the outpatient specialty clinic using standardized questionnaires and a goniometer.

Radiographic protocol

All subjects underwent full-length, weight-bearing lower limb radiography using the Carestream DRX Evolution V1 system (Carestream Health, Rochester, NY) with automatic digital stitching enabled. Patients were positioned in an upright stance, distributing weight evenly across both lower limbs, with patellae oriented anteriorly and upper limbs resting comfortably at the sides. Sequential exposures were acquired from the pelvis to the ankle joints while the radiographer ensured patient stability and absence of motion. Following image acquisition, the radiologic technologist verified correct positioning, confirming central alignment of the patellae and parallel orientation of the joint lines, as well as the absence of motion artifacts. The system automatically stitched the sequential images to produce a single, continuous full-length radiograph. The stitched images were exported to the institutional clinical management system, and radiological parameters were measured using the eUnity platform (Diagnostic Imaging, Vermont).

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under spinal or general anesthesia using a standard medial parapatellar approach. The 2 implant systems utilized were the Journey II (Smith & Nephew) and the Persona (Zimmer Biomet). Navigation was performed with OrthoAlign for the Journey II cases and I-Assist for the Persona cases. All patients received adjusted mechanical alignment, initially targeting the mechanical axis. Postresection verification confirmed that femoral and tibial cuts were within 1° of varus or valgus relative to the mechanical axis. Only the cases achieving overall neutral alignment (0°) were included; deviations were excluded from analysis. Femoral rotational alignment was aimed parallel to the transepicondylar axis, and tibial rotational alignment was aimed toward the medial third of the tibial tubercle. Soft tissue balance was assessed intraoperatively, with final gaps within 2 mm medially and laterally in both flexion and extension. Surgical technique was consistent across the 2 implant groups.

Statistical methods

Logistic regression analysis was used to examine the association between the types of implants used and outcomes for propensity score matching. Independent samples t-test was used to compare the continuous variables between the radiological and clinical outcomes of the 2 implant designs and determine whether the difference was statistically significant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to analyze the normal distribution of data. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical data. Cohen’s d was calculated to assess the effect size for differences in continuous outcomes. The reliability of the measurements was validated by calculating intraclass correlation coefficients to assess interobserver and intraobserver agreement, using a 2-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. All statistical significance was defined as a P value of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using statistical software and findings were reported following the STROBE guidelines.

Postoperative analysis

Significant differences emerged postoperatively. The mean LDFA was 88.0° ± 2.3 in the JLO group compared to 90.7° ± 2.3 in the JLN group (P < .01; Cohen’s d = 1.17). The mean MPTA was 86.8° ± 2.7 for the JLO group vs 89.4° ± 2.8 for the JLN group (P < .01; Cohen’s d = 1.02). The JLO was significantly different at 174.8° ± 3.4 for the JLO group and 180.0° ± 3.6 for the JLN group (P < .01; Cohen’s d = 1.49). No significant difference was found in postoperative aHKA (−1.2° ± 3.2 for JLO vs −1.3° ± 3.7 for JLN, P = .95).

Distribution of aHKA and JLO types between implant designs

Preoperative analysis

The aHKA distribution for the JLO group was 33.3% neutral (35 cases), 61.0% varus (64 cases), and 5.7% valgus (6 cases). The JLN group showed 30.5% neutral (32 cases), 57.1% varus (60 cases), and 12.4% valgus (13 cases), with no significant difference between groups (P = .24). For JLO, 18.1% (19 cases) were neutral, 81.9% (86 cases) apex distal, and 0% apex proximal in the JLO group. The JLN group had 27.6% neutral (29 cases), 71.4% apex distal (75 cases), and 1.0% apex proximal (1 case), also without significant difference (P = .15). (Table 2)

Postoperative analysis

aHKA distributions remained similar between groups: 54.3% neutral (57 cases), 31.4% varus (33 cases), and 14.3% valgus (15 cases) in the JLO group; and 49.5% neutral (52 cases), 33.3% varus (35 cases), and 17.1% valgus (18 cases) in the JLN group (P = .755). However, postoperative JLO types differed significantly, with 26.7% neutral (28 cases), 72.4% apex distal (76 cases), and 1.0% apex proximal (1 case) in the JLO group, compared to 67.6% neutral (71 cases), 16.2% apex distal (17 cases), and 16.2% apex proximal (17 cases) in the JLN group (P < .01).

CPAK type distribution between implants

Preoperative analysis

CPAK types were similarly distributed between groups (P = .359). In the JLO group, type 1 was most common with 51.4% (54 cases), followed by type 2 at 25.7% (27 cases), and type 4 at 9.5% (10 cases). In the JLN group, type 1 accounted for 45.7% (48 cases), type 2 for 18.1% (19 cases), and type 5 for 12.4% (13 cases). (Figs. 7 and 8)Figure 7CPAK distribution of JLO design. Postoperative analysis

CPAK distribution differed significantly (P < .01). The JLO group was most frequently classified as type 2 (41.9%, 44 cases), type 1 (21.9%, 23 cases), and type 5 (11.4%, 12 cases). The JLN group showed a different distribution dominated by type 5 (35.2%, 37 cases), type 4 (21.9%, 23 cases), and type 6 (10.5%, 11 cases).

Reproducibility of aHKA and JLO between the 2 implants

The reproducibility of the aHKA was identical in both implant groups, with 45 cases (42.9%) achieving postoperative alignment within the same aHKA classification for both JLO and JLN protheses (P = 1.0). JLO reproducibility differed significantly, with 71 cases (67.6%) reproducible in the JLO group compared to 37 cases (35.2%) in the JLN group (P < .01). Similarly, CPAK classification reproducibility was significantly higher in the JLO group, with 34 cases (32.4%) maintaining their preoperative CPAK type postoperatively, vs only 15 cases (14.3%) in the JLN group (P = .002). Functional scoring

No significant differences in clinical outcomes were detected between patients with or without restoration of CPAK, JLO, or aHKA, either preoperatively or postoperatively. Effect sizes were uniformly small (Cohen’s d < 0.3), indicating negligible variation in Knee Society Knee Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index, Forgotten Joint Score, and ROM. Discussion

Epidemiology

The distribution of CPAK classifications varies significantly across different populations and geographic regions, emphasizing the need for population-specific approaches in surgical planning and implant selection. In our study, the preoperative CPAK distribution showed a predominance of type 1 alignment (48.6%), followed by type 2 (21.9%) and type 4 (10.5%). This contrasts markedly with MacDessi’s foundational study [6], which reported a predominance of type 2 (39.2%), with type 1 at only 19.4%, suggesting regional or ethnic differences in knee alignment patterns.

Our findings closely align with Gao et al.’s [9] study in China, which reported 43.6% type 1, 21.6% type 2, and 11.5% type 4, and with Toyooka et al.’s [12] study in Japan, showing an even higher prevalence of type 1 knees (53.8%) and significant type 2 (25.4%). These similarities suggest consistent alignment patterns within East Asian populations, which may be influenced by shared genetic and biomechanical factors.

In contrast, European studies show more varied distributions. Huber et al. [13] in Vienna reported a more balanced distribution of types 1 (28.1%), 2 (27.3%), and 3 (20.9%), while Dragosloveanu et al. [14] in Romania observed a dominance of type 1 (42.4%) but with a higher type 4 prevalence (16.2%). Western European cohorts, such as those studied by Leon-Muñoz et al. [15] in Spain and Sappey-Marinier et al. [16] in France, revealed moderate type 1 prevalence (30.7% and 33.4%, respectively) alongside notable proportions of types 4 and 5, possibly reflecting diverse genetic and environmental influences. Unique distributions are seen in other regions; Coetzee et al. [17] reported in South Africa a predominance of type 3 (28.6%) with a much lower type 1 prevalence (15.5%), possibly due to distinct biomechanical or genetic factors in African populations. In the United States, Steele et al. [18] noted a predominance of Type 2 (34.5%) and a lower frequency of type 1 (19.8%), similar to MacDessi’s findings.

Overall, these comparisons highlight substantial global variability in CPAK distributions, with our data aligning most closely with East Asian populations. Such variability underscores the importance of tailoring orthopaedic strategies, including surgical techniques and implant designs, to the dominant alignment phenotypes within specific populations. Moreover, these patterns provide valuable insights for future research exploring the genetic, biomechanical, and environmental factors influencing knee alignment worldwide.

CPAK radiological reproducibility

Few studies have investigated CPAK reproducibility from preoperative to postoperative phases. Prior reports using implants without built-in JLO found low reproducibility rates (5-14.6%) and a consistent shift from types 1-2 to types 4-5 [[19], [20], [21]], a trend also evident in our JLN cohort. In contrast, the JLO prothesis in our series produced a predominantly horizontal CPAK shift with higher restoration (23.3%), likely due to its built-in 3° varus slope.

Our reproducibility rates for aHKA with JLO prosthesis closely matched those reported by Park et al. [22], with overall statistical similarity. The JLO prothesis demonstrated superior preservation of joint line orientation (67.6% vs 35.2% with JLN), minimizing phenotypic changes. By comparison, JLN prothesis showed greater alterations in JLO, emphasizing the influence of implant design. Overall, the JLO design of the Journey II BCS TKA appears to enhance CPAK reproducibility and maintain native alignment more effectively than the JLN prothesis. These findings highlight the role of implant design in alignment outcomes, though whether CPAK restoration translates into improved long-term clinical benefit remains uncertain. Future studies directly comparing surgical techniques that replicate JLO with anatomically preshaped implants are warranted.

Functional outcomes

Despite improved reproducibility of CPAK with JLO prothesis, our study found no clear functional advantage in the short term. This disconnection between radiological alignment and patient-reported outcomes suggests that restoration of CPAK, aHKA, or JLO may not directly translate into meaningful clinical benefit. Several factors may account for this. Patient specific variables, such as prehabilitation, postoperative rehabilitation compliance, age, lifestyle, and baseline functional reserve, likely play a dominant role in recovery, potentially overshadowing subtle biomechanical improvements. The relatively short follow-up of 12 months may also be insufficient to capture differences, as functional outcomes often evolve over 2 years and may later decline [23].

Evidence from prior work supports this mixed picture. Agarwal et al. [20] and Sappey-Marinier et al. [16] reported no significant functional benefit with CPAK restoration, whereas Pangaud [21] and Konishi [24] observed subgroup-dependent differences. Notably, the JLO prosthesis (Journey II BCS) has been linked to improved postoperative range of motion and activities such as squatting or stair climbing [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]], yet these outcomes may reflect implant mechanics (eg, femoral rollback) rather than CPAK restoration itself. In our series, the only signal toward clinical impact was a trend toward higher Forgotten Joint Scores at 6 months with restored aHKA (d = 0.36, P = .07). While not statistically significant, this observation is hypothesis-generating and raises the possibility that restored mechanical alignment may confer early proprioceptive benefits.

Finally, interpretation is limited by potential confounding from prosthesis design and the use of different navigation systems, as well as by our focus on coronal alignment alone. Sagittal and axial alignment, together with rotational factors, were not assessed but may be equally important. Future studies incorporating multiplanar kinematic analysis, longer follow-up, and stratified subgroup evaluation will be critical to determine whether radiological restoration of CPAK ultimately predicts sustained improvements in function, patient satisfaction, and implant survivorship.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, its retrospective design may introduce inherent biases in data collection and analysis. Secondly, although the surgical technique was standardized, the use of 2 different navigation systems (OrthoAlign and I-Assist) could have introduced subtle discrepancies in implant positioning accuracy, potentially confounding analyses related to alignment precision and joint-line restoration. Future prospective studies using a single standardized navigation system could help mitigate this issue.

The inclusion of bilaterally operated knees may also have influenced functional scores, as outcomes from one side could affect perception and performance on the other. Furthermore, the analysis was limited to the coronal plane, excluding axial and sagittal alignments, thereby restricting the comprehensiveness of biomechanical interpretation. Most importantly, functional outcomes were assessed only up to 1 year postoperatively. Such a short follow-up period is insufficient to capture longer-term trends, late-emerging differences, or potential effects on implant survivorship.

In addition, differences in implant design, specifically the use of a bicruciate-stabilized design in one group vs a posterior-stabilized design in the other, may have influenced certain clinical outcomes. These variations in the level of constraint could alter joint kinematics and patient-reported outcomes, introducing another potential source of confounding.

Finally, this study did not include biomechanical or gait analysis, which could help clarify the relationship between CPAK reproducibility and functional performance.

Conclusions

Protheses with built-in joint-line obliquity better restore native knee alignment, especially in Asian populations with predominant varus phenotypes. Although short term functional outcomes show no significant differences, further prospective multicenter trails with standardized protocols, longer follow-up, and comprehensive multi-planar assessment are needed to determine whether CPAK restoration offers lasting improvements in function, satisfaction, and implant longevity.
