Evaluating the impact of chronic kidney disease and the triglyceride-glucose index on cardiovascular disease: mediation analysis in the NHANES
Authors: Heng Pei, Xin Su, Shouling Wu, Zhijun Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores the intricate relationship between Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), the Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index, and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD) in a U.S. adult population. It focuses on understanding how the TyG index, as a marker of insulin resistance, relates to cardiovascular risk in the presence of CKD.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning from 1999 to 2018. The study involved classifying stages of CKD, calculating the TyG index, and applying statistical analyses including logistic regression.ResultsThe investigation revealed that individuals with CKD, who comprised 49% males with an average age of 47 years, exhibited a higher incidence of CVD. The study demonstrated that before adjusting for the TyG index, the odds ratio (OR) for the association between CKD and CVD was 1.77. Importantly, the TyG index was found to mediate 10% of the association between CKD and CVD. Moreover, a significant synergistic interaction was observed between a high TyG index and CKD, with the combined presence of these conditions increasing the risk ratio for CVD to 3.01.ConclusionThe findings highlight the crucial role of insulin resistance in the link between CKD and CVD. The paper discusses the implications of chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction in CKD patients and the importance of the TyG index in assessing cardiovascular risk.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasingly recognized as a significant public health concern [1], primarily due to its profound association with cardiovascular disease (CVD) – the leading cause of global mortality [2]. The interplay between CKD and CVD is mediated through intricate mechanisms, notably the exacerbation of insulin resistance, a well-known risk factor for both conditions [3, 4]. Insulin resistance, often arising from CKD-associated metabolic dysregulations, significantly increases cardiovascular event rates in this population, underscoring the critical need for refined risk stratification and management strategies [5, 6].

In this context, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a promising biomarker for cardiovascular risk assessment [7–9]. The TyG index is a composite measure derived from fasting triglyceride and glucose levels and is considered a surrogate marker for insulin resistance [10]. Elevated levels of triglycerides and glucose are commonly observed in individuals with insulin resistance, a condition that often leads to lipid metabolism abnormalities, such as elevated triglycerides and low HDL cholesterol levels, both of which contribute to atherosclerosis—a primary cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [11, 12]. Associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and high blood pressure, insulin resistance can damage blood vessels and promote the development of atherosclerotic plaques, which increasing the risk of cardiovascular events [13, 14]. Importantly, the association between insulin resistance and cardiovascular disease is not exclusive to diabetic subjects. While insulin resistance is a hallmark of type 2 diabetes, it can also be present in individuals without diabetes, including those with metabolic syndrome or obesity. In these populations, insulin resistance can still contribute to an increased risk of CVD due to the same underlying mechanisms [15, 16]. Recent studies have indicated a significant correlation between the TyG index and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in non-diabetic, non-dialysis-dependent individuals with CKD [17–19]. This correlation, consistent across various demographic subgroups, highlights the index’s potential clinical applicability.

The relationship between CKD, the TyG index, and CVD risk is complex and multifactorial. CKD itself contributes to cardiovascular risk through various mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and dyslipidemia [20, 21]. These conditions can exacerbate insulin resistance, creating a vicious cycle that further elevates cardiovascular risk. Moreover, the TyG index, as an indicator of insulin resistance, captures the cumulative impact of these metabolic disturbances, making it a valuable marker for assessing cardiovascular risk in CKD patients. Our study aims to thoroughly investigate the relationship between CKD, the TyG index, and CVD in the U.S. adult population. We hypothesize that the TyG index notably mediates the association between CKD and CVD risk. Utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this cross-sectional study facilitates a comprehensive analysis spanning a wide demographic range. By elucidating these relationships, our research endeavors to refine risk stratification and management strategies, potentially reducing the CVD burden in individuals with CKD.

Methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a comprehensive, cross-sectional study assessing the health and nutritional status of adults and children across the United States. This extensive survey employs an integrated methodological approach, combining interviews, physical examinations, dietary analyses, and laboratory tests. The survey utilizes a stratified multistage probability sampling technique to ensure rigorous data collection. Conducted from 1999 to 2018, NHANES meticulously selected 19,318 participants. Adhering to ethical guidelines, informed consent was secured from all participants, with signed written consent forms. Furthermore, the study’s protocol rigorously complied with established ethical standards, safeguarding the integrity and ethical soundness of the research.

Outcomes and definitions

CKD stages were classified based on established guidelines: no CKD (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m^2 and ACR < 30 mg/g), CKD stages G1–G3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m^2 or eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m^2 with ACR ≥ 30 mg/g), and CKD stages G4 and G5 (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m^2). The TyG index was calculated using the formula: Ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2]. Participant characteristics, including age, sex, race-ethnicity, and poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), were self-reported. Smoking and drinking statuses were also self-reported and categorized as follows: smoking (never: <100 cigarettes lifetime, former: >100 cigarettes lifetime, not currently smoking; current: >100 cigarettes lifetime, smoking currently) and alcohol use (heavy: ≥3 drinks/day for females, ≥ 4 drinks/day for males, or binge drinking ≥ 5 days/month; moderate: ≥2 drinks/day for females, ≥ 3 drinks/day for males, or binge drinking ≥ 2 days/month; history of daily binge drinking).

Statistical analysis

To ensure statistical representativeness among U.S. adults, our analysis of national health statistics incorporated adjustments for oversampling, stratification, and clustering. We employed analysis of variance, weighted for sampling, for continuous variables to identify group differences. Rao-Scott χ2 analysis was used for categorical variables. Logistic regression models estimated hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals to assess associations between the Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index and adolescent obesity status. Model 1 adjusted CKD status, age, sex, self-reported race, PIR, smoking, and drinking status, while Model 2 additionally adjusted the TyG index. We assessed the TyG index’s mediation role by comparing models with and without this mediator.Additionally, we have added a sensitivity analysis to adjust for the use of serum uric acid, lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering, and antihypertensive medications to exclude their influence.

Additionally, to explore interactions, we included the product term of CKD status and TyG index tertiles. The hazard ratio and 95% CI for this term indicated multiplicative interaction, while relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and its 95% CI were used for additive interaction assessment. Participants were categorized into nine groups based on CKD status and TyG index tertiles for joint association analysis, compared against a reference group with normal CKD status and the lowest TyG tertile. This enabled risk ratio estimation for CVD across groups. Analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.2, Stata 17, and SAS 9.4.

Mediation analysis of TyG index on associations of CKD status with CVD/CHD/CHF/stroke

In accordance with our inclusion criteria, 2,122 cardiovascular disease (CVD) cases were identified within the NHANES in the United States. When comparing patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) to those without CKD, the odds ratio (OR) for CKD was 1.77 (95% CI: 1.50–2.10), indicating a heightened risk prior to adjusting for the Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index. Conversely, for non-CKD patients, the OR associated with the TyG index in CKD patients was 1.39 (95% CI: 1.24–1.55). The TyG index’s mediation of the CKD-CVD association was estimated at 10% (range: 8 − 12%) (Table 3).

Interaction and joint analysis of CKD status and TyG index with CVD/CHD/CHF/stroke

In the NHANES dataset, the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and high Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index is 1.354 (95% CI: -1.061 3.770), a range that includes zero, indicating a potential absence of significant additive interaction. However, the attributable proportion (AP) for CVD risk, calculated at 0.157 (95% CI: 0.083 0.231), suggests that around 15.7% of CVD risk may be attributed to the interaction between CKD and high TyG index. Furthermore, the synergy index (SI) is 1.216 (95% CI: 1.080 1.370), implying that the combined impact of CKD and high TyG index on CVD risk is greater than their individual effects, indicating a synergistic interaction. Despite the inconclusive RERI, the AP and SI findings suggest a notable synergistic effect elevating CVD risk when both CKD and high TyG index coexist (Table 4). Discussion

Our study identified a significant association between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD). This suggests the TyG index’s potential as a marker for assessing cardiovascular risk in CKD patients, particularly highlighting its role in reflecting insulin resistance.

Our study’s observation of a significant association between the TyG index and CVD risk in CKD patients is supported by similar findings in other studies. For example, a study involving a cohort from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study found that both the TyG index and eGFR levels were significantly associated with cardiovascular diseases. This study further highlights that individual with a higher TyG index and decreased eGFR had an elevated risk of cardiovascular diseases, suggesting a potential mediating role of renal function in the relationship between the TyG index and CVD risk [17]. Another study investigating the relationship between the TyG index and mortality in patients with CKD and coronary artery disease revealed a nonlinear association between the TyG index and the probability of in-hospital death. The risk of in-hospital death and one-year mortality increased with the TyG index, particularly when it was beyond certain threshold values [22]. This indicates the TyG index as a significant predictor of mortality in this specific patient population​​. These studies corroborate and expand upon our findings, suggesting that the TyG index is a robust predictor of cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in diverse populations with CKD. The consistency of these findings across different studies emphasizes the relevance of the TyG index in clinical settings, particularly in risk assessment and management of cardiovascular diseases in CKD patients. Our study broadens these insights by employing the NHANES dataset, enhancing the generalizability of the results to the broader U.S. adult population and thereby filling a gap in current research.

The TyG index, indicative of insulin resistance, may play a multifaceted role in exacerbating cardiovascular risks in CKD patients. Insulin resistance in CKD is often accompanied by chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. Chronic low-grade inflammation, as evidenced by elevated levels of inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP), is a common feature in CKD and is closely linked to CVD risk [23–25]. This inflammatory state can further impair endothelial function, leading to decreased vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and enhanced leukocyte adhesion – all key steps in the development of atherosclerosis [26–28]. Additionally, insulin resistance is associated with hypertension, another critical risk factor for CVD. In CKD patients, altered insulin signaling can contribute to sodium retention and increased sympathetic nervous system activity, leading to elevated blood pressure levels [29, 30]. Hypertension, in turn, imposes additional strain on the cardiovascular system, exacerbating the risk of heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke [31, 32]. Emerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota may play a critical role in the link between the TyG index and CVD. The gut microbiota may represent a bridge, whereby changes in microbial composition and function exacerbate metabolic disturbances and cardiovascular outcomes [33, 34].

Moreover, CKD itself can contribute to altered lipid metabolism and increased cardiovascular risk. Impaired kidney function can lead to changes in lipoprotein metabolism, resulting in an accumulation of atherogenic lipoproteins [35, 36]. High uric acid levels can lead to endothelial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and inflammation, all of which are critical pathways in the development of CVD [37–39]. Uric acid may thus contribute to the heightened cardiovascular risk observed in CKD patients with a high TyG index.

The progressive nature of CKD, characterized by declining glomerular filtration rate (GFR), further compounds these metabolic disturbances, elevating the risk of CVD [40, 41]. The interplay of these factors – dyslipidemia, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and hypertension – creates a milieu conducive to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in CKD patients. The TyG index, by capturing the essence of insulin resistance, thus serves as a valuable composite marker reflecting these interconnected pathways. Interventional studies focusing on improving insulin sensitivity, managing dyslipidemia, and reducing inflammation may yield novel strategies to mitigate CVD risk in CKD patients [42–45]. Occlusive renal disease, characterized by the narrowing or blockage of renal arteries, can exacerbate kidney dysfunction and contribute to systemic metabolic disturbances. This condition can lead to increased blood pressure, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia, thereby elevating the TyG index and cardiovascular risk [46].

In conclusion, our study benefits from the use of NHANES data, which offers a diverse and representative sample of the U.S. adult population. This enhances the generalizability of our findings. However, the cross-sectional design of the study introduces limitations, particularly in establishing causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are required to validate the directionality and causality of the associations observed in our research. Our study underscores the importance of the TyG index as a potential tool for cardiovascular risk assessment in CKD patients. The clinical implications of our study are substantial. Regular monitoring of the TyG index in CKD patients could facilitate early identification of individuals at higher risk for CVD. This proactive approach could guide clinicians in implementing targeted interventions to mitigate this risk. Moreover, our findings suggest that the TyG index could serve as a practical tool in clinical settings, assisting in the stratification of CVD risk among CKD patients. As CKD remains a significant public health challenge, comprehending and utilizing such biomarkers could be pivotal in improving cardiovascular outcomes in this vulnerable population [47, 48]. Understanding the interplay between metabolic disturbances and kidney function opens new avenues for research and clinical practice, potentially leading to better management strategies for CKD patients at risk of CVD.

Conclusion

This study, utilizing NHANES data, provides a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in a diverse U.S. adult population. We found a significant association between the TyG index and increased CVD risk in CKD patients, highlighting the TyG index’s potential as a valuable marker for cardiovascular risk assessment. These findings underscore the clinical importance of monitoring the TyG index in CKD patients to identify those at higher risk for CVD and guide targeted interventions. While our study’s cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, it lays the groundwork for future longitudinal research to establish causality and explore effective management strategies for CKD patients at risk of CVD. This research contributes significantly to public health, enhancing our understanding of the interplay between metabolic disturbances and kidney function, and opening new avenues for improving cardiovascular outcomes in CKD patients.
