Topology optimization design of excavator working device based on equivalent static loads
Authors: He Zhang, Xiao-dong Shao, Min-min Jia, Xiao-bo Ge, Yong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the dual requirements of dynamic topology optimization and static strength in the design of excavator working devices, this paper proposes a dynamic topology optimization method based on equivalent static loads. First, a rigid-flexible coupling dynamic model of the working device is constructed under combined excavation conditions to analyze the dynamic response of the arm in terms of stress and deformation. Then, local element stress constraints are converted into global constraints using the P-norm method. A topology optimization model is established with the minimization of the arm’s maximum flexibility as the objective function, constrained by the P-norm stress and volume fraction. Finally, dynamic topology optimization of the arm is performed using the equivalent static loads method, and the topological structure of the arm is redesigned with consideration for manufacturability. Finite element simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves a 24.63% reduction in the mass of the arm, while the maximum stress increases by only 5.26% and remains below the material’s allowable limit, thereby fulfilling the design requirements.

Introduction

With the rapid development of the global economy and urbanization, the demand for infrastructure construction continues to increase, leading to a growing need for engineering machinery. Excavators, as typical engineering machinery products, are widely used in infrastructure construction, mining, water conservancy projects, and other fields1. However, traditional excavator design methods suffer from issues such as over-design, high energy consumption, and high emissions, which cannot meet the needs of the green and low-carbon transformation of the manufacturing industry and modern engineering construction. Topology optimization aims to obtain accurate force transmission paths and optimal material distribution while satisfying specified design constraints2. Topology optimization design plays a crucial role in lightweight and green design, enabling the reduction of component mass under certain constraints while effectively lowering waste emissions and resource consumption throughout the component’s lifecycle3. The working device, a large welded structural component accounting for approximately 20% of the total weight of an excavator, is a vital part of excavator operations and has a significant environmental impact and resource consumption throughout its lifecycle. Therefore, conducting research on topology optimization methods for excavator working device holds important theoretical significance for the green and low-carbon development of the complex equipment manufacturing industry.

Based on different model representations, continuum topology optimization methods are divided into two categories: material layout evolution and geometric shape evolution4. Material layout evolution methods mainly include homogenization method5, variable density method6–9, and evolutionary structural optimization (ESO)10–12, which discretize a given design domain using a finite number of density elements. Geometric shape evolution methods primarily encompass level set method13–15 and moving morphable components (MMC) method16,17, which utilize high-dimensional functions to express the evolution process of structural topology. During the conceptual design phase of a product, topology optimization primarily addresses the distribution of materials within a given design space under specified loads and boundary conditions. Yu et al.18 constructed a parametric finite element model of an excavator based on an assembly method considering the spatial relationship between global and local coordinate systems, conducted strength analysis and structural optimization on the excavator boom, effectively reducing stress concentration issues. Ning et al.19 established an optimization model for an excavator’s arm with the minimum strain energy as the design objective and volume fraction as the constraint, resulting in a new arm structure. Li et al.20 studied a topology optimization model with the minimum compliance as the design objective and volume fraction as the design constraint, yielding a new arm structure. Huo et al.21 built an optimization model for the arm based on equivalent static loads, with the minimum compliance as the optimization objective and volume fraction as the constraint, improving the structural strength of the arm. Zhao et al.22 constructed an optimization model with the minimum mass of the excavator’s second-level arm as the objective and strength and stiffness as constraints, achieving a 33.6% mass reduction after optimization. Wang et al.23 took the rear support of the arm as the research object and established an optimization model with the minimum volume as the design objective and stress as the constraint, resulting in a 50–100 MPa stress reduction in the optimized support. The above topology optimization methods for excavator working device have the following two shortcomings: (1) In actual excavation scenarios, the loads on the working device are dynamically changing, and optimization based on structural static loads cannot meet the requirements of the excavation working range; (2) The proposed topology optimization methods for working device mostly use global responses such as mass, volume, and strain energy as optimization objectives or constraints, leading to high optimization efficiency, but the obtained topology structures often fail to meet static strength requirements. Therefore, considering stress constraints in the dynamic topology optimization design of working device has certain engineering value. Although theoretically, directly performing nonlinear dynamic topology optimization on working device is feasible, the calculation process is complex, inefficient, and difficult to converge, and it may even be impossible to obtain an ideal optimized configuration24. To improve the efficiency of continuum dynamic topology optimization, CHOI et al.25 proposed the equivalent static loads method, which discretizes dynamic optimization problems into multiple static optimization problems by constructing equivalent static loads. Subsequent studies have verified the feasibility of this method. Xu et al.26 conducted lightweight research on vibrating screens, constructed a sub-model of the screen box frame based on the equivalent static loads method for static topology optimization, and achieved a 37.9% reduction in maximum stress. Li et al.27 addressed the nonlinearity of elastoplastic materials by considering a modulus ratio factor in the equivalent static loads calculation process, which was verified through engineering examples to yield smaller plastic strains. Zhang et al.28 took the machine tool carriage as the research object and performed topology optimization design based on the equivalent static loads method to obtain the optimal layout of the box’s rib plates. Rui et al.29 studied the derivation methods of equivalent static loads for different dynamic responses and verified through a crank-connecting rod mechanism that dynamic optimization yields better results compared to static optimization. Gharaibeh et al.30 discussed the mathematical basis for replacing impact loads with equivalent static loads and validated the effectiveness of the equivalent static loads method through experimental measurement data and dynamic finite element analysis (FEA) simulation results. Kim et al.31 studied an optimization solution for hinge deformation caused by impact during the transportation of refrigerators based on the equivalent static loads method and verified it through actual drop tests. Existing studies on topology optimization using equivalent static loads have predominantly relied on standard benchmark cases to validate methodological effectiveness, while demonstrating a notable lack of application to real-world engineering problems.

To meet the requirements of dynamic topology optimization and static strength for the working device of excavators, this paper proposes a dynamic topology optimization method for the working device of excavators based on equivalent static loads. Firstly, under complex excavation conditions, a rigid-flexible coupling dynamic model of the working device is constructed to analyze the dynamic response of the stress and deformation of the arm. The local element stress constraints are converted into global constraints through the P-norm. Taking the minimization of the maximum flexibility of the arm as the optimization objective, and the P-norm stress and volume fraction as the constraint conditions, a topology optimization model for the working device is established. Finally, based on the equivalent static load method, dynamic topology optimization design is carried out for the arm, taking into account the manufacturing process for the redesign of the arm’s topology structure. The effectiveness of the proposed method is verified through finite element simulations.

Topology optimization method for continua based on equivalent static loads

Equivalent static loads principle

At a specific moment, under the action of dynamic loads, a structure undergoes deformation, resulting in the formation of a displacement field. At that moment, if there exists a static load that, when applied to the structure, produces an identical displacement field as that produced by the dynamic loads, this static load is referred to as the equivalent static loads. Based on the finite element theory, element stresses can be obtained by calculating the nodal displacements. Therefore, during the equivalency process of the static load, an identical stress field is also obtained. Topology optimization process

The topology optimization process for continua based on equivalent static loads is illustrated in Fig. 2. The specific steps are as follows:Fig. 2Topology optimization process for continua based on equivalent static loads.

Topology optimization process for continua based on equivalent static loads.

Step 1: Construct a rigid-flexible coupling model containing the structure to be optimized, perform dynamic analysis on it, verify the accuracy of the model, and obtain the displacement response.

Step 2: Define the topology optimization model for continua: specify the design variables, objective function, and design constraints.

Step 3: Based on the displacement generated by the dynamic loads on the model, calculate the equivalent static loads using Eq. (3), and perform topology optimization of the continua based on these equivalent static loads.

Step 4: Determine whether the objective function has converged. If the optimization converges under the given constraints, terminate the optimization; if the convergence conditions are not met, update the design variables and repeat Step 3 until the optimization loop reaches its upper limit.

Topology optimization of working device based on equivalent static loads

Finite element analysis of working device

As shown in Fig. 3, the backhoe working device of a hydraulic excavator is comprised of a bucket, arm, boom, bucket cylinder, arm cylinder, and boom cylinder. The excavator’s operational posture is achieved by controlling the displacements of the bucket cylinder, arm cylinder, and boom cylinder, allowing for the analysis of complex digging conditions.Fig. 3Composition of the working device.

Composition of the working device.

Based on previous design and simulation experiences with working device, simplifications are made to the structure by removing structures or features that have minimal impact on the simulation results. For instance, welded oil passages, wire supports, structural chamfers, and small holes on the working device are omitted. The multi-body dynamics model of the working device was established using HyperMesh and the OptiStruct solver. A mixed mesh of pentahedral and hexahedral elements is used to discretize the working device. The discretized model has a unit length of 15 mm, with a total of 120,850 elements and 196,856 nodes. Considering manufacturing constraints

The element density within the design space serves as the design variable for variable-density topology optimization, with a value range of [0, 1]. By altering the element density during topology optimization, various topological configurations can be obtained. To account for the manufacturability of the topological structure, two manufacturing constraints, namely minimum member size and symmetry, are added to the design variables. Minimum member size.

Minimum member size.

Minimum member size.

Incorporating a minimum member size constraint into the topology optimization model can effectively prevent issues such as checkerboard patterns and thin force transmission paths in the topological structure. Additionally, considering the feasibility of actual processing and manufacturing ensures that the minimum size of the topological structure is greater than a set value. The minimum member size is generally between 3 and 12 times the average size of the elements, with this study adopting 4 times the element size.

Symmetry constraint.

Traditional arm structures are often box-shaped and symmetrical. Therefore, considering actual manufacturability, a symmetry constraint is imposed on the design area of the arm. The symmetry constraint ensures that the topological structure is symmetrical about the set symmetric plane, facilitating practical processing and manufacturing.

The material of the main structure of the arm is Q355B, with an allowable stress of 355 MPa. Considering the complexity of the working environment of the working device, based on previous design experience, a safety factor of 1.2 is taken. Therefore, the strength design requirement for the arm is that the maximum stress should be less than 295 MPa. To investigate the influence of volume fraction on the topological structure, four sets of volume fraction constraints are set, with volume fractions ranging from [0.1, 0.2], [0.2, 0.3], [0.3, 0.4], and [0.4, 0.5], respectively. By defining a reference target, it ensures that the compliance at all time steps during the equivalent static load analysis is included within the reference target. Finally, the minimum value of the maximum compliance contained within the defined reference target is defined as the objective function.

Topology optimization is an optimization method that calculates force transmission paths, material distribution, and determines structural shapes within a design space. For the arm, the positions of each hinge hole are determined during the overall product design process, and the sizes of the hinge holes relate to the assembly with other components. Therefore, during the static topology optimization of the arm, the parts of each hinge hole need to be set as non-design spaces, with the remaining structure constituting the design space. During the topology optimization process, only the element densities within the design space are changed. However, the method proposed in this paper pertains to structural dynamic topology optimization. To facilitate the setting of connection relationships between various components in the simulation model of the working device and the configuration of element properties for each component, the entire arm structure is set as the design area, as shown in Fig. 6. After topology optimization, by using the method of modifying element densities in batches, the element densities of each hinge area (non-design area) are set to 1 to obtain the final topology-optimized configuration.Fig. 6Design space of the arm (HyperWorks-OptiStruct, Version 2022, https://www.altair.com.cn/).

The maximum stresses of the four obtained topological structures are all less than the allowable stress value of the material, which is 355 MPa. As shown in Fig. 9a, when the volume fraction is limited to the range of [0.1, 0.2], a significant decrease in the load-bearing capacity of the arm structure occurs due to the substantial removal of material, specifically manifested by a sharp increase in maximum stress to 354.98 MPa. This high-stress area is concentrated at the critical connection between the arm and the boom mounting hole, indicating stress concentration in this region. Fig. 9b shows that as more material is retained in the arm structure, the overall strength of the arm is effectively enhanced, with the maximum stress reducing to 302.51 MPa. The maximum stress occurs in the connection area between the arm and the boom mounting hole. Topology optimization is often utilized during the conceptual design stage. However, the resulting topology structures may exhibit discontinuities and irregular shapes, which do not meet the requirements of practical manufacturing processes. Therefore, based on the obtained topology model, an improved design of the arm is necessary. Meanwhile, to ensure proper installation between the arm and the boom, bucket, and cylinders, the hinged areas connecting the arm to these components are designated as non-design areas. The element density values of these areas are set to 1 in bulk. To reduce manufacturing costs while ensuring the arm’s torsional stiffness, the upper and lower plates of the arm are left intact without material removal. Only the left and right side plates of the arm are rounded according to the shape optimized by topology optimization, and small hollowed-out areas are filled. Conclusion

To meet the dynamic topology optimization and static strength requirements of the excavator’s working device, this paper conducted a topology optimization design of the excavator’s working device based on the equivalent static load method. A rigid-flexible coupling model of the working device was constructed, and the strength and maximum stress time history of the arm were analyzed under the combined excavation conditions of the excavator. The maximum stress in the arm, which was 254.17 MPa, occurred at the contact area between the rear end of the arm and the mounting seat of the arm cylinder, and it was less than the allowable stress of the material (355 MPa), indicating significant potential for topology optimization design.

Using the P-norm for maximum approximation of local element stress and based on the equivalent static load theory, a topology optimization model for the working device was constructed with the minimization of the maximum flexibility of the arm as the optimization objective, and the P-norm stress and volume fraction as constraint conditions. The model was optimized and solved, resulting in the topology structures and maximum stress time history curves of the arm under four volume fraction constraints. Considering the element density contour plot and the maximum stress change history of the topology-optimized arm, the topology structure obtained with a volume fraction constraint range of [0.3, 0.4] was selected as the final optimization result.

Considering manufacturing process requirements, the optimized arm was reconstructed. The rigid-flexible coupling model of the reconstructed working device was solved based on the equivalent static load method, resulting in a maximum stress of 267.53 MPa for the reconstructed arm during the entire simulation process, with a maximum increase of 5.26%. Although the maximum stress increased slightly compared to before optimization, it remained within the design requirement range (295 MPa), and the stress variation was relatively stable. Additionally, the mass of the reconstructed arm was reduced by approximately 24.63% compared to before optimization, providing guidance for structural lightweight design.
