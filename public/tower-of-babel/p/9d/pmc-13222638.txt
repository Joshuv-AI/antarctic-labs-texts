HACC-TNF-α-VLP Nanoparticles Facilitate HSP90-Dependent Antigen Cross-Presentation to Enhance CD8⁺ T Cell and Mucosal Immunity Against Foot-and-Mouth Disease
Authors: Lan Lv, Ya Su, Shiyu Chen, Wanting Su, Yirui Yin, Peng Ning, Shujia Liu, Wanzhe Yuan, Limin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCurrent foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) vaccines lack the efficient induction of CD8+ T cell-mediated cellular immunity and mucosal protection, highlighting the need for novel immunomodulatory delivery systems.MethodsHACC-TNF-α-virus-like particles (VLPs) nanoparticles were prepared via the ionic cross-linking method, followed by systematic evaluation of morphological characteristics, particle size distribution, in vitro release behavior, stability, and biotoxicity. In vitro experiments, the prepared nanoparticles were co-cultured with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), and flow cytometry (FCM) and cytokine array assays were subsequently used to detect the activation status of dendritic cells. The combined application of laser confocal microscopy and FCM was employed to further explore the antigen cross-presentation ability mediated by the nanoparticles. For the in vivo experiments, mice were administered with the nanoparticles via two routes: intranasal immunization and subcutaneous immunization. The levels of specific antibodies in the serum were quantitatively detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and lymphocyte proliferation assay, immunohistochemical technology, and FCM were combined to comprehensively evaluate the intensity of CD8+ T cell immune response and mucosal immune effects in mice.ResultsHACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles were successfully prepared, showing spherical morphology (~162.7 nm, −11.9 mV zeta potential), 84.40% encapsulation efficiency, 12.82% drug loading, and sustained release (75.83% cumulative release on day 4). They were non-cytotoxic to L929 cells at concentrations of ≤400 μg/mL. HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles promoted BMDCs maturation, facilitated BMDCs endocytosis of FMDV-VLPs, and antigen cross-presentation mediated by heat shock protein 90. Moreover, it can activate CD8+ T cells in vitro via cross-presentation. HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles upregulated cytotoxic T cells, tissue-resident memory T cells, sIgA, and systemic FMDV-specific antibodies in vivo.ConclusionHACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles exhibit potent immunomodulatory activity by efficiently mediating antigen cross-presentation and play a dual role in both induction of CD8+ T cell responses and mucosal immunity, indicating their potential role in FMD novel vaccine development.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is an acute, contagious infectious disease caused by the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) infecting cloven-hoofed animals. It can cause immunosuppression and seriously affect animal health, public health, and international trade.1,2 CD8+ T-cell responses play an essential role in the clearance of FMDV in vivo.3,4 Generally, dendritic cells (DCs) ingest particles with diameters between 100 nm-500 nm via endocytosis, phagocytosis, and pinocytosis. Protein antigens in the cytosol are processed by proteasomes, peptides are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum, where they bind to class I MHC molecules, and CD8+ T cells are activated. When protein antigens degraded in lysosomes bind to class II MHC molecules, CD4+ T cells are activated. Virus-like particles can accurately mimic the native structural conformation of FMDV without containing viral nucleic acids, thus posing no risk of virulence reversion. Foot-and-mouth disease virus-like particle (FMDV-VLPs) vaccines mainly induce humoral immunity and neutralizing antibodies, but are unable to effectively activate cellular immunity. In particular, they exhibit low efficiency of antigen cross-presentation and insufficient CD8+ T-cell responses, which results in failure to completely clear intracellular virus and represents a key bottleneck in current vaccine development.5,6 It is reported that nanoparticles carrying exogenous proteins can be cross-presented by DCs to initiate CD8+ T cell responses.7,8 In addition, encapsulation of certain nanoparticles can enhance cross-presentation of antigens.9,10

TNF-α not only causes tumor cell necrosis but also helps the host fight infection.11,12 Mast cell-derived TNF-α can initiate the migration of cutaneous and respiratory DCs to lymph nodes.11,13 Immunizing mice with synthetic particles containing TNF-α as adjuvants along with hemagglutinin of influenza viruses can enhance adaptive immune responses, thus increasing protection against virulent attacks.14 Therefore, mimicking the biological media released by mast cells (MCs) and producing TNF-α particle with suitable biomaterials can be used as adjuvants to improve the immune response of FMD vaccines.

Nanomaterials, as adjuvants, can improve the effectiveness and safety of vaccination, prevent rapid degradation, and prolong the residence time of antigens in lymphocytes.15 Although chitosan has good biological properties that make it an excellent nanoencapsulation material,16 its poor solubility and low drug-loading efficiency limit its application in biomedical research. Studies have shown that improving the solubility of chitosan facilitates the escape of antigens from endosomes into the cytoplasm, thereby enhancing the cross-presentation of exogenous antigens in an MHC I-restricted manner and activating CD8+ T cell responses.17 The chitosan derivative quaternary ammonium chitosan is a cationic biopolymer with the advantages of chitosan and good water solubility.18,19 Quaternary chitosan can be divided into two categories: N, N, N-trimethylchitosan (TMC) and hydroxypropyltrimonium chloride chitosan (HACC), depending on whether the quaternary ammonium group is directly introduced into the chitosan backbone. Quaternary chitosan can not only induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by activating Toll-like receptors (TLRs) but also protect antigens and target antigens from antigen-presenting cells (APCs),19 thereby enhancing immune responses. Chitosan-quaternized nanoparticles serve as adjuvants by safeguarding the vaccine from degradation caused by tissue-specific proteases and facilitating the sustained release of vaccine antigens into the nasal cavity. Furthermore, the adhesive properties of chitosan improve the adherence of nanoparticles to the mucus layer, which prolongs the retention time of the vaccine in the nasal cavity and boosts mucosal immunity.20,21

The heat shock proteins of 90 kDa (HSP90) are stress-inducible proteins and are widely involved in a variety of intracellular homeostatic maintenance processes.22,23 Notably, extracellular Hsp90 can bind to cell surface receptors (LOX-1, CD91, and SREC-I), thereby activating a series of cellular functions closely related to immune responses, such as cytokine secretion, cell maturation, and antigen presentation, making it a promising target for immunomodulatory research.24–26 Hsp90 is essential for peptide cross-presentation. Studies have suggested that both extracellular and cytosolic Hsp90 may participate in antigen cross-presentation.26–28 Radicicol, an HSP90 inhibitor, was shown to significantly inhibit the cross-presentation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in a dose-dependent fashion.24,28

This study aimed to develop HACC-based composite nanoparticles (HACC-FMD NPs) as immunomodulatory carriers for FMDV antigens and evaluate their capacity to mediate antigen cross-presentation, enhance CD8+ T cell responses, and induce mucosal immunity.

Generation and Characterization of FMDV-VLPs and Recombinant TNF-α Protein

The recombinant plasmid pCDNA3.1(+)-HBcAg-VP1-VP4 (VP1 and VP4 were fused between amino acids 74 and 83 of the HBcAg protein, generating the HBcAg-VP1-VP4 fusion fragment)29 was used as a template to construct the recombinant pET-32a(+)-HBcAg-VP1-VP4 plasmid using Xho I and BamH I. The recombinant protein was expressed in E.coli BL21 (DE3) cells (TransGen Biotech, China) using isopropyl β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at a final concentration of 0.5 mmol/L. After purification by Ni-column affinity chromatography, sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and Western blotting were performed to confirm the quality of the recombinant protein. Rabbit anti-VP1-VP4 IgG and goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (secondary antibody) were used for Western blot analysis at dilutions of 1:800 and 1:20000, respectively.

To further determine whether HBcAg-VP1-VP4 self-assembled into virus-like particles, the protein was negatively stained and observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Recombinant HBcAg-VP1-VP4 protein was incubated with rabbit anti-VP1-VP4 IgG (1:1000) at 37 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation twice, the pellets were suspended in 50 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L/Gold with a dilution of 1:50 was added to the suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Two centrifugal washes were then performed. 10 μL samples were dropped onto the carbon support membrane for 10 min. After staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid at room temperature for 10 min, the film was dried and observed by TEM.

Recombinant TNF-α protein was expressed using a prokaryotic expression system, and the detailed procedures of expression and purification were performed as described in our previous study.30

Preparation and Characterization of HACC-CMC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs Nanoparticles

Preparation of HACC-CMC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs Nanoparticles

A 2 mg/mL HACC solution and 1.5 mg/mL CMC solution (pH 6.0) with a mass ratio of 6:1 was used to prepare the nanoparticles. FMDV-VLPs and recombinant TNF-ɑ protein were added to the HACC solution and magnetically stirred for 1 h, and the CMC solution was then added dropwise at 800 r/min for cross-linking for 30 min. Immunoelectron Microscopy

The prepared HACC-CMC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticle suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting precipitate was suspended in water and centrifuged again, and this washing step was repeated three times. The final pellet was suspended in distilled water. The obtained HACC-CMC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticle suspension was then subjected to ultra-sonication at 130 W for 10 minutes. To determine the successful encapsulation of FMDV-VLPs proteins in the nanoparticles, the morphology of the nanoparticles was observed using immunoelectron microscopy.31

Dynamic Light Scattering Test

Particle size and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK), an electrode was inserted to measure the ζ-potential of the nanoparticles, and the ζ-potential and particle size were measured.

Stability of Nanoparticles

In this study, the prepared HACC-CMC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles were resuspended in distilled water and stored at 4 °C and room temperature, respectively. To determine the stability of HACC-CMC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles, the encapsulation efficiency was measured on days 3, 7, and 14 using the BCA method.

Cytotoxicity Evaluation of Nanoparticles

The CCK-8 method was used to evaluate the nanoparticle cytotoxicity. The L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 105 cells per well. Nanoparticles at concentrations of 10 ng/mL, 100 ng/mL, 1 μg/mL, 10 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL, 100 μg/mL, 200 μg/mL, 300 μg/mL, and 400 μg/mL were dissolved in RPMI-1640 medium (10% FBS), respectively, then added to the cells and cultured for 72 h. After incubation, the cell morphology was observed, and then cells were replaced with fresh RPMI-1640 medium. 10 μL CCK-8 per well was added, and the incubation continued at 37 °C for 1 h. The OD450nm was then measured to calculate the viability of L929 cells.

Isolation and Culturing Bone Marrow-Derived Dendritic Cells (BMDCs)

Murine BMDCs were obtained as described previously.32 Briefly, carbon dioxide was slowly introduced into the Individually Ventilated Caging at a rate of 50%/min, keeping the mice exposed for 3 minutes. After observing that the mice had lost consciousness, they were euthanized by dislocating the cervical vertebrae. Bone marrow cells were extracted from the tibia and femur of BALB/c mice and cultured in RPMI medium containing GM-CSF (10 ng/mL) and IL-4 (5 ng/mL) for eight days. Then, the cells at a density of 1×106 cells/mL were stained with anti-mouse CD11c-PE, anti-mouse MHCII-APC, and anti-mouse CD80-FITC at 4 °C for 30 min prior to flow cytometry (FCM) analysis.

Activation and Maturation of DCs by Nanoparticles

FCM identification: The remaining cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells/well. HACC-CMC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles (400 μg/mL) were added to the BMDCs and incubated with cells for 48 h. BMDCs supernatants were collected and detected using the Mouse Cytokine Array C6 Kit. The cells were harvested and stained with anti-mouse CD11c-PE, anti-Mouse MHC II-APC, anti-Mouse CD80-FITC at 4 °C for 30 min, then for FCM analysis.

BMDCs Uptake of Nanoparticles

Day-8 BMDCs were seeded into 12-well plates containing cell slides at a density of 1×105 cells/well. HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles (400 μg/mL) were added and incubated with BMDCs for 3, 6, and 12 h. After washing, LysoTracker DND99 (50 nM) was added to the BMDCs and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The BMDCs were fixed with pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min after washing. Cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with normal goat serum for 30 min. For staining, a monoclonal antibody against the FMDV VP1 protein (1:100) (prepared by Ziyu Wei in our laboratory) was used as the primary antibody, and goat anti-rabbit-FITC (1:100) was used as the secondary antibody. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min and imaged using a LSM900 confocal microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

To explore the role of HSP90 in antigen uptake, BMDCs were pre-treated with radicicol at a final concentration of 5 μM for 2 h. The procedures were performed as described previously.

Antigen Presentation by Dendritic Cells

The submandibular, anterior shoulder, inguinal, and axillary lymph nodes were collected from the mice to isolate lymphocytes. CD3+ T cells were sorted using the Mouse CD3+ T Cell Magnetic Bead Sorting Kit.

BMDCs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well. Cells were pre-treated for 1 h with Chloroquine (50 μmol/mL), MG132 (10 μmol/mL), chloroquine (50 μmol/mL), and MG132 (10 μmol/mL), and incubated with HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs NPs for 12 h. Then, BMDCs were co-cultured at a ratio of 1:10 with CD3+ T cells for 72 h. Co-cultured cells were harvested and divided into two parts. One part was used for FCM identification, and the other was used to detect the mRNA levels of T cell-specific transcription factors (Runx3, STAT4, and GATA3) by real-time PCR. Extraction of Membrane Protein of BMDCs

BMDCs were seeded into 10 cm cell plates at a density of 1×107 cells. Three groups were established: BMDCs+HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs, BMDCs+Radicicol+HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs, and BMDCs (BMDCs were pre-treated with radicicol at a final concentration of 5 μM for 2 h). HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles (400 μg/mL) were added and incubated with BMDCs for 9 h. Membrane proteins were extracted using a membrane protein extraction kit (Solarbio). BMDCs from the three groups were washed twice with cold PBS and 400 μL of protein extraction liquid A was added to the cells. After oscillating mix at 4 °C for 30 min, then 12000 r/min for 5 min. Finally, the supernatants were in a water bath at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by 1000 r/min for 5 min. The lower membrane protein portion was dissolved in 200 μL of membrane protein lysate C to obtain membrane protein.

Immune Co-Precipitation

Mix 1.2 mg of FMDV VP1 antibody with 200 μL of membrane protein and placed in a flip mixer for 1 h. Next, 100 μL of rProtein A/G Agarose Resin was taken and centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 1 min, and the supernatant was aspirated. Next, 1 mL of binding buffer was added to resuspend the resin, centrifuge at 1000 r/min for 1 min, and the supernatant was aspirated and repeated twice. The antigen-antibody mixture was added to the treated resin, mixed well, and gently flipped at room temperature in a flip mixer for 30 min to ensure full contact and adsorption. Centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 1 min, and the supernatants were discarded. Next, 1 mL of wash buffer was added to the centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 1 min, and the supernatant was aspirated and repeated twice. Finally, add 50 μL of 1×SDS-PAGE loading buffer and heat at 95 °C for 5 min. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 1 min, the supernatant was collected using 1:1000 dilution of Anti-HSP90 alpha Antibody (Huaan, Zhejiang, China) as the primary antibody and 1:5000 dilution of goat anti-rabbit IgG (H L) HRP as the secondary antibody for Western blot analysis.

Animal Immunization and Sampling

100 6-week-old BALB/c mice were divided into four groups randomly, 25 mice per group. HACC-CMC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticle subcutaneous immunization (NPsPI), HACC-CMC-TNF-α-VLPs intranasal immunization (NPsBI) group, FMDV-VLPs subcutaneous immunization, and PBS intranasal immunization groups. The experimental group was administered the drug subcutaneously or intranasally, at an antigen dosage of 25 µg per mouse. The FMDV-VLPs were emulsified with Montanide™ IMS 251C VG adjuvant at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v). Blood samples were collected via the tail vein at days 0, 7, 14, 28, 42, and 72 after the primary immunization for serum isolation. Subsequently, five mice from each group were anesthetized with carbon dioxide inhalation (50%/min, 3 min) and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Samples of spleen, bone marrow, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and pharyngeal mucosa were collected at corresponding time points.

Detection of FMDV-Specific Antibodies and sIgA

The levels of FMDV-specific antibodies in serum were assessed using an indirect ELISA method, as described in our previous study.29 Centrifuge the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from mice at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were collected and analyzed using a mouse sIgA ELISA kit. The specific procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lymphocyte Proliferation

Lymphocytes from lymph nodes were harvested and adjusted to a concentration of 2×106 cells/mL. The detailed protocol has been previously according to the reference.30

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The pharyngeal mucosa was embedded using OCT, cut into 7 μm sections, fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4 °C for 10 min, and then subjected to immunohistochemical staining. Following blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with monoclonal antibodies against purified anti-mouse IgA antibody (1:500), anti-CD11c antibody (1:500), MHC class II monoclonal antibody (1:200), CD69 mouse monoclonal antibody (1:500), and purified anti-mouse CD103 antibody (1:100) as primary antibodies. Subsequently, after washing, they were exposed to secondary antibodies, including donkey anti-mouse IgG-AP (1:500), goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:200), and goat anti-rat IgG-HRP (1:200) at 37 °C for 1 h. Immunoreactions were visualized using DAB and Fast Red as color developers. The nuclei of the tissue were stained with methyl green staining solution for 5 min, followed by dehydration with gradient ethanol, xylene clarification, and sealing with neutral resin. Statistical analysis was performed by randomly observing five fields of view under an optical microscope.

Flow Cytometry

FCM was performed using a panel of antibodies, including APC anti-mouse CD3, FITC anti-mouse CD4, and PE anti-mouse CD8a, to analyze single-cell suspensions from the spleen.

Statistical Analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s Multiple Comparison Test was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software, and graphs were prepared using GraphPad Prism 9. *P <0.05,** P <0.01.

Characterization of HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs Nanoparticles

With 2 mg/mL HACC, 1.5 mg/mL CMC, and a mass ratio of 6:1 (Figure 2A), the cross-linking encapsulation efficiency and drug loading rate of CMC dropwise were 84.40% and 12.82%, respectively, when VLPs and TNF-ɑ recombinant protein were added to the HACC solution to 1.2 mg. The results showed that HACC-CMC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles were successfully prepared, and their morphology was spherical, with good dispersion, uniform size, and no obvious adhesion (Figure 2B). The average particle size of the nanoparticles is 162.7 nm, and the potential is −11.9 mV (Figure 2C and D). The release properties of the nanoparticles showed that the release rate was fast in the first 12 h, reaching 56.53% of the total drug load. The release rate reached a peak at 72 h with 75.87%, then slowed down and transitioned to a steady state (75.83%) (Figure 2E). The encapsulation efficiency of HACC-CMC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles was stable until day 7 at 4 °C. At room temperature, the encapsulation rate began to decrease on the 3rd day, and by the 14th day, the encapsulation efficiency dropped to 75.69% and flocculants appeared in the solution (Figure 2F and G). Discussion

Nanoparticles have shown unique advantages in regulating the immune response, among which, promoting dendritic cell maturation is one of their important immunomodulatory effects.33,34 As markers of dendritic cell maturation, upregulation of CD80 and MHC-II can improve the interaction efficiency between BMDCs and T cells.35,36 The results of this study showed that HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles significantly upregulated CD80 and MHC-II levels (Figure 4D and E). In addition, HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles caused BMDCs to secrete higher levels of IL-6, IL-10, IL-1β, IL-12p70, IL-12p40, and IL-4, as well as the chemokines MIP-1α, RANTES, MDC, and TARC (Figure 4B and C). These results suggest that HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles can effectively activate BMDCs and promote BMDCs maturation.

The cross-presentation of exogenous antigens is highly dependent on antigen escape from the lysosomes.37,38 Chitosan has been shown to promote lysosomal escape, increasing the osmotic pressure of lysosomes through the proton sponge effect, rupturing endosomes, and releasing antigens into the cytoplasm.39,40 Both FMDV-VLPs and HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles could be taken up by BMDCs, but HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles had stronger lysosomal escape ability than FMDV-VLPs. These results indicate that the prepared chitosan nanoparticles can promote endocytosis of BMDCs to FMDV-VLPs, promote the escape of exogenous antigens into the cytoplasm, and increase the chance of exogenous antigens being presented through MHC-I. Although the present study has confirmed that HACC-based composite nanoparticles can effectively promote antigen cross-presentation and enhance downstream CD8+ T cell responses, further in-depth mechanistic studies are still required to fully elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, such as the specific intracellular trafficking pathway of antigens and the roles of related key molecules.

After exogenous antigens are taken up by dendritic cells, they are usually processed in lysosomes and bound to MHC class II molecules for presentation to CD4⁺ T cells.41 However, during the cross-presentation process, exogenous antigens “escape” from lysosomes into the cytoplasm and then enter the proteasome for degradation, after which the peptides bind to MHC class I molecules and are presented to CD8+ T cells.38 Therefore, inhibition of proteasomes and/or lysosomes can be used to determine whether HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLP nanoparticles are cross-presented in BMDCs. The inhibitor of lysosome chloroquine can reduce antigen processing and presentation by increasing the pH to inhibit lysosomal and endocytic fusion and acid-dependent protease activities.40 The proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively blocked the proteolytic activity of the 26S proteasome complex and inhibited the efficiency of exogenous antigen cross-presentation.41,42 The results of this study showed that BMDCs loaded with HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles significantly increased the percentage of CD8+ T cells. However, when MG132 was used to inhibit proteasomes, a significant decrease in the percentage of CD8+ T-cells was observed. In addition, the percentage of CD4+ T cells significantly reduced after treatment with chloroquine. These results indicated that HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles may activate CD8+ T cells via the proteasome pathway, which means that cross-presentation occurred. The detection of transcription factors specific to different subsets of T-cells further strengthens this discussion. RT-qPCR results showed that the relative expression of Runx3 (CD8+ T cells) significantly increased after loading HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles, and there was no effect on the relative expression of Runx3 after Chloroquine treatment. However, after MG132 treatment, the relative expression levels of STAT4 (Th1 cells) and GATA3 (Th2 cells) were significantly increased.

HSP90 plays an essential role in antigen cross-presentation in dendritic cells as a cytoplasmic transporter, and HSP90 is involved in the transfer of exogenous antigens from endocytosomes to the cytoplasm.26,27 Radicicol is a potent HSP90 inhibitor that competitively binds tightly to the ATP-binding pocket of HSP90 and blocks the normal binding of ATP to HSP90, thereby interfering with the ATP hydrolysis cycle of HSP90.43,44 The lysosomal escape phenomenon of HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs NPs decreased after the inhibition of HSP90 by Radicicol (Figure 7A). HSP90 binds to endocytic exogenous antigens mainly in the membrane components.27 Extraction of BMDCs cell membrane proteins was confirmed by immunoprecipitation assay to confirm that HSP90 was associated with internalized FMDV-VLPs, and Radicicol inhibitors reduced this association. Heat treatment can alter the state of the protein complex, disrupt non-covalent interactions between proteins to aid SDS-binding proteins, and restore the electrophoresis pattern to that of native proteins.45 Therefore, HSP90 maintained its original protein size, according to the immunoprecipitation results. Based on the above results, it is speculated that the process of cross-presentation of HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles may be that after HACC-TNF-ɑ-VLPs nanoparticles are taken up by dendritic cells, HSP90 binds to the unfolded antigen from the transport channel on the endocytosome membrane, pulls it to the cytosol, then peptides are degraded in proteasomes, and then the peptides are transported to the endoplasmic reticulum through TAP, binds to MHC class I molecules, and presents them, activating CD8⁺T cells. In addition, antigen cross-presentation is coordinately regulated by multiple molecules. The Sec61 complex mediates the transmembrane transport of antigens from endosomes, while ERAD-related proteins such as Derlin-1 participate in retrograde transport. Endosomal membrane regulators and the actin cytoskeleton facilitate antigen escape by modulating endosomal membrane permeability and structural integrity.46–48

CD8+ T cells are responsible for directly eliminating pathogen-infected cells.49 The findings demonstrate that HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles can activate CD8+ T cells in vivo. However, CD8+ T cell responses are relatively limited. In addition, the CD8+ T responses generated by subcutaneous immunization in mice were slightly better than those generated by intranasal immunization, which may be related to the better cross-presentation of nanoparticles by DCs in subcutaneous tissue. The pharynx is the primary site for FMDV invasion.50 Mice intranasally immunized with HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles did not exhibit detectable FMDV-specific IgG antibodies in their serum; however, elevated sIgA levels were observed in the BALF. This may be attributed to the ability of intranasal immunization to stimulate IgA antibody production in the mucosal tissues.51,52 This may also be linked to the intrinsic mucosal immunostimulatory properties of quaternized chitosan.19 The distribution of TRM in the pharyngeal mucosa induced by nanoparticles indicates that nanoparticles can induce a certain level of T-cell immune memory in mice; however, the duration is relatively short. Enhancing the duration of immune memory is an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and evaluated HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles as potential FMD vaccine candidates. Functionally, they significantly promoted BMDCs maturation while critically facilitating the BMDCs cross-presentation of FMDV-VLPs to activate CD8+ T cells. Mechanistically, HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles activated CD8+ T cells via the HSP90-dependent cross-presentation pathway. Furthermore, HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles upregulated cytotoxic T cells, tissue-resident memory T cells, sIgA, and systemic FMDV-specific antibodies in vivo. These HACC-TNF-α-VLPs nanoparticles exhibit potent immunomodulatory activity by efficiently mediating antigen cross-presentation and play a dual role in both induction of CD8+ T cell responses and mucosal immunity, indicating their potential role in FMD novel mucosal vaccine development.
