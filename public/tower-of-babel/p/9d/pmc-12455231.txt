Is there an optimal sequencing of endoscopic third ventriculostomy and stereotactic radiosurgery for brain metastasis with secondary obstructive hydrocephalus? Illustrative case
Authors: Victor Goulenko, Venkatesh Shankar Madhugiri, Neil D. Almeida, Rohil Shekher, Andrew J. Fabiano, Dheerendra Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSecondary hydrocephalus caused by tumor obstruction of the aqueduct leads to anatomical distortion of the third ventricle (3V) and the surrounding structures. This report quantifies and discusses the dosimetric impact of hydrocephalus in stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) treatment and discusses the optimal sequencing between radiosurgery and endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV).OBSERVATIONSThe authors review a case of intracranial metastasis causing secondary obstructive hydrocephalus that underwent ETV before SRS. Dual SRS plans were made based on the MRI before and after the ETV to evaluate the impact of the ventricular volume on dosimetry. Both plans had the same treatment parameters. The time interval between the MRI studies was approximately 20 days, with a 74% increase in the tumor size. The post-ETV plan revealed a reduced ventricular volume, particularly the 3V, with a noticeable shift in normal brain structures, leading them to receive increased doses.LESSONSThe anatomical changes caused by the ventricular reduction after the ETV associated with the tumor growth were enough to produce a higher dose to the healthy tissue, exposing the patient to a greater risk of adverse radiation effects. Shortening the interval between ETV and SRS can minimize anatomical shifts and the risk of adverse effects while maintaining adequate patient management. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25275

Conformal radiation treatments with stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) can limit the dose adjacent to the treated tumor with greater normal structure protection and have become the preferred treatment method for intracranial metastasis.1–3

The patient’s symptoms will depend on the location of the metastatic lesion. In those that affect the pathway of the CSF flow, secondary hydrocephalus can occur, leading to headache, confusion, somnolence, and, in worse and more acute scenarios, death. The adequate management in these cases is a surgical CSF diversion, using ventricular shunts or endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV), and treatment of the underlying tumor.

ETV is a procedure in which an endoscope is placed into the ventricular system and used to create a stoma in the floor of the third ventricle (3V) to allow CSF flow from the ventricular system into the prepontine cistern. ETV has been proven to successfully treat hydrocephalus secondary to CSF obstruction at the level of the cerebral aqueduct and fourth ventricle.4–6

As the inverse square law describes, the radiation dose decreases as the distance from the tumor increases due to tissue attenuation. Intracranial tumors within or adjacent to the ventricular system may have more CSF around them, with an increase in their distance to normal brain tissue, leading to protection of the normal brain. Tumors causing obstructive hydrocephalus can enlarge CSF pathways, creating additional distance between the tumor and normal brain.

This report discusses the changes in dosimetry that can result from hydrocephalus in SRS for a tumor in the periaqueductal region. The timing and sequence between treatment of the tumor and hydrocephalus can directly affect tumor dose and potentially the risk of adverse radiation effects.

Illustrative Case

A 77-year-old woman with oligometastatic breast cancer with a hormonal panel showing negative estrogen and progesterone receptors and positivity for HER-2 presented during systemic therapy with confusion and urinary incontinence. Brain MRI revealed a well-circumscribed lesion in the right midbrain with exophytic extension to the 3V and compression of the cerebral aqueduct, causing obstructive hydrocephalus without signs of leptomeningeal disease. She underwent ETV without biopsy due to the posterior location of the lesion and was referred for SRS. Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS; Esprit model, Elekta AB) was performed 21 days after the ETV with a prescribed marginal dose of 18 Gy to the 49% isodose line based on the same-day MRI (Fig. 1). The original GKRS treatment was planned based on the 3T 1.5-mm MRI performed after the ETV, while the comparison plan was based on the 3T 1.5-mm MRI performed before the ETV. Both plans were optimized using Lightning inverse planning software of the Leksell GammaPlan (LGP) version 11.4 (Elekta AB). The presetting of “bean-on time” was 99% and “low-dose” was 50%, the marginal prescription dose to the tumor was 18 Gy in a single session, and the brainstem (BS) constraint was 11 Gy to keep a higher conformity to it, but allowing a maximum dose of 12 Gy. Final manual adjustments were made to obtain optimal plans. The gross tumor volume and BS were contoured on LGP, and the ventricles, thalamus, internal capsule (IC), and hippocampi were defined on Brainlab’s Elements autocontouring software and exported to LGP after verification and approval by an experienced physician. Values were obtained for tumor and ventricle volumes, the maximum dose to the tumor, thalamus, IC, BS, hippocampi, and V12Gy (total brain volume receiving 12Gy) for each plan. For quantification of the anatomical deviation of the thalamus, the IC, and the tumor due to the hydrocephalus, their volumes were exported from BrainLab to MATLAB(MathWorks), and the difference in their stereotactic positions was analyzed. The diameter of the 3V and the distance between the IC genus were also measured for distortion quantification.

Discussion

Intraventricular metastases account for less than 1% of intracranial metastases. Surgical approaches can be limited, with their main therapy based on radiation therapy (whole-brain radiotherapy or SRS) and systemic therapy (chemotherapy and targeted immunotherapy). SRS has been widely used due to its conformal dose, sparing brain tissue with high local tumor control.7,8 Leptomeningeal dissemination and hydrocephalus can be present after SRS in 8%–18%7,8 and 24% of cases,7 respectively, as well as intraventricular hemorrhage.8 If left untreated, these tumors continue to increase in volume, with breast metastases growing around 20% over 2 weeks, leading to a risk of worsening symptoms and a change in the prescribed dose.9 This rapid growth rate highlights the need for prompt treatment.

Ventricular tumors undergoing radiotherapy may not require CSF diversion. For some CNS tumors causing early, long-standing hydrocephalus, like central neurocytomas, primary glial tumors, or germinomas, CSF diversion procedures may be avoided by tumor shrinkage after radiation therapy if not in an acute or symptomatic scenario.10–13 In patients with acute or symptomatic hydrocephalus who undergo ETV, the ventricular size may decrease over months, reaching an optimal reduction at 12 months.4,5

Based on the inverse square law of radiation distribution in tissue with dose attenuation, structures at greater distances from the targeted tumor will receive a lower dose. A greater volume of CSF surrounding the tumor will create additional protection by increasing the distance to normal brain tissue, minimizing its dose and the risk of radiation adverse events.

A common finding after SRS is the development of perilesional edema. This creates a significant risk for the patients since the level of obstruction could worsen and cause exacerbation of the hydrocephalus, requiring immediate intervention for CSF diversion for symptom control.

Observations

The interval between both MRI studies was 23 days, with the ETV happening 21 days before the SRS. The original tumor volume was 1.997 cm3 and increased to 3.481 cm3, a significant interval tumor growth of 74% (Figs. 1 and 2).

The pre-ETV ventricular volume was 42.2 ml, and after the ETV, it had a minimal volumetric change of 0.8 ml, but the 3V had a more accentuated decrease of 15% (3.617 ml to 3.092 ml). The 3V diameter was reduced from 12 mm to 10.6 mm (12%), and the intergenu distance of the ICs was reduced from 21.4 mm to 20.3 mm (5%). No significant difference was noted at the Evans index, with values of 0.30 (38.7/128.3) and 0.29 (36.9/128.3) before and after ETV, respectively.

Both plans had adequate tumor coverage, Paddick Conformity Index, and gradient index, as shown in Table 1, with V12Gy values of 0.1% and 0.2% pre- and post-ETV, respectively (Table 2). On a dosimetry comparison, a significant dose variation was observed, with only the right thalamus having a 4% (1 Gy) lower dose on the post-ETV treatment. The other structures had an increase of dose between 1.2 Gy and 2.2 Gy, more evident in the right-sided structures. The BS, which had the most significant relation to the tumor, had an increase of 25.5% of the dose, mostly related to tumor growth (Table 2). Limitations

Because of the rarity of intraventricular tumors and that only a portion of them develop hydrocephalus, added by different severities of hydrocephalus symptoms (impacted by the intraventricular location, size, and prior ventricle size), a prospective study to compare the ideal timing of ETV and SRS is not viable. This study is based on the findings of a single case study that generates good discussion on the topic.

Lessons

The significant increase in tumor size and reduction in ventricular volume during the period between ETV and SRS resulted in an increase in dose to normal tissue, with the BS experiencing a 2.4-Gy increase in total dose. This overall increase heightens the risk of adverse radiation effects. In scenarios in which dose constraints cannot be met due to anatomical changes, treatment options include dose reduction (potentially compromising tumor control) or hypofractionation.

Hydrocephalus introduces an additional layer of complexity. While ventricular dilation can increase the distance between third ventricular tumors and adjacent critical structures, offering some degree of radioprotection, its resolution prior to radiosurgery may paradoxically increase brain dose due to reduced CSF and anatomical shift.

In nonurgent cases, timing the ETV immediately after the SRS can minimize the risk of adverse events while maintaining adequate patient treatment by avoiding tumor growth and ventricular distortion. Alternatively, in urgent cases, ETV can be followed by expedited outpatient SRS after hospital discharge. This is particularly important as SRS can only be delivered in the outpatient setting and may be delayed in cases of prolonged admission, leading to undesirable tumor progression or anatomical changes that impact a safe dose delivery. This factor would also preclude the placement of a prophylactic external ventricular drainage, which also adds a risk of infection and does not provide long-term protection against a possible delayed post-SRS hydrocephalus.

Although the optimal interval between ETV and SRS is not clearly defined, a short interval is preferable to preserve tumor geometry, maintain favorable dosimetry, and reduce the risk of complications. Treatment sequencing should be individualized based on clinical urgency, tumor dynamics, and radiation planning considerations.
