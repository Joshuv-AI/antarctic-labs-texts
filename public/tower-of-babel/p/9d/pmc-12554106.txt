Heavy-chain immunoglobulin locus editing in rhesus macaque B cells to confer antibody production
Authors: Anne-Sophie Kuhlmann, Nawal Madkhali, Eliana Moskovitz, Jacob E. Parrott, Shruthi Shankar Raman, Andrew O. Riker, Jasmin Martinez-Reyes, Megha Gupta, Ryan An Jang, Veronica Nelson, Matthew D. Gray, Justin J. Taylor, Christopher W. Peterson, Hans-Peter Kiem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antibody-based immunotherapies are promising; however, their application remains limited to acute diseases due to rapid clearance of antibodies in vivo. Some chronic conditions could benefit from sustained therapeutic antibody expression. One such instance is human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), where the efficiency of broadly neutralizing antibodies by passive immunization has been limited in clinical trials. B cell editing to enable sustained production of an antibody of interest in vivo could address this issue. However, the long-term potential of this approach and feasibility to perform editing in B cells from people living with HIV remain to be determined. We investigated editing of rhesus macaque B cells from healthy or simian/human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV)-infected animals to model this approach. An antibody-encoding cassette was inserted in the immunoglobulin locus by CRIPSR-Cas9-mediated ex vivo B cell editing. Similar indel efficiencies were achieved in B cells from both uninfected and infected animals, and expression of the antibody of interest was detected in up to 10% of uninfected B cells. This study paves the way for future in vivo work to assess the long-term potential of this approach and its impact on B cell development and function in an immunocompetent in vivo nonhuman primate model of HIV persistence and cure.

Introduction

Despite the antiretroviral therapy (ART) approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) still affects millions of people around the world. Lifelong daily adherence and access to a potent ART regimen may present financial and practical limitations, especially in low- and middle-income countries. These factors highlight the need for alternative therapeutic options, such as broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs). Although bNAbs should be elicited following infection or vaccination, the extreme genetic diversity and mutability of HIV-1 renders the humoral immune system unable to keep pace,1 and less than 10% of individuals, called elite neutralizers, achieve anti-HIV serum neutralizing activity in the absence of any treatment.2,3 Consequently, passive administration of second-generation bNAbs4 has been evaluated in clinical trials as an alternative.5,6,7,8,9,10,11

Clinical trials demonstrated that bNAb injections are safe in both healthy individuals and people living with HIV (PLWH), including adults and children.5,6,7,8,12,13,14,15 Importantly, bNAbs also have a transient impact on the viremia, but their limited half-life has hampered further bNAb therapy development for PLWH.5,7,14,15,16,17,18 Their clearance in simian/human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV)-infected nonhuman primate (NHP) models and PLWH is even faster than in healthy individuals, possibly due to faster clearance of antigen-antibody immune complexes.16,19,20,21 Consequently, passive immunization would require repeated antibody injections over the lifetime of an individual, similar to lifelong ART. Underlining these limitations, study participants with a more frequent schedule of bNAb injections are more likely to miss treatments.22 Therefore, sustained antibody expression will be required to achieve a long-lasting impact on viral replication.

Cell engineering to enable long-term in vivo protein expression offers a promising alternative to bNAb administration. We and others previously engineered hematopoietic stem cells for in vivo secretion of anti-HIV bNAbs using lentiviral vectors23,24 or ex vivo editing.25 In search of better alternatives, B cells are the most proficient antibody-secreting cell types in the body and can persist for decades as differentiated memory or plasma cells. Recent studies demonstrated the potential of CRISPR-Cas9 to edit the heavy-26,27,28,29,30,31 or light-chain26,27,28 immunoglobulin locus, to insert an antibody-encoding cassette. This resulted in the replacement of endogenous antibody secretion with production of an exogenous antibody of interest in B cells from human,27,28,29,30,31,32,33 murine,26,28,29,34,35 or macaque36,37 species. Importantly, the edited B cells conserved essential properties such as differentiation in plasma or memory B cells, which is known to contribute to effective anti-HIV immunity in long-term non-progressors (LTNPs)38 and suggest the potential for life-long bNAb elicitation. Further, edited B cells can secrete the bNAb of interest in an antigen-dependent manner and undergo somatic hypermutation as well as class-switch recombination.26,31,34,35 Through these properties, bNAb secretion by edited B cells has the potential to circumvent several challenges associated with bNAb administration such as persistence and viral escape.

These strategies require evaluation in vivo. Several studies demonstrated the promising potential of B cell editing for bNAb secretion in wild-type26,28,29,35 or humanized mice.32 In addition, while a few studies evaluated ex vivo B cell editing in NHP B cells,36,37 more work is needed to support future in vivo investigations. Additionally, no data are available in B cells collected from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed NHPs that would most closely model and predict the therapeutic potential of this approach in PLWH. Therefore, we assessed ex vivo NHP B cell editing with the goal to express VRC01, an anti-HIV bNAb that has been well characterized in clinical studies. We applied CRISPR-based gene editing methods to permanently reprogram rhesus macaque (RM) B cells to express VRC01 from the endogenous immunoglobulin heavy-chain (IGH) locus and to evaluate the potential of our strategy in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed RMs.

Results

Rhesus macaque B cell phenotype and enrichment

To confirm the cross-reactivity of mostly human-reactive antibodies against RM B cell surface markers and evaluate the B cell composition in RM peripheral blood and tissues, we analyzed a panel of B-cell-reactive antibodies for flow cytometry. The B cell phenotypic composition was analyzed in peripheral blood, lymph nodes (LNs), spleen, and bone marrow (BM) from healthy donor RM. Peripheral blood contained CD20+ B cells with tissue-like memory, activated memory, naive B cell phenotypes, and some rare plasma cells (Figure 1A). Following exclusion of non-B cells using CD3, CD14, CD16, and CD11b markers, 46.5% of the remaining cells were CD20+ cells in the peripheral blood, representing 9.4% of the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (46.5% of 20.3%) (Figure 1A). We used CD21 and CD27 markers to characterize the CD20+ memory B cell phenotype composition as known in humans.39 While these subsets have not been functionally confirmed in rhesus yet, one study validated CD20+CD21+CD27+ cells as memory B cells.40 Among those, 29.5% were activated memory B cells (AM; CD21–CD27+), 30.2% were naive B cells (N; CD21+CD27–), 36.9% were tissue-like memory B cells (TLM; CD21–CD27–), and 3.41% were resting memory B cells (RMem; CD21+CD27+). A similar phenotypic composition was observed in peripheral blood CD20+ B cells obtained from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed RMs (Figures S1 and S2A). The B cell composition was also characterized in spleen, LN, and BM from healthy donor RMs. LN and spleen contained CD20+ B cells with a predominantly naive phenotype, including both activated (CD21low) and resting (CD21++) naive B cells in the spleen (Figure 1C), as well as a large population of CD19–CD20–CD38+ plasma cells (Figures 1B and 1C). While both the LN and spleen contained short-lived plasmablasts (CD38+CD138– cells; 95.5% and 95.6%, respectively), they also contained a minority of long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs) (CD38+CD138+; 3.78% and 3.83%, respectively). BM contained a small population of CD20–CD19low pro-B cells, composed of rare CD38+CD27+ antibody-secreting cells and LLPCs (Figure 1D). Data obtained from several animals are summarized in Figure S2. These data validate our B cell panel of flow cytometry antibodies for RM B cell analyses.

To evaluate B cell editing, we proceeded to enrich not only CD20+ B cells but also CD19+ or even CD19– or low plasma cells using a negative B cell enrichment kit. Following negative B cell enrichment from a representative thawed apheresis cell collection, the B cell purity was over 93% (Figures S3B and S3C). The purified B cells were composed of CD20+, including N (46.3%), AM (33.3%), TLM (17.2%), and RMem (3.22%) B cells. All populations persisted following B cell enrichment, and, with a few exceptions, naive B cells remained the predominant B cell subtype observed before and after enrichment relative to AM, RMem, or TLM B cells (Figures S3A and S3B). This distribution was also similar to peripheral blood, spleen, and LN tissue B cell composition (Figure 1A), demonstrating that the purified B cells are representative of circulating B cells. Negative splenic B cell isolation from the same donor animal as analyzed in Figure 1C has led to high B cell purity (95.6% B cells following non-B cell exclusion) (Figure S4A). While AM, RMem, N, and TLM compositions remain similar between fresh (Figure 1C) and thawed splenic B cells (Figure S4B), plasma cells did not survive freeze-thaw cycle. These observations suggest that, besides plasma cells lost during freeze-thaw cycles, enriched B cells are representative of circulating B cells and that CD19+CD20+, N, RMem, TLM, and AM cells are enriched in our experimental conditions.

Ex vivo B cell culture, stimulation, and expansion

With the goal to perform ex vivo gene editing for therapeutic purposes, we investigated ex vivo RM B cell culture conditions, including proliferation kinetics following stimulation. B cells were isolated from cryopreserved apheresis products using a negative selection bead-based enrichment kit as described above and immediately transferred into culture at 2×105 cells/mL in a commercially available B cell media. CD86 was used as a marker of ex vivo B cell activation to monitor B cell stimulation.41,42,43 CD86 was weakly expressed in isolated peripheral B cells on the day of the isolation (day 0), and its expression increased significantly in the CD20+ cell population ex vivo as early as after 1 day in culture before declining by day 7 (Figures 2A and 2B). CD86 expression was slightly lower in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals relative to B cells from healthy animals; however, these differences were not significant except at day 4 of ex vivo culture (Figure 2B). In parallel, ex vivo B cell expansion was analyzed using a CellTrace CFSE cell proliferation dye to track cell division by flow cytometry in addition to cell counts. Both cell counts and flow cytometric analyses of cell divisions indicated that cell growth started after 3 days of culture in B cells from both uninfected and SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals (Figures 2C and S6), suggesting that genome engineering in cultured RM B cells could be done after 48 h of ex vivo culture, before the cells start proliferating.

Adeno-associated virus serotypes for primary B cell transduction

Ex vivo editing approaches usually require electroporation of the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex (CRISPR-Cas9 and single guide RNA) in addition to delivery of a donor template using an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector. Three AAV capsid proteins (AAV-2, -5, and -6) were evaluated to determine the optimal transduction of primary RM B cells. Healthy donor RM B cells were isolated from apheresis products and kept in culture for 48 h (Figure 3A). The cells were transduced with self-complementary AAV-2 (scAAV-2), -5, or -6 encoding a GFP transgene. To easily compare transduction efficiency between each vector, this reporter was expressed from the viral vector without a requirement for targeted genome integration. Cell viability and GFP expression were assessed by flow cytometry at days 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 14 post-transduction (Figure 3B). We did not observe any important differences in cell viability between the transduced and the untransduced mock cells (Figure 3B, right panel). Additionally, the cells were efficiently transduced, and GFP expression could be detected averaging 95% GFP+ cells at day 1 for AAV-2 and AAV-6 (Figure 3B, left panel). At day 2, 84.85% of the cells transduced with AAV-2 were GFP+, 82% for AAV-5, and 95% for AAV-6 at day 2. No significant differences were observed with the exception of AAV-2 and AAV-5 5 days post-transduction. Anticipating future applications in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed RMs, we also evaluated scAAV-2, -5, or -6 transduction using the same protocol as for uninfected B cells (Figure 3A). Surprisingly, the cell viability was more variable relative to the uninfected counterparts, even in the untransduced conditions, especially after 2 days in culture (Figure 3C, right panel). GFP expression was significantly lower 1 day post-transduction than in uninfected B cells and presented high variability between donors (Figure 3C, left panel). GFP expression following AAV-2 transduction averaged 19% GFP+ cells, 26.8% GFP+ cells for AAV-5, and 11.3% for AAV-6. GFP expression was stable after 2 days before declining. No significant differences could be observed between the serotypes when performing paired t tests. Again, AAV-5 GFP expression appeared more stable than AAV-2 and AAV-6 in both uninfected B cells and B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals. Since AAV-2, -5, and -6 transduction efficiencies were statistically similar in B cells from uninfected or infected RMs, we decided to move forward with scAAV-6, which has been extensively used in human and murine B cell applications.

Targeting the IGH locus

Endogenous antibodies are naturally produced by B cells and encoded by genes in the immunoglobulin locus. Two loci encode the Kappa or Lambda light chain, while a single locus encodes the IGH. To disrupt the formation of functional endogenous antibodies that could compete with the exogenous antibody of interest, the IGH locus was targeted. Similar to previous studies evaluating cell editing in primary human and murine B cells,28,29,34,35,49 as well as NHP B cells,36 we focused on the intronic region downstream of the J variable regions and upstream of the intronic enhancer and constant regions for insertion of a donor template encoding for the anti-HIV bNAb, VRC01.

Primary RM B cells were isolated from an apheresis product from either healthy donors (uninfected) or SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals and put in culture ex vivo as described above. Two days later, the cells were electroporated with a CRISPR-Cas9 and guide RNA (gRNA) RNP complex. Two gRNAs targeting different locations in the intronic region of the IGH (gRNA 1 and gRNA 2) were tested. Insertions and deletions (indels) were quantified 2 and 5 days post-editing (Figure 4A). Indels ranged from 50% to 92% (Figure 4B). Indel rates in uninfected B cells ranged from 70% to 89% and between 50% and 92% in B cells from SHIV+ and ART-treated animals for gRNA 1. Despite indel frequencies associated with gRNA 1 being slightly lower in cells from SHIV-infected animals relative to uninfected cells, the difference was not significant (p value = 0.6512 at day 2 and p value = 0.4679 at day 5 post-editing) and remained stable between days 2 and 5 post-editing (Figure 4B). Additionally, the indels ranged between 63% and 91% for gRNA 2 in B cells from SHIV+ and ART-treated animals (Figure 4B). As reported previously, the frequency of a microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) indel signature relative to nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) is a better predictor of homology-directed repair (HDR) for genome editing than the frequency of total indels50,51 and can guide the choice of optimal gRNA to favor HDR rates. Indeed, higher frequencies of MMEJ deletions led to higher HDR rates.50 As such, we investigated the MMEJ (large deletions ≥3 bp) versus NHEJ signatures (small indels ≥ +1, −1, and −2 bp) associated with gRNA 1 and 2 at the IGH locus. gRNA 1 led to nonsignificant but higher frequency of MMEJ DNA repair relative to NHEJ-associated DNA repair in both uninfected and infected B cells, while gRNA 2 led to higher (2 days post-editing) or similar (5 days post-editing) NHEJ frequency relative to MMEJ (Figure 4C). In addition, predicted off-target sites were identified for gRNA 2, but not for gRNA 1, using the COSMID bioinformatic off-target prediction tool52 (Tables S1 and S2). More specifically, low-scoring sites have a higher probability of being target sites. In RMs, the IGH locus is located on chromosome 7, more specifically between genomic positions 167,900,000 and 169,868,564 in the CM014342 genome assembly. While all the target sites identified for gRNA 1 are on chromosome 7 in the targeted region, gRNA 2 also seems to target one region in chromosome 9 with a low score of 4.11 (Table S2). Additionally, a similar guide (one nucleotide difference from gRNA1) targeting the human analogous site was previously validated to edit human IGH,29 facilitating translations of this editing approach between human and RM B cells. Hence, gRNA 1 was elected to pursue the work.

Insertion of a bNAb in the IGH locus

Having identified a gRNA to target the intronic region of the IGH locus, we investigated HDR in the presence of a donor template encoding for an engineered VRC01 monoclonal antibody (mAb). As previously described,29 the donor template cassette encodes VRC01 light-chain (variable and constant regions) and a recombined VDJ heavy-chain variable region, separated by a Gly-Ser-StrepII linker, controlled by an IGH VH4a B-cell-specific promoter (Figures 5, 6A, and S7), and surrounded by homology arms corresponding to the IGH intronic region of interest. The IGH VDJ heavy-chain region also contains a splicing site that allowed recombination with the endogenous heavy-chain constant region downstream of the insertion site. As described above, RM B cells were isolated from an apheresis product and cultured ex vivo. Two days later, the cells were electroporated with a CRISPR-Cas9 and gRNA 1 RNP complex followed by transduction with an scAAV-6 vector carrying the VRC01-encoding donor template. A range of 76%–93% and 91%–95% of indels were quantified 5 and 8 days post-editing, respectively (Figure 6B). An in-out PCR demonstrated integration of the VRC01-engineered mAb cassette in the IGH locus (Figures 6C and 6D), which was further confirmed by Sanger sequencing of the purified in-out PCR product, demonstrating site-specific insertion (Figure S8). VRC01 mRNA was also detected by reverse transcription PCR (Figure 7A), and VRC01 bNAb was quantified at the surface of up to 10% of B cells by flow cytometry using a StrepTactin-PE conjugate directed against the Gly-Ser-StrepII linker (Figures 7B and 7C). As a control, a mock electroporated control group transduced with the AAV-6 vector carrying VRC01 donor template in the absence of the RNP complex (transduced condition) did not show VRC01 expression as an mRNA or protein, confirming that the VRC01-engineered bNAb expression is not mediated by episomal AAV but is RNP-dependent. Further, VRC01 antibody secretion could be detected by ELISA at low concentrations in the B cell supernatant 5 and 8 days following the editing (Figures 7D and 7E). This experiment was repeated in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals. The indel efficiency was as high as in B cells from uninfected animals (Figure S9A) and associated with higher MMEJ signature relative to NHEJ (Figure S9B). Discussion

In this study, we characterized B cells and purified B cell phenotypes in blood and tissues from healthy or SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed RMs. We developed an effective protocol using CRISPR-Cas9-based editing of the IGH locus to modify RM B cells and express an engineered monoclonal bNAb of interest directed against HIV-1. Since this study would have major applications for PLWH, we also evaluated editing and AAV transduction of B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals. Our data demonstrate efficient macaque B cell editing and lay the foundation for more investigation in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals, prior to evaluation of this approach in immunocompetent NHP models.

A growing number of studies have shown efficient CD20+ B cell editing and differentiation into plasma cells ex vivo, but it remains undetermined which B cell subsets would be the most appropriate for B cell editing and persistent in vivo bNAb secretion. While plasma cells are of interest for their ability to secrete antibodies, naive B cells would also be of interest for their capacity to differentiate into diverse B cell subsets including plasma and memory B cells. Following negative B cell enrichment, we observed that purified B cells from thawed apheresis or spleens were predominantly naive B cells. Similar B cell composition was observed in blood from both healthy and SHIV-infected RM, albeit at different frequencies, as well as tissues and apheresis products from healthy donor animals. Of interest, naive B cells comprise 60%–70% of the human B cell repertoire not only in healthy individuals53 but also in PLWH.39,54,55 This observation suggests a similarity between human and RM circulating B cell phenotypes, which would facilitate translations of our editing approaches between RM and PLWH. Of note, while B cell flow cytometry detection of the markers seems to indicate similar subsets between the two species, more investigations are required to confirm their phenotypes in RMs.40 Plasma cells remain an attractive target for B cell editing and bNAb secretion. However, this cell type is rare, difficult to collect, and does not survive freeze-thaw cycles. While plasma B cells can be differentiated ex vivo,29,32,37 this would expand the time and cost of ex vivo edited B cell manufacture. It is also well documented that HIV-1 is associated with a significant impairment of the B cell population in PLWH39,54,55,56,57,58,59,60 that only partially normalize following ART treatment.59,61 This might also explain the higher frequency of naive B cells and lower frequency of tissue-like memory B cells in SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals relative to uninfected animals. Further, T follicular helper cells (Tfh), which are critical components of germinal center formation, B cell maturation, and antibody response, are also dysfunctional in HIV infection.59 Consequently, the ability of edited B cells to secrete bNAbs in the presence of viral antigens or to differentiate into memory B cells might be affected in ART-treated PLWH. These observations justify the importance of developing preclinical in vivo models of ART-suppressed HIV infection to evaluate the therapeutic potential, persistence, phenotype, and function of edited B cells for sustained bNAb secretion.

AAVs have routinely been used to deliver a donor template of interest to edited cells. However, at the time this work was performed, the optimal AAV serotype for transduction of RM B cells was unknown. As such, we decided to evaluate AAV-6, which has been successfully used in primary human B cells,29,31,32,62 as well as AAV-2 and AAV-5, two other broadly available serotypes. AAV-2, -5, and -6 transduced rhesus B cells from healthy donor with similar efficiencies. We observed that transduction of RM B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-treated animals remains less efficient than transduction of B cells from their uninfected counterparts. This could be attributed to lower recovery from thawing of B cells isolated from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals or a potential impact of in vivo ART administration on the AAV-transduced B cells. However, the impact of ART drugs on AAV transduction efficiency is unclear. For example, while intramuscular administration of AAV-8 in PLWH stably suppressed on ART in clinical trials successfully led to VRC07 bNAb expression, the absence of a comparison in PLWH not receiving ART precludes any conclusion as to the overall impact of antiretrovirals on AAV transduction.63 Another study conducted in healthy adults demonstrated similar PG9 bNAb antibody levels following rAAV-1 administration64; however, the differences in capsid, antibodies, and respective half-life and antibody-antigen clearance between the two studies prevent a comparison between the two studies. In vivo evaluation of the impact of ART drugs on AAV transduction would be required to directly address this point. While more investigations are required, these data suggest that AAV transduction efficiencies might be lower in B cells isolated from PLWH treated with ART relative to B cells from healthy donors.

The ability to edit the IGH locus from RM B cells was investigated using ex vivo RNP electroporation. The indel efficiencies in the IGH locus were similar between uninfected B cells and B cells isolated from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals following RNP electroporation. Additionally, we observed an MMEJ:NHEJ DNA repair pathways ratio predictive of an optimal HDR efficiency50 when using gRNA 1, confirmed by detection of VRC01 bNAb mRNA and protein in up to 10% of B cells edited with the same gRNA. Further, antibody secretion was detected in B cell supernatants, confirming the correct structure of the secreted antibody and its ability to bind to a soluble HIV-1 antigen. Encouragingly, similar levels of MMEJ:NHEJ ratio were observed in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-treated animals. VRC01 mRNA expression was also detected in B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed animals, but the VRC01 protein could not be detected at the cell surface. These observations suggest that the lower editing efficiencies in the latter could be due to lower AAV transductions or other mechanisms decreasing the insertion or expression of the cassette in the targeted site. The low level of VRC01-expressing B cells observed in primary RM cells, combined with the difficulty to transduce B cells from SHIV-infected and ART-treated animals using AAVs, suggests the hurdles that could be faced when applying this approach to PLWH. However, while low levels of modification in B cells isolated from PLWH would represent a challenge, some data also suggest that these approaches might still be applicable. Indeed, several studies demonstrated that edited B cells are responsive to antigens in vitro or in vivo by stimulation of bNAb-expressing B cell proliferation, class-switch recombination, affinity maturation, and bNAb secretion.26,31,35 As such, the levels of engineered B cells and exogenous bNAb secretion could be boosted upon antigen exposure. Such B cell stimulation could be observed during analytical treatment interruption (ATI) usually associated with viral rebound or could be induced by immunization with irradiated HIV-envelope-expressing cell lines65 or with a soluble antigen, a clinically relevant option.26,31,34 According to Luo et al., gene therapy approaches that achieve 1% bNAb-modified B cells in PLWH and maintain similar production rates of IgG as unmodified B cells would yield approximately 200 μg/mL bNAb levels in serum.66 Efficient engraftment of bNAb-modified B cells in relevant secondary lymphoid tissues could lower the threshold concentration for virus remission even further.23 Overall, these studies suggest that low B cell editing efficiencies could still be enough to reach therapeutic bNAb levels in the presence of viral antigens. As discussed previously,23 a therapeutic threshold concentration averaging 50–100 μg/mL of bNAbs might be required in the peripheral blood to have an impact on the plasma viremia in PLWH. However, this therapeutic level might also be dependent on the antibody isotype, the ability to reach tissue sites of virus production, the viral strain, and especially sensitivity to the bNAb. Combining several bNAbs might also lower the therapeutic threshold. Additionally, it should be noted that targeting of the same locus in primary human B cells using a similar gRNA (huIgH296 gRNA) led to 10%–50% of VRC01+ cells,29 suggesting that editing rates could be higher in primary human B cells relative to primary RM B cells. Overall, these observations highlight the necessity of a relevant model such as SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed RMs to perform pre-clinical evaluation of therapeutic cell engineering approaches for PLWH and to facilitate the development of human and RM interspecies applications. While no in vivo work has been attempted in this study, our investigation supports future in vivo investigations, especially in SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed NHP models.

Although this study focused on applications for PLWH, B cell editing holds promise for many chronic diseases in which standard of care includes repeated injections of antibody protein. A clinical trial was recently launched using ex vivo manufactured B cells for the treatment of mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I),67 highlighting the promise of B cell engineering for cell and gene therapy. Therapeutic protein production has tremendous potential for a myriad of malignant, genetic, and infectious diseases, for instance, protein replacement therapy such as protein factor IX for the treatment of hemophilia B67 and enzyme replacement therapy such as arylsulfatase A (ARSA) for the treatment of lysosomal storage disorders or cancers.33

Materials and methods

Peripheral blood and tissue processing

Red blood cells from peripheral blood were lysed in hemolytic buffer for 5 min at room temperature twice. Tissues were resuspended in RPMI 1640 + 10% FBS +1% Pen/Strep + 2mm L-glutamine or RPMI 1640 + 1% Pen/Strep, processed as single-cell suspensions through 70 μm cell strainer and collected in R10 media containing 500 μg of DNAse (Sigma, St Louis MO, cat# DN-25). When needed, red blood cell lysis was performed in ACK lysing solution.

Rhesus macaque B cell isolation and culture

The PBMCs used for isolating B cells were sourced from healthy RM donors or from SHIV-infected and ART-suppressed RMs. The animals were infected with SHIV1157ipd3N4 for 5 to 7 weeks before receiving ART regimen (dolutegravir, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate kindly provided by Gilead Sciences) for 70 weeks until apheresis collection. The PBMCs were collected via automated leukapheresis from the Washington National Primate Research Center (WaNPRC) at the University of Washington. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of the Fred Hutch Cancer Center and UW, protocol #3235-08. RM B cells were isolated by negative selection, utilizing the NHP B Cell Negative Isolation Kit (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver BC Canada, #100-0345) following the manufacturer’s instructions. B cells were cultured in ImmunoCult-XF T cell Expansion Medium (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver BC Canada, #10981) at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL and supplemented with 50× ImmunoCult-ACF Human B Cell Expansion Supplement, a 50×-concentrated serum- and animal-component free supplement to support B cell activation, expansion, and maturation into plasma cells (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver BC Canada, #10974). CellTrace CFSE dye (Life Technologies, Carlsbad CA, #C1157) was added to B cells prior to cell culture according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

AAV cloning and vector production

The AAV-GFP transduction experiments were performed using an EGFP expressed from an scAAV vector backbone under the control of a spleen focus-forming virus (SFFV) promoter. The donor template cassettes for targeted engineering of the NHP IgH locus were cloned between the ITR sequences of the pSUB201 AAV2 vector backbone. For the bNAb-encoding donor template, a single-chain open reading frame, driven by a strong RM VH4a B-cell-specific promoter (VH4-38-2, isolated from variable region VH4, the most highly expressed and abundant allele in the rhesus genome),68,69,70 was constructed, consisting of the RM IGH signal sequence followed directly by the VRC01 light chain (RM codon-optimized VRC01 variable region, plus RM kappa constant region), a 57 amino acid-long 3× Gly-Ser-StrepII linker, and RM codon-optimized VRC01 heavy-chain variable region ending with the RM mu-splice site consensus providing for recombination with the endogenous IGH constant region located downstream of the insertion site. This codon-optimized version of VRC01 was inserted between 450 bp upstream and 450 bp downstream homology arms corresponding to the IgH intronic region of interest located just upstream of RM IGH exon 4. This RM codon-optimized single-chain VRC01 mAb construct was synthesized as a gene fragment (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA) and cloned in-frame between the RM signal peptide and mu-splice site by site-directed mutagenesis using the QuikChange II system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

High-titer scAAV-2, -5, and -6 were produced by the Cell Manipulation Tools Core-Vector Production of Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center. 293T cells were transiently transfected with a PEI-DNA (Polysciences, Warrington, PA) complex in 15 cm2 tissue-culture plates. Transfected cells were harvested 72 h post-transfection. Harvested cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (0.001% Pluronic acid, Gibco, Waltham, MA). Cells were subjected to multiple freeze-thaw cycles followed by Benzonase (Millipore, Burlington, MA) treatment. Benzonase-treated lysate was centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min. Supernatant was filter-sterilized with a 0.45-μm syringe filter and aliquoted in Eppendorf tubes to store at −80°C. QuantStudio 3 TaqMan assay was performed to determine the viral vector titrations.

Electroporation and editing with CRISPR/Cas9

The gRNAs (Table S3) were ordered from Synthego Biosciences (Redwood City, CA) with 2′-O-methyl modifications at the 3′ first and last bases, as well as 3′ phosphorothioate bonds between the first three and last two bases. For each experimental condition, a total of 8×105 primary B cells were used. These cells were subjected to three washes with 1× D-PBS and then centrifuged at 300×g before the electroporation. The CRISPR RNP complex was prepared using 48 pmol of TrueCut Cas9 Protein v2 protein (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) mixed with 240 pmol of gRNA. This mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Subsequently, 4 μL of this mixture was added to the cells, along with 16 μL of supplemented P3 primary cell nucleofector solution. In total, 20 μL was added to the 16-well Nucleocuvette Strips from Lonza (Walkersville, MD). The electroporation program used was CY-100. After the electroporation run was completed, 80 μL of pre-warmed medium was added to each sample, and the total of 100 μL was transferred to a 48-well plate containing 320 μL of pre-warmed media. After a 30-min incubation of the electroporated cells at 37°C, 20% volume of scAAV was added to the cells, which were then incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The cells were washed once with 1× D-PBS, centrifuged at 800×g, and cultured at a concentration of 2×105 cells/mL in supplemented medium. Potential off-target sites associated with gRNA 1 and gRNA 2 were predicted using COSMID (CRISPR Off-target Sites with Mismatches, Insertions, and Deletions)52 (http://crispr.bme.gatech.edu), a bioinformatic-based tool.

Flow cytometry

B cell purity was analyzed using an NHP lineage panel consisting of the following markers: CD45 V450 (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #561291); CD3 BV786 (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #563918); CD16 APC CY7 (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #557758); CD11b PE (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #557321); CD14 PE-CY7 (eBioscience, San Diego CA, #25-0149-42); and CD20 APC (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #559776). The B cell phenotypes were characterized using the following markers: CD19 PC7 (Beckman Coulter, Brea CA, #IM3628U); CD20 APC (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #559776); CD27 eFluor 450 (eBioscience, San Diego CA, #48-0279-42); CD86 PerCP-eFluor 710 (eBioscience, San Diego CA, #46-0869-42); CD38 APC-eFluor 780 (eBioscience, San Diego CA, #47-00389-42); CD138 PE (eBioscience, San Diego CA, #12-1389-42); CD3 BUV395 (BD Horizon, Franklin Lakes NJ, #564117); CD14 BUV395 (BD Horizon, Franklin Lakes NJ, #563561); CD16 BUV395 (BD #563785); CD11b BUV395 (BD Horizon, Franklin Lakes NJ, #563839); and CD21 BUV737 (BD, Franklin Lakes NJ, #612789). All flow cytometry antibodies are listed in Table S4. Engineered VRC01 antibody expression was assessed at 1:50–1:100 dilution of StrepTactin PE (IBA Lifesciences, Goettingen Germany, #6-5000-001). The number of cells used ranged from 2×105 to 1×106 per condition and were run on a BD FACSymphony flow cytometer (Franklin Lake, NJ). All flow analyses were performed using FlowJo (Ashland, OR) software.

Indel quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from 5×104 to 1×105 cells at different time points using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, #56304), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For IGH PCR, 50–100 ng of gDNA from each sample was used and 0.2 μM of IgH forward primer (5′-ACACCTGGAGACAGAAGGTCT-3′) and IgH reverse primer (5′-CAGCCACCCTTTCTGGACT-3′) were mixed with 45 μL of Platinum PCR SuperMix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, #12532016). The PCR program comprised an initial step at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 68°C for 40 s. All primers were ordered from IDT (Coralville, IA). For IGH gel electrophoresis, 10 μL of the IGH PCR product mixed with 2 μL of 6× loading dye per sample was loaded. The expected PCR product size was 670 nt. The remaining PCR product was purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, #28104), and 25 ng of purified DNA mixed with 3 μL of 3 μM IgH primers was submitted for Sanger sequencing by the Genomics Fred Hutch Shared Resources core. The analyses were performed using Inference of CRISPR Edits (ICE) software by Synthego (Redwood City, CA).

All primer sequences can be found in Table S5.

In-out VRC01 PCR

Fifty to one hundred nanograms of DNA from each sample was mixed with added 0.2 μM of forward (5′-GAGCCACCCACAATTCGAGA-3′) and reverse primer (5′-ACTCAGTCCTGCATCAGAGC-3′), along with 45 μL of Platinum PCR SuperMix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, #12532016) per sample. The PCR program included an initial step at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 62°C for 62 s, with an extension step at 72°C for 10 min. Gel electrophoresis was performed using 10 μL of the PCR product mixed with 2 μL of 6× loading dye and the expected PCR product size was 1,030 nt.

Actin PCR

For actin PCR, we utilized 50–100 ng of DNA from each sample and added 0.2 μM of actin forward primer (5′-GAATGAACGCTTCCGATGCC-3′) and reverse primer (5′-AGCCCACGAATATGGAAGCC-3′) to 45 μL of Platinum PCR SuperMix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, #12532016) per sample, resulting in a final primer concentration of 0.2 μM. The PCR program included an initial step at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 68°C for 24 s, with an extension step at 72°C for 10 min. Gel electrophoresis was performed using 10 μL of the PCR product mixed with 2 μL of 6× loading dye per sample, and the expected PCR product size was 399 nt.

VRC01 mRNA PCR

Total RNA was extracted from 5 × 104–1×105 of B cells using the PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, #KIT020) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcriptase was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System [Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, #KIT020418090010]). The PCR was performed using 50–100 ng of cDNA from each sample mixed with 0.2 μM forward (5′TCAAATGAAAAAGCCCGGCG-3′) and reverse primer (5′-CTTACCGCGGGTACAGAAGT-3′). The PCR program included an initial step at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 68°C for 17 s.

ELISA

96-Well MaxiSorp plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) were coated with 50 ng of HIV-1 BaL gp120 recombinant protein (NIH AIDS Reagent Program, Division of AIDS, NIAID, NIH) or 1× PBS for negative controls and incubated overnight at 4°C, washed four times with 0.05% PBS-Tween 20, then incubated in blocking buffer (5% milk and 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples (cell-free supernatants) or purified VRC01 mAb as positive control and standard curve (anti-HIV-1 gp120 monoclonal VRC01, from Dr. John Mascola, NIH AIDS Reagent Program, Division of AIDS, NIAID, NIH) were serially diluted in a U-bottom 96-well plate in blocking buffer. Fifty microliters μL of this mixture was transferred to the MaxiSorp plate, incubated for 1 h at room temperature, and washed four times before the addition of peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-monkey (human cross-reactive) IgG secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich SAB3700766; 1:3,000 dilution) for purified VRC01 or anti-Strep-tag II StrepMAB-Classic (IBA, 2-1509-001, 1:5,000 dilution) for B-cell-secreted VRC01 for 1 h at room temperature. The plates were washed four times before addition of the 3,3′,5,5′ tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature on an orbital shaker. The reaction was stopped by addition of 1 N H2SO4. The absorbance was read at 450 nm using a BioTek EL800 microplate absorbance reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT).
