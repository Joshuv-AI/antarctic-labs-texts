Picking Up the Phone: Clinical Perspectives on Recontacting Patients and Their Families
Authors: Hamish Bulford, Megan Munsie, Catherine Quinlan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsWhen test results are reanalysed after a patient has been discharged from a service, ‘recontact’ can be necessary to inform them of pertinent new information. This scoping review aims to investigate perspectives of clinical healthcare professionals on this recontact and its associated considerations.MethodsWe searched Medline and Embase for studies investigating the perspectives of healthcare professionals on patient recontact.ResultsOf 518 articles identified, 13 articles were included in the review. All 13 articles examined recontact in the setting of genetics. There was a consensus that recontact can be necessary, but there was disagreement about where the responsibility to initiate it lies. Challenges identified included logistical strain and validating patient consent.ConclusionPatient recontact is seen as important, but concerns around workload drive a lack of consensus around where the responsibility to recontact lies. There is scope for more research on the implications of recontact outside the field of genetics, and clarification is needed around reasonable expectations of recontact in different clinical situations.

Introduction

There are numerous clinical situations in which the contact of patients after they have been discharged from a service is deemed necessary. An important reason for this ‘recontact’ is the reanalysis of a patient's test results yielding new information which needs to be communicated to them. A common example is a fracture in the emergency department that is not picked up until formal inspection of the X‐ray, by which time the patient has been discharged. The patient and their family must then be recontacted with this new information.

Perhaps the most widely investigated example of recontact is after genomic sequencing for the diagnosis of genetic disease [1]. Reanalysis of genomic test results has been robustly shown to improve diagnostic yield in cases of rare genetic disease [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. This raises the possibility and potentially the obligation to reanalyse patients' genomic test results and recontact them with pertinent information. For example, a child may present with a neurodevelopmental syndrome for which a genetic cause is strongly suspected, but upon genome sequencing no cause is found. However, if that child's genomic data is reanalysed 2 years later, with new bioinformatic techniques and new variant classifications, it is possible that a cause and thus a diagnosis could then be discovered, necessitating recontact. In this way, reanalysis and recontact, whilst distinct processes, have been considered intrinsically linked. Recontact can also be proactive in that it raises a previously unidentified issue for the patient. A non‐genetic example of proactive recontact is that of paediatric patient families after an incidental finding of microscopic haematuria. Persistent microscopic haematuria has been shown to be a risk factor for genetic kidney disease [7]. Thus these families can be recontacted to recommend genetic testing [8]. This kind of proactive recontact is also prominent in clinical genetics and oncology, in which patients with newly‐identified high‐risk variants might be recontacted to recommend regular cancer screening [9].

Patient recontact comes with various ethical, social and legal dimensions. Exploration of these dimensions has often centred on the extent to which there exists a duty, ethical and/or legal, to recontact patients and how it is incorporated into the traditional duty of care of the physician [10, 11]. A systematic review concluded that whilst recontact is ethically desirable, a general duty to recontact does not exist, neither legally nor ethically [12]. The existence of a general duty is simply not feasible in a resource‐limited environment and clarity is needed on situations in which recontact should be expected as good practice.

Stakeholder perspectives are necessary to inform when and how recontact is performed. Investigation into the attitudes of patients and patients' families towards recontacting shows support for the idea of recontacting as a mechanism for expeditiously bringing relevant information to families [13, 14, 15, 16]. Canvassing the views of healthcare professionals, as another key stakeholder, is crucial in gauging support for recontact and identifying barriers to implementation. This scoping review will examine the attitudes of healthcare professionals towards patient recontact with a view to elucidating avenues of future research.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

The literature was searched in August 2024 on two databases, Medline and Embase. The search strategy involved searching article titles using one of the terms ‘reanalys* or recontact* or re‐contact*/reinterpret*/disclose’ combined by Boolean operators with either ‘challenge*/issue*/ethic*/duty/complex/practice*’ or ‘experience*/view*/perspective*/attitude*/qualitative/survey*/concern*/themes*’. Searches were also limited to English language articles and human research studies.

Selection of Papers

The search of the literature identified 518 articles in total (Figure 1). This was reduced to 307 articles following the removal of duplicate articles, with a further 230 articles excluded due to irrelevance to the topic based on review of titles and abstracts. The remaining 77 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility and another 52 articles were excluded because they (i) did not assess stakeholder perspectives (39); (ii) did not assess clinical perspectives on recontact; (iii) did not prove to examine patient recontact (9); (iv) only addressed recontact in the research setting rather than the clinical setting (4); or (v) had no full‐text available (1). 13 articles canvassing clinical perspectives on patient recontact were ultimately selected for analysis in this review (see Table 1).

Results

Whilst the earliest of the selected publications was from 1999 [17], the majority were published in the last 8 years, suggesting that the interest in this line of investigation has significantly increased in recent years. Of the 13 articles identified, four were from North America; three from the United Kingdom; two from Europe; two from Australasia; and one each from Japan and Israel (Table 1). Seven articles explored healthcare professionals' views through completion of surveys with the remainder using semi‐structured interviews. Participating healthcare professionals included oncologists, genetic counsellors and staff working in clinical genetic services including clinical geneticists. All publications identified investigated recontact in the setting of genetics. Several themes emerged from the articles and are explored below.

A Duty to Recontact?

Overall, there was confusion around whether there existed a general ethical duty to recontact patients when new information became available. Two surveys of genetic counsellors in the field of cancer, one of which included oncologists, generated markedly different results. Makhnoon et al. found a broad support for the return of VUS reclassifications to patients, regardless of its clinical utility [25]. Mueller et al. however, found that only 6% of genetic counsellors surveyed responded that there was either ‘always’ or ‘often’ an ethical duty to recontact patients to offer or recommend genetic testing for cancer, and that it depended on the gravity and actionability of the new information [21].

There were also discrepancies between healthcare professionals and patients in the extent to which they believe that an ethical duty exists. Fitzpatrick et al. surveyed genetic service providers as to whether a duty exists in various scenarios and compared their answers with those of family members of patients with genetic disease [17]. Almost universally across all scenarios, service providers answered that less of a duty to recontact exists than that expressed by family members when answered. This gap in expectation was identified as cause for concern in healthcare professionals elsewhere, both that patients are not getting the care they need and that clinicians may be liable [14]. Concerns about possible liability related to missed recontact were raised in several articles, and there was general uncertainty around how recontact was viewed legally [14, 21].

Where Does the Responsibility Lie?

Where the responsibility lies for initiating reanalysis of test results is a contested issue. Arguments have been made that the laboratory, the clinician, the patient or some combination of them bear this responsibility. However, the consensus position in the majority of articles analysed was that the responsibility should, to some degree, be shared between patient and clinician [14, 17, 20, 23]. Mueller et al. went slightly further, with the majority of participants asserting that it was predominantly a patient responsibility [21]. Several reasons were put forward to justify this view. The most common was simply that clinicians do not have the time to stay up to date with every advancement in knowledge, search their database for patient details, and initiate the recontact. By sharing responsibility with the patient, it minimises unrealistic expectations of the clinician [14]. Another prominent reason was that it helped empower patients to take the lead in their health.

There were, however, real concerns raised in multiple articles regarding apportioning responsibility to patients. One common concern was that patients are diverse in their capacity to take responsibility for their health. Patients with significant social or neurocognitive challenges would likely struggle to either grasp the potential import of recontact or to manage it logistically [14, 23]. This is particularly relevant in the realm of genetic disease, in which patients and their parents may have a neurodisability [14]. As such, too much responsibility on the patient to initiate recontact could result in health inequity.

Current Recontacting Practice

Across the articles analysed there was the lack of standard recontacting practices. Surveys of clinical genetics services across the United Kingdom, Europe, Japan, Israel and Australasia found that some degree of recontact was being conducted, but that it was being done on a completely ad hoc basis [14, 18, 19, 23, 24, 26]. This was often coupled with discussion about the lack of guidelines regarding recontacting in the field of clinical genetics [23, 25]. Sirchia et al. for example, surveyed 105 clinical genetics centres across Europe and found that whilst 100 of them had performed recontact at some stage, only 37 stated that they did it in a routine manner [19].

The concern with this inconsistent approach to recontact is that there are patients for whom recontact would be beneficial who are not receiving it. Vora et al. found that it was often a chance encounter with a family member that prompted the recontact of former patients [24]. Conversely, it is possible that ad hoc recontact occurs with a better understanding of clinical context than guideline‐mandated recontact and thus may be delivered in a more tailored manner.

Recontact: Barriers and Caveats

Perhaps the most common barrier identified regarding the systematic recontact of patients was the logistical burden. A lack of resources to facilitate recontact on top of day‐to‐day clinical work was the most common logistical barrier raised [14, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26]. Many health services reported being under‐resourced to perform everyday clinical duties and that systematically recontacting patients was simply not feasible. An alternate model that alters the discussion on logistics is the automation of reanalysis of old test results. Fehlberg et al. show general support for automated reanalysis in rare genetic disease cohorts as a means of streamlining the return of pertinent information to patients and optimising the use of resources [28]. If automated reanalysis becomes ubiquitous, this may alleviate some of the logistical strain of ad hoc reanalysis.

A foundational idea that supports patient recontact is the theoretical right of patients to know about relevant updates. However, the counterargument, that patients also have a theoretical right not to know, needs to be considered. The notion of psychological harm resulting from an unwanted recontact is raised in several articles as an important caveat [19, 26]. A key consideration that this raises is the need to consent to be recontacted, and how this would be implemented. The simplest idea is that patients, at the time of a test, would be asked their consent for future recontact if their results were reinterpreted. However, even in this case, given that recontact may take place years later, their reaction to an unanticipated recontact could still be negative. Fehlberg et al. put forward the idea of dynamic consent in which patients can later opt out or are asked again when they transition from paediatric to adult care [28]. This could mitigate a situation in which a child's parents opt out of recontact, but subsequently the child wishes to access the information.

Multiple articles examined the case in which a health professional may choose to contact a patient despite the patient being recorded as not wanting to be recontacted. Sirchia et al. found that 64% of the European clinical genetics services surveyed responded that there would be situations in which they would deem this appropriate [19]. Reasons put forward included informing future reproductive decision‐making and the possibility that the patient had changed their mind but had not had a chance to indicate this. Sakaguchi et al. also asked this question of genetic services in Japan and found that approximately half the services answered in the affirmative [26]. Both these articles highlight the lack of consensus in healthcare professionals around the role of consent in patient recontact.

Discussion

This review comes with limitations. The literature search was only conducted across peer‐reviewed journals, and thus national or local guidelines on patient recontact that were not formally published will have been missed. Furthermore, the notion of recontact is broad enough that there may be other terms used for it that the search might not have covered. Whilst limited in its scope, this review provides an overview of the perspectives of healthcare professionals on the recontact of patients. The main themes to emerge were the debate over the extent to which there is an ethical duty to recontact; where the responsibility for initiating recontact should lie; barriers to recontact including logistical and economic challenges; and the fragility of consenting to recontact. There is a general desire among healthcare professionals to implement recontact, but concerns that establishing a duty to do so would further burden the healthcare workforce and open up liability. In turn, these concerns feed a lack of consensus around who should bear the responsibility for recontact.

It should be reiterated that all articles identified pertained to recontact in the setting of genetic reanalysis, emphasising that recontact has largely not been explored in other fields. There are several possible reasons for this. Recontact is relevant when there is a new interpretation of old test results. Clinical genetics has moved rapidly over the last two decades and variants of unknown significance (VUS) are regularly being reclassified. Implications of genetic test results are thus highly dynamic in a way that the implications of non‐genetic tests, such as blood markers of kidney function, rarely are. The focus on recontact in the context of clinical genetics may also reflect the growth in funding over the last two decades [29] that has facilitated empirical studies on stakeholder perspectives. The salience of recontact in genetics has been recognised by professional bodies, but whilst recommendations have been published abroad, no Australian guidelines on recontact exist [30, 31]. Given the increasing relevance of recontact in non‐genetic fields, such as informing patients about new therapies, it is conspicuous that stakeholder perspectives outside of genetics are yet to have been canvassed.

Most studies analysed canvassed the views of genetic healthcare professionals, such as genetic counsellors or clinical geneticists. However, given the rapidly increasing prevalence of genetic testing in all medical fields, it might often be non‐genetic clinical staff who make the recontact after a genetic test. This could be general practitioners or specialists, particularly in fields in which genetic testing is already common such as paediatrics and oncology. Oncologists were featured in several of the studies analysed [25, 27], but the views of paediatricians are yet to be well‐characterised. Recontacting in the paediatric setting comes with certain unique considerations. Firstly, there is greater scope for recontact in paediatrics by virtue of the longer duration for which medical records are held. Secondly, it is possible that the child has developed decision‐making capacity since the original testing was done. This is addressed by Felhberg et al. who propose either a dynamic consent or at least its re‐evaluation upon the transition to adult care [28]. Lastly, genomic testing is particularly common in children, and thus there is increased scope for reanalysis and recontact.

The logistical burden of systematic recontact after reanalysis presents as perhaps the largest factor in the hesitation of healthcare professionals. Given this barrier, the topic of automation is salient. Automated reanalysis to inform recontact was only discussed in depth in one of the articles analysed, but it is a concept receiving increasing attention [28]. Automated reanalysis pipelines have been proposed in both genetic and non‐genetic settings, but without detailed exploration of the accompanying recontact [32, 33, 34]. Contacting patients after receiving an automated message to do so will likely be relatively unfamiliar to many clinicians, and canvassing their views on the topic will be important.

An element of recontact in genetics that was only briefly touched was its impact on health equity. This angle in the genetic setting was explored by Mackley and Chad in a recent review in which it is argued that recontact has the potential to either help or entrench health inequities [35]. They posit that as VUS are more common in non‐European ethnic groups due to a lack of diversity in genomic datasets, patients from these groups have more scope to benefit from VUS reclassification. This positions recontact as a tool to help resolve health inequities. However, in situations in which the patient bears some responsibility for recontact, patients with greater social advantage and the health literacy that comes with that will be better positioned to receive the benefits of recontact. There is scope for greater exploration of this equity dimension.

As healthcare becomes more proactive, recontact will be increasingly important. There is recognition of this amongst healthcare professionals, but accompanying concerns around workload and ethical complexities. Greater clarity is needed around which situations and specialties may warrant a lower threshold for recontact, such as paediatrics. Future work is needed to elucidate gaps between patient and clinician expectations, and construct standards that balance logistical feasibility with patient needs.
