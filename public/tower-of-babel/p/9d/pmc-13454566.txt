Short-term intraocular pressure changes following intravitreal aflibercept 8 mg versus 2 mg in diabetic macular edema: a prospective observational study
Authors: Ken Hoshiyama, Takao Hirano, Erika Sekiya, Shingo Kajiwara, Shuji Mukawa, Yoshiaki Chiku, Marie Nakamura, Noriko Yoshida, Toshinori Murata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis prospective observational study evaluated short-term intraocular pressure (IOP) changes and safety following intravitreal injection of aflibercept 8 mg versus 2 mg in diabetic macular edema (DME). We included 40 eyes from 36 patients with center-involving DME, with 18 patients and 20 eyes in each group. IOP was measured at baseline (sitting and supine), 30 s post-injection (supine, T1), and at 5, 15, and 30 min (sitting, T2–T4). Vitreous reflux (VR), paracentesis, and baseline data were also assessed. Both groups showed a marked but transient IOP rise, peaking at T1 (59.0 ± 17.4 mmHg and 60.1 ± 20.8 mmHg) and returning to near baseline by T4 (14.6 ± 4.6 mmHg and 16.6 ± 6.7 mmHg), without significant between-group differences. No eyes required paracentesis, and VR severity was comparable. At T1, greater VR or prior vitrectomy was associated with lower IOP, but this effect was not sustained, and other baseline factors did not significantly affect IOP. These findings suggest that aflibercept 8 mg induces a transient IOP elevation comparable to that with aflibercept 2 mg, with IOP returning to baseline levels by 30 min. Additionally, aflibercept 8 mg demonstrated an acceptable short-term safety profile in eyes with DME.Keywords: Diabetic macular edema, Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor, Intravitreal injection, Aflibercept 8 mg, Intraocular pressure, Vitreous refluxSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Since its clinical introduction in 2006, intravitreal delivery of anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents has revolutionized the management of retinal diseases such as diabetic macular edema (DME), neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), and retinal vein occlusion1–4. These biologic treatments have significantly improved visual acuity and anatomical outcomes, transforming the prognosis of patients who once faced inevitable progressive vision loss.

Aflibercept, a recombinant fusion protein with high binding affinity for VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and placental growth factor (PlGF), has gained widespread clinical adoption due to its durable efficacy and favorable safety profile5–7. To reduce treatment burden and extend dosing intervals, a high-dose aflibercept 8 mg formulation was developed alongside the conventional aflibercept 2 mg formulation.

The 8 mg formulation requires a larger injection volume (0.07 mL vs. 0.05 mL), introducing a greater amount of fluid into the eye. Phase 3 clinical trials, including PHOTON (for DME) and PULSAR (for nAMD), demonstrated that aflibercept 8 mg sustained long-term visual function while reducing the frequency of injections, leading to its regulatory approval in 20248,9.

Short-term intraocular pressure (IOP) elevations are well-documented after intravitreal injection8–10. Although these post-injection IOP spikes usually normalize within minutes, they may raise safety concerns in eyes with reduced optic nerve reserve or impaired aqueous humor outflow11,12. The magnitude and duration of IOP elevation are influenced by the volume of injected fluid, injection technique, and ocular anatomical characteristics11,13.

Given the larger injection volume of aflibercept 8 mg, there is concern that it may result in a more pronounced or prolonged IOP spike during the immediate post-injection period14,15. However, clinical trial data have not shown sustained or clinically significant IOP elevation attributable to the drug itself16,17. Data on immediate IOP changes within the first 30 min post-injection in real-world clinical conditions remain limited. Furthermore, few studies have simultaneously assessed additional safety factors including vitreous reflux (VR) or the need for anterior chamber paracentesis to resolve high IOP18,19.

This prospective observational study aimed to characterize short-term IOP changes and related safety parameters following intravitreal administration of aflibercept 8 mg compared with aflibercept 2 mg, providing clinically relevant evidence to inform treatment planning and peri-procedural risk management.

Results

Patient characteristics

Ocular parameters showed no significant between-group differences in anterior chamber depth (3.79 ± 0.60 vs. 3.78 ± 0.47 mm; P = 0.753), axial length (23.7 ± 1.1 vs. 24.0 ± 1.3 mm; P = 0.501), or the number of prior intravitreal anti-VEGF injections (15.2 ± 13.1 vs. 11.6 ± 11.8; P = 0.523). Similarly, lens status (phakic/pseudophakic: 3/17 vs. 5/15 eyes; P = 0.695) and history of vitrectomy (2 vs. 3 eyes; P = 0.999) were balanced between the two groups (Table 1).

Vitreous reflux and anterior chamber paracentesis

The severity of VR, graded immediately after injection, was 1.35 ± 0.49 in the 2 mg group and 1.20 ± 0.62 in the 8 mg group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.127). No eyes required anterior chamber paracentesis.

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between VR grade and IOP at T1 in both groups: 2 mg: rs = − 0.513 (P = 0.021); 8 mg: rs = − 0.491 (P = 0.028). These findings indicate that greater VR was associated with lower immediate post-injection IOP levels. Consistently, among the four eyes with IOP < 25 mmHg at T1, three had VR grades of 2 or higher. By T4, however, no significant correlations were observed: 2 mg: rs = − 0.071 (P = 0.767); 8 mg: rs = − 0.231 (P = 0.327) (Fig. 2).

Association between IOP and number of prior injections

The number of prior intravitreal anti-VEGF injections ranged from 0 to 40 in the 2 mg group and 0–35 in the 8 mg group. Effect of vitrectomy history on IOP

At T1, eyes with prior vitrectomy showed lower IOP (31.9 ± 23.9 mmHg) compared with eyes without vitrectomy (62.0 ± 14.5 mmHg, P = 0.042) in the 2 mg group. At T4, mean IOP was 8.4 ± 3.5 vs. 15.3 ± 4.2 mmHg (P = 0.063). In the 8 mg group, mean IOP was 58.9 ± 39.8 vs. 60.3 ± 17.8 mmHg at T1 (P = 0.616) and 18.2 ± 6.8 vs. 16.4 ± 6.8 mmHg at T4 (P = 0.842). Although eyes with prior vitrectomy had significantly lower IOP at T1 in the 2 mg group, interpretation is limited by the very small number of vitrectomized eyes (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

Transient elevation in IOP following intravitreal anti-VEGF injections is well documented8,10,11; however, studies specifically addressing aflibercept 8 mg remain limited18,19. To our knowledge, no previous prospective study has comprehensively assessed IOP fluctuations within the first 30 min after an aflibercept 8 mg injection for DME, while simultaneously accounting for multiple contributing factors, including VR.

The most notable finding of this study was that IOP spikes after aflibercept 8 mg injections were transient and similar in magnitude and time course to aflibercept 2 mg. At 30 s post-injection (T1), peak IOP reached 59.0 ± 17.4 mmHg in the 2 mg group and 60.1 ± 20.8 mmHg in the 8 mg group. By 30 min (T4), IOP spontaneously decreased to 14.6 ± 4.6 mmHg and 16.6 ± 6.7 mmHg, respectively, confirming that the pressure elevation was transient and reversible. These findings are consistent with previous reports documenting transient IOP elevation following intravitreal anti-VEGF injections8,10,11,18–21.

Bracha et al., in a review of anti-VEGF agents—such as pegaptanib, ranibizumab, aflibercept, and bevacizumab—reported that these agents commonly induce transient IOP elevations to approximately 46 mmHg, which spontaneously normalizes within one hour11. Similarly, El Chehab et al. reported that IOP rose to 42.7 ± 3.0 mmHg following administration of aflibercept 2 mg10. In a previous study by the same group, a peak IOP of 46.4 ± 4.8 mmHg was observed after bevacizumab or ranibizumab injections, showing a comparable pattern21. These findings suggest that, when using a standard injection volume of 0.05 mL, a transient IOP spike of similar magnitude may occur regardless of the specific anti-VEGF agent administered.

Because aflibercept 8 mg requires a larger injection volume (0.07 mL), concerns have been raised regarding a potentially greater IOP spike. However, in our study, IOP elevations in the 8 mg group were comparable in magnitude and time course to those in the 2 mg group. This aligns with an in vitro study by Allmendinger et al., who demonstrated that IOP increases exponentially only beyond 100 µL, suggesting that differences within the 0.05–0.07 mL range are clinically negligible15.

Paris et al. primarily investigated IOP changes following aflibercept 8 mg injection and additionally included aflibercept 2 mg and faricimab as comparators18. They measured IOP at 30 s post-injection in the supine position and reported IOP spikes of 54.17 ± 13.47 mmHg in the 2 mg group, 57.16 ± 10.07 mmHg in the 8 mg group, and 46.33 ± 11.64 mmHg in the faricimab group. These values are consistent with our findings for aflibercept. Interestingly, the faricimab group exhibited lower IOP levels, which the authors attributed to differences in syringe type. Use of a prefilled syringe (PFS), as employed in the 2 mg group, has been associated with greater IOP spikes compared to vial-based preparations, potentially due to factors such as excess injection volume, faster injection speed, or increased injection force22–24. In our study, the 2 mg group received aflibercept via a PFS, whereas the 8 mg group used a vial preparation, which may have contributed to the comparable IOP outcomes despite the nominal difference in injection volume.

We also evaluated VR, defined as the leakage of a portion of the injected solution from the eye immediately after intravitreal injection. Prior studies have suggested that VR may mitigate IOP elevation by relieving intraocular volume load9,25, and our analysis likewise demonstrated a significant negative correlation between VR and IOP immediately after injection (T1), indicating that greater reflux was associated with lower peak IOP levels. However, despite the larger injection volume in the 8 mg group, VR scores were not significantly different from those in the 2 mg group. These findings suggest that reflux-related volume loss may not fully account for the comparable IOP profiles observed between the groups. The absence of increased VR severity in the 8 mg group also supports the interpretation that aflibercept 8 mg was not associated with excessive immediate IOP elevation under the present injection conditions.

We further examined whether anatomical and procedural factors—including anterior chamber depth, axial length, the number of prior anti-VEGF injections, lens status, and prior vitrectomy—were associated with acute IOP spikes. To reduce potential confounding, eyes with glaucoma were excluded. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, supporting the validity of these subgroup analyses.

No statistically significant associations were found between anterior chamber depth or lens status (phakic vs. pseudophakic) and acute IOP changes in the 2 mg or 8 mg group. Conversely, several previous studies have suggested that anatomical characteristics of the anterior segment may influence acute IOP fluctuations.

Kerimoglu et al. reported that intravitreal injection of 0.1 mL triamcinolone acetonide led to a significant reduction in anterior chamber depth and volume, with phakic eyes possessing shallow anterior chambers exhibiting greater IOP elevation and slower recovery13. Similarly, the clinical relevance of shallow anterior chambers was highlighted in a case report of acute angle-closure attack following a 0.05 mL bevacizumab injection, suggesting that such anatomical characteristics may predispose eyes to rapid IOP elevation26. Regarding injection volume, Alkin et al. demonstrated that eyes receiving 0.1 mL intravitreal injections experienced more pronounced anterior chamber shallowing and higher IOP elevation compared to those receiving 0.05 mL, suggesting that larger injection volumes may directly affect anterior segment configuration and pressure response27.

Although the presence or absence of the crystalline lens may theoretically alter anterior segment anatomy and intraocular fluid dynamics, its influence on IOP spikes remains inconclusive. Earlier studies reported conflicting results: some observed greater IOP elevation and slower recovery in phakic eyes compared with pseudophakic eyes13, while others found higher initial IOP spikes in pseudophakic eyes after aflibercept injection10. More recent studies, however, have shown no significant association between lens status and post-injection IOP changes following anti-VEGF therapy19,28,29. Our findings are consistent with those of recent reports, suggesting that lens status is not a primary determinant of acute IOP fluctuations.

Taken together, while anterior segment anatomical features such as anterior chamber depth and lens status may have theoretical implications for IOP dynamics, their clinical impact appears limited in anatomically unremarkable eyes undergoing standard-volume (0.05–0.07 mL) intravitreal injections.

Furthermore, we explored the potential influence of prior vitrectomy. The number of vitrectomized eyes was limited, yet subgroup analysis revealed a trend toward lower IOP at T1 among these eyes in the 2 mg group. This observation aligns with that of previous reports showing that eyes without prior vitrectomy are more prone to marked IOP elevation after intravitreal injection, as the intact vitreous body restricts fluid displacement and reduces the capacity to buffer acute volume changes19,30. Although our findings support this concept, the small sample size precludes firm conclusions, and further large-scale studies are needed to clarify the influence of vitrectomy status on post-injection IOP dynamics.

Likewise, no statistically significant association was found between axial length and acute IOP changes. Anatomically, shorter axial length is typically associated with a smaller vitreous cavity, while longer axial length has been linked to decreased scleral rigidity14. These characteristics could, in theory, influence IOP response to intravitreal injections. Some studies have suggested that eyes with shorter axial length may be more vulnerable to IOP spikes, possibly due to reduced intraocular volume and limited buffering capacity28,31,32. Conversely, other investigations have reported no significant relationship between axial length and post-injection IOP changes9,33. Furthermore, Bracha et al. noted that although shorter axial length has been proposed as a potential risk factor for acute IOP elevation, the overall evidence remains inconclusive11. Taken together, while axial length may modulate IOP dynamics to some extent, it does not appear to play a dominant role in determining acute IOP responses following intravitreal injection. Notably, the axial lengths in our study ranged from approximately 21 to 26 mm, representing a relatively standard distribution. Eyes with extremely short or long axial lengths were not included, and the applicability of our findings to such outlier populations remains to be determined.

Finally, we examined whether the number of prior anti-VEGF injections was associated with acute IOP changes. We found no statistically significant correlation between injection count and acute phase IOP elevation. This finding is consistent with that of previous studies reporting that prior injection history has minimal influence on short-term post-injection IOP spikes19. However, repeated intravitreal injections have been linked to an increased risk of sustained IOP elevation and glaucomatous optic nerve damage over the long term34–36. These observations suggest that transient IOP elevation in the acute setting and chronic IOP elevation may arise from distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. Since our study did not assess long-term IOP outcomes, the relationship between injection frequency and chronic IOP elevation should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, no consistent association was observed between acute IOP elevation and most ocular or treatment-related factors examined in this study, including anterior chamber depth, axial length, lens status, and prior anti-VEGF injection history. In addition, the coefficients of determination for anterior chamber depth, axial length, and the number of prior anti-VEGF injections were consistently low, indicating that these parameters accounted for only a small proportion of the variability in acute post-injection IOP. Thus, although these factors may theoretically influence IOP dynamics, their predictive value for acute IOP elevation appears limited in this cohort. Intravitreal injection of aflibercept 8 mg, which involves a volume of 0.07 mL, appears to provide a reasonable degree of acute-phase IOP safety, though these findings should be interpreted within the limitations of the present cohort.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

First, this was a single-center, prospective observational study with a modest sample size and limited subgroup representation. No formal sample size calculation was performed, and secondary subgroup and correlation analyses should be regarded as exploratory. In particular, the very small number of vitrectomized eyes limits the reliability of subgroup comparisons. Additionally, although Bonferroni adjustment was applied to selected post hoc and subgroup comparisons, no formal correction for family-wise error across all secondary analyses was performed. Treatment allocation was not randomized, and potential selection bias could not be excluded. As aflibercept 8 mg is a relatively new therapeutic option, validation through larger, multicenter studies is warranted.

Second, regarding IOP measurements, the immediate post-injection assessment (T1) was conducted solely in the supine position, whereas subsequent post-injection measurements (T2–T4) were all recorded in the sitting position. Since IOP is known to increase in the supine posture37,38, the T1 values may have been relatively higher than they would have been if measured in the sitting position. At baseline, IOP in the supine position was approximately 3–4 mmHg higher than that in the sitting position, consistent with previous reports. Therefore, part of the apparent peak at T1 may be attributable to postural differences, rather than the injection itself. Nevertheless, because T1 was measured under the same supine position in both groups, the between-group comparison at T1 remains internally consistent. Moreover, the magnitude of IOP elevation immediately after injection was substantially greater than the observed 3–4 mmHg postural difference, and IOP subsequently returned to baseline within 30 min.

Third, aflibercept 8 mg was administered from a vial, whereas aflibercept 2 mg was delivered using a PFS. Because the use of a PFS may affect acute IOP changes owing to differences in actual injection volume, injection speed, or injection force22–24, the effects of dose, nominal injection volume, and preparation method could not be fully distinguished in this study. In addition, aflibercept 8 mg is currently available as a PFS in clinical practice, which may limit the direct applicability of our findings to current injection settings.

Fourth, IOP was measured with the iCare IC200 rebound tonometer, rather than via Goldmann applanation tonometry. The IC200 has been reported to show high reproducibility and reasonable agreement with Goldmann applanation tonometry, although some differences between devices have been described39. Furthermore, agreement between rebound tonometry and Goldmann applanation tonometry may be reduced in higher IOP ranges40. Therefore, the T1 values, many of which exceeded 50 mmHg, should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, this study excluded eyes at high risk for IOP-related complications, such as those with preexisting glaucoma or ocular hypertension. As such, the findings of this study are primarily applicable to eyes with relatively preserved aqueous outflow function.

Future studies should aim to evaluate IOP dynamics and their clinical significance in more diverse populations, including eyes with a history of glaucoma, ocular hypertension, or prior vitrectomy. Accumulating real-world data from such heterogeneous clinical settings will be essential for establishing a more comprehensive and generalizable safety profile of high-dose anti-VEGF therapy.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that intravitreal aflibercept 8 mg induces a transient IOP elevation comparable in magnitude and time course to that of the conventional 2 mg formulation, with IOP returning to baseline within 30 min. Furthermore, baseline ocular characteristics—including anterior chamber depth, axial length, lens status, and prior injection history—did not significantly influence acute IOP changes. These findings suggest that intravitreal injection of aflibercept 8 mg is physiologically well tolerated in the acute phase and may provide a short-term IOP safety comparable to that of aflibercept 2 mg, within the limitations of the present study.

Methods

Study design

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Ophthalmology, Shinshu University Hospital, between April 2024 and February 2025. Patients with DME involving the foveal center were enrolled and received an intravitreal injection of aflibercept 8 mg or aflibercept 2 mg according to clinical practice. Treatment allocation was not randomized. For the present analysis, 40 eyes from 36 patients were included, with 20 eyes assigned to the 2 mg group and 20 eyes to the 8 mg group.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible eyes were those scheduled to receive intravitreal aflibercept for fovea-involving DME confirmed via spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT, Cirrus HD-OCT, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA). Eyes with a history of glaucoma or ocular hypertension, intraocular surgery within the preceding three months, or the presence of any other ocular condition that could affect IOP were excluded.

Injection procedure

All injections were performed by a single experienced retina specialist using a standardized technique. The injection site was located 3.5 mm posterior to the limbus at the 10 o’clock meridian and accessed with a 30-gauge needle under an operating microscope, with a guidance device employed to ensure procedural consistency.

The injection volume was 0.07 mL for aflibercept 8 mg and 0.05 mL for aflibercept 2 mg, with the former prepared from a vial and the latter delivered via a PFS.

Anterior chamber paracentesis was not performed routinely but was reserved as a rescue intervention in cases where acute vision loss or ocular pain occurred immediately after the injection. Indirect ophthalmoscopy was conducted to evaluate retinal circulation, and paracentesis was performed only when circulatory disturbance was observed and the event was deemed a complication caused by elevated IOP.

Intraocular pressure measurements

IOP was measured using the iCare IC200 tonometer (Icare Finland Oy) at six time points: before injection in the sitting position (T0-Sit), before injection in the supine position (T0-Sup), 30 s after injection in the supine position following removal of the lid speculum (T1), and 5, 15, and 30 min after injection in the sitting position (T2, T3, and T4, respectively). At each time point, six readings were obtained and averaged, and all measurements were performed by the same examiner to ensure consistency and reliability. Because IOP is higher in the supine than in the sitting position37,38, the T1 value may slightly overestimate the true post-injection pressure. Baseline IOP was therefore obtained in both positions to examine the extent to which posture affected IOP in this study. This measurement schedule was based on prior studies demonstrating that IOP spikes immediately after intravitreal injection and typically returns to baseline within 30–60 min9–11. These IOP dynamics were the primary outcome of this study.

Vitreous reflux

VR, defined as the outflow of a portion of the injected solution through the needle track, was assessed immediately after intravitreal injection. The extent of VR was graded using previously established criteria25, in which grade 1 indicated no reflux, grade 2 indicated subconjunctival reflux measuring less than 3 mm, and grade 3 indicated subconjunctival reflux measuring 3 mm or more. VR was documented as part of the post-injection safety evaluation and was also assessed as a potential factor that may influence post-injection IOP.

Baseline ocular and surgical characteristics

Baseline ocular and surgical characteristics included anterior chamber depth measured using CASIA2 (Tomey, Japan), axial length, measured using OA-2000 (Tomey, Japan), the number of prior intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, lens status (phakic or pseudophakic), and history of vitrectomy. These parameters were evaluated as potential explanatory factors for the primary outcome—acute IOP changes following intravitreal aflibercept injection—and were included in the secondary analysis.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was IOP measured at each post-injection time point (T1–T4) to characterize the time course of IOP within each group and comparing IOP trends between the 2 mg and 8 mg aflibercept groups.

The secondary outcomes were defined from two perspectives: safety and IOP-associated factors. For safety evaluation, the severity of VR immediately after injection and the incidence of anterior chamber paracentesis performed as a rescue intervention were recorded. For analyzing acute IOP changes and IOP-associated factors, relationships were examined with the following explanatory variables: VR; anatomical factors (anterior chamber depth and axial length); and treatment-related factors (number of prior intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, lens status, and history of vitrectomy).

Statistical analysis

Changes in IOP over time within each group were analyzed using the Friedman test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test with Bonferroni adjustment. Between-group differences at each time point and subgroup comparisons by lens status (phakic vs. pseudophakic) and vitrectomy history (yes vs. no) were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni adjustment. Associations between VR grade and treatment group (2 mg vs. 8 mg) were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while relationships between VR grade and IOP change were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. Associations between continuous biometric variables—including anterior chamber depth, axial length, and the number of prior intravitreal anti-VEGF injections—and IOP changes were examined with Pearson correlation and simple linear regression. In these analyses, IOP change was the dependent variable, and each biometric parameter an independent variable. Differences in the distribution of categorical variables (sex, lens status, and history of prior vitrectomy) were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All P values were reported to three decimal places; values less than 0.001 were presented as P < 0.001.
