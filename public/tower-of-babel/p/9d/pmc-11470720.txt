Association between QT prolongation and cardiovascular mortality in cancer patients
Authors: Cheng-Han Chan, Chih-Min Liu, Pei-Fen Chen, Li-Lien Liao, I-Chien Wu, Yu-Feng Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer patients’ vulnerability to QT prolongation contradicts certain anti-cancer drug usage. Until now, the QT prolongation’s impact on CV mortality in cancer patients remains unclear, potentially biasing therapeutic decisions.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort included adult cancer patients with an electrocardiogram (ECG) performed in a tertiary hospital in Taiwan. The first performed ECGs after cancer diagnosis (n = 59,568) were analyzed. The corrected QT intervals by Bazett (QTcB), Fridericia (QTcFri), and Framingham (QTcFra) formulae were used to predict the 90-day and one-year CV mortality according to the Taiwan death registry.ResultsThe AUC of QTcB (90 days: 0.70, 1 year: 0.68) for predicting CV mortality was better than QTcFri and QTcFra (90 days: 0.63 and 0.50, 1 year: 0.65 and 0.56). Using the restricted cubic spline regression model adjusted by age and comorbidities, QTcB increased a significant but trivial risk of CV mortality at 90 days (hazard ratio, 1.007, P = 0.02) and one year (1.006, P < 0.01). Compared to those with QTcB < 500ms, the patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms were older and had more comorbidities and mortalities within one year. The incidence of sudden death and ventricular arrhythmias was only 0.2%. After adjusting for comorbidities, QTcB was neither associated with 90-day nor one-year CV mortality. In the patients already with QTcB ≥ 500ms, the patients receiving the unexpected uses of QT-prolonging drugs were not associated with higher one-year CV mortality than those without (P = 0.14).ConclusionsRather than a prolonged QT interval per se, comorbidities contributed to CV mortality and irreversible outcomes in cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-024-00271-9.

Introduction

The QT interval is a useful electrocardiogram (ECG) parameter measuring ventricular repolarization duration for identifying patients prone to ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death [1, 2]. QT interval is also a sensitive biomarker for evaluating cardiac health as QT prolongation can predict all-cause and cardiovascular (CV) mortality in various diseases [3]. Adverse drug reactions also result in the prolongation of QT intervals. Therefore, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [4] and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [5] guidelines use QTc prolongation > 500ms and a change of QT > 60ms as safeguard criteria to exclude drug usage. Patients with QTc > 500ms cannot participate in clinical trials, and treatment should be temporarily interrupted [4, 5]. The guidelines universally recommend and apply the analysis and management of the prolonged QT interval for all clinical patients. However, the different prognostic impact of the prolonged QT interval in specific diseases is not clearly addressed and supported by clinical evidence. For example, malignancy is usually associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, and benefits from the efficacy of targeted therapies have the potential to outweigh the risk of prolonged QT intervals and arrhythmias.

Cancer patients are especially vulnerable to QT prolongation. Multiple comorbidities, electrolyte disturbances, complex therapies for malignancy, including chemotherapy or target therapies, and concomitant medications, all impose an adverse impact on cardiac electrical conduction and repolarization. It is expected that cancer therapies would bring patients with malignancies a better prognosis, albeit at a significant cost of long and unstable QT intervals and increased cardiac arrhythmias [6]. However, Andreu et al. reported that the incidence of arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death attributable to QTc prolongation from cancer therapy is extremely rare in the meta-analysis [7]. When the literature was carefully reviewed, the prognostic impact of QT prolongation on cardiovascular (CV) mortality in cancer patients has not been comprehensively investigated. It is essential to explore the clinical impact of the QT interval, especially on CV outcomes in cancer patients, before we could consider it a clinical determinant to terminate life-saving therapies.

Different QTc formulae have been proposed to correct QT intervals, including Bazett’s (QTcB) [8], Fridericia (QTcFri) [9], and Framingham’s (QTcFra) formula [10]. The inconsistency between different QTc (the corrected QT interval) formulas leads to a biased classification of QTc prolongation and then inappropriate modifications to chemotherapy [11]. QTcFra and QTcFri are in preference to QTcB for evaluating QT intervals and their potential risk of life-threatening arrhythmias in cancer patients [12–14]. This simply applies the clinical guidelines based on mathematical perspectives. The real-world evidence in cancer patients supporting the favorable differences between QTc formulas regarding patients’ CV mortality remains lacking. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the correlation of different QTc formulae to CV mortality. The differential impact of QT prolongation on short-term (90 days) and long-term CV mortality (one year) among cancer patients was analyzed. We also evaluate the mortality impact of the patients with QT prolongation receiving the unexpected uses of QT interval-prolonging drugs.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a hospital-based retrospective observational cohort study using an electrocardiogram (ECG) database containing adults (over 20 years old) with 12-lead resting ECGs at Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan. The present work aims to analyze the impact of QTc intervals on CV mortality in adult cancer patients. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan.

From 2009 to 2018, 573,187 12-lead ECGs were obtained. The ECG recordings were then enrolled according to the diagnosis of cancer with the age of more than 20 years old (cancer, n = 124,609). The first-performed ECGs after the cancer diagnosis were selected (n = 59,568, average 217 days). All the patients were followed up for their short-term (90 days) and long-term (one year) CV mortality. The adjusted QT intervals by QTcB, QTcFri, and QTcFra formulas were used to predict the 90-day and one-year CV mortality.

The resting 12-lead ECGs were obtained from digitalized cardiographs (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands) according to our previous reports [15–17]. The ECG diagnoses in the hospital cohort for each ECG were first made by the computer-interpreted ECG report (Philips DXL ECG algorithm) and, after that, adjudicated by at least one cardiologist [15–17]. The heart rate and QT interval were captured by the computer-interpreted ECG report (Philips DXL ECG algorithm). The QT intervals were corrected by heart rate using three formulas: QTcB = QT/ RR1/2; QTcFri = QT/RR1/3; QTcFra = QT + 0.154(1-RR) [8–10].

Comorbidities and survival analysis

The information regarding CV comorbidities was retrieved from the hospital database and coded according to the Ninth and Tenth Revision of the International Classification of Disease at or before the date of the index ECG [18, 19]. The following codes apply to the following conditions: hypertension (HTN, ICD-9: 401, 402; ICD-10: I10, I11), diabetes mellitus (DM, ICD-9: 250; ICD-10: E8–E13), myocardial infarction (MI, ICD-9: 410, 412; ICD-10: I21, I22, I25.2), heart failure (HF, ICD-9: 402.01, 402.11, 402.91, 404.01, 404.03, 404.11, 404.13, 404.91, 404.93, 428; ICD-10: I11.0, I13.0, I13.2, I50), stroke (ICD-9: 431–434; ICD-10: I61, I62, I65, I66), chronic kidney disease (ICD-9: 585; ICD-10: N18), chronic lung diseases including bronchitis not specified as acute or chronic, simple and mucopurulent chronic bronchitis, unspecified chronic bronchitis, emphysema, other chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, status asthmaticus, and bronchiectasis (ICD-9: 490–496, 515, 516, 518; ICD-10 code: J40–J44), coronary artery disease (ICD-9: 411.8, 414.0, 414.2, 414.3, 414.4, 414.8, 414.9;ICD-10: I24.0, I24.8, I24.9, I25.0, I25.1, I25.5, I25.6, I25.8, I25.9), hyperlipidemia (ICD-1: 272.0, 272.1, 272.2, 272.3, 272.4; ICD-10: E78.0, E78.1, E78.2, E78.3, E78.4, E78.5) and cancer (ICD-9: 140–208; ICD-10: C00-C97).

All clinical death events were obtained from the Bureau of National Health Insurance of Taiwan death registry [20]. In Taiwan, any death or presumption of death is subject to registration, and therefore, the registry provided 100% follow-up of our patients. The causes of mortality, especially cancer and CV deaths, were also classified during registration by the physicians and convincingly retrieved for analysis. A person’s index date is determined by the date they underwent their index ECG examination.

Cardiovascular mortality prediction by different QTc formulas

We first used the time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) to evaluate QTc formulas’ ability to discriminate between cancer patients who would have a CV death event during the follow-up period and those who would not [21]. In short, the time-dependent AUC analyzed the discriminative ability of various QTc formulas to predict CV mortality at each time point under consideration. The event-time outcome of this study refers to the time from the index ECG date (day 0) to prediction time with a different time component. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn at 90 days and one year, representing the QTc formula’s discrimination capability for short-term and long-term risk of CV death. ROC curves of different QTc formulas were also compared. The QTc formula with the highest discrimination ability for short-term and long-term cardiovascular mortality would be selected. Next, we analyzed the risk of CV death throughout the QTc range. To test the linear assumption of the Cox regression model in this analysis, we created a restricted cubic spline (RCS) function of continuous QTc. We then displayed the association between QTc and the hazard ratio of CV death in graphs and tested the non-linear associations. RCS regression is a relatively mature method that allows us to test the linear assumption of multiple regression models, including the Cox regression model [22, 23].

The conventional definition of prolonged QTc and cardiovascular mortality in cancer patients

In the Food and Drug Administration guidelines in the USA, QTc intervals greater than 500ms are considered a threshold for discontinuing a causal medication [4]. A univariate analysis was conducted to compare the risk of CV mortality between patients with a QTc interval ≥ 500ms and QTc < 500ms at 90 days and one year. Age, gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary artery disease, chronic lung diseases, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease were used to adjust multivariate Cox regression analysis according to previous reports [3, 24, 25]. We evaluated the mortality contribution of cancer and CV diseases in cancer patients during the follow-up period of one year. The estimates of CV survival and arrhythmia could be confounded by cancer mortality. Therefore, cumulative incidence functions (CIF) curves were used to estimate the incidence of CV and cancer deaths with patients QTc ≥ 500ms and < 500ms, which account for the competing risk of death. The causes of CV death among QTc ≥ 500ms were further retrieved from medical records in Taipei Veterans General Hospital.

The unexpected uses of QT interval-prolonging drugs in the patients with QTc ≥ 500ms

A comprehensive list of QT interval-prolonging drugs (more than moderate to high risk) was analyzed (Supplementary Table 1) according to the 2020 scientific statement from the American Heart Association and www.CredibleMeds.org [26, 27]. Using medication records from the hospital electronic database, we retrieved the patients with QTc ≥ 500ms who had used QT interval-prolonging drugs after the ECG examination. The CV mortality was further compared between the patients with QTc ≥ 500ms with and without QT interval-prolonging drugs.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were represented as means ± standard deviations, and their differences were compared using t-tests. The chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables. P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Crude incidence of CV death in the presence of competing risk was estimated using the cumulative incidence function. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was applied to estimate the effect of QTc ≥ 500ms on time to cardiovascular death after adjusting for confounders. We calculated the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Competing risk was addressed by fitting cause-specific hazard models. Time-dependent AUC (cumulative/dynamic ROC curve) analysis was applied to estimate and compare different QTc formulas’ discrimination ability for CV death throughout the follow-up period. The AUC curve and the 90-day and one-year ROC curves were drawn. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was utilized to evaluate the time-to-event analysis for cardiovascular (CV) death. The log-rank test was employed to compare the survival curves between patients with a baseline QTc interval of 500 milliseconds or longer, or concurrently using QT interval-prolonging drugs. All analyses used SAS (Copyright 2002–2012 by SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) statistical proprietary software 9.4 (TS1M3 DBCS3170).

The predictive power of different QTc formulas for CV mortality

First, we assessed different QTc formulas’ ability to predict CV mortality by the time-dependent AUC within one year (Fig. 1A). The time-dependent AUC of QTcB was higher than those by QTcFri and QTcFra throughout the follow-up period. The QTc formulas were further analyzed at 90 days and one year, respectively. For CV mortality at 90 days, QTcB (Fig. 1B, AUC: 0.70) was better than QTcFri (0.63, P < 0.01 vs. QTcB) and QTcFra (0.50, P < 0.01 vs. QTcB). For CV mortality at one year, QTcB (Fig. 1C, AUC: 0.68) was better than QTcFri (0.65, P = 0.02, vs. QTcB) and QTcFra (0.56, P < 0.01, vs. QTcB). Using the RCS regression model adjusted by Age, gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary artery disease, chronic lung diseases, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease, the QTcB had a linear and positive association with the hazard ratio of CV death for both 90 days (Fig. 1D, HR, 1.007, 95% CI [1.00-1.01], p = 0.02) and one year (HR, 1.006, 95% CI [1.00-1.01], p < 0.01). As QTcB is the best formula to predict CV mortality, we used QTcB for the following analysis of QT prolongation.

The QT prolongation and CV mortality

The patients with a QTcB ≥ 500 ms were older, more male gender, and had comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary artery disease, chronic lung diseases, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease (Table 1). The cumulative incidences of mortalities related to CV, cancer, and other mortalities (non-CV and non-cancer) were delineated (Fig. 2). Compared to patients with QTcB < 500ms, those with QTcB ≥ 500ms had higher CV mortality (Table 2, QTcB < 500ms vs. ≥500ms: 0.41% vs. 1.74%, P < 0.001) and cancer mortality (14.75% vs. 24.02%, P < 0.001) during the one-year follow-up. The mortality differences of the patients with/without a prolonged QT were similar at the 90-day or one-year follow-up. The cardiovascular risk associated with QT prolongation ≥ 500ms was analyzed among different types of cancer. QT prolongation ≥ 500ms was associated with CV mortality in patients with colon, lung, and gastric cancer but not in liver, prostate, kidney, and breast cancer (Supplementary Table 2). The etiology of CV mortality was recorded in the fifteen patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms. Only two ventricular arrhythmias and one sudden death could be attributed to pro-arrhythmia from QT prolongation (0.2%). The other causes included acute myocardial infarction, aortic stenosis, heart failure, infective endocarditis, rupture of abdominal aortic aneurysm, and arterial thrombus after percutaneous aortic valve replacement.

Independent predictors for CV mortality in cancer patients

In the univariate analysis, QTcB ≥ 500ms increased a 5.98-fold risk (95% CI [3.40-10.49], P < 0.001) and 4.61-fold risk (95% CI [3.09–6.86], P < 0.001) of 90-day and one-year CV mortality, respectively. However, after adjusting for all comorbidities, QTcB was not significantly associated with CV mortality both at 90 days (Table 3, HR, 1.58, 95% CI [0.88–2.84], P = 0.12) and one year (HR, 1.33, 95% CI [0.88-2.00], P = 0.17). Rather than the long QTc interval per se, multiple comorbidities, including heart failure, myocardial infarction, and chronic kidney disease, accounted for the higher CV mortality (< 90 days or within one year) in cancer patients with a long QTc interval.

CV mortality in the patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms receiving the QT interval-prolonging drugs

We further analyzed whether the use of QT interval-prolonging drugs in patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms would be associated with adverse CV mortality. There were 511 patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms unexpectedly receiving the QT interval-prolonging medications after ECG examinations. By the Kaplan-Meier survival curves, CV mortality in these patients did not differ from the patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms who did not receive any QT interval-prolonging drugs (n = 1061, Fig. 3, p = 0.14) during the one-year follow-up. Among the patients, 217 (0.36%) used cancer drugs with a risk for QTc prolongation before ECG examinations. However, only five individuals (< 0.01%) exhibited a QTc interval exceeding 500 ms. The cardiovascular mortality between patients with or without QT prolongation was not significantly different (P = 0.82 by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis).

CV mortality in patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms on QT-prolonging drugs. The survival rate at the one-year follow-up in the patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms receiving the QT interval-prolonging drugs was 99.3%. The survival rate in the patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms but not receiving the QT interval-prolonging drugs was 99.6%

Discussion

QTcB is more accurate for predicting CV mortality than QTcFri and QTcFra. A long QTcB interval > 500ms was associated with higher CV, cancer mortality, and all-cause mortality. However, after multivariate analysis, QTcB was not an independent predictor for CV mortality. The comorbidities, rather than a prolonged QT interval per se, contributed to mortality and patients’ irreversible outcomes. The incidence of proarrhythmia from a long QTc interval > 500ms was rare. Rather than terminate cancer therapy concerning arrhythmias, the prompt treatment of both comorbidities and cancer might be a better alternative to improve the outcome in patients with a prolonged QTc interval.

Different QTc formulas used in cancer treatment to assess patient prognoses

The measured QT intervals are corrected for heart rate by a mathematical formula to determine whether they are prolonged relative to baseline. The best correction formula is a subject of controversy [4]. The mathematical QT correction that fits all persons individually is probably unobtainable [28]. Bazett and Fridericia corrections are the most widely used [29, 30]. However, Bazett formula overestimates the QTc at the rapid heart rate, and therefore, the number of patients with potentially dangerous QTc prolongation will be overestimated [29, 30]. Therefore, Fredericia’s formula is recommended rather than Bazett’s. Alternatively, some also suggest QTcFra is preferred to QTcB and QTcFri in cancer patients [30]. Intriguingly, despite the Bazett formula having the poorest performance in mathematical methods, it is the only formula that could significantly predict CV events and mortality (hazard ratio from 2.05 to 2.92) in either hospital-based or population-based cohorts, compared to Framingham and Fridericia formula [31–33]. In the present work, we similarly replicated these findings, stating that QTcB was more accurate for predicting CV mortality than the other formulae. Although the guidelines generally recommended using the Framingham and Fridericia formula in preference to the Bazett formula in cancer patients, the conflict between mathematical correction and clinical relevance is a critical issue that should be adequately studied.

QTc prolongation, comorbid conditions, and cancer patient outcomes

FDA guidelines use QTc interval as the safeguard criterion to exclude anticancer drugs based on QTc above 500ms because it predicts ventricular arrhythmias [4, 5]. However, the FDA also recommends that regardless of the degree to which a drug prolongs the QT/QTc interval, decisions about drug approval will depend upon the morbidity and mortality associated with the untreated disease and the demonstrated clinical benefits of the drug [4]. In cancer patients, this is especially true. Cancer therapies frequently bring cancer patients a prolonged QT interval but could be lifesaving. The strategy to terminate potential beneficial treatment in cancer patients by a prolonged QT criterion should be evaluated according to the balance of risk/benefit of arrhythmias and cancer remission.

There is still a debate about whether QTc prolongation affects CV outcomes in cancer patients [5, 34]. In hospital-based or community-based populations, the QTcB level > 500 ms has been independently associated with CV mortality, even after the multivariate adjustment [31–33]. However, it is not the same story in cancer patients. In our present work, only older age and multiple comorbidities, but not a QTcB level > 500 ms, were independently associated with increased CV mortality after multivariate adjustment. Comorbidities, including heart failure, myocardial infarction, and chronic kidney disease, mainly determined the prognosis of CV mortality. Previous studies have indicated that multiple comorbidities are a crucial indicator of QTc prolongation [35, 36]. The QT interval might represent a biomarker of comorbidities but not an independent predictor for CV events. These results suggest that comorbidities underlie QT interval prolongation. For instance, cardiac ischemia or myocardial infarction can lead to significant QT prolongation [37, 38]. QT prolongation appears to be a biomarker of these conditions, reflecting changes in cardiac repolarization rather than serving as a causal factor. Furthermore, our results suggested the incidence of ventricular arrhythmias was rare. This echoes the findings from the others that QTc prolongation did not cause extra-arrhythmic risk or sudden death in cancer patients who received anti-neoplastic agents [7]. In summary, a prolonged QT interval should be considered an alerting biomarker of complex comorbidities for the physician to aggressively treat their comorbidities and improve CV outcomes [39].

The patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms had more than nearly 2-fold cancer mortality than patients with QTcB < 500ms in the present study. It suggests that effective drugs would be vital for preventing disease progression and improving the patient’s outcome. In patients who received novel tumor therapy, severe and life-threatening arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death were rare [7]. We found that most CV mortality was not attributed to drug-related arrhythmias, but rather myocardial infarction or heart failure. Utilization of QT-prolonging medications had no immediate impact on CV mortality within one year in the patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms. Instead of terminating cancer therapy, these patients with QTcB ≥ 500ms should be treated aggressively for cancer and CV diseases, as both are vital for their outcomes.

Limitations

QTc interval could be dynamic in patients and affected by medications. Longitudinal changes of QTc interval and its impact on the prognosis should be explored in the future. Cancer is a multifaceted disease [40]. The work is limited by its retrospective design, which could not provide detailed cancer stages or treatment. Genetic backgrounds and electrolyte levels were not available in this study but could potentially influence QT intervals and clinical outcomes. CV events beyond one year may differ from those within one year. This should be explored in future research. Due to the complexity of conducting a comprehensive retrospective analysis across various cancers, we did not systematically review all cancer medications. Instead, our analysis was limited to the timing of QT interval-prolonging drugs.

Conclusions

Cancer patients with a prolonged QTc interval had both high cancer and CV mortality, but not ventricular arrhythmias. Rather than a prolonged QT interval per se, comorbidities determined CV mortality. Instead of withholding cancer treatment for concerning arrhythmia risk, prompt treatment of both underlying comorbidities and cancer might be a better strategy to improve the outcome in patients with QT prolongation.
