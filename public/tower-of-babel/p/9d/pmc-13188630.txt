Quantitative proteomic profiling of placental extracellular vesicles in recurrent pregnancy loss reveals their role in feto-maternal crosstalk
Authors: Chitra Bhardwaj, Priyanka Srivastava, Ravi Pratap Singh Bhadoriya, Minakshi Rohilla, Anupriya Kaur, Inusha Panigrahi, Seema Chopra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) remains a devastating outcome for many women, with up to 40–60% of cases classified as unexplained despite extensive clinical workup. During pregnancy, extracellular vesicles (EVs) from the placenta enters into maternal circulation and plays a vital role in completion of a successful pregnancy. Profiling of EVs cargo proteins offers a promising avenue for the pathophysiology of RPL, with the potential to revolutionize diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment strategies. Blood samples were collected from 5 pregnant women with history of RPL and 5 gestational aged-matched healthy pregnant controls. EVs were extracted from plasma and characterized for their size, morphology, number and presence of surface markers. Further, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis of EV protein was performed to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). In total 508 proteins with unique peptides were identified. Among these 330 proteins were differentially expressed between patients and controls, out of which 94 were significantly expressed (p < 0.05). Based on the expression pattern, 25 proteins were upregulated (log2FC > 1, P < 0.05) and 69 were downregulated. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment indicated involvement of pathways such as innate immune response, neutrophil degranulation, complement cascade regulation and intrinsic pathway of fibrin clot formation. Protein-protein interaction analysis identified 10 hub proteins (APOH, APOB, APOA1, APOA2, AHSG, TTR, A2M, SERPINC1, ALB, FGB) potentially implicated in RPL pathophysiology. Introduction

Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), defined as two or more consecutive losses of clinical pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation. It is a common fertility problem that affects 2–5% of couples all over the world [1]. Studies have proposed certain risk factors such as, hormonal and metabolic disorders, uterine anomalies, cytogenetic abnormalities and various immunological and genetic factors, responsible for RPL [2, 3] Despite the multiple causes listed above, there remains a significant challenge in determining the cause in approximately 40–60% of patients with RPL, commonly referred to as unexplained recurrent pregnancy loss (URPL) [4]. Therefore, the pathogenesis of RPL remains to be elucidated.

During pregnancy a series of pivotal events occur, starting from gamete formation to fertilization and implantation, which are meticulously regulated by different pathways. A crucial aspect of this orchestration involves intricate communication at feto-maternal interface with immune modification to accept the semi-allogenic foetus. In order to facilitate a successful pregnancy, modulation of peri-implantation microenvironment is essential to regulate trophoblast cells proliferation, invasion, migration and differentiation [5, 6]. Traditionally, different endocrine and paracrine factors were attributed for this cross-talk, however, recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as novel facet of maternal-fetal communication [7]. EVs are nano-sized cell-derived membrane vesicles of heterogenous population (exosomes and/or microvesicles) which mediates intercellular-communication via transfer of their cargo (protein, lipids and nucleic acid) in a secure encapsulated system [8, 9]. These cargos reflect cellular and tissue origin of EVs and thus acts as footprints of their cellular signals [7]. Placental tissues release EVs during pregnancy into the maternal circulation, wherein they aid pregnancy’s vascular changes while regulating placental function and fetal growth [10]. As compared to non-pregnant women, EV cargoes increase in pregnant women, thereby modulating the immune response against fetus [11]. Further, the level of these placental EVs in terms of their concentration, composition and bioactivity, changes during pregnancy related disorders, such as RPL, pre-eclampsia (PE) and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [12–14]. Our previous study showed that circulating EV-miRNAs in maternal blood could provide clinical insights into the pathogenesis of RPL [15]. Thus, differential variation in exosomal cargo directly affects the physiological changes during pregnancy.

In present study, we hypothesized that proteome profiling of EVs obtained from the peripheral blood sample of women with RPL and gestational age-matched normal pregnant women, would allow us to explore the potential of these protein biomarkers as prognostic and diagnostic predictors of RPL. In addition, these protein markers will also shed light on the molecular and pathophysiological changes implicated in the etiology of RPL.

Methodology

Present study was conducted at a tertiary care centre in North India, after getting approval from Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC-12/2021–2203). An informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrolling for the study. In this case-control study, we included 5 women with history of RPL (> 2 abortions in first trimester) and 5 healthy pregnant females of similar gestation age, with no history of miscarriages, visiting the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Department OPD. Women with pregnancy through any medical procedures of assisted reproductive technology (ART), with autoimmune disease or any metabolic disorder, with history of preeclampsia or with any defined cause of RPL, such as chromosomal aberration, inherited thrombophilia or endocrine factors were not included. A detailed clinical history was obtained from women enrolled in the study. We included pregnant women with no history of hypertension, normal thyroid levels (T3, T4, TSH), negative for APLA/ANA, karyotype was normal (both husband and wife) and ultrasound showed no abnormal uterine anomalies.

Sample collection

Peripheral blood sample (~ 3–4 ml) from women with RP and healthy pregnant women, were collected in EDTA-coated BD vacutainer. The freshly collected blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the plasma. Supernatant was collected and again centrifuged at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The serum samples were then stored at -80 °C, until further use.

EV isolation

We collected 2 ml of serum sample, which was used for EV isolation using Total Exosome Isolation Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, serum samples were centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 20 min to remove residual debris and cells. Next, half volumes of PBS and one-third volumes of exosome isolation reagent were added to the supernatant. The specimens were then incubated at 4 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min. Then the EV precipitate was reconstituted in PBS/Exosome suspension buffer and stored at -20 °C until further use.

EV characterization

These EVs were then characterized for their structure, particle size, concentration and cell surface markers using Electron Microscopy, Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) and Western Blotting, as per MISEV guidelines.

Transmission electron microscopy

We used a High Resolution- Transmission Electron Microscope (HR-TEM) [Jeol, JEM-2100Plus, Japan] to study the ultrastructure of exosomes. Briefly, EVs were diluted with PBS and then loaded on carbon formvar grid and negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid. The EVs were then visualized at 80 kV.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The particle size distribution and concentration were detected using Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis. EVs were diluted with PBS and then injected into the instrument capillary. NTA measurements were performed using NanoSight NS300 instrument (NanoSight NTA 3.3; Malvern, Worcestershire, UK).

Western blot

Exosome protein was isolated and separated on polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and then transferred on nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked in 5% BSA and then incubated in primary antibody (CD63 (ThermoFisher#10628D), Flotillin-1 (Invitrogen#PA5-17127) and PLAP (Invitrogen#MA1-19354). The blot was then incubated in secondary antibody, HRP-goat-anti-mouse (BD Biosciences) and the blot was acquired using Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Kit (BioRad) at Azure Biosciences Gel Doc System.

Proteomics

Sample preparation

Protein samples were reduced with 5 mM TCEP and further alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide and then digested with Trypsin for 16 h at 37 °C, in a 1:50, Trypsin/lysate ratio. Using a C18 silica cartridge, digests were cleaned to remove the salt and then dried using a speed vac. The dried pellet was resuspended in buffer A containing 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid.

Mass spectrometric analysis of peptide mixtures

We used an Easy-nlc-1000 system coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris mass spectrometer to conduct the experiments. 1ug of peptide sample were loaded on C18 column 15 cm, 3.0 μm Acclaim PepMap (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and separated with a 0–40% gradient of buffer B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 500 nl/min) and injected for MS analysis. For 110 min LC gradients were run. MS1 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap (Max IT = 60ms, AGQ target = 300%; RF Lens = 70%; R=60 K, mass range = 375 − 1500; Profile data). Dynamic exclusion was employed for 30s excluding all charge states for a given precursor. MS2 spectra were collected for top 20 peptides. MS2 (Max IT= 60ms, R= 15 K, AGC target 100%).

Data processing

All the raw files were analysed using Proteome Discoverer (v2.5) against the Uniprot Human database. For dual Sequest and Amanda search, the precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set at 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. The protease enzyme specificity to generate peptides was set for trypsin/P (cleavage at the C terminus of “K/R: unless followed by “P”). For database search, Carbamidomethyl on cysteine was considered for fixed modification, while, N-terminal acetylation was considered as variable modifications. Both peptide spectrum match and protein false discovery rate were set to 0.01 FDR.

Statistical and downstream bioinformatics analysis

Proteins quantified in at least 4 out of 5 samples in each group were retained for statistical analysis. Missing values were imputed using a normalized distribution (width = 0.3, downshift = 1.4). Differential expression was assessed using Student’s t-test, and p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Proteins with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log2 fold change ≥ |1| were considered significant. Functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) were performed using Fisher’s exact test with FDR correction, and significant categories were defined as those with adjusted p-values < 0.05.

Gene ontology (GO) annotation

For upregulated and down-regulated proteins, Gene ontology and Pathway Enrichments were performed separately. Top 20 significant GO terms and pathways were compiled for further analysis.

KEGG pathway annotation

The FASTA protein sequences of DEPs were blasted against the online Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://geneontology.org/) to retrieve their KOs and were subsequently mapped to pathways in KEGG [16]. The corresponding KEGG pathways were extracted.

Functional enrichment analysis

To further explore the impact of differentially expressed proteins on cell physiological process and to discover relationships between differentially expressed proteins, enrichment analysis was performed. GO enrichment in three ontologies (biological process, molecular function, and cellular component) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were applied based on Fisher exact test, considering the total quantified protein annotations as the background dataset. Derived p-values were further adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Functional categories and pathways with p-values < 0.05 were considered to be significant.

Among these, 330 proteins were differentially expressed between patients and controls (Fig. 4). Discussion

In RPL, EV proteomics has emerged as a promising tool for identifying potential biomarkers and understanding the underlying mechanisms of pregnancy loss. Abnormal changes of EV proteomics are involved in pregnancy related complications, including preterm labour [18], fetal death [19], GDM [20], PE [21, 22], and antiphospholipid syndrome [23]. However, the proteomic profile of EVs has not been reported in RPL yet. In this study, we performed the first proteomic analysis of the EVs circulating in the plasma of pregnant women with history of RPL. Our proteome analysis study showed alterations in EV-protein cargo in women with RPL as compared to controls. Total 330 proteins were differentially expressed between women with RPL and controls, out of which 94 were significantly expressed (p < 0.05). Based on the expression pattern, 25 proteins were upregulated (log2FC > 1, P < 0.05) and 69 were downregulated, (log2 FC < 1). Among the downregulated proteins, we observed different variables of Immunoglobulin kappa, lambda, heavy and light chain variables.

Immunoglobulin kappa, a type of light chain protein which is a component of antibodies (immunoglobulins), is essential to pregnancy because it helps the mother’s immune system tolerate the foetus, thus preventing an immune response against the “foreign” foetal tissue. Its specific role, however, is mostly within the bigger IgG immunoglobulin molecule, which is the main antibody class that is passed from the placenta to the foetus and offers crucial early life protection against pathogens [24]. Similar to our study, Liu Y et al., also observed Immunoglobulin kappa variables expression in IVF follicular fluid of women with thyroid autoantibodies. Another downregulated protein MAPK8-IP2 (C-Jun-amino-terminal kinase-interacting protein 2), is not extensively studied in the context of pregnancy, however, based on the known role of JNK signalling pathway, it is likely to play a vital role in uterine tissue remodelling, placental development and fetal growth. Thus, alteration in its level could disrupt the placental growth and affect fetal health [25].

Among the top 10 significantly upregulated proteins, we observed that the most of them were largely reported in various other pregnancy related complications. During normal pregnancy, levels of B2M gradually increase in blood throughout different trimesters, with peak levels in third trimester. Mona K et al., in their study showed that B2M levels were significantly upregulated in women who developed PE as compared to normal women at 2nd trimester [26]. Another protein Kallikrein-11 protein (KLK-11) was upregulated in our patients, however its specific role in pregnancy is yet not fully understood. In a study by Fernando SC et al., elevated expression of KLK-11 was seen in the porcine endometrium during early stage of pregnancy, but no further studies showed its role in pregnancy [27].

We also found elevated expression of CD14 in our patient samples. It is an important inflammatory marker, which plays a significant role during pregnancy, particularly in relation to interaction with the foetus. High levels of CD14 expression are often observed in form of its protein product on monocytes, which is reported to be associated with pre-eclampsia, indicating an exaggerated pro-inflammatory response in these women [28, 29]. We observed few other significant proteins in EV cargo of women with RPL, such as PRSS3, KRT, ATXN, however, to our knowledge their role in pregnancy or pregnancy related complications has yet not been investigated.

Further, gene ontology analysis showed cellular, molecular and biological pathways associated with these proteins. We observed pathways associated with metabolic changes, endopeptidase activity and secretory vesicles. A significant change in woman’s metabolism takes place during pregnancy, to support the foetus growth [30, 31]. During pregnancy, peptidase activity plays a crucial role in implantation, trophoblast invasion and development of foetus. Abnormal peptidase activity can reduce trophoblast invasion and thus lead to pregnancy related complications, like PE [32, 33].

The KEGG pathway analysis showed significant involvement of innate immune response, neutrophil degranulation, complement cascade regulation and intrinsic pathway of fibrin clot formation. Innate immune response including neutrophils protects the mother and foetus from infection during pregnancy [34]. In many pathophysiological pregnancy related complications, the innate immune cells produce an inappropriate pro-inflammatory microenvironment [35]. At maternal-fetal interface complement homeostasis is crucial for pre-implantation, implantation and placental development [36]. Dysregulated complement activation may lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as pregnancy loss, PE and pre-term birth [37]. During pregnancy a significant rise in fibrin coagulation factors (such as fibrinogen) is seen, which increases the clot formation potential of blood, to minimize blood loss during delivery [38]. However, excessive or abnormal fibrin coagulation can disrupt placental development and often leads to “thrombophilia”, which can lead to miscarriage due to impaired supply to foetus [39].

In present study, we observed top 10 hub genes, namely, APOH, APOB, APOA1, APOA2, AHSG, TTR, A2M, SERPINC1, ALB, FGB. Polymorphism in APOH gene is linked with RPL susceptibility in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) [40]. Levels of APOA1 are significantly elevated in plasma of women with PE as compared to controls [41]. Further, the ratio of ApoB/ApoA-1 is related to elevated risk of PE [42, 43], and also it is a predictor of GDM, large for gestational birth [44] and pre-term birth [45]. In study by Sun Y et al., the levels of apolipoprotein 1/2 were differentially expressed in plasma EVs of women with APS [23]. The results of our study corroborated with other studies, indicate that plasma EVs are associated with lipid metabolism in pregnant women. The critical functions of lipid metabolism in early embryo development, implantation, and uterine receptivity have been validated by several research [46].

The AHSG gene, which codes for the protein fetuin-A, is associated with GDM due to its role in regulating insulin sensitivity; studies show that increased serum levels of AHSG can be observed in pregnant women with GDM [47, 48]. Another gene, TTR, which codes for the protein transthyretin, plays an important role in during pregnancy in transportation of thyroid hormone and retinol to the developing foetus [49]. Yu Z et al., in a recent study showed that decreased level of TTR in placental villi impaired angiogenesis and was associated with occurrence of miscarriages [50].

SERPINC1 encodes for protein “antithrombin,” which plays a crucial role in blood clotting regulation. Mutation in this gene can result in antithrombin deficiency which increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism as well as pregnancy loss [51]. Similar to our study, elevated levels of SERPINC1 were observed in serum protein cargo of GDM patients [20] and pregnant women with APS [23]. This suggests that plasma EVs are associated with regulation of coagulation system during pregnancy. Further, polymorphism in SERPINC1 is associated with increased risk of RPL [52]. According to study by Kovac M et al., homozygous variant of SERPINC1 is associated with pregnancy related complications [53].

Another gene FGB, which codes for the beta chain of fibrinogen, also plays an important role in ensuring proper blood clotting during pregnancy to maintain placental attachment as well as prevent excessive bleeding at the time of delivery [54]. However, deficiency in fibrinogen results in recurrent miscarriages [55–57]. A2M gene plays a crucial role in feto-placental vasculature, but its increased expressed results in a negative effect on uterine spiral artery remodelling and placental angiogenesis, thereby contributing to development of PE [58]. A recent study by Tronco JA et al., showed that increased levels of A2M in circulating EVs is associated with preterm pregnancies [59].

Together, these findings suggest a possible link between altered immune regulation, complement activation, and coagulation pathways in the context of RPL. Specifically, hub proteins such as APOH, SERPINC1, and FGB—known for their roles in coagulation and immune modulation—were significantly dysregulated in the EVs of women with RPL. While these associations provide important clues into potential pathogenic mechanisms, they represent preliminary observations that require validation in larger cohorts and mechanistic models. Despite these limitations, our data offer new biological leads. The involvement of pathways such as innate immune signalling, complement homeostasis and the intrinsic coagulation cascade suggests that dysregulated feto-maternal immune balance and hemostatic dysfunction may contribute to pregnancy instability. These pathways are clinically relevant, as disruptions in complement activation or fibrin clot formation are known to influence implantation success, placental development and risk of pregnancy loss. With adequate validation, protein signatures within these pathways may form the basis for future targeted risk-stratification tools or adjunct diagnostic panels for idiopathic RPL.

Conclusion

In summary, this exploratory study reveals a distinct EV protein signature associated with RPL, implicating dysregulated immune responses, coagulation pathways, and altered placental communication. These findings suggest that circulating EV proteins may serve as informative biomarkers for RPL, with potential applications in early risk assessment especially for idiopathic patients. However, given the limited cohort size and absence of validation, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies with larger, well-defined cohorts and targeted validation are essential to confirm these preliminary observations and explore their clinical utility.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 5 per group) limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Although we applied strict inclusion criteria to reduce clinical heterogeneity and by including idiopathic subjects only, it is not possible to fully account for the complexity of RPL in such a limited cohort. Second, the lack of protein-level validation using orthogonal methods (e.g., ELISA or western blot) or replication in an independent cohort restricts the biological and translational applicability of the results. Lastly, while the pathway and network analyses provide valuable insight, they represent associative data that must be interpreted in the context of exploratory research. These limitations are acknowledged, and future research will prioritize larger sample sizes, subgroup analysis, and validation to better understand the clinical relevance of EV-associated biomarkers in RPL.
