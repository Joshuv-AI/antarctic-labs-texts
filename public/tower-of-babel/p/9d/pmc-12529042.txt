A Cross‐Sectional Study of Quality of Life and Body Image in Myasthenia Gravis Patients: A Novel Approach Using the Individualized Neuromuscular Quality of Life Questionnaire
Authors: Michael Chou, Meg Mendoza, Hans Katzberg, Vera Bril, Carolina Barnett‐Tapia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsMany generic and disease‐specific quality of life (QoL) measures have been used to understand the perspectives of patients with myasthenia gravis (MG). However, there is limited data regarding the use of the Individualized Neuromuscular Quality of Life Questionnaire (INQoL) and the impact of body image in patients with MG.MethodsThis was a cross‐sectional cohort of 258 patients with MG who completed several QoL measures, including the INQoL, 36‐Item Short Form Survey (SF‐36) and 15‐Item Myasthenia Gravis Quality of Life Scale (MG‐QoL15). We compared scores of different QoL measures with each other and also compared SF‐36 scores to the general population. Linear regression models were built to investigate factors associated with QoL and body image in MG patients.ResultsMG patients had lower SF‐36 scores compared to the general population. Of the different QoL measures, the INQoL correlated the strongest with the MG‐QoL15 (r = 0.80, p < 0.05). Worse QoL (measured by the INQoL) was significantly correlated with increased disease severity (p = 0.0054) and fatigue (p = 0.0019), younger age (p = 0.0471), and retirement (p = 0.0450). Worse INQoL body image scores were significantly associated with increased fatigue (p = 0.0189) and ptosis severity (p = 0.0298).DiscussionThe INQoL showed that body image is negatively affected in people with MG, suggesting it poses a burden and may be worth considering when measuring QoL. Further studies are needed to assess other factors associated with reduced body image, besides ptosis and fatigue, in people living with MG.Keywords: body image, INQoL, MG‐QoL15, myasthenia gravis, quality of life

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines Quality of Life (QoL) as “an individual's perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns.” [1]. QoL measures provide valuable insight into the perspectives of patients, as they allow a way to determine the impact of healthcare and burden the disease has caused [2]. Therefore, they can be especially useful for chronic diseases such as myasthenia gravis (MG), in which therapy is targeted at improving symptoms and QoL. [3].

QoL is a useful assessment in the treatment of MG as these patients have a lower QoL compared to healthy individuals [4]. However, given the subjective nature of QoL, it can be influenced by various factors and also the weight these factors hold for an individual. For example, disease severity, depression, and fatigue can all impact QoL in MG patients [5, 6]. This variety of factors has resulted in QoL measures that focus on different aspects of life and range in specificity from general health to disease‐specific. Commonly used QoL measures in MG include disease‐specific scales, such as the 15‐Item Myasthenia Gravis Quality of Life Scale (MG‐QoL15), and generic measures, such as the 36‐Item Short Form Survey (SF‐36) and the EuroQol‐5 Dimension Questionnaire (EQ‐5D) [7, 8, 9]. However, there is little data regarding the use of the Individualized Neuromuscular Quality of Life Questionnaire (INQoL) for MG, [10]. Which is a QoL measure developed for individuals living with a range of neuromuscular disorders. Understanding how the INQoL may differ from other QoL measures used for MG is important as different measures will tap into different aspects of QoL, such as body image, which is not usually assessed in MG.

In this study, we compared different generic and disease‐specific QoL measures in people living with MG. Our main objectives were to assess the INQoL and its correlations with other commonly used measures in MG, and to determine which factors influence QoL and body image in MG patients. We hypothesized that the INQoL would correlate more strongly with disease‐specific than generic QoL measures, and that QoL and body image would be negatively affected in MG patients.

Methods

Ethical Standards

This study was reviewed and approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board and conforms to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study Procedure

This was a cross‐sectional study of adults with confirmed MG who attended the Prosserman Family Neuromuscular Clinic at Toronto General Hospital between 2014 and 2016. The data has been previously used in a study on MG outcome measures [11]. Patients were eligible and invited to participate in the study if they had a confirmed diagnosis of MG. MG diagnosis was based on symptoms and either positive serology including acetylcholine receptor (AChR), muscle‐specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) or low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4) antibodies, or abnormal repetitive nerve stimulation or single‐fiber electromyography.

Patients completed four QoL measures: the EQ5D, SF‐36, INQoL, and MG‐QoL15. The INQoL 2.0 is designed for neuromuscular diseases, [10] and consists of 3 sections as follows: (1) Symptoms: includes muscle weakness, pain, fatigue, ptosis, diplopia, and dysphagia. Locking (myotonia) was excluded, as per developers' guidance, as it does not occur in MG. (2) Life domains: includes activities, independence, social relationships, emotions, and body image. (3) Treatment effects: includes perceived treatment effects that illustrate the patient's perception of their treatment up until the present. In other words, it reflects their mindset on the effects of their treatment so far. It also includes expected treatment effects that illustrate the patient's expectations or hope regarding their treatment in the future. In other words, it reflects their vision on the potential effects of their treatment going forward. These scores depict a “net effect” of the treatment as they consider both benefits and side effects, with benefits causing an increase in the score and side effects causing a decrease.

All the items are scored on a 6 or 7‐point Likert scale; in each domain, raw scores are summed and transformed to a 0–100 metric, and higher scores indicate higher impact of the disease in that domain. For example, in the body image domain, a score of 0 indicates no impact on body image, and a score of 100 indicates severe impact on body image. A total QoL score is calculated using only the life domains and does not include symptom or treatment domains. This allows for inferring whether a change in QoL occurs either independently or in tandem with a change in symptom severity, as well as assessing the effects of expected and perceived treatment effects on overall QoL [10].

The EQ‐5D‐5L is composed of 5 domains that are transformed into a health utility score from 0 to 1, where 0 represents death and 1 represents perfect health [12]. There is also a visual analog scale (VAS) which asks patients to rate their current health on a scale from 0 (worst possible health) to 100 (best possible health). The SF‐36 evaluates 8 domains and provides an individual score for each domain, as well as summary scores for both the physical and mental components of the survey [13]. The scores fall in a range from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating a higher QoL. Canadian normative data for the SF‐36 is also available [14]. The MG‐QoL15 is an MG‐specific QoL measure [15]. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 to 4, resulting in a maximum score of 60, with a higher score indicating a lower QoL.

Disease‐specific severity measures were the Myasthenia Gravis Impairment Index (MGII), which has 22 patient‐reported and 6 examination items; the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL), with 8 patient‐reported items; the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis Score (QMGS), with 13 examination items; and the Myasthenia Gravis Composite (MGC), with 4 patient‐reported and 6 examination items [10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18]. The Neuro‐QoL‐Fatigue‐Short Form (Neuro‐Fatigue) was used as a measure of fatigue [19]. Patients also self‐reported their age, gender, employment, marital status, race, and education level. In addition, clinical data, demographic data, and the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) classification were collected [20].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data is presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data is presented as number and a percentage. We used Pearson's correlation coefficients to determine correlations between QoL and MG severity measures, considering r values of 0–0.10 as negligible, 0.10–0.39 as weak, 0.40–0.69 as moderate, 0.70–0.89 as strong, and ≥ 0.90 as very strong correlations [21]. We compared the mean scores of the SF‐36 domains between MG patients and the Canadian general population through unpaired t‐tests, [14] and also compared mean SF‐36 and INQoL scores in men and women with MG through unpaired t‐tests. We then built multiple linear regression models to determine which factors were associated with QoL, with models for the INQoL and MG‐QoL15 total scores as outcomes. We selected model variables a priori, based on our clinical experience, previous studies, and hypotheses. Because the INQoL has a domain for body image, which is an understudied topic in MG, we also built regression models to assess which clinical and demographic factors were associated with body image, including MGII domain scores (ocular/generalized) to assess the impact of each domain on body image. Using the rule of thumb for linear regression model building, [22] we need approximately 10 observations per variable. We considered p < 0.05 as significant, adjusting these values using the Bonferroni method for multiple testing when appropriate. All data analysis was performed using R statistical software version 4.3.1 (R Statistical Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

When looking at the INQoL domains, the symptoms with the highest overall mean scores—indicating worse symptoms—were muscle weakness and fatigue, with dysphagia scoring the lowest. For the life domains, activities and emotions had the highest scores, indicating higher impact; social relationships had the lowest scores, indicating less impact. Like the SF‐36, there were also gender differences, whereby women had significantly higher burden for every symptom and life domain compared to men except for ptosis, diplopia, and social relationships. However, there were no significant differences in the treatment domains, which had negligible to weak correlations with the other QoL and MG severity measures. Individuals with pure ocular disease had better QoL (mean INQoL score: 22.75 ± 21.12) than those with generalized symptoms (mean INQoL score 40.00 ± 23.95). Multiple regression models (Tables S2 and S3) were built for the study. The INQoL model had an R 2 value of 0.639 and showed that younger age, being retired, and having worse MG severity and fatigue scores were associated with worse QoL. The MG‐QoL15 model had an R 2 value of 0.793 and showed that younger age, being retired, having a thymoma, and increased MG severity and fatigue scores were associated with worse QoL. The regression model for the body image domain had an R 2 value of 0.632 and showed that increased ptosis severity and fatigue scores were associated with worse body image. Discussion

In this study, and using multiple QOL measures, we found that QoL was reduced in MG patients compared to the Canadian general population, which is consistent with previous studies from other countries [4, 23]. However, while women had lower scores in all QoL measures in our cohort, including the INQoL, gender was not significantly associated with QoL scores after adjusting for multiple confounders. In our cohort, the main predictors for reduced QoL were disease severity and fatigue. Therefore, it is possible that the difference in QoL is mostly driven by worse disease severity in women in our cohort, a finding that has been previously reported [24, 25, 26].

Other predictors for worse QoL in both the INQoL and MG‐QoL15 were younger age and being retired. Thymoma was associated with a worse QoL, but only for the MG‐QoL15. However, current prednisone dose was not associated with QoL scores. Although previous cross‐sectional studies have found a relationship between lower prednisone doses and QoL scores, [27] they may be confounded by the relationship between prednisone dose and disease severity. Additionally, given the cumulative effect of steroids, a cross‐sectional study cannot tease out the effects of treatment changes and adverse events over time. Finally, adverse events from other treatments can also affect QoL, and we did not have these data.

Interestingly, perceived and expected treatment effects, which are unique to the INQoL, were not predictors of QoL in either model and had weak correlations with other measures. This may be due to the cross‐sectional nature of this study; prospective studies assessing the relationship between expected treatment effects before starting an intervention and perceived benefits after the intervention, changes in disease severity, and QoL scores are needed.

There are many potential factors that can affect body image in people with MG, such as postural changes due to muscle weakness, thymectomy scar, ptosis, reduced facial expression, and side effects from prednisone such as weight gain, hair loss, and moon facies. However, data regarding body image and its impact on patients with MG is limited. Therefore, the INQoL body image domain provides a perspective of MG patients that is not directly captured by commonly used measures in MG. In our study, the INQoL body image domain was affected similarly compared to the INQoL scores of hallmark MG symptoms such as muscle weakness and fatigue and was more affected than independence and social relationships scores. A study using the INQoL to assess patients with myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) showed similar median scores to our study, [28] but scores were higher in a study of different hereditary myopathies [29]. Body image has also been reported as affected in neuromuscular disorders using other measures, [30, 31, 32] and studies in other diseases have found body image to be a predictor of QoL [33, 34]. This supports including body image when measuring QoL in neuromuscular diseases, including MG.

We found that body image was significantly worsened by increased fatigue and ptosis severity. Fatigue in MG is difficult to characterize as it can either be physical, cognitive, or both [35]. Therefore, whether its impact on body image is physical, cognitive, or both is unclear. Ptosis is also often considered a “mild” symptom in MG; however, the impact of ptosis severity score on body image suggests that it may have a great impact on patients' QoL. Of note, this cohort was mostly composed of patients with generalized disease, so the impact of ptosis in patients with pure ocular disease may be even higher.

Based on a previous study in which women reported more adverse effects from prednisone affecting body image such as weight gain, moon face, and hair loss, [36] we had hypothesized that women and those with higher prednisone doses would have worse body image scores. However, neither gender nor current prednisone dose correlated with body image scores after adjusting for confounders. This is a cross‐sectional study, so the current dose of prednisone does not reflect the overall burden of steroid use over time that could affect body image. Additionally, the body image domain of the INQoL is limited to three questions; there are measures focused on body image more holistically, which might provide more insight into how body image is affected in people with MG.

A possible limitation of our study is the absence of comorbidity data within our sample as they may impact QoL and thus pose as confounding variables. We also recruited patients from a single, tertiary neuromuscular clinic, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Lastly, spectrum bias is a limitation as we compared QoL between MG patients and healthy controls, rather than patients with other chronic diseases.

In conclusion, we found that QoL was decreased in MG patients compared to the Canadian general population and that women with MG had a lower QoL compared to men, likely secondary to a higher disease burden. In addition, using the INQoL we found that increased ptosis and fatigue were predictors of worse body image, and that younger age, worse disease severity and fatigue scores, and being retired were predictors of worse QoL. Our findings introduce the use of the INQoL in MG patients and highlight the importance of body image in MG, which may provide more insight into the patient's perspective. Therefore, a more general QoL measure like the INQoL could be important to add into MG clinical trials to assess other aspects of QoL that MG‐specific measures may not directly capture. Future studies investigating the impact of MG and its effect on body image are needed to better understand the global impact on people living with MG.
