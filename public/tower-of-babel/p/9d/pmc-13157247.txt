Cardioprotective effect of spirulina on cafeteria diet‐induced cardiac injury in silico and in vivo study
Authors: Fatma Arrari, Mohamed‐Amine Jabri, Imen Hammami, Hichem Sebai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDObesity is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, primarily due to its effects on lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of spirulina (SP) on cafeteria diet (CD)‐induced myocardial oxidative stress and inflammation using biochemical, histological, and in silico molecular docking approaches. This study integrates in vivo biochemical and histological analyses with in silico absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiling of spirulina (SP)‐derived phenolic compounds (resorcinol, chlorogenic acid, catechin, syringic acid, sinapic acid, and quercetin) to evaluate the cardioprotective effects of SP. Wistar rats were divided into four groups (n = 8 per group), and treatments were administered orally once daily for 8 weeks as follows: standard diet (SD) (360 g day⁻¹), CD (360 g day⁻¹), CD + SP (500 mg kg−1 body weight), and SP alone (500 mg kg−1 body weight). Body and heart weight, as well as food intake, were recorded. Myocardial oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS)), inflammatory cytokines (interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α)), and histological changes were analyzed. Molecular docking and ADMET prediction were performed to explore the interactions between SP‐derived phenolic compounds and key antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD).RESULTSRats fed with CD exhibited significant weight gain (21.38%), dyslipidemia, increased myocardial oxidative stress, elevated gamma‐glutamyl transferase (γ‐GT) (1.57 U L), and reduced antioxidant defenses. Inflammatory cytokines were markedly upregulated, and histological analysis revealed cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and inflammatory cell infiltration. Spirulina supplementation effectively ameliorated these alterations by improving weight gain (9.85%), lipid profiles, attenuating oxidative stress, reducing γ‐GT (0.83 U L), and downregulating inflammatory responses (P < 0.05). Histological observations confirmed the preservation of myocardial architecture. In silico analysis further demonstrated strong binding affinities between SP phenolic compounds and SOD, and ADMET profiling highlighted their potential – particularly in the cases of catechin, chlorogenic acid, and syringic acid – as safe and effective modulators.CONCLUSIONSpirulina exerts significant cardioprotective effects against CD‐induced myocardial injury by modulating oxidative stress and inflammation, indicating its potential as a natural therapeutic agent for obesity‐related cardiovascular disorders. © 2026 The Author(s). Introduction

Obesity has become a global epidemic, with significant public health implications. Over the past few decades, its prevalence has increased dramatically worldwide, and it is now recognized as a leading risk factor for several chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. 1 Obesity is associated not only with excess body fat but also with metabolic abnormalities that contribute to these diseases. 2 Among the most common causes of obesity are poor dietary habits, particularly diets high in fat, sugar, and calories. Such eating patterns contribute to the development of metabolic syndrome – a cluster of conditions that increase the risk of heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes. 3

Diet‐induced obesity (DIO) is an experimental model widely used to simulate human obesity and its related diseases. 4 The cafeteria diet (CD) model is a more realistic representation of human dietary patterns than standard laboratory chow. It typically consists of a variety of processed foods, including high‐fat and high‐sugar items, which promote rapid weight gain, insulin resistance, and other features of metabolic syndrome in animal models. 5 In this model, animals are given ad libitum access to foods commonly consumed by humans, such as potato chips, biscuits, chocolates, and processed meats, in addition to standard chow. The resulting obesity is accompanied by alterations in lipid metabolism, enhanced inflammatory responses, and increased adipose tissue accumulation, recapitulating the pathological processes seen in obese humans. As such, the CD model serves as an invaluable tool for studying the pathophysiology of obesity‐related diseases, particularly those affecting the cardiovascular system. 6

A critical underlying factor in the development of obesity‐related cardiovascular diseases is oxidative stress. 7 Oxidative stress refers to an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the antioxidant defense systems that neutralize them. Reactive oxygen species, which include hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (OH•), and superoxide anions (O2 •−), are highly reactive molecules capable of damaging cellular components such as lipids, proteins, and DNA. Under normal physiological conditions, ROS are generated as byproducts of cellular metabolism, particularly in the mitochondria. However, during chronic inflammation or metabolic dysfunction, such as obesity, excessive ROS production overwhelms the body's antioxidant defenses, leading to cellular damage. 8 In the heart, this oxidative damage can impair cardiac function by promoting pro‐inflammatory signaling, fibrosis, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which contribute to the development of cardiovascular diseases. Redox imbalance also plays a pivotal role in the progression of atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and heart failure – conditions highly prevalent in obese individuals. 9

In addition to oxidative stress, obesity is often accompanied by an inflammatory response characterized by the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and interleukin‐4 (IL‐4), which further exacerbate the pathophysiological effects of obesity. 10 These cytokines can activate signaling pathways that promote insulin resistance, vascular dysfunction, and myocardial injury. Targeting both oxidative state and inflammatory burden may therefore represent an effective strategy for preventing or mitigating the cardiovascular complications associated with obesity. 11

To address these challenges, recent research has focused on the potential of dietary interventions, particularly the use of antioxidants, to mitigate the harmful effects of oxidant/antioxidant imbalance and inflammatory state. 12 Spirulina, a type of blue‐green algae, has attracted considerable attention due to its rich bioactive compound content, including proteins, vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants. It contains a variety of potent antioxidants, such as phycocyanin, beta‐carotene, and various polyphenolic compounds, which have been shown to scavenge ROS and prevent oxidative damage in multiple tissues. Spirulina has also demonstrated anti‐inflammatory effects in several preclinical studies, making it a promising candidate for alleviating diet‐induced disruption of redox homeostasis and pro‐inflammatory pathways in obesity‐related diseases. 13

Although the beneficial effects of spirulina have been explored in various metabolic disorders, its impact on cardiac health in the context of diet‐induced obesity remains insufficiently studied. 14 Specifically, its ability to reduce oxidative stress, modulate antioxidant enzyme activity, and attenuate cardiac immune response has yet to be fully elucidated. Given the significant cardiovascular risks associated with obesity, investigating the potential cardioprotective effects of spirulina could provide valuable insights into new therapeutic strategies for managing obesity‐related heart disease. 15 , 18

The aim of the current study was to evaluate the effects of spirulina supplementation on impaired antioxidant defense, ROS overproduction, and the activation of inflammatory mediators in the heart tissues of rats fed a CD. Biomarkers of lipid peroxidation, antioxidant enzyme activity, and inflammatory cytokines were assessed to determine whether spirulina mitigates diet‐induced cardiac damage. Spirulina supplementation was hypothesized to attenuate cardiac oxidative stress and inflammation induced by a high‐calorie diet through modulation of antioxidant and inflammatory pathways. In silico profiling of absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET), together with the evaluation of drug‐like characteristics, was performed to assess along with drug‐likeness properties, were made to evaluate the pharmacological profiles of key phenolic compounds. Molecular docking was performed to assess the interactions between spirulina‐derived phenolic components, identified by Arrari et al. (2023), 16 and the active site of superoxide dismutase (SOD), providing insights into their potential antioxidant activity. The findings may contribute to the development of nutritional strategies aimed at preventing or alleviating cardiovascular diseases in obesity, offering a promising alternative to conventional pharmacological treatments.

Material and Methods

Chemicals

Diethyl ether, eosin (E), hematoxylin (H), formaldehyde, epinephrine, bovine catalase, butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), 2‐thiobarbituric acid (TBA), pyrogallol, NaCl, methanol, hydrochloric acid (HCl), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and hydrogen peroxide were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich Chemical Co. (Steinheim, Germany). All other chemicals used were of analytical grade andsourced from standard commercial suppliers.

Preparation of spirulina (SP)

Spirulina flakes were obtained from Eden Life (Kettana, Tunisia). The flakes were ground into a fine powder using an electric blender, then stored in dry bottles protected from light. Prior to administration, 5 g of SP powder was mixed with 50 mL of distilled water and gently shaken for 1 h.

Dietary Model for Inducing Obesity

The CD is an established model for inducing obesity and metabolic disorders by promoting chronic hyperphagia and excessive caloric intake. It mimics the consumption of palatable, high‐calorie human foods that contribute to obesity and metabolic syndrome. In this study, the cafeteria diet (CD) consisted of a combination of peanuts, cheese, potato chips, various biscuits, and chocolates, in addition to standard chow. 17 The macronutrient composition of the CD was approximately 23 g kg−1 protein, 35 g kg−1 carbohydrates, and 42 g kg−1 fat, ensuring a hypercaloric intake that promotes weight gain and metabolic disturbances. 18 The standard diet (SD) provided 300.78 kcal 100 g⁻¹ with 19% protein, 56% carbohydrates, and 8.5% fat, whereas the CD was energy‐dense (420.23 kcal 100 g⁻¹) and richer in fat (30%) but lower in carbohydrates (35%), with protein content of 22%, reflecting its hypercaloric and palatable nature, designed to induce metabolic alterations (Table 1). 5

Animal model and Experimental protocol

The experimental procedures adhered to the ethical standards set by the local ethics committee of Tunisia University, following the recommendations of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) for the care and use of laboratory animals. The protocol was approved by the comité d’éthique biomédicale (CEBM) in Tunisia (ref.: JORT472004/2020). Efforts were made to minimize animal use, in accordance with the 3R (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) principles for ethical animal experimentation.

Male Wistar rats (n = 32, body weight 180–200 g) were housed in standard plastic cages, maintained under controlled temperature (20 °C) and humidity (45%), with a 12 h light/dark cycle. The animals were obtained from the Society of Pharmaceutical Industries of Tunisia (SIPHAT, Ben Arous, Tunisia) and kept on wood shavings, with ad libitum access to food and water.

The rats were divided into four groups (n = 8 per group):

Group SD: The control group rats received an SD along with physiological saline.

Group SD: The control group rats received an SD along with physiological saline.

Group CD: Rats received a CD and physiological solution.

Group CD: Rats received a CD and physiological solution.

Group CD + SP: Rats received a CD and spirulina (500 mg kg−1, administered orally).

Group CD + SP: Rats received a CD and spirulina (500 mg kg−1, administered orally).

Group SD + SP: Rats received an SD and spirulina (500 mg kg−1, administered orally).

Group SD + SP: Rats received an SD and spirulina (500 mg kg−1, administered orally).

Spirulina was administered daily by oral gavage for 8 weeks, and body weight was recorded every 3 days. Following the treatment period, rats were fasted overnight and euthanized by decapitation. The heart was rapidly excised, homogenized in phosphate‐buffered saline, and centrifuged at 10 000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was stored at −80 °C to determine biochemical parameters, including protein content, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), sulfhydryl groups (−SH), reduced glutathione (GSH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and antioxidant enzyme activity.

Blood samples were collected, centrifuged at 3000×g for 15 min, and the plasma was stored at −20 °C for further biochemical analyses. The remaining heart tissue was fixed in 10% formalin for histological examination. A preliminary experiment identified a dose of 500 mg kg−1 as the most effective, providing significant protective and defensive effects, consistent with several in vivo studies that have reported potent effects at this dose. 10 , 16 The CD was provided ad libitum throughout the experimental period. A total of 360 g of the diet mixture was supplied daily per cage to ensure continuous availability. Food intake was monitored daily by calculating the difference between the amount of food provided and the uneaten residue, confirming maintenance of a hypercaloric intake.

Measurement of Body, Heart, and Abdominal Fat Weights

Initial and final body weights were recorded throughout the experimental period to monitor changes in growth and adiposity. At the end of the treatment, animals were sacrificed, and the heart and total abdominal fat, including retroperitoneal and perirenal depots, were carefully dissected and weighed to assess cardiac hypertrophy and adipose tissue accumulation.

Plasma Lipid Profile Analysis

Blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture, and plasma was separated by centrifugation. Triglycerides (TG) (cat. no. 29010, Biomaghreb, Ariana, Tunisia), total cholesterol (TC) (cat. no. 21014, Biomaghreb), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) (cat. no. 24022, Biomaghreb), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) (cat. no. 29010, Biomaghreb), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GT) (Biomaghreb) were measured using enzymatic colorimetric kits. Concentrations were determined using a Selectrapro XL automated biochemical analyzer with commercially available kits from Biomaghreb (ISO 9001 certified).

Assessment of Oxidative Stress

Measurement of Myocardial Lipid Peroxidation

Malondialdehyde concentration was determined using a thiobarbituric acid assay. 19 Briefly, heart tissue supernatants were combined with a butylated hydroxytoluene–trichloroacetic acid (BHT–TCA) solution (1% BHT in 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA)) and centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was mixed with 0.5 mol L−1 HCl and 120 mol L−1 TBA in 26 mol L− 1 Tris–HCl buffer, followed by heating at 80 °C for 10 min. The absorbance of the formed chromophore was then measured at 532 nm using an ultraviolet‐visible (UV‐visible) spectrophotometer.

Activity of Antioxidant Enzymes and Non‐Enzymatic markers

Superoxide dismutase activity was quantified as described by Misra and Fridovich. 20 Catalase (CAT) activity was evaluated using the procedure described by Aebi, 21 and myocardial glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was measured following the method described by Flohé and Günzler. 22 Glutathione levels were determined using the method reported by Sedkal and Lindsay, 23 and thiol groups (‐SH) were assessed according to Ellman's procedure. 24 The protein concentration in heart tissue was determined following the modified Lowry method as described by Hartree. 25

Measurement of Myocardial ROS

Reactive oxygen species levels, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), 26 hydroxyl radicals (OH•), 27 and superoxide anions (O2•−), 28 were assessed using fluorescence‐based assays. These methods quantify specific ROS levels in heart tissue, and provide an index of myocardial oxidative stress. Detection was conducted using appropriate fluorescent probes, and fluorescence intensity was measured with a microplate reader.

The hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentration was determined using Dingeon's method. In this assay, hydrogen peroxide reacts with p‐hydroxybenzoic acid and 4‐aminoantipyrine in the presence of peroxidase, forming a quinoneimine complex, the absorbance of which was measured at 505 nm. 26

The hydroxyl radical (•OH) level was measured according to Payá et al. (1992). Hydroxyl radicals were generated using the Fe3+–ascorbate–EDTA–H₂O₂ system, inducing deoxyribose oxidation. After incubation of heart homogenates at 37 °C for 1 h, the reaction was terminated by adding 2.8% TCA and 1% TBA, followed by heating at 100 °C for 20 min. Absorbance was then measured at 532 nm against a blank containing deoxyribose and buffer. 27

Superoxide anion (O₂•–) production was evaluated following the Marklund method with slight modifications. Samples were incubated in Tris–HCl buffer, and the reaction was initiated by adding pyrogallol. After incubation at 25 °C for 5 min, the reaction was stopped with HCl, and absorbance was measured at 420 nm using an appropriate blank. 28

Inflammatory Marker Analysis

Serum cytokine levels of IL‐6 and TNF‐α were quantified using a standard sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (cat. no. CRS‐B002 and CEA—C010, Bioscience, San Diego, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's guidelines. The results were expressed as pg mg−1 protein.

Histological Analysis

Immediately after euthanasia, the heart tissues were excised and fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde to preserve structural integrity. After fixation, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices. The sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (70% to 100%), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard histological procedures to evaluate cardiac morphology and tissue alterations.

Myocardial structural changes, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, inflammatory cell infiltration, and interstitial fibrosis were analyzed. 29 Histopathological changes were graded using a semi‐quantitative scoring system in which a score of 0 indicated the absence of detectable lesions, 1 denoted alterations involving less than 25% of the examined area, 2 corresponded to changes affecting 25% to 75% of the tissue, and 3 reflected lesions present in more than 75% of the evaluated field. 30

Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity Prediction

Following Arrari et al. (2023), 16 six phenolic compounds identified in spirulina by high‐performance liquid chromatography–diode array detection–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–DAD–ESI–MS/MS) were selected for this study. Their chemical structures were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pccompound) and further processed using the SwissADME and pkCSM platforms. The structures of these phenolic compounds are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Chemical structures of spirulina (SP)‐derived phenolic compounds: resorcinol, chlorogenic acid, catechin, syringic acid, sinapic acid, and quercetin.

A drug's effectiveness can be influenced significantly by its ADMET properties. 31 Pharmacokinetic limitations are a major cause of attrition and increased costs during clinical trials. 32 To address this, computational tools were used to evaluate the ADMET characteristics of spirulina‐derived phenolic compounds and predict their suitability as drug candidates. Physicochemical properties were first predicted, and compliance with Lipinski's Rule of Five was assessed. 33 Pharmacokinetic parameters – including absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion – and three toxicity endpoints (AMES toxicity, hepatotoxicity, and skin sensitization) were analyzed using the SwissADME 32 (https://www.swissadme.ch/index.php#) and pkCSM 34 (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/) online platforms. The BOILED‐Egg predictive model was also applied to estimate the likelihood of the compounds crossing the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and acting as central nervous system (CNS) agents, based on lipophilicity (log PO/W_) and topological polar surface area (TPSA). Oral bioavailability was further evaluated using bioavailability radar plots, which integrate six key properties: lipophilicity (LIPO), polarity (POLAR), molecular size (SIZE), solubility (INSOLU), saturation (INSATU), and molecular flexibility (FLEX).

In Silico Molecular Docking Study

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the interactions between spirulina‐derived bioactive compounds and oxidative stress‐related molecular targets. The aim was to investigate the binding affinity and interaction profiles between SOD and six selected phenolic compounds identified by Arrari et al. (2023) 16 – resorcinol, chlorogenic acid, catechin, syringic acid, sinapic acid, and quercetin – all known for their antioxidant properties. These phenolic compounds were selected based on their documented antioxidant properties, structural diversity, and prevalence in spirulina extracts (Arrari et al., 2023) 16 and their potential to interact with and modulate oxidative stress‐related enzymes such as SOD and CAT.

The three‐dimensional (3D) crystal structure of SOD (PDB ID: 1MFM, the unique identifier assigned to this deposited structure.) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/; accessed June 12, 2025), and the 3D structures of the phenolic compounds were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/; accessed June 12, 2025).

Protein preparation and docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock 4.2. This involved the removal of co‐crystallized ligands and water molecules and the assignment of Kollman charges to the receptor. 35 Docking validation was performed by re‐docking the co‐crystallized ligand to calculate the root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD), which was found to be <2 Å, confirming the reliability of the docking protocol. A grid box with dimensions 60 × 60 × 60 Å, with a spacing of 0.375 Å, was centered on the active site of SOD, using the following coordinates for each compound:

Docking simulations were performed with an exhaustiveness parameter of 8 to ensure adequate conformational sampling of ligand poses. Each ligand was docked individually into the SOD active site to evaluate its binding mode and potential hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. The resulting ligand–enzyme complexes were analyzed and visualized in both two dimensions (2D) and 3D using Discovery Studio Visualizer 2021, 36 providing detailed information on the binding interactions and potential antioxidant mechanisms of these naturally occurring phenolic compounds.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistica 13.0 software (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Prior to analysis, normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Parameters with normally distributed data (e.g., body weight gain, body fat, food intake, and biochemical markers) were compared between dietary groups using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test. Pairwise comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t‐test. Non‐normally distributed data (antioxidant markers and lipid peroxidation) were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney post hoc analysis for pairwise comparisons. Data are presented as means ± standard errors of the mean (SEMs). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Correlations between oxidative stress markers, inflammatory mediators, and lipid profile parameters were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient, and statistically significant associations were identified at P < 0.05.

Results

Effect of SP and CD on body, heart, total abdominal fat weights, and food intake

As Fig. 2(A), (B) shows, initial body weights did not differ significantly across groups (P > 0.05). After 8 weeks, rats fed the CD exhibited a significant increase in final body weight compared with the SD group (329.09 ± 4.21 g vs 263.11 ± 3.01 g, P < 0.01). This weight gain (21.38 ± 1.61% vs 11.31 ± 0.51% in the SD group, P < 0.001) was accompanied by higher heart weight (0.71 ± 0.03 g kg⁻¹ vs 0.36 ± 0.02 g kg⁻¹, P < 0.001), greater total abdominal fat (4.51 ± 1.03 g kg⁻¹ vs 1.65 ± 0.11 g kg⁻¹, P < 0.01), and increased food intake (163.11 ± 5.11 g per day vs 115.21 ± 9.08 g per day, P < 0.01). Spirulina treatment (500 mg kg⁻¹ body weight, orally) attenuated these changes significantly, reducing weight gain (9.85 ± 0.41%, P < 0.001 vs CD), heart weight, and food intake to levels not significantly different from the SD group (P > 0.05), demonstrating statistically confirmed anti‐obesity effects.

Effect of SP and CD on plasma lipid profiles

Figure 2(C) presents the plasma levels of TGs, TC, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL‐c), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL‐c), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (γ‐GT). The CD‐fed rats exhibited significant increases in serum TG (1.03 ± 0.05 vs 0.51 ± 0.01 mM L⁻¹, P < 0.001), TC (1.981 ± 0.05 vs 0.99 ± 0.03 mM L⁻¹, P < 0.001), LDL‐c and γ‐GT levels, along with a reduction in HDL‐c concentrations (0.33 ± 0.02 vs 0.61 ± 0.02 mM L⁻¹, P < 0.01) compared with the SD group. Spirulina supplementation significantly restored TG, TC, LDL‐c, HDL‐c, and γ‐GT levels toward control values (P > 0.05 vs SD), confirming a statistically significant lipid‐modulating effect.

Effect of SP and CD on myocardial oxidative stress

Figure 3 illustrates the effect of the CD and SP on myocardial oxidative stress parameters. The CD‐fed rats displayed significantly higher MDA levels (Fig. 3(A)) (23.42 ± 1.19 vs 12.96 ± 1.13 nmol mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.001), indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation. Cafeteria diet‐fed rats exhibited a significant reduction in key myocardial antioxidant enzymes activity, including that of SOD, CAT, and GPx (P < 0.05 vs SD). Thiol groups (73.8 ± 3.5 vs 135.6 ± 4.9 μmol mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.001) and GSH levels (8.7 ± 0.8 vs 19.8 ± 1.9 nmol mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.001) also decreased significantly compared with the SD group. Spirulina co‐treatment reversed all these alterations (P < 0.05 vs CD), re‐established the pro‐oxidant–antioxidant equilibrium, and attenuated the oxidative damage induced by CD feeding.

Effect of SP and CD on myocardial ROS

Obesity induced by the CD led to a significant elevation of myocardial ROS production (Fig. 4). Levels of hydrogen peroxide (A) (2.72 ± 0.09 vs 1.22 ± 0.05 nmol mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.01), hydroxyl radicals (B) (5.23 ± 0.04 vs 2.14 ± 0.03 nmol mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.001), and superoxide anions (C) (6.96 ± 0.05 vs 3.12 ± 0.03 nmol mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.01) were significantly higher than those of controls. However, SP administration significantly decreased all ROS markers (P < 0.05 vs CD), demonstrating potent antioxidant activity compared with the cafeteria diet.

Effect of SP and CD on myocardial cytokines

An ELISA was used to quantify inflammatory markers. As shown in Fig. 5(A), (B), CD‐fed rats exhibited significantly elevated IL‐6 (9.4 ± 0.7 vs 5.2 ± 0.5 pg mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.01) and TNF‐α (17.9 ± 1.2 vs 10.6 ± 0.9 pg mg⁻¹ protein, P < 0.01) levels compared with controls. Spirulina co‐administration (500 mg kg−1 body weight, administered orally) significantly reduced IL‐6 (6.5 ± 0.9 pg mg⁻¹ protein) and TNF‐α (12.8 ± 0.8 mg protein) levels (P < 0.05 vs CD), confirming the anti‐inflammatory efficacy of spirulina through statistically validated cytokine modulation.

Effect of SP and CD on myocardial histological changes

Histological analysis of myocardial sections stained with H&E revealed substantial morphological alterations in CD‐fed rats compared with the SD group (Fig. 6(A)). The CD group exhibited myocardial fiber hypertrophy, enlarged interstitial spaces, intracellular lipid droplets, micro‐ and macrovascular degeneration, cellular infiltration, and necrotic foci (Fig. 6(A) (b)). In contrast, the SD group displayed normal myocardial architecture without pathological alterations (Fig. 6(A) (a)). Myocardial tissue of obese rats treated with SP showed limited hypertrophy and absence of inflammatory cell infiltration or necrosis compared with the CD group (Fig. 6(A) (c)). Thus, subchronic co‐treatment with SP effectively prevented cardiac fiber hypertrophy and protected against diet‐induced myocardial damage.

The CD also induced pronounced myocardial alterations, evidenced by significantly elevated histological scores (CD: 3 ± 0.2 vs SD: 0 ± 0, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6(B)), increased necrosis (CD: 8 ± 0.5% vs SD: 0 ± 0, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6(C)), and marked cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (CD: 7.4 ± 0.5 μm2 vs SD: 3.1 ± 0.4 μm, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6(D)), reflecting structural disorganization, inflammatory infiltration, oxidative stress, and compensatory remodeling. Spirulina supplementation significantly mitigated these pathological changes, reducing histological injury (CD + SP: 2 ± 0.1, #P < 0.05 vs CD), limiting necrosis (CD + SP: 4.5 ± 0.3%, #P < 0.05 vs CD) denotes a statistically significant difference compared to the cafeteria diet (CD) group, and normalizing myocyte size (CD + SP: 4 ± 0.4 μm2, #P < 0.05 vs CD), thereby preserving tissue architecture. No significant alterations were observed in the SD + SP group (histological score: 0 ± 0; necrosis: 0 ± 0%; cell size: 3 ± 0.3 μm2), confirming that spirulina is safe under physiological conditions. These findings indicate that spirulina exerts clear cardioprotective effects against CD‐induced myocardial injury, likely through antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐hypertrophic mechanisms.

Toxicity Prediction and ADMET

The ADMET predictions and physicochemical properties of the six tested phenolic compounds indicated good drug‐like characteristics with potential therapeutic relevance. All compounds complied with Lipinski's Rule of Five (Table 2), indicating compliance with criteria predictive of oral bioavailability. Among them, sinapic acid (C5) and syringic acid (C4) showed the highest intestinal absorption (93.06% and 73.07%, respectively), supporting their suitability for oral delivery. The low BBB and limited central nervous system (CNS) penetration of all compounds suggests minimal neurological side effects, which is advantageous for cardiovascular applications (Table 3). None of the compounds showed hepatotoxicity, AMES mutagenicity, or hERG I/II inhibition, indicating a low risk of cardiotoxicity. Quercetin (C6) was the only compound predicted to inhibit CYP1A2, suggesting a potential metabolic interaction that may require monitoring.

The BOILED‐Egg diagram analysis further assessed the pharmacokinetic potential of the six natural phenolic compounds in relation to their cardiovascular relevance. Molecules 1 (resorcinol), 4 (syringic acid), 5 (sinapic acid), and 6 (quercetin) exhibited good human intestinal absorption (HIA) and are not P‐glycoprotein (Pgp), substrates, which is consistent with their systemic bioavailability and potential cardiac activity (Fig. 7). Although quercetin lies near the absorption boundary, its cardioprotective efficacy is well established. In contrast, chlorogenic acid (C2) was located outside both absorption and brain‐permeation regions, indicating poor oral bioavailability, whereas catechin (C3), despite being absorbable, was identified as a Pgp substrate (Pgp+), which may limit tissue distribution. Resorcinol, syringic acid, sinapic acid, and quercetin appear to be the most promising candidates for oral therapeutic applications targeting cardiovascular diseases.

Overall, these compounds demonstrated promising pharmacokinetic and safety profiles, supporting their potential as natural therapeutic agents for cardiovascular protection through antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory mechanisms.

Correlation Between Oxidative Stress, Inflammation, and Lipid Profile

Correlation analysis demonstrated a very strong positive association between MDA and IL‐6 (r = 0.90) (Supporting Information, Table S1), indicating that increased lipid peroxidation was closely linked to an intensified inflammatory response (Supporting Information, Fig. S1(A)). This finding supports the concept that oxidative stress and inflammation are tightly interconnected in this model. Conversely, SOD activity showed strong negative correlations with TC, TG, and LDL (r = −0.99, −0.98, and −0.95, respectively) (Supporting Information, Fig. S1(B)–(D)), together with a strong positive correlation with HDL (r = 0.99) (Supporting Information, Fig. S1(E)). These results demonstrate that enhanced antioxidant capacity is associated with a more favorable lipid profile, characterized by reduced atherogenic fractions and elevated protective HDL (Supporting Information, Table S2).

Discussion

This study investigated the cardioprotective effects of spirulina against CD‐induced obesity, focusing on myocardial oxidative stress and inflammation. Biochemical and histological evaluations were conducted to elucidate the potential of spirulina in mitigating cardiac and metabolic alterations triggered by chronic exposure to an energy‐dense diet.

Prolonged consumption of a high‐calorie diet resulted in a marked increase in adiposity, characterized by progressive body weight gain leading to obesity. This weight gain was accompanied by relative cardiac hypertrophy and excessive adipose tissue accumulation, particularly in the retroperitoneal and perirenal compartments, compared with the control group, confirming the obesogenic and lipogenic nature of the CD.

Cafeteria diet‐fed rats also exhibited hyperphagia, as indicated by a significant increase in food intake. The CD promotes obesity by inducing an energy imbalance leading to hyperphagia and disrupted lipid metabolism.37, 38 Overconsumption of fats and carbohydrates contributes to insulin resistance, 39 excessive adipogenesis, and chronic systemic inflammation, 40 which together promote abdominal fat deposition. 37 Increased pericardial adipose tissue may impair cardiac function, particularly by increasing the risk of arrhythmias. 41

Administering SP (500 mg kg−1, body weight, administered orally) for 8 weeks provided significant protection against obesity, helping to mitigate excessive body weight gain and adipose tissue accumulation.

Spirulina regulates body weight and limits the fat deposition through several mechanisms. It activates key metabolic pathways such as adenosine monophosphate (AMP)‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis. 42 It also improves lipid metabolism by activating fatty acid oxidation and inhibiting adipogenesis, reducing excessive fat storage. 43

The CD induced significant dyslipidemia, characterized by increased TC, TG, and LDL‐C, as well as decreased HDL‐C. All these lipid profile alterations are hallmarks of metabolic syndrome and obesity, significantly increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases. 3

Cafeteria diet‐induced hyperlipidemia results mainly from the excessive consumption of saturated fats and refined sugars, promoting abnormal hepatic lipid accumulation. 19 This lipid overload is also accompanied by the disruption of key metabolic pathways, notably those involving sterol regulatory element‐binding proteins (SREBPs) and peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors (PPARs). 44 Several recent studies have demonstrated that these lipid profile alterations are strongly associated with the development of atherosclerosis, increasing the risks of cardiovascular complications. 45

In response to these metabolic disturbances, supplementation with SP, rich in phycocyanin and polyunsaturated fatty acids, has proven effective in improving lipid homeostasis. Several recent studies have indicated that SP promotes reverse cholesterol transport, improves HDL functionality, and modulates lipid metabolism through AMPK activation. 46 , 47

The deleterious effect of the CD is not only limited to lipid alterations but also extends to oxidative stress, an important factor in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular diseases. The CD significantly increased myocardial oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated lipid peroxidation markers like MDA and ROS (H2O2, OH•, and O2 •‐), as well as a decline in endogenous antioxidant defenses, including enzymatic (SOD, CAT, and GPx) and non‐enzymatic (GSH and ‐SH) systems. Diets rich in saturated fats and refined sugars disrupt mitochondrial function, leading to excessive ROS production and reduced efficiency of the electron transport chain (ETC). 48 Prolonged oxidative stress compromises cardiomyocyte membrane integrity through lipid peroxidation and activates pro‐apoptotic pathways, including p53 upregulation and caspase activation, thereby accelerating myocardial cell death. 49 , 50 Recent evidence also suggests that diet‐induced obesity increases nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase activity, contributing to elevated ROS levels in myocardial tissue, and thus exacerbating cardiovascular complications. 7 , 51

Conversely, SP treatment has shown a notable cardioprotective effect by reducing oxidative stress and reinforcing intrinsic antioxidant defense mechanisms. This cyanobacterium is rich in phycocyanin – a blue‐green pigment – and phenolic compounds, which together provide strong antioxidant capacity, 52 , 53 facilitating free radical scavenging and protecting myocardial cells against oxidative stress. Carotenoids present in spirulina, including β‐carotene, contribute to neutralizing free radicals and protecting cardiac cells from oxidative damage. 54 Spirulina also enhances the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, strengthening the body's natural defenses. 48

In line with recent research and following the methodologies previously described by Arrari et al. (2023), 16 the phenolic and flavonoid profiles of SP were characterized by the presence of flavonoids such as catechin and quercetin, as well as phenolic acids including chlorogenic acid, syringic acid, and sinapic acid, along with resorcinol. The extract also exhibited strong antioxidant activity, as evidenced by the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical‐scavenging assay. 55 These findings highlight SP's richness in bioactive compounds and support its therapeutic potential in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation. 19 , 56

Beyond oxidative stress, the CD promotes a myocardial pro‐inflammatory state. This chronic inflammation is characterized by increased levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α, which exacerbate myocardial injury, corroborating previous studies implicating CD in systemic and local inflammation. 5 Multiple molecular mechanisms mediate this response. Excessive intake of saturated fats and refined carbohydrates induce lipid accumulation within cardiomyocytes, triggering mitochondrial oxidative stress. This stress activates pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), enhancing TNF‐α and IL‐6 expression. Dysregulation of AMPK signaling further contributes to impaired cellular energy homeostasis and inflammation, 57 creating an inflammatory microenvironment conducive to myocardial dysfunction. 58

The administration of SP for 8 weeks exerted a significant cardioprotective effect by reducing the pro‐inflammatory cytokines expression and attenuating CD‐induced myocardial alterations. This effect is likely due to its abundance in bioactive molecules, such as phycocyanins, 47 carotenoids, and polyphenols, 52 which possess strong anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties. 13 , 15 Phycocyanin inhibits NF‐κB activation and modulates MAPK signaling, promoting cardiomyocyte energy balance and further suppressing inflammation. 59 Spirulina also contains polyunsaturated fatty acids, including γ‐linolenic acid (GLA), which interact with lipid mediators of inflammation and reduce the biosynthesis of pro‐inflammatory eicosanoids derived from arachidonic acid. 60 , 61

The modulation of the NF‐κB, MAPK, and AMPK signaling pathways by SP contributes to an integrated cardioprotective mechanism, mitigating inflammation, oxidative stress, and myocardial dysfunction induced by a CD. Correlation analyses highlighted the tight interplay between oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic imbalance. A strong positive association between MDA and IL‐6 indicates that enhanced lipid peroxidation parallels an intensified inflammatory response, consistent with the activation of pro‐inflammatory pathways under oxidative conditions. Conversely, strong negative correlations between SOD and the atherogenic lipid fractions (TC, TG, LDL), along with its positive correlation with HDL, underline the protective metabolic role of antioxidant defenses. Increased SOD activity appears to reduce ROS accumulation and limit lipid abnormalities. Overall, these results support the notion that oxidative stress contributes to both inflammation and dyslipidemia, whereas robust antioxidant capacity promotes lipid homeostasis and cardiometabolic protection.

The CD induced marked histopathological alterations in myocardial tissue, including cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and increased inflammatory cell infiltration, consistent with previous reports demonstrating its deleterious effects on cardiac structure and function. 62 Diets rich in lipids and carbohydrates promote myocardial lipid accumulation, leading to lipotoxicity and oxidative stress, which contribute to myocardial degeneration. 63 Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, initially an adaptive response to chronic stress or overload, can progress to heart failure. 64 The presence of inflammatory cell infiltrates reflects immune activation and local inflammation, further exacerbating cardiac damage. 65 , 66

Quantitative histopathological analysis demonstrated that a CD caused substantial myocardial injury, reflected by elevated histological scores, increased necrosis, and pronounced myocyte hypertrophy. These changes indicate severe structural and cellular injury, including fiber disorganization, inflammatory infiltration, vacuolization, necrotic foci, and compensatory hypertrophy, consistent with the cardiotoxic effects of obesity, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress. Spirulina supplementation significantly attenuated these pathological alterations, reducing histological injury, limiting necrotic cell death, and preventing pathological hypertrophy, thereby preserving myocardial tissue architecture. These protective effects are likely mediated by bioactive compounds, including phycocyanin, phycocyanobilin, and phenolics, which exhibit antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties, improve lipid handling, and stabilize cellular membranes. 67 , 68 Spirulina exerted no observable effect on normal cardiac tissue, confirming its safety under physiological conditions. Overall, these findings indicate that SP exerts clear cardioprotective effects against diet‐induced myocardial injury by mitigating structural damage, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses.

In contrast, SP‐treated rats displayed reduced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and a significant decrease in inflammatory infiltrates in the myocardial tissue. The protective effect of spirulina on myocardial histology can be attributed to its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties, which together help prevent or attenuate structural myocardial damage. 69 , 70

The ADMET predictions, BOILED‐Egg diagram, and bioavailability radar plots together indicated that the six tested phenolic compounds possess good drug‐like characteristics, particularly for cardiovascular applications. Sinapic acid and syringic acid demonstrated high intestinal absorption, absence of predicted toxicity, and balanced physicochemical properties, identifying them as promising oral therapeutic candidates. 33 , 71 Resorcinol and quercetin also showed good absorption and bioavailability, although quercetin's inhibition of CYP1A2 suggests a potential for metabolic interactions requiring caution. 72 The BOILED‐Egg analysis confirmed systemic bioavailability of most compounds, except chlorogenic acid, which exhibits low permeability, and catechin, which, despite being absorbable, is a Pgp substrate, possibly restricting tissue distribution. 31 Radar plots further supported these results, demonstrating optimal bioavailability profiles for resorcinol, syringic acid, and sinapic acid, while highlighting the high polarity and reduced membrane permeability of quercetin, catechin, and chlorogenic acid. 73 Overall, these results indicate the potential of these phenolic compounds as candidates for preventing or treating oxidative stress‐ and inflammation‐related cardiovascular disorders. 74

The molecular docking results revealed that the phenolic compounds of SP identified by Arrari et al. (2023), 16 particularly catechin, exhibited the highest binding affinity towards SOD, followed by chlorogenic acid and syringic acid, suggesting a strong potential for direct enzyme stabilization. These compounds are recognized for their antioxidant properties and have demonstrated significant cardioprotective effects. 75 Their cardioprotective potential results primarily from enhancing endogenous antioxidant defense (SOD, CAT, GPx) and scavenging of ROS, thereby reducing oxidative stress‐induced endothelial dysfunction and inflammation. 76 , 77

Catechin has shown strong antioxidant and cardioprotective effects by reducing lipid peroxidation, improving nitric oxide bioavailability, and modulating NF‐κB signaling, protecting against atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction. 78 Similarly, chlorogenic acid exerts antihypertensive and anti‐inflammatory effects by lowering ROS production and inhibiting LDL oxidation, preventing cardiac remodeling. 79 Syringic acid enhances antioxidant enzyme activity and reduces pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α and IL‐6), contributing to cardiovascular protection. 80 Quercetin, possesses potent antioxidants and vasodilatory effects, lowering blood pressure, preventing LDL oxidation, attenuating oxidative stress, and inhibiting platelet aggregation, thereby improving endothelial function and reducing atherosclerosis risk. 81 Sinapic acid, abundant in whole grains and mustard seeds, scavenges free radicals and enhances antioxidant enzyme activity, attenuating myocardial injury and oxidative damage in models of cardiotoxicity and ischemia–reperfusion. 82 Resorcinol displayed the lowest binding affinity towards SOD in the docking analysis but formed multiple hydrogen bonds with key residues and has been reported to reduce lipid peroxidation in other biological contexts. 83

Together, these in silico results support the potential of SP phenolic compounds as modulators of oxidative stress and promising candidates for the prevention or mitigation of cardiovascular disorders. Integration with in vivo and in silico findings demonstrates a comprehensive cardioprotective mechanism: spirulina administration attenuated CD‐induced myocardial inflammation, oxidative stress, and structural alterations, as evidenced by reduced TNF‐α and IL‐6 levels and improved histopathology. Complementing these results, in silico analyses confirmed interactions of six phenolic compounds with SOD, a key antioxidant enzyme involved in the neutralization of reactive oxygen species. Together, these data suggest that SP exerts cardioprotection through a multi‐level mechanism, enhancing antioxidant defenses, mitigating oxidative stress, and preserving myocardial structure and function.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 8) and direct molecular validation, such as Western blotting or reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR), was not performed, which constrains mechanistic interpretation. Second, although the in silico results are promising, they require confirmation in future in vitro or cell‐based assays. Finally, extrapolation to humans should be approached with caution, considering dosage equivalence, bioavailability, and safety. Addressing these limitations in future studies will strengthen the mechanistic understanding and translational relevance of SP's effects.

Conclusion

Spirulina supplementation at a dose 500 mg kg−1 body weight for 8 weeks conferred significant cardioprotective effects against CD‐induced myocardial injury in rats. It attenuated obesity, dyslipidemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation. The antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory potential of spirulina, attributed to bioactive compounds such as phycocyanin, carotenoids, and phenolic compounds, was confirmed through biochemical, histological, and molecular docking analyses. Spirulina restored oxidative balance by reducing MDA and ROS levels while enhancing SOD, CAT, GPx, and GSH activity, and downregulated pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6 and TNF‐α).

Histopathological analysis revealed reduced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, inflammatory infiltration, and interstitial fibrosis. The ADMET profiling and molecular docking analyses further highlighted the potential of SP‐derived phenolic compounds – particularly catechin, chlorogenic acid, and syringic acid – as safe and potent modulators of oxidative stress and inflammation. Overall, these findings highlight SP as a promising dietary intervention for the prevention and mitigation of obesity‐associated cardiovascular diseases.
