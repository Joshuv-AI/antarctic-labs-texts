The association between psychotropic medications and cognitive functioning in bipolar disorder
Authors: Faith Dickerson, Andrea Origoni, Emily Katsafanas, Kelly Rowe, Sabahat Khan, Allana Therese Calahatian, Fahad Mukhtar, Robert Yolken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany individuals with bipolar disorder have decreased levels of cognitive functioning even when in a euthymic mood state. Persons with bipolar disorder are usually treated with psychotropic agents, but the effects of these medications on their cognitive functioning have not been extensively studied.MethodsA total of 567 people with bipolar disorder were assessed on a cognitive battery, the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS), and Trail Making Test, part A (Trails A) and Letter-Number Sequencing. The machine-learning tool of cross-fit partialing-out least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to examine the independent association between the cognitive test scores and receipt of individual psychotropic medications considering relevant demographic and clinical covariates. Ordered logistic regression models were employed to examine the effects of medication dosage on the cognitive scores.ResultsWe found that 3 medications, ziprasidone, benztropine, and clonazepam, were independently associated with significantly reduced cognitive scores compared with individuals not receiving these medications. Benztropine showed a significant dose-related relationship with all of the cognitive measures. Reduced memory and psychomotor speed were the domains most associated with receipt of these medications.ConclusionsPrescribers may consider limiting the administration of the medications which can affect cognitive functioning. Interventions should be further developed for people with bipolar disorder to improve their cognitive functioning and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40345-026-00413-1.Keywords: Cognitive, Psychopharmacology, Memory, Medications, Mood disorder

Background

As a group, people with bipolar disorder show impairment in cognitive functioning relative to age-matched controls, including persons in euthymic mood states (Torres et al. 2007; Kurtz and Gerraty 2009). These deficits are found across cognitive domains and are qualitatively similar, though less severe, than those found in individuals with schizophrenia (Schretlen et al. 2007; Dickerson et al. 2004a, b, c). Among persons with bipolar disorder, there is wide variability in cognitive functioning which cannot be fully explained by clinical subtype or symptom severity (Keramatian et al. 2021; Solé et al. 2017; Flaaten et al. 2024).

Cognitive deficits in bipolar disorder are associated with worse social and vocational outcomes and contribute to the disability associated with the disorder (Baune and Malhi 2015; Dickerson et al. 2004a, b, c). There are not any approved cognitive-enhancing medications for bipolar disorder. The lack of approval of medications stems from a lack of robust results confirming the benefit of medications for cognitive performance in clinical trials (Chen et al. 2021). Psychosocial cognitive remediation strategies may be effective for this population, but studies are limited, and these interventions can be difficult to apply in clinical practice (Samamé et al. 2023; Perra et al. 2025).

The association between psychotropic medications and cognitive performance in bipolar disorder has been the focus of some investigation, e.g. (Xu et al. 2020).,. Lithium, a mood stabilizer and mainstay of medication treatment for bipolar disorder, has been shown to have an adverse effect on psychomotor speed in some studies. However, findings are inconsistent and deficits, when found, appear to be mild (Mora et al. 2013; Paterson and Parker 2017) (Xu et al. 2020).

Second-generation antipsychotic medications are often prescribed to persons with bipolar disorder for their antimanic, mood stabilizing and antidepressant effects. Impairments in verbal memory have been found in persons with bipolar disorder receiving these antipsychotic medications vs. those who are not (Xu et al. 2020). This finding may be confounded by the fact that the subgroup receiving antipsychotics may have more severe psychiatric symptoms. There are few controlled studies examining the effect of antipsychotic medications in bipolar disorder (Daglas et al. 2016), though extensive studies have been performed in other psychiatric populations such as schizophrenia (Dickerson et al. 2025; Feber et al. 2025; Nielsen et al. 2015; Baldez et al. 2021). Medications such as anti-cholinergic agents may also be prescribed to treat the side effects of antipsychotic medications. The adverse cognitive effects of these agents are well-established (Joshi et al. 2021) but have received only limited investigation in bipolar disorder (Erdogan et al. 2025).

Other types of medications are also frequently prescribed to persons with bipolar disorder including anxiolytics and antidepressants to treat symptoms of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance. Investigations of the cognitive effects of benzodiazepines, a major class of anxiolytic medications, in bipolar disorder have been limited but suggest adverse effects (Cañada et al. 2021). Studies in the general population about the effect of benzodiazepines on cognition have had varied results (Edinoff et al. 2021). Similarly, the cognitive effects of antidepressants in bipolar disorder have received only little investigation (Xu et al. 2020), while studies of persons with depression suggest that these medications have a modest positive effect on cognitive functioning in this population (Prado et al. 2018).

Clinical and demographic factors may affect the degree of cognitive deficits in bipolar disorder (Xu et al. 2020). However, there are few studies about the association between psychotropic medications and cognitive functioning in bipolar disorder taking into account the possible effects of factors such as psychiatric symptom severity, illness history, educational status, exposure to environmental factors, and comorbid medical conditions. Furthermore, there are few studies which have examined the effects of medication dosage.

To address these gaps in the literature, we examined the association between psychotropic medications and cognitive functioning in a cohort of 567 adults with bipolar disorder receiving care in community-based psychiatric programs.

Methods

Study population

Participants were recruited at Sheppard Pratt, a large private, non-profit, community-based psychiatric health care system in central Maryland, USA. Participants were enrolled between June 2001 and June 2023 in one of several studies as shown in the Supplement Table 1. The inclusion criteria for all of the study protocols were: (1) diagnosis of bipolar disorder (type 1, type II, or not otherwise specified) meeting criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder Fourth Edition (DSM-IV, 1994) disorders (SCID) (First et al. 1996) supplemented by information from medical records; (2) age 18–65; (3) fluency in English. Exclusion criteria for all of the protocols were: (1) history of intravenous substance abuse; (2) diagnosis of childhood intellectual disability; (3) clinically significant medical disorder that would affect cognitive performance such as epilepsy, HIV, history of encephalitis or serious head trauma, or any other reported neurological disorder of the central nervous system; (4) primary diagnosis of substance abuse or dependence within the past three months; (5) receipt of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) in the previous one month.

Demographic and clinical measures

Participants were interviewed and medical charts reviewed; information was obtained about demographic variables, current tobacco smoking, history of substance use, psychiatric history and other clinical variables as shown in Table 1. Psychiatric symptoms were assessed with the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) (Overall and Gorham 1962), the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (Hamilton 1967), and the Young Mania Rating Scale (Young et al. 1978). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from measured weight and height.

Medications

The psychotropic medications and dosage of each psychotropic medication prescribed to the participant on the day of the study visit were obtained from available medical records and participant interviews.

Cognitive assessment

At the study visit, participants were administered a cognitive test battery, the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) (Randolph et al. 1998), Form A. The RBANS was selected for the measurement of cognitive functioning since it is sensitive to the level and type of cognitive impairments which are often found in individuals with bipolar disorder (Wilk et al. 2004). Test indexes are Immediate Memory (comprised of List Learning and Story Memory tasks); Visuospatial/Constructional (comprised of Figure Copy and Line Orientation tasks); Language (comprised of Picture Naming and Fluency tasks); Attention (comprised of Digit Span and Coding tasks); and Delayed Memory (comprised of List Recall, Story Recall, Figure Recall, and List Recognition tasks). Each index score is expressed as an age-adjusted standard score with a mean of approximately 100 and a standard deviation of approximately 15, based on a previously described normative study group of 540 healthy subjects, ranging in age from 20 to 89, matched to the U.S. Census on gender, ethnicity, and level of education. The index scores are combined to yield a Total RBANS score which is a measure of overall cognitive functioning.

Participants were also administered Letter-Number Sequencing, a working memory task from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale III (WAIS III) (Wechsler 1997); scores were recorded as age-adjusted scaled scores. In addition, participants were administered part A of the Trail Making Test (Trails A), a task of visual scanning and motor speed (Reitan 1979); scores were recorded as scaled scores.

Serological assessment

Participants had a blood sample drawn which was employed to measure antibodies to Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 employing a type-specific enzyme immunoassay. The results were classified as positive or negative based on comparison with a standard sample included in each assay run as previously described (Dickerson et al. 2003). This antibody was chosen for measurement based on previous studies documenting its association with cognitive functioning in individuals with bipolar disorder (Dickerson et al. 2004a, b, c, 2006). The blood sample was also used to measure the level of C-reactive protein which we have also found to be associated with cognitive functioning in this populations (Dickerson et al. 2013).

Statistical analysis

In this cross sectional study, we employed the machine-learning tool of cross-fit partialing-out least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to measure the association between cognitive functioning as measured by the RBANS Total Score and index scores and the receipt of psychotropic medications along with relevant covariates (Zhang et al. 2010). The medications evaluated included all those received by at least 5% of the population at time of assessment. This method of statistical analysis was chosen since it allows for the identification of the independent associations of multiple variables of interest and covariates in an efficient manner with a minimum of confounding. Missing data in the covariates were estimated using multiple imputations. The variables included as covariates along with the number of imputed values are shown in Table 1. Missing data were supplied by multiple imputation as previously described (Dickerson et al. 2025).

The initial LASSO regression model examining RBANS Total score included all the demographic and clinical variables as well as all the 16 medications listed in Table 1.

The LASSO regression models were used to assign regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and probability estimates (p values) (Chernozhukov et al. 2015). All statistical procedures were performed using the “xporegress” and “test” algorithms available in STATA version 19.5. Significance levels (p values) generated by the LASSO models were adjusted to q values reflective of multiple comparisons using the false discovery method of Benjamini et al.(Benjamini et al. 2006) (Benjamani and Hochberg 1995). A q value of ≤ 0.05 was considered to represent statistical significance in light of the multiple medications which were evaluated.

Medications significantly associated with the RBANS Total, Trails A, or Letter-Number Sequencing score were further analyzed by dosage group. The dosage levels tested are depicted in Supplement Table 2. LASSO models were employed to determine coefficients and 95% confidence intervals for the dosage levels for all of the cognitive measures employing the same covariates as the original LASSO models. Ordered logistic regression models using the same covariates were employed to determine significance levels which were adjusted using the false discovery method described above.

Results

A total of 567 participants were enrolled in the study; their demographic and clinical characteristics are depicted in Table 1; there were 16 psychotropic medications that were received by at least 5% of the study population. The total number of psychotropic medications received by study participants is presented in Supplement Table 3.

We employed cross-fit partialing-out LASSO regression models to examine the relationship between RBANS Total score and the covariates and medications listed in Table 1. As depicted in Fig. 1, this model identified significant independent associations between a lower RBANS Total score and the receipt of 3 of the psychotropic medications analyzed. These medications were: ziprasidone (coefficient − 4.87, 95% CI −8.45, −1.29, p=.008, q=0.039); benztropine (coefficient − 7.38, 95% CI −11.08, −3.68, p = < 0.001, q=0.001); and clonazepam (coefficient − 5.13, 95% CI −791, −2.35, p = < 0.001, q=0.001). None of the other psychotropic medications showed a significant association with RBANS Total score in this multivariate analysis.

Discussion

We employed the machine learning tool of cross-fit partialing-out LASSO regression to examine the independent association between psychotropic medications and cognitive functioning in a cohort of 567 people with bipolar disorder enrolled from community-based psychiatric settings. Machine-learning based methods were selected for this study since they overcome limitations inherent in the use of regression models which contain large numbers of potential co-variates. Machine learning models also partially overcome issues such as overfitting, multiple collinearity and model complexity (Geloven et al. 2022) (Lu and Petkova 2014). The use of the LASSO model allowed us to efficiently incorporate the effects of several clinical, demographic, and environmental factors which can affect cognitive functioning.

We found that 3 medications, ziprasidone, benztropine, and clonazepam, were each associated with reduced RBANS Total as well as the RBANS Delayed Memory Index score. We also found that two of the medications, benztropine and clonazepam, were each associated with reduced performance on Trails A. In terms of cognitive domains, reduced memory and psychomotor speed were most associated with these medications.

Benztropine, an anticholinergic medication, was received by 10% of the individuals in our sample. Benztropine was the medication showing the strongest association with cognitive performance in our study and was independently associated with a lower score on several cognitive measures in a dose-dependent manner. Antiparkinsonian agents including benztropine are frequently prescribed to treat and prevent anti-psychotic induced extrapyramidal symptoms. While effective for the treatment and prevention of these side effects, these and other anticholinergic medications have been associated with lower cognitive performance in patients with schizophrenia, as has been reported by our group and others (Joshi et al. 2021) (Dickerson et al. 2025; Mäkipelto et al. 2024; Ogino et al. 2014). In addition, several small studies indicate that discontinuing anticholinergic agents such as benztropine in people with schizophrenia can have a beneficial effect on cognitive functioning (Desmarais et al. 2014) (Lupu et al. 2017). Also consistent with previous studies is the pervasive effect of benztropine on cognitive functioning; 2 of the RBANS index scores as well as performance on Trails A and Letter-Number Sequencing were significantly lower in people receiving benztropine (Fig. 4). The literature on anticholinergic burden in psychiatric patients has largely focused on individuals with schizophrenia, with a small number of studies in bipolar disorder (Erdogan et al. 2025; Vidal et al. 2023). The adverse effects of anticholinergic medications such as benztropine on cognition in this population may not be fully recognized.

Previous studies of the impact of pharmacologic treatment on the cognitive functioning of persons with bipolar disorder indicate that, overall, antipsychotic agents have a negative impact, especially on verbal memory (Xu et al. 2020). It has been suggested that this association may be due, in part, to the fact that the subset of people with bipolar disorder who receive antipsychotic medication may have more severe illness symptoms than those who do not. However, we adjusted for many illness-related covariates in our study, making it less likely that an association between receipt of an antipsychotic medication and reduced cognitive functioning would be affected by confounding.

Ziprasidone is a second-generation antipsychotic medication which has an indication for the treatment of schizophrenia and related psychotic symptoms, the treatment of acute manic or mixed episodes with or without psychotic features, and as an adjunct to lithium for the maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder (Mandrioli et al. 2015). In our sample, ziprasidone was significantly and inversely associated with the RBANS Total and Delayed Memory index scores. Of note, we did not find a negative effect of ziprasidone in a previous study in schizophrenia using essentially identical statistical methods (Dickerson et al. 2025), suggesting differences between schizophrenia and bipolar disorder in the effect of individual anti-psychotic agents on cognitive functioning.

Ziprasidone has been shown to be an effective antipsychotic agent with fewer metabolic side effects and a lower risk for hyperprolactinemia than some other atypical antipsychotic agents (Mandrioli et al. 2015). However, its effects on cognitive functioning in bipolar disorder have not been widely investigated. Early studies in schizophrenia with relatively small samples and short-term follow-up periods or open label designs suggested that ziprasidone improved overall cognitive performance in the domains of verbal memory and executive functioning (Veelen et al. 2010; Gibel and Ritsner 2008). However, as more studies accumulated, these findings were not sustained. Results of a recent meta-analysis on the effects of individual antipsychotic medications vs. placebo on cognitive functioning in stable outpatients with schizophrenia did not find any cognitive enhancing effect of ziprasidone (Feber et al. 2025). However, other meta-analyses, comparing the cognitive effects of individual second-generation antipsychotics to those of each other, found that ziprasidone performed better than other antipsychotics on composite cognitive score, working memory, and verbal learning (Nielsen et al. 2015) (Baldez et al. 2021). This finding is inconsistent with the results of our current study in bipolar disorder in which persons receiving ziprasidone, and not any of the other antipsychotics, had worse performance in overall cognition and a memory measure. It is of note that the finding in the previous study was based largely on individuals with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders whose characteristics likely vary from the persons with bipolar disorder in our study. In addition, our study population was comprised of persons in routine care settings, most of whom were receiving multiple psychotropic medications. In controlled research studies, which form the bases of most of the meta-analyses, study samples are highly selected, and persons typically receive monotherapy, not the varied combinations of medication that are common in clinical practice.

Clonazepam, the third medication which was significantly associated with worse cognitive performance in our study, is a benzodiazepine and commonly used to treat symptoms of panic disorder, severe anxiety, and seizures. The effects of benzodiazepines on cognition have been studied mostly in elderly populations but not specifically persons with bipolar disorder. As a group, these medications have not been consistently associated with adverse cognitive effects, although, in one study, long-term use was associated with relatively greater cognitive decline (Edinoff et al. 2021). There are few studies of the cognitive effects of clonazepam specifically though there is some evidence that it is associated with psychomotor impairment in the elderly (Bierman et al. 2007) (Dokkedal-Silva et al. 2019). In our study, we found an adverse effect of clonazepam on Total RBANS score. We also found a significant association between the receipt of clonazepam and lower performance on Trails A, a task of psychomotor speed. It is of note that other anxiolytic medications, such as lorazepam, were not associated with lower scores on the cognitive measures. The reason for this difference is not known with certainty but may be related to the longer half-life of clonazepam with the potential for drug accumulation over long-term exposure (Greenblatt et al. 1987).

To our knowledge, there are few previous studies examining the dose-related effects of psychotropic medications on cognition in bipolar disorder. It is possible that the higher doses of benztropine associated with worse overall cognitive scores were indicative of more severe psychiatric symptoms leading to the prescription of increased doses, confounding by indication.

An explanation for the adverse effect of these medications on cognitive functioning in bipolar disorder is not known with certainty but may be related to the biological properties of these medications as well as their combined effects on critical neurotransmitter systems and brain regions. These medications disrupt the balance of multiple neurotransmitter pathways, including dopaminergic, GABAergic, and cholinergic systems, all of which are integral to cognitive function. Benztropine’s strong anticholinergic effects disrupt acetylcholine signaling, which may contribute to cognitive impairments (Joshi et al. 2021). Benzodiazepines enhance GABAergic function resulting in widespread CNS depressant effects causing sedation with inattention, and chronic use of these medications has been associated with cognitive deficits (Stewart 2005). Clonazepam is one of the most potent and longest acting benzodiazepines (Chouinard 2004). More extensive consideration of these issues is found in other publications (Feber et al. 2025; Joshi et al. 2021; Chandramouleeshwaran et al. 2024; Risacher et al. 2016).

It is also of note that we did not find any significant relationship between the receipt of lithium and any of the cognitive scores in our sample. The association between lithium and cognition in bipolar disorder has been investigated without consistent findings. Differences among study samples, dose levels, and study designs may account for the different results across studies (Xu et al. 2020; Paterson and Parker 2017).

Strengths of our study include the relatively large sample of people with bipolar disorder, the comprehensive and thorough collection of medication data, standardized protocol and data collection procedures, and standardized and consistent cognitive test scoring throughout the study period. In addition, we applied a machine learning data analytic method that allowed for the inclusion of many covariates. In addition, we were able to analyze the effects of several medication dose ranges. We also had few exclusions to study participation, so our population was likely representative of people with bipolar disorder receiving care in routine, community (non-academic) outpatient and inpatient settings, albeit not including those with recent moderate or severe alcohol or other substance misuse.

Limitations of our study include that the medications analyzed were only those which were received by at least 5% of the study population, so the possible effects of some of the less frequently used medications were not included in the analysis. While the severity of mood symptoms was taken into account in the analyses, we note that participants in the study were in varying mood states and were not all euthymic at the time of cognitive testing. Sample size precluded our ability to study the effects of medication combinations. We also did not have a measure of medication adherence or medication blood levels; however, participants were receiving psychiatric care, and the significant dose-related association with benztropine gives some credence to the findings. In addition, due to the cross-sectional nature of our study, we could not examine the effects of medications on changes in cognitive functioning over time, nor did we include measures of social functioning or medication history. Furthermore, while we controlled for a large number of variables, it is possible that unmeasured variables could contribute to the associations with cognitive functioning. For example, we did not measure total anticholinergic burden of the medications received or patient effort on cognitive tests which can affect cognitive test performance (Joshi et al. 2021) (Morra et al. 2015). Also, while our sample was relatively large, we were underpowered in terms of the ability to examine some relevant factors such as the role of polypharmacy, the dosing regimens of some of the medications, and a history of psychosis. Finally, the medications were not administered following a research protocol but were prescribed by clinicians in community programs based on their clinical judgement. However, we believe that this is also a strength of our study in that it reflects the experience of people with bipolar disorder in real-world settings.

Conclusions

We identified 3 psychotropic medications, ziprasidone, benztropine, and clonazepam, that are associated with poorer cognitive functioning in people with bipolar disorder in community-based care. While these medications have their benefits, prescribers may consider limiting the use of medications which can alter cognitive functioning. Additional interventions, both pharmacologic and psychosocial, should be further developed and evaluated for people with bipolar disorder to improve their cognitive functioning and their quality of life.
