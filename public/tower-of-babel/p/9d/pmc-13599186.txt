Nano-hydrogel-based delivery system for dihydroartemisinin to enhance cuproptosis and synergize with anti-PD-1 therapy in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Qiaoli Yi, Fada Xia, Tong-Fei Li, Jiayu Wang, Liu-Gen Li, Fengyu Tan, Xia Wang, Hailin Tang, Wangrui Liu, Zhijie Xu, Fan Leng, Xiaokang Wang, Yuanliang Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs an aggressive subtype of breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) displays limited therapeutic options and poor clinical prognosis. Thus, there is an urgent need for developing more effective treatment strategies to optimize the clinical management of TNBC. Dihydroartemisinin (DHA), one kind of artemisinin derivatives, exhibits promising antitumor activity mainly through inducing excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, some intrinsic disadvantages, including poor aqueous solubility, short circulation time, and low bioavailability, greatly limit its clinical application. In this research, we constructed an injectable pH/ROS-responsive composite hydrogel (Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel) co-loaded with liposomal DHA (Lip-DHA) and copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles to enhance the therapeutic efficacy against TNBC. The hydrogel system could serve as a localized drug depot, enabling sustained drug release and improved tumor retention. In the tumor microenvironment, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel facilitated efficient intratumoral delivery of DHA and CuS, resulting in mitochondrial copper overload and increased ROS production, thereby synergistically inducing cuproptosis. Copper chelators tetrathiomolybdate (TTM) and D-penicillamine (DPA) could significantly reverse the antitumor effects of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel in TNBC. In the 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel synergistically potentiated the antitumor immunity of PD-1 blockade by inducing the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and promoting intra-tumoral infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells. Taken together, these findings suggested that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel highlights significant therapeutic potential to induce cuproptosis and enhance immunotherapy responsiveness in TNBC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04762-y.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, Nanocomposite hydrogel, Dihydroartemisinin, Cuproptosis, Tumor microenvironment, T cells, Immunotherapy

Introduction

As one of the frequently diagnosed malignant diseases among women, breast cancer (BC) displays a continuously increasing incidence [1–3]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), the most invasive and aggressive type of BCs, is characterized by recurrence potential and unfavorable outcome [4, 5]. Because of distinct molecular pattern, patients with TNBC cannot benefit from HER2-targeted or endocrine therapies, and conventional chemotherapy remains the primary therapeutic option in current clinical practice. However, the therapeutic benefit of chemotherapy is greatly limited by severe side effects and the development of acquired multidrug resistance [6, 7]. Despite the promising progress achieved by immune checkpoint inhibitors (including anti-PD-1 antibodies) in the treatment of TNBC, a substantial proportion of patients still develop therapeutic resistance, which severely limits the long-term efficacy and clinical benefits of such immunotherapeutic strategies [8]. Consequently, fewer than 30% of TNBC patients could achieve complete remission, and the median overall survival (OS) under current treatment regimens is only 10.2 months [9]. Thus, it is urgently required to seek novel and effective therapies for the management of TNBC.

Dihydroartemisinin (DHA), one kind of derivatives from natural compound artemisinin, exhibits better safety, higher bioavailability, and stronger efficacy than artemisinin, and is recommended as a first-line anti-malarial drug [10]. In recent years, DHA is known to possess potential anti-cancer ability in a variety of malignancies. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms responsible for these activities remain to be fully elucidated [11, 12]. Cho and colleague demonstrated that DHA inhibited cancer stemness and metastatic potential in ovarian cancer by regulating the miR-200b–BMI-1/VEGF-A signaling axis [13]. Furthermore, DHA could exert its tumor-killing effects by producing excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately resulting in cellular damage and apoptosis [14, 15]. However, the poor bioavailability and short blood circulation half-life, severely limited the therapeutic potential of DHA as an antitumor agent [16, 17]. To overcome these drawbacks, nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems (DDSs) can be introduced to strengthen the therapeutic efficacy of DHA in cancer treatment. For example, metal-organic framework-encapsulated DHA nanoparticles could significantly enhance the killing activity of DHA against ovarian cancer [18]. More importantly, this DHA-including nanomedicines display attractive biological safety and stable anti-tumor potential, providing the promising candidate strategies for cancer management.

Copper homeostasis provides an essential way for maintaining the normal physiological functions [19]. Dysregulation of copper homeostasis, alongside the identification of copper-dependent cuproptosis [20], has emerged as an important mechanism involved in malignant behaviors and therapeutic resistance [21, 22]. Nevertheless, the ability to induce cuproptosis is substantially limited by poor delivery efficiency of copper ions in vivo and difficulty in perturbing intracellular copper homeostasis, leading to inadequate mitochondrial copper accumulation [23]. In addition, the low selectivity of cuproptosis between malignant and normal cells poses potential biosafety concerns [24, 25]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore strategies that selectively trigger cuproptosis in cancer cells while minimizing damage to normal tissues.

Nanocarrier-based delivery systems have emerged as attractive tool for tumor-targeted therapy, leveraging the unique pathophysiological conditions of the tumor microenvironment to effectively deliver anticancer drugs to the tumor sites [26–28]. Especially, copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles have attracted much attention for their anti-cancer pharmaceutical applications [29]. A hybrid biomimetic nanoparticle system (CuS-PTX-ICG-L-arg@HR) displays excellent tumor-targeting properties, characterized with improved tumor accumulation and circulation time. Administration of CuS-PTX-ICG-L-arg@HR significantly potentiated cell death and enhanced the therapeutic sensitivity in cervical cancer [30]. The CuS-PEG@Apt nanodrug effectively induced immunogenic death (ICD), sequentially sensitizing TNBC to photo-immunotherapy [31]. These reports highlight the attractive ability of CuS-based nanotechnologies on tumor suppression.

Recently, encapsulation of DHA into Lips improves the stability and bioavailability. Furthermore, integration of Lip-DHA with functionalized constituents such as copper ions enriched materials (e.g., CuS) into pH and ROS dual responsive hydrogel may greatly promote the tumor-killing effect of the system. Here, an injectable pH/ROS-responsive hydrogel was constructed to loaded with CuS and liposomal DHA (Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel) (Scheme 1). The hydrogel matrix functions as a localized drug depot, prolonging retention at tumor sites while minimizing systemic toxicity. Enhanced intra-tumoral delivery of Lip-DHA and CuS markedly suppressed TNBC growth by increasing local drug accumulation, promoting mitochondrial copper overload and DHA-induced ROS generation, and thereby synergistically inducing cuproptosis and ICD. By increasing tumor immunogenicity, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel may amplify antitumor response when combined with anti-PD-1 by enhancing intra-tumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration. Our work will provide valuable clues for promoting the clinical application of Lip-DHA and copper ionophores in TNBC treatment.

Schematic diagram of the preparation procedure and anti-breast cancer mechanism of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel. After injection into the TNBC xenograft models, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel undergoes pH/ROS-responsive degradation, enabling sustained drug release of DHA and CuS. The released agents promoted mitochondrial copper overload and excessive ROS generation, thereby inducing cuproptosis in tumor cells. Simultaneously, cuproptosis induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), which in turn led to the secretion of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These signals enhanced the immunogenicity of dying tumor cells and promoted the infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), ultimately strengthening the antitumor immune response

Materials and methods

Preparation of Lip-DHA and Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel

Lip-DHA: 2.5 g Soybean lecithin, 0.1 g cholesterol, 0.2 g DSPE-PEG2000, and 5 mg DHA were dissolved in 2.5 mL of chloroform: methanol: mixture (2:1, v/v). A lipid film was then formed via vacuum evaporation. After adding deionized water and sonicating for 20 min, the liposome (Lip) suspension was extruded using a Lip extruder. Finally, Lip-DHA were obtained through freeze-drying.

Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel: 3 mg of CuS (R-CuS, Ruixitech, China) [32] was dispersed thoroughly in 4 mL of CY-5.5-labeled chitosan solution, followed by adding 400 mg of Lip-DHA powder. 4 mL of oxidized dextran containing phenylboronic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid was blended with the above solution for 10 min. After that, the hydrogel Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel was obtained by adding 0.1 M NaOH. In this study, the hydrogel was formed via a dual cross-linking mechanism. First, oxidised dextran (aldehyde groups) and chitosan (amino groups) formed Schiff base bonds, which served as the pH-responsive motif that hydrolyses and loosens the network in acidic environments. Secondly, phenylboronic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid formed boronate ester bonds, which acted as the ROS-responsive motif that cleaves under high ROS conditions. Together, these two dynamic covalent bonds constructed the stable 3D network and endowed the hydrogel with pH/ROS dual responsiveness. After synthesis, the obtained Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel was purified by repeated centrifugation and dialysis to remove unreacted impurities and free unencapsulated copper ions.

Characterization of Lip-DHA and Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel

Transmission electron microscopy was employed to examine the morphological characteristics and particle size of Lips. And Zetasizer Nano ZS90 was used to analyze the hydrodynamic size and Zeta potential. For Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel, the morphology was observed with a Zeiss Merlin Compact scanning electron microscope. FTIR spectrum was acquired on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 spectrophotometer, and stress-strain curve was obtained with a CTM6010 universal mechanical tester.

Drug release behavior of DHA: First, 0.6 g of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel was added to dialysis bag (MWCO, 8–14 kDa). The dialysis bag was immersed in preheated 50 mL PBS (pH 5.0/7.4, 10 mM H2O2, or pH 5.0 + 10 mM H2O2) in a water bath (37 ℃, 100 rpm). At predetermined time points, 5 mL of release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of PBS. The DHA content released into the medium was measured by HPLC. Cell culture and reagents

Two TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231 and BT549), murine 4T1 cell, and normal breast cell MCF-10 A were acquired from Cancer Research Institute, Central South University. MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cells were grown in 10% FBS-contained DMEM (Corning). BT549 cells were grown in 10% FBS-contained RPMI-1640 (BasalMedia). MCF-10 A cells were grown in the complete medium (Procell).

SLC31A1 antibody (27499-1-AP), FDX1 antibody (12592-1-AP), Actin antibody (66009-1-lg) and LIAS antibody (11577-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech. DLAT antibody (HA721267) was obtained from HUABIO. Ki-67 antibody (ab15580) was procured from Abcam. CD8A antibody (A23305PM) was acquired from ABclonal. The following inhibitors were used in this study: tetrathiomolybdate (TTM, MedChemExpress; #HY-W076067) and D-penicillamine (DPA, MedChemExpress; #HY-B0300), ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, APExBIO; #A4371), 3-methyladenine (3-MA, APExBIO; #A8353), necrostatin-1 (Nec-1, APExBIO; #A4213), and Z-VAD-FMK (APExBIO; #A1902).

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, APExBIO; #K1018) was used to estimate the cell viability. After treatment with the following conditions: (1) control reagent, (2) Gel, (3) CuS@ Gel, (4) Lip-DHA, and (5) Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel, 1 × 103 TNBC cells were seeded into 96-well plates. After then, the cells were incubated with CCK-8 solution (10 µl) for about 2 h. The microplate reader (PerkinElmer, United States) was used to monitor the absorbance at 450 nm in each well.

Cell colony assay

Cell colony formation was employed to evaluate the functional role of nanocomposite hydrogel on cell growth. About 1000 TNBC cells were planted into six-well plates. After treatment with the indicated reagents, the TNBC cells in six-well plates were incubated for approximately 15 days. Cell colonies were stained with 0.3% w/v crystal violet solution, and then photographed and counted.

Live/dead cell assay

Live/Dead cell staining kit (APExBIO; #K2247) was employed to perform live and dead cell level analyses. 5 × 103 TNBC cells were evenly dispensed into 96-well plates. After treatment with the indicated reagents, cells were incubated with Calcein-AM (used for staining living cells) and propidium iodide (used for staining dead cells) for 20 min, and subsequently captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Analysis of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel’s cellular location

Cells were seeded in glass-bottomed culture dishes (Biosharp; #BS-20-GJM), and incubated with Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel (labeled with near-infrared fluorescent tracer Cy5.5). Next, Mito-Tracker Green (APExBIO; #B8811) was used to stain the mitochondria in TNBC cells. The nuclei were then labeled by incubating cells with Hoechst 33,342 (Solarbio; #C0031) for 10 min. The co-localization of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel and mitochondria in TNBC cells was imaged using confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Copper measurement

After treatment with the indicated agents, TNBC cells were incubated with 5 µM Coppersensor 1 (MedChemExpress; #HY-141511) for 20 min in the dark. Attune CytPix flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 561 nm laser was used to evaluate the fluorescence intensity in cells. At least 10,000 single cells were recorded and analyzed for each sample.

ROS analysis and GSH measurement

We used 2′,7′-Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Abcam; #ab113851) to evaluate the levels of intracellular ROS. CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) was employed to analyze the fluorescence intensity of each sample after incubation with 10 µM DCFH-DA. And FlowJo 10 software was used to quantitatively analyze the results of flow cytometry. Intracellular glutathione (GSH) concentration was examined by GSH Assay Kit (Solarbio; #BC1175). Data were normalized to per one million cells.

Evaluation of DLAT aggregation

In 6-well plates, cells were evenly seeded (2.5 × 105 cells/well) and treated with the indicated agents for 24 h. After blocked with 5% normal sheep serum, the TNBC cells were incubation with DLAT antibody at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with HyperFluor™ 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (APExBIO; #K1206) under light-shielded conditions. Ultimately, the cell nuclei were stained blue using DAPI and visualized with a laser confocal microscope.

IFN-γ and granzyme B measurement

IFN-γ and granzyme B levels in 4T1 tumor tissues were examined using the Mouse IFN-γ (Jianglai Bio; #JL10967) and granzyme B (Elabscience; #E-EL-M0594) ELISA Kit following manufacturer’s protocol. Data were normalized by tissue weight.

HMGB1 and ATP measurement

HMGB1 and ATP levels in 4T1 tumor tissue were determined by the Mouse HMGB1 ELISA Kit (Beyotime; #PH406) and ATP assay kit (Beyotime; # S0026), respectively. Using a microplate reader, luminescence of each sample was documented.

Western blotting

As previously described [33], protein expression was examined using immunoblotting analysis. Briefly, proteins from TNBC cells were loaded in equal quantities and separated via 10% SDS-PAGE. After the film transfer reaction, PVDF membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C. Subsequently, the PVDF membranes were incubated with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Ultimately, Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0500, USA) was employed to visualize the protein bands.

TNBC mouse models

Animal experiments followed the guidelines by Animal Care and Use Committee, Central South University. For the establishment of orthotopic TNBC tumors, 1 × 10⁶ MDA‑MB‑231 cells were inoculated into the right mammary fat pads of female BALB/c nude mice, while 5 × 10⁵ 4T1 cells were implanted into female BALB/c mice. Once tumors grew to 50–100 mm³, BALB/c nude mice were randomly assigned to five groups: (1) the control group; (2) Gel group; (3) CuS@Gel group; (4) Lip-DHA group (5 mg/kg); (5) Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel group. To assess the effect of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel with systemic PD-1 blockade therapy, 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were randomly allocated into four groups: (1) control group; (2) anti-PD-1 (clone RMP1-14, MBL; #P362) group; (3) Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel group; (4) combined therapy group. The weight of animals and tumor volume were monitored throughout the experiment. Blood samples were obtained from the mice’s orbital veins at the endpoint of study. Serum biochemical assays were performed to assess the potential systemic toxicity. liver function was evaluated by detecting alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) using commercial activity assay kits (Solarbio; #BC1555/BC1565), while kidney function was assessed by measuring creatinine (CR) using the CR assay kit (Jiancheng Biotech; #C011-2-1). At the endpoint, mice were euthanized, followed by the collection of tumors and essential organs for additional research.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses here were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was utilized to evaluate differences between two groups. Statistical significance was set at *p < 0.05.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel

In this work, we first prepared DHA-loaded liposome (Lip-DHA) by thin-film dispersion method. As shown in Fig. 1A, compared to blank Lip, Lip-DHA maintained intact vesicular morphology without observable aggregation or drug crystallization. The hydrodynamic size of Lip-DHA was slightly larger than that of Lip (Fig. 1B). The polydispersity index (PDI) of Lip-DHA was 0.165 ± 0.008, indicating a narrow size distribution and good homogeneity of the liposomes (Table S1). The drug loading captivity (DLC%) of DHA was 6.85%, and the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) was 97.9% (Table S1), which were obtained by calculating the content based on the Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectrum of DHA. Moreover, the emergence of 290 nm peak in Lip-DHA was absent in empty Lips (Fig. S1). The insertion and loading of lipophilic DHA reduced the exposure of partial polar charged groups, thereby leading to a slight decrease in negative potential from − 35 mV (Lip) to − 30 mV (Lip-DHA) (Fig. 1C). This regular and reasonable charge shift reflects the successful internal loading of DHA, rather than random charge fluctuation. The CuS nanoparticles were purchased from Ruixitech (China). The chemical composition and valence states of the purchased CuS nanoparticles were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The survey spectrum (Fig. S2A) showed the presence of Cu and S elements. High-resolution Cu 2p (Fig. S2B) and S 2p (Fig. S2C) spectra further confirmed the characteristic Cu²⁺/Cu⁺ and S²⁻ signals of CuS, with minor surface oxidation indicated by the SOₓ peaks. The composite hydrogel Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel was then constructed with Lip-DHA and CuS. It can be observed that the gelation transition occurred upon pH adjustment (Fig. 1D). Concurrently, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel possessed fluorescence tracing performance (Fig. 1E) and a porous structure (Fig. 1F). According to the FTIR spectrum (Fig. 1G), the absorption peak at 3294 cm⁻¹ was attributed to the stretching vibration of -OH and -NH₂ in chitosan and dextran. The amide I band at 1625 cm⁻¹ corresponded to the intrinsic amide groups in chitosan and the amide bond formed by the coupling of CY5.5 with chitosan, and the intense peak at 1025 cm⁻¹ originated from the polysaccharide sugar ring and the C-O-C structures of DHA. As depicted in Fig. 1H, within the low strain range of 0–40%, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel showed a near-zero stress growth, reflecting its injectability. Beyond 40% strain, stress progressively increased with strain, providing mechanical support for the continuous release of drugs. Further, the drug release experiments confirmed that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel possessed prominent dual pH and ROS responsive release behaviors (Fig. 1I). Compared with neutral physiological conditions, the tumor-mimicking acidic environment and high ROS microenvironment collectively accelerated carrier degradation and cargo liberation. Especially, the synergistic effect of acid and oxidative stress significantly promoted structural collapse of the hydrogel–Lip system. Consistently, the fastest release kinetics was observed under pH 5.0 plus 10 mM H₂O₂, with the cumulative release rate approaching 88% within 4 d and exceeding 90% at 6 d. Such microenvironment-dependent release kinetics ensures preferential and rapid drug liberation in tumor tissues, which is critical for realizing targeted antitumor delivery and reducing off-target damage to normal tissues. Further in vivo fluorescence imaging revealed persistent drug retention in tumor tissues at day 4 post-treatment (Fig. 1J-K), confirming the tumor-targeted delivery and sustained-release capacity of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel. Notably, considerable fluorescence signal was also detected in the liver at the same time point (Fig. 1L), indicating the liver-mediated detoxification and clearance processes [34].

Anti-proliferative effects of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel on breast cancer cells

CCK-8 assay was performed to explore the potential cytotoxicity of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel on TNBC cells. As depicted in Fig. 2A, the results revealed that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel suppressed growth rate of TNBC cells. Conversely, it exhibited negligible toxicity against the normal mammary epithelial cell MCF-10 A, even at relatively high concentrations. The proliferative effects of TNBC cells were remarkably downregulated in the Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel treatment group, even more significantly than in the Lip-DHA treatment group, which supports its enhanced therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 2B-C). The gradually enhanced cell growth inhibition verified that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel could exert potent and programmable anti-TNBC activity in a quantitative and temporal-dependent pattern. Similarly, cell colony formation assays further confirmed that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel exhibited a higher anti-proliferative effect than Lip-DHA in TNBC cells (Fig. 2D-F). Additionally, we applied confocal microscopy to evaluate cell viability through the live/dead staining assay. Neither the control nor the Gel groups showed noticeable cell death, but the Lip-DHA group exhibited significant cell death, with a higher rate of dead cells in the Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel treatment group (Fig. 2G-I). Collectively, these results indicated that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel had better anticancer effects against TNBC cells.

In vivo evaluation of anti-breast cancer effect and potential toxicity of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel

The antitumor effectiveness of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel was further assessed in two orthotopic TNBC mouse models (Fig. 3A). In MDA-MB-231 tumor-bearing mouse model, Lip-DHA treatment mildly suppressed tumor growth, whereas Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel elicited an additive tumor growth-inhibition effect, as indicated by significant decrease of tumor volume and weight (Fig. 3B-D). During the entire experimental period, no significant alterations in body weight were observed among all groups (Fig. 3E). To explore the potential toxicity of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel in mice, the biochemical indicators for hepatotoxicity (ALT and AST) and nephrotoxicity (CR) were detected. As shown in Fig. 3F, no notable differences were found in serum ALT, AST, or CR levels across all groups, indicating negligible systemic toxicity.

Similar results were replicated in 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mouse model (Fig. 3G). Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel treatment could markedly suppress tumor growth and exhibit superior antitumor effects compared with Lip-DHA, which only exhibited a modest inhibitory effect (Fig. 3H-J). Consistently, both body weight and serum ALT, AST, and CR levels remained comparable among the groups (Fig. 3K-L). Tumor cell proliferation was markedly reduced in the Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel group compared with other groups (Fig. 3M-N). Furthermore, no significant morphological abnormalities were observed in major organs according to H&E-stained results, confirming the favorable biosafety of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel in vivo (Fig. S3). Notably, the biosafety profile observed in the 4T1 murine model was consistent with that previously obtained in the MDA-MB-231 xenograft model, demonstrating the reproducibility of these findings. Thus, these findings demonstrated the effectiveness and safety of CuS@Gel as a nanoplatform to enhance the Lip-DHA antitumor effects.

Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel exerts its antitumor effect by inducing cuproptosis

To elucidate the potential antitumor mechanism of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel in TNBC, mass spectrometry–based proteomic analysis was carried out to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in TNBC cells treated with Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel or Lip-DHA. As shown in Table S2, 2,747 upregulated and 2,217 downregulated proteins were identified in MDA-MB-231 cells, whereas 905 upregulated and 983 downregulated proteins were identified in BT549 cells. The analysis of KEGG pathways indicated the potential roles of DEPs in regulation of oxidative phosphorylation, copper ion homeostasis, mitochondrial respiratory chain, and aerobic tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. 4A), suggesting the involvement of cuproptosis of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel. Next, confocal laser scanning microscopy was used to examine the subcellular localization of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel. The red fluorescence of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel strongly overlapped with the green fluorescence of mitochondria (Fig. 4B-C), indicating that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel can effectively target and accumulate in mitochondria. In TNBC cells, treatment with Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel led to a marked reduction in intracellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ATP production (Fig. S4A-B), two critical metabolic indicators of mitochondrial function. To further confirm the roles of cuproptosis in contributing to Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel-induced cytotoxicity, cells were treated with different programmed cell death (PCD) inhibitors (e.g., copper chelator TTM, ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), and autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA)) and analyzed using a CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 4D, TTM significantly rescued Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–induced cell death, whereas the other inhibitors exerted only minimal or even no protective effects. These results indicated that cuproptosis may be the major mechanism of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–induced cell death, which could be effectively reversed by cuproptosis inhibitor TTM.

The TNBC cells treated with Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel showed a time-dependent progressive increase in intracellular copper ion levels, indicating sustained pH-responsive release and cellular uptake of copper ion (Fig. S5A-B). Compared with Lip-DHA or the control group, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel treatment resulted in a notable elevation in intracellular copper levels in TNBC cells (Fig. 4E-F), suggesting a potential link between Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel and cuproptosis. The hallmarks of cuproptosis are the elevated intracellular ROS levels, reduced GSH levels, and depleted Fe-S cluster proteins (e.g., LIAS and FDX1) [35, 36]. The TNBC cells treated with Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel showed a time-dependent progressive increase in intracellular ROS levels (Fig. S5C-D). Consistently, ROS assays revealed that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel markedly increased intracellular ROS levels, whereas Lip-DHA induced only a modest elevation, and Gel and CuS@Gel alone exerted negligible effects on ROS generation (Fig. 4G-H). In parallel, intracellular GSH levels were significantly reduced following Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel treatment compared with Lip-DHA or control groups (Fig. 4I). Additionally, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel elevated the levels of copper transporter SLC31A1 and concomitantly reduced the expression of FDX1 and LIAS (Fig. 4J). Another hallmark of cuproptosis is the aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial proteins [37]. Accordingly, western blotting and immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel markedly promoted the levels of DLAT oligomerization (Fig. 4K-M), a canonical mitochondrial lipoylated enzyme. Immunohistochemistry analysis further indicated that SLC31A1 expression was significantly increased, whereas FDX1 and LIAS were markedly decreased, in the 4T1 xenografts treated with Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel (Fig. 4N-Q). Taken together, these results provided convergent support that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel regulated cuproptosis in TNBC cells.

Cuproptosis inhibition reversed the antitumor effects of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel

To further investigate the potential contribution of cuproptosis in Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–induced cell death, we examined the effects of copper chelators TTM and DPA on cell survival of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–treated TNBC cells. TTM and DPA treatment partially rescued the suppression in cell proliferation and clonogenic survival caused by Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel (Fig. 5A-C). Consistently, live/dead staining assay showed that cuproptosis inhibitors could significantly reverse the dead cell rates in Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–treated cells (Fig. 5D-E). Moreover, TTM and DPA effectively reversed the increased intracellular copper levels induced by Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel (Fig. 5F-G). This was accompanied by a significant attenuation of intracellular ROS levels and a restoration of GSH levels triggered by Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel (Fig. 5H-J). Further investigations revealed that TTM and DPA abolished the Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–mediated elevation of SLC31A1, and depletion of FDX1 and LIAS protein (Fig. 5K). Furthermore, DLAT oligomerization was markedly suppressed upon TTM or DPA treatment (Fig. 5L-N). Together, these findings suggest that cuproptosis is a crucial mode of cell death underlying the antitumor activity of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel.

Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel potentiates anti-PD-1 Immunotherapy in orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer

The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) also indicated that the potential roles of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel in regulation of several immune-related pathways, such as T cell activation and adaptive immune response (Fig. 6A), indicating the potential immunomodulatory role of this nanoplatform. In order to evaluate whether Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel could improve the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade therapy, we constructed an orthotopic 4T1 model (Fig. 6B). Compared with either monotherapy, the combination therapy of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel with anti-PD-1 significantly delayed tumor growth, as indicated by the decreased tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6C-E). Notably, the body weight of mice and serum ALT, AST, or CR levels in each group remained relatively constant throughout the study, indicating favorable systemic tolerability (Fig. 6F-G).

To explore how Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel improves the anti-PD-1 sensitivity, we next investigated the cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in 4T1 xenograft tumors. Two cytotoxic molecules commonly secreted by CTLs, IFN-γ and granzyme B, were markedly elevated by the combined treatment, compared with monotherapy or control treatment (Fig. 6H-I). Consistently, flow cytometry demonstrated an increased infiltration of CD8+ T cell in both tumor tissues and peripheral blood following combination therapy (Fig. 6J-M). IHC analysis further corroborated these observations, showing reduced Ki67 expression and enhanced infiltration of CD8 CTLs in tumors treated with the combination therapy of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel with anti-PD-1 (Fig. 6N-P). Given the strong stimuli of several nanoparticles in the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) [38, 39], we also evaluated the effects of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel on DC maturation. Expectedly, flow cytometry identified the significantly higher CD80+CD86+ mature DCs observed in Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel groups (Fig. S6A-D). Emerging evidence suggests that DHA could remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by eliciting ICD [40–42]. The defining feature of ICD is the effective secretion and exposure of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as triphosphate (ATP) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). ELISA assay showed a significant increase in HMGB1 and ATP levels in tumors treated with the combined therapy (Fig. 6Q-R). These findings indicate that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel–mediated cuproptosis induces ICD to trigger the release of DAMPs, thereby enhancing the immunogenicity of dying tumor cells and promoting the massive infiltration of CTLs. This process ultimately facilitates robust antitumor immune responses and sensitizes tumors to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.

Discussion

Increasing attention has been directed toward the antitumor potential of DHA, owing to its potent antitumor properties and relatively low systemic toxicity [18]. DHA could suppress cancer stem cell characteristics, tumor angiogenesis, and peritoneal dissemination, and overcome carboplatin resistance in ovarian cancer [13]. Another study by Zhou et al. [41] revealed that DHA could be used to remodel tumor microenvironment and enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy by inhibiting cyclin-dependent kinases, increasing cellular ROS, and then inducing ICD. However, several inherent disadvantages, such as low stability, poor water solubility, short elimination half-life, and low bioavailability, largely limit the clinical application of DHA. Therefore, there is an urgent need to rationally design advanced DHA delivery platforms to improve the antitumor efficacy.

To address these limitations, various nanoscale DDSs have been explored to optimize the stability and bioavailability of DHA, thereby maximizing its therapeutic effect. Among them, Lip nanoparticle formulations have attracted particular interest due to their good biocompatibility and biodegradability, as well as efficient loading ability for hydrophilic drugs [43, 44]. Liposomal DHA has been reported to prolong circulation time, enhance the bioavailability at tumor site, and reduce off-target toxicity [45, 46]. Nevertheless, conventional liposomal delivery systems still face challenges, such as suboptimal tumor targeting efficiency, insufficient tumor retention, undesirable premature leakage, and uncontrolled release of drugs, which may limit sustained therapeutic efficacy in tumor. Thus, delivery strategies that integrate sustained local retention with multifunctional therapeutic modalities warrant further investigation. In this study, we constructed an injectable nanocomposite hydrogel loaded with CuS and liposomal DHA (Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel), featuring ROS and pH dual-responsive drug release properties. The rapid drug release profile of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel under pH 5.0/ROS-rich conditions in vitro clearly illustrate its highly sensitive responsive behavior in a simulated microenvironment. Nonetheless, the actual in vivo tumor milieu is far more intricate than the buffered in vitro system, and the hydrogel scaffold enables prolonged retention and gradual degradation within solid tumor tissues. In practice, even though the loaded payload is effectively liberated upon exposure to the acidic and ROS-abundant tumor microenvironment, the hydrogel matrix remains in the local tumor site to continuously entrap, stabilize, and sustain the intratumoral drug concentration, thus preventing rapid diffusion and systemic clearance of the released drug [47–50]. Thus, this specific delivery system not only improves DHA retention and controlled release within the tumor microenvironment, but also introduces copper-mediated synergistic effects. Both in vitro and in vivo assays indicated that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel exhibits preferable therapeutic activity against TNBC. Especially, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel displays the favorable biosafety. Compared with normal cells, malignant cells inherently maintain higher steady-state ROS levels and display compromised antioxidant defense systems. Moreover, breast cancer cells are highly dependent on aberrant mitochondrial metabolism and thus more susceptible to mitochondrial injury. Their uncontrolled cell proliferation rate and high genetic instability further amplify sensitivity to external stimuli. In contrast, normal mammary cells possess intact redox homeostasis, functional mitochondria, and efficient genetic stability [7, 51, 52]. Together, the inherent biological differences between malignant cells and normal cells lead to the favorable tumor-selective activity and minimal cytotoxicity exhibited by Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel.

Cuproptosis, a newly discovered copper-dependent cell death, is characterized by depletion of Fe-S cluster proteins and oligomerization of lipoylated proteins in the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle [37]. Emerging evidence has highlighted the regulatory function of copper homeostasis in metabolic reprogramming, tumor progression, and treatment resistance [53, 54]. Radiotherapy induced mitochondrial copper accumulation via upregulation of copper transporter 1 and depletion of mitochondrial GSH, thereby promoting cuproptosis in cancer cells. Notably, by acting synergistically with radiotherapy, cuproptosis inducers helped to overcome tumor radioresistance [36]. The copper transporter 1 could mediates cellular copper uptake, while the depletion of mitochondrial GSH facilitates massive mitochondrial copper enrichment. Excessive mitochondrial copper overload disrupts mitochondrial metabolism, triggers proteotoxic stress, and ultimately initiates cancer cell cuproptosis [36, 55]. Moreover, cuproptosis-induced mitochondrial proteotoxic stress could enhance the immunogenicity of dying cells by promoting the DAMP secretion, thereby triggering classical ICD [21, 56]. Previous studies have revealed that DHA could remarkably increase intracellular ROS accumulation and trigger the immunostimulatory signals associated with ICD [40, 41, 57, 58]. Accordingly, we constructed an injectable nanocomposite hydrogel, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel. Functional analyses showed that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel could effectively suppress TNBC growth by inducing cuproptosis, and the tumor-killing effect could be further enhanced upon combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors by reprograming the tumor microenvironment.

Notably, several nanoplatforms targeting cuproptosis have recently been explored for cancer therapy. The majority of these nanosystems utilize copper-based nanoparticles, copper ion delivery vectors, or copper-containing composite materials to trigger FDX1-dependent lipoylated protein aggregation and subsequent cuproptotic cell death. For instance, a pH stimuli-responsive Cu2O/Cu2−xSe nanocapsule could increase intracellular released Cu+ ions, thereby eliciting tumor-specific cuproptosis and ICD [59]. Similarly, a copper-based delivery system GD@MSN-CG could simultaneously stimulate the release of Cu+ and glucose oxidase (GOx), thereby inducing strong cuproptosis and anti-melanoma immunity by producing substantial H2O2 [60]. In comparison, our injectable nanocomposite hydrogel (Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel) possesses multiple unique merits. This system enabled site-specific and long-term sustained release of CuS and liposomal DHA, effectively reducing off-target toxicity while inducing potent cuproptosis in TNBC cells. More significantly, the hydrogel actively remodeled the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and acted synergistically with immune checkpoint inhibitors to reinforce anti-tumor immune efficacy. Collectively, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel constitutes a more comprehensive, biocompatible, and translationally promising strategy, underscoring its considerable potential for clinical combination cancer immunotherapy.

Excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) serves as a critical regulator of tumor development and therapeutic responsiveness. Recently, various strategies aimed at improving intracellular ROS concentration have been established to enhance anticancer therapeutic efficacy. Girigoswami’s findings suggested that CeO2 nanoparticles could be taken up by living cells, resulting in cytotoxic effect against cancer cells through promoting ROS generation [61]. Similarly, this research group also pointed that serving as potential ROS inducer, the nanoformulation CS-Alg-R6G markedly improve cellular ROS yield, sequentially facilitating photodynamic therapy-mediated cell death [62]. Accordingly, we successfully constructed a nano-hydrogel-based system (Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel) to delivery DHA. The in vitro and in vivo analyses demonstrated that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel could induce ROS production and further drive cell death in TNBC cells.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully constructed an injectable pH/ROS-responsive composite hydrogel, Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel, enabling synergistic chemotherapy and immunotherapy. This synthesized Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel demonstrated favorable characteristics, thereby maximizing intratumoral drug accumulation while minimizing systemic exposure and unintended side effects. Moreover, on-demand release of drug triggered by tumor microenvironment (acidic pH and elevated ROS) improved spatiotemporal precision, causing enhanced tumor-fighting capabilities and increased immunostimulatory effects. Studies performed in vitro and in vivo indicated that Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel significantly inhibited tumor growth by inducing cuproptosis. Importantly, the nanoplatform further potentiated anti-PD-1 immunotherapy by promoting DHA-mediated ICD in non-immunogenic TNBC. Overall, these findings highlighted the potential of Lip-DHA-CuS@Gel as a promising nanosystem for synergistic cancer treatment.
