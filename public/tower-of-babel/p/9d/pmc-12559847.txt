Intermediate coronary microvascular dysfunction: a case report highlighting challenges in diagnosis and management
Authors: Anurag Tripathy, Danial Saleem, Abdullah Mahmood, Mustafa Ahmed, Fadi Hage
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is a heterogeneous and underappreciated clinical presentation of cardiac chest pain in the absence of obstructive epicardial coronary disease. While risk factors for CMD are not dissimilar from those that contribute to epicardial coronary disease (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, etc.), CMD requires additional therapy beyond addressing those risk factors.Case summaryA 46-year-old woman presented with cardiac chest pain and non-ST elevation myocardial infarction. Myocardial perfusion imaging demonstrated a large, moderate perfusion defect in the anterior myocardium. Left heart catheterization ruled out epicardial disease, and microvascular testing was intermediate. Nevertheless, her symptoms resolved completely with empiric medical management of presumed CMD.DiscussionInvasive ischaemic testing can facilitate a CMD diagnosis and offers more diagnostic specificity than non-invasive modalities. However, diagnostic thresholds remain debated and can be confounded by coronary anatomy and clinical profiles, posing challenges to accurately diagnosing CMD. A high index of suspicion and a low threshold for empiric treatment may be beneficial in alleviating symptoms.

Introduction

Current literature indicates that a significant proportion (50%–70%) of patients undergoing invasive coronary angiography for cardiac chest pain are found to have normal or non-obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD).1–4 This presentation often leads to diagnoses such as angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) or ischaemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA); coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is thought to drive symptoms in a subset of these patients.1,2,5 Management of these conditions remains less clear, and outcomes are comparatively poorer, with notably increased rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) including risk of death, non-fatal myocardial infarctions and strokes, and heart failure hospitalizations.1,2,5 Several meta-analyses underscore that failure to recognize CMD and heterogeneous management practices can lead to under-treating persistent anginal symptoms, significantly impacting quality of life.1–6 We present the case of a young woman who presented with clinical symptoms consistent with CMD despite inconclusive testing, highlighting diagnostic and management challenges.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with recurrent cardiac chest pain described as severe, mid-sternal chest pressure radiating down her left arm with accompanying numbness. Symptoms worsened with exertion and were relieved with rest. Initial workup was notable for elevated high sensitivity troponin T levels peaking at 201 ng/L (normal ≤ 15 ng/L) with inferior ST-segment depressions concerning for a non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). Given a low pre-test probability of obstructive coronary disease, resolution of symptoms, and normalized troponin after 48 h of medical therapy (unfractionated heparin, statin, and aspirin), an exercise SPECT myocardial perfusion study was planned for further evaluation.

Aside from hyperlipidaemia (HLD) (LDL 131 mg/dL on admission), the patient reported no personal risk factors for CAD. Family history included HLD but no early myocardial infarction, significant CAD, or heart failure. She previously led an active lifestyle with a heart-healthy diet. She was diagnosed with COVID-19 years prior, after which she complained of palpitations and was found to have >10% premature ventricular contraction (PVC) burden on Holter monitoring. She could not tolerate metoprolol due to fatigue. A cardiac MRI at the time showed no late gadolinium enhancement nor features suggestive of ischaemia, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, or arrhythmogenic dysplasia.

The differential diagnosis for her presentation included obstructive epicardial CAD, coronary vasospasm, CMD, and anomalous coronary artery anatomy. Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated normal biventricular function, valvular anatomy, and wall motion. During the exercise myocardial perfusion study, she experienced crushing sub-sternal chest pressure after 7.1 metabolic equivalents (predicted 8.7 for age and sex), resolved by sublingual nitroglycerin. She achieved a peak heart rate of 163 b.p.m. (baseline 77) and peak blood pressure of 162/83 mmHg (baseline 135/82). Stress electrocardiogram (ECG) was notable for diffuse ST-segment depressions > 1 mm in depth (Figure 1A and B). No PVCs were noted during symptoms. Perfusion imaging demonstrated a large area of moderate ischaemia involving 35% of left ventricular (LV) myocardium in the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery territory. Transient ischaemic dilation was noted without LV stunning, and the ejection fraction was estimated at 73% (Figure 1C). Coronary angiography showed a trans-apical (Type III) LAD with no obstructive epicardial disease, anomalous anatomy, or myocardial bridging (Figure 2A–C). Thermodilution CMD testing with adenosine of the LAD was notable for a coronary flow reserve (CFR) of 2.1, and an index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) of 24 under hyperemic conditions (Figure 3). Due to acetylcholine unavailability, intracoronary acetylcholine testing to assess endothelial function for CMD and microvascular spasm could not be performed.

The patient was started on amlodipine 2.5 mg, atorvastatin 40 mg, and aspirin 81 mg daily with complete resolution of her symptoms. Amlodipine, a calcium channel blocker, was chosen for its applicability in both endothelial-dependent CMD and vasospastic angina. Additionally, due to her prior intolerance of beta-blocker therapy and desire to avoid angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEi/ARB) given her childbearing age and low-normal blood pressure, they were deferred. Calcium channel blocker therapy in patients intolerant towards beta-blockers and ACEi/ARBs is also per the recommendations in the latest guidelines.

Over the following 6 months, she was asymptomatic with medical therapy, successfully completed cardiac rehabilitation physical therapy, and returned to her baseline functional status of strenuous exercise five times a week. She tolerated amlodipine and low-dose aspirin and is undergoing evaluation for a PCSK9 inhibitor due to statin intolerance.

Discussion

Coronary microvascular dysfunction is a heterogeneous and clinically wide spectrum of anginal chest pain that highlights impaired responses of the coronary vasculature to vasodilatory stimuli.1,5,7 Inappropriate assessment of the nature of that functional impairment leads to incongruous treatment practices.2,5,8 The heterogeneity of treatment is partially due to the different endotypes of CMD: endothelial-independent (tested by adenosine) and endothelial-dependent (tested by acetylcholine).9 This distinction is significant as it guides appropriate treatment strategies without subjecting patients to extemporaneous treatment and associated medication side-effects.3,10 Traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, HLD, autoimmune illnesses, etc., also need aggressive management.3,4 The ESC Working Group on Coronary Pathophysiology and Microcirculation position paper on ‘coronary microvascular dysfunction in cardiovascular disease’ recommends aspirin, statin, and ACEi as initial therapies for CMD.4 Vasodilatory beta-blockers (e.g. nebivolol, carvedilol, etc.) and calcium channel blockers (e.g. amlodipine or diltiazem) have also demonstrated efficacy in CMD anginal management. Calcium channel blockade is strongly encouraged for endothelium-dependent variants of CMD, as suspected in our case, and offers increased empiric benefit since provocative testing could not be performed and therefore remains in the differential.1,3,4,7

Ong et al. proposed the Coronary Vasomotor Disorders International Study (COVADIS) diagnostic criteria for CMD: (i) presence of myocardial ischaemia symptoms, (ii) no obstructive epicardial CAD, (iii) presence of myocardial ischaemia on functional imaging, and (iv) impaired coronary vascular function evidence.3,7,9 In contrast, the diagnostic algorithm for vasospastic angina involves nitrate-responsive angina either at rest (particularly at night or early morning) or marked diurnal variation in exercise tolerance; responsiveness to calcium channel blockers but not beta-blockers is also suggested.7 Transient spontaneous ischaemic ECG changes and coronary artery spasm are equivocal but not necessary for the diagnosis.4,7 Our patient met three of these criteria and had several risk factors for CMD: female sex, HLD, and a prior COVID-19 infection.11,12 Her invasive microvascular testing was intermediate. While CFR and IMR cut-offs of ≤2.0 and ≥25, respectively, have been described as diagnostic thresholds, recent data suggest that patients with CFR 2.0–2.5 are physiologically indistinguishable (i.e. similar myocardial perfusion and exercise physiology parameters) from those with CFR ≤ 2.0.6,9 Additionally, IMR has been shown to be influenced by the amount of subtended myocardium and vessel length, two potential confounders in our case (35% myocardial perfusion deficit, Type III LAD).13 We also hypothesize that her prior COVID-19 infection (after which her cardiac symptoms began) represents a possible explanation as SARS-CoV-2’s strong affinity for vascular endothelial cells (due to high angiotensin-converting enzyme II receptor density) can mimic endothelial-dependent CMD.11,12

Though invasive testing offers some sensitivity and specificity advantages over certain non-invasive imaging modalities, discordant/intermediate results are common.2,13 Furthermore, full-breadth invasive testing with intracoronary acetylcholine is not widely available.14 In the USA, no commercial intracoronary acetylcholine is available.14 Additionally, acetylcholine carries a risk of epicardial spasms.14 Consequently, newer techniques like cardiac PET have emerged as viable diagnostic options because it can quantitate important hyperemic metrics like myocardial blood flow and myocardial flow reserved for CMD diagnosis.15 Here, we demonstrate some of the challenges of diagnosing CMD with positive non-invasive ischaemic testing and clinical symptoms but intermediate-range invasive microvascular testing.

Conclusions

This is a unique case of CMD isolated to the LAD and demonstrates that despite the increasing availability of invasive microvascular testing, intermediate results are common. In situations where CMD and vasospastic angina remain undifferentiated, empiric treatment approaches with pharmacotherapy like calcium channel blockers are appropriate and can lead to clinical improvement. Continued research into the refinement of CMD diagnosis and treatment is necessary for improved cardiovascular outcomes.
