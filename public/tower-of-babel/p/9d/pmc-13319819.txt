Biomedical waste management in independent dental practices: Interrogating regulatory challenges, sociocultural dynamics, and environmental health implications in Indonesia
Authors: Oktavia Dewi, Herniwanti Herniwanti, Novita Rany, Emy Leonita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effective medical waste management is a critical intersection of public and environmental health that remains a persistent challenge within independent dental practices. These clinics generate hazardous biomedical waste introducing significant risks if inadequately handled due to constrained regulatory oversight and institutional capacity. This study investigated waste management practices among independent dental clinics in Pekanbaru, Indonesia to elucidate the regulatory hurdles, resource deficits, and social and environmental dynamics governing compliance and operational execution. A descriptive qualitative design was employed using an input, process, and output analytical framework. Data triangulation was achieved through detailed interviews, direct observations, and document analyses involving local health authorities, professional associations, and independent practitioners. The evaluation matrix assessed inputs including policy, human capital, financing, and infrastructure, processes ranging from waste identification to final destruction, and outputs such as regulatory adherence. Findings revealed pronounced deficiencies in policy dissemination, workforce competency, and disposal infrastructure. Although baseline waste identification and segregation were generally executed, critical vulnerabilities emerged during the collection, temporary storage, and final disposal phases. Overall regulatory adherence remained moderate because of suboptimal enforcement, economic constraints, and prevailing cultural norms influencing practitioner behavior. Medical waste management in independent dental practices constitutes a multifaceted challenge dictated by regulatory and organizational determinants. Mitigating these risks necessitates robust policy enforcement, comprehensive professional capacity building, and integrating environmental health metrics into primary care governance to ensure safe and sustainable healthcare delivery.

1. Introduction

Biomedical waste generated by independent dental practices encompasses a diverse and substantial array of hazardous materials including sharp instruments, human anatomical waste, expired pharmaceuticals, and dental supplies contaminated with blood and saliva. 1 , 2 Disposing of these hazardous substances into municipal solid waste streams precipitates severe ecological and public health ramifications. 3 Dewi 4 asserted that comprehensive biomedical waste management is imperative to neutralize threats to environmental integrity and community health. Consequently, medical waste laden with infectious pathogens, human tissues, and sharp instruments demands meticulous handling protocols. 5 Implementing robust and appropriate management strategies is therefore indispensable for dental clinics dealing with infectious waste. 6 Standardized techniques involve rigorous waste segregation, cautious physical handling, and the mandatory utilization of personal protective equipment such as masks and gloves. 7

Across the Southeast Asian region, biomedical waste management practices exhibit significant variability contingent upon local regulatory enforcement and available treatment infrastructure. Within Indonesia, empirical data from 2021 indicated that only 27 percent of healthcare facilities achieved national waste management standards, thereby underscoring profound implementation deficits particularly within smaller clinics and remote geographical areas. Recent literature further reveals that while neighboring nations including Malaysia, Thailand, and Vietnam have successfully scaled centralized treatment facilities utilizing licensed incineration services, autoclave technologies, and regulated transportation networks, smaller healthcare providers persistently encounter operational barriers. These obstacles primarily involve inadequate waste segregation, poor documentation, and improper final disposal stemming from restricted technical and financial resources. Such regional disparities illustrate that biomedical waste management continues to be a critical environmental health challenge uniquely affecting small independent healthcare practices. 8 Establishing robust regulatory frameworks, fostering partnerships with specialized waste management enterprises, and adopting autonomous management paradigms collectively contribute to minimizing overall waste generation and subsequent environmental expenditures. 9 Furthermore, integrating advanced technological solutions such as volume reduction equipment with compression mechanisms significantly enhances disposal efficiency. 10 Dental practitioners bear a fundamental obligation to comprehend the legal mandates and social responsibilities inherent in safe medical waste management to effectively curtail waste production. 11

A multitude of scholarly investigations have underscored the pivotal role of dental practices in executing effective biomedical waste protocols. Mariam 12 and Bawa 13 highlighted that rigorous waste management in dental settings is paramount for safeguarding public health and environmental sustainability. Conversely, Vyas 14 documented a concerning knowledge gap, revealing that merely 42 percent of surveyed dentists possessed adequate comprehension of medical waste management protocols. Corroborating these findings, Kamble 15 emphasized an urgent necessity to elevate professional awareness and strict adherence to established clinical guidelines among dental practitioners. While prior research has extensively investigated medical waste paradigms across various Indonesian provinces, the specific operational context within Pekanbaru remains marginally explored, especially concerning its unique sociocultural dynamics and localized municipal regulations. 16 The municipal government of Pekanbaru, operating through the Environmental and Sanitation Agency, promulgated Regional Regulation Number 5 of 2021 regarding Specific Waste Management. This legislative act explicitly mandates the autonomous segregation of hazardous materials at the individual healthcare facility level. Nevertheless, practical compliance among independent dental clinics remains alarmingly inadequate, a phenomenon largely attributed to insufficient policy dissemination and the absence of robust enforcement mechanisms.17,18 Addressing these systemic gaps, the present study comprehensively explores the input, process, and output variables defining medical waste management. This research investigates how the implementation of local regulations, resource constraints, and monitoring frameworks collectively dictate waste management behaviors among independent dental practices situated in Pekanbaru. Preliminary observations suggest that compliance targets for medical waste management within these independent dental facilities in Pekanbaru currently stand at a critical threshold of 65 percent.19,20

2.2. Informants

The study involved three distinct categories of informants: (A) The Chairperson of the Indonesian Dental Association for the Pekanbaru Branch, (B) Head of the Environmental Health Division at the Pekanbaru Health Office, and (C) 25 independent dental practitioners representing every subdistrict within Pekanbaru. These independent practitioners were selected via purposive sampling to guarantee comprehensive representation across all local subdistricts. The inclusion criteria required participants to operate an active independent dental practice, routinely generate medical waste, and express a clear willingness to participate in the research. A total of 25 practices were incorporated to effectively capture the variations in waste management behaviors across different geographical areas. The participant recruitment process continued until data saturation was reached. This saturation point was confirmed when no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews concerning policies, resource allocation, waste management procedures, and regulatory compliance.

2.3. Data collection

Data acquisition was conducted through direct field observations, detailed qualitative interviews, and rigorous document analysis. The research team systematically observed the input, process, and practical implementation of medical waste management within the independent dental practices while utilizing the conceptual framework illustrated in Figure 1 as a foundational reference point.

2.4. Data analysis

The collected data underwent qualitative analysis comprising data reduction, data presentation, and the drawing of conclusions. This analytical approach ensured accurate and thematic reflections of the acquired information. Consequently, the findings are articulated in a narrative format that aligns directly with the primary objectives of the study.

2.5. Ethical considerations

All participants supplied written informed consent for the anonymous publication of their information within this article. The research strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Furthermore, the study protocol obtained official ethical clearance from the Research Ethics Committee of Universitas Hang Tuah Pekanbaru under the approval No. 348/KEPK/UHTP/VIII/2023.

Detailed interviews with the chairman of the Indonesian Dental Association for the Pekanbaru branch provided further context. The chairman confirmed the lack of dedicated policies regulating medical waste management specifically for dental practices in Pekanbaru, Indonesia. Furthermore, this informant noted that while human resources such as dentists and dental nurses are available, all associated management fees are funded entirely by the dentists themselves without any external financial assistance. Additionally, there is a distinct lack of dedicated land designated for the storage and processing of medical waste.22,23 Discussions with the head of the Pekanbaru City Health Office and the head of its environmental health section corroborated that a general policy regulates waste management across all healthcare facilities. However, they acknowledged that regulations specific to dental practices in Pekanbaru remain insufficiently detailed.24,25 These officials also observed a general deficit in highly competent human resources specializing in medical waste management within this sector. Because no external funding is allocated to support these processes, independent dental practices are compelled to independently manage and finance their medical waste disposal.

Finally, interviews conducted with 25 independent dental practitioners operating across Pekanbaru City reinforced the absence of specific regulatory policies. These practitioners revealed a clear disparity in workforce competency. Specifically, 16 waste processing staff members were identified as lacking necessary competencies. Conversely, 5 individuals were considered competent due to prior training, while another 4 personnel demonstrated high competency supported by formal nursing education, specialized training, and a solid grasp of standard operating procedures. The financial burden of waste treatment falls entirely on the practicing dentists without any government subsidies. Furthermore, the lack of dedicated land for waste storage and management is a persistent issue, with only 6 practitioners utilizing their personal yards to bury solid medical waste.

4. Discussion

The input variables assessed in this study comprised regulatory frameworks, human capital, financial expenditures, and land availability for biomedical waste management within independent dental clinics. Interviews conducted with informants A and B indicated an absolute absence of formal regulatory policies specifically governing waste management protocols in these independent facilities. Furthermore, operational costs for waste disposal were exclusively financed by the practitioners themselves, coupled with a complete lack of designated physical spaces for appropriate biomedical waste disposal. Current regulations established by the Ministry of Environment and Forestry regarding biomedical waste stress the fundamental necessity of explicit policy frameworks to properly control and eliminate healthcare waste. Executing an effective waste management infrastructure demands not only a proficient workforce but also comprehensive professional education alongside continuous training initiatives for healthcare personnel.29,30 In addition, securing adequate physical areas for waste disposal and integrating sustainable management systems remain substantial developmental challenges across emerging economies.

Numerous preceding investigations have highlighted that strict adherence to health and environmental regulations forms the cornerstone of appropriate medical waste management. 31 Fundamental components encompass waste segregation directly at the point of origin, the utilization of standard receptacles and personal protective equipment, alongside advanced treatment technologies including autoclaves or incinerators.32,33 Explicit standard operating procedures combined with comprehensive legal documentation cultivate a robust culture of regulatory compliance among clinical staff. A holistic strategy addressing medical waste must incorporate environmentally sustainable technologies and specific containment units exclusively designated for hazardous materials. Biomedical refuse frequently harbors dangerous infectious pathogens that present profound risks to environmental integrity and public safety. 34 The primary environmental footprint of medical waste involves global warming potential ranging from 0.588 to 1.122 kg CO2 equivalent, followed by human noncarcinogenic toxicity varying between 0.002 to 0.003 kg CO2 equivalent, and ecotoxicity scaling from 0.000 to 0.001 kg CO2 equivalent. 35 Vigorous governmental supervision remains imperative to monitor waste recycling networks, enforce regulatory compliance, and eradicate illicit operations operating within the medical waste sector.36,37

Although independent dental practices successfully executed the majority of parameters within the process category, profound vulnerabilities were identified concerning waste collection, temporary containment, and final eradication. Frequently, the absence of designated disposal zones in Pekanbaru forced practitioners to perform localized destruction methods including direct burial or open incineration. Pioneering strategies are urgently needed to resolve the logistical hurdles associated with waste transportation and storage. 38 Automated recycling systems for medical waste that seamlessly combine transportation, secure packaging, precise weighing, and transparent cloud integrated data logging possess the capacity to significantly decrease contamination risks and limit direct human contact. 39 Mobile collection vehicles outfitted with active air-drying mechanisms effectively mitigate leakage incidents and suppress noxious odors throughout the transit phase.40–42 To curtail environmental pollution and occupational health hazards, strict obedience to specific transportation regulations remains absolutely vital. 43 Given that numerous Indonesian hospitals lack proprietary treatment infrastructure, these institutions frequently establish partnerships with certified waste management corporations to ensure appropriate processing. 44 Established legislative frameworks govern secure processing methodologies and guarantee the protection of both the public and the surrounding ecosystem. 45 Uncompromising adherence to these statutory provisions is mandatory because procedural mismanagement directly threatens community health, environmental sustainability, and regional sanitation benchmarks. 46

Transportation vehicles dedicated to medical waste necessitate frequent sanitation protocols to avert cross contamination. Technological innovations including vehicular high temperature processing units, advanced sterilization modules, and ultraviolet disinfection systems have been recommended to uphold stringent hygiene standards during transit operations.47,48 The Indonesian Ministry of Health Regulation Number 18 of 2020 establishes the fundamental legal baseline governing biomedical waste operations within independent clinical facilities. Nevertheless, the prevailing deficit of allocated land for secure containment and specialized treatment frequently drives hazardous practices, notably open incineration, amalgamation with municipal domestic refuse, or direct localized burial. 49 Such actions represent direct violations of the prevailing regulatory mandate, which explicitly demands safe and ecologically sustainable protocols for the segregation, collection, containment, and ultimate destruction of clinical waste.

The present investigation did not yield significant empirical evidence to suggest that local cultural norms directly affect medical waste management methods. The main constraints discovered were directly related to poor enforcement of regulations, extensive financial limitations and extremely inadequate disposal infrastructure. These findings are consistent with recent studies in Indonesia that have shown that tiny health institutions have always operational barriers for interim containment and logistical transport due to infrastructural deficiency. Similar barriers have been well documented across Southeast Asia, where significant variation in compliance between urban medical centers and independent private clinics is partly attributed to differential availability to novel treatment technology. 50

Fatlawi and Al Alwani 45 emphasize the urgent need to develop effective solid waste management systems to proactively manage anticipated increases in biomedical waste quantities. Their empirical evaluations suggest that effective communication channels, fair resource allocation, committed leadership, and active staff involvement are essential foundations for successful regulatory implementation. Strict adherence to these strategic suggestions is essential for clinical facilities to ensure safe waste processing while reducing negative impacts on local residents and nearby ecosystems. Inadequate processing of biomedical waste unavoidably leads to significant environmental pollution, with soil deterioration being a particularly notable outcome.

Uncontrolled subterranean disposal and indiscriminate open burning of hazardous chemicals may eliminate soil qualities and greatly diminish agricultural productivity. Scientific literature further confirms that pharmaceutical drugs that are not disposed of properly have the potential of greatly changing physical soil matrices and adding radiological, chemical, and biological toxicity to terrestrial substrates and ground aquifers. 51 This widespread contamination severely disrupts subsurface microbial habitats by substantially lowering biological diversity, population density and metabolic activity. Un-treated discharge of pharmaceutical active compounds into environment destabilizes vital soil microbial populations, hence threatening core ecological services. 52 The application of stringent waste processing procedures, such as enhanced disinfection techniques, and a strict prohibition on direct environmental disposal or the open-air combustion are basic prerequisites for the reduction of these ecological threats. The present practice of uncontrolled incineration and local burial by individual dentistry practices in Pekanbaru has been shown to negatively impact the acidity of the soil, the elemental nutrients, and general agricultural fertility. These adverse consequences unmistakably highlight the urgent need for an all-encompassing upgrading of waste management systems.

5. Conclusion and recommendations

The result of this study shows that the management of biomedical waste in independent dentistry clinics is a serious environmental and public health problem in Pekanbaru, Indonesia. Most of these clinics were able to perform basic waste identification and methods of segregation, but there were substantial gaps in provisional containment, logistical transit and final disposal protocols. Hazardous waste is still disposed of through open-air incineration, underground burial on private land and mixing of medical waste with municipal waste mainly due to weak regulatory frameworks, limited financial resources, poor infrastructure and unregulated local disposal practices. The results of this empirical study indicate that the management of biological waste in small clinics is shaped not only by technical expertise, but also by administrative governance and sociocultural variables. The absence of explicit operational instructions for the independent dentistry practitioners, restricted access to the approved disposal companies and irregularity in policy communication have led to the low regulatory compliance of the studied group.

The current study has several methodological shortcomings. The observational sample was limited to 25 independent dental clinics in Pekanbaru, which may limit the generalizability of our findings in other geographical situations. Moreover, the ecological pollution was measured by means of observation field work and not detailed laboratory investigations of soil, water or air samples. Behavioral assessments and participant perspectives of sociocultural impacts were studied, which demonstrated a high need for a full examination through ethnographic or integrated methodological approaches.

The Pekanbaru City government has to step up the enforcement of Regional Regulation Number 5 of 2021, by strengthening the legislative communication, doing a deep evaluation on the compliance of the regulation and widening the access for the registered medical waste transport businesses. Continuing education programs should be developed by dental professional associations to improve the waste management skills of clinical practitioners. Additional research by academicians should explore broader geographical focus and critically evaluate innovative and economically sustainable waste management solutions for small healthcare facilities in underdeveloped countries.
