Aripiprazole versus Bupropion Augmentation in Patients with Anxious Depression Resistant to Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors: A Randomized, Prospective, Open-label, Multicenter Study
Authors: So-Hye Jo, Bon-Hoon Koo, Kwang-Hun Lee, Jong-hun Lee, Seung-Jae Lee, Young-Woo Park, Hyung-Mo Sung, Eun-Jin Cheon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveResearch on which adjunctive therapies are more effective for treatment resistant anxious depression is lacking. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of bupropion versus aripiprazole as adjunctive therapies in patients with anxious depression who are resistant to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs).MethodsA total of 48 patients with anxious depression, defined as a score ≥ 7 on the anxiety/somatization factor of the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), were enrolled in this 6-week, randomized, prospective, open-label, multicenter trial. Participants, having shown at least moderate to severe depressive symptoms despite four weeks or more of SSRI monotherapy at maximum recommended or tolerated doses, were randomized to receive either aripiprazole or bupropion.ResultsBoth treatment groups exhibited significant improvements in HAM-D and Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale scores from baseline to week 6 (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the groups in the changes observed for Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale scores (p = 0.370), HAM-D (p > 0.999), anxiety/somatization factor scores (p = 0.177), Inventory of Fatigue Scale (p = 0.600), Clinical Global Impression-Severity (p = 0.620), or the rates of remission (p = 0.130) and response (p = 0.452). Additionally, the Drug-Induced Extrapyramidal Symptoms Scale and Psychotropic-Related Sexual Dysfunction Questionnaire scores showed no significant differences.ConclusionAripiprazole and bupropion are both effective and well-tolerated as augmentation strategies in SSRI-resistant anxious depression. While these findings provide clinical insights into individualized augmentation for anxious depression, they should be considered exploratory, necessitating larger-scale, definitive trials.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Anxiety, Augmentation therapy, Aripiprazole, Bupropion

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a widespread and severe mental disorder that greatly diminishes quality of life and contributes significantly to the global socioeconomic burden [1]. Particularly, the prevalence of MDD and anxiety disorders has increased following the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, underscoring the importance of establishing effective treatment strategies [2,3].

The current standard approach to treating MDD primarily involves antidepressant monotherapy; however, large-scale studies indicate that more than half of the patients show only a partial response or no response to this treatment approach [4,5]. Moreover, nearly half of the patients with MDD exhibit persistent or recurrent symptoms despite adequate antidepressant treatment, presenting significant challenges for clinical management [6]. These limitations highlight the necessity of optimizing therapeutic strategies that not only target symptom reduction but also enhance the overall quality of life.

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is one of the most significant therapeutic challenges in the field of psychiatry and is associated with increased utilization of healthcare services, need for higher-intensity interventions, and rising treatment costs [3,7]. TRD poses considerable social and economic burden due to severe outcomes such as increased risks of psychiatric and medical comorbidities and higher suicide rates [8,9]. Inadequate response to initial antidepressant therapy frequently leads to the development of TRD, further exacerbating both individual distress and healthcare system costs. Therefore, the establishment of optimal treatment strategies for TRD is crucial for mitigating this burden and facilitating more effective and personalized therapeutic interventions.

Depression accompanied by anxiety is recognized as a distinct subtype of depressive disorder. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) differentiates MDD from anxiety distress [10]. Although the prevalence estimates vary across studies, evidence suggests that anxiety symptoms are present in approximately 54.2% to 78% of individuals with MDD, indicating a high rate of co-occurrence of MDD and anxiety [11]. This subtype is characterized by greater depressive severity, poorer clinical outcomes, and the need for more careful and intensive management. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that anxious depression is associated with increased symptom severity, marked impairment in psychosocial functioning, more frequent and prolonged depressive episodes, and heightened risk of suicidal ideation and attempts [12-15]. These findings suggest that anxious depression should be conceptualized as a more severe and treatment-resistant subtype instead of simply a comorbid manifestation of MDD.

Therapeutically, anxious depression is associated with poorer medication adherence and diminished treatment responsiveness relative to non-anxious MDD [16-18]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that this population experiences delayed remission and a decreased probability of achieving remission following antidepressant therapy [19]. These characteristics highlight the importance of therapeutic approaches that simultaneously target both anxiety and depressive symptoms.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are widely prescribed as first-line agents for moderate-to-severe depression and anxiety owing to the favorable safety and tolerability profiles. Nevertheless, over half of the patients with anxious depression do not achieve an adequate response to initial SSRI treatment [19,20]. This finding indicates that targeting serotonin alone may not adequately address the complex neurobiology of anxious depression; abnormalities in other monoamines, notably norepinephrine and dopamine, could play a role. The clinical severity and reduced SSRI responsiveness in anxious depression suggest a neurobiological profile distinct from that of typical MDD, supporting the rationale for treatment approaches that act on multiple neurotransmitter systems beyond serotonin.

Augmentation therapy is a common strategy for antidepressant-resistant depression, with aripiprazole and bupropion among the frequently used adjuncts. Aripiprazole has established efficacy as an add-on in TRD [21,22], and bupropion is widely chosen when primary antidepressant response is inadequate [5]. Both drugs have shown positive therapeutic effects as adjunctive treatments in patients who do not respond sufficiently to monotherapy [23].

Although both aripiprazole and bupropion are effective for treating typical TRD, head-to-head comparisons are limited [24]. Notably, no prior studies have directly compared the efficacy and safety of these agents for the treatment of SSRI-resistant anxious depression. This paucity of data leaves clinicians without clear guidance on selecting the most suitable adjunctive treatment for this hard-to-treat subgroup, as the existing evidence largely pertains to broader TRD populations and not populations with anxiety-dominant conditions.

This study aimed to address this gap by targeting SSRI-resistant anxious depressive conditions. We compared the therapeutic efficacy and safety of bupropion and aripiprazole as adjunct treatments, and examined the effects on specific depressive symptoms. Our goal was to develop optimized personalized augmentation strategies for patients with anxious depression who did not respond adequately to SSRI therapy.

METHODS

Study Design

This was a 6-week, prospective, randomized, open-label, multicenter clinical trial. The present cohort represents a post hoc subgroup analysis derived from a larger prospective randomized study comparing the efficacy and safety of adjunctive aripiprazole versus bupropion in patients with MDD who are resistant to first-line treatment. Resistance was defined by the Thase and Rush [25] criteria as non-response to at least one adequate course of SSRI therapy. This analysis was conducted in an exploratory manner to compare the efficacy and safety of aripiprazole versus bupropion in anxious depression, with a particular focus on differential effects on core depressive symptoms and related symptom subdomains.

Participants

Participants aged 19−70 years who met the DSM-IV-TR criteria for MDD were enrolled. Inclusion criteria required the presence of significant and persistent depressive symptoms, defined as a Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) total score ≥ 14, despite at least 4 weeks of SSRI monotherapy at the maximum recommended or tolerated dose.

Anxious depression was defined by a HAM-D anxiety/somatization factor score ≥ 7, a criterion reflecting high levels of anxiety symptoms. The anxiety/somatization factor comprised subscales assessing psychological anxiety, somatic anxiety, gastrointestinal somatic symptoms, hypochondriasis, and insight. Importantly, although this study used the HAM-D anxiety/somatization factor score to define anxious depression, this definition is distinct from the DSM-5 “with anxious distress” specifier for MDD, which uses a broader clinician‑rated set of criteria. This method enabled a focused assessment of anxiety features within the context of depression and aligns with previous studies using factor‑based approaches. Exclusion criteria included a primary diagnosis of an anxiety disorder (e.g., generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder, panic disorder, or post-traumatic stress disorder); a current or past comorbid diagnosis of schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, schizophreniform disorder, delusional disorder, psychotic disorder not otherwise specified, mood-congruent or mood-incongruent psychotic features, bipolar disorder, alcohol or substance use disorder, organic mental disorders including dementia, eating disorders, or obsessive-compulsive disorder; the presence of a seizure disorder; comorbid serious medical illnesses, including hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism; prior treatment with electroconvulsive therapy; high suicide risk; or current pregnancy or lactation. Furthermore, individuals with TRD, defined as failure to respond to at least two prior antidepressant trials during the current or a past depressive episode, were also excluded.

Treatment Protocol

A total of 48 participants who met all inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were randomly assigned to either the aripiprazole adjunctive therapy group (hereafter referred to as the aripiprazole group) or the bupropion adjunctive therapy group (hereafter referred to as the bupropion group) for a 6-week prospective study. Drug dosages and administration schedules were determined according to the recommended guidelines in each product’s prescribing information and the clinical judgment of the participating physicians (aripiprazole, 2.5−10 mg/day; bupropion, 150−300 mg/day). Throughout the study period, the type and dosage of each participant’s existing SSRI were maintained without change. Medications for the management of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS; benztropine and procyclidine), as well as benzodiazepines and hypnotics, were permitted. The use of other psychiatric medications, including antipsychotics, antidepressants, mood stabilizers, opioid analgesics, and psychostimulants, was not permitted.

Efficacy and Safety Assessments

Participants were evaluated at baseline and at weeks 1, 2, 4, and 6, with week 6 designated as the study endpoint. Primary efficacy measures comprised changes in the total HAM-D score and the anxiety/somatization factor score. Secondary efficacy measures included changes in the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Clinical Global Impression–Severity (CGI-S), and Inventory of Fatigue Symptoms (IFS) scores. Treatment response was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction from baseline in HAM-D scores, while remission was defined as a HAM-D score ≤ 7 or a MADRS score ≤ 10 at the end of treatment. Safety and tolerability were assessed by monitoring adverse events using the Drug-Induced Extrapyramidal Symptoms Scale (DIEPSS) and the Psychotropic-Related Sexual Dysfunction Questionnaire (PRSexDQ), as well as dropout rates due to intolerability or adverse events. Clinicians were aware of treatment assignment since this study was not a double-blind design.

Statistical Analysis

Participants were divided into the aripiprazole and bupropion groups. Frequency analyses and descriptive statistics were used to examine the demographic characteristics of the study participants. Efficacy outcomes were analyzed using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) to estimate least-squares (LS) means, accounting for missing data and baseline characteristics. Changes in HAM-D total scores, HAM-D anxiety/somatization factor scores, MADRS scores, CGI-S scores, and IFS total scores over time were compared between groups using LS means and independent-samples ttests. Response and remission rates were compared using chi-square tests. Safety analyses were conducted using descriptive statistics and appropriate comparative tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 21.0 for Windows (IBM Corp.).

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Detailed explanations of the nature and procedures of the study were provided to all participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients after a complete explanation of the study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yeungnam University of Hospital (IRB No. 2025-03-052) at each study site.

RESULTS

Demographic and Patient Characteristics

Of the 48 participants, 28 were randomized to receive aripiprazole and 20 were randomized to receive bupropion. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups. The mean baseline HAM-D total score was 22.07 ± 5.42 in the aripiprazole group and 23.60 ± 5.57 in the bupropion group, indicating moderate to severe depressive symptoms in both groups. The mean HAM-D anxiety/somatization factor score was 8.68 ± 1.28 in the aripiprazole group and 8.50 ± 1.57 in the bupropion group, suggesting high levels of anxiety among participants. No significant intergroup differences were observed in demographic characteristics, physical or psychiatric comorbidities, concomitant psychiatric medications, or mean baseline 17-items Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D17), MADRS, CGI-S, or IFS scores (Table 1). There were no significant between-group differences in the use of concomitant medications during the study period. No medications for EPS were administered.

Efficacy Outcomes

Overall improvement in depressive and anxiety symptoms

Both aripiprazole and bupropion groups showed significant within-group improvements in HAM-D and MADRS scores from baseline to week 6 (both p < 0.001). No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in MMRM analyses of LS mean changes in HAM-D total scores (−12.25 vs. −12.25, p > 0.999), HAM-D anxiety/somatization factor scores (−4.71 vs. −3.70, p = 0.177), or MADRS total scores (−15.18 vs. −12.90, p = 0.370) (Table 2).

Response rate and remission rate

Based on MMRM-estimated HAM-D17 scores, the response rate at week 6 was 75.0% in the aripiprazole group and 65.0% in the bupropion group, while the remission rates were 57.1% and 35.0%, respectively (Table 4). There were no significant differences in response or remission rates (Figs. 2, 3). A post-hoc power analysis for the difference in remission rates (57.1% vs. 35.0%, two-sided α = 0.05) showed that the achieved power was 33.4%, indicating that the study was substantially underpowered to detect the observed between-group difference.

Changes in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale Subitems

In HAM-D subscale analysis, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two treatment groups for most items including suicidal ideation, psychological and somatic anxiety, guilt, and insomnia. However, for the general somatic symptoms item (fatigue, heaviness in the limbs, and pain), the aripiprazole group showed significantly greater symptom improvement than the bupropion group (LS mean change: 0.54 vs. 0.15, p = 0.049). In contrast, for the genital symptoms item (decreased libido), the bupropion group demonstrated significantly greater improvement than the aripiprazole group (LS mean change: 0.40 vs. 0.00, p = 0.020) (Table 5).

Safety and Tolerability

Among the 48 participants included in this study, none of the participants dropped out due to adverse events. No significant between-group differences in DIEPSS scores were observed over time. On study completion, the mean changes in the PRSexDQ scores were −1.21 in the aripiprazole group and −1.45 in the bupropion group with no statistically significant differences between the groups. Furthermore, no significant intergroup differences were observed in mean PRSexDQ scores. However, adverse event profiles differed between the two groups: neurological symptoms were significantly more common in the aripiprazole group, whereas autonomic symptoms predominated in the bupropion group (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this study is the first prospective, randomized, controlled trial comparing augmentation therapy with aripiprazole versus bupropion in patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms who did not show a sufficient response to SSRI monotherapy. In this study, aripiprazole and bupropion emerged as effective and well-tolerated augmentation therapies for patients with anxious depression who were resistant to SSRIs. Overall, both treatments significantly improved depression- and anxiety-related somatic symptoms without causing serious adverse events. At treatment completion, 57.1% of the aripiprazole group and 35.0% of the bupropion group achieved remission based on the mean change in total HAM-D scores. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.130). The small sample size (n = 48) substantially limited statistical power. Post-hoc power analysis indicated that the achieved power was 33.4%, suggesting a considerable risk of type II error. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be taken as evidence of no difference between groups. Taken together, these results suggest comparable short-term efficacy between the two augmentation strategies in this sample rather than a clear therapeutic superiority of one treatment over the other.

Similar response and remission rates observed for aripiprazole and bupropion in this study are consistent with the findings of previous studies involving a broad range of patients with MDD [26,27]. A previous study that compared adjunct antidepressant therapy with aripiprazole and bupropion in depression showed that aripiprazole had adjunct efficacy which was equivalent to that of bupropion [28]. This is similar to the results of a recent nationwide population-based cohort study conducted in Korea which compared the efficacy and tolerability of aripiprazole and bupropion in TRD, and showed that bupropion augmentation therapy in TRD demonstrated therapeutic superiority with a lower risk of depression-related hospitalization than aripiprazole [29].

However, the results of this study are in contrast with those reported in some systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which have suggested that aripiprazole adjunct therapy is associated with significantly higher response rates and potentially higher remission rates than bupropion adjunct therapy in general TRD or MDD patient populations [6,19,26,30]. In this study, although there were no statistically significant differences in the remission rates between the two drugs, a numerical difference of approximately 22% was observed. This can be interpreted as follows: first, although the overall degree of improvement in depressive symptoms was similar, differences in the improvement of certain symptom clusters could have resulted from the distinct mechanisms of action of the two drugs; second, the relatively small sample size could have limited the ability to detect statistically significant changes. Additionally, it is possible that the specific neurobiological profile of patients with anxiety-prone depression who exhibit treatment resistance, differs from that of patients with typical TRD. In other words, bupropion could be a more effective strategy for treating anxious depression than for the general TRD patient population. Although it is possible that the positive effects of bupropion on anhedonia and energy levels could be due to the management of anxiety symptoms, further studies are required to elucidate the exact mechanisms.

Aripiprazole showed significantly greater improvement than bupropion on the HAM-D subscale of general somatic symptoms (fatigue, heaviness in limbs, and pain), whereas bupropion showed significantly greater improvement on the genital symptoms (decreased libido). Both aripiprazole and bupropion may influence somatic symptom domains through modulation of dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems. Previous studies have suggested that low-dose aripiprazole augmentation may improve somatic symptoms in patients with MDD [31], while bupropion has been associated with improvements in perceived energy levels, possibly related to its pharmacodynamic profile as a norepinephrine–dopamine reuptake inhibitor [32]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as only a limited number of subscale differences were observed and the analyses were exploratory in nature. Therefore, the results may reflect domain-specific variations rather than a consistent or generalized treatment effect.

No serious adverse events or treatment discontinuation due to tolerability was observed, and there were no statistically significant between-group differences in the overall frequency of psychotic or other adverse events across the predefined categories. However, overall neurological adverse effects including dystonia (10.7%), tremor (17.9%), akathisia (14.3%), and rigidity (7.1%), were reported to be significantly more frequent in the aripiprazole group (p = 0.035), whereas autonomic nervous system–related adverse events such as nausea/vomiting, reduced salivation (10%), constipation (10%), and palpitations (5%), were more frequently reported in the bupropion group (p = 0.014). These findings could be related to the pharmacological properties of aripiprazole as a partial dopamine D2 receptor agonist, and bupropion as a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor with sympathomimetic effects. The DIEPSS assessment of EPS showed no statistically significant differences between groups. Discrepancy between adverse event reports and DIEPSS findings may be attributable to differences in assessment scope and methodology. Specifically, adverse event reporting captured a broader range of neurological symptoms including headaches and syncope, which were not evaluated by the DIEPSS and in part relied on patient-reported subjective symptoms, whereas the DIEPSS was based on structured observer-rated assessments. Therefore, subjective symptoms without clear external manifestations may not have been reflected in the DIEPSS scores. Taken together, these findings indicated distinct adverse event profiles between treatments and may warrant consideration when interpreting tolerability outcomes.

It is also noteworthy that PRSexDQ scores showed similar ratios. SSRIs are known to cause sexual side effects, whereas bupropion has a lower tendency for this and is often preferred due to its improvement effect on the ‘Genital Symptoms-Decreased Libido’ subscale of the HAM-D scale. The results of this study also showed that aripiprazole augmentation therapy did not significantly worsen sexual function compared with bupropion.

Despite differences in their primary mechanisms of action, the comparable clinical efficacy observed in this study suggests that modulation of dopaminergic and noradrenergic pathways may play a role in treatment response among patients with SSRI-resistant anxious depression. Aripiprazole, through partial agonism at D2/D3 and 5-HT1A receptors and antagonism at 5-HT2A receptors, is known to exert neuromodulatory effects across multiple neurotransmitter systems, including dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine, which may be relevant to anxiety-related symptom dimensions [33,34]. In contrast, bupropion selectively inhibits the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine, thereby directly enhancing these systems, which have been implicated in symptoms frequently observed in depressive states, such as anhedonia, psychomotor slowing, and fatigue. These findings may suggest that serotonin alone is insufficient for these complex subtypes and demonstrate the need for personalized treatment strategies based on specific symptoms rather than an overall diagnosis.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a post hoc subgroup analysis from a larger randomized trial; therefore, the findings should be considered exploratory and may be associated with an increased risk of type I error. Second, the small sample size (n = 48) limited statistical power. Third, the involvement of multiple centers may have introduced bias into the assessment and treatment outcomes. Fourth, the cohort consisted of homogeneous nonresponders defined by a single optimal SSRI course, excluding patients with a high degree of treatment failure. Therefore, the study results are difficult to apply to patients with treatment-resistant anxiety and depression who do not respond to multiple medications. Fifth, the concomitant use of sedative and anxiolytic medications may have influenced anxiety, sleep, or symptom assessment outcomes. Sixth, the study period was short (6 weeks), making it impossible to apply the findings to long-term treatment.

Overall, the results of this study suggest that aripiprazole and bupropion showed similar short-term efficacy and tolerability as augmentation options for SSRI-resistant anxious depression. Both strategies significantly improved depressive and anxiety symptoms with similar efficacy. Regarding tolerability, aripiprazole caused significantly more neurological side effects than bupropion, whereas bupropion caused significantly more autonomic nervous system side effects. Caution is warranted when using these methods in patients with related vulnerabilities. Aripiprazole and bupropion demonstrated similar tolerability in terms of sexual dysfunction.

These findings are encouraging. However, considering their limitations, a larger-scale, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial with a longer follow-up period is required to confirm the current findings. Nevertheless, this study provides important clinical information for selecting adjunctive therapies in patients with anxious depression who are non-responsive to SSRI treatment.
