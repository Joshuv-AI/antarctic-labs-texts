Advanced management of electrical storm: beyond antiarrhythmics
Authors: Roopesh Sai Jakulla, Yazan Almohtasib, Raul Angel Garcia, Sanjaya Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe acute management of patients presenting with electrical storm secondary to ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) can be quite challenging, even with traditional attempts at rhythm control. Novel approaches are necessary to incorporate contemporary diagnostic investigations and therapeutic interventions to improve outcomes. Endomyocardial biopsy is an important but underutilised diagnostic tool that can rapidly guide the selection of tailored interventions, such as cardiac transplantation and other non-pharmacological interventions, to maximise survival. In our case, a previously healthy woman in her early 50s was hospitalised for symptomatic, pleomorphic ventricular tachycardia. Early rhythm control was achieved, but VAs recurred, consistent with electrical storm. Cardiac MRI demonstrated biventricular patchy fibrosis and late gadolinium enhancement. Endomyocardial biopsy confirmed giant cell myocarditis. She continued to deteriorate, developing cardiogenic shock requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation followed by urgent cardiac transplantation, eventually making a full recovery. We propose a contemporary algorithm for the management of electrical storm to maximise survival.Keywords: Arrhythmias, Heart failure, Pacing and electrophysiology, Cardiovascular system

Background

Electrical storm is a life-threatening condition caused by recurrent ventricular arrhythmias (VAs), either ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF). This condition portends a grim prognosis, and acute management requires emergent pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions.1 Commonly, rhythm control may be the sole focus when managing electrical storm, especially at institutions with limited resources. Beyond rhythm management, the use of certain proven diagnostic studies and interventions that are known to improve outcomes is key to maximise survival. To highlight the utility of a contemporary algorithm for advanced management of patients with refractory VAs, we present a case involving a previously healthy patient who presented with acute onset of electrical storm and cardiogenic shock and required several urgent investigations and interventions to achieve electrical and haemodynamic stability and an overall favourable outcome.

Case presentation

A woman in her early 50s with no significant medical history presented to the emergency room with a 5-day history of progressive dyspnoea and palpitations. She also reported an isolated syncopal episode 2 days prior to the presentation. She worked in a bank. There was no history of ethanol, tobacco or recreational drug use. Family history was unremarkable. She was afebrile with a systolic blood pressure of 70 mm Hg, a heart rate of 186 bpm, a respiratory rate of 34 per minute and an oxygen saturation level of 94% in ambient air. Physical examination revealed normal heart sounds without a murmur, clear lungs, no lower extremity oedema and skin that was warm to touch.

Investigations

The ECG revealed a wide complex tachycardia with intermittent, non-sustained variations in QRS complexes, consistent with pleomorphic VT (figure 1).

The patient’s initial lab work revealed a normal haemoglobin level, an elevated white blood count at 12.9 TH/μL (4–11 TH/μL) and a normal platelet count. Subsequent lab studies revealed sodium (140 mEq/L), potassium (3.9 mEq/L), magnesium (1.9 mEq/L), creatinine (0.84 mg/dL), B-natriuretic peptide (1587 pg/mL) and undetectable high-sensitivity troponins (<3 pg/mL) on four separate occasions from presentation until 18 hours after admission. Thyroid stimulating hormone level was 2.39 IU/mL (0.55–4.78 IU/mL), and the C reactive protein level was elevated at 151 mg/L (0–19 mg/L), but other lab work was unremarkable. An urgent coronary angiogram revealed angiographically normal coronaries without obstruction. Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated severe global reduction in left ventricular (LV) function, with an ejection fraction (EF) of 24%, normal LV thickness and dimensions, normal right ventricular (RV) function and size, and moderate eccentric mitral regurgitation. Cardiac MR showed patchy fibrosis and late gadolinium enhancement in both ventricles (figure 2). Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) showed diffuse inflammatory cells with multinucleated giant cells and myocyte necrosis (figure 3), consistent with giant cell myocarditis (GCM).

Differential diagnosis

Our patient presented with cardiogenic shock and electrical storm. So, initially, acute myocardial infarction was suspected as it is the most common cause of VAs, and the patient was taken for emergent coronary angiography. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection was also considered prior to coronary angiography. Due to the absence of ischaemia, conditions such as acute fulminant myocarditis and stress-induced (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy and other non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy aetiologies were considered. Brugada syndrome was one of the differentials as the ECG post cardioversion demonstrated coved ST elevations, but these resolved on repeat ECGs.

Treatment

The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit for further evaluation and management. She underwent synchronised cardioversion at admission for VT, and sinus rhythm was restored briefly. She went into VT again, for which an amiodarone intravenous bolus was given, followed by an infusion. When she had recurrent VT despite the amiodarone infusion, intravenous procainamide was administered.

After receiving procainamide, she returned to sinus rhythm (figure 4) and amiodarone was discontinued thereafter. The electrical storm continued to progress despite the use of high-dose glucocorticoids, sedation and mechanical ventilation, and the addition of lidocaine to procainamide. Subsequent acute decompensated heart failure and cardiogenic shock ensued. Given the patient’s fulminant GCM and clinical course, cardiothoracic surgery was consulted, with continued discussions with a multidisciplinary team. A pulmonary artery catheter and an intra-aortic balloon pump were placed. Due to this patient’s progressive clinical deterioration, her mechanical circulatory support was exchanged for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). After stabilisation and expedited review, she was listed for transplant and underwent urgent orthotopic heart transplantation on day 7 of hospitalisation.

Post cardioversion, the ECG demonstrates coved ST segment elevations in early precordial leads, raising the possibility of myocardial infarction. Brugada syndrome was also considered as one of the differential diagnoses. However, the ST-T changes resolved on a repeat ECG. QTc measures about 470 ms.

Outcome and follow-up

Our patient’s post-transplant recovery was complicated by pulmonary thromboembolism with RV and renal failure requiring temporary support via percutaneous RV assist device and renal replacement therapy. However, her RV and renal function recovered, and she was discharged and has continued to do well 2 years post-transplant. The ECG prior to discharge demonstrated sinus rhythm (figure 5).

The patient’s last ECG prior to discharge shows normal sinus rhythm and non-specific ST-T changes (after orthotopic heart transplantation).

Discussion

This case highlights several complexities which might occur while managing advanced cases of electrical storm. This otherwise healthy patient presented with acute fulminant GCM, heralded by haemodynamically unstable VAs and cardiogenic shock requiring orthotopic heart transplantation. The biventricular involvement of our patient’s GCM helps explain the refractory VT encountered and its pleomorphic characteristics. The mechanism of VT in our patient was predominantly from triggered activity given the pleomorphic nature of the VT as a result of myocarditis. Despite the expected grim prognosis—given the culmination of persistent electrical storm and cardiogenic shock—early EMB confirmed a diagnosis of GCM, which then guided a favourable outcome with heart transplantation.

Outcomes on electrical storm and LV dysfunction

Electrical storm is defined as ≥3 episodes of sustained VT or VF, or ≥3 appropriate shocks from an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) within 24 hours, and has been reported to yield mortality rates as high as 38%.2,4 The morphological variations of VT include monomorphic, polymorphic or pleomorphic VT. While monomorphic VT has a single QRS complex morphology, polymorphic VT continuously changes QRS complexes from beat to beat. Contrary to this, however, pleomorphic VT is represented by more than one distinct QRS complex during the same episode but without continuous changes in the QRS complex morphology.5 Interestingly, real-world data have shown that about 54% of electrical storms are attributable to the pleomorphic morphology, independently associated with increased mortality.6 7 The presence of LV systolic dysfunction is highly prevalent among those with electrical storm, and this portends poorer outcomes. One study found that in over 200 patients with electrical storms, >90% had reduced LVEF and approximately 67% had severe LV dysfunction (EF<35%).8 In another study, the mortality rate among patients with refractory VAs and heart failure was reported to be 34%.9 This relationship between electrical storm and LV dysfunction highlights the fact that without the appropriate interventions, mortality is high among such patients. As such, in those with electrical storm and LV dysfunction, definitive diagnostics are absolutely imperative to guide advanced care and to maximise survival.

The prevalence of myocarditis in patients who present with electrical storm is poorly understood. Unfortunately, EMB—which holds a Class I recommendation in patients with unexplained VAs and new heart failure—is only performed in less than 4% of cases with suspected myocarditis.10,13 This underscores the importance of performing EMB early on in the management of patients with electrical storm and LV dysfunction, as the results may direct acute management and impact survival outcomes. To speak to the yield of EMB in providing diagnostic clarity, one study demonstrated that in patients with acute heart failure requiring ECMO, the risk of performing an EMB was offset by a confirmed histopathological diagnosis in 78% of patients, for which the majority revealed myocarditis.14 In another study that used EMB and cardiac MRI, a definitive diagnosis for unexplained cardiomyopathy was provided when cardiac MR showed inconclusive results, and EMB was helpful in detecting other rare conditions.15 As such, the more consistent performance of EMB in appropriate conditions has the potential to improve survival outcomes in advanced cases of electrical storm and LV dysfunction.

A systematic approach to the advanced management of electrical storm

Given the outcomes associated with refractory VAs, we propose a contemporary algorithm for simultaneous diagnostic and therapeutic approaches towards the advanced management of those with electrical storm (figure 6: central illustration). The initial approach to electrical storm focuses on haemodynamic stability, providing immediate advanced cardiac life support (ACLS).1 16 17 Early transfer to the appropriate, medically capable facility, or urgent cardiology and/or electrophysiology consultation, is also key.

Initial pharmacological management typically includes intravenous amiodarone as a first-line therapy. It is a Vaughan-Williams class III antiarrhythmic that also holds classes I, II and IV antiarrhythmic properties, making amiodarone a great first choice towards acute rhythm control.118,20 Since ischaemia is the most common precipitating cause of electrical storms, urgent coronary angiography should be performed, with revascularisation if necessary. Aggressive and rapid correction of underlying metabolic or endocrinological abnormalities is also important.

If electrical storms should continue during this period, sedation with mechanical ventilation, along with consideration of beta blockers and/or ICD optimisation, should be pursued.21,23 Furthermore, the addition of a second intravenous antiarrhythmic like lidocaine or procainamide can precede or follow such interventions. Lidocaine is a class IB antiarrhythmic that is recommended in the setting of VF or polymorphic VT cardiac arrests that are unresponsive to cardiopulmonary resuscitation, defibrillation and vasopressor therapy.1 Procainamide, on the other hand, is not included in the ACLS algorithm for VA-related cardiac arrests, but it is recommended to manage wide-complex tachycardia with a pulse.18 Commonly reported combinations of intravenous antiarrhythmics include class III (ie, amiodarone) with class IB (ie, lidocaine), class IA (ie, procainamide) with class IB or sotalol with either class IA or IB.24 However, knowledge of patient-limiting factors, adverse effects and drug interactions is necessary for the appropriate and safe selection of antiarrhythmics and patients to be monitored closely for any toxicity.1 17 25 26

If there is no evidence of ischaemia on ECG with the absence of troponin elevation, the workup for structural heart disease begins with an echocardiogram. If an echocardiogram reveals a structural abnormality, then additional diagnostic investigations such as cardiac MRI and EMB are to be considered next. Should MRI findings suggest the presence of an infiltrative cardiomyopathy, nuclear imaging modalities may be necessary to rule out conditions such as cardiac sarcoidosis or amyloidosis and to direct more tailored therapies. For patients with severe acute LV dysfunction along with electrical storm, it is important to consider pulmonary artery catheters in cases of cardiogenic shock and to treat with diuretics, appropriately selected inotropes and/or mechanical circulatory support.27 28 Considerations towards advanced therapies, such as orthotopic heart transplantation or durable LV assist device, should occur early in the management of patients with electrical storm and severe LV dysfunction to potentially offer higher chances of survival in those who are candidates.29

In the absence of any structural abnormalities, channelopathies are to be considered and ECGs and telemetry are to be reviewed carefully for long QT, Wolff-Parkinson-White or Brugada syndrome. For torsades de pointes secondary to long QT syndrome, administration of magnesium intravenously as well as tachypacing to acutely shorten the QT interval is the first step.1 17 For pre-excited atrial fibrillation in Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, administration of procainamide intravenously, and/or emergent cardioversion, is recommended.30 31 Finally, for electrical storm secondary to Brugada syndrome, it is important to note that conventional therapy with amiodarone, procainamide, lidocaine and beta blockers may exacerbate the refractory VA, and acute management is best achieved with isoproterenol initially followed by maintenance therapy with quinidine once electrical stability is achieved.1 17

Non-pharmacological and novel therapies for electrical storm

Beyond initial pharmacological interventions, managing sustained VAs requires a comprehensive approach targeting the underlying arrhythmogenic substrate. In review of the more contemporary methods used to manage advanced cases of electrical storm, there are several therapies that are targeted towards eradicating the anatomical nidus for the refractory VA. However, such therapies require haemodynamic stability prior to any of the following interventions.

Ventricular ablation is a catheter-based procedure to specifically isolate or eliminate the arrhythmogenic tissue responsible for the electrical storm.22 32 However, success rates vary significantly, ranging from 50% to 90%, depending on the heart structure and the complexity of the arrhythmia.5 33 The stellate ganglion block involves injecting local anaesthetic around the ganglion, typically at the C6/C7 level of the cervical spine to reduce sympathetic nervous system activation. Clinical evidence from multiple studies has shown that the stellate ganglion block significantly reduces arrhythmias in the first 24 hours following treatment, with minimal complications reported.34,36 Finally, cardiac sympathetic denervation is a surgical procedure that disrupts the sympathetic nerve fibres supplying the heart for which research demonstrated that 60% of patients were free of VA at 1 year, with 55% maintaining this benefit for 4 years.37,39 Complications of each intervention are specific to clinical expertise, the methods used to intervene and the anatomic structures involved. As such, careful consideration must be taken to determine the best intervention for each patient.

Conclusions

Electrical storm secondary to VAs portends a poor prognosis for patients, and survival outcomes worsen with concomitant cardiomyopathy. Through our case presentation and suggested algorithm, we highlight the importance of a simultaneous diagnostic and therapeutic approach involving the appropriate selection of definitive diagnostic strategies, such as coronary angiography, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI and EMB, to successfully guide timely and targeted care for those patients with advanced cases of electrical storm, in order to maximise survival.
