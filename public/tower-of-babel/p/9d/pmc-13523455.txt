Pulmonary artery aneurysms are exceedingly rare vascular abnormalities, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 14,000 autopsies and mortality exceeding 50% after rupture. Their low incidence and the absence of standardized management strategies make multimodality imaging essential for diagnosis, anatomical characterization, and individualized therapeutic decision making.1

A 49-year-old woman with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) presented with progressive dyspnea at rest and increasing oxygen requirements over 3 months. Imaging demonstrated a giant pulmonary trunk aneurysm with a maximal diameter of 79 mm (Figures 1A to 1F). Invasive pulmonary angiography revealed marked dilation of the pulmonary trunk (Figure 1A, Video 1) and findings consistent with chronic thromboembolic disease, including obstruction of the left lower lobar pulmonary artery involving the middle and anteromedial basal segments, along with moderate dilation of the right pulmonary artery (Figure 1B). Right heart catheterization confirmed severe pulmonary hypertension (PH), with a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 44 mm Hg. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated severe aneurysmal enlargement of the pulmonary trunk in axial, coronal, and sagittal reconstructions (Figures 1C to 1E, Video 2), while 3-dimensional reconstruction further defined aneurysm extent and its anatomical relationship with adjacent structures (Figure 1F). This case illustrates the exceptional rarity of a giant pulmonary trunk aneurysm occurring at the intersection of 3 high-risk conditions: CTEPH, SLE, and APS. The mechanisms contributing to aneurysm formation are likely multifactorial and involve the interaction between chronic hemodynamic stress and immune-mediated vascular injury. Chronic PH induces progressive medial degeneration with elastic fiber fragmentation, smooth muscle cell loss, and extracellular matrix remodeling. According to Laplace law, progressive vessel enlargement increases wall tension and perpetuates aneurysm expansion. Simultaneously, autoimmune-mediated vascular injury associated with SLE and APS may further compromise arterial wall integrity through chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and recurrent thromboembolic events.1,2

Integrated multimodality imaging enabled detailed anatomical and hemodynamic assessment of this rare complication. Angiography defined pulmonary vascular anatomy and thromboembolic burden (Figures 1A and 1B), whereas computed tomography and 3-dimensional reconstruction characterized aneurysm extent and its relationship with the surrounding structures (Figures 1C to 1F), enabling comprehensive assessment of the underlying pathophysiological substrate.

Importantly, CTEPH is a potentially treatable cause of PH. Current management prioritizes pulmonary thromboendarterectomy in patients with surgically accessible disease or balloon pulmonary angioplasty in those considered inoperable.2 Treatment of the underlying thromboembolic condition before aneurysm-directed management may reduce pulmonary artery pressure and vascular wall stress, potentially stabilizing aneurysm progression. Although definitive surgical guidelines for pulmonary artery aneurysms are lacking, surgery is generally considered in symptomatic patients or in those with high-risk features, including PH, progressive aneurysm enlargement (>5 mm over 6 months), or large aneurysm size, with proposed diameter thresholds ranging from >55 to >75 mm according to clinical context and associated risk factors.1,3

This case highlights the value of multimodality imaging in identifying rare vascular complications of CTEPH and underscores the complex interaction between chronic hemodynamic stress and immune-mediated vascular injury.
