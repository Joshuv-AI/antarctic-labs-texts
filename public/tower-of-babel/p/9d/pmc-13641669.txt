Quality Indicators of Endoscopists for Both Index and Surveillance Colonoscopy are Associated With Risk of Metachronous Colorectal Neoplasia
Authors: Aasma Shaukat, Jennifer Holub, Peter Liang, Mohammad Bilal, Seth Gross, Mark Pochapin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:An association between higher adenoma detection rate (ADR) at index screening colonoscopy and lower risk of metachronous advanced neoplasia (AN, defined as colorectal cancer [CRC] or advanced adenoma [AA]) has been reported. However, the relationship between ADR at both index and surveillance colonoscopy and subsequent AN is unknown. We examined the association between ADR and withdrawal time (WT) at index and surveillance colonoscopy and risk of metachronous AN at surveillance colonoscopy.METHODS:We used GIQuIC, a repository of colonoscopies across the United States. Each patient has a unique ID at a participating site. Endoscopist national provider identifiers are associated with each examination. We included patients with 2 colonoscopies at least 3 years apart (index and surveillance) between 2011 and 2022 and calculated the ADR and average WT for the endoscopist performing the index and surveillance colonoscopies, respectively. We built a multivariable logistic regression model with metachronous AN as the outcome and ADR and WT as independent variables, controlling for patient age, sex, and race.RESULTS:We included 768,274 patients and 3,425 endoscopists. Mean patient age was 61 years and 48% were men; 66% were White, and 3% were Hispanic. Indication for index colonoscopy were screening (43.4%), surveillance (39.0%), and diagnostic (17.6%). ADR quartiles were ≤29.7%, >29.7%–37.2%, >37.2%–45.0%, and >45%. WT quartiles were ≤7.1 minutes, >7.1–8.2 minutes, >8.2–9.7 minutes, and >9.7 minutes. AN detection was lowest when low ADR endoscopists performed both index and surveillance examinations (5.4%, Table 1) and high ADR index examinations were followed by low ADR surveillance examinations (4.0%). Compared with low ADR endoscopists for both index and surveillance examinations, advanced neoplasia detection was significantly higher when both examinations performed by a high ADR endoscopist (AA 7.4%; OR [odds ratio] for AN 1.10 [1.05–1.16]) or low ADR index examinations were followed by high ADR surveillance examinations (AA 13.3%; OR for AN 1.448 [1.37–1.51]). Compared with short WT endoscopists for both examinations (AA 7.2%; CRC 0.3%), AN detection was higher when both examinations were performed by a long WT endoscopist or short WT index examinations were followed by long WT surveillance examinations (AA 7.0% P = 0.53 and 9.9%, P < 0.001) but similar CRC detection of 0.2% and 0.2% (P 0.14). Other factors associated with finding of metachronous AN were older age (76 years and older vs 45–55 years OR 1.64; 95% CI 1.48, 1.82), male sex (Male vs female OR 1.15; 95% CI 1.10–1.19), White race compared with non-White (OR 1.10; 95% CI 1.06, 1.14), 7–10 years between examinations compared with 3–5 years between examinations (OR 1.24; 95% CI 1.11, 1.37), indication of surveillance vs screening for the index examination (OR 1.1.7; 95% CI 1.13, 1.22), AA or sessile serrated lesion finding on the index examination (OR 2.08; 95% CI 1.97, 21.9 and OR 1.23; 95% CI 1.16, 1.30 respectively).DISCUSSION:Our findings show endoscopist ADR and WT for both index and surveillance colonoscopy are associated with risk of metachronous neoplasia. Future studies on metachronous neoplasia should include both sets of quality indicators.

INTRODUCTION

Adenoma detection rate (ADR) is a valid measure of colonoscopy quality that is associated with subsequent neoplasia and postcolonoscopy CRC (PCCRC) (1,2). Current postpolypectomy guidelines use adenoma features (i.e. size, number, histology) to determine colonoscopy surveillance intervals (3) and assume the colonoscopy was high quality (complete to cecum, with adequate bowel prep and physician ADR ≥25%). However, it is clear that the quality of the colonoscopy may determine findings at surveillance colonoscopy, in addition to polyp and patient-related factors (4,5). Authors have reported that the ADR of the colonoscopist performing the index colonoscopy determines whether advanced neoplasia (AN, defined as CRC or advanced adenoma [AA]) is found at surveillance colonoscopy (5). Furthermore, in a Polish cohort (6), authors have reported an association of ADR <20% for the endoscopist performing the index colonoscopy and PCCRC. These studies report the ADR for the endoscopist performing index colonoscopy, but not surveillance colonoscopy. The effect of ADR of the endoscopist performing the index colonoscopy, and the ADR of the endoscopist performing the surveillance colonoscopy on metachronous neoplasia (finding of AN at the surveillance examination) has not been studied. Specifically, it is unknown whether the low ADR of the index colonoscopist, or the high performance of the colonoscopist performing the surveillance colonoscopy is responsible for detection rate of metachronous neoplasia. The relationship between ADR at both index and surveillance colonoscopy and subsequent AN has not been reported. In addition, the relationship between withdrawal time (WT) of index colonoscopy and subsequent AN has not been reported. The aim of our study was to examine the association between ADR and WT at index and surveillance colonoscopy and risk of metachronous AN at surveillance colonoscopy in a large national quality benchmarking database of colonoscopies.

METHODS

We used GIQuIC, a repository of colonoscopies across the United States. GIQuIC members are a mixture of academic and community practices, with the latter being dominant. Each practice is a GIQUIC reporting site, which can be a single endoscopy unit or multiple endoscopy units in the same practice. Each patient has a unique ID at a participating site. Endoscopist national provider identifiers are associated with each examination. We included patients with 2 colonoscopies at least 3 years apart (index and surveillance) between 2011 and 2022 in the database and calculated the ADR and average WT for the endoscopist performing the respective examinations. We did not include any examinations beyond the first (index) and second (surveillance), with the exception that if the 2nd examination had inadequate preparation or cecum was not reached, we included the third colonoscopy as the surveillance examination. Individuals with colonoscopies between the minimum 3 years were excluded The ADR was calculated based on screening colonoscopies performed by the endoscopist in the year before the index colonoscopy using definitions as per the 2015 American College of Gastroenterology/American Society of Gastrointestinal Disease guidelines (7). Patients with inflammatory bowel disease, hereditary gastrointestinal conditions and high risk family history of CRC were excluded. WT was the average time spent on withdrawal, in colonoscopies where no interventions such as biopsies or polypectomy were performed, in the year before the index colonoscopy. We restricted the analysis to patients where 2 different endoscopists performed the index and surveillance examination. We categorized the ADR and WT into quartiles for the analysis. We included colonoscopies with adequate preparation and photodocumentation of the cecum for both index and surveillance examinations. Examinations with missing pathology were excluded. AN was defined as any CRC, AA (≥10 mm OR HGD (high grade dysplasia) or villous component), Advanced SSL (sessile serrated lesion) (≥10 mm or SSL with dysplasia or traditional serrated adenoma). Adenoma was defined as 1–2 tubular adenoma, 3+ adenomas, or adenoma without further specificity. Supplementary Table 1 (http://links.lww.com/CTG/B500) provides the flowchart of cohort assembly.

Proportions were compared using the Pearsons χ2 test. Endoscopist ADR and average WT was categorized in quartiles to summarize the data distribution and lessen the impact of potential outliers. We built a multivariable logistic regression model with metachronous AN as the outcome and ADR and WT as independent variables, controlling for patient age, sex, race, interval since index, index colonoscopy type, and findings on index examination. Metachronous neoplasia refers to pathology found on the surveillance examination. We performed a time trend analysis to assess the effect of year of index colonoscopy on results. All analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

DISCUSSION

Our findings show endoscopist ADR and WT for both index and surveillance colonoscopy are associated with risk of metachronous neoplasia, including CRC. To our knowledge, ours is the first study evaluating the effect of endoscopist quality indicators of ADR and WT for both the index and surveillance examination. Others have reported the association of ADR of endoscopist performing the index examination, with AN at surveillance colonoscopy. Kim et al (5) reported a 3% decrease in risk of metachronous neoplasia with each 1% increase in ADR of the endoscopist performing the index examination. Sekiguchi et al recently reported higher odds of AN detection at the 2 year surveillance examination for low ADR endoscopist at index colonoscopy. (8). However, the question whether the quality of the endoscopist performing the surveillance colonoscopy matters has not been addressed. This has practical implications for surveillance guidelines. Current surveillance recommendations start with a caveat of high quality colonoscopy, i.e. endoscopist meeting and exceeding minimum ADR benchmarks (9). In a recent updated guidance by American College of Gastroenterology-American Society of Gastrointestinal Disease, the ADR benchmark is now ≥35% (10). To extend this work, we found that the ADR of the endoscopist performing surveillance examination also matters. This brings up an important question of whether surveillance intervals should be tailored to the ADR of the endoscopist from the index and surveillance examination. Although such efforts are far from practical, instead we should emphasize the importance of ADR as a quality indicator, for reducing risk of PCCRC but also optimizing detection of AN at surveillance. A variety of tools can help optimize ADR, including feedback and monitoring of colonoscopies, endoscopic devices, and the use of artificial intelligence software and other technologies (11–15). Whether low detection of AN at surveillance is associated with higher risk of future CRC is not known.

Our study has several limitations. Although GIQUIC has representation from thousands of endoscopist across the United States, the generalizability is not known. Effect of changes in technology, technique, or other temporal changes on colonoscopy quality over time is not known. We do not have complete information on previous colonoscopies on the included patients. We assume index colonoscopy as the clearing colonoscopy, and only evaluate a surveillance colonoscopy 3 or more years later. Our analysis is limited to patients that had 2 examinations in the same practice. The endoscopists performing the index and surveillance examinations, although different individuals, are from the same practice If the patient went to a different practice, we are unable to include them. Because only patients with both examinations performed within the same practice are included, there may be systematic differences between included and excluded patients. Finally, unmeasured confounders (e.g., comorbidity, lifestyle factors, referral patterns) could affect the associations.

Strengths of our study are the large national quality benchmarking database, with representation of community and academic endoscopists, highly curated and verified data, and the retrospective nature ensures endoscopist are not aware of the study hypothesis that could bias their performance.

In conclusion, we found that prevalence of AN at surveillance colonoscopy is highest when the endoscopist performing the index and surveillance examinations have ADR and WT in the highest quartiles. Surveillance guidelines may need future refinement to incorporate colonoscopist quality metrics in addition to polyp findings.
