Disinvesting in equity will lead to an inefficient and unstable world: an economic perspective on huge development assistance cuts
Authors: João Vasco Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current collapse in development assistance, including development assistance for health, is anchored in budgetary efficiency, geopolitical reorientation, or the need for national prioritization. However, it is producing one of the largest self-inflicted efficiency losses in modern global health policy by ignoring essential concepts of development economics and health. In contexts of high inequality and with the inherent market failures in these areas, equity is not the opposite of efficiency but rather a necessary condition for achieving it.

Why is cutting expensive?

Investing in health in low- and middle-income countries yields one of the highest returns on public policy investments. The Lancet Commission on Investment in Health estimated that every dollar invested in health in these countries yields a return of 9–20 times, accounting for economic growth, productivity, and the value of years of life gained [1].

In fact, in nutrition, for example, every dollar invested in addressing malnutrition yields a return of £23 in health and productivity outcomes. In addition, the health sector has a multiplier effect: each job in the health sector generates 3.4 additional indirect jobs in related industries in low-income countries [2].

Investing in health is also investing in human capital and social infrastructure.

What does it mean for “real” health and lives?

Between 2002 and 2023, the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria saved 70 million lives and more than £100 billion in healthcare costs by reducing the burden of HIV, tuberculosis (TB), and malaria. This is a clear example of the potential impact of cuts to development assistance. For example, cuts to the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) and to the US President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) are estimated to lead to 7–15 million new HIV infections and 4–28 million additional AIDS-related deaths in PEPFAR-supported countries.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), we are already seeing considerable disruptions to health services similar to the worst periods of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, including shortages of essential medicines, weakening of epidemiological surveillance, difficulties in responding to emergencies, impacts on humanitarian aid, and significant losses in the health workforce. Figure 1 summarises the scale of disruption across core health system functions reported by WHO country offices following suspensions and reductions in development assistance back in April 2025. More worrying is that, by disinvesting in essential sectors, we will face serious consequences and are already experiencing disruptions in 40% of countries’ critical information systems, including vital statistics [3]. Without measuring simple metrics of human life, such as births and deaths, planning becomes impossible, plunging us into a tsunami of misinformation and structural opacity in governance.

The funding shock and the foreign affairs repositioning

It is important to note that the funding shock to development assistance for least-developed countries is not exclusive to the USA; several other countries are also cutting such funding. However, it is more likely that a world increasingly threatened and torn by conflict will lead to a world that is more competitive and less solidary, a trend already evident in development assistance, including for health. The United States’ withdrawal from several United Nations and development-support agencies, as well as from the WHO, will bring about a world more aligned with this trend. The repositioning of other countries, such as Europe and the BRICS, will shape the world and health in the coming decades.

Initiatives such as the European Global Gateway point to greater conditionality and alignment with geopolitical interests. Although the strategic rationale is understandable, the risk is that investments in global public goods will be replaced by projects with immediate political returns, weakening global health resilience.

It is essential to provide support to the least developed countries, but the dynamics of international affairs are changing rapidly, with unforeseen consequences. Certainly, until recently, we lived in an unequal world, far from what we could achieve globally, but we are now moving further from that goal.

The new world: more unequal, more inefficient, and more unstable

Global health is a classic example of a huge source of externalities, including outbreak control and surveillance, antimicrobial resistance reduction, and vaccine coverage, with clear cross-country spillovers. Health hazards have no borders, as we have been recently reminded. In such a system, leaving populations behind is not only unfair but also inefficient and dangerous.

There is also a structural economic argument: marginal returns to health are highest where baseline coverage is lowest. Transferring resources to the most vulnerable groups tends to generate greater aggregate gains in health and social welfare [4]. This is not just moral redistribution, but rather social productivity optimisation.

Of course, low governance capacity or mis-targeting reminds us of the “leaky bucket.” However, in global health, leaving “others” behind will render it inefficient and unstable.

Below minimum coverage, systems don’t “work,” leading to surveillance gaps, untreated TB/HIV, vaccine coverage gaps, as well as outbreaks and resistance. Once you cross key thresholds, returns can be non-linear (meaning big system gains). In global health, “leaving no one behind” is often not the price of efficiency; it’s the condition for efficiency itself in systems with externalities, threshold dynamics but also for avoiding regional collapses, forced migration pressures, or instability to trade and security.

If we judged global welfare as we judge national welfare, the current reduction in development assistance for health would be difficult to justify on economic grounds. Health and education are great examples of how large inequalities can reduce growth.

Even if you strip ethics out, the economic argument stands. But it’s also coherent to add that health is a human right and that catastrophic deprivation is not an acceptable global norm. Leaving people behind amplifies epidemic risks, reduces crisis-response capacity, and increases long-run costs, primarily for risk management.

Of course, when the economic valuation of human life, whether in terms of years of life gained or quality of life, is removed from policy assessments, as the US EPA recently did in environmental assessments, outcomes and goals become explicitly opaque [5]. Even if we disregard social cohesion and a sustainable planet as ultimate goals, which seems increasingly obvious given the latest threats, economic development and well-being are also at risk if we fail to value human life. At the same time, we know that investment and disinvestment are based on values, and group values and global values are the sum of individual values.

In an interconnected world, disinvesting in global health does not create sustainable savings but rather vulnerability. Thus, the question is not whether high-income countries should financially support low-income countries in the health sector. The correct question is whether they can afford not to do so, or for how long? In global health, leaving no one behind is not charity; it is macroeconomic stabilization.
