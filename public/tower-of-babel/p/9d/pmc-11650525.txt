Costs, efficiency, and patient‐reported outcomes associated with suture‐mediated percutaneous closure for atrial fibrillation ablation: Secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Soroosh Kiani, Joel Eggebeen, Mounir Al‐Gibbawi, Paige Smith, Thomas Preiser, Suprateek Kundu, Ziduo Zheng, Neal K Bhatia, Anand D Shah, Stacy B Westerman, David B De Lurgio, Christine M Tompkins, Anshul M Patel, Mikhael F El‐Chami, Faisal M Merchant, Michael S Lloyd
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo evaluate the cost and efficiency of suture‐mediated percutaneous closure (SMC) compared to manual compression (MC) after atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation.SMC has been demonstrated to be efficacious in reducing hemostasis and bedrest times after AF ablation. To date, randomized data comparing the direct and indirect cost between the two techniques have not been described.MethodsWe conducted a 1:1 randomized trial comparing SMC to MC following AF ablation. The primary endpoints have been previously published. However, secondary endpoints pertinent to indirect cost including complication rates, hospital utilization (i.e., delays in discharge, additional patient encounters, nursing utilization), pain, patient reported outcomes, as well as the direct costs of care associated with AF ablation were collected. We also performed secondary analysis of the primary endpoint to evaluate for a learning curve, and subgroups analysis comparing efficacy across different numbers of access sites and compared to those in the MC group with a figure‐of‐eight suture (Fo8), that could potentially have impacted the relative efficiency of the procedure.ResultsA total of 107 patients were randomized and included: 53 in the SMC group and 54 in MC. A learning curve was observed in the SMC group between the first and second half of the study group (p = 0.037), with no such difference in the MC group. After accounting for the number of access sites, time to hemostasis remained shorter in the SMC Group (p = 0.002). Compared to those in the Fo8 arm (n = 37), the time to hemostasis remained shorter in the SMC group (p = 0.001). Among those planned for same‐day discharge, there were more delays in the MC group (31.5% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.0144). Rates of major and minor complications were similar between SMC and MC groups at discharge (p = 0.243) and 30 days (p = 1.00), as were nursing utilization, self‐reported pain, and overall patient reported outcomes. The overall cost of care related to the procedure was similar between the MC and SMC groups ($56 533.65 [$45 699.47, $66 987.64] vs. $57 050.44 [$47 251.40, $66 426.34], p = 0.601).ConclusionSMC has been shown to decrease time to hemostasis and ambulation and facilitate earlier same‐day discharge after AF ablation without an increase in direct or indirect costs.Keywords: ambulation, atrial fibrillation ablation, early discharge, hemostasis, perclose, percutaneous closure, proglide, prostyle, same day discharge, suture mediate closure, vascular closure

INTRODUCTION

Atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation is one of the most common procedures performed by cardiac electrophysiologists. 1 The periprocedural management of patients undergoing AF ablation pose a unique set of challenges. These include the need for multiple points of venous access with large diameter sheaths, balancing the competing risk of thrombosis and bleeding with systemic anticoagulation during the procedure (while typically requiring normalization of clotting times before removing sheaths), and the need for therapeutic oral anticoagulation initiated immediately after the procedure to mitigate ongoing stroke risk. 1 These factors culminate in longer times to hemostasis and bedrest before ambulation and raise concerns for postprocedure access related complications.

We performed a randomized clinical trial and previously reported the primary endpoints that, compared to the standard practice of manual compression (MC), suture‐mediated vascular closure (SMC) after AF ablation resulted in shorter times to hemostasis, facilitated early ambulation, and was associated with faster times to same‐day discharge. 2 As part of this study, we also collected pre‐specified secondary endpoints to evaluate the efficiency, as well as indirect and direct cost of SMC for AF ablation. Herein we report those findings in an effort to better understand the direct and indirect cost of SMC for AF ablation.

METHODS

Trial design and endpoint ascertainment

Our study design is described in detail elsewhere. 2 In brief, this was an investigator‐initiated, single center, prospective, randomized, post‐market efficacy trial comparing SMC to MC after AF ablation (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04180540). This trial was funded by Abbott Vascular. However, the study was developed, designed, implemented, and run independently and solely by the investigators. This included trial design, data collection, and data analysis. The authors of this study wrote this manuscript and endorse the fidelity of the trial protocol, accuracy, and completeness of the data, as well as reporting of adverse events to the safety monitoring board. This trial was approved by our institutional review board (IRB00115958).

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria are previously reported. 2 In brief, patients were eligible for participation if they were 18 years or older of age, ambulatory and were undergoing an ablation procedure for AF at Emory University Hospital, Emory University Hospital Midtown, and Emory Saint Joseph's Hospital. Exclusion criteria are detailed in the online Supporting Information.

Trial treatments

A detailed reporting of the trial treatment is included in the online data Supporting Information. In brief, the Perclose ProGlideTM (Abbott Vascular) system was already Food and Drug Administration‐approved for the indication of vascular closure of femoral arteries and veins at the start of trial enrollment. 3 Detailed descriptions of the use of this system have been previously reported elsewhere. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 All operators were experienced with and certified in SMC.

Randomization strategy

The randomization strategy is reported elsewhere 2 and detailed in the online data Supporting Information.

Trial endpoints

Post hoc subgroup analysis of the primary endpoint

The primary endpoint and key secondary endpoint are described and reported elsewhere. 2 The primary endpoint was time to hemostasis. 2 We stratified the primary endpoint by chronologically splitting the study into chronologic halves (based on individual operator case volume) to evaluate for temporal changes in efficacy and to describe the learning curve of the SMC procedure. We also performed a post hoc secondary analysis of the primary endpoint stratified by the number of access points/sheaths to determine if there may be different efficiencies in SMC with different access strategies. We performed a sub‐group analysis, including only those in the MC group who had a figure‐of‐eight suture (Fo8) suture placed to compare efficacy of SMC to Fo8 in this context.

Prospectively collected, prespecified secondary endpoints

We included and collected data on several secondary endpoints at discharge and routine 30‐day follow up. Details of these endpoints are available in the online Supporting Information (Supporting Information Methods). In summary, these included failure of initial ambulation at goal time, composites of all major and minor access site complications at discharge and follow up, as well as resultant additional unplanned clinic and hospital visits and associated diagnostic testing to evaluate those complications (see Supporting Information Methods), postprocedure length of stay, frequencies in delays of planned discharge, the number of nursing encounters required before discharge, opiate pain medication requirements, and patient reported outcomes as follows:

We also collected direct costs (reported in USD, $) associated with the procedure including overall cost of care, as well as cost related to hemostasis, groin site complications, and unplanned visits and diagnostic testing related to issues with the groin/access site through follow up time. Cost was determined using facility charges. Additional details on the calculation of costs are available on the online Supporting Information appendix (Supporting Information Methods).

Statistical analysis

The normality of distribution of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro−Wilk test and visual comparisons of histograms for each variable. Comparisons of continuous variables across groups was performed using Student's t‐test and Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests for normally and non‐normally distributed data, respectively. Comparison of categorical variables was performed using Chi‐squared and Fisher's Exact tests for binary categorical variables where appropriate, and Mann−Whitney U test for ranked ordinal level variables. A two‐way analysis of variance was used to compare outcomes across different categories of more than one factor (e.g., treatment group and number of access points). Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non‐normally distributed variables were presented as median (quartile 1, quartile 3), and categorical data were represented as percentage of the cohort (n), unless otherwise specified. Significance tests, where required, were conducted at 95% confidence level. For categorical variables, the estimated odds ratios between each group were tested using logistic regression. And for continuous variables, the model‐based least square mean differences and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were shown based on the linear regression model. If the continuous variable was not normally distributed, then it was log‐transformed, which is a well‐known transformation for non‐Gaussian variables. A description of our sample size estimation is included in the online Supporting Information appendix (Supporting Information Methods). All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (2020; SAS Institute, Inc.) and IBM SPSS ver. 27 (2020; IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, Version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). All patients remained in the group to which they were randomized, and all analysis was done using the intention‐to‐treat principle, unless otherwise specified.

Post hoc analysis of the primary endpoint (efficiency endpoints)

Learning curve

We have previously shown that SMC resulted in shorter times to hemostasis than MC in AF ablation. 2 When the study was split into a chronologic first and second half based on the number of cases performed by each operator (Figure 1), the difference in time to hemostasis between MC (entire cohort) and SMC groups was larger in the second half (10.8 [8.5, 14.6] min vs. 2.75 [0.6, 8.0] min, p < 0.001) than in the first half (10.2 [10.0, 14.03] min vs. 7.9 [3.35, 15.1] min, p = 0.092). This appears to have been driven by a decrease in time to hemostasis in the SMC group (p = 0.037) with relative stability in the MC group (p = 0.972).

We also compared time to hemostasis between the MC and SMC groups after stratifying groups by number of access sites. However, the difference in time to hemostasis was most pronounced among those with two points of access, and less so among those with four (Figure 2).

Prespecified secondary endpoints

Delays in ambulation and discharge

We performed a secondary randomization to compare early (1 h) and usual (2‐h) goal ambulation times (n = 40). 2 We prospectively evaluated the failure rate of initial ambulation (i.e., a delay > 30 min from goal ambulation time), as well as the reasons for delay (if any) in ambulation between groups. There was no difference in the proportion of patients that experienced a delay > 30 min from the planned ambulation time between the 1 and 2‐h goal ambulation time groups (31.6% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.9058), including those delayed for access related issues (10.5% vs. 9.5%, p = 1.00). Likewise, there was no significant difference in the distribution of any reason for delayed ambulation between groups (p = 0.1336). A detailed breakdown of the reasons for delay of ambulation are available in the online appendix (Supporting Information S1: Results Table 3). Protocoled initial ambulation time was earlier in the SMC group than MC. In this context, the time to successful ambulation (i.e., free of delays including for vascular issues) was significantly shorter in the SMC group compared to the MC group (2.2 h [1.3, 3.3] vs. 4.2 h [4.0, 4.7], p < 0.0001).

There was a significantly higher frequency in delays from planned time of discharge among the MC group (31.5% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.0144), though there was no significant difference in the distribution of reasons for those delays (p = 0.1313) including no difference in the frequency of access related complications (online Supporting Information appendix, Supporting Information S1: Results Table 4).

There were no pseudoaneurysms, retroperitoneal bleeds, hemorrhage, AV‐fistulas, venous thromboembolisms, nor access site infections in either group in the study. There were no significant differences in the rates of major hematomas between the MC and SMC groups (1.9% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.555) including at discharge and follow up (Table 3). Likewise, there was no statistically significant difference in the rates of rebleeding or oozing, nor minor hematomas between the MC and SMC groups (Table 3). The frequency of unplanned hospital encounters for a complication of rebleeding or hematoma were statistically similar between the MC and SMC groups (9.3% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.2663).

Nursing utilization and pain control

There was no significant difference in the number of additional nursing encounters (above planned and routine) during recovery between the MC and SMC group (0.09 ± 0.564 vs. 0.06 ± 0.231, p = 0.6414) nor the proportion of patients between groups that required at least one additional nursing encounter (5.6% vs. 4.0%, p = 1.00).

The proportion of patients that required opiate pain medication in the postprocedure area was similar between the MC and SMC groups (9.3% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.3599) as was the mean dosage in morphine milligram equivalents (p = 0.8583). Similarly, the rates of a prescription for opiates on discharge were similar between the MC and SMC groups (0.0% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.9592).

Patient satisfaction and self‐reported outcomes

The median patient‐reported visual analogue pain score (ranging from 0 to 10) at discharge was similar in the MC and SMC groups (2 [0, 3.5] vs. 1 [0, 2], p = 0.4248), as was satisfaction with the level of back pain (0 [0, 1] vs. 0 [0, 1], p = 0.5876) and discomfort associated with bedrest (0 [0, 1.25] vs. 0 [0, 2], p = 0.4288) as reported in the patient satisfaction questionnaire. There was higher overall patient satisfaction in the MC group (indicated by a lower score) based on the patient satisfaction survey administered at the time of discharge (0 [0, 4] vs. 3 [0.5, 8], p = 0.0098). This was driven primarily by satisfaction with the level of tenderness at the access site (Table 3). At the time of discharge, the proportion of patients that felt ready for discharge was statistically similar between the MC and SMC group (82% vs. 70%, p = 0.172) among those that responded to this question (101/107 patients). The results of each question from the survey are reported in Table 3. Follow up surveys were obtained from 95.1% (100/105) of the patients that completed follow up. The results of the patient satisfaction score from the survey at follow up (Table 3) were similar between the MC and SMC groups (0 [0, 3] vs. 0 [0, 5.5], p = 0.8194). The results of the short form inguinal pain questionnaire, administered at follow up, were similar between the MC and SMC groups both for the worst pain felt in the groin over the past 7 days (0 [0, 0] vs. 0 [0, 0], p = 0.2477) and the number of activities limited by groin pain (0 [0, 0] vs. 0 [0, 0], p = 1.000).

Direct cost of hemostasis and care

The cost related to hemostasis was higher in the SMC group ($4 [$4, $163] vs. $882 [$747, $1176], p < 0.0001) and are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Results Table 5. The primary driver of costs in the SMC group was the SMC devices themselves. The range of cost seen in the MC group was driven by the occasional use of pressure assisted device bandages (Supporting Information S1: Results table 5). After including the cost of additional unplanned encounters and diagnostic studies for issues related to vascular access (Supporting Information S1: Results Table 6), the total cost remained higher in the SMC group ($42 [$4, $163] vs. $882 [$814.5, $1176], p < 0.0001). However, the cost due to unplanned encounters and diagnostic studies related to vascular access was similar between the MC (n = 5) and SMC (n = 2) groups ($1326 [562.50, 4521.40] vs. 6826.59 [1182.0, 6826.59], p = 0.571), though only a small proportion of the cohort were included in this analysis (n = 7). The overall cost of care related to the procedure was similar between the MC and SMC groups ($56 533.65 [$45 699.47, $66 987.64] vs. $57 050.44 [$47 251.40, $66 426.34], p = 0.601).

DISCUSSION

This was a report of post hoc analyses of the primary endpoint, as well as prespecified secondary outcomes, of a randomized clinical trial that evaluated the efficacy of using SMC for multipoint venous access in AF ablation. 2 We observed a demonstrable learning curve which resulted in a shortening of time to hemostasis in the second half of the study. Despite the presence of the learning curve, the current study demonstrates persistent efficacy of SMC in comparison to MC irrespective of the presence of a Fo8 suture, the number of access points, without increasing the direct costs of care, nor observed metrics pertinent to indirect costs of care. We also observed less frequent delays from the expected time to discharge among those undergoing SMC. Other secondary endpoints including rates of complications, unplanned visits for complications, additional nursing utilization, pain, the need for pain medication both before and after discharge, and cost of care were similar between groups. Interestingly, patient satisfaction was lower at the time of discharge among the SMC group but were similar between groups at follow up (Table 4).

Shorter times to hemostasis and ambulation after vascular closure are now well established. 2 , 7 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 However, despite being performed by certified operators, we identified a demonstrable learning curve of the SMC technique. This may be due to numerous factors, including the number of steps necessary to deploy SMC devices, as well as suture management with up‐front placement (aka “pre‐close”), any of which may be compounded in the context of multiple access sites. After surmounting this curve, the time to hemostasis with SMC was typically less than 4 min. This would suggest that the full benefit of SMC might not be realized immediately after adoption and, even among experienced operators, likely requires sufficient experience to navigate nuances of closure of multiple points of access before achieving optimal efficiency. We also found that the difference in time to hemostasis was statistically not related to the number of access points, per se. However, the benefit was less pronounced among those with four access points (and without statistical significance, though among a small number of patients). We suspect this is because SMC is performed in serial (i.e., hemostasis is achieved at one site before pulling the next sheath), as opposed to in parallel (all sheaths typically pulled at once with compression being applied to all venotomy sites simultaneously), as is the case with MC. Among operators who use a larger number of access points, this may be a more salient consideration.

We previously showed that early ambulation (1 h) after successful SMC was feasible. 2 In the current study, we also found no significant differences in the frequency of delays from planned ambulation, including for vascular or groin complications. Despite this improved efficacy with SMC, there was higher initial patient satisfaction in the MC group, driven primarily by issues with tenderness in the groin site. This finding is disparate from other recent reports of patient reported outcomes after vascular closure in this context. 10 , 14 One possible explanation for this finding may have been how the question was presented or the context in which it was asked. Because this study was not blinded, it is possible that this finding was a result of changes in patient expectations before their procedure. It may also signal that SMC could result in additional groin discomfort, which has been suggested by prior studies 15 and warrants further investigation.

In addition to the above, further metrics of indirect cost, including frequency of major and minor complications, unplanned outpatient or emergency room visits, additional unplanned nursing utilization and pain mediation requirement, were not significantly different between groups. Perhaps not surprisingly, the direct cost associated with vascular closure per se was higher in the SMC group (driven by the cost of the SMC devices themselves). However, these did not impact the overall direct cost of care which was both qualitatively and statistically similar between groups (given the relatively small cost of the SMC devices in context of the entire procedure cost). These findings are corroborated by other nonrandomized studies. While indirect costs are difficult to measure, given similar direct costs of care, our findings suggest that decreasing time to hemostasis, ambulation and (same‐day) discharge 2 without incurring other indirect costs, suggest that SMC use may increase overall procedural efficiency, especially once operators have gained sufficient experience with the technique.

Our study has notable limitations. First, while our data was collected as part of a randomized controlled trial, these were post hoc subgroups analyses of the primary endpoint, and secondary endpoints, that this study was not powered to evaluate. Fortunately, complications after vascular closure and hemostasis are infrequent 16 and would require sample sizes too large for a study of this type. Likewise, utilization of narcotic pain medication was uncommon in our cohort, rendering it difficult to assess for true differences between groups. Second, the trial was not blinded. 2 However, the study team was blinded to the data during the study and no interim analyses were performed before conclusion of the study. Given the above, we viewed these endpoints as hypothesis generating with exploratory p‐values. However, the findings of this study more broadly are corroborated in the literature. 17 Third, we used facility charges to evaluate direct cost which has known inherent limitations. However, our goal was to determine the direct cost to the system and felt that hospital charges most directly reflected this. Using hospital charges and facility costs allowed us to draw conclusions on the cost related to SMC from the perspective of the hospital and health insurer. Fourth, we acknowledge that indirect costs are somewhat subjective and difficult to generalize. Nonetheless, we do feel that it is important for providers, healthcare systems, and patients to recognize these types of costs when evaluating the cost impact of treatment options and overall delivery of care. Lastly, our trial was done at a single center which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, our data were consistent with multiple other prospective and retrospective studies of vascular closure, including with SMC, in other contexts.

In conclusion, SMC has been shown to produce shorter times to hemostasis, facilitate early ambulation, 2 without a significant increase in measures of indirect and direct costs despite a demonstrable learning curve associated with the technique. Despite increased efficacy in this regard, there was lower patient satisfaction among those undergoing SMC at discharge, but not at follow up. Further, because complications after vascular closure and compression are infrequent, further studies to surveil for rare or subclinical complications are warranted.
