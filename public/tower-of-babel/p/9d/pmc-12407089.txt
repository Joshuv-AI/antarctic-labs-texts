Climate change increases the interannual variance of summer crop yields globally through changes in temperature and water supply
Authors: Jonathan Proctor, Lucas Vargas Zeppetello, Duo Chan, Peter Huybers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Year-to-year variance of agricultural productivity is an important determinant of food security. Previous global analyses described increased yield volatility from warming, but it has become increasingly clear that changes in water availability are also a key determinant of yields. Here, we provide the first global quantification of climate change impacts on maize, soybean, and sorghum yield variance due to changes in temperature and soil moisture. Pairing an empirical crop model with CMIP6 simulations indicates that changes in temperature and soil moisture increase interannual weather-induced yield variance by 7 to 19%°C across crops. This increase is driven roughly equally by previously quantified increased temperature stress variance, as well as by increased covariance between temperature and soil moisture stresses. Results using a simple land surface model are consistent with those using CMIP6 simulations in indicating that this increased covariance is driven by warmer air more quickly drying soils and by dry soils promoting greater warming.

INTRODUCTION

Agricultural productivity is a key determinant of food security, especially in developing countries. After decades of improvement, global food security has worsened in the past 5 years, with climate extremes such as drought or extreme heat playing an uncertain but potentially important role (1). It is relatively well understood that warming temperatures and changes in water supply from climate change have caused and are projected to increasingly lead to reductions and global redistribution of yields (2–7). Along with mean yields, interannual variance in crop yields is a key determinant of food security, especially in regions with limited storage or access to international markets (8). Yet how climate change will affect yield variance is substantially less well understood than how it will affect mean yields (9–12).

Annual agricultural productivity tends to be highest at intermediate temperature and moisture levels. Extreme heat and cold as well as drought and sodden conditions generally reduce yields (5, 13–15). Extreme heat can reduce net photosynthesis rates, shorten grain filling, and damage plant structures, especially those key for reproductive development (13, 16, 17). High temperatures can also increase water demand by increasing the vapor-pressure deficit (VPD), which controls evaporation and transpiration. Under high water demand and low water supply, i.e., low root-zone soil moisture, plants may close their stomata, thereby reducing photosynthesis rates and carbohydrate production (18). Low water supply can also encourage root growth at the cost of grain production (19), and in severe conditions induce cavitation, embolism, and crop mortality (20). Empirical response curves between crop yields, temperature, and soil moisture capture the cumulative effects of these mechanisms (fig. S1) (5, 21, 22).

The weather-induced interannual yield variance that we examine is composed of variance due to temperature stress, soil moisture stress, and their covariance. Climate change could increase interannual yield variance by shifting average temperature and soil moisture conditions to portions of the yield response curves that are more sensitive, by increasing the variance of temperature and soil moisture, and by strengthening their covariance. We detail these three mechanisms below and diagram them in Fig. 1.

Schematic overview: Changes in the distribution of growing season temperature and soil moisture increase summer crop interannual yield variance under climate change.

Projections indicate (Fig. 3) that, compared to the present (blue), climate change (red) will, on average, lead to warmer and more variable temperatures, insubstantial changes in soil moisture, and a strengthening of the covariance between temperature and soil moisture. This leads the joint distribution of temperature and soil moisture to shift warmer, to widen, and to stretch along the temperature-moisture diagonal (A). Hotter temperatures lead to more variable temperature effects on yields, as does the increased variance in temperature (B). Soil moisture effects on yield, in isolation, are essentially unchanged (C). Greater covariance between temperature and soil moisture, however, leads to greater covariance in their effects on yield (D). In turn, the distribution of combined temperature plus soil moisture effects on yield is widened and shifted toward greater damages (E).

First, changes in mean surface air temperature and root zone soil moisture could induce growing season conditions wherein crops are more sensitive. Previous work shows that warming increases interannual yield variance in US maize (9, 10) and global maize (11) by causing temperature variability to occur at hotter temperatures, where maize is more sensitive to changes in temperature. Such increases in yield variance could have substantial costs, including increases in premium rates and government outlays for crop insurance in the US (23). Welfare losses from increased yield variance may be even larger in agricultural systems where crop insurance markets are less developed.

A second mechanism by which climate change could increase interannual yield variance is by directly increasing the variance of growing season temperature and soil moisture, which increases the variance of their effects on yields. Prior work indicates that climate change is likely to increase summertime temperature variance (24) and that such changes can be largely explained by local land surface processes related to temperature, soil moisture, and VPD (25). Increased radiative forcing on the land surface driven by increased greenhouse gas concentrations drives increases in the magnitude of temperature anomalies associated with low rainfall events (25). In addition, higher ambient vapor pressure deficits may more quickly deplete soil moisture, leading to a positive feedback whereby the lower latent cooling associated with drier soils leads to higher temperatures, higher VPD, and even drier soils (26). Previous studies have shown that increases in temperature variance could contribute to increases in the variance of US maize yields (9, 10). Changes in the distribution of temperature, rainfall, and atmospheric CO2 concentrations have also been projected to alter the variance of wheat yields (27), although with the projected coefficient of variation predominantly decreasing across global cropped areas due to projected increases in mean yield (12).

A third mechanism through which climate change could influence yield variance is by changing the covariance of temperature and soil moisture. The same local VPD-induced positive feedbacks between surface drying and warming that lead to increased temperature variance, described above, also increase the coupling of hot and dry conditions. Prior work indicates that climate change will likely increase the coupling between hot and dry growing season conditions, thereby decreasing mean yields (7, 28). Because yield variance due to temperature and soil moisture stress can be mathematically decomposed into contributions from temperature effects, soil moisture effects, and their covariance, it follows that increased covariance of thermal and moisture stress can be expected to affect not only mean yields but also overall yield variance. Put another way, hot and dry conditions occurring together more frequently leads to stronger negative yield shocks and increased yield variance (Fig. 1, D and E).

The collective influences of these three factors on yield variance under climate change have yet to be globally analyzed for staple crops other than wheat (12, 27), and their specific contributions to changes in yield variance have yet to be quantified. While Tigchelaar et al. (11) examined influences of changes in mean temperature, they did not include effects from changes in temperature variance or the covariance between temperature and soil moisture. In addition, to our knowledge, the influence of strengthened temperature and moisture coupling under climate change on yield variance has not been previously quantified. The relative paucity of research on how climate change might influence yield variance limits our ability to project and understand future risks to food security and to inform agricultural adaptive measures for climate change.

Here, we pair recent advances in understanding of how the variance and covariance of temperature and soil moisture are likely to evolve under climate change (24, 25, 28–31) with a set of empirical yield models that account for the joint influence of daily growing season temperature and soil moisture (5) to explore how climate change influences yield variance of three staple crops globally. In contrast with prior work on global wheat variance (12, 27), we focus our analysis on the influence of changes in soil moisture, rather than precipitation, due to the ability of soil moisture to better predict historic yields (5, 6, 32) and because doing so enables us to quantify the effects of a strengthened coupling of temperature and soil moisture on yield variance. We focus on summertime crops, as that is the season in which projected changes in temperature and soil moisture variance under climate change have a relatively better understood set of physical mechanisms (24, 25, 29, 30), as well as to avoid uncertainty with respect to winter and spring planting seasons for crops like barley, oats and wheat. Specifically, we study maize, soybean, and sorghum. Maize production is the largest of any grain; soybean is a globally important source of protein and calories for livestock and humans; and sorghum is the fifth most produced grain globally and an important food crop in parts of Africa, Central America, and South Asia (33). This analysis provides the first global quantification of climate change impacts on maize, soybean, and sorghum yield variance due to changes in temperature and soil moisture and identifies increased covariance of temperature and water stresses as a substantial and previously unquantified driver of future increases in yield variance.

To quantify the impact of weather on yield variance, we use an approach that accounts for substantial differences in yields across time and space, allows for decomposition of yield variance into its constituent parts, and is easily interpreted. Specifically, for each location and year from 2015 to 2100, we predict logarithmic yield as a function of temperature and soil moisture. We then detrend these values within a given 21-year interval and compute their variance (Materials and Methods). Changes in variance between different 21-year intervals are reported as percentages. Modeling the influence of weather shocks on yields logarithmically is consistent with plant physiology models that posit exponential growth over the growing season (10, 21, 34) and ensures that mean differences do not affect calculations of weather-induced variance around the trend. Detrending values isolates the interannual variance that we are most interested in from decadal trends. Calculating yield variance, as opposed to SD or coefficient of variation as in prior work (11, 12, 27), allows for the decomposition of yield variance into its constituent parts. The variance of the sum of any two random variables—here, temperature, and soil moisture effects on yield—is the variance of each plus twice their covariance. Last, reporting variance changes as percentages allows for more readily interpretable quantities.

We quantify the influence of climate change on weather-induced yield variance (hereafter, “yield variance”) using 15 earth system models from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) (31) following SSP5-8.5 paired with empirical yield models. Following the empirical yield modeling literature (6, 10, 21, 22), yields are nonlinearly related to daily temperature and soil moisture exposure through models that are trained and tested against a global set of observations (5). We debias the CMIP6 data using temperature and soil moisture observations prior to analysis (35–38) (Materials and Methods).

To allow for quantification of yield variance changes under a range of warming scenarios, we report our primary results as variance changes per degree Celsius warming over global croplands, which is justified by the linearity of variance changes with warming (fig. S2). We analyze the spatial distribution of changes in yield variance under climate change, along with implications for the future frequency of negative yield shocks. Increases in yield variance are decomposed into components due to changes in growing season mean temperature, temperature variance, soil moisture mean and variance, and covariance between temperature and soil moisture. Projected yield variance changes between empirical models based on temperature and soil moisture, and temperature and precipitation are compared.

To better interpret the mechanisms driving our results, we also use a simple land-surface model to illustrate how summertime temperature variance and covariance with soil moisture both strengthen under climate change as a consequence of local thermodynamic changes. Combining the results from the simple land surface model with empirically based parameterizations of yield relationships to temperature and soil moisture permits diagnosing the mechanisms through which climate change causes increased yield variance. The simple land surface model and state-of-the-art CMIP6 models both indicate that changes in yield variance are driven by the influence of warming on temperature and its covariance with soil moisture.

RESULTS

Changes in temperature and soil moisture from climate change increase the variance of maize, soybean, and sorghum yields globally

We project that changes in the distribution of daily growing season temperature and soil moisture will increase the interannual variance of staple crop yields (Figs. 2 and 3 and figs. S2 to S4). On average, interannual yield variance increases by 7.1%°C for maize, 19.4%°C for soybeans, and 9.8%°C for sorghum. Percent changes are reported relative to the total weather-induced interannual yield variance in the initial period, 2015–2035 (fig. S5) after taking global averages weighting by cropped area (39).

To estimate uncertainty in these projections, we analyze the distribution of projected effects using 1500 combinations associated with 15 climate models and 100 empirical models. Empirical model realizations are obtained from fitting to bootstrapped yield samples (Materials and Methods). The interquartile range (IQR) of estimates is 3.9 to 9.6%°C for maize, 12.8 to 23.7%°C for soybeans, and 2.2 to 15.3%°C for sorghum. Ninety-two percent of estimates are positive for maize, 100% for soybeans, and 82% for sorghum. Climate model uncertainty and empirical model uncertainty contribute relatively equally to the spread of projected values around the mean estimate (fig. S6), indicating that improvements in both empirical crop models and climate models will improve the precision of projected changes in yield variance.

These increases in yield variance are substantial and larger in proportional magnitude than projected decreases in mean yield of −4.8%°C for maize, −7.7%°C for soybeans, and −2.3%°C for sorghum (fig. S7). Yield variance increases across most of cropped area for each crop (Fig. 2). In some locations, particularly those with small projected effects, there is model disagreement on the sign of the change. The general increase in projected yield variance across three staple crops globally indicates a substantial risk of climate change to future food security.

Increases in mean temperature, temperature variance, and temperature–soil moisture covariance drive increased yield variance

Weather-induced interannual variance of yield can be partitioned into contributions from temperature effects, plus soil moisture effects, plus their covariance. Projected increases in yield variance for all three crops are due largely to increases in the variance due to temperature effects, which increase yield variance relative to the initial variance by 6.9%°C (IQR of 3.7 to 8.6%°C) for maize, 12.7%°C (7.7 to 16.3%°C) for soybeans, and 3.3%°C (−0.1 to 4.7%°C) for sorghum (Fig. 3). Increased covariance between temperature and soil moisture effects also plays an important role for soybeans and sorghum, changing yield variance by −0.1%°C (IQR of −1.2 to 2.2%°C) for maize, 6.2%°C (3.2 to 7.5%°C) for soybeans, and 6.4%°C (1.6 to 8.2%°C) for sorghum (Fig. 3 and fig. S8). Changes in yield variance due to soil moisture effects play a relatively minor role, increasing total yield variance by 0.1%°C (IQR of −1.2 to 1.2%°C) for maize, 0.5%°C (0.0 to 1.2%°C) for soybeans, and 0.1%°C (−2.1 to 2.8%°C) for sorghum. Similar to total yield variance, uncertainty in yield variance from temperature effects, soil moisture effects, and their covariance is driven roughly equally by uncertainty stemming from the climate model and empirical model (fig. S6).

These changes in yield variance due to temperature and soil moisture effects can be explained by changes in the distribution of daily growing season temperature and soil moisture, along with their nonlinear effects on yield (Supplementary Discussion S1). Increased variance in the temperature effect on yield stems from increased climatological mean temperature and increased temperature variance. Warming shifts temperature variability toward steeper portions of the yield-response curve (11) and thereby increases interannual variance in the temperature effect on yield (Fig. 1B and fig. S1). Warming accounts for 73% of the total increase in yield variance due to warmer and more variable temperatures for maize, 71% for soybeans, and 53% for sorghum (fig. S9). This calculation is made by comparing projected yields from our main analysis to those projected by adding only mean warming to simulations of present values to conserve present variance.

The remaining increase in yield variance due to temperature effects is from increased variance of growing season temperature. The variance of interannual average growing season temperature increases by 4.6%°C (IQR of −1.9 to 9.2%°C) for maize, 5.8%°C (1.4 to 11.8%°C) for soybeans, and 5.8%°C (−1.6 to 12.9%°C) for sorghum (Fig. 3 and fig. S10). In addition to greater differences in average temperature from season to season, daily temperature variance within growing seasons also increases by 2.7%°C (IQR of 1.4 to 3.9%°C) for maize, 1.8%°C (0.1 to 3.4%°C) for soybeans, and 2.9%°C (0.9 to 4.9%°C) for sorghum (Fig. 3 and fig. S11). Increased temperature variance within the growing season increases yield variance due to the nonlinear response of yields to temperature. In particular, larger magnitude heat waves give larger magnitude yield anomalies.

The increase in the covariance between temperature and moisture effects on yields stems from a strengthened negative covariance between growing season temperature and soil moisture. The interannual covariance between growing-season temperature and moisture becomes more negative by −2.0%°C (IQR of −6.5 to 2.4%°C) for maize, −3.5%°C (−9.1 to 1.2%°C) for soybeans, and −3.5%°C (−9.4 to 0.2%°C) for sorghum (Fig. 3 and fig. S10). This strengthened coupling is consistent with prior analyses of historic and projected future changes (7, 40–43). Increased interannual coupling between temperature and soil moisture largely reflects strengthened negative covariance at the daily timescale by −4.7%°C (IQR of −6.4 to−2.3%°C) for maize, −5.7%°C (−7.8 to−2.2%°C) for soybeans, and −3.1%°C (−7.4 to 0.5%°C) for sorghum (Fig. 3 and fig. 16). These results indicate that heatwaves and droughts are expected to coincide more frequently with climate change, compounding the individual effects of thermal and moisture stresses on yields (Fig. 1, D and E, and Supplementary Discussion S1).

Moderate changes in the variance of soil moisture effects on yield reflect relatively minor changes in the mean and variance of soil moisture. Mean soil moisture changes by −0.002cm3cm3 per degrees Celsius for maize, −0.002cm3cm3 per degrees Celsius for soybeans, and 0.003cm3cm3 per degrees Celsius for sorghum. These changes are less than 2% of the average soil moisture across cropped areas. Furthermore, interannual variance changes by only −0.4%°C (IQR of −2.1 to 1.9%°C) for maize, 0.6%°C (−1.5 to 2.6%°C) for soybeans, and 3.1%°C (−0.7 to 6.1%°C) for sorghum, and daily within growing season variance changes by only 1.0%°C (IQR of 0.3 to 2.0%°C) for maize, 1.0%°C (0.0 to 2.1%°C) for soybeans, and 1.8%°C (−2.7 to 5.5%°C) for sorghum. We do not explicitly separate the effects on yield variance of changes in soil moisture mean and variance because these are small compared to that of temperature and its covariance with soil moisture.

Collectively, these results indicate that future yield variance will increase due to mean temperature increases as well as increases in the variance of temperature and its covariance with soil moisture, particularly increases in the co-occurrence of hot and dry days. These findings are consistent across most global agricultural regions (figs. S12 and S13). Consideration of changes in mean temperature increases alone, as in previous studies of maize (11), leads to substantial underestimates of the increase in future yield variance, capturing only 82% for maize, 46% for soybeans, and 18% for sorghum of the total variance change calculated here (fig. S9). This indicates that accounting for both increases in future temperature variance and strengthened covariance with soil moisture is key for quantifying how the variance of yields will change under climate change.

Changes in local thermodynamics explain increases in temperature variance and covariance with soil moisture

The above changes in the distribution of growing season temperature and soil moisture calculated using state-of-the-art CMIP6 earth system models can be largely explained by local land surface processes related to temperature, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture. We illustrate this using a simple land surface model (29) that we connect to a version of the empirical crop model used earlier in this text (Materials and Methods). The simple model can provide mechanistic understanding for why the variance of growing season weather changes and help to interpret the more complex CMIP6 model simulations. Note that, although simple models are biased in general with respect to missing processes, the more complex models also have biases; for example, their representation of historical temperature variance has been shown to be up to 20% too high (44).

We use a surface energy and moisture budget (SEMB) model that represents coupling between temperature, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture (29, 45). The SEMB model simulates the coupled evolution of summertime surface temperature and soil moisture through parameterizing closures for the surface energy and moisture budgets (Materials and Methods). A nonlinear relationship between temperature and soil moisture emerges from the basic equations of the SEMB, with a given reduction in soil moisture leading to a larger change in temperature than an increase in soil moisture of equal magnitude (Fig. 4, A and B). This asymmetry also implies that dry days have larger temperature changes associated with global warming than days when soil moisture is high, leading to an increase in temperature variance even if the underlying soil moisture distribution does not change. Potential changes to the soil moisture distribution can also influence the temperature variance, although these are more uncertain in climate models than temperature projections are (46, 47).

In experiments with the SEMB designed to emulate the influence of global climate change on surface temperature and soil moisture (see Materials and Methods), we find increased temperature variance and increased (negative) covariance between temperature and soil moisture (Fig. 4), whereas the simulated variance of soil moisture changes little; all of these are consistent in sign and magnitude with the CMIP6 simulations. As documented in prior work (25, 28, 30), increased radiative forcing disproportionately increases the magnitude of warm temperature anomalies that occur during periods of low rainfall relative to cool temperature anomalies that are associated with wet soils. This leads to both increased temperature variance and greater covariance between low soil moisture and high temperature anomalies. Put another way, droughts in a warmed climate, on average, lead to hotter heat waves than droughts in the present climate, thereby strengthening the association between the two. Little change in soil moisture variance under warming can be understood from the offsetting effects of higher average VPD, which promotes drying and reduced soil moisture variance (29, 48), and increased precipitation and precipitation variance (2), which promote wetting and increased soil moisture variance.

Combining yield response curves with output from the SEMB leads to projected changes in interannual yield variance under climate change similar to those found using CMIP6 simulations paired with empirical crop models (Figs. 3 and 4 and Materials and Methods). Note that, in the SEMB-crop model, we consider a single representative summertime crop for simplicity, motivated by the similarity in empirical response functions across crops. The SEMB results indicate that the increased temperature variance and covariance with soil moisture that drive increased yield variance can be explained by local land surface responses to warming, although this does not rule out more difficult-to-trace processes such as circulation changes or cloud feedbacks (24). Insomuch as local processes are a sufficient explanation for changes in temperature and water availability, such an explanation suggests that modification of local thermodynamics through changes in water management may provide for effective means of adaptation, a topic that is taken up further in the Discussion.

Effects on yield variance are consistent, although exaggerated, when considering changes in temperature and precipitation instead of soil moisture

Water supply is commonly represented in empirical crop models using precipitation, rather than soil moisture [e.g., (3, 11, 21)]. We focus our primary analysis on models using soil moisture due to its improved predictive performance, physiological relevance, and recent improvements in data availability. Here, we reestimate effects of climate change on interannual yield variance using empirical crop models specifying yield as a function of temperature and precipitation (TP), rather than temperature and soil moisture (TS) (5). We find similar projected increases in yield variance for soybeans but over twice as large increases in yield variance for maize and sorghum (fig. S14). This estimation of relatively larger yield variance changes when using TP rather than TS models is consistent with, although substantially larger than, previously described overestimation of mean yield changes (5).

A relatively larger increase in yield variance for maize and sorghum in the TP model compared with the TS model is largely driven by stronger temperature effects (13 and 53% of the total increase in effect for maize and sorghum) and stronger water supply effects (53 and 69% of total increase in effect), with changes in covariance explaining the remaining difference (34% for maize and −23% for sorghum). Stronger temperature effects on yield variance in the TP model stem from stronger concavity in the temperature response function, which is due to damages from dryness being combined with damages of heat when soil moisture is not accounted for [see figure 2 of (5)]. Stronger water supply effects on yield variance in the TP model stem from stronger increases in precipitation variance relative to changes in soil moisture variance, four times greater in magnitude for sorghum and more than 10 times greater for sorghum, combined with differences in their respective influences on yield. Increased precipitation variance relative to soil moisture variance, in part, stems from the fact that soil moisture values are bounded, whereas rainfall values are not, allowing for simulation of greater precipitation extremes than soil moisture extremes in a warming climate. Due to the presence of these extremes, we analyze median, rather than mean values in this comparison of TS and TP models.

The substantial role that water supply plays in determining changes in yield variance under climate change in both TS and TP models indicates the importance of considering water supply effects when calculating future yield variance, which have been omitted in prior global empirical analyses of yield variance (11). In addition, the substantially larger projected variance increases under climate change in TP models relative to those in TS models for maize and sorghum indicate the importance of accurately specifying these water supply effects.

Yield variance increases most where yields fall, temperatures warm, and soils dry

Using the results from the combined CMIP6 data and empirical crop models, we find that locations with the highest rates of projected yield losses, largest rates of temperature increases, and most severe rates of drying also see the largest increases in yield variance (fig. S15, P < 0.05 for each relationship and crop). A 1%°C decrease in mean yields is, on average, associated with a greater rate of yield variance increase with warming by 0.66%°C for maize, 0.83%°C for soybeans, and 0.20%°C for sorghum. Locations that warm at twice the rate of average croplands have greater rates of increased yield variance by 6.4%°C for maize, 18.2%°C for soybeans, and 9.1%°C for sorghum. Similarly, a location that dries at a rate of 0.01 cm3/cm3 more per degrees Celsius has a greater increase in yield variance by 3.3%°C for maize, 23.1%°C for soybeans, and 9.3%°C for sorghum. The quantitative consistency between effects of warming on yield variance identified analyzing spatial differences in rates of yield variance change and rates of warming (fig. S15; 6.4%°C for maize, 18.2%°C for soybeans, and 9.1%°C for sorghum), with those identified analyzing global average temporal changes in yield variance and temperature (Fig. 3; 7.1%°C for maize, 19.4%°C for soybeans, and 9.8%°C for sorghum) further indicates the robustness of the identified relationship.

These results collectively illustrate a concerning relationship whereby decreases in mean yield are accompanied by increases in yield variance and that these combine to substantially increase the frequency of weather-induced negative yield shocks (Fig. 5A and fig. S3). We project that 1-in-100-year weather-induced yield losses during 2015–2035 will instead occur once every 49 years for maize, 25 years for soybeans, and 54 years for sorghum under 2°C of warming during the growing season (Fig. 5B). Rates increase to once every 33 years for maize, 14 years for soybeans, and 40 years for sorghum under 3°C of warming. Following a high-emission scenario, SSP5-8.5, maize and soybean growing seasons warm by 4.3°C on average and sorghum growing seasons warm by 3.2°C on average by the end of the 21st century (2080–2100 relative to 2015–2035). Under these hotter conditions, a 1-in-100-year loss occurs every 17 years for maize, 8 years for soybeans, and 28 years for sorghum. Note that this reduction in return times primarily reflects that a hotter climate is projected to lead to reduced yields and that we do not account for technology trends or adaptation in the projections. Allowing only mean temperature to shift, as in (11) and fig. S9, and thereby not accounting for changes in temperature variance or covariance with soil moisture also indicates yield shocks of increasing frequency by the end of the century under SSP-8.5, but less so, with 1-in-100-year losses occurring every 19.6 years for maize, 8.2 years for soybeans, and 34.2 years for sorghum.

DISCUSSION

Climate change is projected to substantially increase yield variance of maize, soybean, and sorghum across most of global cropped areas, with concerning implications for food security. This increase in variance stems from increased mean temperature, increased temperature variance, and increased covariance between temperature and soil moisture, with the relative influences of these three factors differing across crops and regions. Consistent results when using empirical crop models paired with earth system models, as well as a simple coupled crop and land surface model, indicate that increases in yield variance under climate change stem from relatively fundamental mechanisms. Along with warming increasing sensitivity to temperature variability, increased radiative forcing and VPD under climate change increases temperature variance and covariance with soil moisture, which leads to increased yield variance because both hot and dry conditions damage yields (Fig. 1). For these same reasons, the results from the simple model are qualitatively consistent with the findings that regions with greater warming or greater drying experience greater increases in yield volatility.

Our finding that increased covariance between temperature and soil moisture contributes substantially to increased yield variance under climate change demonstrates the importance of considering proximate measures of crop water supply. Prior analyses using rainfall as a proxy for crop water supply are unable to capture this important mechanism because projected changes in the covariance between temperature and precipitation differ from those of temperature and soil moisture and because precipitation is a relatively weak measure of crop water supply (5). Failure to account for increased coupling between temperature and soil moisture under climate change generally leads to underestimates of increases in yield variance. We find that coupling between temperature and soil moisture adds to projected yield variance negligibly for maize, but by 49% for soybeans and 196% for sorghum, relative to consideration of temperature alone as in prior global empirical analyses (11). The importance of accurately modeling crop water supply is further emphasized by our finding that projecting changes in yield variance accounting for temperature and precipitation, rather than temperature and soil moisture, leads to substantially larger increases in yield variance.

Our results indicate that climate change will increase weather-induced yield variance by 7 to 19%°C across crops. Weather-induced yield variance is, however, only a portion of total yield variance. If 25% of total yield variance is due to weather, estimates range from 10 to 40% (5, 49), then our results suggest that climate change will increase total yield variance by 1.8 to 4.9%°C across crops.

There are several important limitations to this analysis. While projections of changes in future temperature are relatively well constrained, projections of how water supply will change in the future are less certain (28, 50), and, thus, the range of possible values indicated by the spread of CMIP6 models is less likely to represent the full range of possible values. Furthermore, the climatology and historical variability in soil moisture simulations contains biases relative to observations (5). To evaluate the implication of these biases for our results, we explored three options for debiasing the CMIP6 simulations with respect to observations: no debiasing, removing biases in the mean, and removing biases in both the mean and variance (see Materials and Methods). All three give consistent results (Fig. 3 and figs. S16 and S17). We do not take away from this result, however, that soil moisture representation is unimportant because debiasing does not account for the interactions of soil moisture with other variables. Rather, improved representations of soil moisture and its covariance with temperature in climate models is critical for improving projections of climatic influences on global agricultural productivity and food security.

Another avenue for improving empirical crop models is to improve the quality and amount of yield data used to train and test them. Using only better fitting yield data in a previous analysis led to greater estimated sensitivities to changes in temperature and soil moisture (5), which, in the present context, implies larger projected increases in yield variance. Global data on annual crop progress through key phenological stages, in which crops can be differentially sensitive to temperature and water stresses (15, 51), could further improve global empirical crop models and estimates of changes in yield variance under climate change. For example, the finding that strengthened covariance between temperature and soil moisture increases yield variance of soybeans and sorghum but not maize (Fig. 3) could be due to estimated benefits to maize of extremely low soil moisture (fig. S1), which may reflect the particular need of maize for dry weather to reduce kernel moisture following maturity during the drydown phase of development (51). Additional research is needed to determine whether the increase of maize yields at very low soil moisture is an actual agricultural phenomenon or whether yields continue to decrease with drying.

The empirical yield models that we use do not account for interactions between temperature and moisture in influencing yield. Including interaction effects in our empirical yield models leads to reduced out-of-sample performance [see (5) and supplementary discussion 4 therein]. Some studies, however, have found evidence for such physiological interaction effects between temperature and soil moisture on yields in the US (6, 10) and globally (52), although differences in empirical model structures and data hamper direct comparisons. If interactive physiological responses to temperature and soil moisture pointed to in other studies (6, 10, 52) exist for global agriculture, distinct from and in addition to land surface interactions between temperature and soil moisture themselves, then these would lead to even greater increases in yield variance under climate change.

We considered climate change impacts on yield variance stemming from changes in the distribution of growing season temperature and soil moisture. It is possible that increases in CO2 concentration could mitigate the sensitivity of yields to these factors by increasing water use efficiency and attenuating the effects on variance calculated here; however, such effects remain uncertain and difficult to estimate (53, 54). Increased water use efficiency could, all else being equal, leave more water in the soil; though, increased crop growth from CO2 fertilization could potentially offset this effect. Previous analyses that have included CO2 effects find that doing so reduces the degree to which climate change increases the coefficient of variation of yields (10, 12) through a combination of CO2 effects on both mean yields and yield variance; although CO2 effects are generally smaller than those of temperature and rainfall globally and occur primarily through changes to mean yields. Carbon fertilization effects are likely to be larger for crops using C3 photosynthesis (wheat and soybeans) than C4 photosynthesis (maize and sorghum) whose intercellular CO2 concentration is already saturated (55).

Our findings of increased yield variance under climate change for three staple crops globally indicate the need for adaptive measures. Optimized sowing times, seed coatings, and breeding for thermal and drought resistance could help mitigate increases in yield variance from heat and moisture shocks, although the ability to develop and apply such technologies is generally lower in poorer countries (16). Migration of global cropped areas could also potentially mitigate climate damages (56) and has occurred somewhat in recent decades (57), although the feasibility of substantial future agricultural migrations is unknown. Studies also suggest that crop diversification can stabilize national agricultural harvests (58) and buffer productivity from temperature and water stress, especially in regions of Sub-Saharan Africa (59), where projected increases in yield variance are larger than the global mean (fig. S13) and where food insecurity is high (1).

Our finding that increases in yield variance under climate change are controlled by local thermodynamic mechanisms indicates that local adaptation measures related to crop water supply could be particularly effective. Irrigation, which is well understood to ameliorate temperature and water stress on crops (5, 60), is also expected to mitigate increases in yield variance under climate change (61). We explore this potential using the SEMB by applying irrigation treatments to the 4.5°C warming experiments (Materials and Methods). Application of 1 mm/day of irrigation roughly halves the yield variance increase caused by the prescribed warming (table S1) through reducing temperature and temperature variance as well as by weakening the coupling between temperature and soil moisture stresses. Application of 2 mm/day of irrigation water returns yield variance to the prewarming level, even in the presence of a 4.5°C increase in the background mean temperature. These results suggest that irrigation is a highly effective tool for mitigating increases in yield variance under climate change, when and where sufficient water is available. Water demand for irrigation, however, already outstrips sustainable water supply in many global agricultural regions (62), indicating the importance of concomitant plans for managing water resources and improving irrigation water efficiency through, for example, optimization of irrigation scheduling, crop residues, or reduction of application losses (63, 64).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Yield model

Empirical crop models are used to quantify global maize, soybean and sorghum responses to changes in daily maximum surface (2 m) air temperature (hereafter, “temperature”) and surface soil moisture (hereafter, “soil moisture”). These models are panel fixed effect regression models (22) of the formYit=fT(Tit)+fS(Sit)+λi+αit+ϵit(1)where Yit are log yields in a country i during year t; fT(.) and fS(.) are restricted cubic spline expansions of daily maximum temperature and soil moisture during the growing season. λi and αit are flexible spatiotemporal controls that account for time-constant differences across countries like soil type, as well as time-trending variables like improvements in technology. ϵit captures all remaining determinants of yields not explained by temperature, soil moisture, and the spatiotemporal controls, such as year-to-year differences in pest pressure or fertilizer applications.

Equation 1 is fit using national yield data (65) as well as subnational temperature (66) and soil moisture (67–69) observations from 2007 to 2018, following (5). The empirical crop models leverage fine resolution weather data to estimate microlevel (i.e., weather data–level) nonlinear responses of crop yields to environmental factors [for details see Supplementary Discussion S2, as well as (5) and section 4.1 of (22)]. Nonlinearities of the weather variables are calculated at the finest resolution possible (grid cell by day) before aggregating to the country-by-year resolution. This captures the effects of local, within-country, heat waves and droughts by capturing the weather grid cell–level distribution of daily temperature and soil moisture within each growing season. Thus, we calculate effects and show resolution in all maps at the resolution of the weather data used in model estimation, 0.5°. Mapping effects at the grid resolution rather than national resolution also focuses visualization on cultivated areas for each crop and shows within-country heterogeneity in effects.

For soil moisture observations, we use the European Space Agency (ESA) Climate Change Initiative Plus (CCI) Soil Moisture Project v06.1 dataset, which compiles active and passive satellite measures of surface soil moisture up to a depth of ~5 cm at daily 0.25° by 0.25° resolution (67, 68). We use ESA CCI satellite–based soil moisture data because it well represents surface soil moisture over a relatively long time period (67, 68) and is produced using relatively few assumptions about land surface processes compared with reanalysis data (70) or data from land surface models (71). ESA CCI measurements tend to be representative of soil moisture within at least the top 15 to 25 cm (69). Whereas crop roots can extend much deeper than this (maize roots can reach more than 2 m), herbaceous crop species tend to use water from depths less than 25 to 30 cm, with water use higher nearer the surface (69). Thus, these measurements provide a useful measure of root zone soil moisture. Consistent with this, Proctor et al. (5) obtain nearly identical response functions and model performance when (i) using measures of surface soil moisture from ESA CCI directly and (ii) estimating soil moisture down to a depth of 1 m using an autoregressive exponential framework, following (6). Such consistency likely stems from the relatively strong correlations between surface and 1-m soil moisture and the preferential use of water by crops in the upper soil layers (72). For projections of yield variance under climate change, detailed below, we use CMIP6 simulations of surface (10 cm) soil moisture (“mrsos”), which accords well with the ESA CCI measurements.

To quantify how uncertainty stemming from the empirical yield model influences calculations of yield variance, we bootstrap and retrain the yield model 100 times and then use each of those 100 models to project variance changes under climate change. For each bootstrap, we resample from the yield, temperature, and soil moisture observations with replacement after projecting out the model fixed effects. Resulting response curves are shown in fig. S18. The spread of projections using these models indicates the uncertainty in projected variance changes under climate change due to sampling uncertainty of the empirical model (73).

The model performs well out of sample in predicting historical yield anomalies relative to prior empirical models and captures yield responses consistent with plant physiological expectations (5). Response functions showing the influence of changes in temperature and soil moisture on yield are shown in fig. S1. The empirical crop model structure, training, validation, and performance are detailed in (5). Empirical crop models accounting for temperature and precipitation take a similar structure to those accounting for temperature and soil moisture and are trained and validated as described in (5).

Following (5), we also project climate change influences on the distribution of millet yields and find increases in variance of 29% between the periods 2015–2035 and 2080–2100 following SSP5-8.5, which is consistent with projected increases for the other crops, which range from 25 to 80%. However, we exclude millet from further analysis and exposition, first, because the historical model performance is the lowest of the four crops and, second, because much of the projected increase in variance stems from increased variance from soil moisture effects, which, in turn, stems from the strong but highly statistically uncertain and physiologically unjustified estimated benefits to yield of extremely high soil moisture.

CMIP6 data

To project future changes in yield variance under climate change, we apply the empirical crop models described above to simulations of future climate from CMIP6 (31). We project yields for each crop, year, and climate model from 2015 to 2100, following the SSP5-8.5 scenario. We use the SSP5-8.5 scenario because it allows us to analyze a wide range of warming levels (+1° to +4°C) and because it had the most models available on the Google Cloud CMIP6 archive with daily temperature and soil moisture at the time of analysis. Throughout the text, we show results per degrees Celsius of warming over global croplands so that results are generalizable to a range of warming levels, allowing for comparisons of changes in yield variance under different warming scenarios (e.g., Fig. 5). Showing results per degrees Celsius is further motivated by the linearity of changes in variance with warming shown in fig. S2.

We use 15 CMIP6 models: ACCESS-ESM1-5, BCC-CSM2-MR, CanESM5, CMCC-ESM2, EC-Earth3, GFDL-CM4, INM-CM4-8, INM-CM5-0, IPSL-CM6A-LR, KACE-1-0-G, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, MRI-ESM2-0, and NorESM2-LM. We selected all models from the Google Cloud CMIP6 archive with available daily soil moisture (mrsos) and maximum temperature (tmax) from 2000 to 2100. The output of each model is regridded to a common 0.5° by 0.5° resolution using bilinear interpolation to match the resolution of the temperature and soil moisture observations used in estimation of the empirical model. Uncertainty stemming from the structure and parameterization of different climate models is quantified using the spread of projected changes in variance across the 15 climate models analyzed.

Before calculating yield effects, we mean-debias the CMIP6 data using the delta method (35–37) by calculating the mean difference between CMIP6-simulated (2000–2014) and observed (2007–2018) values in each grid cell during this historical period and correcting the values accordingly for each CMIP6 model. We take observed values from 2007 to 2018 according to availability of soil moisture and yield data and take CMIP6 data from 2000 to 2014 because 2014 is the end of the historical scenario for the CMIP6 data. Mean changes in climate between these two epochs are minor, and intervals are selected to be roughly commensurate. For temperature, we add the difference between the historical observed and historical simulated data to the CMIP6 values in each grid cell. For soil moisture, which has a minimum value of zero, we scale the simulated values by the ratio of historical observed and historical simulated means (38).

To test the robustness of our primary results to the type of debiasing, we replicate a portion of our analysis using a pixelwise mean and variance debiasing approach (74). As described in (5), we first debias the variance of the CMIP6 values by demeaning the values of each grid cell, multiplying the values by the ratio of the SD of observed values to the SD of simulated values, and then adding back in the mean. We then mean-debias the CMIP6 data using the delta method as in the mean-debiasing approach described above. In the CMIP6 data that was mean and variance debiased, we winsorize values for soil moisture to a maximum of 0.5 to correct a small number of extreme values that were introduced during the variance debiasing step. This approach gives consistent results to the delta method (fig. S16), as does applying no debiasing (fig. S17).

Analysis

Yield, temperature, and soil moisture variance

To denote our calculation of yield variance and its decomposition into constituent parts (i.e., from temperature effects, soil moisture effects, and their covariance), let weather-induced log yield anomalies from location-specific means and trends, Y, be an additive function, f(.), of anomalies in features describing daily temperature, T, and soil moisture, S, during the growing seasonY=fT(T)+fS(S)This partition reflects the specification or our empirical yield model (Eq. 1). The variance, V(.), of Y is thenV(Y)=VfT(T)+fS(S)which can be further decomposed into terms related to the variance of temperature effects, soil moisture effects, and their covarianceVfT(T)+fS(S)=VfT(T)+VfS(S)+2CovfT(T),fS(S)This latter step uses the fact that the sum of two random variables is equal to the variance of each plus twice their covariance; if A and B are random variables, V(A + B) = V(A) + V(B) + 2Cov(A, B).

We calculate yield variance due to temperature, soil moisture, and their combination by first calculating anomalies from trend of annual log yield for each CMIP6 model and grid cell using the empirical yield models and daily growing season temperature and soil moisture values. For each CMIP6 model and 21-year time period (2015–2035, 2016–2036,…, 2080–2100), we detrend the weather-induced yield anomalies by grid cell to avoid any influence of change in mean yields on the calculation of yield variance. We then calculate the variance of the weather-induced yield anomalies for each grid cell and time period. We calculate the change in yield variance by subtracting values from the future period (one of 2016–2036,…, 2080–2100) from a baseline period (2015–2035). For each time period, we use 21 years of CMIP6 simulations to estimate the distribution of daily climate variables and their influence on yield variance to have a sample that is large enough to average over internal variability but also small enough to be representative of a given time period and, therefore, allow for analysis of changes over time periods. Calculations are done identically for each crop. Covariances between yield effects due to temperature and soil moisture are calculated using the same steps as calculation of the variances. An alternative approach to understanding how changes in temperature and soil moisture influence yield variance would be to calculate interannual yield variance for each country during multiple time periods and regress that on climatological temperature and soil moisture from each time period. However, the limited span over which high-quality global soil moisture observations are available, 12 years in this analysis, makes such an approach infeasible.

Calculations for the interannual variance and change in interannual variance of temperature and soil moisture take the same form as for yields. To calculate the within-season variance and covariance of growing season daily temperature and soil moisture, we calculate the values for each year and then average over the years in each time period for each crop and CMIP6 model. Mean changes in yield, temperature, and soil moisture (fig. S7) are calculated using the same models and data as yield variance.

We evaluate calculations of weather-induced yield variance for each crop and find that the variance due to temperature and soil moisture effects is roughly equal in the present period (fig. S5). This is consistent with each of these factors explaining similar amounts of historical yield variability in observations (5).

Note that all mapped yield variance values have been winsorized to their 5th and 95th percentile values for display purposes only; all calculations include unwinsorized values. All maps show areas where greater than 0.2% of the land area is cropped with the specific crop of interest as of the year 2000 (39).

To calculate percent change in yield variance globally (Fig. 2), we take the difference in yield variance between the periods of 2080–2100 and 2015–2035 and divide by the variance from 2015 to 2035 for each CMIP6 model and then average across models. This fractional change in variance is then divided by the crop-specific average warming for each crop to get changes in variance per degrees Celsius. Warming over cropped areas is 4.3°C for maize and soybeans and 3.2°C for sorghum (fig. S19). A grid cell value of 10%°C, for example, indicates a 10% increase in yield variance per degrees Celsius average warming over cropped areas for that crop.

Projected changes in global cropped-area average variance and covariance for temperature and soil moisture (figs. S2 and S9) are normalized by their present (2015–2035) global cropped-area average values, such that a value of 100% indicates a doubling of variance. All changes in yield variance are normalized by the present average yield variance from temperature and soil moisture, such that a value of 100% indicates a doubling. This normalization also means that the total variance change is the sum due to the contributions from temperature, soil moisture, and their covariance. We report changes in the effects of covariance of temperature and soil moisture on yields after multiplying by two because total yield variance due to temperature and soil moisture is the sum of yield variance due to temperature, to soil moisture, and to twice their covariance. All global averages are calculated weighting by crop-specific cropped area (39). Regional calculations (fig. S13) are identical, with normalization done using regional, rather than global, values. To calculate global average change in variance per degree warming (%°C) as in Fig. 3 and fig. S6, we use the slopes from fig. S2, which are calculated by regressing percent change in variance in each period (2016–2036, 2017–2037,…, 2080–2100) relative to the initial period (2015–2035) against the change in temperature over the same periods. Calculating the change in variance from 2015–2035 to 2080–2100 and dividing by the change in temperature gives consistent results.

Distributions of weather-induced yield anomalies (Fig. 5) are sampled weighting by cropped area so that distributions are representative of where crops are grown. Values are given equal weights across CMIP6 models. Quantile values of climate-induced yield anomalies are calculated from these values. For each warming level (i.e., +2°, +3°, and +4°C) values are taken from the 21 year period (2016–2036, 2017–2037,…, 2080–2100) for which the increase in global average temperature over cropped areas is closest to the given level of warming for each crop and CMIP6 model.

SEMB model

To complement our analysis of CMIP6 output, we analyze output from a simple land surface model and a single representative empirical model connecting temperature and soil moisture variations to yield. The simple land surface model simulates temperature T (units, kelvin) and soil moisture θ (units, cubic meter per cubic meter) variability in a 1-m slab of soil and is used to interpret and illustrate the physical basis of the full-complexity results derived from the CMIP6 output. The SEMB model is specified using two prognostic equations for these state variablesCsdTdt=F−H−LE(2)μdθdt=P−E(3)where all terms in Eq. 2 are in units of watts per square meter and all terms in Eq. 3 are in units of kilograms of H2O per square meter per second. The Cs constant is the effective surface heat capacity, F is the net radiation (shortwave and longwave) absorbed by the surface layer, H is the flux of sensible heat out of the surface layer, E is the evapotranspiration, and L is the latent enthalpy of vaporization. In Eq. 3, μ is a geometric constant that controls the rate at which precipitation and evapotranspiration alter the surface soil saturation. P is the precipitation rate. For a list of model parameters and their values, see table S2.

In our simulations, F is taken as a constant of 300 W/m2 to approximate the summertime climatology over the US corn belt. The sensible heat flux is parameterized as a linear function of the surface temperatureH=νH(T−To)(4)where νH is a constant that controls the magnitude of the sensible heat flux and To is a reference temperature that has a mean value of 10°C and varies in time independently of the land surface state to emulate synoptic variations in weather. Red noise with a memory timescale of 3 days is used to force synoptic variability in the simple model. The red noise is drawn from a Rayleigh distribution to emulate the skewed character of mid-latitude temperature distributions. We tune the variance of the red noise distribution such that the temperature variance simulated by the SEMB closely matches that found in the temperature distributions over maize croplands (see Fig. 4A with fig. S1).

Evapotranspiration is parameterized in terms of both temperature and soil moistureE=ρaθθFCrsqs(T)−q¯(5)where ρa is the density of air, θFC is the field capacity of the soil layer, rs is the surface resistance to evapotranspiration, and q¯ is the climatological specific humidity. These four parameters are set as constants in our simulations. This parameterization of the surface energy budget depends nonlinearly on temperature via the saturation specific humidity qs(T) and linearly on soil moisture and has been shown to accurately describe key features of land surface climate variability, including the relationship between soil moisture and temperature (45) and the variance and higher statistical moments of the growing season temperature distribution (29).

In our simulations, precipitation is generated stochastically in a two-step process. The first step is determining whether precipitation occurs at a particular time step. At each time step in the model integration, a random number is drawn from a uniform distribution; if this number is below an assigned threshold value, then precipitation occurs. The intensity of the precipitation is drawn from a Gamma distribution with mean value of 11 mm and a shape factor κ that is set to unity in our control simulations and increased under climate change (see below). The threshold for precipitation is set such that rainfall occurs once every 3 days on average.

We run 500 years of 200-day simulations with the red noise reference temperatures and stochastic precipitation to generate a large sample of surface temperature and soil moisture variability designed to emulate growing season conditions in the US corn belt. For consistency with the observed daily maximum near-surface air temperature distribution, the mean daily land surface temperatures output by the SEMB are increased by 5°C after the model is run, but before the temperature effects on yield are calculated. Our results are insensitive to changes in this scaling factor. Land surface and near-surface air temperatures are not identical, particularly during the warmest part of the day, but have been shown to be tightly coupled on daily timescales in observations; this justifies our use of a constant offset parameter between the two variables (75).

To simulate the effects of global warming with the SEMB, we run 11 ensembles of 500 experiments with the SEMB where To in Eq. 4 increases linearly from 283 to 292 K. We also experimented with applying a linear trend in net radiation F across the ensemble simulations; this did not change the results. The 9 K trend in Eq. 4 is chosen so that mean surface temperature in the model warms by roughly 4.5 K in the warmest ensemble of simulations, consistent with end-of-century warming projections over the central US under SSP5-8.5. To emulate increasing rainfall with global warming, as predicted on average across the land surface in climate models (2), we increase the shape factor κ of the Gamma distribution from 1 to a maximum value of 1.5.

For the irrigation experiments, we applied the To=292 K and κ=1.5 values from the most extreme climate change ensemble to four additional 500-member simulations, where an extra water flux is added to Eq. 3. These fluxes are constant in time and emulate the addition of soil water directly into the surface layer at each time step via irrigation.
