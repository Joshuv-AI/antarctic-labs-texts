Dominance and natural suppression of bacterial plant pathogens across global soils
Authors: Min Gao, Manuel Delgado-Baquerizo, Chao Xiong, Tadeo Sáez-Sandino, Juntao Wang, Jinsong Liang, Emilio Guirado, Miriam Muñoz-Rojas, Raul Román, Fernando T. Maestre, Brajesh K. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soils are the primary environmental reservoir of plant pathogens impacting food production and ecosystem productivity worldwide. Yet, some soils can also suppress pathogens through environmental and microbial regulation. Here we integrate 1602 soil metagenomes from 59 countries with a greenhouse experiment to identify 32 dominant pathogens, including Ralstonia solanacearum, Clavibacter michiganensis, and Streptomyces europaeiscabiei. Pathogen hotspots occur primarily in warm ecosystems and agricultural soils, whereas higher soil microbial diversity, increased soil organic carbon and colder climatic conditions are associated with lower pathogen prevalence. Non-pathogenic Streptomyces spp., arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and biosynthetic gene clusters encoding terpenes and polyketides are associated with reduced pathogen prevalence. Predictive modelling suggests that several dominant bacterial pathogens are likely to increase in prevalence under future climate scenarios, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. By identifying global drivers of dominant pathogens and their suppression, this study provides a foundation for improved surveillance and management of plant disease risks under climate change.

Introduction

Plant diseases caused by bacterial pathogens substantially reduce global primary productivity and threaten food security1–3. In agricultural systems, they directly undermine crop yields, while in natural ecosystems their increased prevalence can lower primary productivity, diminishing biodiversity and ecosystem carbon storage4. Although soils are the largest reservoir of plant pathogens, they also possess an inherent capacity to suppress pathogen abundance and disease outbreaks5. Understanding how bacterial pathogens are distributed across soils and how they interact with co-existing microbial communities is therefore essential for identifying the environmental and biological conditions that promote or limit their prevalence.

Despite evidence that soils play a central role in regulating the survival, proliferation, and suppression of plant pathogens, major knowledge gaps persist regarding the ecology of soil-borne bacterial plant pathogens. Three factors largely explain this deficiency. First, most previous research has focused on fungal and oomycetes pathogens6–8, while bacterial plant pathogens have received comparatively less attention, in part because conventional markers, such as 16S rRNA genes, have limited power to distinguish pathogenic from nonpathogenic species. Second, we currently lack a comprehensive, standardized assessment of the global biogeography and environmental controls of bacterial pathogens in soils. Unlike fungal and oomycete taxa, whose global distributions are known to be strongly shaped by temperature and precipitation6, comparable insights for bacterial plant pathogens remain unresolved owing to limited taxonomic resolution and the absence of global-scale metagenomic datasets. Finally, although some soils naturally suppress pathogen abundance and infection, the specific biological and environmental determinants of this suppression remain poorly defined across global ecosystems. In particular, the roles of microbial diversity, functional traits, and competing microbial taxa in constraining bacterial pathogens in soils have not been quantified at a global scale. Together, these gaps severely limit our ability to anticipate pathogen emergence, identify soils with inherent suppressive properties, and develop targeted strategies to monitor and mitigate plant disease risks under rapid environmental change. Given that plant diseases already impose multibillion-dollar annual losses9–11 and threaten global food security2,12–15, resolving these fundamental unknowns is urgently needed to inform future surveillance, management, and policy interventions.

To address existing knowledge gaps, we combined data from public databases (1402 samples) with a standardized global field survey (200 samples)16, together comprising 1602 soil metagenomes from 59 countries (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1), to: (1) determine the global distribution of dominant soil-borne bacterial plant pathogens; (2) identify the environmental conditions, soil microbial diversity, and microbial and metabolic traits associated with pathogen-suppressive soils; and (3) predict the current and future global distributions of these dominant bacterial plant pathogens. To achieve this, we integrated metagenomic and pathogen genomic analyses with amplicon sequencing and qPCR data from a global field survey, together with machine-learning and predictive modeling approaches. To further validate the microbial diversity–suppression relationship identified in the global dataset, we conducted a controlled greenhouse experiment to directly test the effects of soil biodiversity on pathogen suppression. Emerging global evidence suggests that higher microbiome diversity can constrain human and animal pathogens (identified based on 16s rRNA genes) in the plant rhizosphere17. Thus, we predict that soil biodiversity similarly functions as a key biotic barrier that suppresses soil-borne bacterial plant pathogens by outcompeting pathogens in the soil. We also expect that warmer and drier climates, and lower soil organic carbon (OC) would be associated with a higher prevalence of soil-borne bacterial plant pathogens6,7,18. Our overarching aim was to identify globally dominant soil-borne bacterial plant pathogens, characterize their distribution and ecological drivers, and determine the environmental and biological factors that promote natural pathogen suppression.

Results and discussion

Dominant bacterial plant pathogens across global soils

First, we constructed a comprehensive bacterial plant pathogen database comprising 310 pathogen genomes and identified their abundance across global soils using a global soil metagenome dataset. One hundred and seven distinct taxa were identified as potential plant pathogens, among which 32 species emerged as dominant pathogens based on their frequency (detected in 80% of samples) and relative abundance (top 30%, with average relative abundance ≥ 0.1%) (Fig. 1). These dominant pathogens included several species from the genera Streptomyces, Rhodococcus, Agrobacterium, Clavibacter, Ralstonia, Burkholderia, Paracidovorax, Pseudomonas, and Xanthomonas, all known to cause diseases in a broad range of plants across diverse ecosystems. For example, Ralstonia solanacearum, the causative agent of bacterial wilt, ranks as the second most destructive bacterial pathogen globally due to its extensive strain diversity, broad host range, and deleterious impact on plant productivity19,20. Notably, we identified four Streptomyces species (i.e., S. europaeiscabiei, S. acidiscabies, S. turgidiscabies, S. scabiei), ranked highly among the dominant pathogens (Fig. 1a, c). This lineage is prominently linked to potato common scab disease21. Comparative genomic analyses further revealed that dominant pathogen species were enriched in genes associated with signaling and cellular processes, particularly those involved in sugar transport systems, energy metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism, relative to nondominant species (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). These genes are integral to cellular communication and function, regulate cell growth and interactions, all of which are critical to ecological adaptability23,24. Moreover, these genomic signatures suggest that the pathogenicity in dominant pathogens may involve sophisticated signaling mechanisms and enhanced capacity to interact with and penetrate cell wall and membranes25, potentially contributing to their ecological success and prevalence in soil environments.

We next investigated the correlation between the prevalence of key pathogens identified in our database and independent global observation resources. Our correlation analysis revealed a positive association between the relative abundance of R. solanacearum (rho = 0.44, P = 0.02, 28 countries) in our dataset and independent observation resource of this pathogen26 (Fig. 2b). Similarly, the relative abundances of Clavibacter michiganensis (rho = 0.42, P = 0.0047), Robbsia andropogonis (rho = 0.61, P = 0.0058), and Xanthomonas translucens (rho = 0.53, P = 0.0066) were positively correlated with their recorded observations in various countries by the EPPO Global Database (https://gd.eppo.int)27 (Fig. 2b). These country-level correlations indicate that the relative abundance patterns inferred from our metagenomic database are consistent with independent global surveillance records, supporting the reliability of our pathogen prevalence estimates. If these findings are further confirmed at local and regional scales by real farm data, our framework could support the development of more effective disease surveillance systems, enabling timely and targeted management and prevention strategies. Hotspots of soil bacterial plant pathogens

Next, we sought to understand the conditions that promote the global distribution of bacterial plant pathogens. Their relative abundance showed distinct patterns across terrestrial soil biomes, with significantly higher relative abundance observed in farmland compared to other land-use types (Fig. 2a). This suggests that soils influenced by agricultural management (e.g., monoculture and use of agrochemicals) may harbor higher pathogen abundance than natural soils28. In particular, pathogens from the genus Pseudomonas, including P. corrugata, P. fuscovaginae, P. marginalis, P. mediterranea, and P. palleroniana, as well as Pectobacterium carotovorum, were notably more abundant in farmlands (> 50%, Fig. 1b). These findings highlight the potential role of agricultural management in shaping the prevalence and distribution of bacterial pathogens.

Random forest analyses identified mean annual temperature (MAT) as a significant factor affecting the relative abundance of most dominant pathogens (29 out of 32) (Fig. 1c). The influence of MAT varied among pathogens but generally showed a positive effect on their total abundance (Figs. 1c and 2c), suggesting that rising temperatures may increase the prevalence of most pathogens6,29,30. This increased prevalence is likely promoted by temperature-induced shortened incubation periods of pathogens and extended growing seasons of hosts3. The influence of mean annual precipitation (MAP) varied across pathogen taxa. For example, Streptomyces spp. and Clavibacter michiganensis showed negative correlations with MAP (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3), whereas Rhizobium rhizogenes, Burkholderia spp., Ralstonia solanacearum species complex, and Xanthomonas spp. exhibited positive correlations with MAP (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3). These patterns suggest niche partitioning among pathogens based on water availability and specific ecological preferences31–36. Taken together, we showed that warmer ecosystems and farmlands support the largest soil bacterial plant pathogen prevalence worldwide.

The ecology of soil pathogen suppression

We evaluated which set of soil properties and environmental conditions were associated with the lowest prevalence of pathogens, reflecting natural soil pathogen suppression. Correlation analyses showed that cold ecosystems, as well as soils with higher organic carbon (OC) content, finer soil textures, and greater microbial diversity, consistently exhibited lower relative abundances of pathogens (Fig. 1c). To further elucidate the ecological pathways driving these patterns, we conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) using available metadata. SEM analyses indicated that MAP exerts direct positive effects on vegetation (measured by NDVI), which in turn enhances microbial diversity and, ultimately, lowers pathogen relative abundance (Fig. 2c). Consistent results from both correlation and SEM analyses showed that regions characterized by higher microbial diversity, colder climates, finer soil textures, and elevated soil OC content tend to exhibit lower pathogen prevalence at the global scale (Figs. 1 and 2). These findings align with research conducted at plot and farm scales37–41. For example, increasing soil OC, such as through organic amendments, has been reported to reduce soil-borne diseases by stimulating microbial activity42. However, the relationship between soil OC and pathogen abundance is complex and varies among different pathogens and regions. Some studies have reported that increased OC may also lead to an increase in the prevalence of certain fungal pathogens43. This divergence is likely linked to nutrient preference for soilborne bacterial and fungal/ oomycetes pathogens44, reflecting the distinct environmental preferences of various pathogens. Overall, our study shows that microbial diversity and specific soil properties are associated with lower abundances of bacterial plant pathogens across terrestrial ecosystems.

To further support the observed relationship between soil microbial diversity and pathogen suppression, we quantified microbial diversity using both shotgun metagenomic and amplicon sequencing data and conducted a greenhouse experiment in which soil microbial diversity was experimentally manipulated. We first assessed the microbial taxa diversity (based on the Kraken 2 database)45 and functional diversity (based on KEGG database)46 from 1602 metagenomic samples and explored their association with the relative abundance of bacterial plant pathogens (Figs. 1 and 3a). For the 200 globally standardized field samples, we quantified bacterial abundance using 16S rRNA qPCR and characterized bacterial and fungal community diversity through amplicon sequencing, and further explored their relationships with pathogens. Microbial taxonomic diversity estimated by Kraken 2 and KEGG functional diversity both exhibited modest yet statistically significant negative correlations with the total relative abundance of dominant bacterial plant pathogens (Kraken 2: rho = −0.051, P = 0.039; KEGG: rho = −0.13, P = 2.44 × 10−7; n = 1602; Fig. 3a). Analyses of bacterial and fungal diversity indices from amplicon sequencing data also showed a significant negative correlation with the total relative abundance of bacterial plant pathogens (bacteria: rho = −0.20, P = 0.006; fungi: rho = −0.18, P = 0.013; n = 200) (Fig. 3b). To experimentally validate these patterns, we manipulated soil microbial diversity using a dilution-to-extinction approach47, incubated the inoculated soils for six months, and subsequently introduced pathogens for short-term monitoring. For this validation experiment, we selected two model and dominant pathogens, which have differential mode of infection and environmental preferences, S. scabiei (a necrotrophic pathogen favoring drier soils)21 and R. solanacearum (a vascular wilt pathogen thriving in moist environments)20, allowing us to assess whether the observed diversity–pathogen relationship is consistent across two contrasting and ecologically distinct model pathogens during early invasion stages. Pathogen abundance was quantified by qPCR and decreased with increasing soil microbial diversity (Fig. 3c), providing experimental support for a negative association between microbial diversity and early pathogen abundance dynamics. Although similar diversity–pathogen patterns have been reported in plot- and pot-scale studies for other pathogens41,47, future studies incorporating a broader range of pathogen taxa will be required to assess the generality of this relationship. Additionally, we observed a significantly negative correlation between soil total bacterial abundance (measured by 16S rRNA qPCR) and the relative abundance of bacterial plant pathogens (rho = −0.30, P = 2.529e-05) in our standardized global survey samples (Fig. 3d), consistently supporting the notion that increased microbial diversity and bacterial abundance correlate with reduced pathogen load and disease incidence48. Soils with high microbial diversity and biomass are likely to facilitate complex interactions among coexisting microbes, encompassing competition, predation, symbiosis, and parasitism48–50, at the expense of pathogen dominance. Collectively, our results provide global-scale evidence, supported by a greenhouse validation experiment, that higher soil microbial diversity and bacterial abundance are associated with reduced abundance of bacterial plant pathogens. Microbial taxa and potential metabolic traits linked to soil pathogen suppression

Given the importance of soil microbial diversity in shaping patterns of pathogen prevalence, we further examined specific microbial taxa within soil microbiome that are associated with lower pathogen abundance. Correlation analyses involving 9619 bacterial taxa identified using the Kraken 2 database revealed 526 taxa that were negatively correlated with the total relative abundance of dominant pathogens (using thresholds of rho < −0.2 and P < 0.0001). Taxa belonging to the phyla Actinomycetota and Bacillota were predominant among these negatively correlated groups, accounting for 34.6% and 16.5% of all negatively correlated taxa, respectively. In particular, the relative abundance of nonpathogenic Streptomyces spp. was negatively associated with pathogen abundance (rho = −0.08, P = 0.00195) (Fig. 3e), consistent with their known capacity to produce a wide array of antibiotics and secondary metabolites that inhibit plant pathogens54. Beyond bacterial communities, we also identified several fungal functional groups that were negatively associated with pathogen prevalence, including Arbuscular Mycorrhizal fungi (rho = −0.17, P = 0.017) and lichenized fungi (rho = −0.17, P = 0.017) (Fig. 3f). These fungal groups were also linked to significant increases in soil bacterial and fungal diversity and abundance (P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting their potential contribution to pathogen-suppressive soil contexts across terrestrial ecosystems. Overall, these findings highlight the multifaceted microbial features associated with lower pathogen abundance in soils across broad ecological contexts, involving both bacterial antagonists and beneficial fungal symbionts.

To further support our findings, we examined the potential microbial metabolite traits associated with pathogen-suppressive soils. Metagenomic data were annotated using antiSMASH55 to assess correlations between biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) encoding microbial metabolites and pathogen abundance. We found that gene clusters encoding terpene (rho = −0.11, P = 6.063e-06), and type III polyketide synthases (T3PKS; rho = −0.11, P = 2.494e-05) showed the strongest negative associations with pathogen abundance (Fig. 3g). Terpenes, one of the largest class of natural products56, are produced by diverse bacteria and fungi, particularly members of the Streptomycetaceae, and are well documented for their antimicrobial activity57, including effects on cellular respiration58 and membrane integrity57. Polyketides, another diverse class of natural products with antimicrobial properties, similarly showed significantly negative associations with pathogen abundance in our dataset and have been widely reported to contribute to disease suppression by microbes, such as Bacillus spp.59, Paenibacillus spp.60, Trichoderma spp.61, and Flavobacterium spp.5. In addition to terpenes and T3PKS, gene clusters encoding indole, ranthipeptides, and NRPS-like compounds also exhibited negative correlations with the relative abundances of specific dominant pathogens (Fig. 3h). Notably, these negative associations between biosynthetic gene clusters and pathogen abundance were consistent across both total dominant pathogens and most individual dominant pathogen taxa (Fig. 3h). Together, these findings provide insights into specific microbial taxa and biosynthetic gene clusters associated with the secondary metabolite biosynthetic capacity of pathogen-suppressive soils at a global scale, highlighting their potential as candidate microbial features or putative indicators of reduced pathogen prevalence.

Expansion risk of bacterial pathogens under climate change

Having identified current pathogen hotspots and the ecological drivers associated with soil pathogen suppression, we next projected the distributions of dominant bacterial plant pathogens under global change scenarios. By integrating key environmental predictors, we generated predictive maps of both current and future distributions across global soils, explaining 64–77% of the spatial variation in pathogen prevalence (Supplementary Table 2). Our analysis indicated a notable prevalence of pathogens in areas with high temperatures, particularly in northern regions of South America, North Africa, and South Asia (Fig. 4a). Future climate projections, especially under global warming scenarios, suggest not only increased relative abundance in these areas but also the emergence of new hotspots in northern Asia (Fig. 4b). This projected expansion mirrors the predicted trends for fungal plant pathogens, which are similarly expected to intensify under global change scenarios6. S. europaeiscabiei, a species predominant in arid regions, is currently distributed across all continents, with higher relative abundance in Africa, Australia, the southwestern region of North America, and the southwestern part of Asia (Fig. 4c). Predictive models forecast a future expansion of S. europaeiscabiei into northern Asia (Fig. 4d), suggesting range shifts and increased dominance. Similarly, R. solanacearum species complex, which thrives in humid conditions, currently dominates equatorial regions including South Asia, northern South America, northern Australia, eastern North America, and Central Africa (Fig. 4e). Future projections indicate an increased dominance of this pathogen near equatorial zones (Fig. 4f). Importantly, and despite employing independent analytical approaches, the distribution patterns of R. solanacearum from our models closely matched records from the EPPO Global Database (https://gd.eppo.int/)27, providing independent validation of our predictive framework. Overall, our predictive maps indicate projected increases in dominant bacterial plant pathogens across much of the globe under anticipated future climatic conditions (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Overall, our findings have important implications for future research and for the monitoring and management of plant disease risks. Future work should assess how the global drivers of pathogen prevalence and suppression identified here interact with local factors, such as land-use practices, crop management (e.g., resistant varieties, crop rotation), and regional socio-economic constraints—to shape disease outcomes. While this study focuses on pathogen dominance and suppression, predicting pathogen virulence and disease severity associated with dominance represents an important next research direction. Establishing locally relevant thresholds for microbial diversity, the abundance of key microbial taxa, such as nonpathogenic Streptomyces spp., or the presence of specific biosynthetic gene clusters could also enable the development of practical indicators of pathogen-suppressive soils. Such indicators would provide a valuable basis for improved surveillance systems and for designing targeted interventions, including microbial inoculants or metabolite-based approaches, to mitigate disease risks in both agricultural and natural ecosystems.

We present a comprehensive catalogue of globally dominant soil-borne bacterial plant pathogens and provide insights into the environmental and biological factors associated with natural pathogen suppression. Our results show that high soil microbial diversity, increased soil organic carbon, and colder climates consistently coincide with lower pathogen prevalence worldwide. We also identify specific microbial groups, including nonpathogenic Streptomyces spp., arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and lichenized fungi, and biosynthetic gene clusters encoding terpenes and polyketides as potential indicators of pathogen-suppressive soils. Our analyses indicate that several dominant bacterial pathogens are likely to expand or increase in prevalence under future climate scenarios, highlighting regions where disease risks may intensify. These findings advance our understanding of the ecological processes shaping pathogen distributions and provide a foundation for developing improved surveillance tools, management practices, and future research aimed at mitigating the impacts of soil-borne bacterial diseases on global food production and ecosystem functioning.

Methods

Global soil metagenomics (shotgun sequencing based) database

We developed a global soil metagenomic database by combining metagenomes obtained from public databases (including 1402 metagenomes) and a standardized global soil sampling (including 200 newly generated metagenomes). Public data included a published global standardized soil metagenome dataset and soil metagenomes from global regions available in the NCBI SRA database62,63. For the standardized global soil samples, topsoil (~0–10 cm) samples were collected from a global soil survey conducted between 2016 and 2019, employing standardized field protocols for all sampling procedures16. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was conducted at the Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) Facility (Western Sydney University, Australia) using the Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate approximately 10 Gb of 150 bp paired-end high-quality reads per sample. In total, the combined database comprises soil metagenomes derived from 317 independent NCBI BioProjects, spanning data release years from 2011 to 2024 (Supplementary Data 1).

Our final global dataset comprises 1602 soil metagenomes collected from 23 terrestrial biomes spanning 59 countries across all continents (Supplementary Fig. 1). This dataset covers a broad range of climates (arid, temperate, tropical, cold, and polar) and major vegetation types, including grasslands, shrublands, forests, wetlands, and farmlands. We included soils from both agricultural and natural ecosystems because natural soils provide an essential ecological baseline, can act as reservoirs of pathogen inoculum, and offer an optimal context for examining natural pathogen suppression, an ecological process often obscured by intensive management in agricultural systems. Sampling locations were selected to capture a robust representation of Earth’s geographical, climatic and edaphic diversity, and all sites corresponded to natural or agricultural soils rather than controlled experimental samples. For example, the latitude of the sampling locations ranges from –77.6 to 80.7; the longitude ranges from –159.6 to 172.2, and plant cover ranges between 0.1 and 100%. The mean annual temperature (MAT) varies between –24.8 and 27.8 °C; the mean annual precipitation (MAP) ranges from 6 to 5895 mm, and the soil pH ranges from 3.7 to 9.1. This broad coverage improves the representativeness of the dataset, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of how environmental factors shape the global distribution of soil bacterial plant pathogens.

Metagenomic analysis and potential bacterial plant pathogens detection

The global soil metagenomics database (1602 samples) was used to detect potential bacterial plant pathogens. Metagenomic tools such as Kraken 245,64, MetaPhlAn265, and MG-RAST66, enable high-resolution identification of microbial taxonomic information. However, these tools are generally not tailored for the specific detection of bacterial plant pathogens. To enhance the accuracy of pathogen identification in soil samples, we developed a specialized, high-quality database focused on bacterial plant pathogens (BPPDB). Guided by previous literature, this database comprises 113 recognized bacterial plant pathogen species represented by 310 complete genomes obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). By capturing the majority of globally important bacterial plant pathogens, those responsible for a substantial proportion of plant diseases worldwide, BPPDB provides a biologically meaningful and robust framework for large-scale pathogen identification, while maintaining an appropriate balance between taxonomic coverage and genomic quality. The nucleotide sequences of these pathogen genomes were then translated into protein sequences, followed by the construction of a protein database using DIAMOND67.

For the analysis of 1602 shotgun metagenomic datasets, we first quality-filtered the raw sequences using fastp (v0.20.1)68. The resulting high-quality reads were then assembled using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9)69, retaining contigs ≥1000 bp in length. We next used Prokka (v1.14.5)70 for gene prediction to identify open reading frames (ORFs, i.e., protein-coding genes). These ORFs were then clustered at a 95% similarity threshold using CD-HIT (v4.8.1)71 to generate a nonredundant gene catalogue. The read counts and Reads Per Million (RPM) values for each predicted nonredundant gene were quantified using Salmon (v1.6.0)72. Subsequently, for the taxonomic annotation of bacterial plant pathogens, we conducted an alignment of the translated protein sequences from our soil metagenomic datasets against our customized bacterial plant pathogens database using DIAMOND. This alignment was conducted under specific parameters: a maximum e-value threshold of 1 × 10−10, a minimum percent identity of over 80%, and baseline alignment length of at least 100 amino acids. The relative abundances of these bacterial phylotypes were expressed as RPM across all samples, allowing direct comparison among samples with differing sequencing depths and read yields73. In total, we identified 107 bacterial pathogen species, of which 32 were classified as globally dominant based on their high frequency of occurrence (present in at least 80% of samples) and high relative abundance (top 30%, with a mean relative abundance ≥ 0.1%) across global soils (Fig. 1).

Cross-validation of pathogen detection

Comparison with Kraken 2 database. We used a combination of Kraken 2 (v2.1.1)45 and Bracken (v2.6.2)74 tools based on the standard Kraken 2 database (as of March 2023) for read-level taxonomic annotation. We identified 98 bacterial phylotypes that overlapped with our custom plant pathogenic bacterial database, from a total of 10,014 bacterial phylotypes detected. The resulting table was rarefied to 100,000 reads per sample for alpha-diversity estimation and normalized using the cumulative sum scaling (CSS) method for beta-diversity analyses. Our results indicated a significant and positive correlation between the alpha and beta diversity of bacterial plant pathogens identified from our custom database workflow and those identified from the Kraken 2 database (alpha diversity: r = 0.25, P < 2.2 × 10−16; beta diversity: r = 0.39, P < 2.2 × 10−16) (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Furthermore, the relative abundances of most pathogens identified from our database showed significantly positive correlation with those identified by the Kraken 2 database (selected 15 pathogens, Pearson’s r = 0.25–0.67, P < 2.2e-16; Supplementary Fig. 7).

Comparison with the PHI-base. We mapped translated protein sequences from soil metagenomic datasets to the validated virulence genes curated in the Pathogen-Host Interactions database (PHI-base)75 using DIAMOND67 tool (e-value threshold of 1 × 10−10, percent identity > 80%). This method effectively identified a significant count of 2250 functional genes associated with bacterial pathogenicity and virulence. There were 23 annotated bacterial plant pathogen species overlapping between the PHI-base and our custom database. Among them, the relative abundance of most overlapping species showed a significant positive correlation between the two methods. In the analysis of individual species correlations, the top 12 most positively correlated species exhibited Pearson's r values ranging from 0.14 to 0.80, with P values ranging from 6.591e-09 to 2.2e-16 (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Comparison with CoverM quantification. We further performed an independent validation using an alternative read-alignment–based method, CoverM76, to assess the robustness of our pathogen abundance quantification. Estimates derived from Salmon and CoverM were highly consistent (Pearson’s r = 0.95; P < 2.2e-16 for total pathogen abundance), with many individual pathogens showed correlations exceeding 0.90 (P < 2.2e-16, Supplementary Fig. 9).

Comparison with expanded pathogen database. To address the potential overestimation arising from closely related nonpathogenic taxa, we expanded the bacterial plant pathogen database (BPPDB) to include 20 genomes from closely related nonpathogenic species. Pathogen abundance estimates derived from the expanded database were highly concordant with those obtained using the pathogen-only database (Supplementary Fig. 10, Pearson’s r > 0.98; P < 2.2e-16).

Comparison with quantitative PCR of specific dominant pathogens

From the standardized dataset, we retrieved DNA and performed pathogen-targeted qPCR assays to further validate the abundances of bacterial plant pathogens identified using our custom database. Specifically, the absolute abundance of Ralstonia solanacearum, the dominant plant pathogen identified in our data, was quantified via real-time qPCR employing the primers FliCF (GAACGCCAACGGTGCGAACT) and FliCR (GGCGGCCTTCAGGGAGGTC)77. For these assays, we randomly selected 70 samples spanning a range of biomes. The qPCR reactions were assembled in a total volume of 10 μl, comprising 1 μl of DNA template, 5 μl of 2 × PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 3.2 μl of PCR-grade water, and 0.4 μl of both the forward and reverse primers (10 μmol μl−1 each). The assays were carried out on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) using a three-step cycling program under the following parameters: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, succeeded by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s. Following qPCR, amplification specificity was assessed through melting curve analysis, with samples showing specific amplification retained for further analysis. The R. solanacearum isolate, purchased from the Department of Primary Industries (DPI, NSW), provided genomic DNA for generating standard curves in the qPCR assays. Standard curves were constructed using tenfold serial dilution. Comparison between the relative abundance of R. solanacearum from our custom database and the absolute abundance from qPCR revealed a significant positive correlation (rho = 0.28, P = 0.018; Supplementary Fig. 6c), supporting concordance in the detection and quantification of this pathogen.

Taxonomic microbial diversity and qPCR-based abundance

Soil microbial diversity was assessed using both metagenomic and amplicon sequencing data, with amplicon sequencing conducted only for the 200 standardized global soil samples. For the 1602 metagenomic samples, taxonomic profiles were generated using the Kraken 2 database. The standardized dataset comprising the 200 global soil samples included amplicon sequencing data generated on the Illumina MiSeq platform, consisting of 16S rRNA gene and 18S rRNA gene amplicon datasets (n = 200 each, 400 datasets in total). Bacterial and fungal communities were characterized by amplifying the 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA genes using the 341F/805R and Euk1391f/EukBr primer sets, respectively78,79. Samples were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp paired-end) at the Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) Facility (Western Sydney University, Australia). Microbial diversity in these 200 amplicon sequencing data was characterized using QIIME (v1.9.1)80, USEARCH (v10.0)81, and UNOISE382. Amplicon sequencing variants (ASVs) were identified at 100% sequence identity. Representative sequences of bacterial ASVs were annotated against the SILVA database83, and fungal ASVs were classified using both SILVA and the Protist Ribosomal Reference (PR2) databases84. ASVs that could not be confidently assigned to bacterial or fungal lineages were excluded from downstream analyses. The remaining reads resulted in 37,653 bacterial and 6083 fungal ASVs. For alpha-diversity estimation, the bacterial and fungal ASV tables were rarefied to 10,000 sequences per sample (resulting in the removal of one sample) and 4000 sequences per sample (removing 25 samples), respectively, as supported by the rarefaction curves shown in Supplementary Fig. 12. Despite the inherent limitations of short-read 18S data, the fungal dataset provides reliable comparative diversity patterns suitable for large-scale cross-biome analyses85. Microbial alpha-diversity was calculated in QIIME.

We also carried out 16S rRNA gene qPCR assay for the 200 standardized global dataset to measure the bacterial abundance in soil, using the primer set Eub338/Eub51886,87. The qPCR reactions were assembled in a total volume of 10 μl, comprising 1 μl of DNA template, 5 μl of 2 × PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 3.2 μl of PCR-grade water, and 0.4 μl of both the forward and reverse primers (10 μmol μl−1 each). The assays were carried out on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) using a three step cycling program under the following parameters: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 57 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 30 s. Standards were generated by cloning target sequences from pure cultures into the pUC57-Amp vector and were then transformed into chemically competent Escherichia coli cells. Standard curves were generated using a tenfold serial dilution of plasmid DNA.

Greenhouse experimental validation of soil microbial diversity–pathogen abundance relationships

To validate the observed correlation between soil microbial diversity and pathogen abundance, a controlled greenhouse experiment was conducted. Soil samples were collected from the Hawkesbury Forest Experiment (HFE) site at the Hawkesbury Institute for the Environment (HIE), Western Sydney University in December 2023. The soils were homogenized and sieved (2 mm mesh) prior to the experimental setup. A portion of the soil was temporarily stored at room temperature (~25 °C) and maintained at a 70% water-holding capacity for soil inoculation preparation. The other portion of the soil was sterilized by 50 kGy γ radiation using a 60Co source. Soil microcosms were established using a dilution-to-extinction approach47. An initial soil inoculum (100-fold diluted inoculum) was prepared by mixing 20 g unsterilized soil with 100 mL sterilized demineralized water using a blender for 2 min. Four microbial diversity gradients were established, including sterilized soils, 10−6 diluted soils (by adding 150 mL 106-fold diluted inoculum to 1.5 kg soil), 10−3 diluted soils (by adding 150 mL 103-fold diluted inoculum to 1.5 kg soil), and natural soil. The inoculated soils were incubated at room temperature for 6 months to facilitate microbial community assembly and stabilization.

The representative pathogen strains S. scabiei and R. solanacearum were obtained from the New South Wales Department of Primary Industries (DPI, Australia), originally isolated from diseased potato and tomato plants, respectively. Bacterial cultures were grown in nutrient broth medium under constant agitation (180 rpm) for 72 h at 28 °C. Cell densities were normalized to OD600 = 1.0 using sterilized water. Soil microcosms with defined microbial diversity gradients were established in 3-L polyethylene pots containing 1.5 kg of sterile soil. Following a 7-day pre-incubation period with daily watering to 60% water-holding capacity, each microcosm was inoculated with 15 mL bacterial suspension through soil drenching. Each treatment was replicated five times. The applied inoculum corresponded to an estimated initial concentration of ~10⁶ CFU per gram of soil, which lies within the commonly used range for soil-borne pathogen introduction assays in controlled experiments88. All experimental units were maintained in controlled greenhouse conditions (without host plants) with standardized irrigation protocols. Soil samples were collected at 3 days, 14 days, and 21 days post-inoculation and immediately stored at −20 °C for subsequent molecular analysis. At each time point, approximately 5 g of soil was collected from the top 0–5 cm soil layer using sterile corers, pooled from three subsites within the same pot to reduce spatial heterogeneity. Soil was also sampled prior to pathogen inoculation to assess background pathogen levels.

Total DNA was extracted from collected soil samples using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of DNA was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The absolute abundances of the inoculated pathogens were quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR). The absolute abundance of S. scabiei was quantified using primers Stx1a (GTGGACCGTGGAGCATCT) and Stx1b (CAGTTCGGCGTAACTCAGC)89. For R. solanacearum, primers FliCF (GAACGCCAACGGTGCGAACT) and FliCR (GGCGGCCTTCAGGGAGGTC)77 were used. Each qPCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 10 μl, comprising 1 μl of DNA template, 5 μl of 2 × PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 3.2 μl of PCR-grade water, and 0.4 μl of both the forward and reverse primers (10 μmol μl−1 each). The assays were carried out on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) using a three-step cycling program. For S. scabiei, the thermal profile consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 62 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. For R. solanacearum, the profile included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s. Amplification specificity was verified by melting curve analysis, and only samples showing specific amplification were retained for further analysis.

Profiling functional genes of metagenomic data and pathogen genomes

For the metagenomic data, the functional genes of the ORFs were annotated using the eggNOG-mapper v2.1.12, based on the eggNOG database (version 5.0)90. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthology (KO)46 were retrieved from the eggNOG output to generate KO profiles. Comparative genomic analysis was conducted to explore the genomic drivers determining the distribution of pathogens. Initially, we categorized our pathogens into dominant and nondominant groups and compared the functional gene differences between these two groups. Differential abundance analysis of functional genes between dominant and nondominant pathogens was performed using the generalized linear model (GLM) approach of EdgeR R package91, applying a trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalization method and a threshold of significance set at P < 0.001. To identify potential microbial metabolic traits contributing to pathogen suppression, we further annotated bacterial biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in our metagenomic dataset and examined their correlations with pathogen abundance. BGCs were predicted using antiSMASH (v7.1)55 on contigs larger than 3 kb. For precise product identification, the predicted BGCs were aligned to those in the MIBiG92 repository using BLASTP.

Correlation between pathogen abundance and their known global distribution

We downloaded reported global distribution data of some bacterial plant pathogens from previous studies and public databases to assess the potential correlation between the distribution of bacterial plant pathogens and their documented global occurrence. Distribution records for multiple bacterial plant pathogens were retrieved from the European and Mediterranean Plant Protection Organization (EPPO) Global Database in September 2024 (https://gd.eppo.int/)27. These data were then correlated with our data to further elucidate the links between the abundance of these pathogens and their known global distribution. We also extracted the global distribution data of R. solanacearum species complex from a previous study26, and performed a correlation analysis with our dataset.

Environmental factors and soil property measurements

For both the standardized global soil sampling and public databases (1602 samples in total), climatic information, including mean annual temperature (MAT), mean annual precipitation (MAP), diurnal temperature range (MDR), and seasonality of temperature (TSEA) and precipitation (PSEA), was extracted from the WorldClim 2.0 database93 (1 km2 resolution). Aridity index data were acquired from the global aridity index and potential evapotranspiration climate database (version 3)94. Assessment of plant cover (the fraction of ground covered by green vegetation) was obtained from Copernicus Global Land Service95. The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) was derived from Landsat 7 enhanced thematic mapper plus (ETM+) imagery (with a pixel resolution of 30 m × 30 m), employing methods previously described96. In the case of the standardized global dataset, soil properties were determined using standardized protocols. Soil properties, such as pH, texture (quantified by clay percentage), salinity, total organic nitrogen, and OC content were analysed using methodologies detailed in previous studies96–98.

Evaluation of ecological drivers of bacterial plant pathogens

Machine learning random forest analyses were then performed using the ‘randomForest’99 and ‘rfPermute’ R packages100 to explore the ecological factors (such as MAT, MAP, NDVI, soil properties, and microbial diversity) associated with the relative abundance of bacterial plant pathogens. Each RF model was trained with 9999 trees (ntree = 9999), using the default number of variables randomly sampled at each split. A fixed random seed was used to ensure reproducibility. Variable importance was quantified based on the mean decrease in accuracy (%IncMSE) metric obtained via the importance() function. The top-ranked predictors were exported for reproducibility. Statistical significance of predictor importance was further assessed using rfPermute, which applies permutation-based testing to derive p-values for each predictor’s contribution. All analyses were repeated across 32 pathogen variables using a standardized workflow to ensure consistency of variable importance ranking and model performance. Spearman correlation analyses were further conducted to evaluate the correlations between ecological factors and the relative abundance of all plant pathogens and the dominant plant pathogens using the R package ‘psych’101. All statistical analyses were conducted and visualized in R.

Structural equation modeling (SEM)102 was further used to identify the direct and indirect impacts of climatic factors, vegetation, soil properties, and microbial diversity on the relative abundance of dominant bacterial plant pathogens. The SEM constructs were developed using IBM SPSS Amos 21 software (Amos Development Corporation), following the methodology established in previous studies6,52. Model estimation was performed using maximum likelihood. Considering the non-normal distribution of some introduced variables, bootstrap tests were applied to evaluate the probability of path coefficients differing from zero. Model fit was evaluated through a chi-square test, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the Bollen–Stine bootstrap test. Our models showed a robust fit, as indicated by the following metrics: chi-square/df = 0.892, P = 0.345; RMSEA = 0.00, P = 0.821; and bootstrap P = 0.383.

Global mapping of bacterial plant pathogens

We next used ordinary least squares (OLS) regression to predict the current and future global distribution of bacterial plant pathogens. Predictors for our global maps were selected through machine learning using random forest regression, including climatic factors (MAP, MAT, MDR, and PSEA), elevation, slope, vegetation (NDVI), and soil properties (organic carbon, texture, and pH). Multicollinearity was assessed and highly correlated predictors were removed prior to model fitting. To assess the accuracy of the models, we quantified the discrepancy between the parameter space of the predictors and the original dataset103. Areas in the projection distant from the sample points were pinpointed using a masking criterion based on a P-value less than 0.01. We used the Mahalanobis distance to every point in the multidimensional space, which spans across ten dimensions as determined by the exogenous variables. This distance was measured in relation to both the center of the known distribution and the convex hull encompassing all data points used in the model. Environmental outliers were defined as locations whose Mahalanobis distance exceeded the 0.95 quantile of the χ² distribution (df = 10), indicating that their environmental profiles did not fall within the multivariate envelope of the sampled observations104.

To project the distribution of bacterial plant pathogens globally in future climates (2050), we reran our original model, using climatic and available land-use datasets following previous studies6,105–108. Land-use projections were provided by the Land-use Harmonized v2.0 datasets (http://luh.umd.edu/)106. Climatic variables were derived from the MIROC5 global climate model under the CMIP5 framework using Representative Concentration Pathways (RCP) 2.6, 6.0 and 8.5109. Key constant variables in these projections were elevation, slope, and soil properties. Climate datasets were obtained from ISIMIP2a and correspond to the year 2050105,110.

Map images in Fig. 4a–f and Supplementary Figs. 1, 5a–m were created in R using ggplot2111.
