Introduction

The drive for reparative justice—to restore or make right—is a critical part of the human condition. Today, from caste supremacy and chattel slavery to colonialism and racial capitalism,2 demands for reparations and redistributive justice are proliferating globally.37 The moral, legal, economic, historical, ecological and political evidence for such claims is abundant, as are the possible modes of redress. Across this diversity, however, reparative efforts are premised on the idea that equitable societies cannot come into being without redress of the physical and social harms that have accrued generationally as a consequence of historical and persisting injustices. Public and individual health are domains where these harms manifest prominently, and our collective efforts to prevent, treat and palliate suffering require both understanding and addressing historically deep social structures that perpetuate health inequities.

Our late Commissioner, physician-anthropologist Paul Farmer, argued that health inequities are not haphazard in distribution and effect.8 They are pathologies of power, produced through a complex interaction between human biology and the social world. One of the primary aims of this Supplement, which describes some of the findings of the Walter and Patricia Rodney Commission on Reparations and Distributive Justice, is to inspire those involved in improving health to understand how these dynamics operate, and how they can be transformed.9 Such inequities are a direct result of centuries of violently seized privilege and extraction. As such, reparations and distributive justice, as critical moral and material projects, are essential to achieving global health equity.

Recognition of the interactions underpinning health inequities requires a comprehensive awareness of not only the biological determinants of health but also the sociohistorical determinants. That is one of the goals of this series of articles. Akin to the ‘history of present illness’ that conceptually frames much of the diagnostic, therapeutic and prognostic contributions of clinical medicine, contemporary inequities in health and well-being arise from cumulative assaults that perpetrate harm from the cellular to the population level. Enforced material and social deprivations can insidiously damage the human body—as amply elaborated in the growing literature on structural violence.10 Building on Rudolf Virchow’s maxim that ‘politics is nothing more than medicine on a large scale’,11 this Supplement departs from a sole focus on the biomedical drivers of ill health, attending also to the pathologies of social and historical injustice.

Towards reparations and health equity

Reparations require cultural transformation, but they can be achieved by centring healing and redress as they emerge out of and in resistance to historically rooted patterns of depredation and human rights abuses.12 13 From this perspective, current health outcomes are linked to both contemporary and historical violence including, but not limited to, genocide, enslavement, extractivism, colonisation, racism, casteism and patriarchy.41421 The ultimate aim of this series of articles is to advance propositions for reparative justice: ways to build and support healing processes and material redress for deep historical harms.

What would that look like? A vast literature across multiple disciplinary traditions has explored what reparations could entail. One paradigm has included the notions of ‘acknowledgement, redress and closure for a grievous injustice’. Acknowledgement constitutes admission of culpability on the part of the perpetrator of the harms; redress, the act of restitution for the acts of the perpetrator (including medical and psychological care); and, closure—the victims’ agreement that the perpetrator has met the debt owed with no further claims forthcoming, unless old harms are renewed or new harms are inflicted.3 As a recent example of reparative approaches that meet these criteria, a Māori tribe secured an apology and millions of pounds in redress for atrocities committed by the British empire, including for its ‘indiscriminate’ killings and ‘massive’ alienation of tribal land.22 On the other hand, an intergovernmental decision by Germany in 2021 to pay Namibia €1.1bn over the Herero-Nama genocide did not meet these criteria for true reparation, as there was no agreement (closure) with descendants of the genocide.23 Other frameworks have stressed alternative components, including the guarantee of non-repetition and redress through international law to alter the very foundations from which the violence emerged initially: In 2025, the African Union elevated reparations as a continental priority through its official theme, ‘Justice for Africans and People of African Descent Through Reparations’.24 This agenda has also surfaced in national arenas; on 25 December 2025, Algeria’s parliament approved a law declaring France’s colonisation a crime and demanding a formal apology and reparations.25

Whatever the approach, it should be undertaken within the framework of healing. While harm cannot be undone for serious violations, new meaning and possibilities can be found, and this can create space for a wider range of efforts to move towards greater well-being for those who have experienced sociohistorically rooted harms. A successful programme of reparations entails not only acknowledgement and compensation for historical injustices, but also wholesale reckoning with how oppressive power dynamics—along the axes of class, race, gender, caste, ability, etc—continue to permeate society. And while there is a difference between liability, responsibility and acknowledgement—and strict liability models may be difficult in complex situations in which there may not be a direct tie between perpetrator and survivor—what must follow is a commitment to eradicate these dynamics rather than to simply transform one system of extraction and exploitation into another. Reparations and distributive justice necessitate that the parties who have enriched themselves from sociohistorical wrongs adhere to this commitment.

An invitation

The Walter and Patricia Rodney Commission on Reparations consists of a diverse cadre of scholars and activists, with multidisciplinary expertise and experience in a broad range of issues related to health and reparative justice. Over a period of 3 years, the Commission held several consultations and gathered evidence for ten case studies (five of which are published in BMJ Global Health9), representing a small subset of potential claims worldwide. Our hope is that clinicians, global health professionals, governments, multilateral agencies, non-governmental organisations and justice advocates—all relevant stakeholders—will embrace these propositions as paths to a healthier society. While some of this material may be new to readers, we hope that the arguments presented by this Commission will expand awareness of the socio-historical determinants of health inequities, so that large-scale structural interventions may be understood as necessary for their redress. To this end, we present the five case studies, ranging from generalised analyses of global phenomena including climate change,26 structural adjustment loan programmes27 and sexual violence28 to focused interrogations of country-specific harms, exemplified by the differing legacies of enslavement in the USA29 and nations of the Caribbean.30 For each case, we make concrete recommendations for how the proposals may be accomplished, based on the following five guiding principles:

Conclusions

We see the 21st century as an era of reparative struggle, extending the efforts of the 19th century’s abolition movements and the 20th century’s advancement of political decolonisation and human rights. While these movements have not fully dismantled the oppressive legacies of racist-colonial expropriation, we look to how such legacies relate to contemporary health disparities with the conviction that societal healing and reconciliation are possible through responding to past and present harms. Yet as we write, political momentum in multiple countries is moving in the opposite direction—towards the retrenchment of exclusionary nationalism, racialised hierarchy and punitive governance that aims to foreclose claims for reparations rather than advance them. This reversal does not diminish the foundational necessity of reparative justice; it clarifies the stakes. Deferring these demands will not cause the underlying harms to dissipate—it will deepen them, widen inequities and leave us with a far greater and more urgent burden of redress in the future.
