Profile of the BREATHE cohort for risk-based breast screening in Singapore
Authors: Peh Joo Ho, Jenny Liu, Su-Ann Goh, Alexis Jiaying Khng, Nur Khaliesah Mohamed Riza, Ying Jia Chew, Desmond Luan Seng Ong, Yi Wang, Fook Ming Hew, Pooja Jagmohan, Premilla Gopinathan Pillay, Yonggeng Goh, Han Yang Ong, Abrar Mohammed Alsunbul, Han Boon Oh, Christopher Hang Liang Keh, Chi Hui Chin, Sing Cheer Kwek, Zhi Peng Zhang, Swee Tian Quek, Sujith Wijerathne, Jingmei Li, Philip Choong Tsau Iau, Mikael Hartman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The BREAst screening Tailored for HEr (BREATHE) study was established to pilot a personalised, risk-based breast cancer screening programme for a multi-ethnic Asian population. Between October 2021 and December 2023, 4592 women aged 35–59 were enrolled (73% response rate). Follow-up from February 2022 to June 2024 included 4112 participants (8.6% loss to follow-up). Data collected encompassed demographics, lifestyle factors, reproductive risks, breast cancer awareness, screening behaviours, programme satisfaction, mammography outcomes (density and recall status), and genotype data. Above-average breast cancer risk increased with age: 2% in women aged 35–39, 31% in 40–49, and 42% in 50–59. Findings suggests that women valued the knowledge of their breast cancer risk and were motivated to attend mammography. The BREATHE study confirms the feasibility and acceptability of a personalised risk-based screening programme in a multi-ethnic Asian population, highlighting the value of tailored protocols to improve early detection and outcomes. Further details are available in the study protocol and online at https://blog.nus.edu.sg/breathe/.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19205-1.

Introduction

The BREAst screening Tailored for HEr study (BREATHE) was set up to pilot risk-based breast cancer screening in Singapore’s population1. BREATHE’s objectives are to (1) test the feasibility and acceptability of risk-based screening, (2) identify risk factors that can lead to recommendation for breast cancer screening in the younger (aged < 50 years) population, and (3) conduct cost-effectiveness studies on the implementation of a risk-based screening paradigm for breast cancer. In the long term, BREATHE aims to study mammography screening patterns in individuals predicted at different risk levels (above average, average, below average) by their birth cohort and to establish optimal screening recommendations (e.g. intervals or starting age) according to predicted risk.

Mammographic screening programmes in most high-income countries, e.g. the UK, US, and Canada, are based on the decades-old trials conducted in the 1990s and early 2000s2–5. More recent reports have raised concerns about the efficacy of age-based mammography screening programmes, citing potential overdiagnosis and smaller breast cancer mortality reduction in the screening arm6–8. Risk-based screening may offer a more targeted approach than the current age-based paradigm by focusing on individuals at higher risk of developing breast cancer, potentially reducing overdiagnosis and improving the overall effectiveness of early detection. The European Collaborative on Personalized Early Detection and Prevention of Breast Cancer (ENVISION) consensus statement (2020) outlines the key elements for effective risk-based mammography9. It emphasises that mammography should focus on identifying and treating breast cancer with adverse consequences, such as those with poor prognosis, while avoiding the treatment of indolent cancers that people may die with but not die of9.

Setting up risk-based breast cancer screening in Asia is important for several reasons. Breast cancer mortality has risen among Asian women, while it has declined in North America, Western Europe, and Oceania10. This increase is linked to a higher prevalence of breast cancer risk factors in Asia, such as lower parity, delayed childbirth, and increased obesity, which have become more common with economic development in the region11. However, since the nationwide mammography screening programmes, the screening rates in most Asian countries (e.g. Japan, Singapore, and Taiwan) have historically remained below those in Europe countries12. Risk-based screening can incentivise and enhance participation by targeting those at higher risk who may benefit most from regular screening. Besides accommodating diverse risk profiles, risk-based breast cancer screening can optimise healthcare resources in a region facing rising mortality rates13–15. This approach potentially enhances early detection, improves screening efficiency, reduces economic costs, and aligns with culturally sensitive practices to improve healthcare outcomes16.

No country has yet fully adopted a national risk-stratified breast cancer screening programme. However, various approaches to risk assessment and stratification are being evaluated in several European countries (e.g., My PeBS, BC-Predict, and KARMA), the United States (the WISDOM study), and Canada (PERSPECTIVE I&I)17–22. Additionally, the feasibility and acceptability of risk-based screening are being assessed in other regions beyond Europe, the US, and Canada19,23–27. BREATHE is designed to explore the potential of channelling resources to identify, inform and encourage high-risk women to attend routine screening. Furthermore, BREATHE will be a resource that can address the gaps in risk estimation, refining and calibrating breast cancer risk calculation tools in a multi-ethnic Asian country28. BREATHE aims to contextualise a personalised risk-based screening approach in an Asian setting. This cohort profile reports on the demographic, predicted and perceived breast cancer risk of the BREATHE population. In addition, we report the prospective and retrospective acceptability and expressed willingness to screen based on risk recommendations.

Methods

Ethics approval

Informed consent was obtained by trained study coordinators in the participant’s preferred language (English, Chinese or Malay). Ethics approval was obtained from the National Healthcare Group Domain-Specific Review Board (ref no. ​​2020/01327, on 7 June 2021) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Singapore’s Human Biomedical Research Act. Individuals who withdrew consent before 1 August 2024 (n = 74) were excluded from all analyses presented in this study. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Statistical analyses

Differences in demographic variables, breast cancer risk factors, and predicted breast cancer risk between participants who completed follow-up and those loss-to-follow-up were assessed with univariate analysis (Chi-squared test for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables). All analysis was done using R version 4.2.2.

BREATHE protocol in brief

The protocol for recruitment and follow-ups of (BREATHE) has been published1. In brief, women without a diagnosis of breast cancer were invited to participate in BREATHE between October 2021 and December 2023 (Fig. 1). Women aged 35 to 59, without a cancer diagnosis, cognitive impairment, or pregnancy, were invited to participate. Recruitment targeted attendees of polyclinics (i.e. government-subsidized clinics that offer services that are offered by General Practitioners, selected specialist services, and health screening), medical centres, and hospitals within the National University Health System (NUHS), one of Singapore’s three public healthcare clusters serving the western region. Initially, recruitment occurred at two polyclinics (Bukit Batok and Choa Chu Kang) and two hospitals (Ng Teng Fong General Hospital and National University Hospital). In August 2022, the cohort expanded to include Alexandra Hospital and Jurong Medical Centre. These recruitment locations are collectively referred to as clinics.

The calculation of five-year absolute risk depends on the individual’s age at enrolment. Among Asians, breast cancer incidence is low before age 40, increases rapidly between ages 40 and 50, and plateaus after age 5029. A woman in the 80th percentile of the polygenic risk score (PRS) distribution has a lower absolute risk at younger ages (below age 40) but a rapidly increasing risk as she ages. To capture genetic information that could be relevant for decision-making on screening in the younger age group where the traditional reproductive risk factors may be incomplete, BREATHE’s targeted recruitment follows: 20% aged 35–39, 40% aged 40–49, and 40% aged 50–59. The stratification by age 40–49 and 50–59 references Singapore’s current national breast cancer screening guidelines, which recommend biennial mammograms for women aged 50–69, with screening for those aged 40–49 advised after consulting their healthcare providers. Additionally, the BreastScreen Singapore programem offers subsidised mammogram screenings for eligible women in the target age group (i.e. aged 40–69)30.

Risk classification criteria were based on non-genetic risk (i.e. the Gail model31), genetic risk32 and information from recent (maximum of 1 year before enrolment) mammographic screening. Participants were initially classified as average risk and reclassified as above or below average risk based on specific criteria. Above average risk was defined as meeting at least one of the following: (1) five-year absolute risk prediction by polygenic risk score (PRS) > 3%25, (2) five-year absolute risk prediction by the Gail model (GAIL) > 1.3%, (3) high mammographic density (BIRAD 4), or (4) recall for additional mammography tests. Women under 50 who did not meet high-risk criteria were classified as below average risk if both PRS and GAIL scores were < 1.3%. The five-year absolute risk of developing breast cancer for an average 50-year-old Caucasian woman is approximately 1.3%33. The PRS and the Gail model differ in their risk estimation34. To minimise the risk of misclassification of individuals to the below average risk group, where we are uncertain if they would stop screening, we required both absolute risk estimates from PRS and the Gail model to be below 1.3%.

Changes in BREATHE’s protocol

The current risk report provides only overall risk classification (above average, average, below average) without detailing specific risk factors. Verbal feedback from participants classified as above average risk during their first follow-up visit indicated interest in understanding the factors behind their classification. To address this, nine questions on risk report preferences were added to the first follow-up survey (Annex A). Version 2 of the survey was implemented from 29 July 2022.

Study population

Of the 6278 women screened, 944 declined participation, and 742 were ineligible (Fig. 1). A total of 4592 women enrolled in BREATHE. By 31 July 2024, 74 had withdrawn consent, and 17 were diagnosed with breast cancer within six months of enrolment (Fig. 2). The analytical dataset included 4501 participants, excluding those who withdrew consent before this date. Seventy-five per cent joined after being invited by study coordinators at participating clinics, while others responded to advertisements (11%), referrals from relatives or friends (8%), or other sources (6%).

Participant’s characteristics of cohort at enrolment (n = 4501)

A total of 4501 participants were enrolled, comprising 77% Chinese, 11% Malay, 7% Indian, and 5% other ethnicities (Table 1). The median age was 48 years (IQR 42–53), with 15% aged 35–39, 44% aged 40–49, and 41% aged 50–59 (Table 1). Risk stratification was conducted using the Gail model. Participants provided demographic, lifestyle, and breast cancer risk factor data, including height, weight, smoking status, alcohol consumption, menstruation status, and number of children. Buccal swab samples were collected to extract DNA for genotyping. The resulting genotyping data was used to calculate the participant’s PRS and estimate the five-year absolute risk based on the PRS32. Details are published in the protocol1. The mean PRS of 0.082 (standard deviation 0.570) was statistically significantly different from the Singapore Multi-ethnic Cohort II (MEC) which was used as our reference population (mean = 0.130, standard deviation = 0.565, n = 4098, Welch Modified Two-Sample t-Test p-value = 9.9e−5)34. BREATHE is likely to underestimate the five-year absolute risk. Five-year absolute risk (dependent on current age) by PRS was 0.410% (0.259 to 0.616), 0.697% (0.473 to 1.061), and 0.887% (0.607 to 1.325) for age groups 35 to 39, 40 to 49 and 50 to 59 years, respectively (Table 1).

Self-reported data on age at menarche (< 12, 12–14, ≥ 14 years), age at first live birth (< 20, 20–24, 25–29, ≥ 30, nulliparous), number of benign breast biopsies, presence of atypical hyperplasia (yes, no), and first-degree family history of breast cancer (mother, sisters, or daughters) were used to estimate breast cancer risk via the Gail model. Breast biopsy and atypical hyperplasia data were updated for participants who underwent mammograms after enrolment. The Gail model’s five-year absolute risk was calculated using Asian-American weights (“Asian.AABCS,” BCRA package in R) and adjusted for Singapore’s incidence and mortality rates to align PRS and Gail criteria. Absolute risks were 0.411 (0.333–0.525) for ages 35–39, 0.856 (0.682–1.109) for ages 40–49, and 1.117 (0.936–1.481) for ages 50–59 (Table 1).

Mammography screening data was unavailable for 35% of participants aged 40–49 and 50–59, as most had not undergone screening in the past year and did not plan to do so in the next six months. Positive recall rates were higher in the 40–49 age group (13%) compared to the 50–59 age group (8%) (Table 1).

Survey frequency

By 26 June 2024, the first follow-up was completed, involving an in-person explanation of the risk report and a questionnaire to assess participants’ understanding (Fig. 1). We contacted 4531 individuals (excluding 61 who withdrew soon after enrolment), but 389 (8.6% of 4501) could not be reached despite at least three attempts and were considered lost to follow-up. These participants were more likely to be of Malay ethnicity, have lower education, live in smaller public housing, have lower incomes, and perceive themselves at lower breast cancer risk (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 2). Seventeen participants were diagnosed with breast cancer within six months of recruitment, and 13 withdrew consent upon contact. A total of 4112 individuals completed the follow-up 3–9 months post-enrolment (median [IQR]: 6.2 [5.1–7.6] months).

Reports of breast cancer diagnosis (n = 17)

Breast cancer status was obtained when participants were contacted to schedule a return of report, medical records were verified to determine cancer status and obtain cancer characteristics. Additionally, in cases where participants had further examinations due to abnormal findings on their recent mammogram, medical records were verified for breast cancer diagnosis. Seventeen participants (0.4% of 4518 enrolled) were identified as having prevalent breast cancer, defined as a diagnosis within 6 months of enrolment. Of these, 15 met the BREATHE’s criteria for above-average risk and 2 were classified as below average risk.

Twelve patients were both recalled for abnormal mammography findings (i.e. true positives) and had five-year absolute risk by the Gail model ≥ 1.3%, with two who had high breast density (BIRAD density 4). One patient, aged 41, had no other indicator of above-average risk, except having been recalled. The only indicator of above-average risk for two patients (aged 38 and 43) was a 5-year absolute risk by the Gail model ≥ 1.3%, these patients did not share their recall status. Eight of the 15 individuals had a five-year absolute risk ≥ 1.3% by PRS. With the exception of the youngest participant, who perceived herself to be at high risk of developing breast cancer, participants perceived themselves to be at average (n = 10) or low (n = 4) risk at enrolment. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Both breast cancer cases (aged 48 and 49) identified as below average risk of Chinese ethnicity, were without a family history of breast cancer, and had a < 1.3% five-year absolute risk by the Gail model and PRS. They did not attend mammography screening at enrolment but subsequently attended screening due to breast symptoms. One perceived herself to be at high risk of breast cancer and the other at low risk.

Results

Prospective acceptability of risk-based breast cancer screening

To assess prospective acceptability, we analysed data from 4418 individuals invited by BREATHE’s study coordinators (includes 944 who declined and 3474 who enrolled when approached by our research team; here we excluded individuals who signed-up through other platforms as we are not able to assess the rejection rates) (Fig. 1), with 79% agreeing to participate. Among 3383 participants who did not withdraw or receive a breast cancer diagnosis within six months, the main reason (question 1 of the recruitment experience questionnaire1) for joining BREATHE (reported by 60%, n = 2022) was to learn about their breast cancer risk, while 4% aimed to adjust their screening practices if identified as above average risk.

Retrospective acceptability, importance and impact on motivation of risk stratification (n = 4112)

Four questions, rated on a scale of 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree), were analysed to assess opinions on disease risk classification at first follow-up: (1) importance of knowing risk classification, (2) motivation to screen based on risk, (3) acceptability of genetic risk classification for other conditions, and (4) impact of learning about risk on daily activities.

Ninety-one percent of participants valued knowing their risk classification, and 89% felt it motivated them to attend screening. Eighty-six percent expressed interest in genetic risk classification for other conditions, and despite 22% reporting that learning about their risk affected daily tasks, 92% still wanted genetic risk information for other conditions (Fig. 3).

Birth cohort trends in breast cancer risk and health-related quality of life (n = 4501)

Older participants tended to have reproductive risk factors associated with lower breast cancer risk, including a later age at menarche (≥ 14 years: 27% in ages 50–59 vs. 21% in 40–49 and 20% in 35–39) and more children (≥ 2: 69% in 50–59 vs. 58% in 40–49 and 44% in 35–39). They were also younger at their first live birth (< 30 years: 56% in 50–59 vs. 43% in 40–49 and 33% in 35–39). However, they were more likely to report a history of benign breast disease (16% in 50–59 vs. 14% in 40–49 and 5% in 35–39) (Fig. 4; Table 1).

Fig. 4Trends in reproductive risk factors across the age groups.

Trends in reproductive risk factors across the age groups.

Regarding lifestyle, fewer than 1% reported high physical activity levels, with ~ 17% reporting moderate levels. Older participants reported the lowest physical activity. Most participants (54%) had a healthy BMI (18.5–25 kg/m2), with the youngest group having the highest obesity rates (19% with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²). Smoking and alcohol use were rare (4% and 15%, respectively), with younger participants having higher proportions of both (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Participants reported no differences in overall health during the week before enrolment, but older participants had a higher quality of life, with 41% rating it 6 or 7, compared to 35% and 34% in the 40–49 and 35–39 age groups, respectively (Supplementary Table 2).

Risk classification of participants who completed the first follow-up (n = 4112)

Participants at above average breast cancer risk were predominantly older. Overall, 31% met at least one high-risk criterion: 21% (n = 883) had a 5-year absolute risk ≥ 1.3% (Gail model), 9% (n = 366) had a false-positive recall, 6% (n = 237) had high mammographic density, and 1% (n = 31) had a 5-year absolute risk ≥ 3% (PRS) (Fig. 5A). Few participants aged 35–39 (2%) were identified as above average risk, compared to 31% of those aged 40–49 and 42% of those aged 50–59. This increase was primarily driven by higher Gail model risk scores in older participants (Fig. 5B–D).

Discussion

As an assessment of the readiness of Singapore’s female population to adopt risk-based mammography screening, BREATHE has shown presence of interest for risk information and in using it to inform their health behaviour. At recruitment, BREATHE found that a large proportion (~ 60%) of women were curious about their breast cancer risk, whereby a small proportion (4%) having entered BREATHE with the main aim of modifying their screening practices if above average risk. When our participants learnt of their risk at follow-up, ~ 90% valued this knowledge and were motivated to screen.

Screening recommendations for individuals receiving below average prediction have been a source of debate35. Past studies highlighted concerns of potential resistance to decreasing the frequency of screening35,36 and of individuals discontinuing screening following below average risk prediction37with some further demonstrating evidence supporting adjusting the age at which screening start, instead of the frequency of screening14,38. In BREATHE, we demonstrated that maintaining the same recommendations for below average risk and average risk as a viable measure to increase acceptability of screening recommendations particularly in a setting whereby baseline screening uptake is suboptimal12. Changes to screening recommendations in interval of age of starting screening can be assessed and implemented progressively following further evaluation of breast cancer incidence in BREATHE cohort and cost-effectiveness of varying age to start screening by risk level32.

The expected strengths and limitations were detailed in the study protocol1. BREATHE has a comprehensive record of known breast cancer genetic and non-genetic risk factors for the three major ethnicities (Chinese, Malay, Indian) in Singapore, that is comparable to our existing breast cancer cohort (The Singapore Breast Cancer Cohort39). The comparability offers an opportunity for BREATHE to validate or contrast findings in a prospective setting. In the past decades, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and reproductive factors have changed rapidly across the generations in Singapore. BREATHE took a snapshot of women currently aged 35 to 59 and will follow them actively for their next mammography screening (two years post-enrolment) and passively via linkage to the Singapore Disease Registries. A unique feature of BREATHE is its focus on understanding participants’ reactions to risk-based screening, which includes genetic risk factors. This is especially novel in the Asian cultural context, where genetic testing and personalised medicine are still emerging. It will provide insights into how women from different ethnic and cultural backgrounds respond to this form of screening.

A weakness of BREATHE is the selection bias; our participants were likely more health-conscious and likely to use the health system. This can be attributed to (1) the use of email advertisement and referrals, where participants interested in and accept risk assessment self-selected themselves into the study, and (2) to recruitment at existing mammography providers and wellness centres, where participants were less likely to come from less-deprived backgrounds40. We expected BREATHE’s participants to be aware of breast cancer, as 96% of Singapore’s population is aware of mammography screening41. However, our participants were more conscious of attending breast cancer screening than the general population, as reported by the national survey, highlighting a selection bias41. When we studied participants’ acceptance of risk-based screening, to reduce the impact of selection bias, we excluded self-selected individuals (i.e. participants not approached by our study team and thus could not have rejected enrolment). However, the high interest in understanding personal disease risk in our study population, who agreed to be part of the research, is likely an overestimation of the interest level in the general population. Engaging general practitioners to assist in enrolling women for mammography screening will improve the socio-economic diversity of our participants40.

Methodology difference in the derivation of PRS of BREATHE as compared to the reference population may have led to the difference in the mean of the PRS distributions. The reference population used the OncoArray-500 K v1.0 for genotyping while BREATHE used the Global Screening Array v3.0. With the different variants available on the arrays, there may be differences in the imputed variants. However, the standard deviation is similar to the reference population signalling the need for changes in our implementation strategy to include calibration before applying the risk stratification criteria.

Participants valued the knowledge of their breast cancer risk. BREATHE achieved the goal of overcoming a barrier to screening—the lack of perceived risk42. In an earlier study using data from BREATHE, we showed that perceived risk was modifiable by knowledge of prediction risk43. However, it is premature to conclude that the impact of personalised risk on the actual uptake of routine mammography. Additionally, our participants were provided support to reduce self-efficacy barriers to screening, including scheduling mammography sessions. Future work will assess the impact of risk stratification on routine screening, where participants will schedule and attend screening without the assistance of the study team.
