Validation of a self-report measure for diagnostic screening and assessment in complex regional pain syndrome
Authors: Stephen Bruehl, Alexandra Sideris, Tina Chen, Semih Gungor, Storm Horine, Vladimir Kramskiy, Frederic T. Billings, Sara Anderson, R. Norman Harden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score-Self Report is the first validated self-report measure of complex regional pain syndrome symptoms; it is accurate for tracking complex regional pain syndrome symptoms and diagnostic screening.

1. Introduction

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a chronic pain condition characterized by allodynia/hyperalgesia, edema, vasomotor and sudomotor changes, and motor and trophic changes.3,4 It usually begins regionally in a single limb, and inflammatory, autonomic, and central mechanisms all appear to contribute.3,4 There are currently no treatments for CRPS proven effective in large scale clinical trials.16

Complex regional pain syndrome is considered an orphan condition,10 and acquiring adequate sample sizes for definitive clinical trials is challenging. One barrier to recruitment into CRPS trials is the need to conduct in-person evaluations to determine CRPS diagnostic status. Unlike conditions such as chronic low back pain, which can be identified with high accuracy based on questions that can be administered remotely (ie, pain intensity, location, frequency, and impact),8 diagnosing CRPS using the 2012 International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) criteria (ie, the Budapest criteria) requires that a history and physical examination be conducted. This situation is similar to that formerly encountered in fibromyalgia studies before 2010 when diagnosis was based on clinical tender point evaluation, a barrier ultimately overcome by publication of the 2010 self-report diagnostic screening criteria that could be administered remotely.25

The present study sought to develop a self-report measure suitable for remote delivery to assess CRPS-related symptoms and determine (1) its accuracy as an initial screening tool for subsequent clinic-based CRPS diagnosis and (2) evidence for its validity (eg, sensitivity to change). Secondary analysis of a previously described dataset3,4 of patients with total knee arthroplasty (TKA) provided an initial opportunity to evaluate the proposed self-report measure (≈12% of patients meet CRPS criteria at 6 months post-TKA5,13,19). Given debate over whether CRPS post-TKA is the same clinical entity seen in the typical chronic pain setting,22 we subsequently validated the measure prospectively in a traditional pain clinic sample.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate correspondence between the proposed self-report measure (CRPS Severity Score-Self Report; CSS-SR) and an existing validated measure of CRPS signs and symptoms completed by clinicians based on history and physical examination (CSS-Clinician version).12,15 We also tested the concurrent validity of the CSS-SR and evaluated its accuracy as a diagnostic screener relative to criterion-based CRPS diagnosis (Budapest criteria).11

2. Methods

The 2 samples in which the CSS-SR was validated and the methods used in each are summarized separately below.

2.1. Sample 1: postsurgical complex regional pain syndrome

2.1.1. Design

The postsurgical CRPS study employed a longitudinal design including assessment of CRPS-related outcomes as well as pain and psychosocial measures at 6 weeks and 6 months post-TKA. The current dataset was part of a larger project detailed elsewhere.7 All procedures were approved by the relevant Institutional Review Board.

2.1.2. Participants

Participants were individuals of both sexes, age ≥55, scheduled to undergo unilateral TKA for treatment of degenerative knee joint changes related to chronic osteoarthritis. Participants were recruited from the orthopedic practice at a large tertiary care hospital. Recruitment was conducted in-person at required preoperative TKA education sessions by the research coordinator or via telephone calls to patients scheduled to undergo TKA (during the COVID-19 pandemic). Interested individuals were provided with study information, and if interested, were screened for eligibility and provided written informed consent. All participants were enrolled between August 2017 and June 2021. The targeted sample size (n = 120) for the parent study was determined based on a power analysis indicating that it would lead to >0.80 power to detect associations between the primary oxidative stress measure (F2-isoprostanes) and continuous pain and CRPS outcomes.

Inclusion criteria for sample 1 were (1) intact cognitive status and ability to provide informed consent (based on a Mini Mental State Examination score ≥24),9 (2) ability to read and write in English sufficiently to complete study questionnaires, (3) age ≥55 years, (4) undergoing unilateral TKA, and (5) diagnosis of osteoarthritis. Exclusion criteria were (1) a diagnosis of CRPS pre-TKA; (2) undergoing TKA revision; (3) presence of lower extremity vascular disease, inflammatory or autoimmune disorders, or malignancy; and (4) presence of other conditions that the orthopedic surgeons believed would make participation unsafe. Participants received $200 for completing the full study.

The initial pool of sample 1 participants was n = 985, and of these, n = 303 expressed interest in the study. Of these, n = 120 consented, with n = 7 subsequently failing further screening (cognitive dysfunction, medical exclusions). The final sample was n = 113 (no participants withdrew or were lost to follow-up), with the final analyzed sample including all participants (n = 112) with CSS-SR data and Budapest diagnostic information at either 6 weeks or 6 months post-TKA. 2.1.3. Psychometric measures

2.1.3.1. Pain

A 0 to 10 numeric rating scale anchored with “no pain” and “worst possible pain” was used to separately rate the worst and average knee pain intensity in the 24 hours preceding each assessment.20

2.1.3.2. Function

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Short Form v1.0—Pain Interference 8a scale (PROMIS Interference) was used to assess pain-related life interference over the week before each assessment.1,23

2.1.4. Psychosocial status

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression scale (20 item version), a widely used measure of depressive symptoms validated for use in older populations.2,17 The trait form of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) was used to measure tendency to experience anxiety symptoms.24

2.1.5. Sleep disturbance

Sleep disturbance was assessed using the PROMIS Sleep Disturbance—Short Form 8a, an instrument assessing sleep quality, sleep depth, and restoration associated with sleep over the preceding week.26

2.1.6. Assessment of complex regional pain syndrome

To ensure standardized assessment of CRPS signs and symptoms, a CRPS checklist like that used in our past research was used to guide the history and physical examination.6,11,14 This checklist presented a complete list of CRPS diagnostic signs and symptoms per the 2012 IASP criteria (ie, Budapest criteria),11 with instructions provided for assessment. All evaluations were conducted by a single research coordinator (S.A.) who was trained by a neurologist with CRPS expertise (R.N.H.).11 Objective temperature asymmetry between the affected and unaffected sides was assessed via infrared thermometry conducted 8 cm towards midline at the level of the top and bottom of the incision (2 readings at each location on each leg, all averaged), with an average bilateral asymmetry of ≥1.5°C indicating asymmetric temperature.

The previously validated clinician version of the CRPS Severity Score (CSS-Clinician) was scored based on the clinical information obtained during the standardized CRPS evaluation as in prior work.15 The CSS-Clinician total score was calculated by summing the responses indicating presence/absence (coded 1/0) of each sign and symptom as recorded on the CRPS checklist. This measure provides a continuous index of extent of CRPS diagnostic signs and symptoms. The potential range of CSS-Clinician scores was 0 to 16. Validity of the CSS-Clinician has been demonstrated in 2 prior studies.12,15

Proposed content of the self-report version of the CSS (CSS-SR) was rationally developed before initiating the postoperative CRPS study. Lay language items were created that paralleled the diagnostic symptoms assessed in the clinician-obtained history that provides the basis for the symptom component of the CSS-Clinician. Thirteen questions in yes/no (presence/absence) checklist format assessed the following symptoms: more prolonged or severe pain than expected, allodynia/hyperalgesia, skin temperature and color changes, edema, sweating changes, trophic changes (skin, hair, nails), tremor, dystonia, weakness, and range of motion limitations. Scoring of the CSS-SR paralleled the CSS-Clinician, with items coded as 1 for “Yes” and 0 for “No,” with responses summed to create a total CSS-SR score (range: 0–13). For use in the post-TKA sample only, CSS-SR items referred specifically to the “affected knee” to ensure participants described symptoms in the same region on which the CSS-Clinician was focused. The final CSS-SR measure used in the pain clinic validation sample below (Fig. 1) employed broader wording applicable to all potential CRPS populations (“affected limb”).

Content of the final version of the CSS-SR. CSS-SR, self-report version of the Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score.

As a criterion for determining accuracy of the CSS-SR for diagnostic screening, dichotomous CRPS diagnosis at 6-week and 6-month follow-up was determined for each participant based upon meeting 2012 IASP/Budapest symptom and sign criteria (ie, ≥3 of 4 symptom categories positive and ≥2 of 4 sign categories positive).11 “Pain disproportionate to the inciting event” was not explicitly required for diagnostic categorization in the postoperative CRPS study. At the 6-week follow-up, disproportionate pain and CRPS features might be attributable to delayed healing that will resolve; therefore, CRPS diagnosis at 6 weeks was considered only provisional. We further assumed that any significant ongoing pain 6 months post-TKA would be considered disproportionate given that TKA had corrected the osteoarthritic knee joint and subacute healing should be largely resolved. Using the above approach, 63.3% of participants met criteria for provisional CRPS diagnosis at 6-week follow-up, with 12.7% meeting CRPS diagnostic criteria at 6-month follow-up (see Bruehl et al.5).

2.1.7. Procedure

Full study procedures are detailed elsewhere.5,7 Procedures directly related to this study included in-home assessments conducted by an experienced research coordinator. Preoperative assessment was conducted ≈3 days preoperatively, with postoperative assessments at 6 weeks (6- to 8-week window) and 6 months (24- to 28-week window) post-TKA. On each visit, participants completed the CSS-SR, the pain and psychosocial measures, and other assessments detailed elsewhere.5,7 Then, a formal CRPS-focused history and physical examination was conducted, guided by the CRPS checklist and standardized procedures described above.

2.2. Sample 2: chronic pain clinic

2.2.1. Design

The pain clinic validation study was prospective and included completion of the CSS-SR and pain ratings by participants (self-report) followed by independent clinical evaluation of CRPS-related signs and symptoms by a study physician. All procedures in the pain clinic validation study were IRB-approved.

2.2.2. Participants

Participants (both sexes) were enrolled between February 2025 and September 2025 at a single high-volume, urban outpatient pain management clinic. Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥18 years and (2) either suspected or diagnosed unilateral CRPS type I/II affecting the upper or lower extremities or non-CRPS extremity pain. Exclusion criteria were (1) bilateral CRPS, (2) CRPS triggered by TKA, and (3) inability to comprehend the study protocol or questionnaires due to cognitive deficits or limited English language skills. The final sample was primarily female (70.0%), White (75.0%), and of non-Hispanic ethnicity (81.3%). Of the 34 patients in the sample later determined to meet Budapest CRPS criteria, 88.9% were classified as CRPS-Type I. The majority reflected chronic CRPS (58.8% pain duration >12 months), although nearly one-quarter reported very acute CRPS (23.5% pain duration ≤3 months). Of those later classified as non-CRPS (eg, ulnar neuropathy, osteoarthritis, tenosynovitis, bursitis), pain duration was similar, with the highest percentage reporting pain duration >12 months (50.0%).

2.2.3. Psychometric measures

Beyond the CSS-SR and CSS-Clinician described above, the only additional measure obtained in this study was a 0 to 10 numeric rating scale measure of pain intensity (anchored with “no pain” and “worst possible pain”). Participants separately rated their worst and average pain intensity in the 24 hours preceding assessment.

2.2.4. Procedure

Potentially qualifying participants were identified before their clinic visit using customized screening reports generated from the electronic health record (Epic) indicating upcoming office visits or interventional procedures. On the day of the visit, patients were approached in the examination room by study staff before seeing the physician. After obtaining written informed consent, patients were given a paper copy of the CSS-SR to complete. The completed CSS-SR was collected before physicians entered the room. A clinical evaluation of the patient was then completed by a study physician with expertise in CRPS diagnosis who was blinded to the patient's CSS-SR responses. During the evaluation, physicians conducted a semi-structured history and physical examination like that used in the post-TKA CRPS study described above. The study physician then used evaluation results to complete the CSS-Clinician, which was collected by study staff before any intervention. Due to logistical constraints, 7 patients completed the CSS-SR after the physician evaluation had been conducted (4 CRPS, 3 non-CRPS).

2.2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Participant demographics and descriptive statistics for pain and psychosocial measures were summarized using percentages or means (SD); these are presented in Table 1 (post-TKA CRPS sample) or text (pain clinic validation sample). Internal consistency reliability of CSS-SR items was assessed using Cronbach alpha. Nonparametric Spearman correlations were used to evaluate concurrent validity of the CSS-SR by comparing associations of the CSS-SR vs the CSS-Clinician with contemporaneous pain, function, and psychosocial measures. Concordance between CSS-Clinician and CSS-SR scores was assessed using intraclass correlations (ICCs; two-way random effects model; potential range = −1.00 to 1.00). Strength of agreement based on ICCs was evaluated based on published recommendations21: <0.50 = poor, 0.50 to 0.75 = moderate, 0.76 to 0.90 = good, and >0.90 = excellent. To evaluate sensitivity to change (post-TKA sample only), ICCs were used to evaluate concordance between changes in CSS-Clinician and CSS-SR values from 6-week to 6-month post-TKA follow-up. Area under the curve (AUC) values based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses are presented to index overall accuracy of the CSS-SR in discriminating between CRPS and non-CRPS cases. Cut scores on the self-report version of the Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score to optimize its accuracy for predicting subsequent criterion-based CRPS diagnosis were identified based on results from the pain clinic sample to maximize generalizability to the typical pain clinic CRPS population. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive power (PPP) and negative predictive power (NPP), and overall accuracy of potential cutoffs were determined using an online calculator (https://www.medcalc.org/en/calc/diagnostic_test.php). All analyses used the maximum number of cases available with a two-sided P < 0.05 criterion for statistical significance.

3. Results

3.1. Descriptive statistics for the self-report version of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score

In the post-TKA sample, mean (SD) for the CSS-SR at 6 months (a point at which responses are unlikely to be confounded by acute healing) was 2.0 (2.06). This low mean value is consistent with the 12.7% CRPS prevalence at 6 months post-TKA. Internal consistency reliability of the 13 CSS-SR items at 6-month follow-up was acceptable (alpha = 0.71) and consistent with the measure capturing a single underlying construct (ie, CRPS symptom severity).

In the chronic pain clinic validation sample, the overall mean (SD) for the CSS-SR was 7.1 (9.51). Table 2 summarizes CSS-SR mean (SD) values as a function of CRPS diagnostic status in this clinical validation sample, with CSS-Clinician mean (SD) values provided for comparison. Internal consistency reliability of the 13 CSS-SR items was acceptable and higher than in the post-TKA sample (alpha = 0.80). Table 3 compares frequency of endorsement of CSS-SR items to corresponding CSS-Clinician symptom items in the pain clinic sample. Perhaps not surprisingly, chronic pain clinic patients self-reported notably higher rates of CRPS-related symptoms on the CSS-SR compared to reports of the same symptoms obtained in the context of a physician-led clinical history. The greatest similarity between CSS-SR and CSS-Clinician symptom reports was noted for allodynia; skin temperature and color changes; sweating changes; and trophic changes to nail, hair, or skin. All of these are key distinguishing features of CRPS.

3.2. Correspondence between the self-report version of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score and Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score-Clinician

The most critical evidence supporting validity of the CSS-SR is correspondence between CSS-SR scores and CSS-Clinician total scores based on ICCs that correct for chance agreement. Intraclass correlation values for associations between patients' CSS-SR scores and their CSS-Clinician scores are summarized for both samples in Table 4. For the CSS-Clinician, we present ICCs for both total score and the symptom subtotal score, given that the latter most directly corresponds to symptoms reported in CSS-SR items. Agreement between CSS-SR and CSS-Clinician total scores was good (0.75–0.90) to excellent (>0.90) across the 2 samples. As expected, correspondence between CSS-SR total scores and symptom subtotal scores on the CSS-Clinician was stronger than for CSS-Clinician total scores, given that the latter also reflects objective assessment of CRPS features based on physical examination.

The longitudinal nature of the assessments in the post-TKA sample provided an opportunity to examine correspondence between changes in CSS-SR total scores and changes in CSS-Clinician total scores over the 4 ½ month period between 6-week and 6-month post-TKA follow-up. The CSS-SR was sensitive to changes in CRPS features over time, as reflected in the good agreement between changes in CSS-SR total scores and changes in CSS-Clinician total scores over this follow-up period (ICC = 0.84). Comparable longitudinal data were unavailable for the pain clinic validation sample.

3.3. Concurrent validity of the self-report version of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score

Concurrent validity was evaluated by comparing the pattern of associations of CSS-SR vs CSS-Clinician total scores with contemporaneous measures of pain and psychosocial status. These analyses assumed that if the CSS-SR were valid, higher levels of CRPS features would be associated with greater pain and psychosocial distress, and lower function. As indicated in Table 5, higher total scores on the CSS-SR and the CSS-Clinician in the post-TKA sample at 6-week follow-up were both similarly and significantly associated with greater reported pain intensity, pain interference, psychological distress, and sleep disturbance (small to moderate effect sizes). Similar associations for both the CSS-SR and CSS-Clinician, primarily for pain intensity and pain interference, were also noted at 6-month follow-up in the post-TKA sample.

Longitudinal assessments in the post-TKA sample also permitted examination of extent to which changes in CSS-SR and the CSS-Clinician values from 6-week to 6-month follow-up were associated with changes in the measures of pain and psychosocial status over the corresponding period. Changes in CSS-SR values from 6-week to 6-month follow-up were significantly associated with changes in worst (spearman r = 0.22, P = 0.02) and average (spearman r = 0.20, P = 0.04) past 24-hour pain intensity, changes in pain interference on the PROMIS measure (spearman r = 0.30, P = 0.001), and changes in anxiety on the STAI (spearman r = 0.19, P = 0.04). The only comparable significant finding using the CSS-Clinician was with changes in anxiety on the STAI (spearman r = 0.22, P = 0.04). All other associations were nonsignificant. Overall, the CSS-SR appeared somewhat more sensitive to change than the original CSS-Clinician in the post-TKA sample.

In the pain clinic validation sample, only pain intensity ratings were available to address concurrent validity (Table 5). Higher CSS-SR scores were associated with significantly higher past 24-hour worst and average pain intensity (moderate effect sizes). Associations between pain intensity and CSS-Clinician values in the pain clinic validation sample were notably smaller and only approached statistical significance (small effect sizes). However, across both study samples, the CSS-SR displayed expected patterns of associations in which greater CRPS symptom severity was associated with greater pain intensity, functional limitations, and psychosocial distress.

3.4. Utility of self-report version of the Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Severity Score for complex regional pain syndrome diagnostic screening

Receiver operating characteristic curves for predicting criterion-based dichotomous Budapest CRPS diagnosis based on CSS-SR values were generated for both study samples. In the post-TKA sample, AUC = 0.88 at 6-week follow-up and AUC = 0.94 at 6-month follow-up. Both AUC values indicate an excellent ability to distinguish between CRPS cases and non-CRPS cases, supporting the potential diagnostic utility of the CSS-SR.

Similar ROC analyses in the pain clinic sample (Fig. 2) also indicated excellent ability of the CSS-SR to discriminate between patients with CRPS and non-CRPS (AUC = 0.84). To maximize its clinical utility, we sought to determine the optimal clinical cutoff on the CSS-SR for predicting Budapest CRPS diagnosis, focusing on the pain clinic data to maximize generalizability to the chronic pain clinic setting. Table 6 summarizes diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, PPP and NPP, and overall accuracy for several potential CSS-SR cutoffs. PPP indicates probability that a CRPS diagnosis is correct, whereas NPP reflects probability that a non-CRPS diagnosis is correct. The optimal CSS-SR cutoff for self-report diagnostic screening in the research context should demonstrate reasonably good diagnostic sensitivity (so CRPS cases are not missed) but also prioritizes diagnostic specificity to minimize false positive CRPS diagnoses in research samples. In this scenario, the optimal CSS-SR cut score was ≥8 (sensitivity = 0.79, specificity = 0.78). Using this cutoff, there is a 79% probability that an individual screening positive for CRPS on the CSS-SR will receive a Budapest CRPS diagnosis after detailed examination. In clinical settings where diagnostic sensitivity is prioritized, a CSS-SR cut score of ≥7 may be preferable, given that it improves sensitivity to 0.88 (ie, misses fewer CRPS cases) but retains adequate specificity (0.72).

4. Discussion

Conducting CRPS intervention trials is challenging in part due to the need for in-person diagnostic evaluation to screen for CRPS features. The only validated measure of CRPS features is the CRPS Severity Score (CSS-Clinician Version),12,15 a measure requiring an in-person history and physical examination. The current study developed and validated a self-report measure of CRPS symptomatology (the CSS-SR) suitable for remote use both as a diagnostic screener and to track changes in CRPS features over time.

Results strongly supported the validity and potential diagnostic utility of the CSS-SR. Correspondence between total scores on the previously validated CSS-Clinician and the proposed CSS-SR was good to excellent. The original CSS-Clinician has previously demonstrated sensitivity to change in CRPS features over time.15 The CSS-SR is similarly sensitive, with good agreement observed between changes in CSS-SR and CSS-Clinician scores over an approximately 4-month period. Elevated total scores on the CSS-Clinician have also previously been shown to be associated with significantly worse CRPS patient status in multiple domains, including pain intensity and function limitations.12 The CSS-SR showed a similar pattern of association with pain and psychosocial status to that observed with the CSS-Clinician. Moreover, changes in CSS-SR scores over a 4-month period were also significantly associated with changes in pain and psychosocial status over the same period. Finally, the CSS-SR exhibited good to excellent ability to predict CRPS diagnostic status after full evaluation. Overall, results support the utility of the CSS-SR as a diagnostic screening measure and for remotely tracking changes in CRPS status over time.

There are several implications of these findings. First, although the CSS-SR is not intended for use in making a formal CRPS diagnosis, it may provide an efficient means of remotely screening large numbers of patients for potential CRPS study inclusion before conducting in-person evaluations. The CSS-SR has good sensitivity and specificity, with the proposed diagnostic screening cutoff (total score of ≥8) leading to a 79% probability of accurate CRPS diagnosis when Budapest criteria are applied. Second, as a brief and simple validated self-report measure of CRPS status, the CSS-SR could have research utility in studies tracking the natural history of CRPS over time or assessing effects of proposed interventions on CRPS patient status. Finally, in clinical settings, the CSS-SR could enable systematic self-report tracking of changes in CRPS status over the course of clinical care without requiring diagnostic examinations at each clinic visit (eg, using REDCap18).

The current study has potential limitations. The CSS-SR and the data used to score the CSS-Clinician were both obtained during the course of the same assessment session. It is possible that this procedure may have inflated degree of correspondence between the measures. This possibility was limited in the pain clinic validation study because the CSS-SR was completed by patients before physician evaluation was conducted and with physicians blinded to CSS-SR scores. Although CRPS in patients who have undergone TKA may differ to a degree from CRPS as typically seen in pain clinics, the general consistency of validation findings across the post-TKA and pain clinic samples was notable and broadly supports the validity of the CSS-SR. Finally, because the CSS-SR reflects only symptom reports, it cannot serve as a substitute for full in-person clinical diagnostic evaluation. However, our results suggest that it may serve as a reasonable proxy for CRPS diagnosis in settings where full clinical evaluation is not possible, and as an initial screen for individuals likely to be diagnosed with CRPS upon full clinical evaluation.

In summary, the CSS-SR appears to be a valid self-report measure of CRPS symptoms that shows strong correspondence with the existing CSS-Clinician. It demonstrates good accuracy as a self-report CRPS diagnostic screening tool. It has potential applications in the research setting as a remote diagnostic screener and in both clinical and research settings as a means of remotely tracking changes in CRPS status over time. Further evaluation of the clinical utility of the CSS-SR is warranted.
