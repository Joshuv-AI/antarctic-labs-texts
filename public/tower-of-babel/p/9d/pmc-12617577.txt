ABPA in post-tuberculosis lung disease: A diagnostic pitfall or genuine entity?
Authors: Inderpaul S. Sehgal, Valliappan Muthu, Ritesh Agarwal
Published: 2025

Depending on the host’s immune status, Aspergillus species can cause a spectrum of pulmonary diseases, ranging from invasive aspergillosis (IA) in immunocompromised individuals to allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) and chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) in immunocompetent hosts.[12] IA is easy to recognize due to its characteristic setting (immunocompromised host) and classic presentation (fever and nodular infiltrates on imaging, and others). In contrast, ABPA and CPA are chronic respiratory conditions that are frequently misdiagnosed or mislabeled.

ABPA is a Th2-mediated allergic response, usually directed against Aspergillus fumigatus, and is classically associated with asthma, cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis or occasionally chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.[134] CPA, on the other hand, results from impaired Th1-mediated immunity, leading to persistent Aspergillus colonization of the airways in individuals with underlying structural lung damage, most commonly residual cavities following treated pulmonary tuberculosis.[2567]

The diagnosis of ABPA relies on a composite of clinical, radiological and immunological findings, as outlined by the International Society for Human and Animal Mycology ABPA Working Group (ISHAM-AWG) guidelines.[18] Hallmark diagnostic criteria include elevated serum total immunoglobulin E (IgE), A. fumigatus-specific IgE and IgG, and peripheral eosinophilia. In contrast, CPA is diagnosed based on characteristic clinical and radiological features and confirmed by microbiological evidence of chronic Aspergillus infection, heralded by raised A. fumigatus-IgG levels.[7]

WHY IS THERE CONFUSION BETWEEN ABPA AND CPA?

Although ABPA and CPA are traditionally considered distinct entities, diagnostic confusion is common due to non-specific symptoms, overlapping clinical features and the absence of a reference standard test to distinguish between them. Furthermore, several immunological markers traditionally associated with ABPA, such as elevated serum total IgE, A. fumigatus-IgE and IgG, and blood eosinophil counts, may also be seen in patients with CPA.[9101112]

A recent study reported that 2.27% of patients with post-tuberculosis lung abnormalities (PTLA) fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for ABPA.[13] This finding raises an important question: is ABPA genuinely prevalent in PTLA, or are these cases misclassified CPA due to overlapping features? Historically, ABPA has not been recognized as a frequent complication of PTLA,[14] and this observed association is more likely to reflect diagnostic overlap or misclassification rather than an actual increase in ABPA prevalence.

WHEN THE PENDULUM SWINGS: FROM UNDERRECOGNITION TO OVERDIAGNOSIS?

In this editorial, we explore the association between ABPA and pulmonary tuberculosis, examine how ABPA and CPA can coexist or complicate prior pulmonary tuberculosis and underscore the critical importance of accurately distinguishing between ABPA and CPA. These conditions differ markedly in their underlying pathophysiology, therapeutic approaches and clinical outcomes, making diagnostic precision essential to avoid both underrecognition and overdiagnosis.

COEXISTENT ABPA AND ACTIVE PULMONARY TUBERCULOSIS: POSSIBLE BUT RARE

The coexistence of ABPA and active pulmonary tuberculosis is distinctly uncommon. In a case report by Min et al.,[15] a microbiological diagnosis of tuberculosis was established following a lobectomy performed to investigate chronic granulomatous lesions in a patient initially diagnosed with ABPA. In another report, active pulmonary tuberculosis was diagnosed using bronchoalveolar lavage, which revealed acid-fast bacilli, and tested positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis using the Xpert MTB/Rif assay in an ABPA patient presenting with lung collapse.[16] In two other cases, the diagnosis of PTB was made using the Xpert MTB/Rif assay.[17]

Conversely, ABPA patients who are treated with prolonged or high-dose systemic glucocorticoids may develop reactivation of latent tuberculosis or new-onset pulmonary tuberculosis. Over the past decade, we have observed approximately 15 such cases among 1,500 ABPA patients under our care. Additionally, ABPA patients with extensive bronchiectasis may be predisposed to non-tuberculous mycobacterial infections.[1819]

ACTIVE PULMONARY TUBERCULOSIS, MISTAKEN AS ABPA: RARE BUT DANGEROUS

ABPA, once an underrecognized entity, is now increasingly overdiagnosed. In India, awareness of ABPA has grown significantly over recent decades,[20] and is now often suspected even in patients lacking classical risk factors like asthma.[212223] However, in rare instances, active pulmonary tuberculosis may be misdiagnosed and treated as ABPA,[24] particularly in settings where substandard or poorly validated diagnostic tests are widely used. In addition, failure to adhere to established diagnostic guidelines frequently results in misinterpretation of test results and inappropriate clinical decisions.[2526]

COEXISTENT ABPA AND PREVIOUSLY TREATED PULMONARY TUBERCULOSIS: A WORD OF CAUTION

The association between ABPA and previously treated pulmonary tuberculosis may arise through multiple mechanisms. In high tuberculosis-burden countries, ABPA is frequently misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis, resulting in unnecessary anti-tuberculosis therapy. In one study, nearly 37% of ABPA patients reported prior exposure to anti-tuberculosis therapy, many of them radiologically diagnosed and lacking microbiological confirmation.[27]

In the more frequent scenario, patients with residual cavitary disease from prior tuberculosis may also show elevated Aspergillus-related immunological markers. Despite the apparent overlap with ABPA, these cases usually represent CPA. Indeed, nearly one-fifth of CPA patients fulfil diagnostic criteria conventionally applied to ABPA, underscoring the significant clinical and immunological overlap between these entities.[12]

Accurate differentiation between CPA and ABPA is crucial due to their differing therapeutic approaches. ABPA typically responds to short-term (4 months) therapy with systemic glucocorticoids or oral triazoles,[128293031] whereas CPA requires prolonged antifungal treatment, often extending for 12 months or longer.[232333435] Inappropriately treating CPA with systemic glucocorticoids may worsen disease progression and increase the risk of IA.[36]

DISTINGUISHING FEATURES

Clinically, patients with ABPA typically present with asthmatic symptoms. In contrast, patients with CPA more often have systemic features such as fever, weight loss and recurrent hemoptysis, although these symptoms may occasionally occur in ABPA as well [Table 1]. Imaging plays a pivotal role in differentiation: ABPA manifests as free-standing bronchiectasis without surrounding fibrosis [Figure 1], whereas CPA is defined by fibrocavitary lesions, traction bronchiectasis, pleural thickening and architectural distortion [Figure 2]. The presence of fungal balls (aspergillomas) is a hallmark of CPA, irrespective of the patient’s immunological profile.

In patients with a prior history of treated pulmonary tuberculosis, it is not uncommon to encounter coexisting asthma, fibrocavitary disease with fungal balls, both tractional and free-standing bronchiectasis, along with immunological markers fulfilling diagnostic criteria for both ABPA and CPA. In such scenarios, distinguishing ABPA from CPA is impossible, and true ABPA-CPA coexistence should be considered. However, it is prudent to label and manage such patients as CPA rather than ABPA in clinical practice. Initiating systemic glucocorticoids, appropriate for ABPA, may exacerbate fibrocavitary lesions in CPA and potentially precipitate progression towards IA.[36]

CONCLUSION

In patients with fibrocavitary sequelae of tuberculosis, even when classical features of ABPA, such as free-standing bronchiectasis and positive immunological markers, are present, the diagnosis of ABPA should be made with caution. The underlying lung architectural distortion, particularly cavitation, creates a milieu more compatible with CPA. Misclassifying such cases as ABPA and treating them with systemic glucocorticoids may worsen disease progression and increase the risk of IA. Therefore, in this context, the threshold for diagnosing CPA should be low, and patients should not be labelled as ABPA solely based on immunological features.

Future directions

With increasing awareness of Aspergillus-associated lung diseases, structured education and appropriate clinical training are essential. Over-reliance on immunological investigations may lead to misdiagnosis of ABPA in patients with previously treated tuberculosis, subjecting them to inappropriate and potentially harmful therapy. Future research should aim to refine diagnostic tools and develop novel markers to better differentiate between Th1- and Th2-mediated immune responses. Such advances could significantly improve diagnostic accuracy and guide more tailored and effective management strategies.
