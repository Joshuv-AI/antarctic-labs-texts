Exploring emotions beyond the laboratory: A review of emotional and physiological ecological momentary assessment methods in children and youth
Authors: Linda Sosa‐Hernandez, Natasha Vogel, Katelyn Frankiewicz, Chelsea Reaume, Abbey Drew, Sadie McVey Neufeld, Kristel Thomassin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advancements in methodologies such as ecological momentary assessment (EMA) and ambulatory physiology devices have enhanced our ability to measure emotions experienced in daily life. Despite the feasibility of EMA for assessing children's and youth's emotional self‐reports, the feasibility of combining it with physiological measurements in a real‐life context has yet to be established. Our scoping review evaluates the feasibility and usability of implementing emotional and physiological EMA in children and youth. Due to the complexities of physiological EMA data, this review also synthesized existing methodological and statistical practices of existing studies. Following the PRISMA‐ScR guidelines, we searched and screened PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science electronic databases for studies that assessed children's and youth's subjective emotions and cardiac or electrodermal physiological responses outside the laboratory. Our initial search resulted in 4174 studies, 13 of which were included in our review. Findings showed significant variability in the feasibility of physiological EMA, with physiology device wear‐time averaging 58.77% of study periods and data loss due to quality issues ranging from 0.2% to 77% across signals. Compliance for emotional EMA was approximately 60% of study periods when combined with physiological EMA. The review points to a lack of standardized procedures in physiological EMA and suggests a need for guidelines in designing, processing, and analyzing such data collected in real‐life contexts. We offer recommendations to enhance participant engagement and develop standard practices for employing physiological EMA with children and youth for emotion, developmental, and psychophysiology researchers.

INTRODUCTION

Methodological and technological advancements have revolutionized researchers' ability to measure and understand children's and youth's emotional experiences in real‐world settings, offering novel insights into both experiential and physiological aspects of their emotions. The use of ecological momentary assessment (EMA), a method that repeatedly assesses individuals' experienced emotions and physiological responses in real‐time, has become increasingly popular. These methods are feasible in adult populations for both emotional and physiological EMA (Milojevich et al., 2023). Although research indicates the feasibility of emotional EMA in children and youth (Wen et al., 2017), it is unknown if this extends to ambulatory physiological assessments or both simultaneously. Moreover, there are currently no established guidelines for the design, collection, processing, and analysis of physiology data collected from pediatric samples in real‐world settings. This is problematic, as there are ample methodological and statistical decision points that warrant consideration when working with the intensive and longitudinal data that result from EMA approaches. Our review had two main objectives. First, we assessed the feasibility and usability of physiological EMA in children and youth, focusing on cardiac and electrodermal activity. Second, we synthesized the methodological characteristics of studies that employ both emotional and physiological EMA in children and youth, with particular attention to study design, device selection, data processing, and analysis. Our review focused on children aged 0–12 years old and youth aged 13–21 years old to capture a broad spectrum of developmental stages, from infancy to adolescence. These stages show rapid changes in individuals' physical, cognitive, emotional, and social development compared to adulthood, presenting unique challenges and opportunities for EMA (Russell & Gajos, 2020). This review offers recommendations to guide developmental and emotion researchers in the application of emotional and physiological EMA methodologies.

Monitoring children's and youth's emotions in real‐world settings

Recent advancements in the field have seen researchers adopting more ecologically valid momentary assessment methods to enhance the accuracy of assessing children's emotional experiences in real‐life settings (Arbel et al., 2022; Stone et al., 2019). EMA methods capture intensive longitudinal data that entails repeated self‐reports of emotional experiences. Not only does a real‐time assessment of children's emotions enable researchers to monitor emotional experiences in daily life, but EMA also has the potential to reduce retrospective bias that can occur when asking participants to recall their thoughts, feelings, or behaviors (Mill et al., 2016; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). EMA has been applied in children and youth to study a range of experiences, including emotions (Reitsema et al., 2022), eating behaviors (Mason et al., 2020), and physical activity (Bourke et al., 2021). Research indicates that children and youth have comparable compliance with EMA protocols to adults, with youth completing about 78% (Wen et al., 2017) and adults completing about 80% (Williams et al., 2021; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023) of expected ratings during the study period. This suggests that emotional EMA, repeatedly assessing children's and youth's self‐reported emotional experiences, is feasible.

Various theories of emotion posit that emotions manifest across experiential, behavioral, and physiological levels (Barrett & Campos, 1987; Ekman, 1992; Fogel et al., 1992; Gross & Thompson, 2007; Hollenstein & Lanteigne, 2014), making monitoring children's emotional experiences across multiple levels in real‐life a critical pursuit for researchers. While laboratory‐based studies have provided valuable insights into the contributions of physiological responses to emotional experiences and functioning across development, they lack ecological validity and may not accurately reflect real‐world responses. Recognizing these limitations, technological advancements now allow for the real‐time assessment of children's and youth's experiential and physiological emotion functioning. Ambulatory physiology devices, like wearables and wireless technologies (e.g., Apple Watch and Fitbit), enable either continuous data recording during individuals' daily activities (e.g., Hoemann et al., 2021) or repeated measurements, such as every 15 min, over a specified timeframe (Pannicke et al., 2020). Assessing physiological responses in real‐world settings has multiple advantages, including capturing individuals in natural environments (Timmons et al., 2017), observing rare or ethically sensitive events (e.g., suicidal ideation; Stange et al., 2019), and studying longer durations than possible in lab settings (Carpenter et al., 2016).

Although reviews suggest that it is feasible for children and youth to use wearable technology to monitor physical activity (Creaser et al., 2021; Ridgers et al., 2016), it remains unclear whether children and youth comply with wearing physiological devices in real‐world settings or if the data collected are viable. Further, it is unknown whether children and youth adhere to emotional EMA when it is paired with wearing a physiological device. This is crucial to understand in children and youth, especially given the reduced experimental control in EMA methods and the increased complexity of study designs that involve both subjective reports and the use of physiological devices. Compared to adults, children and youth have less mature cognitive abilities and executive functioning skills, which may make it more challenging for them to consistently follow and adhere to EMA protocols. Furthermore, children and youth are more susceptible to peer influence and may have different preferences or comfort levels than adults that may impact their willingness to wear devices. As such, the first objective of our review is to examine the feasibility and useability of physiological EMA applied in children and youth.

Ecological momentary assessment of children's physiological activity

Physiological aspects of children's and youth's emotions are often indexed through their autonomic nervous system, including cardiac and electrodermal activity (Naranjo et al., 2021; Porges, 2007, 2009). The autonomic nervous system consists of two branches that work in complementary ways to regulate bodily processes and support adaptive functioning: the parasympathetic nervous system and the sympathetic nervous system (Beauchaine, 2001; Propper & Holochwost, 2013). According to Polyvagal theory (Porges, 2007, 2009), the parasympathetic nervous system is active during states of safety and social connection and exerts a restorative effect on the heart via the vagal nerve. This influence decreases heart rate (i.e., beats per minute) and increases heart rate variability (i.e., length between consecutive heart beats). Heart rate is linearly associated with emotional arousal such that a higher heart rate reflects greater emotional arousal (Wascher, 2021). There are multiple parameters of heart rate variability, including frequency‐domain measures where heart rate is separated into low‐frequency and high‐frequency measures, and time‐domain measures such as root mean square successive differences (Berntson et al., 1997; Camm et al., 1996). Both metrics of heart rate variability are associated with emotion regulation, especially during the recovery from stressors (Beauchaine & Thayer, 2015; Shaffer et al., 2014), as well as the expression of emotions such as joy and anger (Shi et al., 2017). Research indicates that on average youth exhibit higher resting heart rate and heart rate variability than adults (Estévez‐Báez et al., 2019). Cardiac activity is typically measured using electrocardiogram (ECG) sensors and photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors. ECG sensors placed on the chest detect electrical signals from the heart, while PPG sensors placed on the wrist or finger use light to measure changes in cardiac activity by assessing light absorption rates in blood vessels (Sinex, 1999). Research shows that heart rate and most heart rate variability parameters are comparable between ECG and PPG methods (Jeyhani et al., 2015), however, the ease of wear of PPG wrist‐worn devices is thought to facilitate real‐world data collection.

Conversely, the sympathetic nervous system becomes active during states of stress or threat and has an excitatory effect on the heart through the vagal nerve (Porges, 2009; Thayer et al., 2012), increasing heart rate and reducing heart rate variability. In addition to the heart, the sympathetic nervous system also influences sweat glands in the presence of a perceived threat, increasing electrodermal activity (Christopoulos et al., 2019; Critchley, 2002; Gatti et al., 2018; Horvers et al., 2021). Electrodermal activity, changes in electrical characteristics on the skin, is typically measured by two main parameters, including skin conductance level where slow, tonic activity is measured, and skin conductance response where faster, more precise alterations in activity are measured (Kleckner et al., 2024). Electrodermal activity is also associated with changes in emotional arousal and can provide useful insights into understanding responses to emotional stimuli (Arabian et al., 2023). Given the relevance of these physiological indices to emotions, our review focuses on the measurement of heart rate, heart rate variability, and electrodermal activity in real‐world EMA protocols.

Methodological considerations of monitoring children's and youth's cardiac and electrodermal activity in real‐world settings

Despite the advantages of monitoring children's and youth's emotions both experientially and physiologically in daily life, there are numerous methodological challenges associated with designing and collecting such intensive longitudinal data remotely. One challenge is selecting a study design that participants are able to understand and adhere to (Iida et al., 2023). This is particularly pronounced in child and youth samples, given that their developmental stage, such as their less mature cognitive and executive functioning abilities compared to adults, may lead to difficulties in tracking parts of the study protocol (Heron et al., 2017). Also, noncompliance and attrition can result from study design decisions (Reis & Gable, 2000); as such, it is imperative that researchers engage in thoughtful decision‐making to maximize data complexity while minimizing participant burden. The three primary categories of study designs for EMA are time‐based, event‐based, and device‐contingent protocols (Bolger et al., 2003). Time‐based protocols involve monitoring physiological data at predetermined or random intervals within a specified study period, necessitating decisions on study length, spacing between assessments, and timing. These considerations can be especially complex when researching pediatric populations due to school and extracurricular activities. In event‐based protocols, data collection is triggered by a focal experience of the participant. In device‐contingent protocols, participant's physiology may be monitored to prompt them to report on their emotional experiences and situations.

Additionally, researchers must decide on suitable devices for collecting cardiac and electrodermal data, considering that children tend to have smaller bodies, necessitating knowledge of available options for this population. Further complicating matters, researchers typically monitor both physiology and subjective aspects of children's emotional experiences and must determine how to integrate these into the study design. However, there are currently no guidelines synthesizing children's and youth's compliance with these study designs and device choices for monitoring cardiac and electrodermal activity in real‐life settings.

Finally, there are considerations related to signal processing. Real‐world data collection can lead to data quality issues, such as missing values and measurement errors, with physiology data collected outside the laboratory being particularly prone to motion artifacts (Moeyersons et al., 2019). The quality of children's and youth's physiology collected in the real world may differ from adults due to their higher levels of physical activity and movement (Sigmund et al., 2007), which may introduce more noise and artifacts. Further, the complexity of cardiac and electrodermal data requires multiple processing steps, such as removing movement artifacts and extracting meaningful features. Additionally, intensive longitudinal methods often result in large volumes of data that researchers need to reduce for analysis. It is well‐established that seemingly minute discrepancies in analytic decision‐making (e.g., exclusion of outliers, imputation, or deletion of missing data) can lead to vastly different results (Schweinsberg et al., 2021). Further, real‐world data may also be influenced by various environmental factors, such as activity level or dietary habits (Strüven et al., 2021). When designing studies, researchers would benefit from knowledge of which covariates to include. As such, amalgamating methodological and statistical considerations and current decisions being made within developmental and emotion science is a critical first step to determining a standard of practice when employing physiology monitoring methods with children and youth in the real world.

The current review

Real‐time monitoring of children's and youth's physiological responses stands to expand our understanding of how emotional experiences unfold in daily life. However, for researchers hoping to use these methods, many questions remain about their feasibility and implementation. Our scoping review examines the feasibility and useability of emotional and physiological EMA in child and youth populations. We define feasibility as the percentage of completed emotional reports or the percentage of time the physiological device is worn out of the total expected by the EMA protocol. Data usability is defined as the percentage of physiology data collected that is viable for data analysis out of the total physiology data collected. We also provide a synthesis of methodological aspects of cardiac and electrodermal activity collected in real‐world settings and recommendations for designing, cleaning, processing, and analyzing this data. Addressing these aims will facilitate the utilization of EMA methods with children and youth, and advance research on emotion development.

METHODS

This scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines and checklist (Peters et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018).

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed in the following electronic databases: PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science. A comprehensive search strategy was developed by the first author in collaboration with a research librarian with extensive experience contributing to knowledge syntheses. This process included the first author conducting initial searches to identify keywords related to physiological EMA assessments in emotion science and subsequently collaborating with the librarian to refine the search. Keywords related to the study design (e.g., “ecological momentary assessment”), physiological measurement (e.g., “heart rate” and “skin conductance”), and emotion construct (e.g., “mood”) were incorporated. Searches were limited to keywords being identified in the titles and abstracts of articles. Additional Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Index Terms were employed for the literature search in PubMed and PsycINFO, respectively. See Table S1 in Supplemental Materials for the complete list of search terms. The initial search was conducted on April 20, 2023, with no restrictions on the publication date. The search was rerun on January 19, 2024, with the publication date restricted to April 2023 to January 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review considered both published and unpublished (e.g., dissertations) empirical articles, including experimental and correlational studies. The inclusion criteria included articles conducted in human populations with a continuous or repeated study design, such as EMA, experience sampling, or diary studies, used to measure common autonomic physiological responses, including heart rate, heart rate variability, beats per minute, respiratory sinus arrhythmia, electrocardiogram, cardiac arousal, electrodermal activity, galvanic skin level, or skin conductance level or response. Physiological measurements were required to be collected outside of laboratory settings, with no restrictions on the length of the assessment period. Inclusion also required articles to incorporate a measure of emotion, such as emotional experience, expression, awareness, flexibility, and/or emotion recognition, regulation, or reactivity. Emotion constructs could be measured through subjective reports, observational, and/or cognitive measures collected at a single time point or through repeated assessments. Exclusion criteria included articles that (1) were theoretical, commentaries, book chapters, bibliographies, proposals, or did not report data, (2) were not published in English, (3) implemented a cross‐sectional, virtual, or augmented reality study design, (4) had physiological outcomes only collected in the laboratory, and (5) used psychopathology, symptoms, well‐being, satisfaction, personality traits, self‐attitudes, hunger, or outcomes of emotional experiences (e.g., stress, pain, and aggression). Articles were divided into two groups: children and youth, and adult populations. This article focuses on children and youth between the ages of 0 and 21.

Data extraction

All exported references from the final search were uploaded and managed in Covidence (Covidence Systematic Review Software, 2023). First, duplicates were removed. Then, the titles and abstracts of the articles retrieved were screened by two independent reviewers against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After screening, full‐text versions of eligible articles were obtained and uploaded into Zotero (Zotero 5.0, 2020). Eligibility was further assessed by two independent reviewers through full‐text screening, where the reason for the articles excluded was documented. Discrepancies that arose between reviewers were resolved through full group discussions or with another member of the research team.

Data from studies that met our inclusion criteria underwent data extraction across multiple categories. First, general information including authors, publication date, and country. Second, sample characteristics including size, age, biological sex, ethnicity, and population type. To evaluate our first objective, we focused on extracting key metrics related to children's and youth's compliance with and the useability (i.e., the percentage of unusable data) of emotional and physiological EMA. Consistent with our second objective, methodological characteristics including the study design, monitoring period, emotion measure, physiological signal, device used to measure physiology and corresponding sampling rates, as well as information about processing and analyzing the physiology data, including filtering, cleaning (exclusion of movement artifacts), extraction methods, and covariates included in the analysis were extracted as well. To ensure extraction accuracy, a second team member verified all extracted information. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between team members.

Sample characteristics

Table S2 shows the sample characteristics of the included studies. Sample sizes ranged from 14 to 549 participants, with most studies having fewer than 100 participants (n = 11, 8384%). Studies were carried out with school‐aged children and youth, ranging from 6 to 21 years old (M age = 15.31, SD age = 9.64). Most studies were conducted with youth 12 years old or older (n = 8, 66%). While half of the studies had an almost even split between sexes, four included only females (Doyle et al., 2015; Fortier et al., 2011; Leonard et al., 2018; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016; Sigrist et al., 2023), two included only males (Ivarsson et al., 2013; Myrtek et al., 1996), and one did not report sex (Wingler et al., 2021). The ethnicity of participants varied, with five studies including mostly Black or Hispanic youth (Doyle et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2023; Leonard et al., 2018; Murray et al., 2022; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016), one including mostly White youth (Thomassin et al., 2023), and the other half not reporting on ethnicity. Only three studies reported on the sample's socioeconomic status, but it was inconsistently indexed (e.g., income and education). Notably, inclusion criteria in Murray et al. (2022) required a household income below the poverty threshold in the United States. About half of the studies involved youth from community settings (n = 7, 54%), three selected youth based on mental health or other symptoms (e.g., insomnia and non‐suicidal self‐injury), one focused on surgical patients undergoing tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy, and two on adolescent mothers. Studies were primarily conducted in the United States (n = 8, 62%), three in Germany, one in Sweden, and one in Canada.

Compliance with and useability of emotional and physiological EMA

Table 1 provides an overview of children's and youth's compliance with emotional and physiological EMA. Among the studies included in our review, only seven provided data on the feasibility of physiological EMA. For physiological EMA, compliance rates were calculated as the percentage of time participants wore the physiological device relative to the total expected wear during the study duration. Compliance rates varied widely, ranging from 21% to 99% across studies. On average, children and youth wore the physiology device for just over half of the study period (M compliance = 58.77%, SD compliance = 26.88). Compliance rates for physiological EMA were not significantly related to the length of study periods, r(5) = .29, p = .578, or the sample's mean age, r(5) = .02, p = .963. However, studies employing physiological EMA during sleep periods had the highest compliance. Menghini et al. (2023) and Sigrist et al. (2023) reported high compliance rates of approximately 80% and nearly 100%, respectively, and monitored children's and youth's physiology during sleep. In contrast, the lowest compliance rate of 21% was observed in a sample of economically disadvantaged adolescents (Murray et al., 2022). Further, Sigrist et al. (2023) found differences in the compliance based on population type; specifically, adolescents engaging in non‐suicidal self‐injury had longer physiological device non‐wear times (5.14 min) than community controls (2.76 min, p < .001). Due to small and unequal group sizes, we could not test whether compliance rates varied by device type (i.e., PPG‐based vs. ECG‐based devices). However, in the one study that reported compliance for PPG wrist‐worn devices, the compliance rate was 84.50%. In the three studies that reported compliance for ECG‐based chest‐worn devices, the average compliance rate was 70.52%. Overall, there is significant variability in children's and youth's compliance with physiological EMA protocols, with some concerns.

Most included studies provided an initial training session to instruct participants on the study protocol and proper use of the physiological device (n = 9, 75%). Some studies also reported using practice days (n = 2, 17%) and reminders (n = 5, 42%) to promote proper use and compliance. Despite these measures, non‐compliance was reportedly due to technical and connectivity problems with the physiology devices. Leonard et al. (2018) participants reported challenges with the device, particularly with not knowing when it was charged and when it was working properly. Thomassin et al. (2023) noted difficulties with keeping the physiological device connected to their phone application. Further, Kim et al. (2023) collected feedback from adolescents who participated in emotion and physiological monitoring for 2 weeks. Participants in their study noted that they were less likely to comply with the study when their stress levels at school were high.

Data quality issues such as noise and artifacts also contribute to data loss and impact the volume of usable physiological data. Half of the studies included in our review reported on usability—that is, the percentage of heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), and electrodermal activity (EDA) data not viable for inclusion in analyses. Regarding HR usability, Myrtek et al. (1996), Murray et al. (2022), and Sigrist et al. (2023) observed unusable data percentages of 0.2%, 9%, and 19.33%, respectively. HRV data were less usable, with Murray et al. (2022) and Zenker et al. (2021) reporting 22.86% to 77% unusable data. In Zenker et al. (2021), the method of HRV computation impacted usability, with longer intervals yielding more unusable data. Only Murray et al. (2022) provided data on EDA usability, noting a 9% rate of unusability. Further, participant's demographics, such as sex and ethnicity, impacted data useability. Murray et al. (2022) found that boys had more useable heart rate data compared to girls (28% vs. 15%, p = .016), and White participants had more heart rate variability data compared to African American participants (54.13% vs. 18.77%, p = .020).

Of the eight studies that employed emotional EMA, compliance rates for children's and youth's emotion reports were provided for six. In these studies, compliance rates varied, ranging from 23% to 88%, with an average compliance rate of 61% for emotion reports throughout study periods. This compliance rate is notably lower than the average rate identified in a systematic review of children and adolescent's engagement with EMA (i.e., 78.3%; Wen et al., 2017). However, in Kim et al. (2023) participants were given the option to select whether to complete the physiological EMA in addition to emotional EMA. The emotional EMA compliance rate did not significantly differ between those who opted for both components and those who did not, with rates of 23% and 24%, respectively. However, only one participant (total N = 14) had sufficient physiological data and emotional reports for concurrent analysis.

Regarding the validity of emotion reports in relation to physiological responses, 9 out of the 13 studies did not report correlations between physiological and emotional variables. Among the four that did, Doyle et al. (2015) and Menghini et al. (2023) found no correlation between negative mood and HR, while Murray et al. (2022) found a correlation with HR but not EDA. Zenker et al. (2021) found a correlation between mood and HR. Sigrist et al. (2023) found no correlation between emotion dysregulation and either HR or heart rate variability.

Methodological features of emotion and physiological ecological momentary assessment

The methodological features of studies employing emotional and physiological EMA in child and youth populations are reviewed below, including study design, devices, and signal processing.

Study design

The designs of the included studies are summarized in Table S2. Five studies monitored children's and youth's physiology during specific events in daily life, including watching television (Myrtek et al., 1996), playing video games (Ivarsson et al., 2013), sleeping (Sigrist et al., 2023), and undergoing a medical procedure (Fortier et al., 2011; Wingler et al., 2021). In these studies, emotion reports, including emotionality, anxiety, and emotion dysregulation, were made before and/or after the events.

Most studies monitored children's and youth's emotions and physiology concurrently within an EMA method (n = 8, 66%; Doyle et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2023; Leonard et al., 2018; Menghini et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2022; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016; Thomassin et al., 2023; Zenker et al., 2021). In these studies, the physiological EMA period was at a minimum of 1 day to a maximum of 6 months, with most studies (n = 8, 67%) lasting fewer than 14 days. One study had participants self‐select whether they wanted to complete the physiological monitoring or only monitor their experienced emotions (Kim et al., 2023). Another study required that participants complete the emotion monitoring for part of the broader EMA period (2 of 14 days; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016). Only two studies mentioned that physiological EMA was planned around the school day after 3 p.m. (Ranzenhofer et al., 2016; Thomassin et al., 2023).

The most common measure of emotion was positive and negative affect or mood, as well as emotion intensity. Participants were provided a list of emotions and asked to rate how much they were feeling each emotion in the current moment. These emotion lists were often created by the researchers or well‐validated measures, such as the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule and Multidimensional Mood State Questionnaires. The number of daily emotion reports ranged from once per day to continuous assessment over the study period, with most studies prompting participants to complete at least 5 emotion reports per day. Diverse EMA schedules and prompt types were used to gather these emotion reports, including stratified random‐interval schedules and random, event‐based, self‐initiated, and EDA‐contingent prompts. Two studies used EDA‐contingent prompts (Leonard et al., 2018; Thomassin et al., 2023). While Leonard et al. (2018) did not report the EDA levels they used to execute these prompts, Thomassin et al. (2023) used increases in EDA levels of 20% from baseline to trigger emotion reports. Most studies administered emotion reports through a smartphone application. These included researcher‐generated apps such as Calm Mom (Leonard et al., 2018), Time2Feel (Thomassin et al., 2023), and a self‐developed app by Zenker et al. (2021), as well as the commercially available app RealLife Exp (Kim et al., 2023). All but one of these studies that used a smartphone application (Menghini et al., 2023) provided children and youth with a phone for the study period. A couple of studies used a personal digital assistant handheld (Doyle et al., 2015; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016), or online survey platforms such as SurveySparrow and MetricWire (Menghini et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2022).

Devices to measure heart rate, heart rate variability, and EDA in children and youth

Studies used a variety of devices to capture children's and youth's HR, HRV, and EDA responses in real time. All devices were either wearables or wireless, except Myrtek et al. (1996) and Fortier et al. (2011) which used an analog device with two ECG leads.

The location of electrodes on the devices differed across studies. HR and HRV measurements are mostly commonly derived from electrocardiogram activity recorded on the chest, photoplethysmography assessed via the wrist or finger, or blood pressure measured from the arm. Of the nine included studies that collected cardiac measurements, five collected data via the chest, four used a wristband, and two used an armband or arm cuff. EDA is most reliably collected from sweat glands, which are most prominent on the palms and soles of an individual's hands and feet. Only the Everion armband, iCalm wristband, and Empatica E4 wristband were used to measure EDA. The Everion armband measured EDA from the upper arm. The iCalm and Empatica E4 measured EDA from the wrist on the non‐dominant hand.

The sampling rate of the devices also varied across studies and physiological signals. The sampling rate is the number of samples obtained in 1 s (Hz). Higher sampling rates ensure the signal is accurately and reliably measured. The sampling rate was not reported or available for the four following devices. In the included articles, sampling rates ranged from 4 to 1024 Hz across studies. The sampling rate was higher in chest‐worn devices (M chestbandSamplingRate = 482.90 Hz) than the Empatica E4 wristband (M E4SamplingRate = 4 Hz). Wearable devices, such as the Empatica E4, that use photoplethysmography typically sample at a much lower rate than ECG devices (Bent et al., 2021).

Signal processing: Filtering, cleaning, extraction, and analysis

Procedures for processing HR, HRV, and/or EDA signals differed across studies. Below is an overview of HR and HRV, and then EDA signals (see Table S3 for the summary).

Heart rate and heart rate variability

Ten studies examined HR and/or HRV, of which four did not describe filtering and cleaning procedures (Doyle et al., 2015; Fortier et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2023; Myrtek et al., 1996; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016). Mainly, the processing of HR and HRV data was conducted using custom‐built scripts in R and MATLAB. Other software included Kubios Premium, UnisenseViewer, and Firstbeat analysis.

The first step in processing HR data typically involves applying a filter to reduce noise and remove artifacts. Artifacts are detected by identifying values that are outside the physiologically feasible range for humans—that is, heart rate <30 and >240 bpm. In our review, four studies described omitting HR values outside these parameters but varied on the cut‐off points they applied. Ivarsson et al. (2013) excluded HR values >240 bpm in 13‐ to 16‐year‐old boys. Menghini et al. (2023) omitted values outside of 43–104 bpm in 16‐ to 19‐year‐old adolescents. Murray et al. (2022) excluded values outside of 40–180 bpm in 7‐ to 19‐year‐old adolescents. Sigrist et al. (2023) excluded HR values outside of 30–200 bpm in 12‐ to 17‐year‐old females using an automatic smoothing detrending method (λ = 500). A rationale for these specific cut‐offs was not provided.

Next, falsely detected extra, missed, or premature beats (i.e., ectopic beats) need to be discarded either manually or automatically using an algorithm (Berntson & Stowell, 1998; “Heart Rate Variability,” 1996). Three studies reported on excluding ectopic beats. Ivarsson et al. (2013) identified 5‐min periods with more than 4% of ectopic beats using a linear interpolation filter algorithm. Sigrist et al. (2023) visually inspected for ectopic beats and manually corrected them. Zenker et al. (2021) applied an artifact detection filter to detect and exclude ectopic beats. An additional consideration for cleaning cardiac data when it is collected using a wearable device is filtering out values based on physiologically invalid temperature—that is, temperature <32 and >43°C. Murray et al. (2022) time‐aligned and excluded occasions of 5‐min intervals of HR where the skin temperature was below 32°C.

The cleaned HR data are then segmented into non‐overlapping time intervals for feature generation, three included studies did not report on the length of the interval (Doyle et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2023; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016). In the other studies, data were segmented into either 1‐min (Ivarsson et al., 2013; Murray et al., 2022) or 5‐min intervals for feature generation (Menghini et al., 2023; Myrtek et al., 1996; Sigrist et al., 2023; Zenker et al., 2021). The interval length was either automatically selected by the physiology device or no rationale was provided. Some studies also used these intervals to exclude segments of data or participants based on missing data or potential cofounders. Sigrist et al. (2023) discarded segments with <30 s of consecutive data. Similarly, Menghini et al. (2023) removed all participants with 20 or more missing intervals (i.e., more than 100 min). Beyond listwise deletion of participants, most studies did not specify the strategies they used to handle missing data prior to analyses. Zenker et al. (2021) discarded intervals wherein participants reported they had smoked, drunk alcohol or coffee, or taken medication because of their potential influence on cardiac activity. HRV is typically derived from the cleaned and segmented HR data.

A range of HR and HRV features were used in the included studies. Descriptive features of HR, such as the mean and median, were extracted in 8 of the 10 studies. The most commonly used feature of HR (n = 6, 46%) is the mean of an individual participant across the entire study period (Doyle et al., 2015; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016), within a specific time period/event (Fortier et al., 2011; Ivarsson et al., 2013; Menghini et al., 2023; Myrtek et al., 1996), or in successive equally spaced intervals over time (i.e., time series; Kim et al., 2023; Sigrist et al., 2023). For example, Doyle et al. (2015) calculated the mean HR over the 24‐hr ambulatory study period. Ivarsson et al. (2013) computed the mean heart rate within participant‐reported instances of playing and sleeping and validated these reports through the absence of gross motor activity during sleep using a movement sensor (i.e., Actiheart). While Kim et al. (2023) computed the average HR for each day of the study period, Murray et al. (2022) calculated the median HR for each 5‐min segment of the study period. In addition, Myrtek et al. (1996) used a moving window to calculate the mean number of instances when a 1‐min interval of HR was greater than the previous 3 min while accounting for increases in physical activity and computed a heart rate difference score subtracting mean HR during TV consumption and mean HR at rest.

Regarding HRV, there are two main feature categories researchers extracted: time‐domain and frequency‐domain measures. Of the six studies that generated HRV metrics, half extracted the mean of time‐domain measures of HRV, specifically the root mean squared of successive interbeat intervals (RMSSD; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016; Sigrist et al., 2023; Zenker et al., 2021), one extracted the mean of frequency‐domain measures of HRV within specific events (e.g., high‐frequency low‐frequency ratio; Ivarsson et al., 2013), and two did not specify the HRV measurement extracted (Kim et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2022). For example, Zenker et al. (2021) calculated RMSSD across 90‐min intervals of the 4‐day study period and 5‐min intervals during the time span of EMA emotion reports. In addition to descriptive features of HRV and HR, Sigrist et al. (2023) calculated individual‐level cosine function parameters of HR and RMSSD to measure the diurnal rhythmicity of HR and HRV over the study period.

Another crucial aspect of analyzing physiology data collected in real‐world settings is controlling for factors that may affect children's and youth's cardiac activity. Five studies did not report on the inclusion of covariates (Doyle et al., 2015; Fortier et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2022; Myrtek et al., 1996). Most studies included participant characteristics as covariates, including age (Ranzenhofer et al., 2016; Sigrist et al., 2023; Zenker et al., 2021), biological sex (Menghini et al., 2023), race (Ranzenhofer et al., 2016), and body mass index (Menghini et al., 2023; Ranzenhofer et al., 2016; Sigrist et al., 2023). Some also included indices of participants concurrent physical activity, including torso movement (Ivarsson et al., 2013), total number of steps per day (Menghini et al., 2023; Sigrist et al., 2023), and energy expenditure (Zenker et al., 2021), as well as time or day of recording (Menghini et al., 2023; Sigrist et al., 2023). In studies monitoring physiology during children's and youth's sleep, the duration and onset of sleep were also included as covariates (Menghini et al., 2023; Sigrist et al., 2023). However, studies either did not report on the statistical significance of the covariates or they did not relate them to cardiac activity.

Electrodermal activity

Five studies incorporated EDA, two of which did not report information about processing because EDA was primarily used to trigger emotion reports (Leonard et al., 2018; Thomassin et al., 2023). Murray et al. (2022) processed EDA data in MATLAB. First, motion artifacts and inaccurate EDA measurements were reduced by excluding skin conductance values larger than 21 as well as values where the corresponding activity levels were below 0.8 and skin temperature was below 32°C. Next, the signal was decomposed into skin conductance level and skin conductance response using a 300‐point third‐order polynomial filter. The skin conductance data were then segmented into 5‐min non‐overlapping windows and the median skin conductance level and median number of skin conductance responses were calculated. Finally, this data were time‐aligned with the EMA emotion report data. Covariates included subjective stress levels and biological sex. In contrast, Fortier et al. (2011) and Wingler et al. (2021) provided minimal information on EDA processing. A value was then created for waiting and preoperative environments. It was not clear whether how these values were calculated. No covariates were reported.

DISCUSSION

Researchers are increasingly employing EMA methods to assess children's and youth's emotional experiences in everyday life (Arbel et al., 2022; Colombo et al., 2019). Our review synthesizes empirical studies that monitored both cardiac and/or electrodermal activity alongside subjective emotions in children and youth outside the laboratory. We examined children and youths' compliance with and the data useability of monitoring physiology and emotions using EMA methods in real‐world contexts. Given the complexity of physiological EMA data, we also describe and discuss methodological considerations and recommendations for physiological applications of EMA, including study design, data processing, and analysis.

Considerations for enhancing compliance and data usability when implementing physiological EMA with children and youth

A notable challenge identified in our review was that many studies did not report compliance rates. Moreover, inconsistencies were noted in how compliance was calculated across studies. Researchers may overlook reporting compliance data if their study does not specifically focus on feasibility. However, it is crucial for researchers to provide information on both how many participants out of their total sample engaged in physiological EMA and the average device usage over the total expected period. This ensures proper data interpretation and facilitates comparisons across studies. Standards for reporting compliance have been presented (Stone & Shiffman, 2002; Trull & Ebner‐Priemer, 2020), and a checklist for EMA studies that measure physiological measures has also been proposed (Liao et al., 2016). We build on this by providing recommendations for reporting on physiological EMA. For an overview of recommendations for reporting and enhancing compliance in children and youth, see Table 2.

Papers that did report compliance demonstrated considerable variability in compliance rates. On average, children and youth wore their devices for 58.77% of the study period, which is lower than most adult studies that typically range between 80% and 93% (Morren et al., 2009; Timmons et al., 2017, 2023; Tomko, 2017). Compliance rates for physiological EMA were notably higher in sleep studies, possibly due to minimal interference and integration into daily routines. Consistent with a meta‐analysis that examined compliance with subjective reports within EMA (Wrus et al., 2023), we did not find that age was related to compliance with wearing the physiological device across studies. However, because only six studies included in our review reported on compliance, future research is needed as the field continues to grow to better understand the potential impact of age on device adherence within an EMA framework. Our review included no studies with children under 7 years old, with most focusing on youth 12 years or older. This restricted age range may be due to our inclusion criteria requiring both emotional and physiological EMA and the lack of ambulatory physiological devices suitable for younger children. Previous reviews on wearables in children and youth with broader criteria (Creaser et al., 2021; Sequeira et al., 2020) identified only two studies with children under 7 (Byun et al., 2018; McGinnis et al., 2019). However, both studies were conducted in laboratory settings, underscoring the need for future work to develop and validate wearable physiological monitoring devices suitable for very young children in real‐world contexts.

The average compliance rate for emotion reports across studies in our review (60%) was significantly lower compared to a recent meta‐analysis assessing children and adolescent's engagement with EMA which was reported to be 78% (Wen et al., 2017). The lower compliance might stem from the added burden of monitoring children's physiological responses alongside emotion self‐report measures outside the laboratory. Specifically, children may find wearing bulky physiological devices cumbersome. This aligns with findings showing that children and youth are less compliant and accepting of wearables that are not appropriately sized or uncomfortable (Kleiman et al., 2019; Schaefer et al., 2014). Therefore, identifying strategies to enhance participant engagement and reduce the perceived burden of these methods is essential for advancing children's and youth's compliance with both emotional and physiological EMA. While a few included studies provided reminders, it is unknown whether such strategies increased compliance. Future research should explore the role of experimenter reminders and parental involvement in bolstering children's and youth's compliance with EMA protocols.

Multiple factors may contribute to lower compliance with physiological EMA in children and youth. Studies in our review noted technological issues with the physiology devices, such as device charge and connectivity to EMA applications. Technological challenges have also been noted in several adult studies (Burke et al., 2017; Cummings et al., 2022). To prevent data loss, studies emphasized the importance of training participants on device use in EMA and ambulatory monitoring studies, while also ensuring the availability of staff to troubleshoot technical issues. Similar to findings in other studies where healthy adult samples show higher compliance rates compared to clinical adult populations with mental health disorders (Ottenstein & Werner, 2022), our review also demonstrated that demographic factors such as sex, ethnicity, or population type may influence compliance rates. For example, child and youth clinical populations may be more likely to experience external stressors that impede their time and mental resources to complete all EMA prompts throughout the study period. When conducting EMA research with these populations, it will be essential to consider their unique needs and work to reduce barriers that will increase compliance.

Our review indicates significant data useability challenges, with noise and artifacts in cardiac and electrodermal data leading to substantial data loss. Alarmingly, less than half of the studies included in this review reported the proportion of data viable for analysis. Research suggests that data loss in ambulatory settings could be twice as high as that observed in laboratory settings (Kunkels et al., 2021). Reported losses in our review included 19.33% for HR, 77% for HRV, and 9% for EDA data. While adult studies align with our HR and EDA findings, HRV losses are typically lower (Ballesio et al., 2023; Juarascio et al., 2020; Parker et al., 2020; Schilling et al., 2020). Some research suggests wearables may provide more accurate HR estimates than HRV indices, exhibiting fewer noise and artifacts (Menghini et al., 2019). These findings may imply that certain metrics may be less prone to data loss in child and youth samples, warranting consideration in future studies. However, reasons for missing data such as user error, technical issues, or motion artifacts, are not always disclosed, posing challenges in determining the causes of data loss and finding solutions to mitigate these.

While our review advances our understanding of children's and youth's compliance with emotional and physiological EMA protocols, and the useability of the data acquired, the validity of these measures remains unknown. Only four studies included in our review reported correlations between emotional self‐report and physiological variables, with mixed findings: some reported associations (Murray et al., 2022; Zenker et al., 2021), while others did not (Doyle et al., 2015; Menghini et al., 2023; Sigrist et al., 2023). As the use of emotional and physiological EMA increases, it is crucial to examine the overall validity of these measures, especially given the evidence that emotional concordance across subjective and physiological levels is rare even in laboratory settings (Hollenstein & Lanteigne, 2014).

Methodological considerations for physiological monitoring with children and youth

In designing and implementing physiological EMA methods for children and youth, various factors require careful consideration at different research stages. We outline key considerations and offer recommendations for researchers at pivotal decision points, including study design, device selection, data processing, analysis, and reporting (see Table 2 for a summary).

Study design and device selection

When integrating physiological EMA into emotion research, researchers must first consider the study design. Key considerations during the planning stage involve the sampling strategy (e.g., interval schedule, event‐based, etc.), the duration of the EMA period, and the frequency of prompts. When selecting the sampling strategy, it is important to consider the specific aims of the research; for instance, event‐based sampling may be suitable when focusing on specific events or episodes, while time‐based sampling (fixed or random) may be more appropriate when trying to capture an individual's overall experience day to day (Reichert et al., 2020; Shiffman et al., 2008). Regarding study durations, our review found that EMA durations varied, although most studies were <14 days, which is consistent with EMA studies in the literature (de Vries et al., 2020; Heron et al., 2017; Wen et al., 2017). While our study did not find that shorter monitoring durations increased compliance, EMA studies with adults identify that emotion reporting and physiological compliance decrease over extended monitoring periods (Adler et al., 2021; de Vries et al., 2020; Fuller‐Tyszkiewicz et al., 2013). It is plausible that we did not observe this trend as our review only included 12 papers. On average, children and youth in our review completed five prompts per day, aligning with recommendations that suggest limiting the number of prompts to a maximum of five per day to enhance participant compliance (Burke et al., 2017; Ottenstein & Werner, 2022). It may be that when combining physiological and emotional EMA, researchers will need to limit the number of prompts even further.

The next step involves selecting an appropriate physiological device. In our review, we found that while there was a range of devices used to measure HR and HRV, there was less variety in devices used to measure EDA. Though wristband photoplethysmography sensors are commonly used to collect HR and HRV data, they are more prone to motion artifacts, resulting in a greater proportion of unusable data compared to chest‐worn electrocardiogram devices which are considered the “gold standard” for collecting HR and HRV (Sartor et al., 2018). For EDA, devices were either worn on the wrist or arm. Though research suggests that the optimal site for EDA collection is on the palmar surface (van Dooren et al., 2012), this location is often not practical for physiological monitoring given that subjects use their hands regularly during their daily activities. Alternatively, the wrist provides a comparable signal, albeit with a higher susceptibility to motion artifacts. The arm has been found to be less responsive for EDA collection, and therefore is not an ideal location (van Dooren et al., 2012).

Different devices also come with varying sampling rates, which are essential for ensuring accurate and reliable signals. Troublingly, sampling rates are not always reported, which is not always the fault of researchers, but rather because the information is unavailable as it is kept proprietary. The adequacy of sampling rates for capturing physiological signals varies across parameters. While a sampling rate above 5 Hz is sufficient for deriving heart rate, HRV analyses require higher sampling rates (Béres & Hejjel, 2021). Specifically, a sampling rate of at least 250 Hz is required for frequency‐domain analyses of HRV and at least 100 Hz for time‐domain analyses (Kwon et al., 2018). This suggests that the Polar H7 Monitor, Mortara H12+ Holter Monitor, EcgMove 3, and Firstbeat Bodyguard 2 chest bands with higher sampling frequencies may be acceptable for both HR and HRV, while the Actiheart sensor and Empatica E4 wristbands may be acceptable only for HR. For EDA, sampling rates between 4 and 8 Hz are sufficient for physiology monitoring when measurements are longer than an hour (Braithwaite et al., 2013). However, higher sampling rates are often recommended (Geršak & Drnovšek, 2020). This indicates that Empatica E4's sampling rate of 4 Hz is adequate for longer monitoring periods, but otherwise, there are limited options for measuring EDA in children and youth.

Data processing and reporting

EMA results in large quantities of data that, once collected, researchers need to process and analyze. Processing HR, HRV, and EDA signals involve several steps, including filtering, cleaning, and feature extraction. However, this review identified a lack of consensus on signal processing methods, resulting in variability in how HR, HRV, and EDA were processed and analyzed. This lack of consistency poses challenges in interpreting and comparing findings across studies. The first step in processing data often involves applying a filter to reduce noise, removing artifacts, and removing values outside human physiological ranges. However, there were inconsistencies in artifact removal techniques, with authors varying in their considerations of physiologically reasonable cut‐offs, often without providing rationales for these decisions. Toolkits and standards suggest that physiologically feasible ranges of HR are between 30 and 240 bpm (Föll et al., 2021). Consistent with this, Mindware technologies flags HR values below 40 and over 200 bpm. In comparison, BIOPAC suggests that typical skin conductance levels fall within the range of 2–20 μS (Braithwaite et al., 2013). However, future research should explore whether the physiological thresholds for HR and EDA in adults are also acceptable for children and youth, as existing literature on this topic is limited. Another method used to filter abnormalities in the data was using changes in skin temperature to identify artifacts in photoplethysmography data. Studies in this review also used both automatic and manual methods to inspect for ectopic beats (i.e., missing or extra beats), with it being standard practice to use both. In our review, most studies concentrated on HR and HRV, with only two discussing EDA processing, despite its importance in measuring physiological arousal. This discrepancy may be due to the limited availability of ambulatory EDA assessment devices outside laboratory settings.

The next step in analyzing EMA data involves data reduction to extract meaningful features of cardiac and electrodermal activity. Our review found that most studies relied on calculating means of physiological metrics over entire study periods or within specific events, which may oversimplify the complexity of children's physiological and emotional dynamics. For example, Doyle et al. (2015) calculated a mean HR over their 24‐hr study period. In comparison, Murray et al. (2022) adopted a more sophisticated data reduction method by calculating the median HR for each 5‐min segment of the study period. This method allows for the capture of the dynamic and time‐series nature of children's physiological and emotional processes throughout the day. Using more granular and repeated measures would align with calls in emotion research recognizing that emotions are dynamic and vary across different time scales (Granic & Hollenstein, 2003; Reitsema et al., 2022).

The extent to which covariates were included and their statistical significance varied across studies. However, studies that reported on covariates often used age, physical activity (movement), and BMI. Some studies also used biological sex and race as covariates. These covariates are similar across laboratory studies with children and youth (Coulombe et al., 2019; Klosowska et al., 2020). Physiological EMA studies in adults also have similar covariates, with the addition of factors that may not be relevant to child populations but could influence more adolescent populations (e.g., smoking, alcohol, and caffeine intake; Ballesio et al., 2023). Further exploration of covariate effects on cardiac activity and EDA in children and youth could enhance the understanding of individual differences and contextual influences on emotional and physiological responses.

Conclusions

Our review highlights the potential of integrating physiological measurements with emotion reporting to capture the daily emotional fluctuations of children and youth in real‐world settings. We examined the feasibility of this approach and provided key considerations for each stage of data collection. The key findings from this review suggest that while compliance and data usability in ambulatory physiological monitoring paired with emotion reporting are acceptable, there is substantial room for improvement. Additionally, researchers should carefully consider study design, device selection, and data acquisition methods when employing physiological EMA with children and youth and provide recommendations for standardizing our protocols. Ultimately, these insights contribute to refining and advancing the integration of physiological and emotional EMA for capturing the multifaceted nature of emotions in child and youth populations. This article underscores the importance of methodological rigor and transparent reporting to advance research in this field.
