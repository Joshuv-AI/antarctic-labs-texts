Clinical characteristics and prognosis of antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis: A single-center cohort study in Liuzhou, China
Authors: Yanyan Liu, Qianqian Liu, Zhijian Liang, Mengqi Yang, Baoquan Lin, Shijian Chen, Shan Deng, Liya Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This retrospective observational study primarily aims to analyze the clinical characteristics and prognosis of patients with antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis (AE) in Liuzhou, China. We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 32 patients diagnosed with acute AE between January 1, 2017, and August 31, 2024, for the following parameters: clinical features, results of laboratory and imaging studies, treatment, and clinical outcomes. Anti-N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor encephalitis was the most prevalent subtype (50%, 16/32). Other subtypes included glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy encephalitis (12.5%, 4/32), anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein encephalitis (12.5%, 4/32), anti-leucine glioma inactivating protein 1 encephalitis (6.3%, 2/32), anti-GAD65 encephalitis (6.3%, 2/32), anti-GABABR encephalitis (6.3%, 2/32), and anti-contactin-associated protein-like 2 encephalitis (6.3%, 2/32). The most common prodromal manifestation were fever or influenza-like illness (56.25%). The primary clinical manifestations included seizures (25.68%), psychiatric and behavioral disorders (17.57%), disturbances in consciousness (9.46%), and dyssomnia (9.46%). Pneumonia, observed in 43.75% (14/32) of patients, was associated with older age (P = .017), higher intensive care unit admission rates (P = .042), increased need for mechanical ventilation (P = .002), and worse modified Rankin Scale scores at diagnosis (P = .005). Immunotherapy led to clinical improvement in most cases (78.13%), and these patients achieved favorable outcomes (modified Rankin Scale ≤ 2; median follow-up 37 months). In conclusion, the AE characteristics in our single-center retrospective study were consistent with previous findings. The proportion of anti-N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor encephalitis was the highest. Once AE is diagnosed, immunotherapy should be initiated promptly to reduce disease severity and improve outcomes. In clinical practice for AE management, emphasis should be placed on pneumonia prevention and treatment to reduce the rate of intensive care unit admission and mechanical ventilation and shorten the length of hospitalization.

1. Introduction

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) constitutes a category of immune-mediated neurological disorders characterized by the presence of specific autoantibodies targeting surface proteins, ion channels, or receptors on neurons.[1] AE is now recognized as a leading etiology of noninfectious encephalitis.[2,3] Since the initial characterization of anti-N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis by Dalmau and colleagues in 2007,[4] a growing spectrum of neural autoantibodies has been identified, including NMDAR, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolpropionic acid receptor, γ-aminobutyric acid receptor (GABAR, which includes GABA-aR and GABAbR), leucine glioma inactivating protein 1 (LGI1), contactin-associated protein-like 2 (CASPR2), and glutamate dehydrogenase (GAD).[5]

Beyond classic neuronal targets, antibodies against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) are now recognized within the AE spectrum. Approximately 20.7% of individuals with MOG immunoglobulin G-related disease present with classical encephalitic manifestations, and 70.5% of affected patients with GFAP astrocytopathy involve encephalitis or meningitis.[6–9]

Early and aggressive immunotherapeutic intervention is critical to improving clinical outcomes.[10] However, diagnosis remains challenging due to the complexity of symptoms. In resource-limited settings like Liuzhou, the high cost of comprehensive antibody testing often renders it inaccessible. Consequently, physicians in these areas often must rely heavily on clinical evaluation, neuroimaging, and other supportive diagnostic modalities to establish a probable diagnosis and initiate empirical treatment.

In this retrospective study, clinical data from 32 patients with AE at the Department of Neurology, Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, were collected and analyzed. The study aims to enhance recognition of these disorders among neurologists and to contribute clinical evidence to inform diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for AE.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient inclusion and exclusion

This retrospective study enrolled 32 patients diagnosed with AE at the Department of Neurology, Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, between January 1, 2017, and August 31, 2024. Patients enrolled in this study were diagnosed in accordance with the 2016 criteria established by Graus et al.[11] The diagnostic criteria comprised the following: first, clinical presentation: an acute or subacute onset (duration of <3 months) accompanied by one or more of the following symptoms: symptoms of the limbic system, including psychiatric and behavioral disorders, memory impairment, and seizures; encephalitis syndrome, characterized by signs of diffuse or multifocal brain parenchyma involvement; syndromes of the basal ganglia, diencephalon, or hypothalamus; and psychiatric disorders not attributable to a primary psychiatric condition (as assessed by a psychologist). Second, supportive paraclinical evidence: presence of one or more of the following: cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings suggestive of encephalitis; electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrating epileptic or slow-wave activity. Third, etiological confirmation: anti-NMDAR encephalitis: Detection of anti-NMDAR antibodies in CSF or serum using a cell-based assay (Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany); other neuronal surface antibodies: positivity in CSF was considered diagnostic. Positivity in serum alone required corroboration by typical clinical manifestations and/or high titers (e.g., >1:32). The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: patients with infectious encephalitis; patients diagnosed with toxic encephalopathy, metabolic encephalopathy, or brain tumors prior to the AE; patients diagnosed with definite limbic encephalitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis, or Hashimoto encephalopathy; and patients with incomplete medical records.

2.2. Data collection and analysis

We collected and analyzed the following clinical data: age at onset, gender, prodromal symptoms, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, antibody test results, neuroimaging studies, EEG data, treatment, and clinical outcomes. Patient follow-up was conducted at 3-month intervals via outpatient visits or telephone interviews, and neurological functional outcomes were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), with a score of ≤ 2 indicating a favorable prognosis, whereas a score of ≥ 3 representing an unfavorable prognosis.

2.3. Antibody identification

Antibody detection in both CSF and serum samples was performed using a cell-based assay.

2.4. Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM). First, the normal distribution of each variable was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Then, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables, and count (percentage) for categorical variables. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables, Student t test for normally distributed variables, and Pearson χ2 or Fisher exact test for comparing categorical variables. A two-sided P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic features and characteristics of AE patients

A total of 32 patients with AE were enrolled in this study, comprising 19 males (59.4%) and 13 females (40.6%), with a median age of 38 years (range: 8–70). The distribution of autoantibody subtypes was as follows: anti-NMDAR encephalitis (n = 16, 50.0%), GFAP astrocytopathy (GFAP-A) encephalitis (n = 4, 12.5%), anti-MOG encephalitis (n = 4, 12.5%), anti-LGI1 encephalitis (n = 2, 6.3%), anti-GAD65 encephalitis (n = 2, 6.3%), anti-GABABR encephalitis (n = 2, 6.3%), and anti-CASPR2 encephalitis (n = 2, 6.3%). Antibody testing showed dual positivity in both serum and CSF in 17 patients (53.1%), isolated CSF positivity in 9 (28.1%), and isolated serum positivity in 6 (18.8%). Three cases exhibited dual antibody positivity: 1 with concurrent anti-NMDAR and anti-MOG antibodies, 1 with GFAP and anti-MOG antibodies, and 1 with anti-NMDAR and anti-GABABR antibodies. Demographic data and tested sample types in AE patients are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. Seasonal distribution of disease onset was as follows: spring (n = 8), summer (n = 5), autumn (n = 9), and winter (n = 10).

The median time to diagnosis (onset of clinical symptoms until positive autoantibody results) was 14.5 days (range: 3–81 days). The most common prodromal manifestation was fever or influenza-like symptoms, occurring in 56.3% (18/32) of patients. This was most common in patients with GFAP-A encephalitis (4/4, 100%), anti-MOG encephalitis (3/4, 75%), and anti-NMDAR encephalitis (8/16, 50%). Viral nucleic acids were detected in the CSF by polymerase chain reaction in several cases: Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) was detected in 3 patients with GFAP-A encephalitis, human herpesvirus type 1 in 2 patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis, and parvovirus B19 in 1 patient with anti-CASPR2 encephalitis.

Seizures, psychiatric and behavioral disorders, disturbances in consciousness, and dyssomnia were the most common symptoms. Additionally, some patients experienced hallucinations, paralysis, autonomic dysfunction, cognitive deficits, visual disturbances, sensory abnormalities, speech difficulties, ataxia, tremors, and limb stiffness (Fig. 1). In the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group, the most prevalent symptoms were seizures, psychiatric and behavioral disorders, and dyssomnia (Fig. 2). In the GFAP-A encephalitis group, seizures, psychiatric and behavioral disorders, and disturbances in consciousness predominated; autonomic dysfunction and cognitive deficits were also prominent features (Fig. 3). In the anti-MOG encephalitis group, seizures were the most frequently observed symptom, followed by visual disturbances, disturbances in consciousness, ataxia, and speech difficulties (Fig. 4). Faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS) was observed in 1 patient with anti-LGI1 encephalitis.

All patients underwent tumor screening. Tumors were identified in 2 patients: one in the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group was diagnosed with an ovarian teratoma, and another in the anti-GABABR encephalitis group was found to have lung cancer.

Ten patients required intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and 5 patients received mechanical ventilation. The ICU admission rate was highest among patients with GFAP-A encephalitis (100%), followed by those with anti-MOG encephalitis (50%) and anti-NMDAR encephalitis (25%). In contrast, the rate of mechanical ventilation was highest in the GFAP-A encephalitis group (75%), with anti-NMDAR encephalitis ranking second (12.5%). The median duration of hospitalization was 18 days (interquartile range: 13.5–25.5 days).

3.2. Auxiliary examinations of AE patients

Detailed CSF analysis was available for 27 of the 32 patients. We analyzed intracranial pressure (ICP), white blood cell count, and protein concentration. Among these, increased ICP was observed in 7 patients (25.9%), pleocytosis in 21 patients (77.8%), and increased CSF protein levels in 18 patients (66.7%). Subgroup-specific CSF profiles were as follows: In the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group (n = 13/16 with CSF data), increased ICP was observed in 1 patient (7.69%), while pleocytosis and increased CSF protein were observed in 11 patients (84.62%). In the anti-MOG encephalitis group (n = 4/4 with CSF data), pleocytosis, increased ICP, and CSF protein were observed in 3 patients (75%). In the GFAP-A encephalitis group (n = 4/4 with CSF data), increased ICP was found in 3 patients (75%), and pleocytosis and increased CSF protein were observed in all patients (100%), all of whom had slightly elevated levels (Fig. 5).

EEG assessments were performed in 27 patients, with abnormalities detected in 24 (88.9%). The distribution of EEG abnormalities across antibody subtypes was as follows: anti-NMDAR encephalitis(n = 11), GFAP-A encephalitis (n = 3), anti-MOG encephalitis (n = 3), anti-LGI1 encephalitis (n = 2), anti-GAD65 encephalitis (n = 2), anti-GABABR encephalitis (n = 1), and anti-CASPR2 encephalitis (n = 2). Focal and diffuse slow waves were the most common EEG abnormalities observed, and epileptic discharges were observed during seizures. however, the δ brush was not observed in any patient.

Brain MRI was performed in all 32 patients, with abnormalities identified in 18 (56.2%). The most frequently involved regions were the temporal lobe and hippocampus, followed by the frontal lobe, basal ganglia, thalamus, insula, parietal lobe, occipital lobe, and cerebellum. In the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group, 9 patients showed brain MRI abnormalities, with the most commonly affected areas being the temporal lobe and hippocampus. In the anti-MOG encephalitis group, 2 patients had abnormal MRI findings, with the most commonly affected areas being the temporal lobe and hippocampus. In the GFAP-A encephalitis group, 2 patients presented with MRI abnormalities, with the most commonly affected areas being the basal ganglia and thalamus (Fig. 6).

3.3. Treatment and outcomes

In our study, 30 patients received first-line immunotherapy, the remaining 2 patients declined all treatment. Among the treated patients, 21 patients received corticosteroids alone, 3 patients received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) alone, and 6 patients received a combination of corticosteroids and IVIG. 1 patient with GFAP-A encephalitis who showed a poor response to corticosteroid therapy subsequently received rituximab treatment. During the maintenance therapy phase, 30 patients continued treatment, while the 2 patients who had declined first-line therapy did not receive any maintenance therapy. The maintenance regimens included corticosteroids alone (n = 9), corticosteroids plus mycophenolate (n = 15), mycophenolate alone (n = 1), tacrolimus alone (n = 1), corticosteroids plus tacrolimus (n = 1), and corticosteroids plus azathioprine (n = 1). All 32 patients were followed up as outpatients or by telephone. The median follow-up time was 37 months (range: 1–72 months). A total of 25 patients (78.13%) achieved a good prognosis (mRS score ≤ 2), distributed as follows: anti-NMDAR (n = 14), GFAP-A (n = 3), anti-MOG (n = 4), anti-LGI1 (n = 2), anti-GAD65 (n = 1), and anti-GABABR (n = 1). Among the 7 patients with poor outcomes, 2 had an mRS score of 3, 3 had an mRS score of 4, and 2 died. One death occurred in a patient with anti-NMDAR encephalitis who abandoned treatment after 1 month, and the other was a patient with anti-GABABR encephalitis who succumbed to lung cancer after 1 year.

The AE patients were divided into 2 groups based on the presence of pneumonia (Table 2). The AE patients with pneumonia group (n = 14) was significantly older (P = .017), had higher rates of ICU admission (P = .042) and mechanical ventilation (P = .002), and had greater mRS scores at diagnosis (P = .005) compared to the group without pneumonia. Although the duration of hospitalization was longer in the pneumonia group, the difference was not statistically significant (P = .062). A significant difference in immunotherapy was also observed between the 2 groups (P = .047).

We also analyzed AE patients based on the type of neuronal autoantibodies (Table 2). Patients were classified as anti-NMDAR positive and positive for other AE-related antibodies. Anti-NMDAR encephalitis patients showed a trend toward less severe condition at follow-up (P = .089). 4. Discussion

In this investigation, we sought to characterize the profile of AE within the Liuzhou region by examining its clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, neuroimaging findings, and prognostic outcomes. In our study, the proportion of anti-NMDAR encephalitis was the highest, which is consistent with previous studies.[10,12,13] The gender ratio in the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group showed a slight male predominance. A similar gender ratio was also observed in patient cohorts with AE in China and Hungary.[12,14] Meanwhile, our study found that anti-NMDAR encephalitis mainly affected middle-aged people, although previous studies showed that anti-NMDAR mainly affected young women.[13,15,16] This discrepancy may be attributed to racial differences, the small sample size, and the potential referral bias whereby pediatric cases might seek care at specialized children’s hospitals. In our study, male patients in the GFAP-A encephalitis group were predominant, which was consistent with a previous study.[17] Previous research[1] reported male predominance in anti-LGI1, and anti-GABABR encephalitis, which was also observed in our study. Our study reported female predominance in anti-MOG encephalitis, which was consistent with a previous study.[18] In this study, the gender ratio of anti-GAD65 and anti-CASPR2 encephalitis was basically balanced, which was inconsistent with previous studies. Previous studies reported female predominance in anti-GAD65 encephalitis[19] and male predominance in anti-CASPR2 encephalitis,[20] which might be caused by sample size. The number of patients was basically consistent in spring, autumn, and winter, and fewer patients occurred in summer. This was inconsistent with previous studies,[21] which may be related to the high temperatures of summer in Liuzhou.

In the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group, 50% of patients had prodromal symptoms such as headache, fever, which was lower than the 70–86% reported in previous studies.[15,22,23] In the GFAP-A and anti-MOG encephalitis groups, 100 and 75% of patients had prodromal symptoms, which were higher than previously reported.[17] In our study, there were 2 patients developed anti-NMDR encephalitis after HSV encephalitis. Previous studies[17,24] reported that HSV infection was the most common viral trigger in patients with AE, especially in those with anti-NMDAR encephalitis. However, the exact mechanism by which HSV triggers AE has not yet been clearly defined. In the GFAP -A encephalitis group, 3 patients had EB virus infection. Previous studies have shown a close relationship between EBV and GFAP-A encephalitis.[25] EBV infection is more likely to induce the occurrence of GFAP-A encephalitis,[26] which suggests that if a patient has encephalitis symptoms and is infected with EBV, the possibility of GFAP-A encephalitis should be considered. Seizures, psychiatric and behavioral disorders, and dyssomnia were the most common symptoms in the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group, which was consistent with a previous study.[13] A previous study showed that the most common symptoms of patients with GFAP-A encephalitis were motor disorders, disturbances in consciousness, and autonomic dysfunction.[27] In contrast, our study found that seizures, psychiatric and behavioral disorders, and disturbances in consciousness were more common. In the anti-MOG encephalitis group, seizures and visual disturbances were the common symptoms, which was consistent with a previous study.[18] Previous research reported that FBDS was one of the typical symptoms of anti-LGI1 encephalitis, appearing in 26 to 71% of patients, which usually occurs several weeks before cognitive deficits appear.[28] In our study, FBDS appeared in 1 patient with anti-LGI1 encephalitis, with a prevalence (50%) consistent with the previous study.

Ovarian teratoma is the most common tumor of anti-NMDAR encephalitis,[29] although in our study, only 1 patient with anti-NMDAR encephalitis had an ovarian teratoma. A similar phenomenon was also observed in patient cohorts with AE in China.[14] This discrepancy may be attributed to racial differences and sample size, as well as the limitation of chest and abdominal CT in detecting small or occult tumors. Consideration should be given to an FDG PET/CT scan and long-term follow-up. In our study, 1 patient with anti-GABABR encephalitis had lung cancer, which is consistent with Hermetter[10] findings.

CSF examination plays an important role in diagnosis, as CSF pleocytosis is one of the diagnostic criteria for AE.[11] However, anti-LGI1 encephalitis and anti-CASPR2 encephalitis sometimes lack signs of inflammation in the CSF. A previous study[30] reported the prevalence of CSF inflammatory markers, including CSF pleocytosis, protein concentration, and OCB, in 95 patients with early AE. In our study, the rate of CSF pleocytosis (77.8%) and increased intracranial CSF protein (66.7%) in patients with AE was higher than that reported by Hébert et al.[30] The inflammatory profiles varied substantially across AE subtypes. A study by Marc Durr showed that 94% of patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis had signs of inflammation.[31] In the anti-NMDAR encephalitis group, 84.6% of patients had signs of inflammation, a proportion lower than that reported by Durr et al (94%).[31] In our study, the rates of CSF pleocytosis and elevated CSF protein in patients with GFAP-A encephalitis were both 100%, which were higher than those in previous reports.[17]

Previous studies showed that EEG abnormalities were observed in approximately 85% of patients with AE.[32,33] These abnormalities were usually nonspecific, including focal and diffuse slow waves and epileptic discharges, located in the temporal lobe or other brain regions. Our findings are consistent with these reports. However, while some previous studies reported the observation of extreme delta brush in some patients with AE,[32,33] this pattern was not observed in our cohort. A previous study[29] indicated that extreme delta brush can be observed in patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis, especially those in a coma. Regarding prognosis, normal EEG results are associated with a positive outcome, regardless of other prognostic variables,[34] and the severity of EEG abnormalities is a strong predictor of prognosis.[35] However, some studies have found that seizures or EEG changes do not retain significance as independent predictors in multivariate analyses.[36,37] Therefore, the independent prognostic value of specific EEG patterns in AE requires further investigation.

Our cohort showed that 56.25% of patients with AE had MRI abnormalities, mostly involving the temporal lobe and hippocampus. The affected areas varied with different antibody types. For instance, previous studies have shown that 40–70% of patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis had normal MRI results.[22,38,39] MRI abnormalities in these patients are nonspecific, with dispersed and variable involvement including the temporal lobe, hippocampus, basal ganglia, and thalamus. In the GFAP-A encephalitis group, 50% of patients had normal MRI findings, which was higher than the 21% reported in previous studies,[27] but the affected areas were consistent with previous research.[27] In the anti-MOG encephalitis group, 75% of patients had abnormal signals on MRI, most commonly in the temporal lobe and hippocampus, which was consistent with previous studies.[18]

Currently, the treatment of AE includes first-line and second-line immunotherapy, alternative therapies, and long-term maintenance therapy.[40] In our study, 30 patients received first-line immunotherapy, including corticosteroids and/or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Due to medical resource limitations, the patients in our study did not receive plasma exchange. During the maintenance phase, the most common regimen was a combination of mycophenolate mofetil and prednisone. 78.13% of patients in our study had a good prognosis, which is consistent with previous studies.[13] At the last follow-up, the recurrence rates were 25% for anti-NMDAR encephalitis, 0% for anti-MOG encephalitis, and 50% for GFAP-A encephalitis. The recurrence rate of AE may be related to antibody subtype, sample size, genetic background, follow-up time, and immunotherapy regimen.[41]

Consistent with previous study,[42] the presence of pneumonia in AE patients was associated with prolonged hospital stay and increased hospitalization costs. In our cohort, AE patients with pneumonia (43.75%) were significantly older and had higher rates of ICU admission and mechanical ventilation than those without pneumonia. They also showed a trend toward longer hospitalization. Previous studies have identified pneumonia as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in AE.[13,40] Therefore, in clinical management, it is necessary to strengthen the prevention of pneumonia. At the same time, the rehabilitation intervention should be enhanced for pneumonia patients.

Our study had several limitations. First, this was a single-center cohort study, which may introduce the risks of unmeasured confounding factors and the potential for selection bias due to some examination data being unavailable. Second, the limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power of our findings and precluded a separate prognostic analysis for each AE subtype. Third, due to constraints in medical resources, none of the patients received plasma exchange, and only 1 patient was treated with rituximab, which may have influenced the outcomes and limited the generalizability of our results. Finally, the mRS has inherent limitations in assessing neurological function in AE, highlighting the need for a more detailed and disease-specific evaluation tool. These findings should be validated in larger, prospective, randomized controlled trials.

In conclusion, the AE characteristics in our single-center retrospective study were consistent with previous findings. The proportion of anti-NMDAR encephalitis was the highest. Once AE is diagnosed, immunotherapy should be initiated promptly to reduce disease severity and improve outcomes. In clinical practice for AE management, emphasis should be placed on pneumonia prevention and treatment to reduce the rate of ICU admission and mechanical ventilation and shorten the length of hospitalization.
