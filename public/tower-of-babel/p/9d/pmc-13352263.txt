Solid-State Reactive Processing of Al–Fe3O4 Nanothermites for In Situ Formation of Soft-Magnetic Fe Nanoparticles in an Aluminum Matrix
Authors: Md Jasim Uddin, Michael Lastovich, Fu-Yun Tsai, Aniruddha Malakar, Farhan Ishrak, Charles Perkins, Sourabh Saptarshi, Qiaofu Zhang, Manoj Mugale, Pankaj Kulkarni, Kumar Kandasamy, Tushar Borkar, Matthew J. Olszta, Bharat Gwalani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a solid-state reactive processing (SSRP) route that enables in situ formation and uniform dispersion of metallic Fe nanoparticles within an aluminum matrix through controlled nanothermitic reactions. Using friction-assisted plastic deformation of an Al–Mg alloy embedded with a small fraction (∼4 wt %) of Fe3O4 particles, localized heat and shear deformation promote concurrent mechanical mixing, thermal activation, and chemical reduction. The reaction between Fe3O4 and Al/Mg produces Fe cores encapsulated by MgO and Al2O3-rich shells, effectively suppressing the formation of brittle Fe–Al intermetallics that typically hinder Fe-reinforced Al systems. The resulting microstructure exhibits fine-scale dispersion of core–shell nanoparticles and significant matrix refinement, leading to enhanced hardness and electrical resistivity. This proof-of-concept demonstrates that multistimuli coupling during solid-state processing can activate in situ controlled thermite reactions at moderate temperatures, providing a new pathway for fabricating lightweight, multifunctional metal–oxide nanocomposites.

Introduction

The increasing demand for lightweight and multifunctional materials in aerospace, automotive, and robotics sectors has driven intensive research into aluminum-based composites with integrated structural and functional performance. , Reinforced aluminum matrix composites (AMCs) combine high stiffness and strength-to-weight ratios with tunable thermal, electrical, and magnetic properties, offering a pathway toward next-generation components for energy-efficient transportation and adaptive systems. ,

Conventional AMC fabrication methods such as casting and powder metallurgy, however, face persistent challenges including particle agglomeration, interfacial reactions, and porosity-induced inhomogeneity. These issues originate from high processing temperatures and poor control of diffusion at particle–matrix interfaces, which promote brittle intermetallic formation and weak bonding. To overcome these limitations, solid-state processing techniques based on frictional heat and severe plastic deformation have emerged as powerful nonequilibrium routes to refine microstructures and achieve uniform reinforcement dispersion. − Friction stir processing (FSP), in particular, enables localized plasticization and rapid cooling, leading to microstructural states such as amorphous or nanocrystalline phases − and the in situ formation of reinforcing particles. −

Among possible in situ routes, thermite-type oxidation–reduction reactions offer a unique opportunity to create fine-scale metallic or ceramic phases within a metal matrix. Reactions between aluminum and metal oxides such as Fe2O3 or Fe3O4 are highly exothermic and can generate metallic Fe and Al2O3 products. , While conventional thermite systems require external ignition, transient frictional heating during solid-state deformation can locally trigger such reactions under controlled, submelting conditions. When fuel and oxidizer particles are reduced to the nanoscale, enhanced interfacial contact and diffusion kinetics further accelerate reaction initiation. ,

Embedding Fe nanoparticles in aluminum has long been sought to combine magnetic and structural functionality, but the approach is hindered by the high reactivity between Fe and Al, which rapidly forms brittle intermetallics (Al13Fe4, FeAl3, Fe2Al5, and so forth). , No prior study has demonstrated the controlled in situ formation of metallic Fe nanoparticles within an Al matrix without intermetallic contamination. The addition of Mg provides a secondary reducing agent and a source of MgO, potentially stabilizing Fe through encapsulation by MgO/Al2O3 shells, as predicted by thermodynamics.

Here, we introduce a solid-state reactive processing (SSRP) approach that exploits concurrent mechanical, thermal, and chemical activation during friction-assisted deformation of an Al–Mg alloy containing Fe3O4 particles (Figure a,b). This proof-of-concept demonstrates that localized, shear-induced thermite reactions can produce Fe nanoparticles encapsulated by MgO/Al2O3 shells directly within the Al matrix, thereby suppressing intermetallic formation. The resulting composite exhibits a refined microstructure and improved mechanical and functional characteristics, establishing SSRP as a new pathway for far-from-equilibrium synthesis of lightweight, multifunctional metal–oxide nanocomposites. This type of composite is most effectively synthesized through a solid-state, nonequilibrium processing route due to the highly exothermic Al–Fe3O4 reaction. A dynamically evolving, low-temperature pathway allows controlled formation of transient reaction products while suppressing runaway thermite behavior. In contrast, conventional equilibrium processing methods are unlikely to yield the distinctive Al–Fe–Al x Oγ composite architecture obtained here, as they do not access the same metastable processing regime.

We used SolidStir Extrusion (SSE) − to embed Fe3O4 particles into an Al–Mg matrix and induce in situ thermite reactions under friction-assisted shear deformation (SSRP) (Figure c). During SSRP, severe plasticization of the Al–Mg alloy generated localized frictional heat, promoting reduction of Fe3O4 by Al and Mg and releasing exothermic energy that enhanced mixing and diffusion. The resulting dynamic environment refined the base microstructure and dispersed the Fe3O4 particles uniformly throughout the matrix (Figure d1–d4). High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging and corresponding STEM-EDS mapping confirmed the reduction of Fe3O4 and the formation of Fe cores encapsulated by MgO/Al2O3 shells (Figure f1–f2). The corresponding indexed SAED pattern confirms the presence of BCC Fe within these core–shell particles (Figure f3), consistent with the proposed reduction pathway illustrated in Figure g. More details on the microstructural characterization are presented in the forthcoming sections. Thermodynamic analysis (Figure h) shows that both Al and Mg possess favorable Gibbs free-energy profiles for reducing Fe3O4 at 400 °C, with Mg-derived oxides stabilizing the shell structure.

The present approach overcomes the limitation of embedding Fe nanoparticles by achieving in situ formation and encapsulation of Fe nanoparticles directly within the Al matrix, demonstrating for the first time a viable pathway toward lightweight, multifunctional composites for extreme-environment actuation, thermal management, and adaptive magnetic applications.

Materials and Methods

Thermodynamic simulations were performed using Thermo-Calc software to predict phase formation and stability in Al and Al–4Mg alloys containing Fe3O4 additions. The simulations evaluated composition- and temperature-dependent transformations to identify favorable conditions for in situ solid-state reduction. Thermodynamic phase equilibria and Gibbs free-energy calculations were performed at 400 °C to reflect the processing temperature.

The base material was an Al–4Mg–1Fe–0.3Si alloy (nominally similar to AA5083. Round billets (Dia 9.5 mm, length 45 mm) were purchased from McMaster-Carr Supply, and Fe3O4 powders (∼5 μm average size) were sourced from Atlantic Equipment Engineers, Inc. To facilitate the introduction of the reinforcement, a preprocessing step was performed: a 3 mm diameter drill bit was used to drill an axial hole into the Al–Mg billets, which were subsequently packed with Fe3O4 particles. SolidStir Extrusion (SSE) (developed and patented by Enabled Engineering) was employed to achieve bulk consolidation and thermomechanical activation of the reactive system under solid-state conditions. The Fe3O4 powders, embedded within the predrilled Al–Mg billets, were subjected to the severe high-shear deformation characteristic of the SSE process to promote mechanical mixing, frictional heating, and in situ redox reactions between Fe3O4 and the surrounding matrix. The process parameters, such as tool rotation and feed rate, were optimized to balance heat generation and material flow, ensuring homogeneous dispersion of the reinforcement particles. The processing parameters were a rotation speed of 800 rpm and a feed rate of 50 mm/min.

The processed samples were sectioned along the transverse plane and characterized using a Thermo Fisher Helios Hydra dual-beam FIB–SEM instrument operated at 10 kV for secondary electron (SE), backscattered electron (BSE), energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted using a Thermo Fisher Titan 80–300 microscope at 200 kV to examine nanoscale reaction products and interfaces.

Mechanical and functional properties were evaluated using a Mitutoyo HM112 digital microhardness tester, a Quantum Design SQUID-VSM for magnetic measurements, and Keithley 6221/2182A instruments for four-probe electrical resistivity.

The Al (base) and Al–Fe3O4 SSE composite samples were machined into mini dog-bone tensile specimens with nominal dimensions of 5 × 1 × 1 mm using electric discharge machining (EDM; Sodick VL400Q, Singapore). Uniaxial tensile tests were conducted under ambient conditions using a universal testing machine (Kammrath and Weiss MZ0-00, Germany) at a constant strain rate of 0.001 s–1. To ensure reproducibility, three specimens from each type were tested under identical loading conditions.

The magnetic measurements were highly repeatable, with the measurement error remaining below 1% for each specimen. To maintain consistency and enable direct correlation between magnetic behavior and microstructure, the same specimen used for magnetometry was subsequently examined by electron microscopy.

After SolidStir Extrusion (SSE), the extruded material exhibits a refined microstructure, which can be attributed to dynamic recrystallization induced by the intense shear deformation during the process. Moreover, the intense deformation fragmented the coarse second phase of the base material and redistributed homogeneously throughout the aluminum matrix (Figure b,c). A dense population of bright-contrast particles corresponding to the Fe3O4 additions is uniformly dispersed. The EDS maps (Figure b1–b5) show enrichment of Mg around Fe-rich particles, depicting the formation of new phases separated from the precipitates which were evident in the base alloy. Higher-magnification imaging (Figure d) shows compositional contrast within individual particles, suggesting partial reduction during processing and the formation of a core–shell structure. Density measurements confirm a slight increase from 2.67 g cm–3 (base Al) to 2.72 g cm–3 for the composite, consistent with approximately 4 wt % Fe3O4 incorporated, based on rule-of-mixtures estimation.

Transmission electron microscopy of the processed composite (Figure ) provides direct and unambiguous evidence of the in situ reduction of Fe3O4 and the formation of Fe nanoparticles during solid-state processing. The HAADF–STEM image (Figure a) reveals a partially reduced Fe3O4-derived particle embedded within the Al matrix, exhibiting a distinct core–shell morphology with a Fe-rich core surrounded by an oxide shell. Corresponding STEM-EDS elemental maps (inset) confirm pronounced Fe enrichment at the particle center, while Mg, Al, and O are preferentially distributed toward the particle periphery, indicating the formation of MgO/Al2O3-rich reaction products. The EDS line profile across the particle (Figure b) further substantiates this chemical partitioning, showing a peak in Fe concentration at the core and a concurrent increase in O and Mg toward the shell region, consistent with oxygen scavenging by iron and magnesium during reduction. A bright-field TEM image acquired with the particle oriented near a zone axis (Figure c) displays dark-contrast regions associated with a high-density Fe-rich core. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) obtained from this region (Figure d) is indexed to body-centered cubic (BCC) Fe (α-Fe), confirming the formation of metallic Fe nanoparticles. These observations are fully consistent with the thermodynamic predictions (Figure c,d), which indicate that Mg-driven reduction of Fe3O4 is favorable at the processing temperature according to the reaction (Fe3O4 + Mg → Fe + MgO), followed by interfacial reactions between MgO and Al2O3, leading to the formation of a MgAl2O4 spinel at the particle–matrix interface. Collectively, the results demonstrate that the reduction reactions are highly localized and confined to nanoscale reaction fronts, producing Fe-rich cores encapsulated by stable oxide shells without altering the bulk Al matrix, thereby validating a solid-state, thermite-assisted reaction pathway.

The overall architectureFe core, MgO/Al2O3 shell, and sparsely distributed Al–Fe intermetallicsis consistent with the thermodynamic predictions shown in Figure and demonstrates that controlled in situ redox reactions can generate embedded Fe nanoparticles within an Al matrix while limiting excessive intermetallic formation. Atomic-resolution imaging of the outer oxide shell (Figure c) reveals a nanocrystalline-to-amorphous structure, indicative of rapid reaction kinetics and limited long-range ordering. Furthermore, high-resolution imaging of the adjacent Al matrix (Figure e), together with geometric phase analysis (GPA) strain mapping, reveals localized lattice distortions and dislocation structures surrounding the core–shell particles.

These strain fields likely arise from the combined effects of severe plastic deformation and localized exothermic redox reactions during processing. The presence of these distortions suggests the formation of nonequilibrium Fe-rich domains at the particle–matrix interface, further supporting the transient and diffusion-limited nature of phase evolution during SSE.

Magnetic and Functional Properties

The magnetic, mechanical, and electrical properties of the SSE-processed Al–Fe3O4 composite are summarized in Figure . Magnetometry measurements (Figure a) reveal a clear transition from the paramagnetic response of the base Al–Mg alloy to soft-ferromagnetic behavior in the Al–Fe3O4 composite after solid-state extrusion (SSE). Given the intrinsically paramagnetic nature of aluminum and the negligible magnetic response of both the as-received and SSE-processed Al base alloys (M s ∼ 0.005 emu g–1), their magnetization curves collapse essentially onto the X-axis.

In contrast, the Al–Fe3O4 SSE composite exhibits a measurable saturation magnetization (M s) of ∼ 1.6 emu g–1 and a coercivity (Hc) of ∼285 Oe. Although the magnetization does not fully saturate even at 2 T, the emergence of hysteresis clearly indicates the formation of ferromagnetic phases during processing.

To contextualize this magnetic response, it is useful to consider limiting cases. If no reduction of Fe3O4 occurred during SSE, the composite would be expected to exhibit a magnetization corresponding solely to the ferrimagnetic contribution of Fe3O4 (∼92 emu g–1 for bulk magnetite). For a 4 wt % addition, this would yield an upper-bound composite magnetization on the order of ∼3–4 emu g–1 (neglecting nanoscale suppression). Conversely, if complete reduction of Fe3O4 to metallic Fe occurred, the resulting Fe (M s ∼ 218 emu g–1) would produce a significantly larger magnetic response, potentially exceeding ∼6 emu g–1, depending on the retention of metallic Fe and the absence of secondary reactions.

However, TEM analysis confirms the formation of Al13Fe4 intermetallic particles, which are essentially paramagnetic and, therefore, do not contribute significantly to the overall magnetization. This observation implies that at least a portion of any reduced Fe reacts with the Al matrix rather than remaining as metallic Fe. If one assumes an intermediate scenario, for example, partial (∼50%) reduction of Fe3O4, the measured magnetization suggests that a substantial fraction of the reduced Fe must subsequently react with Al to form Al13Fe4. Otherwise, a significantly higher Ms would be expected. The experimentally observed value (∼1.6 emu g–1) therefore reflects a competitive balance between:retention of ferrimagnetic Fe3O4,partial reduction to metallic Fe, andconsumption of Fe into nonmagnetic Al–Fe intermetallic phases.

While the present study does not attempt to quantitatively extract phase fractions from magnetometry alone, the combined magnetic and TEM evidence indicates that controlling the Fe–Al reaction pathway will be critical for tailoring magnetic performance. In particular, suppression of Al13Fe4 formation while stabilizing nanoscale Fe or exchange-coupled Fe/Fe3O4 architectures could enable the development of mechanically robust, exchange-coupled composite magnets processed via solid-state extrusion. Future work will therefore focus on controlling reduction kinetics, oxygen activity, and interfacial reactions to promote magnetic phase retention and optimize exchange interactions.

Tensile testing (Figure b) demonstrates that the incorporation of Fe3O4 particles enhances the composite’s strength without severely compromising ductility. The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) increases to ∼384 MPa, approximately 1.5× that of the base alloy, while elongation remains at ∼12%. This improvement arises from synergistic strengthening mechanisms, including Orowan looping around oxide and intermetallic dispersoids, grain refinement from dynamic recrystallization, and dislocation accumulation near oxide–matrix interfaces. The corresponding work-hardening curve (inset of Figure b) exhibits a slower decay rate for the composite, indicating improved strain accommodation and delayed necking.

Figure c compiles hardness, resistivity, and magnetic data for the base alloy and composite. The Vickers hardness of the SSE-processed Al–Fe3O4 material increases by approximately 50% relative to the unreinforced alloy. The electrical resistivity is slightly higher in the composite, attributed to enhanced grain-boundary scattering, the presence of nonconductive MgO/Al2O3 phases, and lattice distortions surrounding the Fe nanoparticles.

Together, these results confirm that localized in situ thermite reactions during SSE can produce uniformly dispersed Fe nanoparticles that impart simultaneous improvements in mechanical strength, microhardness, and functional (magnetic and electrical) properties, demonstrating the potential of SSRP for fabricating lightweight, multifunctional metal-matrix nanocomposites.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a proof-of-concept approach for synthesizing lightweight, multifunctional aluminum matrix composites through solid-state reactive processing (SSRP) using SolidStir Extrusion (SSE). By coupling mechanical shear, frictional heating, and chemical potential gradients, in situ nanothermitic reactions between Fe3O4 and Al–Mg alloying elements were successfully activated under solid-state conditions.

Thermodynamic modeling and microstructural analysis collectively confirm the sequential reduction of Fe3O4 to metallic Fe and the concurrent formation of MgO/Al2O3-rich shells at particle interfaces. The resulting core–shell architectureFe cores encapsulated by mixed oxide shellssuppresses the formation of brittle Fe–Al intermetallics and stabilizes the Fe nanoparticles within the Al matrix. Transmission electron microscopy revealed Fe nanoparticles with a BCC structure and nanoscale oxide shells, consistent with the predicted redox pathway.

The composite exhibited significant enhancements in microhardness (∼50%) and tensile strength (∼1.5×) compared to the base alloy while maintaining reasonable ductility. Magnetometry confirmed the emergence of soft ferromagnetic behavior, validating the partial formation of metallic Fe domains within an insulating matrix.

Overall, this work establishes SSE-driven SSRP as a viable route for creating in situ metallic–oxide nanocomposites in aluminum systems. By enabling controlled redox reactions without melting or excessive intermetallic formation, this strategy opens pathways for designing lightweight, structurally robust, and functionally adaptive materials for applications in extreme-environment actuation, electromagnetic components, and advanced energy systems.
