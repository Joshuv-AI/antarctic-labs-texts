Bronchoscopic cryotherapy for biopsy and debulking of tracheal myxosarcoma in a cat
Authors: Wei-Tao Chang, Kurt Williams, Yung-Shun Chang, Robert T O’Brien, Olivia F Hsieh, Ching-Kai Lin, Chung-Hui Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case summaryA 10-year-old, castrated male domestic shorthair cat presented with stertor and severe respiratory distress caused by intratracheal masses occluding the majority of the tracheal lumen. Conventional endoscopic forceps biopsy yielded small tissue fragments, which were insufficient for a definitive histopathological diagnosis. Given the perceived high risk of tracheal resection and anastomosis, bronchoscopic cryotherapy with a 1.1 mm, flexible cryoprobe was performed for cryobiopsy and cryo-debulking. The masses occluded 80–90% of the lumen and were completely removed with cryotherapy, with immediate clinical improvement and decreased airway resistance (279 to 31 cmH2O/l/s). Haemorrhage was mild and controllable, and recovery was uneventful. Histopathology revealed tracheal myxosarcoma, a neoplasm not previously described in the feline trachea. The cat remained free of clinical signs for 7 weeks, after which clinical signs recurred, with radiographic evidence of regrowth. Pulmonary function testing suggested no substantial ventilatory compromise compared with preoperative findings; therefore, continued observation was chosen given the early recurrence. Three months after the initial intervention, bronchoscopy was repeated owing to gradually worsening clinical signs and confirmed tumour regrowth occluding approximately 50% of the lumen. A second cryotherapy session again achieved complete debulking and rapid resolution of signs.Relevance and novel informationThis report documents the first described tracheal myxosarcoma in a cat and demonstrates that bronchoscopic cryobiopsy can overcome the limitations of forceps biopsy while providing concurrent, airway-sparing palliative debulking. Bronchoscopic cryotherapy could serve as a feasible, minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic intervention for intraluminal tracheal masses in cats.

Introduction

Bronchoscopic cryotherapy is an interventional technique in which a cryoprobe is applied to target tissue, rapidly freezing intracellular water and inducing cytotoxic effects.1 –7 This process freezes the surrounding tissue, allowing for the removal of larger en bloc specimens compared with conventional forceps biopsy.2,4 –6 Given these advantages, it is widely used in human medicine for the diagnosis of various respiratory diseases and the management of endobronchial lesions.2,3 The ability to obtain larger samples with preserved architecture and improved histopathological assessment is a significant advantage.4 –7 Because cryotherapy is minimally invasive and can provide both diagnostic tissue and therapeutic palliation of airway obstruction, it is often preferred when bronchoscopic access to a lesion is feasible but surgical biopsy or resection is considered high risk.1,7 –10 However, its use in feline patients has not yet been reported.

Tracheal neoplasia in cats is uncommon and reported to date include lymphoma, adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, with a guarded to poor prognosis.11 –13 Clinical signs often develop when the airway lumen has already become substantially compromised in a later stage. Affected cats may present with stridor, stertor, cyanosis or respiratory distress. Therapeutic options include tracheal resection and anastomosis, bronchoscopic debulking using biopsy forceps or wire snares, tracheal stenting and radiation therapy.11,12,14 –18 This report describes minimally invasive bronchoscopic cryotherapy for concurrent diagnostic sampling and therapeutic debulking of a severely obstructive tracheal mass, with histopathology identifying a tracheal myxosarcoma not previously reported in feline medicine.

Case description

A 10-year-old, male castrated domestic shorthair cat was presented with a 10-month history of progressive laboured breathing, stertor, coughing, open-mouth breathing, weight loss and reduced activity. The cat was first referred to a minimally invasive and endoscopic specialist for rhinoscopic investigation of suspected upper airway obstruction. A right nasopharyngeal mass obstructing the choanae was identified and successfully removed endoscopically, resulting in complete resolution of clinical signs. Histopathology revealed chronic-active lymphoplasmacytic inflammation without malignancy.

Clinical signs recurred within 2 weeks and progressively worsened. Repeat rhinoscopy revealed no nasopharyngeal mass recurrence. Cone-beam CT performed under the same anaesthesia identified intratracheal masses, and tracheoscopy identified 50–60% occlusion at the thoracic inlet (Figure 1). Multiple biopsy samples were collected with biopsy forceps. Cytological evaluation of the tracheal mass initially raised suspicion for carcinoma. Histopathology identified small numbers of spindle cells and myxomatous extracellular matrix in the lamina propria, raising concern for an underlying sarcoma, but overt evidence of neoplasia could not be confirmed because of the small tissue samples.

Tracheoscopic images. (a) A large intraluminal tracheal mass at the level of the thoracic inlet causing approximately 50–60% airway obstruction. Mild intraluminal haemorrhage is present after a forceps biopsy attempt. (b) A smaller mass arising from the dorsal tracheal membrane, located just proximal to the carina

Three-dimensional volume-rendered images demonstrated that the larger tracheal mass extended approximately 1.7 cm in length and had ill-defined, irregular margins (Figure 2). Given the intratracheal location and the perceived high risk of tracheal resection and anastomosis, the cat was evaluated for bronchoscopic therapeutic planning. At presentation, the cat exhibited persistent respiratory distress with increased inspiratory effort and frequent open-mouth breathing. Pulmonary function testing suggested dynamic upper airway obstruction. Bronchoscopic cryotherapy was elected as a diagnostic–therapeutic approach to obtain larger specimens for definitive histopathology and to debulk the intraluminal mass to relieve obstruction. The owner was informed that studies in veterinary medicine are limited; risks and benefits were discussed based on the human literature and anecdotal experience. With consent, the procedure was performed.

Three-dimensional volume-rendered cone-beam CT image of the trachea. The larger intraluminal tracheal mass (bracket) extends approximately 1.7 cm in length and has ill-defined, irregular margins. A smaller additional intraluminal mass is visible more caudally (asterisk)

Tracheobronchoscopy (3.1 mm video bronchoscope, BF-XP290; Olympus) revealed a large intraluminal mass at the thoracic inlet occluding approximately 80–90% of the lumen (Figure 3), and a second smaller lesion just cranial to the carina, with no additional bronchial lesions. A 1.1 mm, flexible bronchoscopic cryoprobe was used under direct visualisation, with freezing applied for 3–6 s, followed by en bloc withdrawal of the bronchoscope and cryoprobe to retrieve adherent tissue and sequentially debulk the lesions until all the visible intraluminal mass was removed (Figure 4; see S1 in the supplementary material). Mild haemorrhage was self-limiting or controlled with topical 1:100 diluted epinephrine. A single perioperative enrofloxacin dose (2.5 mg/kg IV) was administered. The procedure lasted 77 mins, and oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry (SpO2) remained stable. Recovery was uneventful. Immediately after anaesthesia, the cat’s breathing normalised and laboured breathing resolved. Buprenorphine (10 µg/kg IV) was administered for analgesia, and three as-needed oral transmucosal doses were dispensed. Oral prednisolone was prescribed to mitigate residual airway oedema and inflammation.

Histopathology of the removed tracheal mass identified a myxosarcoma. The mass comprised abundant basophilic lacy myxomatous material with moderate numbers of neoplastic spindle to stellate mesenchymal cells and numerous small blood vessels (Figure 5). Minimal cellular atypia and no mitotic figures were noted in 10 high-power fields.

Histopathology of the tracheal mass, haematoxylin and eosin stain (4× magnification). Abundant pale basophilic myxomatous extracellular matrix and neoplastic spindle cells extend from beneath the papillated mucosal surface epithelium into the submucosa

The cat remained free of clinical signs for 21 days and gained weight. At recheck, stertor was absent and auscultation was unremarkable. Compared with radiographs obtained a few days before cryotherapy, radiographs at 21 days demonstrated restored airway patency (Figure 6a,b). Medical treatments were discontinued based on clinical improvement and radiographic resolution of the intraluminal obstruction.

Left lateral thoracic radiographs obtained (a) a few days before bronchoscopic cryotherapy and (b) 21 days after cryotherapy, illustrating marked preprocedural tracheal narrowing and postoperative restoration of tracheal lumen patency. (c) Left lateral thoracic radiograph obtained 7 weeks after cryotherapy, showing recurrent tracheal narrowing at the thoracic inlet compatible with regrowth of the intraluminal tracheal mass

Seven weeks after the cryotherapy, mild coughing and intermittent noisy breathing recurred, with occasional transient open-mouth breathing. Thoracic radiography suggested possible regrowth at the thoracic inlet (Figure 6c). Pulmonary function testing indicated that ventilatory function was not yet substantially compromised compared with the preoperative pattern, consistent with early recurrence (see S2 in the supplementary material). Continued observation was recommended.

Three months after the first intervention, a second tracheobronchoscopy and cryotherapy were performed because clinical signs had gradually progressed. Bronchoscopy revealed regrowth occluding approximately 50% of the tracheal lumen. Cryotherapy again removed all visible intraluminal tissue with mild haemorrhage that did not impede the procedure (Figure 7). The same enrofloxacin dose was administered during the second procedure. Total procedure time was 55 mins, recovery was uneventful and clinical signs resolved immediately. Because no buprenorphine was needed after the first procedure, no analgesic was prescribed after the second. At the time of writing, the cat remained asymptomatic 6 months after the second cryotherapy.

Discussion

This clinical report is the first description of the use of bronchoscopic cryobiopsy and cryo-debulking in a cat with intratracheal myxosarcoma. The cat initially presented with an inflammatory nasopharyngeal mass, which was successfully removed; however, recurrence of respiratory clinical signs led to the subsequent identification of an intratracheal mass. Histopathology indicated an inflammatory nasopharyngeal lesion, whereas the intratracheal lesion was neoplastic, supporting two distinct disease processes. This temporal coincidence may have contributed to delayed recognition of the intratracheal mass. Although radiography can suggest tracheal masses through focal or segmental narrowing or intraluminal soft-tissue opacity,12,14,19,20 tracheal narrowing may also occur secondary to upper airway obstruction in cats, 21 which can complicate interpretation when concurrent nasal/nasopharyngeal mass is present. CT provided critical clarification by defining the presence, location and extent of intratracheal masses and by enabling targeted bronchoscopic assessment. 22 The diagnostic course of this case highlights the importance of reassessing lesion localisation when clinical signs recur and prior findings do not fully explain the obstruction.

Endoscopic forceps biopsy is commonly used in veterinary practice, but small sample size and frequent crush artefacts can limit accurate histopathological interpretation.4,8 In cats, these limitations may be further amplified by the need for thin or ultrathin bronchoscopes with narrow working channels that restrict instrument diameter and tissue acquisition, increasing the risk of non-diagnostic sampling and tissue distortion. In the present case, these constraints likely contributed to the non-diagnostic forceps samples, whereas cryobiopsy provided larger en bloc specimens with better preserved architecture and fewer crush artefacts,4 –6,8,23,24 allowing definitive diagnosis of tracheal myxosarcoma. Although comparable veterinary data are lacking, human studies support this advantage, with one study showing that cryobiopsy yielded approximately fourfold larger samples with better epithelial preservation than forceps biopsy. 8 This benefit is attributable to cryoadhesion, in which freezing of the surrounding tissue permits extraction of more intact specimens and may also provide sufficient tissue for additional analyses when needed. 5

Beyond its diagnostic value, cryotherapy also offered immediate therapeutic debulking by removing larger tissue fragments and restoring airway patency during the same session.1 –3,6 Tracheal resection and anastomosis have been associated with substantial morbidity and mortality in dogs and cats, limiting their clinical applicability. 25 In human interventional pulmonology, endobronchial cryotherapy is commonly used for central airway obstruction when surgical resection is considered high risk;1,2,6 this human-based rationale also supported the choice of cryotherapy in the present cat.

Compared with reported human experience, the safety profile in this cat was likewise favourable. Haemorrhage was mild and controllable, and recovery was uneventful. In human studies, bleeding and transient hypoxemia are the most commonly reported adverse effects, whereas severe complications such as pneumothorax or major airway injury are uncommon, particularly for centrally located interventions.4,6,23,26 As a cautious translational extrapolation, bronchoscopic cryotherapy may also warrant consideration in future feline cases involving bronchial or lung neoplasia, foreign bodies or mucus plugging,1,2,6 although broader feline data are still needed. Case selection should favour bronchoscopically accessible, centrally located intraluminal lesions, with bleeding control and airway oedema plans in place.

Primary tracheal tumours in cats are uncommon and typically carry a poor prognosis once clinically significant airway obstruction develops;11,12,25,27 in a case series, median survival after diagnosis was only 1–2 weeks despite heterogeneous treatments. 27 Outcomes vary by tumour type,12,13,20,28 and therapeutic options for intratracheal masses include tracheal resection and anastomosis, bronchoscopic debulking with wire snares, electrocautery loops or grasping forceps, tracheal stenting, chemotherapy and radiation therapy.11 –18,25,29 Open surgical debulking plus cryotherapy provided palliation up to 16 months vs 9 months with snare debulking alone, suggesting cryo-based approaches may prolong, but not eliminate, disease control. 15

Myxosarcoma often exhibits highly locally invasive, infiltrative growth, with moderate metastatic potential.30 –35 In dogs, myxosarcomas are most frequently reported in the integument of the trunk and limbs. 30 In cats, published reports of myxosarcoma are scarce and have documented lesions in the subcutaneous inguinal region, retroperitoneal space, kidney, eyelid and ciliary body,31 –35 leaving its local behaviour and metastatic potential unclear in the trachea. In the present case, recurrence after bronchoscopic cryotherapy and the need for repeat debulking are consistent with reported palliation intervals and the anticipated behaviour of tracheal sarcoma, suggesting that cryotherapy is a palliative and airway-sparing approach rather than a curative treatment.

Conclusions

This case provides proof of concept for the application of bronchoscopic cryobiopsy and cryo-debulking in a cat with myxosarcoma causing severe intraluminal tracheal obstruction. The technique overcame limitations of conventional forceps biopsy by yielding high-quality diagnostic tissue while simultaneously providing effective palliative airway function restoration within the same session, with only mild, controllable haemorrhage and no major adverse events. Although the procedure was palliative rather than curative for tracheal myxosarcoma, the successful restoration of airway patency and favourable outcome in this single cat suggest that cryotherapy may be a viable interventional tool for selected intratracheal lesions. Bronchoscopic cryotherapy may represent a valuable minimally invasive option for carefully chosen feline patients with obstructive intratracheal masses, particularly when conventional forceps biopsy is non-diagnostic and when surgical resection carries substantial risk. Further studies and additional clinical reports are warranted to establish standardised procedural protocols, safety considerations and expected palliation intervals across different tracheal lesion types in small animals.
