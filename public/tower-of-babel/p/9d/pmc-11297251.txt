Benserazide is neuroprotective and improves functional recovery after experimental ischemic stroke by altering the immune response
Authors: Meike Hedwig Keuters, Velta Keksa-Goldsteine, Taisia Rõlova, Merja Jaronen, Pinja Kettunen, Aurora Halkoluoto, Gundars Goldsteins, Jari Koistinaho, Hiramani Dhungana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStroke is a leading cause of permanent disability worldwide. Despite intensive research over the last decades, key anti-inflammatory strategies that have proven beneficial in pre-clinical animal models have often failed in translation. The importance of neutrophils as pro- and anti-inflammatory peripheral immune cells has often been overlooked in ischemic stroke. However, neutrophils rapidly infiltrate into the brain parenchyma after stroke and secrete an array of pro-inflammatory factors including reactive oxygen species, proteases, cytokines, and chemokines exacerbating damage. In this study, we demonstrate the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effect of benserazide, a clinically used DOPA decarboxylase inhibitor, using both in vitro models of inflammation and in vivo mouse models of focal cerebral ischemia. Benserazide significantly attenuated PMA-induced NETosis in isolated human neutrophils. Furthermore, benserazide was able to protect both SH-SY5Y and iPSC-derived human cortical neurons when challenged with activated neutrophils demonstrating the clinical relevance of this study. Additional in vitro data suggest the ability of benserazide to polarize macrophages towards M2-phenotypes following LPS stimulation. Neuroprotective effects of benserazide are further demonstrated by in vivo studies where peripheral administration of benserazide significantly attenuated neutrophil infiltration into the brain, altered microglia/macrophage phenotypes, and improved the behavioral outcome post-stroke. Overall, our data suggest that benserazide could serve as a drug candidate for the treatment of ischemic stroke. The importance of our results for future clinical trials is further underlined as benserazide has been approved by the European Medicines Agency as a safe and effective treatment in Parkinson’s disease when combined with levodopa.Keywords: Benserazide, Ischemic stroke, Neuroinflammation, NETosis, Human iPS neuronal cells, Functional improvementSubject terms: Stroke, Acute inflammation, Preclinical research

Introduction

Cerebral ischemic stroke results from insufficient delivery of oxygen and nutrients into the brain due to obstructed blood flow. With stroke being a leading cause of death and disabilities worldwide and with an increasingly aged population, the costs of direct and indirect care are expected to rise exponentially over the coming decades1. Currently, intravenous thrombolysis or thrombectomy are the exclusive treatment options, only applicable to a small subset of patients due to a narrow time window or further limitations2,3. Ischemic damage to brain tissue initiates an acute inflammatory response, in part due to rapid infiltration of neutrophils into the brain parenchyma within the first hours post-stroke. After infiltration, these cells produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), and secrete enzymes and proteases (peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)), cytokines (e.g. IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), and chemokines (e.g. CCL2, CCL3, CCL5), thereby disrupting the blood brain barrier (BBB) and exacerbating brain injury4,5. Furthermore, neutrophils promote the formation of blood clots through the secretion of pro-thrombotic neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and several tissue factors. While resident microglia and infiltrating macrophages have been the target for immunomodulation after ischemic stroke to find curable solutions, neutrophils have re-emerged as a treatment target just recently. Like microglia, with their classification into pro-inflammatory M1- and anti-inflammatory M2-phenotypes, the view of neutrophils as a homogenous population is changing, leading to a similar classification of N1- and N2-type neutrophils6. While N1-type neutrophils have been suggested to be involved in neurotoxicity and the breakdown of the BBB, N2 neutrophils appear to influence the fate of microglia/macrophages towards the protective phenotypes, alleviating stroke pathology. Strikingly, the ratio of N1-/N2-phenotypic neutrophils has been proposed to be a determining factor in the post-ischemic outcome7,8.

Benserazide, a European Medicines Agency- (EMA-) approved drug, is a peripherally acting aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase inhibitor and is already used in combination with levodopa for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease (PD)9–11. Even though the effects of levodopa/benserazide (LB) have been studied mainly in PD, recent data have shown that the peripheral administration of high concentrations of levodopa with benserazide improves the functional recovery in an animal model of transient ischemic stroke by normalizing T-cell functions12–14, a finding thought to indicate neuroprotective effects of levodopa when reaching sufficient concentration in the brain.

Here, we examined whether benserazide alone can reduce ischemic damage in mouse models of both permanent and transient cerebral ischemia. Furthermore, we tested whether a reduction of the ischemic damage is attributed to reduced neutrophil infiltration into the brain parenchyma, hence the resolution of acute inflammation. To increase the translational relevance of this study, we performed some mechanistic studies on human material.

Methods

PMA-stimulated whole human blood chemiluminescence assay

For in vitro experiments involving human blood, either used as full blood or for the isolation of neutrophils, blood samples were provided by the Finnish Red Cross, Kuopio, from healthy volunteers who had consented to donate their blood for research purposes. For all in vitro experiments, benserazide (benserazide hydrochloride, Cayman chemical company, MI, USA) was diluted to a stock solution of 1 mg/ml in DMSO and further diluted in cell media or PBS to the desired concentration. Whole blood chemiluminescence assay was performed on fresh human full blood samples to elucidate antioxidative properties of benserazide on mainly neutrophils, and to a much lesser extend on haloperoxidase-deficient macrophages and leukocytes. Briefly, whole blood samples were collected into 2 mM EDTA-coated tubes and the red blood cells were lysed using Red Cell Lysis Buffer (BD Pharm Lyse, BD Biosciences, CA, USA). Following lysis, phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) was added to the samples at a final concentration of 1 µg/ml (= 1.6212 µM). Samples were simultaneously treated with 0, 10, 20, 40, or 80 µM benserazide and incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃ at 5% CO2. Next, luminol (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the samples and absorbance was measured at 425 nm using a Victor2 Microplate reader (Wallac 1420 Victor2 Microplate Reader, PerkinElmer, MA, USA). Unstimulated blood was used as control.

NETosis assay of human neutrophils

Human neutrophils were isolated from fresh blood samples using Lympholyte-Poly (Cedarline, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, EDTA blood was layered on top of Lympholyte-Poly (in a 1:1 ratio) in a 15 ml-tube. By spinning the samples for 35 min at 450 rcf without brakes at room temperature (RT), the blood was separated into 6 distinct layers. The 3 upper layers (plasma, monocytes, isolation media) were discarded; the layer with neutrophils plus the layer of separation media below were collected and washed with 10 ml HBSS without Ca2+ and Mg2+(HBSSw/o, Sigma Aldrich). After centrifugation (300 rcf for 10 min), the supernatant was discarded and the remaining red blood cells in the cell pellet were lysed using sterile MilliQ water. After a second wash in HBSSw/o (centrifugation at 250 rcf for 5 min), the cells were resuspended in 250 µl HBSSw/o and counted. Next, the isolated neutrophils were plated on a poly-L-lysine-coated 96-well plate at a density of 1–2 × 104 cells/well. Cells were treated immediately with 400 nM PMA in presence or absence of 2.5 or 5 µM benserazide in HBSSw/o. Simultaneous to the stimulants, cells were incubated with SYTOX green nucleic acid stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 30 nM. After 10 min of incubation, imaging was carried out to detect extracellular chromatin fibers and NETs. Therefore, fluorescence images (2–4 fluorescent images/well, 3–6 wells/condition; 20× magnification; 400 ms exposure time at 488 nm wavelength) were acquired every 15–30 min for ~ 6 h using time-lapse function (IncuCyte S3 imaging system, Sartorius, Essen Bioscience, Royston, UK). Unstimulated, stained cells were used as control. The SYTOX-reactivity of neutrophils undergoing NETosis was quantified on 1–2 fluorescent images per well at the relevant time point of 300 min using the IncuCyte program (Sartorius). Results were achieved by dividing the area by fluorescent intensity and normalization of control (untreated) cells to 100%.

SH-SY5Y cells in co-culture with PMA-stimulated human neutrophils

SY-SH5Y is a neuroblastoma derived human neuronal cell line that has adrenergic as well as dopaminergic characteristics. The neuronal cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco 21331; supplemented with GlutaMax (2 mM), 10% FBS and Pen/Strep (50 IU/ 50 g/ml); all reagents Thermo Fisher Scientific), on a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. Next, human neutrophils, isolated and plated as described before, were treated with 0, 25, or 50 µM benserazide in presence of PMA (0.5 µg/ml in HBSSw/o). Due to the reactivity of isolated neutrophils, the mixture (neutrophils in HBSSw/o including PMA and 0, 25, or 50 µM benserazide) was added immediately to the SH-SY5Y cells. After an over-night incubation at 37 ℃ at 5% CO2, the cell survival was assessed using the resazurin assay. In brief, cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 ℃ at 5% CO2 with pre-warmed resazurin at a working solution of 10 µM in HBSS (Sigma-Aldrich). Resorufin fluorescence was measured at 530 nm/590 nm (excitation/emission) on a Victor2 Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer). The results were expressed as a percentage of the mean fluorescence value (minus the fluorescence of the no-cell blanks) from control wells that contained cells that were not exposed to toxicants and were set as 100%.

Benserazide treatment of human iPSC-derived neurons

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were differentiated to cerebral cortical neurons as described elsewhere15. Neurons were plated on a 48-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well for 2 weeks in neural maturation medium (NMM, 50%/50% DMEM/F12 and Neurobasal, Na-pyruvate, GlutaMax (2 mM), B-27 with vitamin A, N2 supplement, Pen/Strep (50 IU/ 50 g/ml), NEAA, β-mercaptoethanol, and insulin (Sigma-Adlrich); all reagents from Thermo Fisher Scientific, unless otherwise stated). Three weeks later, neurons were re-seeded to a polyornithine/laminin-coated 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/ well using Accutase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 h in NMM, cells were treated with 2.5, 5, or 10 µM benserazide (in NMM without Pen/Strep) for 24 h at 37 °C at 5% CO2. Neuronal survival was assessed using the resazurin assay, as described before, using 20 µM resazurin; resorufin fluorescence was measured at 530 nm/590 nm (excitation/emission) on a EnSpire Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer).

Stimulation of human iPSC-derived neurons with PMA-primed human neutrophils

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were differentiated to cerebral cortical neurons as described above. After two weeks, 2/3 of the NMM media were exchanged with NMM media without Pen/Strep. In a separate set, neutrophils (isolated as described earlier) were plated at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well on a 48-well plate in 70 µl of NMM media (without Pen/Strep). Cells were then treated with different concentrations of benserazide (1 or 2.5 µM) in presence or absence of PMA (400 µM) for 1 h at 37 °C at 5% CO2. In addition, two separate groups with or without PMA stimulation were included to compare and contrast the protective effect of benserazide on cortical neurons. After stimulation, whole cell suspensions of conditioned neutrophils were transferred to the neurons and incubated for 24 h after which neuronal viability was assessed using thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Sigma-Aldrich) as described elsewhere16. In brief, the conditioned media were replaced with MTT at a concentration of 1.2 mM and the cells were incubated for 3 h or until purple crystal formation was visible. The media were removed, the crystals were dissolved in DMSO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for approximately 4 h at RT and the absorbance was measured at 585 nm with a Victor2 microplate reader (Perkin Elmer). The results were calculated as a percentage of relative MTT reduction compared to the control wells.

Western blotting

To elucidate the protective effect of benserazide on RAW 264.7 macrophages, cells were plated on a 12-well plate at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well for 24 h. The cells were divided into following treatment groups: untreated control; LPS only (50 ng/ml); LPS (50 ng/ml) + benserazide (2.5 µM); and LPS (50 ng/ml) + benserazide (5 µM); cells were treated for either 24 h or 48 h. Subsequently, cells were washed, lysed, and collected in SDS sample-buffer (0.0625 M TRIS-HCl, 2.3% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, 10% glycerol, and bromophenol blue). Ten µg of protein were loaded and run on 10–15% SDS-PAGE gels. The proteins were transferred onto a Hybond P membrane (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden) using a Mini TransBlot (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) chamber. Membranes were then blocked in 5% skimmed milk in PBST for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4 ⁰C with primary antibodies against arginase-1 (Arg1, 1:2000, Santa Cruz, TX, USA) (diluted in 5% BSA in PBS-Tween 20 with 0.02% NaN3). After washing three times with PBST, membranes were incubated in secondary antibody (1:2000; ECL HRP; GE healthcare) overnight at RT, followed by three washes with PBST. The proteins were visualized using ECL-plus kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and scanned with a Storm 860 scanner (Molecular dynamics, Caesarea, Israel). β-actin (1:5000 dilution, Sigma-Aldrich) antibody (diluted in 5% BSA in PBS-Tween 20 with 0.02% NaN3) was used as a loading control for total cell lysates and their immunoreactivity was detected with Cy3-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch laboratories Inc., PA, USA), followed by visualization using a Typhoon 9400 variable mode imager (GE Healthcare). Densitometric analysis was performed using imagequant TL software (GE healthcare). The protein expression data are shown relative to the expression of β-actin.

Ischemic surgery and treatment

All animal experiments were approved by the National Animal Experiment Board of Finland and followed the Council of Europe’s Legislation and Regulation for Animal Protection. Altogether, 99 adult (3-4 months of age), male mice were randomized to four individual experiments for either permanent occlusion of the middle cerebral artery (pMCAO) or transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) surgery. For the pMCAO study (Table 1, MRI and IHC), a total of 48 BALB/cOlaHsd mice (Envigo, Limburg, Netherlands) were subjected to either vehicle (per experiment: N = 24) or benserazide (per experiment: N = 24) treatment. A separate experiment was carried out to perform expression analysis for selected genes post-permanent ischemia. Here a total of 16 BALB/cOlaHsd mice were divided into vehicle- (N = 8) and benserazide-treated groups (N = 8) (Table 1, qPCR). For the tMCAO study, a total of 35 five C57BL/6OlaHsd mice (Envigo, Limburg, Netherlands) were assigned for vehicle (N = 17) and benserazide (N = 18) treatment to study protective effects of benserazide on lesion size and functional outcome (Table 1, functional assessment). All animals were housed in a temperature-controlled room on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. The mice were initially anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and maintained with 1–2% isoflurane in 70% N2O/ 30% O2. Body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C throughout the surgical procedure using a feedback-regulated heating system (Harvard apparatus; PanLab, Cornella, Spain). pMCAO was carried out as previously described17. After the exposition of the temporal bone, a burr hole of approximately Ø 1 mm was drilled right above the middle cerebral artery (MCA). The dura was removed, and the MCA was electro-coagulated (Aaron Medical Industries Inc., FL, USA). tMCAO was carried out as previously described18. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and maintained in isoflurane anesthesia. Following a neckline incision, a silicon coated monofilament (Ø 0.21 ± 0.02 mm) was inserted to the external common carotid artery and advanced through the internal carotid artery to occlude the MCA. The filament was advanced approximately 10 mm from the internal and external carotid artery bifurcation or until a resistance was felt and the filament was fixed in place. After 45 minutes, the filament was retracted to restore the blood flow. Following permanent and transient ischemia, mice received a single intraperitoneal administration of either benserazide (25 mg/kg, diluted in PBS) or PBS (as the vehicle treatment). For pain relief, buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously. To compare post-stroke weight changes between treatment and vehicle groups, mice were weighed before and seven days after transient ischemia.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed one day post-ischemia (dpi) using a 9.4 T Oxford NMR 400 magnet. Multislice T2 weighted images (repetition time 3000 ms, echo time 40 ms, matrix size 128 × 256 and Field of view 19.2 × 19.2 mm2, slice thickness 0.8 mm, and number of slices 12) were obtained using a double spin-echo sequence with adiabatic refocusing pulse. The images were analyzed using an in-house made software Aedes under Matlab environment (Math-Works, MA, USA), blinded to the study groups. The relative percentage of infarction volume was calculated using the following formula: Infarct volume = [volume of contralateral hemisphere – (volume of ipsilateral hemisphere – measured infarct volume)] / volume of contralateral hemisphere19. Data are presented as percentages ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Functional assessment post-surgery

Neuro scoring

Three days after transient ischemia, general and focal deficits were accessed in both benserazide- and vehicle-treated groups on a combined scale of 0 (healthy) to 56 (worse performance) as described previously20. Briefly, general deficits were scored based on fur (0–2), ears (0–2), eyes (0–4), posture (0–4), spontaneous activity (0–4), and epileptic status (0–12). Focal deficits on the scale of 0 (no deficits) to 4 (worst outcome) were scored based on body asymmetry, gait, climbing on a 45°-inclined surface, circling behavior, front limb symmetry, circling behavior when lifted from tail, and whisker response to light touch. The testing was carried out in a dimmed room (< 20 lux of illumination).

CatWalk gait analyses

Gait and locomotor deficits were accessed at 7 dpi using CatWalk, an automated gait analysis system (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, Netherlands). In a dimmed room (< 20 lux of illumination), animals were allowed to cross a horizontal glass plate (9 × 60 cm), illuminated with a green, internally reflected light. Footprints were recorded using a charged coupled (CCD) camera installed 40 cm underneath the glass plate and connected to a personal computer. The Catwalk XT software (Noldus) was used to record and analyze the paw parameters21. The camera gain and intensity threshold were set prior to experiments based on average mouse weight. The maximum speed variation was set to 50% and three uninterrupted runs were recorded per animal for analysis. Mice underwent training in both light and dark at two different time points before the baseline (BL) measurement. At least three successive measurements of gait parameters were done.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were terminally anesthetized with 250 mg/kg Avertin (Sigma-Aldrich) and perfused with ice-cold saline containing 2500 IU/L heparin (Leo Pharma A/S, Ballerup, Denmark), either 1, 3 or 7 dpi. The brain was post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 18–22 h, followed by cryoprotection in 30% sucrose for 48 h, and snap freezing in liquid nitrogen. Six to eight, 20-µm thick sections (400 µm apart) were cut at a cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Sections were blocked in 10% normal goat serum and incubated with primary antibodies: Glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP, 1:500, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule-1 (Iba1, 1:250, Wako Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan), myeloperoxidase (MPO, 1:100, Abcam, Oxford, UK) and Arg1 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA). For MPO staining, the sections were blocked for endogenous peroxidase (0.3% H2O2 in MeOH for 30 min) before serum blocking. Following overnight incubation with primary antibody, the sections were washed and incubated in secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or 568 (1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sections for MPO were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody (1:200, Vector, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 2 h and then with avidin-biotin complex reagent (Vector). Nickel-enhanced 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Sigma-Aldrich) was used for visualization of the immunoreactivity. Negative controls were included for each IHC staining and performed in parallel but incubated overnight without primary antibodies. A peri-ischemic area corresponding to 718 µm × 532 µm was imaged at a single plane with 10× magnification using AX70 microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to determine immunoreactivity of Iba1. For MPO and Arg1 staining, equivalent areas in the lesion were taken for analysis. In addition, a corresponding area from the contralateral hemispheres was also imaged to compare between and contrast with the ipsilateral hemispheres after stroke. For Arg1 staining, four consecutive sections, where Arg1-positive cells were evident, were taken. GFAP-stained brain sections were imaged using Zeiss Axio Observer (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany). For all stainings, the acquired images were quantified using ImagePro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, MD, USA) at a predefined threshold and presented as percentage of the positively stained immunoreactive area. All analyses were done blinded to the study groups.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Quantitative PCR analysis was performed to elucidate the effect of benserazide in ischemic stroke. Three days post-stroke, animals underwent intracardial perfusion with heparinized saline as previously described. Following perfusion, brain tissue samples were dissected from the peri-ischemic and lesion area of the ipsilateral hemisphere and from corresponding tissues of the contralateral hemisphere. The dissected samples were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at – 70 ℃ until analysis. To quantify gene expression, RNA was extracted from tissue homogenates using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to manufacturer’s guidelines. Reverse transcription was performed using 500 ng of total RNA, maxima reverse transcriptase, random hexamer primers, and dNTPs in the presence of ribonuclease inhibitor. The final cDNA concentration for all gene expression analyses was 2.5 ng/μl. The relative expression levels of mRNAs were analyzed for Hmox1 (Mm00516004_m1), Tnf (Mm00443258_m1), Aif1 (Iba1, Mm00479862_g1), PADi4 (Mm01341658_m1) and Gfap (Mm01253034_m1) following normalization with housekeeping gene b2m (beta-2 microglobulin, Mm00437762_m1) (all Thermo Fisher Scientific, if not stated otherwise). Relative mRNA expression was calculated with the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method, with Ct as the threshold cycle number and results presented as fold changes in relation to the control conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) using two-tailed student’s t-test or One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test; p < 0.05 was considered statically significant. All analyses of the animal experiments (MRI analysis, functional assessment, and IHC) were performed blinded to the study groups. Animals with incomplete occlusion or hemorrhagic lesion, as revealed by MRI, bleeding during surgery, or reaching a humane endpoint (weight loss of ≥ 20% and inability to ambulate) were excluded from further analyses as exclusion criteria were set prior to the experiment. Altogether, seven animals were excluded from the experiments due to incomplete occlusion (N = 5), humane endpoint (N = 1) and hemorrhagic transformation (N = 1). Statistical outliers calculated by Grubb’s test were excluded from analyses. Data are reported as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated.

All animal experiments were carried out according to national regulation of the usage and welfare of laboratory animals and approved by the Animal Experiment Committee in State Provincial Office of Southern Finland.

Results

Benserazide significantly reduced PMA-induced chemiluminescence in human full blood samples

PMA-stimulation of full blood induces a strong respiratory burst as well as the production of hydroperoxides and superoxides in neutrophils that can be assessed using the luminol chemiluminescence assay22–26. We tested whether benserazide can attenuate the formation of a strong chemiluminescence signal by preventing a respiratory burst. Chemiluminescence measurements from human EDTA-full blood samples revealed a strong signal of ~ 65 Ruthenium units (RU) after PMA-stimulation. However, 10 µM benserazide lowered the luminescence signal significantly. The luminescence signal further decreased with rising concentration of benserazide, reaching a luminescence signal as low as ~22 RU after adding maximum of 80 µM benserazide to PMA-stimulated full blood (Fig. 1a).

PMA-stimulated human neutrophils show less NETosis when treated with benserazide

Neutrophils are fragile and short-lived cells that serve an important role in the innate immune system. They provide defense against invading pathogens through phagocytosis, degranulation and through the important formation of NETs. This process, called NETosis, is one of the major programmed cell death pathways of neutrophils. However, in the condition of sterile inflammation, either by chemical or mechanical stimuli, NETosis can have detrimental effects on tissues. We tested whether benserazide can reduce the formation of NETs by PMA-stimulated neutrophils. NETosis was significantly increased when neutrophils were stimulated with 400 nM PMA compared to unstimulated neutrophils (control) as measured by SYTOX green area (p < 0.001). Importantly, administration of 5 µM benserazide to PMA-stimulated neutrophils significantly inhibited NET formation (p < 0.05). Benserazide alone had no effect on the formation of NETs when compared to control cells (all results Fig. 1b, c).

Benserazide prevented SH-SY5Y-neuronal cell death when co-cultured with PMA-activated human neutrophils

While NETosis is an integral part of the innate immune system, an excessive release of NETs by neutrophils can be harmful for cell survival and tissue integrity. Hence, we determined the neuroprotective effect of benserazide in a mixed culture of PMA-stimulated human neutrophils and neuronal SH-SY5Y cells. The addition of PMA-stimulated neutrophils to SH-SY5Y cells increased neuronal cell death by over 8% compared to untreated cells as measured with the resazurin assay. When neutrophils were stimulated simultaneously with PMA and benserazide (at different concentration), we detected significant neuroprotection against neutrophil-induced toxicity with 50 µM benserazide being the most protective (p = 0.006; Fig. 1d).

Benserazide reduced the harmful effect of NETosis on human iPSC-derived neuronal cells in vitro

We next tested whether the compound is equally protective in human cortical neurons challenged with activated neutrophils. Therefore, we differentiated human iPS-cell lines to mature cortical neurons, exposed them first to 2.5, 5, 10 µM benserazide to ensure the compound has no neurotoxic effect. In fact, benserazide promoted neuronal viability significantly at a concentration of 5 µM (p < 0.05; Fig. 1e). Next, we exposed the neurons to PMA-activated neutrophils and assessed the neuroprotective effect of benserazide using the MTT assay. While adding neutrophils to the culture reduced cell viability by half, co-stimulation of neutrophils with benserazide at both 1 and 2.5 µM significantly mitigated this reduction (p < 0.006; Fig. 1f).

Benserazide majorly increased the expression of Arg1 and HO-1 in LPS-challenged Raw 264.7 macrophages in vitro

In addition to neutrophils, microglia/macrophages are well-known mediators of inflammation and phagocytosis in stroke. When activated by either internal or external stimuli, these cells secrete several inflammatory factors that are damaging cells nearby. Therefore, we tested whether benserazide can modulate the inflammatory response of Raw 264.7 macrophages when stimulated with LPS (50 ng/ml). By using Western blotting, we observed increased protein levels of Arg1, a marker for protective M2a-type polarization, 24 h and 48 h after LPS stimulation of macrophages when benserazide was added at 2.5 or 5 µM concentrations (p < 0.05, p < 0.005 respectively; Fig. 2a–c). In addition, protein levels of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), an important factor in the Nrf2-pathway with respect to the redox balance post-stroke, was increased at 24 h by benserazide at both concentrations (2.5 and 5 µM) in LPS-exposed RAW 264.7 macrophages (p < 0.005; Fig. 2a, d). At 48 h, however, we detected a significant reduction of HO-1 protein level of macrophages stimulated with LPS and 5 µM benserazide when compared with LPS-stimulated cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 2a, e) (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for uncropped WB images).

Benserazide significantly attenuated the infarct volume in mouse models of permanent and transient middle cerebral artery occlusion

Due to both neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of benserazide in in vitro studies, we next tested whether it also provides neuroprotection in mouse models of permanent and transient cerebral ischemia. Administration of benserazide at a dose of 25 mg/kg right after ischemia ameliorated brain damage when compared to vehicle-treated mice in both the permanent (p < 0.05; Fig. 3a) and the transient model (p < 0.05; Fig. 3b) of ischemic stroke, when quantifying lesion volumes from MRI images at 1 dpi. Benserazide reduces neurological deficits and improves gait and locomotor abilities post-stroke

Neurological deficits and gait impairment are evident in both clinical and pre-clinical models of stroke. Thus, we examined whether benserazide can improve both general and focal deficits 3 days after transient ischemia using a neuro score assessment described by Orsini and colleagues 9–11,20,27. We found that benserazide-treated mice showed significantly better neurological outcome when compared with vehicle-treated mice (p = 0.043; Fig. 4a). Additionally, we observed a marked loss of body weight in vehicle-treated but not in benserazide-treated animals (p = 0.031; Fig. 4b), signifying the improved well-being of stroke mice after drug administration. Furthermore, kinetic and spatial parameters, typically used to detect ischemia-induced motor deficits, were significantly improved in benserazide-treated mice as revealed by CatWalk Gait analysis. The SwingSpeed, a kinetic parameter with which changes of the paw velocity when not touching the glass walkway can be assessed, was ameliorated in the right hind (RH) paw of benserazide-treated mice (p = 0.005; Fig. 4c). The StandIndex of the left hind (LH) paw, a kinetic parameter to describe the speed at which the paw is removed from the glass walkway, was also reduced in stroke mice treated with benserazide (p = 0.019; Fig. 4d). In addition, spatial parameter, the MinIntensity of the left front (LF) paw was decreased for benserazide-treated mice in comparison with vehicle-treated mice (p = 0.0365; Fig. 4e).

Benserazide reduces inflammation and induces a phenotypic shift of microglia/macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory immune response in the ischemic brain

Neutrophils respond and react rapidly after ischemic stroke and infiltrate into the brain parenchyma thereby further disrupting the BBB and aggravating brain damage. Neutrophils have been mainly reported to be present in brain parenchyma after permanent models of ischemic stroke. Hence, we investigated whether benserazide has any effect on the infiltration of peripheral neutrophils into brain parenchyma. We observed that benserazide was able to reduce the infiltration of neutrophils into brain parenchyma after stroke as revealed by MPO immunohistochemistry (p = 0.0003, Fig. 5a–d). As expected, ischemia induced a significant upregulation of Iba1 immunoreactivity in the peri-ischemic area (data not shown) and the ischemic area respectively (Iba1, Fig. 5e), when compared with contralateral. However, we did not observe notable alteration in iba1 immunoreactivity in ischemic area of mice treated with benserazide compared to control. Likewise, we observed unaltered GFAP immunoreactivity in the peri-ischemic area between control and treated mice at 1 dpi in the permanent stroke model (data not shown), we found a significant reduction of the ischemia-induced GFAP-immunoreactivity after benserazide treatment when compared to vehicle treatment (p = 0.004, Fig. 5f–h) in the transient model at 7 dpi. In addition, we also observed a significant upregulation of Arg1-positive cells in the lesion area of benserazide-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (p = 0.0022, Fig. 5i–k) after transient ischemia.

Benserazide altered the expression of inflammatory markers in the peri-ischemic brain tissue at three days post-stroke

We analyzed whether benserazide treatment modulates the expression of genes involved in inflammation and redox signaling after ischemic stroke and observed a downregulation of stroke-induced Tnf expression (p = 0.0009; Fig. 6a) in the peri-ischemic area 3-days post-permanent ischemia of benserazide-treated mice. Furthermore, the stroke-induced expression of the antioxidant gene heme oxygenase 1 (Hmox1) was reduced in the same area of the benserazide-treated mice (p = 0.0096, Fig. 6b). We detected a trend towards downregulation of the NETosis-regulating enzyme, PAD4 (Padi4, Fig. 6c) and the microglia marker Aif1 (Iba1, Fig. 6d) after benserazide treatment. Discussion

Benserazide is a DOPA decarboxylase inhibitor used to prevent early decarboxylation of levodopa to dopamine prior its entrance into the patient’s brain during the treatment of PD and restless leg syndrome9–11,27. In animal models, the treatment with levodopa combined with benserazide has been shown to modulate adaptive immune responses and to improve sensorimotor deficits post-stroke12–14 with the conclusion that levodopa, and especially its carboxylation to dopamine in the brain, enhances recovery, possibly via dopamine receptors expressed by astrocytes in the peri-infarct area. Here we show that a single peripherally administered dose of benserazide at 25 mg/kg, without any link to levodopa or dopamine, alters the inflammatory response, provides neuroprotection, and improves the behavioral outcome after ischemic stroke. There are various studies demonstrating favorable effects of benserazide at higher doses28–30. Benserazideadministration at lower concentration than 50 mg/kg resulted in complete inhibition of decarboxylase activity in the periphery and the CNS31. Repeated administration of 15 mg/kg benserazide in combination with 20 mg/kg levodopa was able to reduce cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells in a murine model of ischemia13. Therefore, the dosing for benserazide treatment was chosen such that the concentration of benserazide is low yet effective in mouse models of ischemia. Our additional in vitro and in vivo data suggest that these beneficial effects of benserazide may be due to reduced neutrophil infiltration into the brain parenchyma, reduced astrogliosis, and enhanced polarization of microglia/macrophages towards protective M2-phenotypes.

Neutrophils are the primary responders to ischemic stroke, infiltrating into brain parenchyma within 6 h post-stroke32,33. In the early phase after stroke, neutrophils can further limit the supply of nutrients and oxygen by clogging blood vessels, exacerbating BBB damage and lesion volume34. Therefore, dampening the activation and limiting the early infiltration of neutrophils is likely an efficient strategy to contain brain damage. In addition, several studies have emphasized the importance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) in the formation of NETs23,26 and their excessive production during sterile inflammation, such as ischemic stroke, suggesting NETs to be destructive for neuronal survival and tissue integrity8,35. Our in vitro data suggest that benserazide can limit the formation of hydroperoxide, superoxides and NETs after PMA stimulation, an indication of early protective effects of benserazide. Hence, we hypothesized that peripheral administration of benserazide immediately after ischemic stroke would dampen neutrophil activation and their infiltration into the brain parenchyma. Indeed, we observed reduced infiltration of neutrophils into the brain of ischemic mice after treatment with benserazide as revealed by MPO immunohistochemistry; an important finding as aberrant infiltration and activation of neutrophils are harmful to nearby neurons in the brain parenchyma32,35–37. While benserazide is central nervous system-impermeable (at recommended doses)38, damage to the BBB in conditions of ischemic stroke is assumed to allow easy passage of benserazide into the brain. We therefore investigated whether benserazide could also be directly neuroprotective. In fact, benserazide alone appeared to promote cell viability of iPSC-derived neurons slightly and was able to prevent iPSC-derived neuronal cell death when stimulated with PMA-activated neutrophils. The overall neuroprotection achieved by systemic treatment with benserazide was further validated in in vivo mouse models of both transient and permanent ischemia.

In addition to acute brain damage in the early phase after stroke, neutrophils also contribute to a delayed brain damage, which lasts for days. This secondary brain damage evolves from subsequent NETosis, release of inflammatory mediators, and a continuous disruption of the BBB, which is in turn associated with a high degree of neurological deficits and mortality32,38–40. Therefore, the impact on the neurological outcome post-stroke is an important measure of therapeutic efficacy of drugs. In this study, we observed reduced neurological deficits in mice treated with benserazide compared to untreated mice as reveled by neuro scoring. In clinics, almost 80% of all stroke survivors show gait impairments and, even after post-stroke rehabilitation, 25% of the survivors remain with residual gait impairments41. Therefore, improved kinetic and spatial motor functions are warranted from therapeutic regimen after stroke42,43. Here, we detected notable improvements in coordination and posture of benserazide-treated mice at 7 days post-stroke. Precisely, the decrease in StandIndex and MinIntensity of the left paws is indicative of improved coordination, while reduced SwingSpeed (RH paw) is a measure of balanced movement, as validated previously43. In addition, reduced weight loss of benserazide-treated mice after transient ischemia is an indication of positive wellbeing, reduced infarct size, and active motor and neurological functions 44.

Inflammatory responses, mediated by infiltrating immune cells and resident microglia, are known to exacerbate brain damage. Hence, anti-inflammatory drugs have been proposed to be suitable candidates for the treatment of ischemic stroke. To this end, we determined whether benserazide was able to modulate the inflammatory response elicited by immune cells, namely microglia/macrophages, both in vitro and in vivo. We observed rapid increase of Arg1-positive microglia/macrophages (in vitro and in vivo), suggesting an enhanced, ongoing polarization of these cells towards protective, anti-inflammatory phenotypes. In our previous studies, we have demonstrated that microglia/macrophage polarization is accompanied by reduced infarct size and resolution of inflammation45,46. For in vitro experiment, we chose Raw 264.7 macrophage as a model to study inflammation by exposing them to LPS due to their reliability and reproducibility. While experiments in primary murine microglial cells and neutrophils can provide more valuable information, it requires a lot of animals due to low yields.

TNF-α is an important mediator of stroke and correlates positively with stroke damage47. Likewise, increased secretion of TNF-α has been proposed to enhance neutrophil cytotoxicity and pro-inflammatory polarization of astrocytes48. We observed a significant reduction in mRNA levels of Tnf at 3 days (pMCAO model) and astrogliosis, as shown by GFAP immunoreactivity, at 7 days after transient ischemia in benserazide-treated animals, when compared with vehicle-treated mice, further confirming the protective potential of the drug. At earlier time points after permanent occlusion, however, mRNA expression levels of Gfap remained unchanged by the treatment post-stroke. This was, in combination with unaltered GFAP immunoreactivity with respect to the treatment at the early 1-day time point post-stroke, in agreement with previous reports showing a stable, weak-positive GFAP immunoreactivity between 6 h to ≤ 5 dpi49,50. Previous data showed a pronounced upregulation of HO-1 within 12 to 72 h after transient ischemia. HO-1 expression increases upon activation of glial cells, especially astrocytes and microglia/macrophages51. Accordingly, we measured at 24 h an early and strong increase in the HO-1 protein levels of LPS- and benserazide-co-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages in vitro. At 48 h however, the HO-1 expression was significantly decreased in the benserazide-stimulated macrophages. These results are in line with our in vivo data, showing a significant decrease of Hmox1 mRNA levels at 3 dpi in benserazide-treated mice. While in humans an early upregulation of HO-1 appears protective and beneficial post-stroke, pronounced and elongated HO-1 activity is suggested to cause pro-oxidant and neurotoxic effects, e.g. via increased production of carbon monoxide52. Besides ROS, PAD4 has been shown to be an important mediator for NETosis thereby contributing to BBB damage, blood-clot formation, delayed revascularization, and enhanced neurological impairment. Previous work has demonstrated a strong upregulation of protein and mRNA levels of PAD4 in peri-ischemic brain tissue in the subacute phase post-stroke53,54. In our study, we observed a trend towards reduced PADi4 mRNA expression in benserazide-treated mice compared to controls, after the overall infiltration of neutrophils has already been shown to be reduced by benserazide administration.

Various small molecules and biological drugs have been discovered and tested for the treatment of ischemic stroke with no success in clinical trials. As de novo-drug discovery is a lengthy and costly process, repurposing studies offer an attractive alternative due to already established clinical safety as well as pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data and properties in humans. Our promising data on the beneficial effects of benserazide in two stroke models supported with in vitro studies call for additional studies of replication and more detailed analyses to find out its potential to proceed in clinical trials of stroke or other brain insults involving strong inflammatory responses.

Conclusions

Immune cells, including neutrophils and microglia/macrophages, are crucial during brain inflammation and restoration of brain homeostasis. However, under the condition of brain damage, these cells migrate towards the site of injury and secrete an array of mediators that further amplifies the damage. Here, we have shown for the first time that benserazide reduces the harmful effect of neutrophils and microglia/macrophages both in vitro and in vivo, and that it provides neuroprotection and behavioral improvement after ischemic stroke. The beneficial effect of benserazide on stroke outcome is associated with microglia/macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory M2-phenotypes, and reduced infiltration of reactive neutrophils into the brain, thus limiting their harmful effects in conditions of sterile inflammation, such as stroke. Further studies are necessary to elucidate the exact mechanism underlying the benserazide-mediated neuroprotection after ischemic stroke.
