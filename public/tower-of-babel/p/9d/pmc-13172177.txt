Exploring the Association of Genetic Determinants of SIRT1, MTHFR, and MIR146A Gene Polymorphism with the Ischemic Stroke Predisposition: A Case Control Study
Authors: Abdullah Hamadi, Rashid Mir, Osama M. Al-Amer, Mohammed Alasseiri, Mamdoh S. Moawadh, Jameel Barnawi, Mohammad A. Alanazi, Atif Abdulwahab A. Oyouni, Abeer Al-Tuwaijri, Fawzyah Obeedallah Albaldi, Hassan A. Madkhali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke is a major cause of disability and the second greatest cause of death. Both acute and chronic strokes have few available treatment options. Nonetheless, the technique known as genome-wide association study (GWAS) is beginning to significantly influence our comprehension of the genetics of stroke. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to examine the associations between Sirtuin-1 (SIRT1), Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), and microRNA 146a (MIR146A) gene variations with ischemic stroke (IS) risk. The study included 200 participants: 100 with clinically confirmed IS and 100 healthy controls. Genotyping of SIRT1 (rs7069102), MTHFR (rs1801131), and MIR146A (rs2910164) was performed using the Amplification-Refractory Mutation System Polymerase Chain Reaction (ARMS-PCR). Blood biochemistry, including lipid profiles and glycemic markers, was determined using spectrophotometric enzymatic colorimetric assays. Statistical techniques employed included the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and multivariate logistic regression analysis to determine Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). In the codominant model, heterozygosity for MIR146A GC (OR = 1.97, P = 0.040), SIRT1 GC (OR = 1.84, P = 0.044), and MTHFR AC (OR = 2.55, P = 0.0019) was significantly associated with increased stroke susceptibility. In the dominant model, MIR146A (GC + CC), SIRT1 (GC + CC), and MTHFR (AC + CC) genotypes were strongly associated with increased risk (P < 0.05). In the recessive model, MIR146A CC and MTHFR CC genotypes were significantly associated with stroke risk, whereas the SIRT1 CC genotype did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.065). Allelic comparison revealed that the C alleles of MIR146A, SIRT1, and MTHFR were all strongly associated with stroke predisposition (P < 0.05). We conclude that there is a possible strong association between the SIRT1 rs7069102 C > G, MTHFR rs1801131 C > A and MIR146A rs2910164 C > G gene variants and ischemic stroke susceptibility. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first study to highlight the association between these genetic variations and the predisposition of stroke in our populations. Large-scale case–control studies in the future are required to confirm these results. These results, when confirmed, can be used in genetic testing for the screening and prognosis of stroke.

Introduction

Stroke is a prevalent global health concern, and Saudi Arabia is no exception. It plays a significant role in causing illness and mortality in Saudi Arabia, which is consistent with the broader global trend. More than 80% of strokes are ischemic rather than hemorrhagic, and they constitute a significant global health issue. Recent research has uncovered a troubling trend in the frequency and incidence of stroke in Saudi Arabia (Maalouf, et al. 2023). The World Health Organization (WHO) asserts that non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including stroke, make a substantial contribution to the country's mortality rate. Stroke accounts for around 6% of all deaths in Saudi Arabia (Tash and Al–Bawardy 2023). To advance precision medicine, genetic variables underlying polygenic and multifactorial illnesses, such as stroke, must be identified and validated.

The MTHFR gene is localized on the short (p) arm of chromosome 1 at position 36.22. It spans approximately 20 kilobases (kb) and consists of 11 exons. The rs1801131 (1298A > C) variant is located within exon 7. This specific transversion (adenine to cytosine) results in a glutamate to alanine substitution at codon 429, which is situated in the C-terminal regulatory domain of the enzyme. While less impactful than the C677T variant, this polymorphism is thought to decrease enzyme activity and potentially influence homocysteine metabolism (Araszkiewicz et al. 2025). The MTHFR gene codes for the 70–77 kDa subunits of dimeric proteins. A specific genetic variation, MTHFR rs1801131 (p.Glu429Ala), has been examined for its potential influence on metabolic syndrome and the risk of stroke (Raghubeer and Matsha 2021). The MTHFR enzyme is crucial for converting homocysteine to methionine, which is essential for DNA methylation and synthesis. Disruptions in this process might result in increased homocysteine levels, a recognized risk factor for many diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, which are a fundamental element of metabolic syndrome (Esse et al. 2019). Serum homocysteine is an amino acid that is produced when the body metabolizes methionine. Increased concentrations of serum homocysteine, a medical condition referred to as hyperhomocysteinemia, have been associated with the development of stroke and other cardiovascular disorders (Zaric et al. 2019). Polymorphism in the MTHFR C677T location has been linked to an increased risk of stroke in previous epidemiological studies (Mejia Mohamed et al. 2011; Chang et al. 2019). While the MTHFR C677T variant has historically been the primary focus in stroke genetics, the A1298C (rs1801131) polymorphism deserves separate attention. We recognize the known significance of C677T; however, our study examines explicitly the A1298C variant to understand its role in this population. Unlike the well-studied C677T, the A1298C variant is much less understood in this context, creating an important gap in our knowledge of local genetic susceptibility. Using this case–control study, we investigated the link between the MTHFR gene polymorphism (MTHFR: A1298C) and the risk of ischemic stroke.

The SIRT1 gene is located on chromosome 10q21.3, comprising 9 exons and spanning over 33 kb. The rs7069102 C > G polymorphism investigated in this study is an intronic variant. Intronic variations in SIRT1 are increasingly recognized for their ability to influence gene expression levels or splicing efficiency, thereby potentially altering the protein’s capacity to regulate vascular resilience and cellular stress responses during ischemic events (Kilic et al. 2015). SIRT1 plays a role in controlling lipid and glucose metabolism. It affects the function of multiple metabolic pathways and enzymes, including those involved in insulin sensitivity and fat storage. The rs7069102 C > G variant in the SIRT1 gene has been linked to various metabolic outcomes (Strycharz et al. 2018). Several studies indicate that persons with specific genotypes may have an increased susceptibility to developing metabolic syndrome, which is defined by disorders such as abdominal obesity, hypertension, excessive blood sugar, and abnormal cholesterol levels. SIRT1 possesses neuroprotective characteristics and plays a role in the reaction to ischemic injury, such as stroke (Kurylowicz 2016).

miRNAs are a class of tiny, non-coding RNAs that prevent messenger RNAs (mRNAs) from being translated or degraded. The MIR146A gene is situated on chromosome 5q33.3. It is a relatively small non-coding gene, with the rs2910164 C > G polymorphism located within the passenger strand (miR-146a*) of the pre-microRNA (pre-miRNA) sequence. This C to G substitution is critical as it can alter the secondary structure of the pre-miRNA, potentially leading to reduced processing and lower expression levels of the mature, functional miR-146a. Such changes disrupt the regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby predisposing individuals to chronic vascular inflammation (Jazdzewski et al. 2008; Curtale et al. 2010). The MIR146A gene harbors a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in which a cytosine (C) is replaced by a guanine (G). MIR146A is known for regulating genes involved in the inflammatory response (Laberge et al. 2023). Changes in MIR146A expression can impact lipid metabolism, a crucial component of metabolic syndrome (Elfaki et al. 2019). MIR146A is involved in the control of atherosclerosis, a disorder characterized by the accumulation of lipids, cholesterol, and other substances on the walls of arteries, which can lead to stroke (Malekmohammad et al. 2021). Given that MIR146A regulates inflammation, the rs2910164 polymorphism may affect specific pathways involved in stroke development (Bastami et al. 2019). It was recently shown that miRNAs have a critical role in regulating several pathogenic processes associated with ischemic stroke, including excitotoxicity, oxidative neuronal damage, and post-ischemic inflammation (Qin et al. 2022). In particular, it has been demonstrated that MIR146A plays a substantial role in downregulating the pro-inflammatory cytokines linked to neurological diseases (Chavda and Madhwani 2021). These results underscore the potential involvement of MIR146A as a viable therapeutic target during the acute and recovery stages of ischemic stroke. However, it is still unknown how SIRT1, MTHFR, and MIR146A affect ischemic stroke diagnosis and prognosis.

Given the complexities of stroke pathology and the growing role of genetic factors, this study aims to clarify the impact of specific polymorphisms in SIRT1 (rs7069102), MIR146A (rs2910164), and MTHFR (rs1801131) on the risk of stroke. While genome-wide association studies have expanded our knowledge of cerebrovascular genetics, data specific to populations is still limited. Therefore, we aimed to identify the risk profiles associated with these variants in a local group. This provides, to our knowledge, the first detailed analysis of these genetic interactions and their role in increasing the risk of ischemic stroke in this demographic.

Methodology

Study Population

This case–control study included 200 participants, of whom 100 were clinically confirmed stroke patients, while the remaining 100 were clinically confirmed healthy individuals.

All study participants were recruited from the outpatient neurology divisions at King Khaled Hospital and King Salman Military Hospital in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. In contrast, individuals attending routine medical examinations at these facilities provided the samples for the control group. All participants completed the informed consent form.

This research project was approved by the local ethics committee of the University of Tabuk (UT-91–23-2020). The study adhered to the tenets of the Helsinki Declaration and the accepted procedures for the use of human participants in research. All patients and control subjects provided informed consent prior to sample collection.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Consultant neurologists diagnosed ischemic stroke (IS) at the participating hospitals according to the World Health Organization (WHO) clinical criteria. These criteria define a stroke as the rapid onset of clinical signs indicating either a focal or global disturbance of cerebral function, with symptoms lasting 24 h or longer or resulting in death, and with no obvious cause other than a vascular origin.

All cases of IS were further validated by neuroimaging, specifically Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), to identify the area of infarction and differentiate ischemic events from hemorrhagic strokes or other non-vascular neurological conditions. Patients with hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), or strokes caused by trauma, tumors, or infections were excluded from the study.

Clinically confirmed stroke patients were included. The study comprised stroke patients of all ages, both male and female, and exclusively of Saudi heritage. The study also eliminated those who were not Arabic and stroke patients who had a history of any severe clinical problems.

Healthy participants without a history of severe clinical problems met the inclusion requirements for controls. Both males and females were included. Only Saudi participants were included.

Specimen Collection

Because the control samples were scheduled to coincide with routine blood draws as part of a routine check-up, no additional phlebotomy was required. Each healthy control participant received a signed informed consent form and a questionnaire. While the samples from stroke patients (about 3 mL) were collected in EDTA or lavender-top tubes. Blood samples were promptly kept at temperatures between -20 °C and -30 °C.

Data Collection from the Stroke Patients

A standardized questionnaire was employed to collect data, encompassing demographic information such as age and sex, as well as cerebrovascular risk factors, including systemic hypertension (defined as two blood pressure readings exceeding 140/90 mmHg or prior use of antihypertensive medication for this purpose). Dyslipidemia (characterized by total serum cholesterol exceeding 200 mg/dl, LDL surpassing 100 mg/dl, HDL below 50 mg/dl, triglycerides exceeding 150 mg/dl, or the utilization of medications such as statins or lipid-lowering agents), diabetes mellitus (identified through prior diagnosis or current pharmacotherapy), atrial fibrillation identified in previous assessments, and the current smoking status.

Genomic DNA Extraction from Stroke Patients and Healthy Controls

Genomic DNA was extracted from patient and healthy group samples using a standard DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen-Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA samples were subsequently dissolved in nuclease-free water and kept at 4 °C. The purity and integrity of the extracted DNA were assessed using NanoDrop™ (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The extracted DNA samples were screened for purity by measuring optical density (OD) at 260 and 280 nm. The A260/A280 ratio varied from 1.80 to 1.96, signifying high-quality DNA.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (Version 16.0, Chicago, IL, USA) to evaluate and compare data collected from healthy controls and stroke patients.

The distribution of genotype frequencies and the comparison of biochemical properties between cases and controls were assessed using the Welch Two–Sample t–test and the Chi–squared (χ2) test. Specifically, the χ2 test was used to compare the observed genotype frequencies in the case and control groups with the expected frequencies to test for Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE).

The initial association between the genotype frequencies of SIRT1 rs7069102 C > G, MTHFR 1298A > C, and MIR146A rs2910164 C > G and stroke risk was first tested using the Chi–squared (χ2) test to assess significant differences in distribution between cases and controls.

To better model and account for the effects of confounding factors on stroke risk, a multivariate logistic regression was performed. The presence of stroke (case vs. control) served as the primary outcome variable. The model incorporated the genetic variants as primary independent variables along with several covariates to account for potential confounding effects, including age, gender, and key lipid profile parameters (e.g., cholesterol, LDL, and HDL). The analysis yielded adjusted ORs and their corresponding 95% CIs. A P–value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all observations.

SIRT1 C Allele Confers Substantial Risk

The SIRT1 rs7069102 C > G variant showed a robust and significant association. Under the codominant model, the homozygous CC genotype was strongly associated with stroke risk, with a nearly fivefold increase in odds (OR = 4.93, P = 0.007). The heterozygous GC genotype also showed a significant increase (OR = 1.84, P = 0.044). Furthermore, the risk was confirmed in the dominant model (GC + CC vs. GG), with a two–fold increase in risk (OR = 2.07, P = 0.012). The allelic comparison showed a clear, independent association for the SIRT1 C allele (OR = 1.58, P = 0.048).

MTHFR 1298A > C Shows Dominant Risk

The MTHFR 1298A > C variant demonstrated the strongest statistical association with stroke susceptibility across all models. Under the codominant model, the heterozygous AC genotype was associated with a greater than two–fold increase in risk (OR = 2.55, P = 0.0019). Crucially, the homozygous CC genotype conferred a nine–fold increase in the odds of stroke (OR = 9.16, P = 0.006), indicating a powerful genetic effect. The dominant model comparison (AC + CC vs. AA) further supported this finding, showing a highly significant risk elevation (OR = 2.85, P = 0.0004), and the MTHFR C allele itself exhibited the strongest allelic association observed (OR = 2.17, P = 0.0004).

MIR146A Association Across Models

The MIR146A rs2910164 C > G variant consistently demonstrated an association with stroke across the inheritance models. In the codominant model, both the GC (OR = 1.97, P = 0.040) and CC (OR = 2.74, P = 0.009) genotypes were associated with increased susceptibility. The dominant model comparison (GC + CC vs. GG) also revealed a two–fold elevation in risk (OR = 2.10, P = 0.013). This consistent pattern across inheritance models was validated by the highly significant allelic comparison, where the MIR146A C allele was strongly associated with stroke susceptibility (OR = 2.01, P = 0.0007).

Discussion

Stroke is a major global health issue. It currently ranks as the second leading cause of chronic disability and death worldwide, with incidence rates still rising (Venketasubramanian 2023). Its complex causes include known environmental and medical risk factors, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, smoking, high blood lipids, obesity, and systemic conditions like coronary artery disease (Hankey 2020). These factors mainly lead to atherosclerosis. In this condition, lipids accumulate and harden the arterial walls, narrow the vessel lumen, and promote clot formation, which can impede or block blood flow to the brain. Progress in genomics has been driven by Genome–Wide Association Studies (GWAS). Researchers have linked many genetic locations to stroke and related systemic diseases (Tcheandjieu et al. 2022; Gharahkhani et al. 2021). Large–scale GWAS meta–analyses across different ancestry groups have identified over 100 distinct genetic risk locations (Gharahkhani et al. 2021). Targeted sequencing has also revealed potentially harmful variants in previously unconnected genes, such as GPR142, PTPRN2, and IKBKB. This highlights the complexity of the genetic factors underlying conditions such as embolic stroke of undetermined origin (ESUS) (Ilinca et al. 2020).

In light of this broad, yet often heterogeneous, genetic landscape, our study specifically addressed the concurrent impact of polymorphisms in three critical pathways: metabolic regulation (MTHFR), vascular resilience (SIRT1), and chronic inflammation (MIR146A). We utilized a case–control design to evaluate the association of MTHFR 1298A > C, SIRT1 rs7069102 C > G, and MIR146A rs2910164 C > G variants with stroke susceptibility in our previously uncharacterized cohort. Overall, we found that the presence of the minor C allele across all three polymorphisms significantly increased stroke risk, with the MTHFR 1298CC genotype conferring the highest observed risk (OR = 9.16). The subsequent sections discuss these specific findings in detail.

SIRT1 rs7069102 C > G Polymorphism and Vascular Resilience

Our findings regarding the SIRT1 rs7069102 C > G polymorphism underscore the critical role of the SIRT1 enzyme in stroke risk. As an NAD+ –dependent deacetylase, SIRT1 is a highly valuable therapeutic candidate due to its ability to regulate gene expression and adjust cell metabolism in response to ischemic stress (Iside et al. 2020). SIRT1 is involved in numerous biological processes, and its protective roles span aging, neurodegeneration, and cardiovascular health (Jiao and Gong 2020). Consistent with its established neuroprotective functions against cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) damage (Mei et al. 2022), our results showed that the presence of the minor C allele was significantly associated with increased stroke risk. Specifically, the heterozygous GC genotype (OR = 1.84, P = 0.044) and the homozygous CC genotype (OR = 4.93, P = 0.007) conferred a substantially higher risk in the codominant model. The dominant model comparison (GG vs. GC + CC) confirmed this elevated risk (OR = 2.07, P = 0.012), demonstrating that the C allele—which was more prevalent in the stroke group—is linked to compromised vascular resilience against ischemic injury.

MTHFR 1298A > C Polymorphism and Metabolic Risk

The MTHFR 1298A > C polymorphism (rs1801131) displayed the most potent association with stroke risk in our cohort, despite conflicting findings in the broader literature. While some studies, such as those by Lin et al. in Chinese adults (Yao et al. 2012) and Akar et al. in Turkish children (Akar et al. 2001), have reported no discernible variation in the MTHFR A1298C polymorphism and stroke incidence, others provide strong evidence for association. For example, Sazci et al. identified distinct risk profiles, linking the C allele specifically to ischemic stroke (Sazci et al. 2006). Furthermore, meta–analyses, including those compiled by Zhang (Zhang et al. 2014) and Kumar et al. (Kumar et al. 2020), suggest that the A1298C polymorphism may serve as a predictor for ischemic stroke, particularly in adults (Dong et al. 2021). Our findings strongly corroborate this positive association. We identified a potential association between the heterozygous AC genotype and heightened susceptibility (OR = 2.55, P = 0.0019), with the homozygous CC genotype conferring a highly significant, nearly nine–fold increase in risk (OR = 9.16, P = 0.006). These results have implications for genetic screening and risk stratification in clinical practice, emphasizing the importance of considering this variant in population–specific assessments. Given that variations in MTHFR are implicated in hyperhomocysteinemia, a well–established mechanism for promoting endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and thrombosis, these results highlight a critical genetic lever operating within the metabolic pathways contributing to stroke (Mazdeh et al. 2021). The elevated frequency of the C allele in our stroke patients further reinforces the necessity of considering this variant in population–specific risk assessments.

MIR146A rs2910164 C > G Polymorphism and Inflammation

The robust association found for the MIR146A rs2910164 C > G polymorphism supports the emerging role of non–coding RNA in modulating stroke risk via inflammatory pathways. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) can fundamentally alter a variety of biological processes by influencing the maturation, expression levels, secondary structure, or even target selection of microRNAs (miRNAs), leading to disease (Nalbant and Akkaya–Ulum 2024). MIR146A is a recognized negative regulator of the inflammatory response, and its overexpression has been observed in aging human endothelial cells, potentially inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and influencing apoptotic sensitivity (Jeon et al. 2013). These mechanisms are relevant because chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction are key contributors to stroke pathogenesis. Our results demonstrated that the heterozygous GC genotype (OR = 1.97, P = 0.040) and the homozygous CC genotype (OR = 2.74, P = 0.009) were significantly associated with stroke susceptibility. The C allele itself was strongly linked to increased risk (OR = 2.01, P = 0.0007). While some population studies suggest rs2910164 may be a better predictor of stroke prognosis rather than incidence (Qiao et al. 2023; Bargiel, et al. 2021), our data, aligned with those reporting an association in Asian populations, suggest a direct link to susceptibility in our cohort. These findings collectively suggest that genetic variation in this key inflammation regulator may predispose individuals to chronic vascular damage, emphasizing the importance of understanding inflammatory pathways in stroke risk.

We observed a statistically significant difference in the mean age between the stroke patients (58 ± 9 years) and the healthy control group (36 ± 13 years) (P < 0·0001). We acknowledge that age is a potent, non–modifiable risk factor for ischemic stroke, and the older age of the patient cohort reflects the natural epidemiological prevalence of the disease. While this age disparity may confound age–dependent biochemical variables (such as lipid profiles and hormone levels), it does not compromise the validity of the genetic association results. The genetic variants investigated in this study (SIRT1 rs7069102, MTHFR rs1801131, and MIR146A rs2910164) are germline polymorphisms that remain stable throughout an individual's lifespan. Therefore, the genotype frequencies observed in the younger control group are representative of their genetic makeup regardless of their future age. However, we recommend interpreting the associations observed between these genotypes and biochemical markers (e.g., cholesterol, LDL) with caution, as these markers are physiologically influenced by aging.

Although there is a significant commitment required to translate these discoveries, many of the genetic connections that have previously been found probably hold the key to innovative approaches for stroke prevention and treatment. The need for translational prospects only intensifies with the ongoing fast advancements in stroke genetics. However, only a small percentage of these examples involved medication development driven by genetics.

Conclusion

This study shows a strong and meaningful link between single–nucleotide polymorphisms in three important genes, SIRT1 (rs7069102 C > G), MTHFR (rs1801131 1298A > C), and MIR146A (rs2910164 C > G), and a higher risk of ischemic stroke. Our findings reveal that the minor C allele in all three variants significantly raises stroke risk. This highlights the complicated relationship between metabolic regulation, vascular resilience, and chronic inflammation in the disease's development. Specifically, individuals with the MTHFR 1298CC genotype showed the highest risk, indicating a strong genetic influence on susceptibility in our group. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine and emphasize the combined association of these genetic variations with stroke risk in our population. These results are crucial for identifying individuals who are at high risk and for creating a genetic risk profile for the region. While these findings are strong, we need large–scale, multicenter case–control studies to confirm the significance and impact of these associations. Once validated, the results of this study hold considerable promise, suggesting that these genetic markers could be included in genetic testing panels to improve screening, risk assessment, diagnosis, and prognosis of ischemic stroke in clinical practice.
