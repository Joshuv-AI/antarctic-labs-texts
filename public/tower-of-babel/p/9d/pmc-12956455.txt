Comparing Susceptibility-Weighted Imaging and T2* Gradient-Recalled Echo for Cerebral Microbleeds Detection: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Su Jeong Yang, Jae-Sung Lim, Yangsean Choi, Ho Sung Kim, Sang Joon Kim, Jae-Hong Lee, Chong Hyun Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeCriteria for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities in anti-amyloid therapy are based on T2* gradient-recalled echo (GRE), but susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) is widely used, creating uncertainty. This study quantitatively compared the detectability of SWI and GRE for cerebral microbleeds and established evidence supporting distinct microbleed criteria for each.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines. PubMed and Embase were searched for studies directly comparing SWI and GRE up to August 8, 2024. Study quality was assessed with QUADAS-2. The pooled proportion of microbleed detection and detection ratio were calculated. Subgroup analyses were performed based on magnetic field strength (1.5 T vs. 3 T) and SWI slice thickness (<2 mm vs. ≥2 mm), equipment vendor, and study quality.ResultsThirteen studies were included. SWI detected cerebral microbleeds approximately 1.6 times more effectively than GRE. At 3.0 T and 1.5 T, SWI exhibited 1.7-fold and 1.5-fold greater detectability, respectively. SWI with thinner slices (<2 mm) showed a 1.9-fold improvement, while thicker slices (≥2 mm) showed a 1.3-fold improvement. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences between vendors (0.61 vs. 0.60, p=0.89), or by study quality (0.61 vs. 0.59, p=0.89).ConclusionsSWI detects cerebral microbleeds about 1.6 times more effectively than GRE, highlighting important differences between the two techniques. Cautious exploration of adjusted thresholds may be needed, and prospective validation in therapy-specific cohorts will be essential before clinical application.Keywords: cerebral microbleed, susceptibility-weighted imaging, T2* gradient-recalled echo, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, anti-amyloid therapy

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease is a major neurodegenerative disease, and its prevalence has increased worldwide with the aging population.1,2 Although two types of medication—cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist (memantine)—alleviate symptoms, they do not slow disease progression.3 Anti-amyloid therapy, a new class of therapeutic agents targeting amyloid accumulated in the brain, has been approved for Alzheimer’s disease treatment. This therapy works by inducing an immune response that facilitates amyloid elimination through antibody administration.4,5 Anti-amyloid therapy is expected to serve as a disease-modifying treatment that may delay cognitive decline.5

A well-documented complication of this therapy is amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which manifest as edema (ARIA-E) and hemorrhage (ARIA-H).4,6,7,8 These abnormalities can increase vascular permeability, leading to the extravasation of protein components (ARIA-E) and blood components (ARIA-H).4,8 ARIA-H typically presents as microbleeds and superficial siderosis.4,8 The severity of ARIA-H and the eligibility criteria for anti-amyloid therapy are determined based on the number of microbleeds, which are currently assessed using gradient-recalled echo (GRE) imaging.6,7 Additionally, a recent study demonstrated that the number of microbleeds is proportional to the risk of developing ARIA.9 Therefore, microbleed count is a critical factor in clinical decision-making, influencing whether to initiate, continue, or discontinue anti-amyloid therapy.

Although microbleed criteria for anti-amyloid therapy are based on GRE, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) has already been widely adopted in many hospitals, leading to considerable uncertainty. While adding GRE for microbleed evaluation remains an option, SWI is recognized for its superior microbleed detection and enhanced ability to identify superficial siderosis.10 SWI is ultimately expected to replace GRE for microbleed evaluation; however, specific microbleed criteria using SWI have not yet been established in ARIA-H assessment. Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively assess the superior detectability of SWI over GRE for cerebral microbleed detection. Additionally, this study sought to establish evidence for applying distinct microbleed criteria for SWI and GRE, respectively.

METHODS

This meta-analysis was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (number: CRD420250654456).

Literature search strategies and study selection

A literature search was conducted using the PubMed and Embase databases to identify studies that directly compared microbleed detection between GRE and SWI, regardless of disease. The search terms were as follows: ([microbleed*] OR [microhemorrhage]) AND ([gradient recalled echo] OR [gradient recall echo] OR [gradient echo] OR [GRE] OR [“T2*-weighted”]) AND ([susceptibility-weighted imaging] OR [susceptibility-weighted] OR [SWI]). The database search was updated until August 8, 2024.

The search was conducted by a single reviewer (C.H.S.) with support from a medical research librarian. Two independent reviewers (S.J.Y. and C.H.S. with 1 and 12 years of experience in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, respectively) reviewed and selected studies, resolving any disagreements through consensus.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies comparing both the SWI and GRE in the same patients for direct comparison of microbleed detectability between SWI and GRE were eligible for inclusion, regardless of the underlying disease of the cohort. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) studies that performed only indirect comparisons between GRE and SWI or used only one sequence (either GRE or SWI) for microbleed detection were excluded; 2) review articles, case reports, letters, comments, and conference abstract and note; 3) animal studies; 4) studies with a partially overlapping patient cohort; 5) studies not written in English; and 6) studies without assessable full text.

Data extraction

The total number of microbleeds detected using GRE and SWI was evaluated in each selected study. Data were extracted using a standardized form that included the following: 1) study characteristics, including the author, publication year, institution name and country, number of patients, gender distribution, and study design; 2) the total number of detected microbleeds in GRE and SWI; and 3) MRI protocols, including slice thickness, slice gap, echo time (TE), magnet strength, vendor, and scanner model. One reviewer (S.J.Y.) performed data extraction using the standardized form, while another reviewer (C.H.S.) verified the extracted data. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers (S.J.Y. and C.H.S.) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using tailored questionnaires and criteria from the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2).11

Data synthesis and analysis

The pooled proportion of microbleed detection was evaluated across all included studies. The detection ratio was defined as the proportion of microbleeds identified on GRE relative to those detected on SWI. Microbleed detection in SWI was considered the reference standard. Additional subgroup analyses were performed based on magnetic field strength (1.5 T vs. 3 T), SWI slice thickness (<2 mm vs. ≥2 mm), equipment vendor (GE vs Siemens), and study quality. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Higgins’ inconsistency index (I2), with values above 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test, with p<0.1 indicating significant bias. Statistical analyses were conducted using the metafor package in R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Eligible study characteristics

A literature search of PubMed and Embase identified 243 articles, with one additional article included through a manual search. After removing five duplicates and excluding 161 studies based on abstract reviews for not meeting the inclusion criteria, the full texts of the remaining 77 articles were reviewed. Of these, 64 studies were excluded for being indirect comparisons (n=4), review articles or case reports (n=6), unrelated to the field of interest (n=48), published in languages other than English (n=3), or having inaccessible full texts (n=3). Ultimately, 13 eligible studies were included in this analysis (Fig. 1).

The slice gap in SWI was 0 mm in most studies, although two studies employing GRE used gap. The inclusion criteria varied across studies and encompassed healthy controls, individuals with memory impairments, and those with cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), among other conditions (Table 3).

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed using QUADAS-2 (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). In evaluating the risk of bias, two studies had an unclear risk in the patient selection domain due to a lack of exclusion criteria. In the index test domain, three studies had a high risk because independent readings were not conducted between GRE and SWI. Two studies had an unclear risk due to insufficient details regarding the time interval between SWI and GRE readings and whether independent readings were conducted. The remaining studies had a low risk of bias. Most studies had a low risk regarding the reference standard; however, three studies had an unclear risk because they did not provide detailed microbleed definitions. All studies had a low risk concerning flow and timing. No studies were deemed to have a high risk in terms of applicability concerns, but three studies had unclear concerns regarding the reference standard due to the absence of detailed microbleed definitions. Assessing funnel plot, asymmetry was suspected (Supplementary Fig. 3 in the online-only Data Supplement); however, using Egger’s test, a significant publication bias was not identified (p=0.415).

Comparison between GRE and SWI.

We analyzed the pooled proportion of microbleed detection using GRE relative to SWI. The overall pooled proportion was estimated at 0.61 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.49–0.71) (Fig. 2). Thus, SWI detected cerebral microbleeds approximately 1.6 times more effectively than GRE (SWI/GRE=1/0.61=1.6). At magnetic field strengths of 3.0 T and 1.5 T, the pooled proportions were 0.59 (95% CI: 0.48–0.69) and 0.65 (95% CI: 0.34–0.87), respectively (Fig. 3). Therefore, SWI showed approximately 1.7-fold greater microbleed detection at 3.0 T (SWI/GRE=1/0.59=1.7) and 1.5-fold greater detection at 1.5 T (SWI/GRE=1/0.65=1.5). Heterogeneity was observed overall (I2=98%) and at both 3.0 T (I2=92%) and 1.5 T (I2=99%). Although microbleed detection was higher at 3.0 T than at 1.5 T, the difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (p=0.73).

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on SWI slice thickness (<2 mm vs. ≥2 mm) (Fig. 4). The pooled proportions were 0.52 (95% CI: 0.41–0.62) for slice thicknesses <2 mm and 0.77 (95% CI: 0.60–0.88) for thicknesses ≥2 mm, with heterogeneity of 93% and 91%, respectively. For SWI slice thicknesses <2 mm, SWI exhibited a 1.9-fold increase in microbleed detection (SWI/GRE=1/0.52=1.9), whereas for thicknesses ≥2 mm, the increase was 1.3-fold (SWI/GRE=1/0.77=1.3) compared with GRE. This increase was significantly greater for slices <2 mm (p=0.01). These findings suggest that SWI slice thickness may be a contributing factor to heterogeneity, although this should be interpreted cautiously. Additional subgroup analysis was conducted based on equipment vendor (GE vs. Siemens). The pooled proportions were 0.61 (95% CI: 0.48–0.73) for GE equipment and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.40–0.77) for Siemens equipment. There were no significant differences between the two vendors (p=0.89). In addition, based on quality assessment, we performed a subgroup analysis excluding three high-risk studies. When these studies were excluded, the pooled proportion was 0.61 (95% CI: 0.49–0.58), while the pooled proportion of the three high-risk studies was 0.59 (95% CI: 0.24–0.86). There were no significant differences based on quality assessment, QUADAS-2 (p=0.89).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the comparative efficacy of microbleed detection between SWI and GRE. SWI exhibited a 1.6-fold improvement in detecting cerebral microbleeds compared to GRE. Subgroup analysis indicated that microbleed detection was more efficient at higher magnetic field strengths and with thinner SWI slices. At 3.0 T and 1.5 T, SWI demonstrated 1.7-fold and 1.5-fold higher detection rates, respectively. With SWI slice thicknesses <2 mm, detection improved by 1.9-fold, whereas slices 2 mm showed a 1.3-fold improvement. Due to the variability in detection efficiency between GRE and SWI, these findings underscore the need to consider technique-related factors when microbleed quantification is critical for clinical decision-making, particularly in the context of anti-amyloid therapy eligibility, ARIA-H and CAA.

Consistent with previous studies,14,15,16 SWI demonstrated superior detection capabilities compared to GRE, as previously reported. However, unlike earlier studies,14,15,16 this study focused exclusively on direct comparison cohorts, thereby providing a more robust pooled estimate and quantitative approach of the relative detection efficacy between the two modalities. In contrast, a separate meta-analysis on strictly lobar microbleeds found no significant differences between SWI and GRE in microbleed detection.17 However, unlike our study, that analysis did not count each microbleed in the same patients and included a disproportionate number of studies favoring GRE (11 for GRE vs. only three for SWI), underscoring differences in methodology.

Current imaging guidelines for anti-amyloid therapy and ARIA-H rely on GRE-based microbleed criteria.6,7 A previous meta-analysis reported pooled incidence rates of ARIA-E at 6.5%–9.5% and ARIA-H at 7.8%–17.7%.18,19 Applying GRE-based criteria for ARIA-H to SWI could lead to the exclusion of eligible candidates due to the higher detection sensitivity of SWI. Based on current criteria, GRE based thresholds are set at 4 microbleeds for the treatment eligibility and mild/moderate ARIA-H, and at 10 for moderate/severe ARIA-H. Importantly, when these thresholds are applied to SWI without adjustment, Monte Carlo simulation results of Sima et al.20 demonstrated that using SWI instead of GRE increased the proportion of patients classified as having moderate-to-severe ARIA-H by more than 50% across three monoclonal antibody therapies (aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab). Since SWI detects microbleeds 1.6 times more effectively than GRE, our findings suggest that numerical adjustments (e.g., 4 GRE-detected microbleeds corresponding to 6–7 in SWI for anti-amyloid therapy eligibility), however, these proposals remain exploratory. In addition, any proposed thresholds should undergo prospective validation in anti-amyloid therapy cohorts before being considered for clinical use.

Importantly, SWI frequently identifies smaller or more ambiguous cerebral microbleeds, which may not carry the same clinical implications as GRE-defined lesions. While microbleeds have been associated with prognosis in CAA21 and ARIA-H,9 it is unclear whether the additional lesions detected by SWI represent GRE-defined microbleeds of equal prognostic significance. Therefore, simply equating all SWI-detected lesions with GRE microbleeds risks misclassification of disease burden in clinical practice. Further consideration may be needed regarding how microbleeds could be defined in a technique-sensitive manner. Beyond lesion counts, features such as size, signal intensity, morphology, and anatomical distribution may need to be incorporated into future criteria. For such refinements, combined with validation against clinical outcomes, will be essential to determine whether the additional lesions detected by SWI meaningfully improve risk stratification in ARIA-H or CAA.

In this study, subgroup analyses were conducted based on magnetic field strength and SWI slice thickness. Although microbleed detection was higher at 3.0 T than at 1.5 T, the difference was not statistically significant (1.7-fold vs. 1.5-fold, p=0.73). This result was not consistent with prior studies,22,23 possibly due to the limited number of included studies and a high level of heterogeneity. Additionally, SWI slice thickness influenced microbleed detection, with slices <2 mm showing significantly higher detection rates than slices 2 mm (1.9-fold vs. 1.3-fold, p=0.02). According to Nandigam et al.,24 reducing slice thickness decreases the apparent microbleed diameter but increases lesion contrast, thereby improving detectability in thin slices.25 Furthermore, SWI with thin slices at both 1.5 T and 7.0 T identified more microbleeds.25

In this study, high heterogeneity (e.g., I2 exceeding 90%) was observed, which may reflect the diversity of the included disease cohorts. To address potential heterogeneity, we additionally performed subgroup analyses based on equipment vendor and study quality. The pooled proportions were nearly identical between GE and Siemens scanners (0.61 vs. 0.60), with no significant difference (p=0.89). Regarding study quality, exclusion of the three high-risk studies yielded a pooled proportion of 0.61 (95% CI, 0.49–0.58), while the three high-risk studies themselves showed a pooled proportion of 0.59 (95% CI, 0.24–0.86). Again, no significant difference was observed (p=0.89). These results suggest that our findings were not meaningfully influenced by scanner vendor or study quality, supporting the robustness of the pooled estimates. Formal assessment of whether specific factors, such as slice thickness, field strength, or disease cohort, contributed to this heterogeneity would require analytic approaches such as meta-regression or meta-ANOVA. However, given the limited number of available studies, such analyses were not feasible in the present meta-analysis and should be addressed in future investigations with larger and more homogeneous datasets.

For CAA diagnosis, the Boston criteria version 2.0, which incorporates the number and distribution of microbleed, is widely used.26 However, these criteria do not distinguish between SWI and GRE sequences. Given that SWI exhibits greater detectability than GRE, applying uniform criteria to both modalities could increase sensitivity but potentially reduce specificity when using SWI. According to the Boston criteria v2.0, even a single strictly lobar microbleed in a patient with transient focal neurological episodes or cognitive impairment may qualify as possible CAA. However, because the prevalence of strictly lobar microbleeds in the general population has been reported to be approximately 7.1%,17 applying these thresholds without considering imaging technique could lead to overestimation of CAA. Thus, it may be necessary to specify the imaging sequence within diagnostic criteria for CAA and establish technique-specific thresholds.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only 13 included studies. This limitation could reduce the reliability of formal publication bias testing, even though no significant asymmetry was observed using Egger’s test. Second, the study analyzed cohorts with diverse disease conditions, which may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. To address this, we conducted subgroup analyses based on magnetic field strength, SWI slice thickness, equipment vendor, and study quality (QUADAS-2). In addition, this study did not include individuals undergoing anti-amyloid therapy for dementia or mild cognitive impairment. While our findings indicate that SWI is superior to GRE for detecting microbleeds, it remains unclear whether this extends to microbleeds associated with ARIA-H in patients receiving anti-amyloid therapy. Therefore, the proposed threshold adjustments for ARIA-H should be regarded as exploratory and require validation in therapy-specific cohorts showing ARIA-H before any clinical application. Targeted, prospective research in anti-amyloid therapy populations will be essential to confirm the clinical relevance of these findings. Fourth, the definition of microbleeds varied across studies: some used criteria consistent with Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging 1 (STRIVE-1) (low signal intensity nodules generally 2–5 mm in diameter, up to 10 mm, on GRE or SWI)27,28,29,30,31; others allowed diameters up to 5 mm,25,32 and several did not clearly specify their definition.13,24,33,34,35,36 It is also known that SWI yields higher contrast indices than GRE and may slightly enlarge the apparent diameter of microbleeds.22 Moreover, the included studies did not differentiate lesions by signal intensity, anatomical location, or size when counting microbleeds, which limits the ability to propose criteria that incorporate these factors. Future research will be needed to establish criteria that integrate lesion size, signal intensity, morphology, and distribution, and to determine whether these characteristics improve risk stratification and clinical relevance in ARIA-H and CAA. Lastly, subgroup analyses based on TE, slice gap, or disease cohort were not performed due to the limited number of included studies, which precluded meaningful stratification by these imaging parameters.

In conclusion, SWI detects cerebral microbleeds approximately 1.6 times more effectively than GRE. These findings highlight important differences between the two modalities. Our findings raise the possibility that existing GRE-based thresholds for anti-amyloid therapy eligibility, ARIA-H evaluation, and CAA diagnosis may not be directly applicable to SWI. Considering these differences, we cautiously propose exploring an adjustment of the GRE threshold from 4 to 6 or 7 microbleeds for SWI in the context of amyloid antibody therapy eligibility; however, this proposal remains exploratory. Future refinements of microbleed criteria should also incorporate factors such as size, signal intensity, morphology, anatomical distribution, and their prognostic significance. Most importantly, prospective validation in therapy-specific cohorts is required to determine whether these refinements improve risk stratification and clinical decision-making in ARIA-H and CAA.
