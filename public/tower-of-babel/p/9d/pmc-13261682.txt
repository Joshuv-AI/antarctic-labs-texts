Structure–activity relationship study shows versatility in substrate specificity for heterodisulfide reductase in methanogenic archaea
Authors: Mahdi Faal Maleki, Joakim Bøgelund Jakobsen, Eva Danielle Schuiten, Tristan Wagner, André Luis Alves Neves, Lennart Bunch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogenotrophic methanogens are anaerobic archaea that convert carbon dioxide and hydrogen into methane. Central to this process is the heterodisulfide reductase (Hdr), which catalyzes the reduction of the heterodisulfide made of coenzyme M and coenzyme B. In vivo, Hdr functions in association with electron‐donating modules such as the [NiFe]‐hydrogenase (HdrMvh), which supplies reducing equivalents derived from hydrogen oxidation. Here, we isolate the catalytic properties of Hdr by evaluating substrate turnover independently of its native electron‐donating modules using an artificial electron donor system. The molecular features governing substrate recognition and turnover by Hdr were investigated through the design and synthesis of 18 non‐endogenous analogs of the native heterodisulfide substrate (compounds 2a–r). We show that several non‐endogenous disulfides can serve as alternative substrates for Hdr, including analogs with variation in the coenzyme B‐derived amino acid moiety (O‐phosphono‐Ser, L‐Asp, L‐Glu, and 2‐aminoadipic acid), and that the coenzyme M sulfonate can be replaced by a carboxylate group. In contrast, modifications that disrupt charge balance, chain length, or stereochemical compatibility result in loss of substrate activity and, in some cases, lead to inhibitory behavior, underscoring the narrow constraints required for productive catalysis.

INTRODUCTION

Methanogenic archaea are strict anaerobes that retrieve cellular energy through methane generation. They are fueled by hydrogen/carbon dioxide, formate, methyl‐compounds (e.g., methanol), or acetate (Kurth et al., 2020; Thauer et al., 2008). The final step (Figure 1) of methane formation in methanogenic pathways is catalyzed by methyl‐coenzyme M reductase (MCR), which covalently joins methyl‐coenzyme M (CH3‐S‐CoM) with coenzyme B (CoB‐SH) to form the heterodisulfide CoB‐S‐S‐CoM (1a) and methane (Ginovska et al., 2025; Lemaire & Wagner, 2022; Thauer, 2019). The regeneration of HS‐CoM and CoB‐SH by reduction of the disulfide bond in 1a is carried out by heterodisulfide reductase (Hdr), a core enzyme found in all methanogens. Soluble Hdrs are electron‐bifurcation enzymes functionally coupled to different electron‐donating modules, including hydrogenase (Mvh), formate dehydrogenase, or F420H2‐oxidase systems, depending on the organism and growth conditions (Kaster et al., 2011; Nomura et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2017; Watanabe et al., 2021). Membrane‐bound Hdr uses methanophenazine as an electron donor (Thauer et al., 2008). X‐ray structural data of the soluble Hdr have provided molecular insights into the organization of the complex and the heterodisulfide binding pocket formed by HdrB and HdrC (Wagner et al., 2017; Watanabe et al., 2021). The active site is composed of two non‐cubane [4Fe‐4S] clusters coordinated by five perfectly conserved cysteines constituting the so‐called “CCG” motif, a hallmark of this family (Hamann et al., 2007). Cryo‐trapping experiments highlighted the binding of the reaction products in the methanogen Methanothermococcus thermolithotrophicus, in which CoB‐SH and HS‐CoM are still bound to the two non‐cubane clusters (Wagner et al., 2017). Based on this structural snapshot, it has been proposed that the heterodisulfide CoB‐S‐S‐CoM will bind to a specific crevice organized by HdrB and HdrC and would react at the two non‐cubane sites. Here, the exposed Fe of each cluster would attack the disulfide, generating a covalent bond with each sulfur. The electrons of high potential coming from the electron bifurcation will first reduce the Fe‐S‐CoB bond, followed by the Fe‐S‐CoM bond, as suggested by a second cryo‐trapped structure in which the CoB‐SH is leaving the active site (Wagner et al., 2017).

Despite its central role in energy conservation in methanogenic archaea, the determinants governing substrate recognition and turnover by Hdr remain poorly understood. Hdr is known to exhibit high selectivity toward non‐endogenous ligands, and only limited efforts have been reported to systematically explore this selectivity (Hedderich et al., 1989, 1990). In this study, we investigated the molecular features governing substrate specificity at the HdrABC–MvhAGD complex (referred here as Hdr) from the marine thermophile M. thermolithotrophicus by designing and synthesizing a focused library of non‐endogenous heterodisulfide analogs of the endogenous substrate 1a. The native heterodisulfide is composed of three distinct moieties (Table 1): the O‐phosphorylated L‐threonine (blue) linked via an amide bond to a C7‐spacer (green), which via a disulfide bond is connected to an ethyl sulfonic acid moiety (light pink, CoM). In the present ligand set, the charge state and chain length of the CoM‐derived fragment were varied on one side of the disulfide, while the chain length, amino‐acid functionality, and stereochemistry of the CoB‐derived moiety were modified on the other. The resulting analysis revealed an asymmetric tolerance to structural modification, providing insight into structure–activity relationships governing how Hdr balances catalytic specificity with functional flexibility and identifying experimentally grounded principles relevant to the rational design of Hdr‐targeting inhibitors for antimethanogenic applications.

RESULTS

Computational study

In previous studies on Hdr from Methanothermobacter marburgensis, a thioether analog of 1a (1b; Table 1), in which the disulfide bond is replaced by a C–S–C linkage, was synthesized to probe substrate recognition and turnover (Hedderich et al., 1989, 1990). The observed activity for 1b at high concentrations after removal of the disulfide functionality likely reflects non‐productive turnover or assay related‐interference rather than true substrate behavior. While it seems possible that 1b could adopt a similar binding mode as 1a, it was a weak‐to‐neglectable inhibitor of Hdr (K i = 2 mM) (Hedderich et al., 1990). The one‐carbon truncated disulfide 1c (with a CoB exhibiting a hexanoyl chain instead of the normal heptanoyl chain) is also not recognized as a substrate by Hdr and with neglectable inhibitory activity (K i >2 mM) (Hedderich et al., 1989, 1990). The two homo‐disulfides CoM‐S‐S‐CoM and CoB‐S‐S‐CoB (structures not shown) are also not recognized as substrates by Hdr (Hedderich et al., 1989, 1990).

With these pioneering works in mind, we performed a computational study using the software suite Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) based on the x‐ray structure of Hdr when bound with the products CoB‐SH and HS‐CoM (distance of 3.47 Å between the two sulfur atoms) (Figure 2a; see Figure 1 for more details on the Protein Data Bank (PDB) structure code: 5ODR). First, we docked 1a–c into the substrate binding pocket of Hdr using the MOE software suite (see details on protein preparation and docking in the experimental section “computational study”). Key interactions of 1a with the Hdr protein were well reproduced in comparison to free CoB‐SH and HS‐CoM (simplified as CoB and CoM, Figure 2b). The sulfonate group of CoM engages in hydrogen bond interactions with the backbone NH of Gly46 and NH of Gly198, as well as structural water 403. The α‐carboxylate group of CoB engages in a salt‐bridge interaction with Arg201, while the O‐phosphonate group engages in salt‐bridge interactions with Arg14 and Arg240.

Furthermore, a lipophilic tunnel is formed by the phenyl ring of Phe233 and one of the [4Fe‐4S] clusters in which the linker L containing the six methylene groups is placed. The disulfide functionality is positioned 3.5–4.0 Å away from one of the two [4Fe‐4S]. The scoring function returned calculated binding affinities of −8.0 kcal mol−1 for 1a and comparable values for 1b and 1c, which are not substrates nor inhibitors of Hdr (−7.0 and −8.1, respectively). Computed binding modes of sulfide 1b and disulfide 1c differed significantly in the orientation of the O‐phosphono‐L‐Thr and its interactions with the three Arg residues of the Hdr protein. All in all, the in silico results were ambiguous and not promising as to predict substrate or inhibitory potencies. But this may not be surprising, since Hdr is crystallized in its product conformation, which could be significantly different from substrate binding conformation. It was therefore decided to proceed with the design of 1a analogs based on rational, systematic exploration and considering synthetic tractability of the designed analogs.

To better understand what governs substrate selectivity in Hdr, we focused on modifying specific regions of the endogenous heterodisulfide 1a while keeping its core architecture intact. In all analogs, the reducible disulfide bond and the C7 linker (L) were preserved, while changes were introduced either at the amino acid‐derived CoB moiety (R1) or at the terminal functionality of the CoM‐derived fragment (R2). These modifications were chosen to examine the roles of charge, structural complexity, and stereochemistry in productive enzyme engagement. On the CoM side, analogs 2a–f (Table 1) were designed to probe the requirement for the sulfonate group and its replacement with alternative functionalities. In parallel, analogs 2g–r (Figure 2) explore variation at the CoB moiety, including changes in amino acid side‐chain identity, chain length, and stereochemistry. Together, this design allowed us to assess how structural features on either side of the disulfide linkage influence Hdr recognition and turnover.

Synthesis

The synthesis of 2a–r followed a route via homodisulfide 3a–h as depicted in Scheme 1. Starting from commercially available 7‐bromo‐heptanoic acid, disulfide 6 was obtained in two steps in 92% yield. Activated ester 7 was obtained with an 87% yield using N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) and N,N′‐dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) as the coupling reagent. With NHS ester 7 in hand, we synthesized the variety of homo‐disulfides 3a–h by reaction with the specific amino acid (or derivative 4a,b) in an alkaline isopropanol/water mixture. Finally, the target heterodisulfides 2a–r were obtained by reaction with a large excess (15–20 equivalents) of appropriate sulfide.

Biochemistry

To test the effect of the compounds as potential Hdr substrates or inhibitors, the protein was natively purified from M. thermolithotrophicus. The whole process from the cultivation to the storage of the pure protein has been performed under strict exclusion of oxygen to protect the numerous metalloclusters of the protein and the catalysts of the Hdr (i.e., non‐cubane [4Fe‐4S] clusters). The assay relied on the artificial electron donor benzyl‐viologen (BV) reduced with dithionite, for which the redox potential is insufficient to allow the endogenous hydrogenase to perform proton reduction. This approach allows native electron‐donating modules such as Mvh to be bypassed, thereby isolating the disulfide‐reduction activity of Hdr. After measuring the kinetic parameters of the natural substrate 1a, each compound was tested at the final concentration of 0.2 and 1 mM with or without 1a to check if the compound could serve as an inhibitor or surrogate. The results are summarized in Table 1, and kinetic parameters were measured for the most active analogs. Kinetic analyses for compounds 1a, 1b, and 2a are provided in Figure S3.

The study showed that Hdr accepts a range of amino acids which comprise a negatively charged group on the sidechain of CoB (O‐phosphono‐L‐Ser [2g], L‐Asp [2i], L‐Glu [2l], and L‐2‐amino‐adipic acid [2o]), but also that the sulfonate group in CoM can be replaced by a propanoic acid group (2b and 2m), but not a cysteine (2a) nor elongated into homo‐CoM (2e). However, it was unexpected to observe that analog 2j comprising L‐Asp and a propanoic acid fragment was inactive (hybrid analog of 2i/2l). The highest scoring docking poses of 2i and 2l were selected for visualization in Figure 3. Finally, the kinetic data obtained for 2b, 2c, 2f, 2i, 2l, and 2m (see Figures S4–S6) show that these compounds exhibit substrate‐like activity toward Hdr, although with activities generally lower than that of the endogenous substrate 1a. Compound 2f exhibited apparent substrate activity; however, self‐reactivity under assay conditions complicates interpretation and suggests that part of the observed signal may not arise from enzymatic turnover. In addition to substrate activity, some analogs exhibited inhibitory effects when co‐incubated with the endogenous substrate 1a. For example, compounds 2a, 2d, and 2n reduced Hdr activity in a concentration‐dependent manner, while others showed weaker or negligible effects. These observations indicate that certain non‐endogenous analogs can interfere with the native reaction under the assay conditions.

DISCUSSION

All compounds displaying substrate activity retain a disulfide linkage, consistent with the observations of Hedderich et al. (1990). Loss of activity upon disulfide replacement demonstrates that further substrate optimization is constrained to scaffolds preserving this linkage. The net charge state of the CoM‐terminal group plays a crucial role in substrate activity. This observation is supported by comparison of 2a and 2c: compound 2a contains an L‐cysteine moiety, resulting in a zwitterionic CoM terminus bearing both a carboxylate and a protonated amine, which leads to loss of substrate activity. While it cannot be fully excluded that acetylation itself contributes to the observed activity, the presence of inactive or non‐productive analog counterparts, 2d and 2f, bearing neutral CoM‐derived substituents (trifluoropropyl and cyanoethyl, respectively), might argue against acetylation being the primary determinant of activity. This finding is most consistent with the requirement for a negatively charged, CoM‐like terminus, rather than the presence of a specific functional group. However, the existence of inactive ligands that retain a net negative charge may indicate that electrostatics alone is insufficient and that productive turnover might additionally depend on other factors, such as precise spatial and stereochemical alignment. Although the computational analysis could not predict substrate or inhibitory potency, it provided insight into possible binding modes and structural constraints within the Hdr active site. The discrepancy between computational predictions and experimental observations emphasizes the need for experimentally validated approaches.

In addition to charge effects, substrate recognition and turnover by Hdr are strongly influenced by the distance between the disulfide linkage and the functional anchor pointing at both the CoM and CoB termini. These distances are defined by the number of methylene units separating the disulfide sulfur atoms from the carbonyl group on the CoB side and from the anionic moiety on the CoM side. This is supported by our result with compound 2e bearing homo CoM, an analog of CoM with an extra methylene group, in which the substrate activity decreased. The same phenomenon was reported by Hedderich et al. (1989), when the productive state of the ligand disappeared by truncating a methylene group in the CoB part of the endogenous substrate. Taken together, these observations support the hypothesis in which Hdr operates effectively only when electrostatic anchoring and precise spatial alignment across both sides of the disulfide bond requirements are met.

Previous studies have reported that stereochemical inversion of the endogenous heterodisulfide substrate, specifically the incorporation of the D‐configured amino acid at the CoB‐related position, reduced enzyme activity (Hedderich et al., 1989). Based on this observation, we sought to evaluate whether a similar stereochemical effect could be reproduced within the present non‐endogenous ligand series. To this end, a targeted comparison was performed using a single pair of glutamate‐derived analogs differing only in absolute configurations (2l and 2n). While the L‐glutamate analog 2l functioned as a substrate, inversion to the D‐configuration in compound 2n displays weak substrate activity at higher concentrations but predominantly behaves as a weak inhibitor, despite identical composition, charge, and chain length. Kinetic characterization of compounds 2n–2p is shown in Figure S7. This outcome mirrors the previously reported behavior of stereochemically inverted endogenous analogs (Hedderich et al., 1989), suggesting that stereochemistry at the CoB‐derived moiety can determine whether ligand engagement with Hdr is productive or non‐productive. Consistent with this distinction, some analogs exhibited inhibitory behavior under the assay conditions, indicating that non‐endogenous ligands can engage the Hdr active site in a non‐productive manner. These compounds likely interfere with the endogenous reaction by occupying key binding interactions required for disulfide reduction, without supporting efficient electron transfer or bond cleavage. Together, these observations highlight that small structural variations can shift ligand behavior from substrate turnover to inhibition.

While direct identification of the reduction products for each disulfide‐containing analog was not performed in this study, reduction by Hdr is expected to yield the corresponding thiol components based on the established disulfide‐reducing mechanism. The observed activity primarily reflects substrate recognition, turnover, and, in some cases, competitive interference with the endogenous reaction. While the present study focuses on steady‐state kinetics to define substrate recognition and turnover, further mechanistic insight into Hdr function will require further investigations of substrate binding, product release, and pre‐steady‐state kinetic events. Because BV was used as an artificial electron donor, the present study isolates the catalytic properties of Hdr from its native electron‐donating partners. While this enables controlled assessment of substrate specificity on the Hdr site, the influence of coupled modules such as Mvh together with the flavin‐based electron bifurcation system on modulating electron flow and catalysis remains to be addressed in future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we investigated how structural features of non‐endogenous heterodisulfide analogs influence their ability to act as substrates or inhibitors of Hdr. By modifying the CoM‐ and CoB‐derived moieties of the ligands while retaining the disulfide bond, we were able to probe the factors that govern productive engagement of Hdr with the ligand beyond the native substrate. Our findings showed that Hdr operates under a clear set of constraints. The presence of a reducible disulfide bond is essential for activity, but this alone is not sufficient. Productive turnover further requires a net negatively charged CoM terminus and a precise spatial relationship between the disulfide bond and the functional anchor pointing on both the CoM and CoB sides. Even small deviations in chain length on either side of the disulfide result in loss of activity, indicating that Hdr tolerates limited structural variations but requires strict alignment of electrostatic and geometric features. In addition, stereochemistry at the CoB‐derived amino acid can determine whether a ligand behaves as a substrate or as an inhibitor, highlighting a clear distinction between binding and catalysis.

These findings indicate that Hdr combines strict electrostatic, geometric, and stereochemical requirements with limited tolerance to structural variation. Rather than being broadly promiscuous, the enzyme appears finely tuned to recognize ligands that satisfy all of these criteria simultaneously. This mechanistic insight helps explain both the narrow substrate scope of Hdr and its susceptibility to inhibition by carefully designed non‐endogenous ligands, and it provides a rational basis for future efforts aimed at modulating methanogenic metabolism through Hdr‐targeting compounds.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All reagents and materials were obtained from commercial sources and used as received without further purification. All reactions requiring inert conditions were carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere using standard Schlenk and air‐free techniques, using oven‐ or flame dried glassware. Anhydrous solvents were obtained from a SG WATER solvent purification system (commercialized by Pure Process Technology) or obtained from a commercial source. To monitor the progress of reactions, thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) was performed using Silica Gel 60F254 aluminum plates obtained from Merck. Flash column chromatography (FCC) was conducted using silica gel (230–400 mesh particle size, 60 Å pore size) as the stationary phase.

The 1H, 13C, and 31P NMR spectra were recorded using a 400 MHz Bruker AVANCE III or a 600 MHz Bruker AVANCE III HD spectrometer in the indicated solvents at 300 K, with radio frequencies of 400 and 600 MHz for 1H; 100 and 150 MHz for 13C; and 162 MHz for the 31P nuclei. Chemical shifts are reported in ppm relative to the residual solvent peak, and NMR spectra are reported as follows: chemical shift (peak multiplicity; J‐coupling constant(s) in Hz; integration). The abbreviations for peak multiplicities are: s = singlet, bs = broad singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, p = pentet, hept = heptet, m = multiplet.

Liquid Chromatography / Mass Spectroscopy spectra were acquired using an Agilent 1200 system equipped with a ultraviolet detector, a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent 6400), and an electrospray ionization source and an XDB column (4.6 mm × 50 mm). A binary solvent system consisting of H2O/acetonitrile/formic acid (solvent A = 95:5:0.1 and solvent B = 5:95:0.1) was used in a linear gradient method at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 over 5 min, progressing from 0% to 100% solvent B.

An Analytical high performing liquid chromatography​ (HPLC) system equipped with a Gemini‐NX C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm), an Ultimate 3000 pump, and a photo diode array detector was used to assess the purity of the target compound. A binary solvent system consisting of H2O/acetonitrile/TFA (solvent A = 90.9:9:0.1 and solvent B = 9:90.9:0.1) was applied using a linear gradient at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 over 20 min, increasing from 0% to 100% solvent B. Using the same solvent combination, all target compounds were purified by preparative HPLC on an Ultimate 3000 system equipped with a Gemini‐NX C18 column (21 mm × 250 mm) and a ultraviolet / visible detector. The separation was performed with a linear gradient from 0% to 100% solvent B over 20 min at a flow rate of 20 mL min−1.

General procedure for heterodisulfide compounds ( 1a , 2a–r )

A round‐bottom flask was charged with a magnetic stir bar and homodisulfide (3a–h) (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) and taken up in water (2 mM). Mercaptane (2.0 mmol, 20 equiv.) was added and allowed to stir, then aq. NH3 until reaching pH 11 (~0.5 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred overnight and KI3 (0.5 M in H2O) was added dropwise until the brown color disappeared (or stayed stable). The mixture was purified by preparative HPLC using a C‐18 column.

General procedure for homodisulfide linkers ( 3a–h )

A 25 mL round‐bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stir bar, was charged with the appropriate amino acid or amino acid derivative (1.25 mmol, 2.5 equiv.), NaHCO3 (214 mg, 2.55 mmol, 5.1 equiv.), and water (2.5 mL), and the mixture was heated to 45°C to allow quenching of acidic protons. When evolution of carbon dioxide had subsided, the mixture was diluted with an aq. NaHCO3 solution (0.36 M, 10 mL, 3.6 mmol). Bis(2,5‐dioxopyrrolidin‐1‐yl) 7,7′‐disulfanediyldiheptanoate (7, 258 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) was suspended in iPrOH (6.5 mL) and added dropwise to the solution, additional iPrOH (6 mL) was used to complete the transfer. Immediately, a white precipitate appeared. After 3 h, the mixture was clear and iPrOH was removed under vacuum and aq. HCl (cons.) was added until the mixture turned acidic. The resulting mixture was either directly purified by preparative HPLC or extracted with an appropriate solvent/solvent mixture to obtain the desired product, which was used in the next step without any further purification.

Biochemistry

Cultivation of methanogens

M. thermolithotrophicus (DSM 2095) was obtained from the Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany). M. thermolithotrophicus was routinely grown at 60°C in a fermenter. The growth medium was similar to Jespersen, Pierik (Jespersen et al., 2023) and Jespersen and Wagner (Jespersen & Wagner, 2023) and consists of the following elements added under constant stirring per liter of medium: 558 mg KH2PO4 (final concentration 4.1 mM), 1 g KCl (13.4 mM), 25.13 g NaCl (430 mM), 840 mg NaHCO3 (10 mM), 368 mg CaCl2 · 2H2O (2.5 mM), 8 g MgCl2 · 6H2O (39.3 mM), 2.67 g NH4Cl (50 mM), 1.42 g Na2SO4 (10 mM), 61.16 mg nitrilotriacetic acid (0.32 mM), 6.16 mg FeCl2 · 4H2O (0.031 mM), 10 μL 2 mM Na2SeO3 · 5H2O stock (0.02 μM), 3.3 mg Na2WO4 · 2H2O (0.01 mM) and 2.42 mg Na2MoO4 · 2H2O (0.01 mM). After dissolving all elements in distilled water (dH2O), a 100 fold‐concentrated trace element solution was added. To prepare 1 L of the 100 fold‐concentrated stock, 1.36 g of nitrilotriacetic acid (7.1 mM) is first dissolved in dH2O under magnetic stirring, then the pH was shifted to 6.2 by adding NaOH pellets before transferring the different elements: 89.06 mg of MnCl2 · 4H2O (0.45 mM), 183.3 mg of FeCl3·6H2O (0.68 mM), 60.27 mg of CaCl2 · 2H2O (0.41 mM), 180.8 mg of CoCl2 · 6H2O (0.76 mM), 90 mg of ZnCl2 (0.66 mM), 37.64 mg of CuCl2 (0.28 mM), 46 mg of Na2MoO4·2H2O (0.19 mM), 90 mg of NiCl2 · 6H2O (0.38 mM) and 30 mg of VCl3 (0.19 mM). The final pH of the medium was set to 6.2 by adding a final concentration of 50 mM 2‐morpholinoethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer for the flasks and 10 mM MES for the fermenter. Resazurin at a final concentration of 1.5 μM was added to monitor the redox state of the solution.

The 10‐L glass vessel fermenter was filled with 7 L of oxic medium, and oxygen was removed by flushing H2/CO2 at a rate of 2 L min−1 with 250 rpm agitation. The fermenter was inoculated with 540 mL of a preculture grown overnight at 60°C fed with 1.2 bar of 80:20% H2/CO2. For this, nine flasks containing 60 mL of the preculture grown in 1 L Duran Bottles were necessary. The culture in the fermenter was pH controlled with a concentrated solution of NH4OH, and cells were grown with a H2/CO2 at a rate of 2 L min−1 and with 250 rpm agitation, until an Abs600nm of 1.0 was reached. Then the rate was increased to 4 L min−1 (600 rpm agitation). The gas rate was further increased to an optical density at 600 nm of 2.0 to 6 L min−1. A supplementation of 5 mL h−1 of 9 mM Na2S stimulated the growth and final yield of the culture. The culture was harvested when the cells entered the late exponential phase, where Abs600nm reached a value of 4 to 6, a process that takes 10–14 h after inoculation. The cells were harvested by centrifugation under anoxic conditions (in an atmosphere containing a N2/CO2 at a 90:10 ratio) at 20°C, yielding approximately 3.3 g cells (wet biomass) per liter medium. The cell pellets were stored at −80°C under 0.7 bar N2 before enzyme purification.

Purification of the Hdr complex from M. thermolithotrophicus

The entire purification procedure is similar to that described by Wagner et al. (2017). The following protocol has been designed to process a batch of 12–15 g of wet‐weight cells. The cells were thawed at room temperature and transferred to a homemade anaerobic glovebox with rigid glass filled with an N2/CO2 atmosphere with a 90:10 ratio. Lysis buffer (50 mM Tris/HCl pH 8.0 and 2 mM dithiothreitol) was added at a ratio of 3 mL g−1 cells to resuspend the cell pellet and perform osmotic shock. Cells were further lysed by sonication (6 × 30‐s pulses at 75% power with a KE76 sonication probe, Bandelin, Germany). The suspension was centrifuged at 45,000 × g for 30 min at 20°C, and the lysate was separated from the cell debris. The lysate was sonicated again (4 × 30‐s pulses at 75% power), centrifuged at 45,000 × g for 30 min at 20°C and transferred into a glovebox containing a N2/H2 atmosphere with a 97:3 ratio. The supernatant containing the cell‐free extract was filtered through a 0.2 μm filter (Sartorius). To avoid column overload, the filtered cell extract was split into two aliquots and successively applied to weak anion‐exchange chromatography using the following protocol. The sample was loaded at 5 mL min−1 on a 15 mL DEAE Fast Flow (Cytiva) column preequilibrated with the lysis buffer. The column was washed with 50 mL of the lysis buffer supplemented with 100 mM NaCl at 3 mL min−1. The protein was eluted at a flow rate of 3 mL min−1 with an increasing NaCl gradient from 100 to 450 mM NaCl in 180 mL and fractions of 5 mL were collected. The resulting protein fractions were pooled. The pooled samples were diluted by the same volume of lysis buffer and filtered through a 0.2 μm filter. The filtered sample was applied to a 10 mL Q Sepharose High Performance (Cytiva) column pre‐equilibrated in the lysis buffer at 4 mL min−1. The column was washed with 20 mL of lysis buffer supplemented with 250 mM NaCl, and the proteins were eluted by a gradient of 250–500 mM NaCl for 90 mL at 2 mL min−1. Fractions of 2.5 mL were collected. The fractions of interest were pooled, and the pool was diluted with two volumes of lysis buffer before filtration. The sample was loaded at 4 mL min−1 on the same Q Sepharose column equilibrated with lysis buffer. The washing and gradient were performed by combining two buffers, the lysis buffer and a malate buffer (500 mM malate/NaOH pH 5.0 and 2 mM dithiothreitol). The washing step was performed with 10 mL of 25% malate buffer at 5 mL min−1 and the elution was done by applying a gradient from 25% to 50% malate buffer for 50 mL at 1.5 mL min−1 collection 1 mL fractions. The pooled fractions of interest were diluted with one volume of hydrophobic interaction buffer (HIB, 25 mM Tris/HCl pH 8.0, 2M (NH4)2SO4 and 2 mM dithiothreitol). The sample was filtered and applied on a 1.7 mL Source15Phe (Cytiva) pre‐equilibrated with HIB at 1 mL min−1 flow rate. The column was washed with 25 mM Tris/HCl pH 8.0, 1M (NH4)2SO4 and 2 mM dithiothreitol for 10 mL at 1 mL min−1. The protein was eluted with a gradient from 1 to 0M (NH4)2SO4 for 40 mL at 1 mL min−1, with collection of 1 mL fractions. The fractions of interest were pooled and concentrated on a centricon with a 30,000 Da cut‐off (Sartorius). Depending on the preparation purity, the sample was either exchanged with a 1000‐fold dilution (via centricon) with the storage buffer (25 mM Tris/HCl pH 8.0, 10 % v/v glycerol, and 2 mM dithiothreitol) or injected on a Superdex 200 10/300 increase (Cytiva) equilibrated with the storage buffer and eluted at 0.4 mL min−1. Following the elution on the gel filtration, the protein was concentrated on a centricon with a 30,000 Da cut‐off. The final preparation with a protein concentration ranging from 5 to 30 mg mL−1 was stored at −80°C. The protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method using bovine serum albumin as standard. To follow the purification of the Hdr complex and ensure the correct fractions are selected for the different steps, the eluted fractions were analyzed using denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (see Figure S1).

Enzymatic assay

All reactions were performed in a homemade anaerobic chamber with rigid glass filled with an atmosphere containing 100% N2. The measurements were performed at 40°C in a BMG Labtech FLUOstar Omega Microplate reader using the automatic path length correction. A 200 μL volume was used in 96‐well Greiner flat‐bottom plates. The displayed values are the normalized absorbance per centimeter values. The experiment was performed in 50 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.6, 200 μM freshly prepared sodium dithionite, 2 mM benzyl viologen, and different concentrations of the compounds. These compounds were incubated for 10 min with the enzyme at room temperature prior to activity measurements. The reaction was initiated by mixing the enzyme together with the compounds in the reaction mixture. The final concentration of Hdr ranged from 1.25 to 31 μg/mL. BV oxidation was monitored spectrophotometrically at 600 nm for a maximum of 70 min. As an initial test, the compounds were tested at a final concentration of 0.2 and 1 mM in the absence (testing the compound as a substrate) or in combination with 0.2 mM 1a in the final mix (to test if the compound is an inhibitor). Hdr activity was tested by measuring the activity with 1a alone as a positive control. Background activity was determined in control assays performed under identical conditions (i.e., with the buffer, HdrMvh, dithionite, and BV) but in the absence of added compounds. The absorbance traces from the kinetic assays are shown in Figure S2. This background rate, corresponding to non‐specific BV reduction, was subtracted from all measurements prior to calculation of enzymatic activity. For inhibitor measurements, the 100% activity was determined with 0.2 mM 1a in the absence of additional compound, in the same plate and conditions. When enzymatic oxidation of BV was detected with the tested compound, the kinetic parameters of the enzyme with the compounds were determined by measuring the activity in the presence of concentrations of molecules ranging from 0 to 0.5 mM or 0 to 3 mM substrate. Specific activities were calculated using an experimentally determined molar extinction coefficient of 6384.2 M−1 cm−1 for reduced BV (Lemaire et al., 2024), considering a one‐electron reduction of the molecule (BV+●), and are displayed as μmol of electron transferred per minute per mg of Hdr. All reactions were measured in triplicate. Kinetic parameters were determined using the Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) webserver (https://ic50.org/kmvmax.html). The maximal specific activity measured on Hdr with 1a, correlating with what has been reported in earlier studies (40 U mg−1) (Wagner et al., 2017), has been measured on freshly purified enzyme. Other measured activities present lesser specific activities due to medium‐term storage at −80°C.

Computational study

MOE Version 2024.0601 was used in the computational study reported herein. The pdb file 5ODR was acquired from the Protein Data Bank, and chains H + I isolated (protein, waters, [4Fe‐4S] clusters and CoB + CoM products). Protein preparation was done by the build‐in function Protonate 3D with standard setup. Induced fit docking of 1a–c and 2a–r was carried out with standard setup, and structural waters and two of the [4Fe‐4S] clusters included. Binding modes were inspected manually and those with a “flipped” binding mode discarded. For each analog, the binding mode with lowest scoring function and orientation with overlap of CoB and CoM was reported in Table 1 and following shown in the figures.
