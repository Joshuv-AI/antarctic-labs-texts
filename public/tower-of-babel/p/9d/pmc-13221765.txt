Partnering with Latino communities for dementia research: a mixed-methods study of engagement
Authors: Sara S. Masoud, Byeong Yeob Choi, Juana Escareño, Belinda Flores, Karla Daniela López Lorenzo, Carole L. White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLatinos are disproportionately affected by dementia but remain underrepresented in dementia research. Engaged research approaches, characterized by co-leadership between academic and community partners, can strengthen the representativeness, responsiveness, and impact of scientific evidence. There is a need to better understand how methods of engagement may deepen partnerships with underrepresented communities. This study examined engagement with a Community Council of research partners who were co-conducting dementia research among Latino communities in South Texas.MethodsAn explanatory sequential mixed-methods study assessed Council member engagement over two years. The Research Engagement Survey Tool (REST), measuring engagement quality and quantity across eight engagement principles and levels, was administered at three time points. Repeated measures ANOVA and pairwise t-tests assessed changes; Bonferroni correction was applied for significance. Semi-structured interviews with a Council member subset (N = 10) were analyzed using thematic content analysis and integrated with survey findings.ResultsCouncil members (N = 13), including caregivers, persons living with dementia, community health workers, community organizations, clinicians, and dementia researchers, completed all survey administrations. Overall REST quality scores increased from baseline to study end (mean 4.18 to 4.77 on a 1–5 scale; p=.003). Significant increases after Bonferroni correction were observed in: focusing on community perspectives (p=.008), valuing partner input (p=.015), co-learning and capacity building (p=.017), fostering collaborative partnerships (p=.015), and involving partners in dissemination (p=.013). When examined by engagement level, ‘collaboration’ remained highest across all time points; the largest increases occurred in ‘partnership’ (quality +6.9; quantity +6.8, each out of 20). Qualitative findings reinforced survey results across three themes: (1) establishing partnerships through diversity, shared experiences, and leadership; (2) building capacity through co-learning, trust, and being heard; and (3) sustaining the work. Together, findings illustrate a trajectory in which structural diversity and inclusive processes produced stronger trust, partnership, and sustained community leadership.ConclusionThe Council model can effectively support and deepen engagement with Latino community partners when structured around cultural values and community-centeredness. Through the Council’s structural diversity, relational processes, and shared leadership, engagement strategies strengthened trust and fostered a sustained partnership. These findings contribute to the engagement sciences and provide a model for reducing underrepresentation in dementia research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00899-0.

Background

Latinos are 50% more likely to develop Alzheimer’s disease than non-Latino whites, with numbers expected to increase more than nine-fold from 379,000 in 2012 to 3.5 million by 2060 [1, 2]. They are also reported to have a younger onset of dementia, which adds to disability-adjusted life years and overall disease burden [3, 4]. Clark et al. [3] examined age of onset among participants across five Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers and reported the mean age at onset for the first dementia symptom was 6.8 years (95% confidence interval [CI], 3.5–10.3 years) earlier in Latinos compared with non-Latino White patients. Time to diagnosis is also often delayed among Latinos, with greater severity of cognitive impairment at time of diagnosis [5, 6]. These stark disparities underscore the need to develop effective strategies to reduce inequities in dementia and dementia care for Latinos, particularly given the growing number of affected individuals and the wide-ranging health, social, and economic consequences for families, communities, and society.

Yet, despite their greater risk for Alzheimer’s disease and other types of dementia, Latinos are substantially underrepresented in dementia research [2, 7]. Although they make up nearly one-fifth of the U.S. population and face disproportionate dementia risk, Latinos account for as low as ~ 3% of participants in federally-funded Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias research [8, 9]. This underrepresentation perpetuates gaps in prevention, diagnosis, and care, contributing to later detection, reduced access to culturally-aligned services, and worse health outcomes [10, 11]. Growing recognition of this discrepancy in disease burden and research representation among Latinos and other highly impacted populations has prompted increasingly urgent calls for more inclusive dementia research [11–13].

The Dementia Care Summit is a national convening of researchers, clinicians, persons with lived experiences, and policymakers, hosted every 2–3 years by the National Institute on Aging with a goal to produce recommended priorities for dementia care research [14]. The 2020 and 2023 Summits heavily underscored the need to include persons living with dementia and their family caregivers as engaged research partners [14, 15]. Community engaged research is broadly characterized as the meaningful involvement of community members as partners across the research process [10, 16, 17]. There is a growing body of literature demonstrating the effectiveness of engaged approaches, including improved quality, impact, and relevance of scientific work to communities experience health disparities [12, 18–21].

A recent scoping review of participatory approaches in Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias research [22] found that while several studies have involved varied levels of engagement with community partners, there remains a need for more engaged research that specifically aims to include historically marginalized groups, like the Latino community. Some recent dementia research efforts demonstrate the inclusion of community partners among the Latino community through establishing community advisory boards and training community health workers to raise awareness about dementia [23, 24]. Yet few reported efforts have tied their participatory approaches to engagement outcomes of the Latino/a partners [25–27]. Measuring and reporting these outcomes is essential in deepening our understanding of the reproducibility, sustainability, and equity of engaged research with marginalized groups. This is a priority that funders like the Patient Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) have identified as a foundational gap in the science of engagement, noting that there is presently little consensus on how to define or measure engagement outcomes and that methods for including underrepresented populations as study partners remain critically underdeveloped [28–30].

To address these gaps, a mixed-methods study was conducted to examine engagement among community partners who were co-conducting dementia care research in South Texas. The study assessed whether the engagement strategies employed throughout the study period were effective in strengthening the partnership over time.

Methods

This project was submitted to the Institutional Review Board and deemed to be exempt (HSC22-0277E). The work was funded by a Patient Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) Eugene Washington Engagement Award.

The Community Council for South Texas dementia research

Building on prior community-engaged research efforts [26, 31–33], a Community Council comprised of 16 members was established in 2021 to connect with families and communities impacted by dementia in South Texas and identify community-driven dementia care research priorities and outcomes [34]. Although the specific project funding that supported the Council to identify research priorities ended in 2023, the partnership has continued and remains active at the time of this publication. All Council members were included in the interpretation of findings and invited to contribute to this manuscript as co-authors in whatever manner felt appropriate and accessible to them (e.g., verbal versus written contributions). Three members of the Council were interested in a higher level of involvement in the manuscript development process and were included as co-authors.

Founding members of the Council included individuals who had served on an advisory board that supported an earlier dementia-focused study [26], community health workers who co-developed a brain health training program with the study team in a prior partnership [35], and others who were recruited via word of mouth through the extended networks of the study team and founding Council members (see “Setting and Sample” for more details) Council membership includes Latino family caregivers, individuals impacted by dementia, community health workers (CHWs), representatives of community-based organizations, researchers, and a geriatrics and palliative care physician. Council members represent different geographic regions of the South Texas area including the Rio Grande Valley, Laredo, South Coastal region, and San Antonio. Many Council members and the study team identify with multiple roles (e.g., family caregiver/researcher or CHW impacted by early-stage dementia). To ensure the project deliverables and timeline were met, one caregiver was designated by the Council as a temporary Council Chair for the duration of the study period (2021–2023). Their role was to work closely with the study team between the monthly meetings to finalize meeting guides, co-facilitate meetings, ensure adherence to the project protocol, and to support the integration of Council input into all deliverables. The Chair role was dissolved after the project period concluded and subsequent projects to date have been co-managed with all Council members during scheduled monthly meetings.

Monthly Council meetings were held online via Zoom to support the participation and collaboration of members across different regions of South Texas. Meetings were 90-minutes and followed meeting guides that were co-developed with Council members and later finalized by the Council Chair and study team. The study team coordinated the logistics of the monthly meetings (e.g., scheduling Zoom calls, sending out the meeting guides, and compiling meeting notes) and facilitation was shared between the study team and the Council Chair. Activities of the Council included but were not limited to: (a) establishing “community agreements” to achieve mutually agreed upon approaches for decision-making and collaboration; (b) planning and engaging in capacity building activities both internally for the Council and with the wider community; (c) outreach and engagement activities with wider networks in South Texas; and (d) partnership with the project team to coordinate the identification of research priorities among Latinos impacted by dementia and the research outcomes that matter to them. The study team supported the Council to determine the logistics of the planned activities and to work through any challenges related to the successful accomplishment of their shared goals.

Community pláticas

The key activity of the Council was to plan and implement community pláticas (conversations) with families impacted by dementia and others in their communities to identify patient- and family-centered research priorities. As a research and/or engagement method, pláticas are an informal, semi-structured form of conversation in groups or one-to-one that rely on dynamics of “reciprocity, vulnerability, and reflexivity” [36] about personal experiences and perspectives around a particular theme or topic [37–40]. The Council developed a program model that includes live musical performances, a brief level-setting presentation, coffee and pan dulce (sweet breads), and a warm meal served by the study team and Council to conclude the gathering. The main feature of the program are small group conversations, where 5–6 participants are seated with two members of the Council and/or study team as facilitators and note-takers and engaged in a guided conversation. During the conversations, participants share rich, nuanced cultural knowledge and information about their perceptions and experiences of dementia and dementia research.

Six community pláticas in different regions of South Texas were planned and implemented by the Council and study team. The Council was engaged by the study team to determine plática logistics, including the selected dates, locations, and overall structure of the gatherings. Members of the Council who lived in the regions where pláticas were held served as designated local leaders in the planning, outreach, and implementation of the pláticas. The Council and project team co-developed conversation guides to learn from community members about their experiences around dementia. Notes were taken by Council members and/or study team members during plática conversations, sometimes supplemented with audio recordings (upon attaining consent of participants), that they then analyzed to identify community-driven dementia research priorities and outcomes. All pláticas were planned in a bilingual manner, where the Council and study team translated all aspects of the event into English and Spanish, including verbal presentations and their accompanying slides, conversation guides, and resource lists provided to all guests. Breakout tables were organized by language preference and assigned to Council and study team members who were proficient in the corresponding languages. Following the implementation of each plática, the Council and project team would debrief and reflect on the successes and challenges of each session. Subsequent plática(s) would be adapted based on feedback from the debriefing sessions.

Study design

To examine engagement over time with the Council as they carried out their research activities, an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design study was conducted (Fig. 1). The initial phase of the study involved the collection of quantitative data using the Research Engagement Survey Tool (REST) [19, 41] administered at three time points to identify any potential differences in levels of engagement with the Council prior to and following the implementation of pláticas. The project team examined quantitative findings to inform the creation of interview guides used for the subsequent qualitative assessment phase. Interviews with members of the Council were conducted and analyzed to further examine their experiences of engagement. Upon completion of all analyses, quantitative and qualitative findings were interpreted together to elucidate a more nuanced assessment of engagement with the Council.

Setting and sample

The study team and their community partners have extensive experience providing community resources and research for family caregivers of persons living with dementia, and thus brought to the study a robust network of key individuals and partnering organizations with whom they collaborated in the South Texas region. The South Texas Area Health Education Centers Program (ST-AHEC) had partnered with the study team for prior initiatives, including the development of a CHW-focused training to promote brain health, address dementia, and advocate for patient-centered research [35]. The ST-AHEC has an extensive geographic catchment area and a wide-ranging network of promotoras and CHWs. Initial invitations to participate in the project as Council members were extended through the study team’s networks and ST-AHEC networks at the start of the project period. Recruited CHWs, caregivers, persons living with dementia, and other partners were asked if they could identify additional members to join the Council. Members of the Council were invited if they were located in a region of South Texas or surrounding areas, had a personal or professional connection to dementia, and were able to commit to attending all or most of the monthly meetings. Being Latino/Latina or Hispanic was not a requirement to join the Council, though all members of the Council and project team shared a commitment to centering the Latino community in the project.

Data collection

During phase 1 of the study, the validated REST was used to quantitatively examine engagement among the Council [19, 41]. The REST is designed to assess how engagement occurs among all partners involved in engagement activities for research. It is comprised of 32 items that align with eight engagement principles (EPs): EP1, “Focus on community perspectives and determinants of health”; EP2, “Partner input is vital”; EP3, “Partnership sustainability to meet goals and objectives”; EP4, “Foster co-learning, capacity building, and co-benefit for all partners”; EP5, “Build on strengths and resources within the community or patient population”; EP6, “Facilitate collaborative, equitable partnerships”; EP7, “Involve all partners in the dissemination process”; EP8, “Build and maintain trust in the partnership”. Each EP corresponds with survey items that are assessed using Likert-type scales to rate the quality of engagement (how well: poor, fair, good, very good, excellent, and not applicable) and quantity of engagement (how often: never, rarely, sometimes, often, always, and not applicable). The REST generates quality and quantity scores for each of the eight EPs as well as quality and quantity scores for the overall measure ranging from 0 to 5 for each score. This measure has strong internal consistency for each of the eight EPs measured for both quality (Cronbach’s alpha range: 0.83–0.92) and quantity (Cronbach’s alpha range: 0.79–0.91) and the overall measure for quality (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.98) and quantity (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.97). Given their significance as the primary activity of the Council and study team, the quantitative phase of this study was anchored around the pláticas. There were three quantitative data collection timepoints during the study period: T1 took place prior to planning and implementing the pláticas; T2 followed the implementation and debrief of the first three pláticas; T3 took place upon completion of three more pláticas. The REST was administered to Council members via REDCap [42, 43].

After conducting a preliminary analysis of the quantitative data, the study team and Council discussed the findings to inform the development of a semi-structured interview guide. Analysis of the REST data revealed high baseline scores with some significant increases within certain engagement principles and trends toward deepened level of engagement. The interview guides were developed to better understand what explained the high baseline scores, what activities may have contributed most to improved engagement outcomes, and what Council members feel may have contributed to the observed deepened level of engagement. Interview topics included Council members’ perceptions of their engagement experiences, the structural and relational factors they felt contributed to partnership development, the role of the pláticas in shaping their experiences, and reflections on trust-building and collaboration over the course of the project. Interviews were conducted online using Zoom, audio recorded and transcribed. One participant who was living with dementia was provided the opportunity to participate in the interview alongside their family caregiver who was also a member of the Council. All members are either bilingual in English and Spanish or monolingual English speakers so all data collection was conducted in English.

Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using STATA 16 [44]. After screening missing data, descriptive statistics including means and standard deviations were calculated for each EP and the overall measure. The mean of the 3–5 items within each EP was calculated for EP-specific scores, then the overall means of the EP-specific scores were calculated to determine the overall REST scores for quality and quantity. Our primary hypothesis is that both the quality and quantity of engagement, as measured by REST, will be improved following pláticas. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to examine whether REST scores for each EP and the overall measure were different across three consecutive timepoints. In cases where Mauchley’s test indicated a violation of the assumption of sphericity (p < .05), and the Greenhouse-Geisser epsilon values less than 0.75, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Following, ANOVA, pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were used to compare mean scores at T1-T2, T2-T3, and T1-T3. The threshold for statistical significance was a 2-sided p-value of 0.05.

Scores were also examined in alignment with five levels of engagement identified during the Delphi process to develop the REST: (1) outreach and education, (2) consultation, (3) cooperation, (4) collaboration, and (5) partnership. Scoring in alignment with levels of engagement involves calculating a percentage score for REST items by each level of engagement, determined by classifications assigned by the REST development team. Scoring instructions and codes for performing analyses using statistical software is publicly accessible [45].

Thematic content analysis of individual interviews was conducted. All transcripts were reviewed for accuracy prior to analysis. Three members of the study team served as coders; all had been active participants in the study throughout the project period. An additional study team member who had read all transcripts and observed all coding meetings was available to provide perspective in the event of unresolved disagreement, though this was ultimately not needed. Using inductive line-by-line coding in Microsoft Word, relevant sections of the transcripts were identified and summarized. Relevant segments of the transcripts were grouped based on similarities in content. Upon organizing data into categories, the study team then developed themes by examining patterns of meaning across the categories [46]. Coding decisions, categorization, and thematic analysis were reviewed in collaboration between study team members until consensus was achieved. Themes were presented to the Council members for their review and interpretation before finalizing the qualitative analysis phase of the study.

Following the principles of explanatory mixed-methods design, data collected from the qualitative and quantitative phases of the study were reviewed by members of the project team [47]. Findings from the REST data were connected with and compared to categories and themes identified through qualitative analysis, bringing to light any convergence or divergence across datasets. This integration phase allowed for a more nuanced, richer assessment of the Council’s engagement experiences.

Results

Quantitative data

A total of 16 Council members participated in the study. Of the 16 Council members, 13 completed the REST at all time points. One member had resigned prior to the second data collection time point (T2), and another had joined after the first data collection time point (T1) had already been completed. All 13 Council members who completed surveys at all three time points attended at least two of the three pláticas implemented between each data collection period, ensuring that each timepoint comparison reflects meaningful exposure to the Council’s research activities. Except for the intentionally limited representation of providers on the Council, representation was evenly distributed between persons living with dementia, caregivers, community organization, and researchers. Most Council members were women and identified as Hispanic or Latino. Half of Council members represented the San Antonio region, six Council members represented the Rio Grande Valley and surrounding areas, and the remainder represented the South Coastal region of Corpus Christi and surrounding areas (Table 1).

Results of repeated measures ANOVA tests and pairwise t-tests are presented in Table 2. Overall, engagement quality total scores increased significantly across the three time points (F(2,24) = 7.67, p = .003), with mean scores reflecting a statistically significant rise from T1 (M = 4.18, SD = 0.69) to T3 (M = 4.77, SD = 0.24), (t(12) = -3.35, p = .006). Engagement quantity total scores showed an upward trend (F(2,24) = 3.47, p = .047), though pairwise differences did not remain significant after correction.

Significant increases in mean scores for quality of engagement were seen for EP6 (Facilitate collaborative, equitable partnerships) over time (F(2,24) = 6.97, p = .004), with T1 scores (M = 4.02, SD = 0.93) increasing at T2 (M = 4.6, SD = 0.6), t(12) = -3.34, p = .007. Similar changes were observed for EP7 quality scores (Involve all partners in the dissemination process) across time (F(2,24) = 5.02, p = .015), with a statistically significant increase from T1 (M = 3.95, SD = 1.06) to T3 (M = 4.79, SD = 0.4), t(12) = -3.27, p = .013. Quality of engagement within EP1 (Focus on community perspectives and determinants of health) increased over the study period (F(2,24) = 5.67, p = .010), with a statistically significant increase identified from T1 (M = 4.17, SD = 0.64) to T3 (M = 4.77, SD = 0.24), t(12), =-3.19, p = .008. EP8 (Build and maintain trust in the partnership) quality scores also increased significantly (F(2,24) = 6.45, p = .006) from T1 (M = 4.37, SD = 0.69) to T2 (M = 4.85, SD = 0.25), t(12) = -3.11, p = .011). An additional notable increase was identified in EP2 (Partner input is vital) across time (F(2,24) = 5.57, p = .010), and between T1 (M = 4.33, SD = 0.70) and T3 (M = 4.88, SD = 0.24).

Average quantity of engagement scores within most EPs showed upward but non-significant patterns. Statistically significant changes in quantity scores over time were observed for EP6 (F(2,24) = 4.83, p = .017) and EP7 (F(2,24) = 4.14, p = .029), though no statistically significant changes across between time points were identified. EP5 (Build on strengths and resources within the community or patient population) remained stable across the three time points. Results suggest that while Council perspectives of quantity of engagement did not change significantly, their perceived quality of engagement strengthened over time.

Qualitative data

Ten Council members (N=10) participated in individual semi-structured interviews. Three themes were identified through analysis of the qualitative data: (1) establishing partnerships, (2) building capacity together, and (3) sustaining the work. Subgroupings of similar content were organized together to depict nuanced findings within each theme. The qualitative data reflect a cyclical process, from the structure of the Council to the application and sustainment of the work, showing an ongoing progression of engagement. Theme 1: Establishing partnerships

Participants tied their engagement to structural features of the Council, including diversity in perspectives and regional representation, shared experiences with dementia, and the supportive approach to leadership from the project team.

Diversity of the council

Participants emphasized the importance of diverse community perspectives, including current and former family caregivers, persons living with dementia, community organizations, health workers, and researchers. The inclusion of those directly impacted by dementia was described as especially valuable to members of the Council: “…Having those special individuals in the group made it more interesting because we’re getting firsthand experience from somebody actually living with dementia and somebody caring for someone living with dementia” (P3, CHW/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). Hearing the personal experiences of Council members who were living with dementia and caring for family members supported the development of trust and formed a shared motivation to address the needs of communities. Witnessing the vulnerability of members who shared personal experiences with dementia during meetings and study activities fostered empathy and connection across the diverse group: “Some of our council members have really gone through very difficult situations. So, for them to open up… I think that really helped build the trust that people are just people, and all people need help” (P1, CHW, South Coastal Texas). Regional representation across South Texas was also critical, as members recognized cultural and resource differences while working toward shared goals: “San Antonio versus Laredo versus Corpus Christi versus the Rio Grande Valley — we’re all different in so many ways… For me, my community is my priority… But yet, I still respect, and I want to also support other areas” (P7, Family Caregiver/CHW, Rio Grande Valley).

Shared experiences with dementia

While diversity was valued, participants stressed the importance of shared experiences with dementia. All Council members had some personal connection—living with dementia, caregiving, or working professionally in the field. As one member shared, “Everybody in some form or fashion has been touched by Alzheimer’s… parent, grandparent, sibling, spouse. So, everybody understands what’s going on or what’s at stake” (P8, Family Caregiver, San Antonio). This shared experience was seen as a defining feature of the Council’s engagement.

Supportive leadership

Participants highlighted the role of leadership of the study team and the designated Council Chair in supporting the Council to provide guidance over project activities. “What really jumped at me, and I think this is the most powerful aspect of it, is the variety of people on this council, experiences, backgrounds, I guess, locations… and that kind of variety really needs a ringleader… that will allow everyone to participate” (P8, Family Caregiver, San Antonio). Supportive leadership was described as central to members’ willingness and confidence to share feedback: “All the members are encouraged to really provide feedback… it’s an open forum where we feel safe, and know that whatever we bring about is going to be taken constructively” (P10, Community Organization Representative, San Antonio).

Theme 2: Building capacity together

From the foundation provided by the partnership structure, participants shared their engagement experiences related to the process of capacity building to implement the community pláticas together. They described the Council as a learning experience, in which they were able to adapt and grow through the engagement process. Participants spoke of a shared purpose in hosting the community pláticas among the group formed through the Council experience and the importance of cultivating trust to facilitate meaningful collaboration. A key part of this process was the importance of being heard by one another and the project team when shaping how the pláticas were approached.

A learning experience

Regardless of prior experience on councils or boards, participants described the Council as a unique learning opportunity. “For me, it was the first time being considered a member of something…it gave me a different sense of cohesiveness and a sense of unity than I had experienced before” (P3, CHW/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). The Council created space to exchange information and resources: “I’ve learned being on this council that… if I come up with, with something or somebody tells me something, I know where to go to get more resources. You know, that’s been awesome” (P4, PLWD/CHW, South Coastal Texas). Activities with the project team also built members’ own experiences and capacities: “I have (previously) hosted what they call ‘community pláticas’, but… it was not the same… Now I can make comparisons” (P3, CHW/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley).

Uniting through shared goals

Participants described developing camaraderie through shared goals in planning and implementing the pláticas. “I’ve learned to appreciate everybody’s input… we all have different roles, but we share that commonality of trying to help others” (P1, CHW, South Coastal Texas). Collaboration was supported by a sense of unity and shared mission: “Everybody understood that we all had a purpose and a mission… we each took our role within the group and that’s how we all did the work” (P6, Community Organization Representative/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). Some described the Council as an extension of themselves: “They’re just another part of me, an extension of what I do, but maybe in a different capacity” (P10, Community Organization Representative, San Antonio).

Cultivating trust for collaboration

Trust was cited as essential for collaboration despite differences in perspective or region: “All of us are different… and continuing to build on that trust… I think the outcomes have been wonderful, and it stems from the trust” (P3, CHW/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). Those directly impacted by dementia emphasized respect as key to building trust: “I like the way I’m treated. And if you can treat me like that, I know you can treat other people that way” (P9, PLWD, San Antonio). Others described trust through openness to share personal experiences: “For me to share some personal experiences about my family, you just don’t do that with everybody, especially in the Hispanic community… but everybody felt comfortable… truly, you could feel the love of the Council members” (P1, CHW, South Coastal Texas).

Being heard

A commonly shared experience among participants was a feeling of being listened to by the other members of the Council and the project team. This was a vital component of their engagement experiences, often cited as a facilitator of trust and a key aspect of the Council’s ability to achieve consensus for decision-making for the pláticas as a group. “I feel like being part of this council, yes, we have very opinionated people, but never spoke over each other. We all allow each other time to, you know, to voice our opinions or our thoughts” (P1, CHW, South Coastal Texas). Being heard was sufficient for Council members who expressed that as long as they felt listened to, they understood if their input was not ultimately the direction taken, “we may not go with what some of us recommend, but at least we were heard and were given that opportunity to be heard and listened. And that’s important” (P3, CHW/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). Feeling listened to was an important experience for persons living with dementia to be included in providing oversight and input in project activities, “They listen to me, specifically, specially. And I really appreciate that” (P9, PLWD, San Antonio). Another person living with dementia shared, “sometimes I don’t (give input), because I feel like, like, everybody’s much more educated and I don’t know how to, to express myself. But my daughter… she’s always saying, ‘Mom, even if it’s simple, you bring your point across’… It’s getting easier because I’m getting to know them” (P4, PLWD/CHW, South Coastal Texas). This experience reflects a dynamic in which persons living with dementia may feel hesitant to contribute in group settings alongside professionals and caregivers [48] and that relational trust-building within the Council may help mitigate these power imbalances.

Theme 3: Sustaining the work

Participants reflected on outcomes of their collaboration, noting that the Council built capacity to implement pláticas across South Texas and engage wider communities. The pláticas were described as responsive to community needs, providing opportunities for those impacted by dementia to share and connect. Looking ahead, participants emphasized the importance of sustaining the work by following through on what was learned from these conversations.

Engaging communities (pláticas)

Participants described the pláticas as successful in connecting with wider communities and providing insight into how experiences with dementia could inform research: “We want to report that input to someone at the research level… to say, this is what the community is asking for” (P10, Community Organization Representative, San Antonio). Facilitating the pláticas also allowed the Council to model their own engagement strategies: “The way the pláticas were coordinated… you heard every single individual speak… there was a lot of active listening and clarifications” (P3, CHW/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). Participants described the culturally tailored approach for each plática as contributing to the communities’ willingness to share in the plática setting: “We bring out the food, we bring out the music… the plática right away opens up, because we’ve used that for years and years… We get in a circle, you know, so that everybody can see each other and hear each other, you know… that’s the Latino culture right there” (P7, Family Caregiver/CHW, Rio Grande Valley). Members emphasized that this approach created safety for vulnerability about experiences and challenges: “At each session, people really let their hair down… there was crying… and I don’t think people will do that if they don’t feel comfortable. I feel like we did that” (P1, CHW, South Coastal Texas).

Responsiveness to communities

Participants emphasized that the pláticas were directly responsive to community needs, particularly for Latino families impacted by dementia. In many South Texas regions, few opportunities exist to support persons with dementia and their families. “There is such a need for the Latino community… they live in the shadows… and when they do come out, they’re kind of hesitant” (P10, Community Organization Representative, San Antonio). Providing a platform for open communities to talk through their experiences, alongside professionals and community organizations, raised awareness of resources and reduced isolation: “It makes the people feel a bit better… they weren’t alone… having a few physicians and healthcare personnel there made them feel like what they were going through was being heard” (P8, Family Caregiver, San Antonio).

Following through

A strong motivation was shared among Council members that the work needed to continue, especially in following up the pláticas with action to address the needs addressed through the conversations. “How do we become sustainable beyond the project? … there has to be a conversation about sustainability of these programs” (P6, Community Organization Representative/Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley). Recognizing that change takes time, they saw continued community engagement as the next step: “It’s not like from one day to another… it takes a lot of effort… from here, from the community, that’s the first step” (P5, Clinical Researcher, Rio Grande Valley). The participants felt the framework of the Council and their role in planning the pláticas is a model that can be replicated to continue engaging and learning from Latino communities impacted by dementia: “Its simplicity was what served it best… however that could be replicated is very powerful” (P2, Family Caregiver, Rio Grande Valley).

Mixed-methods interpretation

Engagement with the Council was facilitated through partnering to plan, implement, and evaluate the pláticas. Council perspectives on engagement are best understood by interpreting the quantitative and qualitative findings together. At baseline, T1 REST scores reflected high engagement (quality = 4.18, quantity = 4.45). Interviews with the Council explained these high scores in describing the structural components of the Council that were present at the onset of the study, diversity, shared experiences and familiarity with dementia, and supportive leadership.

Repeated measures ANOVA tests demonstrated that strategies used to engage the Council in the pláticas nonetheless resulted in statistically significant changes over time in overall REST scores for both quality and quantity of engagement, despite already high baseline levels. Increases were observed across most EPs, with significant improvements in quality scores within EP1, EP2, EP4, EP6, EP7, and EP8. EP5 (Build on strengths and resources within the community or patient population) showed little change, explained through interviews as a result of a strong emphasis at the project onset on community strengths and resources, a value that remained consistent over the study period.

Pairwise comparisons reflected significant changes in quality of engagement for EP1 (focus on community perspectives and determinants of health) and EP2 (partner input is vital) from T1–T3. Qualitative findings reflect that the inclusion of diverse groups on the Council, including persons living with dementia and family caregivers alongside CHWs, enabled the focus to remain on the community. Further, the use of pláticas as the culturally-specific approach to engagement with the Council and wider community most likely contributed to the noted increase in scores for EP1. Capacity building activities and consistent meetings between the study team and Council that intentionally sought partner input to shape plática content and processes likely contributed to the observed increase in EP2 scores.

EP6 (facilitate collaborative, equitable partnerships) improved from T1–T2 and T1–T3, supported by qualitative accounts that prolonged involvement in the project fostered stronger relationships between community and academic partners. EP7 (involve all partners in the dissemination process) improved only at T1–T3, reflecting that longer participation created opportunities to present work and co-author publications. EP8 (build and maintain trust in the partnership) also increased significantly from T1–T2, consistent with member accounts that sustained involvement cultivated trust and confidence in the Council’s collaborative work.

When quantitative data were assessed within levels of engagement, REST scores were consistently highest in the collaboration theme, explained by leadership’s role in guiding discussions and consensus. Over time, Council members described becoming more comfortable to take leadership in planning and implementing the pláticas. The greatest increases were from T1–T3 in the partnership category (quality + 6.9, quantity + 6.8), explained by interviews describing more opportunities for Council input, decision-making, and trust-building through the plática process as the project progressed.

Discussion

Including communities as partners in research, where their perspectives and experiences are centered is key to addressing the persistent underrepresentation of Latinos in dementia care research. This study assessed engagement with a Council convened to conduct culturally-specific outreach and engagement with Latinos in South Texas. Despite the growing urgency to address dementia research gaps among communities of color, this study is one of few to assess community partner engagement for dementia research using a council model [18, 33].

Findings can be interpreted through a Donabedian-type framework of structure, process, and outcome [49]. Structural components of the Council influenced engagement processes and outcomes, helping explain high baseline REST scores. While the numerical difference between baseline and follow-up REST scores may appear modest, the shift from 4.17 to near 4.77 on the quality scale represents a potentially meaningful progression in engagement quality. The real-world significance of this change is most clearly understood through the qualitative findings, which document a concrete trajectory from structured participation at baseline to co-leadership of community pláticas over time. Similar to findings from other studies assessing the Council structure for research partnerships [18, 50], the establishment of a diverse council to oversee the project was an effective facilitator of engagement at the onset of the project. Participants stressed that representation from different regions of South Texas and varied lived or professional dementia experiences strengthened the shared knowledge and capacity of partners. While increases were observed across most EPs, no statistically significant difference was identified for EP5 (Builds on strengths and resources within the community or patient population). This may be explained by the study’s grounding in key community-based participatory research (CBPR) principles, as the project was designed from the outset around the needs and experiences of the target population [51].

Past community-engaged research identified a need for culturally specific approaches to engage Latinos in dementia research [26, 33, 52]. To address this, CHWs were integrated as a critical component of the Council structure. CHWs strengthened the overall project through their deep connections to community members in their areas and their ability to bring the perspectives of highly impacted and underserved communities to the project. While CHWs have been leveraged as key partners in health research broadly [53], they remain mostly excluded as co-leaders in dementia care research [24, 54]. By partnering with CHW leaders, the pláticas were ultimately better aligned with the needs and experiences of South Texas communities.

Processes of engagement applied during the study period included monthly online meetings, capacity building workshops, and the development and implementation of community pláticas. Despite the geographic diversity of members across South Texas regions, the virtual monthly meeting format supported consistent participation of Council members and was not identified as a barrier to engagement. The accessibility of online meetings may have facilitated broader regional representation than would have been feasible with in-person meetings alone. Through these activities, partnership with the Council evolved as the study progressed, as reflected in the increasing REST scores within EP6, ‘Facilitate collaborative, equitable partnerships”. This study demonstrates that trust-building activities facilitated over time were central to cultivating equitable partnerships with the study team. This is consistent with community-engaged research methodology that relies on mutually beneficial long-term relationships of rapport-building and trust [17, 55, 56]. As the project progressed, Council members gained new skills, developed shared goals with a mutual sense of purpose, and enacted decision-making and co-learning while tailoring engagement to their communities. With each opportunity to listen and be listened to, the Council was able to build and maintain trust in each other and in the project team.

The Council’s structure and processes supported successful outcomes from the partnership, most notably the implementation of six community pláticas in South Texas. As a reciprocal, bi-directional storytelling practice rooted in Latino culture, pláticas have been described by community researchers as an effective qualitative method [38]. Yet this approach had not been formally examined as a strategy for strengthening community-academic partnerships for research. Interviews with members of the Council highlighted the personal impact of their role in planning and implementing pláticas that were tailored to their respective regional communities. This approach built on their lived experiences, knowledges, and connections, emphasizing the importance of their role as leaders in the planning and implementation process. When assessed for the five levels of engagement, the REST data reflected the greatest increase in scores within “partnership,” the highest category of engagement. Council members’ growing sense of ownership and leadership over time as they implemented the community pláticas explains these increases and their expressed commitment to sustain and expand the work.

This study demonstrates that aligning partnership structures with key principles of community-centered engagement generates outcomes of strengthened trust, partnership, and sustained leadership. Together, these findings point to a pathway for transforming dementia research to more fully share power and leadership with highly impacted communities.

Limitations

Few statistically significant findings from the comparative analysis of REST scores for quantity of engagement are likely due to the study being underpowered by the small sample size. Quantitatively measuring engagement is inherently challenging, as meaningful engagement activities often operate with smaller groups or councils. While the recommended size of a community advisory board or council varies, most guidance suggests staying below 10–15 members [55, 57]. Engaging more partners on the Council may have added some power to the analysis but would have potentially come at a cost to the quality of engagement possible.

Engagement was assessed through Council member self-report using the REST, which may be subject to bias given that the same team who facilitated engagement also administered the survey instrument. This is an inherent challenge in studies on engagement, where the relational nature of the work makes it difficult to fully separate the overlapping roles of the researchers and community partners. Notably, interviews reflect some members’ willingness to share critical observations alongside positive experiences, suggesting at least a degree of comfort with honest reflection that tempers concerns about social desirability in this context. The qualitative coding team were all members of the study team, which may have introduced bias in the interpretation of findings. While consensus coding procedures and the availability of a fourth observer were employed to enhance rigor, community partners were engaged in the integration and interpretation of findings across the qualitative and quantitative phases of the study, strengthening the credibility and community-relevance of the conclusions drawn.

Persons living with dementia in this study expressed hesitancy to contribute in group settings alongside professionals and caregivers, reflecting the possible effects of power imbalances in the research context. Some Council members described growing more comfortable over time as trust within the group developed, suggesting that the relational approach employed by this Council may help attenuate these dynamics. Lastly, this study was conducted with Latino communities in South Texas, which may limit generalizability. The approach and methods warrant further study to determine their applicability to other populations.

Conclusion

There is a critical need for evidence on effective strategies to recruit and retain underrepresented populations in research. Engagement science is a developing discipline and research is needed to identify effective strategies for community partner engagement, especially among highly impacted marginalized populations [11, 19]. This study contributes to the science of engagement by reflecting on outcomes from the implementation of culturally-specific engagement strategies among Latino communities of South Texas. Results from this study provide evidence that can support researchers and communities to meaningfully partner with each other for research around dementia care. Findings provide evidence that researchers and communities can draw on to build equitable partnerships and address the persistent underrepresentation of highly impacted populations in dementia research.
