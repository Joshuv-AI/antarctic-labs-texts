Neonatal developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with movement disorder and arthrogryposis: A shared phenotype across brain‐expressed sodium channelopathies

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeonatal developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with movement disorder and arthrogryposis (NDEEMA) represents the most severe end of the gain‐of‐function (GOF) SCN1A disorder spectrum. Sporadic cases of congenital arthrogryposis have also been reported in individuals with SCN2A‐, SCN3A‐, and SCN8A‐related developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Here, we investigated whether NDEEMA occurs in other brain‐expressed sodium channelopathies and characterized its features.MethodsIndividuals with the clinical phenotype of NDEEMA were identified through internal databases, an international network of epileptologists and geneticists, and the literature. Their clinical and genetic information was analyzed. A literature survey was conducted to review studies describing the functional effects of the pathogenic variants.ResultsOf 46 NDEEMA individuals, 25 harbored variants in SCN1A, 13 in SCN2A, one in SCN3A, and seven in SCN8A. Thirty‐five different pathogenic/likely pathogenic missense variants were identified, all of which clustered in evolutionary conserved paralogous NaV positions. Five individuals died in utero. Thirty‐nine of 41 (95%) liveborn individuals developed neonatal epilepsy with tonic seizures and/or apnea. Thirty‐one individuals tried sodium channel blockers, of whom 21 (68%) experienced seizure reduction. All individuals for whom information was available developed movement disorders, with myoclonus, dystonia, and tremor being the most common features. Literature review of functional studies revealed that nine NDEEMA variants, and the corresponding paralogues of 16 additional NDEEMA variants, have been biophysically characterized as GOF.SignificanceThis study expands the phenotype of NDEEMA from SCN1A to its paralogue sodium channel genes expressed in the brain: SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A.Keywords: congenital arthrogryposis, DEE, SCN1A, SCN2A, SCN3A, SCN8A

INTRODUCTION

Voltage‐gated sodium channels (SCNs) are critical for the initiation and propagation of action potentials in neurons and excitable tissues. 1 The four SCN genes expressed in the brain, SCN1A, SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A, have been associated with a broad spectrum of epilepsies and neurodevelopmental disorders. SCNs share a common evolutionary origin, with a similar amino acid sequence and basic structure. 2 Their phenotypic variability is thought to be related to their age‐specific expression in different neuronal subtypes, as well as differences in the functional effects caused by the variants: gain‐of‐function (GOF), loss‐of‐function (LOF), mixedfunction, or no effect.

SCN1A, encoding NaV1.1, is mainly expressed in inhibitory interneurons 3 and is associated with Dravet syndrome, genetic epilepsy with febrile seizures plus, familial hemiplegic migraine, and a spectrum of early infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs). 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 This group of disorders includes neonatal DEE with movement disorder and arthrogryposis (NDEEMA), early infantile (onset <3 months) DEE with movement disorder, and the least severe, later onset (onset ≥3 months) DEE without a movement disorder, without any fever‐induced or prolonged seizures. SCN2A and SCN8A, encoding NaV1.2 and NaV1.6, respectively, are expressed primarily in excitatory neurons. 3 Monoallelic variants in both genes are associated with a phenotypic spectrum ranging from self‐limited epilepsies to severe DEEs, developmental delay (DD)/intellectual disability (ID), autism spectrum disorders, and movement disorders in individuals without epilepsy. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 SCN3A, encoding NaV1.3, is expressed in radial glia, intermediate progenitor cells, and neurons of the developing brain. 13 Pathogenic variants in SCN3A have been associated with severe DEEs, polymicrogyria, and other types of epilepsy phenotypes such as mild focal epilepsy. 14

Brain‐expressed SCN genes have also been identified in individuals with congenital arthrogryposis, also known as arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC). 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 AMC is characterized by congenital contractures in more than two joints across multiple body regions. 22 AMC with central nervous system (CNS) involvement often leads to severe functional disability and is associated with a poor prognosis. 23

A recent study highlighted this by linking SCN1A GOF variants to AMC as part of a severe neurodevelopmental disorder termed NDEEMA. 5 NDEEMA is characterized by neonatal seizures, AMC, and movement disorder that develop in the first or second year of life. In contrast to individuals with SCN1A LOF variants, who typically experience worsening seizures after SCN blocker (SCB) use, those with NDEEMA usually experience a decrease in seizure frequency with SCBs, reflecting the GOF effect of these variants.

Building on the established link between SCN1A GOF variants and NDEEMA, we investigated whether similar clinical presentations occur in other brain‐expressed sodium channelopathies. Although a few cases involving SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A variants and AMC have been reported, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 our study expands this knowledge by describing additional cases and by demonstrating that SCN1A‐, SCN2A‐, SCN3A‐, and SCN8A‐related disorders share the NDEEMA phenotype.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

Affected individuals with SCN gene variants and AMC/NDEEMA were identified in two ways. First, a PubMed search was performed (up to December 2024) using the terms “SCN1A”, “SCN2A”, “SCN3A” or “SCN8A” in combination with “arthrogryposis”, “contractures”, “fetal akinesia”, “limb deformities”, or “skeletal malformations”. These terms were also used to search patient databases/registries (international SCN2A and SCN8A database at the Danish Epilepsy Center). Second, using the SCN viewer, 2 the paralogue positions of known NDEEMA variants in SCN1A, SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A were identified. Then, it was checked whether individuals with pathogenic variants at these positions were reported in the literature or patient registries of each gene, and their phenotypes were reviewed. After identifying the individuals, all relevant information was collected, which is shown in the Supporting Information.

Evaluation of unpublished individuals

Fifteen newly identified individuals were included in the study. In addition, the clinical data of nine previously published individuals were updated. 5 , 9 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 19 A questionnaire was completed for each individual, which included medical and developmental history, epilepsy, neurological findings, and electroencephalographic (EEG) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. Seizure semiology and epilepsy syndromes were classified according to the International League Against Epilepsy recommendations. 24 , 25 Information on antiseizure medications (ASMs) was collected, and their effect was evaluated when possible. Genetic testing was performed in various diagnostic/research laboratories using next generation sequencing. Variants were classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines. 26 The presence of each variant in control populations was assessed using gnomAD, and the pathogenicity of variants was determined using in silico tools such as CADD, REVEL, and Grantham scores (available in Supporting Information).

The study was approved by the institutional review board at the Danish Epilepsy Center (project ID: EMN‐2024‐01992). New individuals (or their legal guardians in the case of minors or those with ID) provided written informed consent.

Functional interpretation of the variants

To assess the functional effect of the variants associated with NDEEMA, literature reports on the functional characterization of each variant were reviewed. For the variants whose functional characterization was not available, paralogue SCN gene variant functional characterization results were evaluated. 2 , 27

Protein structure mapping and variant hotspot identification

The cryo‐EM structure of NaV1.1 encoded by SCN1A (PDB‐ID: 7DTD) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank. Variants from SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A were aligned to SCN1A using the multiple sequence alignment across SCN gene paralogues. NDEEMA‐associated variants across all voltage‐gated SCNs were mapped onto the NaV1.1 structure. Visualizations of the protein structure were created in PyMOL (v2.5.8).

To assess whether NDEEMA variants cluster in distinct protein regions, we compared their distribution to pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants from ClinVar, a public archive of human genetic variation with clinical classifications. We collected 2005 missense variants in SCN1A, SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A that were annotated as pathogenic or likely pathogenic with at least one review star (ClinVar accessed July 2025). To identify protein regions significantly enriched for NDEEMA‐associated variants relative to other pathogenic variants, we applied the three‐dimensional (3D) hotspot detection approach described by Iqbal et al. 28 In brief, we tested for enrichment of NDEEMA variants compared to ClinVar variants within a 12‐Å 3D window around each residue.

RESULTS

In addition to the 13 individuals with SCN1A‐related NDEEMA described by Brunklaus et al. in 2022, 5 we identified 33 individuals with AMC/NDEEMA (15 new, 18 published including nine with updated clinical data), bringing the total number to 46. This cohort expands the known genetic spectrum of AMC/NDEEMA to include individuals with variants in SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A. Of the 46 individuals, 25 were female, 19 were male, and the sex of two individuals was not reported. The median age at last assessment was 2 years (range = 17 weeks' gestation to 28 years). Twenty‐four individuals were deceased. Clinical data for each gene are summarized in Table 1.

Clinical findings

Because AMC was an inclusion criterion, it was present in all 46 individuals, with varying degrees of severity; 36 (78%) individuals had AMC affecting upper and lower limbs, seven (15%) had AMC affecting only the upper limbs, and three (7%) only the lower limbs (Figure 1). In 14 (30%) individuals, AMC was diagnosed in utero on a prenatal ultrasound scan. Among the 46 individuals, disease‐causing variants were identified prenatally in four and postnatally in 28. The timing of diagnosis was not reported for the remaining individuals; nonetheless, because none was described as receiving genetic diagnosis prenatally, we infer that genetic diagnoses were likely obtained after birth. AMC was associated with additional features of the fetal akinesia deformation sequence (FADS) in 28 (60%) individuals. The most common features were congenital hernias (including abdominal wall, diaphragmatic, umbilical, and inguinal), dysmorphic ears, micrognathia, hip dysplasia, scoliosis, and chest wall/pulmonary deformities.

Of the 41 liveborn individuals, 39 (95%) developed epilepsy at a median age of 1 day (range = 1–28 days). One individual died on the third postnatal day of life, without having overt seizures. For another individual, evident seizures were not reported.

The most common seizure type at onset was tonic seizures and/or apnea, occurring in 26 of 41 (63%) individuals. Migrating focal seizures were reported in three individuals with an SCN1A variant and two with an SCN2A variant. Three individuals had status epilepticus (SE) at birth. Hyperekplexia/startle episodes (nonepileptic myoclonus) without an EEG correlate occurred in 13 of 41 (32%) individuals. Seizure types at follow‐up were tonic in 17 (41%) individuals, focal in 14 (34%), bilateral tonic–clonic in 12 (29%), hemiclonic in six (15%), atypical absence in six (15%), myoclonic in six (15%), epileptic spasms in seven (17%), and eyelid myoclonia in five (12%). Convulsive SE occurred in 14 (34%) individuals, and nonconvulsive status epilepticus in six (15%) cases. Focal (n = 15, 36%) and multifocal (n = 14, 34%) epileptiform discharges, generalized spike–wave (n = 6, 15%), burst–suppression patterns (n = 7, 17%), and background slowing (n = 3, 7%) were observed on EEG. The epilepsy outcome was known for 35 individuals. Seizures were drug‐resistant in 34 of 35 (97%) individuals; one (3%) individual was seizure‐free on valproic acid. Of the 31 individuals who tried SCBs (phenytoin, lacosamide, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, zonisamide, lamotrigine), seizures reduced in 21 (68%), whereas 10 (32%) showed no seizure improvement.

Movement disorders were observed in 24 of 41 (59%) liveborn individuals, namely, nonepileptic myoclonus, hyperkinetic movement disorder, dyskinetic movement disorder, orolingual dyskinesia, dystonia, and tremor. The remaining individuals either died before the age of 2 years (n = 8) or were younger than 2 years at follow‐up (n = 6), or a movement disorder was not reported (n = 3).

All 26 individuals older than 6 months had global DD (n = 15) or profound ID (n = 11). Three individuals reached the milestone of sitting independently at 7–9 months of age, and only one could walk with support at the age of 2.5 years. Seventeen of 26 individuals were nonverbal, and one individual with SCN1A could speak in simple sentences but later experienced regression, whereas information about the nine remaining individuals was not available.

Brain imaging studies collected in 31 individuals (30 MRI, one computed tomography) were abnormal in 17 (55%). Cerebral atrophy/ventricular enlargement was seen in 10, white matter abnormalities in six, corpus callosum abnormalities in five, malformations of cortical development (polymicrogyria) in five, and signs of ischemic lesions in one individual. All four individuals with polymicrogyria had SCN2A pathogenic variants.

Electromyographic studies, performed in three individuals, revealed slow conduction time and demyelinating pattern of all four limbs in one individual, possible myopathy in one, and normal results in the other.

Of 41 liveborn individuals, 20 (48%) died within the first years of life (Figure 3). Four newborns died due to either respiratory failure (n = 2) or unknown cause (n = 2). Twelve infants died in the first 2 years of life due to respiratory problems (n = 8), ventriculoperitoneal shunt infection (n = 1), SE (n = 2), or unknown cause (n = 1). Three individuals died at 3 years of age, due to respiratory failure (n = 1), SE (n = 1), and complications of recurrent gastrointestinal disorder (n = 1), and one individual died at 7 years of age due to heart failure (n = 1). In addition, elective terminations of pregnancy were performed following an abnormal prenatal ultrasound and genetic test results in five instances. Using the Kaplan–Meier method to analyze time to death, we found that survival was significantly poorer in individuals with SCN2A and SCN8A compared to those with SCN1A (log‐rank test, p = .045). The estimated probability of survival to the age of 5 years for SCN1A was 70%, whereas it was approximately 25% for SCN2A and SCN8A.

Genetic findings

Of the 46 individuals, 25 harbored monoallelic pathogenic variants in SCN1A, 13 in SCN2A, one in SCN3A, and seven in SCN8A. A total of 35 pathogenic/likely pathogenic missense variants were identified. In 39 of 46 individuals, the variant occurred de novo; in seven, segregation was unknown.

Nine variants were identified in two or more individuals: SCN1A T162I, SCN1A I236V, SCN1A L893F, SCN1A S1346P, SCN1A I1347V, SCN2A V251I, SCN2A R1626Q, SCN8A V409M, and SCN8A I1327V.

All variants were plotted according to their corresponding SCN1A index position, showing specific paralogous positions, where variants were present in more than one individual (Figure 4). Six individuals had variants at the SCN1A‐I236‐equivalent position (SCN1A‐I236, SCN2A‐I237, SCN8A‐I240); four at the SCN1A‐V421‐equivalent position (SCN1A‐V421, SCN2A‐V423, SCN8A‐V409); two at the SCN1A‐A1339‐equivalent position (SCN1A‐A1339, SCN2A‐A1329); five at the SCN1A‐I1347‐equivalent position (SCN1A‐I1347, SCN8A‐I1327); and two at the SCN1A‐M1780‐equivalent position (SCN1A‐M1780, SCN2A‐M1770).

We assessed the location of the NDEEMA variants in the SCN protein. Variants were observed in the S4–S5 linker regions (n = 13, 37%), pore‐forming S5 and S6 regions (located close to the intracellular surface; n = 11, 31%), voltage‐sensing domain (n = 4, 11%), S1–S3 domains (n = 4, 11%), D3–D4 linker domain (n = 2, 6%), and D2–D3 linker domain (n = 1, 3%). Compared to ClinVar pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, which represent the full spectrum of disease phenotype associated with these four channels (accessed July 2025), NDEEMA‐associated variants were enriched in specific structural hotspots (Figure 5B), indicating a nonrandom distribution distinct from other pathogenic variants in the same genes.

NDEEMA variants show GOF effects

Nine NDEEMA variants, SCN1A T162I, SCN1A I236V, SCN2A V423L, SCN2A R856Q, SCN2A A1329D, SCN2A R1626Q, SCN2A M1770L, SCN3A L855P, and SCN8A I1327V, have been functionally characterized as GOF. 5 , 6 , 10 , 29 , 30 , 31 For the remaining 26 variants that had not been functionally characterized, we checked the functional testing results of their paralogue SCN gene variants, which was available for 17 of 26. Of 17 paralogue SCN gene variants with the same amino acid change, 16 were characterized as GOF and one as mixed function. NDEEMA variants led to GOF effect by either elevation of persistent current or a hyperpolarizing shift in half‐activation voltage in steady‐state activation. Overall, based on the phenotype homogeneity and the results of the functional characterization of the variants or their paralogues, all identified variants are likely to be GOF.

Individuals with paralogue variants but without an NDEEMA phenotype

We also collected clinical data from the literature for individuals who had the same or paralogue NDEEMA‐associated variants but did not have all the features of NDEEMA. Fifteen individuals had exactly the same variants as individuals with NDEEMA (SCN8A I1327V, n = 5; SCN2A V856Q, n = 3; SCN2A V251I, n = 2; SCN2A V423L, n = 2; SCN2A M1770L, n = 2; SCN2A R1626Q, n = 1), whereas 73 had paralogous variants (SCN1A, n = 12; SCN2A, n = 2; SCN3A, n = 15; SCN8A, n = 44).

All 15 individuals who harbored NDEEMA‐associated variants developed early onset DEEs, but they did not present with AMC. Movement disorders were reported in four individuals (myoclonus, tremor, hyperkinesia, dystonia). Six individuals developed severe developmental delays. Age at last follow‐up was known for eight individuals and ranged from 1 month to 12 years. One individual was mosaic for the SCN1A T782I variant and presented with severe DEE with movement disorders but no AMC.

For the 62 individuals with paralogue variants, all but two had epilepsy, and information about two was unavailable. Age at seizure onset ranged from 1 to 10 months. Twenty individuals had movement disorders. Fifty‐seven had DD/ID. Information for the remaining 11 individuals was unknown. Age at the last follow‐up, known for 48 individuals, ranged from 2 months to 35 years.

DISCUSSION

NDEEMA has so far only been described in individuals with GOF variants in SCN1A. A few case reports have described similar clinical presentations for SCN2A, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 SCN3A, 14 and SCN8A 19 , 20 , 21 ; however, recognition of the broader association of sodium channelopathies with the NDEEMA phenotype has not been made. Our comprehensive comparative study identifies AMC to be the earliest presentation of brain‐expressed sodium channelopathies and shows striking similarities between individuals with SCN1A NDEEMA and those in whom the syndrome is associated with SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A variants. Our study demonstrates that NDEEMA is not restricted to SCN1A, but occurs in individuals with pathogenic variants in SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A as well. This has important implications for diagnosis and treatment, as early recognition of NDEEMA may guide treatment with SCBs. This study also emphasizes the utility of paralogue genes in assessing the effect of a gene variant and highlights the overlap in symptoms between different SCN disorders.

AMC is not a diagnosis in itself but rather a clinical sign that describes a highly heterogeneous group of conditions characterized by multiple joint contractures identified at birth. 22 The unifying feature across all AMC cases is reduced fetal mobility, which can result from a wide range of underlying causes. These include CNS abnormalities (involving the brain or anterior horn cells), neuromuscular junction defects, peripheral nerve or muscle pathology, connective tissue disorders, teratogenic exposures, maternal illness, or mechanical constraints in utero. 23

AMC is an extremely genetically heterogeneous disorder, associated with more than 400 genes. Pathogenic variants in the membrane receptors/ion channels form the second largest group of genes implicated in AMC. 20 , 32 Some of them are expressed in the brain, others in the spinal cord, peripheral nervous system, and muscle. When AMC is linked with CNS disorders, it often results in severe functional disability and poor prognosis. 23 In the previous large studies on genetics of AMC or FADS, brain‐expressed SCN genes have been reported in single cases (up to 2%). 20 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36

Our findings represent the largest cohort to date of individuals with AMC associated with pathogenic variants in brain‐expressed SCN genes. Variants in these genes are likely to cause CNS dysfunction, leading to reduced fetal movements and AMC/FADS as well as severe intrauterine or neonatal onset DEE and movement disorders. This expands the phenotypic spectrum of SCN‐related disorders beyond epilepsy and neurodevelopmental delay to include AMC and, in more severe cases, FADS.

Twenty‐three individuals from our cohort (15 with SCN1A, three with SCN2A, five with SCN8A) exhibited a phenotype fully consistent with NDEEMA, with all features present: neonatal onset DEE, movement disorder, and arthrogryposis. In contrast, 23 individuals (10 with SCN1A, 10 with SCN2A, one with SCN3A, two with SCN8A) had incomplete NDEEMA phenotype, lacking either movement disorder or epilepsy component. This is likely because many of these individuals died early in life or were too young at the time of follow‐up to develop the whole spectrum of NDEEMA features. The presence of SCN gene variants with AMC and DEE warrants diagnosis of a severe phenotype, with high likelihood of developing hyperkinetic movement disorder later in life and high risk of early mortality.

The clinical similarity across individuals with NDEEMA is also reflected in their genetic variants. The variants identified in NDEEMA are most frequently found in the functionally important intracellular regions, in the S4–S5 linker regions that lead to a disruption of the coupling between the voltage‐sensitive and pore regions of the channel, in the S4 segment, which is the voltage‐sensitive domain, and in the intracellular part of the S5–S6 segments that line the pores. NDEEMA variant clusters are similar to those described to be enriched for GOF variants in SCN1A. 5 Nine NDEEMA variants and 16 paralogue variants have been characterized as GOF. According to Brunklaus et al., 2 if an identical missense variant was functionally characterized in a paralogous SCN, there is up to 92% likelihood that the reported results also apply to the new variant. Here, we show that the identical variants in paralogous channels likely lead to coupled functional effects and phenotypes.

Although SCBs target the underlying GOF properties of NDEEMA‐causing variants, they reduced seizures in only 68% of individuals. Epilepsy remained challenging to manage in nearly all individuals, with 97% developing drug‐resistant epilepsy. The number of ASMs used over a lifetime ranged from 1 to 14, underscoring the urgent need for more effective treatment strategies.

One promising new treatment approach is the use of antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs). The first case of ASO therapy in an individual with SCN2A NDEEMA was recently reported, 31 where a preterm infant with intrauterine onset seizures and SE at birth was treated with an SCN2A‐specific ASO, elsunersen, from 7 weeks of life. Treatment with the ASO, in combination with SCBs (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin), led to >60% seizure reduction and cessation of SE. Future studies are needed to evaluate the impact of ASOs on neurodevelopmental outcomes. Their effect will likely depend on the timing of diagnosis and administration of treatment.

In exceptional cases, we observed individuals with the same pathogenic variants as those with NDEEMA, but without the same phenotype. Instead, they presented with DEEs with or without movement disorder. Such phenotypic variability has been observed in recurrent variants of SCN1A, SCN2A, and SCN8A as well. 9 , 10 , 37 Several factors could influence phenotypic variability, as it could be due to other monogenic causes or background genetics having a modifying effect or environmental factors, as has already been described in SCN1A LOF cases. 38 , 39 Mosaicism could also play a protective role. For example, an individual with an SCN1A T782I variant with 18% mosaicism presented with a severe DEE and dyskinetic movement disorder, but not AMC. 40 Phenotypic variability could also be due to underreporting of skeletal malformations in individuals with severe DEEs, because comorbidities in cases of severe DEEs are often overlooked. Further studies regarding the variable phenotype would be useful to study these effects.

Our findings highlight the role of SCNs in the early development of the brain. It is well established that the expression levels of SCN1A, SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A change with age. SCN3A is expressed earliest during development, and its expression declines over time. SCN2A is highly expressed across all ages, whereas the expression of SCN1A and SCN8A is low prenatally and increases steadily throughout childhood into adulthood. 41 The presence of early features (AMC and early infantile seizures) in all individuals could demonstrate a more essential early developmental role not only of SCN3A and SCN2A, but also of SCN1A and SCN8A, than previously considered.

It remains unclear through which mechanisms different brain‐expressed SCNs cause the similar phenotype of AMC and NDEEMA. SCNs are expressed in different neuronal subtypes; SCN2A/3A/8A are mainly in excitatory neurons, whereas SCN1A is mainly expressed in inhibitory neurons. 3 How GOF in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons leads to similar clinical features is unknown. It may be because γ‐aminobutyric acid is an excitatory neurotransmitter in the developing brain, 42 and early life effects of GOF in SCN1A could lead to disruption of similar networks as GOF in SCN2A/3A/8A. Another mechanism could also be that GOF variants in SCN1A directly involve excitatory neurons, as SCN1A is also expressed in the excitatory neurons, and could contribute to this disorder through the excitatory pathway, which is not involved in the Dravet syndrome phenotype. 40 SCN1A GOF could also have an effect on the network. 43 Modeling of neuronal firing in SCN1A GOF variants revealed that parvalbumin interneurons with GOF variants become hyperexcitable, which in turn leads to network instability, damage of compensatory mechanism, and predisposition to epileptiformlike activity. 43 , 44 More studies with stem cell and animal models are required to investigate these mechanisms of action.

AMC has also been observed with other brain‐expressed ion channel genes, such as CACNA1E, 45 NALCN, 46 KCNQ2, 47 ATP1A2, 48 and GRIN1. 49 Studies are also needed to identify the common pathways these different conditions share that lead to a similar phenotype across genes. Interestingly, AMC has also been observed in individuals with pathogenic variants in other SCNs, such as SCN4A and SCN5A, 20 , 33 , 50 but their mechanism of action is likely different from the brain‐expressed SCNs.

There are several limitations to this study. We collected data retrospectively using questionnaires filled in by clinicians using original medical health records or through literature review. Therefore, detailed assessments of the treatment response were not always available or reported. In addition, most of the individuals were deceased, or had a variable duration of follow‐up. Information on functional testing was available for only a subset of variants, and functional characterization was based for the remaining variants on the literature and in silico tools. To better delineate the epilepsy and movement disorder components and to confirm phenotypic similarities across SCN‐related NDEEMA, prospective natural history studies are needed.

This study expands the phenotype of NDEEMA beyond SCN1A to its paralogue brain‐expressed SCN genes (SCN2A, SCN3A, SCN8A) and highlights NDEEMA as the most severe end of the phenotypic spectrum for these disorders. The association of congenital AMC with pathogenic variants in one of the SCN genes appears to be a strong predictor of poor prognosis. With the increasing introduction of genome‐wide prenatal genetic testing in the clinic, more cases of AMC/FADS will be revealed. This underscores the need to consider brain‐expressed sodium channelopathies as a differential diagnosis of AMC, especially when accompanied by CNS disturbances, as a correct diagnosis enables an estimation of the prognosis important for prenatal decisions. Furthermore, recognizing the shared functional and phenotypic implications of paralogue SCN gene variants supports the use of similar therapeutic strategies, such as SCBs or gene‐specific ASOs, and informs genetic counseling and personalized care planning.
