Green synthesized selenium nanoparticles-based amelioration of Cd toxicity in radish using seed priming
Authors: Saba Maqsood, Shakil Ahmed, Sobia Ashraf, Muhammad Nauman Ahmad, Tariq Aziz, Manal F. Elkhadragy, Ashwag Shami, Ibtisam A. M. Alghabban, Aziza Mahdy Nahari, Zoarilala Rinah Razafindrakoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growth and productivity of vegetable crops are adversely affected by abiotic stress, particularly heavy metals such as Cadmium. A study was conducted in which Raphanus sativus L. was treated with green-synthesized SeNPs to mitigate the adverse effects of Cd. Green synthesized selenium nanoparticle concentrations of 15 mg L-1 (SeNPs-1), 30 mg/L (SeNPs-2), and 45 mg/L (SeNPs-3) were used to prime radish seeds. The optimal concentration of SeNPs was determined to be 30 mg/L, which improved the biochemical (total soluble proteins and proline) and physiological (photosynthetic pigment) characteristics. In comparison to the control, SeNPs-2 increased growth attributes and biomass production at both vegetative and reproductive stages. In addition, compared to the control, SeNPs-2 increased the stomatal conductance (3.31-fold), transpiration rate (85.33%), and net photosynthetic rate (1.95-fold). Proline content was elevated in response to SeNPs-2 + Cd (64.65%) treatment in comparison to SeNPs-2, which were not exposed to Cd stress. Cadmium content was significantly reduced in SeNPs-primed plants compared to Cd-only treated plants. These findings suggest that SeNPs seed priming is a promising, eco-friendly strategy for enhancing Cd tolerance in radish and warrants further field validation.

Introduction

Abiotic stress is one of the limiting factors that impair plant development and adversely affect crop production, quality, and quantity1. Abiotic stresses affecting plants’ metabolic processes include salt, drought, extreme temperature, and heavy metals (HMs)2. Due to the potential environmental concerns, heavy metal (HM) pollution in agricultural soils has emerged as an important concern for today’s society. The natural environment has deteriorated in the last few decades due to increased organic and inorganic contaminants, damaging the natural environment3. Heavy metals (HMs) hinder the process of photosynthesis and respiration, water relations, organic matter transportation, ability to tolerate stress, and mineral nourishment, among other physiological and biochemical functions, which are crucial to plant development and survival4.

Cadmium is one of the hazardous heavy metal5. It is discharged into the environment by both man-made and natural processes. The excessive utilization of cadmium-based pesticides and fertilizer products, extraction of minerals, metalworking operations, dyes, the electroplating process, and the burning process are examples of anthropogenic activities6. However, the primary methods by which Cd accumulates in the natural environment are through forest fires, wastewater, weathering of cadmium-containing rocks, and volcanic eruptions7. Cadmium may exhibit detrimental impacts even when present in minute amounts8. Its harmful consequences on human well-being are linked to the retention of substances in the respiratory system, the liver, kidney, bones, and gastrointestinal system, which can result in chronic disorders, for example, diabetes, kidney stones, emphysema, tumors, renal tubular harm, and osteoporosis, among other health issues9. Plants grown in cadmium-polluted soil rapidly absorb the heavy metal and retain it in the edible parts of the plants, whether fruit or seed10.

In at least two dimensions, the NPs diameters vary from 1 to 100 nm, making them incredibly fine micro-elements11–13. NPs have unique catalytic capabilities and have made tremendous progress in the past few years. Potential nanoparticle advances have been utilized in agriculture biotechnology, the security of food, water, and sewage treatment, biosensors, medical diagnosis, and therapeutic uses14–19. Researchers from across the world are looking into the use of NPs to increase plant development and yield to boost the quality of crops and overall productivity. There are many types of nanoparticles (NPs), including inorganic, carbon-based, dendrimer, and composite materials20.

In addition to boosting agricultural productivity and quality, NPs have been demonstrated in several studies to enhance plant photosynthesis, germination, and resistance to oxidative stress. Exogenous applications of NPs have an effective and beneficial impact on the development and growth of plants21. Several distinct techniques contribute to the production of the nanoparticles. The initial strategies that are used consist of the physical and chemical methods. Green and biological synthesis are two novel approaches that have been noticed to reduce the severity of metals22.

Selenium is a trace micronutrient that is crucial for the functioning of the ecosystem. It is essential for improving the body’s immune system, animal reproduction, and healthy plant growth23. One vital agronomic approach that has plenty of evidence supporting its beneficial impact on the germination and growth of seedlings is seed priming. It boosts the process of germination and increases the growth of seedlings, their vigour and nutrient content24.

Seed priming is a conventional approach in agriculture that involves the pre-treatment of seeds before planting them to enhance the growth of seeds and plant establishment25. Seeds are often submerged and then dried using a water-based technique, or physical techniques such as UV priming are used26. Appropriate water absorption is essential to initiate pre-germinating metabolic process while preventing radicle formation27. Seed priming (SP) agents can include hormones, growth stimulants, nutrients, metal ion- nanoparticles, fertilizers, microbes, and even radiation28. A newly developed technique called seed nano-priming utilizes nanoparticles for seed priming29. Seed coating, also referred to as dressing, is mostly produced by adsorbing on the seed using the standard seed priming method, which uses solutions containing nutrients, hormones, biopolymers, or hydro-priming30. It was previously reported that radish seed hydro-priming can improve some physiological and biochemical parameters of radish plants31.

Selenium (Se) is absorbed and accumulated differentially in the tissues and organs of various plant species. Therefore, when compared with species of other plant families, the Brassicaceae family tended to absorb and retain more selenium32. Many economically significant crops are members of the Brassicaceae family. These crops produce condiments, leafy and root vegetables, plastic, salad dressing, cooking oils, margarine, animal feed, and green manures for restoring deficient soil. The most widely produced Brassica oilseed crop for commercial purposes is radish. Due to its high content of indigestible erucic acid, radish seed oil is widely used in cosmetic products33. Beyond its use as a food crop, radish seed oil is valued in cosmetic and industrial applications, including lubricants and biofuels, making it relevant to Industrial Crops and Products85.

Root vegetables like radish are frequently planted because they have tender young roots, which are consumed raw or cooked in salads. Minerals such as (phosphorous), Ca (calcium), K (potassium), and vitamin C (citric acid) are abundant in radish. Due to the root’s ability to absorb heavy metals from the soil, root, and vegetable crops are crucial and vulnerable to a variety of heavy metal stresses. The contamination of the soil may have occurred in the last several years as a result of the prolonged irrigation of crops with hazardous metal-laden effluent, both municipal and industrial34. The prime objectives of the current study are to determine the extent of toxicity caused in radish under heavy metal stress and investigate how nano-priming helps alleviate the stress of cadmium. Additionally, also examine the overall effect of selenium nanoparticles on the growth and yield production of the crop under heavy metal stress.

Materials and methods

Green synthesis and characterization of SeNPs

Selenium nanoparticles were prepared using tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) fruit extract. The 10 mL of tomato fruit extract was mixed with 40 mL of 12 mM sodium selenite solution (1:4 v/v). An extract and salt mixture was heated to 75 ℃ for approximately three hours using a rotatory shaker. The mixture was then moved to 37 ℃ for approximately ninety-six hours using an incubator. During incubation, a brick red color changed due to the reduction of Na2SeO3 caused by the plant extract. The presence of a brick-red color was an indication that SeNPs were being synthesized35.

Seed priming and screening test

Certified radish seeds were obtained from Roshan Seed Center, Lahore. Seeds were soaked in 0.5% sodium hypochlorite for five minutes. After that, distilled water was used three times to rinse them. Radish seeds were primed in different concentrations of selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) solutions (15, 30, 45, 60, 75, and 90 mg/L, regarded as Se1, Se2, Se3, Se4, Se5, and Se6), respectively, for 24 h, then air-dried at room temperature. Seeds primed with water were considered the control. Primed seeds were kept over blotting paper for 2 h at room temperature. For all treatments, five seeds were placed among three layers of Whatman filter paper (No. 1) in the petri dishes. Every three days, filter papers were replaced, and to the designated petri plate, 10 mL of distilled water was added. For every treatment, there were three duplicates. Seedlings were cautiously removed from Petri plates after 15 days to examine multiple growth parameters such as germination percentage, shoot length, root length, leaf area, and biomass. Samples of roots and shoots were dried in the oven for 72 h at 80 ℃ to determine the dry weight. The treatments that had the highest growth, biomass, and germination percentage were evaluated, compared, and chosen for a further pot experiment. Concentrations above 45 mg/L (SeNP-4 to SeNP-6) inhibited seedling growth and were therefore excluded from the pot experiment.

Pot experiment

In the pot experiment, soil was contaminated with cadmium chloride (CdCl₂) at a rate of 50 mg Cd per kg of dry soil. Both Cadmium-contaminated and non-contaminated (control) pots were placed in a wire-house for 15 days before sowing the treated radish seeds. Afterward, treated radish seeds were planted per pot in a completely randomized manner. After 55 days (vegetative stage), the plants were harvested and stored in the refrigerator for further evaluation. The final harvest was taken at 95 days after sowing (DAS) to evaluate quality related characteristics of radish.

Measurement of plant growth attributes

All harvested plants were washed thrice with distilled water and then dried on blotting paper. The number of leaves, leaf area, shoot length, root length and biomass of plants was evaluated at vegetative harvest. The fresh and dry weight and the length of roots and shoots were measured along with leaf area, number of leaves, pods, flowers, and number of fruits at final harvest. Plant samples were oven-dried at 70 ℃ for 2 days to assess their dry weights.

Estimation of gas-exchange attributes and net photosynthesis rate

Gas exchange characteristics i.e., stomatal conductance (Gs), transpiration rate (E) and net photosynthesis (A) were estimated during the day (10:00–11:00 a.m.) at 26 ℃ from the uppermost fully stretched leaves with the help a portable infra-red gas analyzer LCA-4 system (ADC, Ltd)36.

Quantification of photosynthetic pigments

Fresh leaves of the plant (0.5 g) were taken right after harvest and were cut into small fragments of 0.5 cm. In pestle and mortar, 10 ml of 80% acetone was added for extraction.

After adding some extract to a test tube, it was covered with aluminum foil. Then aliquots from the extract in a test tube (3 mm) were shifted to cuvettes. The supernatant was observed at 480 nm, 645 nm, and 663 nm with the help of a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800). The amount of chlorophyll “a”, chlorophyll “b” and total chlorophyll was estimated by Chazaux et al.37 and carotenoid content was calculated according to Davies38.

DPPH (2,2- diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) free scavenging activity assay

The Rapahnus sativus L. potential to eliminate radicals that are free with DPPH was calculated via the methodology designed by Chen et al.,39. To make the methanolic plant extract, 1 g foliage of plant was dissolved in 20 ml of methanol. After mixing 1 milliliter of methanolic plant extract with 5 milliliters of a 0. l mM DPPH methanol solution, the resulting solution was allowed to stand in a darkened room for 60 min. Using a spectrophotometer, optical density was determined at a wavelength of 517 nm. Total phenol estimation

Two grams of fresh radish leaves were extracted with 10 milliliters (80%) of methanol, and the resulting mixture was then heated at 65 ℃. Consequently, an appropriate mixture of 1 milliliter of methanolic extract, 5 milliliters of distilled water (DW), and 250ul of the Folin-Ciocalteau reagent (1 N) was mixed. A spectrophotometer was used to determine the mixture’s absorption at 725 nm. The optical density measurement and the Gallic acid standard curve were used to assess the total amount of phenolic compound40.

Ascorbic acid

A portion of 0.5 g of radish leaves was taken to determine the amount of ascorbic acid present in the plant. To prepare 100 ml of extraction solution, 5 g of oxalic acid and 0.75 g of EDTA were dissolved in 100 ml of distilled water (DW). After completely crushing the radish leaves, 20 milliliters of extraction solution were added to the mixture to homogenize it. For fifteen minutes, an Eppendorf tube with one milliliter of extract was centrifuged at 6000 rpm. Then, DCPIP (2,6-dichlorophenol indophenols) was added to the supernatant in a test tube to give it a pink color. Using a spectrophotometer, the absorption of acid was calculated at a wavelength of 520nm41.

Assessment of the total soluble protein content

Using Peterson methodology42, the total soluble protein content was calculated. Utilizing 2 ml of 1 N phosphate buffer, one gram of leaf material was crushed, and the resulting slurry was centrifuged for 15 min at 6000 rpm. Following centrifugation, 0.4 milliliters of supernatant and 2 milliliters of Folin mixture were combined, and the mixture was left for 15 min. After adding (0.5 ml) Folin Ciocalteau reagent to each sample, it was left to stand at room temperature for 45 min. After that, the spectrophotometer was used to determine optical density at a wavelength of 715 nm. The values of soluble proteins were calculated using bovine serum albumin.

The estimation of proline content

To test the proline in plant leaves, a 0.25 g ice-cold sample was taken. Then crush this plant sample with 10 ml of 3% sulpho-salicylic acid. The crushed samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. A mixture of 2 ml of ninhydrin (2.8 g ninhydrin, 48.16 ml 85% phosphoric acid, and 72.8 ml acetic acid) and 2 ml glacial acetic acid. The homogenized mixture was kept in a water bath for 1 h at 100 °C (N.S. Engineering Concern, Model XMTG-9000). After cooling for an hour, the mixture was allowed to stop the reaction. The liquid was vigorously vortexed for 30 s after 4 mL of toluene was added. A chromophore containing toluene was utilized for the aspiration phase. The solution was left on for about thirty minutes at 25 °C. The absorption value was obtained in a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer at 520 nm, and it was further compared to the L-proline standard curve43.

Assessment of Cd accumulation

Radish leaves were cleaned and dried at 65 ℃ for two days in a drying oven (Wiseven, Model WOF-105, Korea). The plant sample was powdered in pestle and mortar so that it can pass through a 60 mesh screen. The 0.5 g of sample was taken then 5 ml of 70% HNO3 and 1.5 ml of 60% HClO4 were added to it. This mixture was heated until the brown fumes disappeared. Then it was cooled and 5 ml of diluted (1:1) HCl was added. This digested material was filtered through Whatman filter paper. Its volume was made up to 25 ml by adding distilled water44. The concentration of Cd was assessed using an Atomic Absorption Spectrometer (XPLOR AA-Dual)45. Calculations were done according to a standard curve of Cd. For the approximation of accumulation factor (AC), Cd concentration present in the shoot was divided by that in the root46. Statistical analysis

The data obtained were investigated using one-way ANOVA via SPSS software. Duncan’s multiple range test was employed for separation of means for significant treatment at p ≤ 0.05. The stated values are means of 5 replications ± SE. To assess relationships between the numerous studied variables, Pearson’s correlation analysis was used. R Studio software was used to calculate the principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation coefficients among the observed variables in Raphanus sativus L.

Results

Characterization of selenium nanoparticles

a) Ultraviolet Visible Spectrophotometry.

Prepared selenium nanoparticles were analyzed using UV-1800 240 V. CAT. No. 206-25400-38 SHIMZDZU Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) at different wavelength spectrums (200 to 800 nm). At 321.72 nm, the absorption peak was the maximum [Figure 1(a)].

b) FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy Analysis).

This analysis was done to find the functional groups associated with SeNPs-. The range of absorbance (4000 to 400 cm-1 was set. Green synthesized SeNPs- showed the nine absorbance peaks at 3269.3 cm-1, 2924.09 cm-1, 2129.41 cm-1, 1637.96 cm-1, 1462.04 cm-1, 1261.45 cm-1, 1091.71 cm-1, 1020.34 cm-1, and 675.09 cm-1. This absorbance was the confirmation of the presence of (N–HO–H), (C–H), (–C ≡ C–), (C═C), (C–H), (C═O), (C–H) respectively. Fourier transform infrared Spectroscopy (R-prestige-21, Shimadzu, Japan) analysis confirmed the presence of Alcoholic groups, alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, carbonyl, Amide, and alkyl halide groups at these absorbance peaks [Figure 1(b)].

c) Scanning Electron Microscopy and Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis.

The Scanning Electron Microscope (Make: FEI, Model: Inspect S 50) was used to examine the nanoparticles’ shape. The average size of the synthesized SeNPs was 30 to 70 nm [Figure 1(b)]. Because of their size variety, the produced SeNPs were easily distinguished in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) studies. The SEM image revealed that the size of the nanoparticles is spherical. The elemental percentage and proportion of selenium in tomato extract-synthesized nanoparticles were determined using the Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX, FEI, Model: Inspect S 50, USA) analysis assay. The results showed that the elemental presence of selenium was 28.02% weight% and 7.09% atomic percentage [Figure 1(d)].

Evaluation of plant growth attributes, biomass and yield of radish

Table 3 shows the variation in the morphological parameters under the influence of Cd stress. Pots with Cd-stressed plants were observed to show lower root length than control seedlings at vegetative (by 20.64%). The application of SeNPs-2 (30 mg/L) enhanced shoot length by 74.17% and 63.41% over control at vegetative and yield stages respectively. Compared to control, Cd affected plants, the root length and shoot length augmented due to SeNPs supplementation. Escalation of SeNPs dosage (SeNPs-2 and SeNPs-3) progressively enhanced the root length and shoot length except for SeNP-1 treatments both in control and under Cd-regimes. Rather this dose (SeNP-1) slightly decreased the root length and shoot length. Treatments with SeNPs-2 + Cd show about 40.55% increases compared to plants grown under only Cd stress. Seedlings of radish treated with Cd stress showed a 42.49% decrease in leaf area, respectively, when compared with the control. A significant increase in the fresh weight was observed when radish seedlings were primed with selenium nanoparticles. Seed priming with SeNP-2 (30 mg/L) increased the fresh and dry weight of the root and shoot by 1.19 fold, 1.77 fold, 2.16 fold, and 2.68 fold, respectively, over the control (Table 4). Cadmium reduced the dry weight of the roots and shoots by 22.14% compared to the control. The SeNPs-2 increased the number of silique by 1.25fold compared to the control plants (Table 5). However, SeNPs-2 (30 mg/L) improved silique fresh and dry weight by 2.48 fold, 1.92 fold, respectively, alleviating Cd stress. Cadmium reduced the fresh and dry weight of the root and shoot respectively (50.82%, 13.01%) compared to control plants at the yield stage (Table 6). The application of Cd (50 mg kg− 1) reduced the decrease in taproot diameter by 48.82% compared to the control.

Compared to the control, plants exposed to Cd showed the greatest reduction in transpiration rate (56%), stomatal conductance (45.45%), and photosynthetic rate (19.61%). SeNPs improved photosynthetic activity; SeNP-2 (30 mg/L) demonstrated an increase in transpiration rate, stomatal conductance, and photosynthetic rate of 0.85fold, 3.31fold, and 1.95fold respectively, compared to the control. This indicates that seed priming was effective in improving transpiration rate, stomatal conductance, and photosynthetic rate as shown in the radar plots (Fig. 2).

Ascorbic acid

Figure 4 (a) summarizes the results for the effects of cadmium on ascorbic acid content of leaves of radish seedlings. Ascorbic acid content increased 26.85% and 13.61% in cadmium as compared to control. The results showed that the ascorbic acid content was significantly enhanced in SeNPs-2 + Cd treatments by 85.53% compared to control plants.

Antioxidant activity (DPPH scavenging activity)

SeNPs, and Cd, stress improved the ability of DPPH to scavenge free radicals. Compared to the control, Cd stress increased DPPH activity by 21.5% respectively. SeNPs-2 + Cd exhibited a maximum increase of 15.53% respectively, over the Cd alone [Figure 4 (b)].

Total proline content

The proline content increased in plants stressed with Cd by 61.12% over the control. The SeNPs also increased the values; SeNPs-2 + Cd increased by 1.31fold over the control. Maximum proline content was revealed by Cd-affected plants supplemented with SeNPs-2. The Cd-affected plants treated with SeNPs-2 exhibited (64.65%) higher free proline content in comparison with SeNPs-2-only treatment [Figure 4 (c)].

Discussion

The element cadmium (Cd) ranks as one of the most lethal heavy metals, and its presence in soils used for agriculture is an enduring issue worldwide. Plants exhibit an array of toxicological symptoms when confronted with Cd stress, such as a lowered process of photosynthesis, chlorosis, stunting of growth, and reduced crop productivity48. The human body’s defense mechanism requires selenium (Se) as an essential cofactor and coenzyme; it serves as a micronutrient that has been helpful to humans as well as animals49. The current research work explored the consequences of cadmium stress on radish and its alleviation through green synthesized selenium nanoparticles.

The fruit juice of Solanum lycopersicum was utilized in the current study to synthesize selenium NPs, using green synthesis method. An ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (UV-1800 Schimadzu) was implemented for determining the absorption at a wavelength range of 200–800 nm, following the production of selenium nanoparticles. There were two distinct peaks detected at absorbance of 321 nm and 300 nm. The maximum peak of absorption appears at 321 nm. Iqbal et al.50 also demonstrated that the highest peak of absorption at UV spectrophotometry study was detected at a range of 300 nm to 600 nm, leading to the confirmation of SeNPs synthesis. The evaluation of SeNPs through Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) showed the existence of (N–H, O–H), (C–H), (–C ≡ C–), (C⅐C), (C–H), (C⅐O), and (C–H), respectively, at an absorbance that ranged from 3269.3 cm− 1, 2924.09 cm− 1, 2129.41 cm− 1, 1637.96 cm− 1, 1462.04 cm− 1, 1261.45 cm− 1, 1091.71 cm− 1, 1020.34 cm− 1, and 675.09 cm− 1. Utilizing FTIR analysis, Iqbal et al.50 examined the availability of multiple functional categories on the surface of SeNPs, which were produced from Solanum lycopersicum plant material or fruit juice, including OH bond alcohols, phenols, N-H amide bond stretches, proteins, amides, such as I and II, C = C, etc. at 1600 cm− 1 to 1700 cm− 1 and 3200 cm− 1 to 330 cm− 1. Our outcomes are in agreement with the results reported by Yang et al.51. The results of SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy) are supported by Anu et al.52. preparation of SeNPs from plant extracts of Diospyros montana, Capsicum annuum, and Allium sativum, which resulted in SeNP sizes of 80 nm, 50–150 nm, and 40–100 nm, respectively. Strong metallic selenium ion absorption maxima at 12.5 KeV were observed by the EDX (Energy Dispersive X-ray Analysis) (Fig. 1). Selenium, oxygen, and carbon are the main components of SeNPs, according to Gunti et al.53 who supported our study and synthesized nanoparticles utilizing fruit extract from Solanum lycopersicum.

Six different concentrations (ranging from 15 mg/L to 90 mg/L) of selenium nanoparticles were utilized to prime the seeds of radish for the present investigation. The findings revealed an increase in the germination of seeds, length of root, length of shoots, and biomass of seedlings up to 45 mg/L. From 45 mg/L to 90 mg/L, there was an impairment in the establishment of seeds and a reduction in the development of seedlings and biomass as the concentration of SeNPs increased. SeNP influences on Hordeum vulgare L. seed germination were reported by Siddiqui et al.54. Selenium nanoparticles exhibit dose-related, substantial impacts on seedling germination and development. García et al.55 also supported the current experiment on radish. In this study selenium was used as priming agent to check the impacts on tomato where selenium nanoparticles improved the seedling growth at optimum concentration (15 ppm).

When compared to the control plants, the application of green synthesized SeNPs (30 mg/L) strengthens the height of the plant, length of shoot, length of root and fresh weight of root, root dry weight, leaf area, leaf number, and length as well as shoot fresh weight and shoot dry weight. According to Ikram et al.57 wheat plants exposed to drought stress showed an increase in biomass production, length of roots, and shoots are by selenium nanoparticles application. Our results are in agreement with the findings of Gowayed57 who revealed that Cd accumulation has deleterious effects on the seed germination, growth, and yield of plants by inducing various physiological and biochemical changes. A study on Brassica chinensis L. indicates the impact of SeNPs on its growth under Cd stress87. According to Huybrechts et al.58, Cd toxicity affects seed germination by reducing the seed imbibition process, inhibiting the mobilization of storage food, altering cellular osmoregulation, and disrupting proteolytic enzymes. A study on wheat also suggested that seed priming with silicon nanoparticles improved the overall growth and yield of plants under Cd stress which also supported the current study on radish22.

Selenium nanoparticles augmented the concentration of photosynthetic material in plants under Cd stress. Further investigation reveals that selenium (Se) has an advantageous influence on the reversal of the breakdown of chlorophyll caused by cadmium59,60. The results we obtained indicated that Se promoted the process of producing the amount of chlorophyll (Fig. 3). According to Silva et al.61 selenium provides stability to antenna complexes, hence strengthening their photosynthetic component. Selenium enhances the concentration of starch that accumulated in the chloroplasts of stressed mustard (Brassica juncea L.) plants, as reported by Ahmad et al.33. The results of current investigation are consistent with those of Quiterio-Gutiérrez et al.62. In addition to changing the structure of the chloroplast and chloroplast membrane, cadmium stress hinders the crucial Calvin cycle enzymes. This lessens the plant’s chlorophyll content, which eventually prohibits it from growing63. Nevertheless, absorption, translocation, and Cd accumulation in plants are influenced by the participation of many transport channels and metabolic reactions64. Selenium maintains calcium homeostasis and disulfide bond formation, which lessens the concentration of Cd in plants, according to Wu et al.65. The opening and closing of stomata controls the rate of transpiration. Plants cultivated under Cd stress showed reduced transpiration (Fig. 2) and photosynthetic rate (Fig. 2) due to reduced stomatal conduction. These results are consistent with a study done on lettuce where zinc nanoparticles were used as priming agents for alleviation of Cd-stress66. Another study on Brassica napus showed similar results where selenium nanoparticles improved the gaseous exchange attributes88. Under conditions of stress, stomatal conductance and photosynthetic rate reduced67. Similar to our findings, Giannakoula et al.68 observed that plants under heavy metal stress had a lower net photosynthetic rate. Furthermore, in accordance with the conclusions of Abbas et al.69.

In current study, the proline content in radish was increased under Cd stress. The possible underlying mechanism involves the disturbance of glutamyl kinase (first enzyme involved in the proline synthesis pathway), which is triggered by the application of cadmium and selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs). Its ability to function is concentration-dependent70,71. Se-NPs and Cd both enhance plants’ capacity to scavenge free radicals, according to Huang et al.72. The external intake of Se activates plants’ antioxidant system against Cd stress, claim Guo et al.73. According to Zhou et al.74, the treatment of Se-NPs diminishes ROS levels via strengthening the antioxidant activity of wheat plants under Cd stress. Similar study was conducted by Emamverdian et al.75 in which TiO2 NPs have been used for alleviation of heavy metal stress in bamboo forest. The inert solutes, also known as inactive metabolites, play a crucial role in preventing osmotic stress brought on by excessive heavy metal stress and salinity76. For this reason, they are called osmolytes. According to studies, plants under Cd stress exhibit higher proline accumulation77. Results showed that the radish plant treated with SeNPs-2 + Cd had the highest proline content (78.98 mg g-1; Fig. 4), while plants treated with SeNPs-1 and SeNPs-3 had the lowest proline content.

Phenolic can chelate heavy metals in addition to being efficient at scavenging or neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), reducing oxidative damage to proteins, DNA, and lipids, and ultimately reducing oxidative damage to cells and their constituent parts78. Consequently, phenolic derived from cells have the potential to scavenge free radicals. Radish seeds primed with SeNPs generally demonstrated a substantial favorable effect on the buildup of phenolic content, comparable to the findings of Ahmad et al.79.

Antioxidant activity is determined by DPPH free radical scavenging activity. Our findings coincide with Osama et al. findings80. Certain secondary metabolism products that have antioxidant abilities are generated by plants in response to stress. Free radicals that exist in cells of plants can be neutralized by several of these metabolites. It is widely acknowledged that under stressful circumstances, DPPH forms a persistent complex by emitting H atoms, and the color intensity of the complex signifies its ability to eliminate the reactive oxygen species in plants. The results in Fig. 4 show that DPPH scavenging activity increased in SeNPs-primed radish seedlings, indicating enhanced antioxidant potential. A study on Brassica napus L. indicates that selenium and its nanoparticles have the potential to alleviate the Cd stress86.

Together with other components like ascorbate peroxidase, monodehydro ascorbate reductase, dehydro ascorbate reductase, and glutathione reductase, ascorbic acid, also known as vitamin C, is a non-enzymatic antioxidant molecule with a high capacity for self-recycling. It is involved in ROS scavenging, either directly stabilizing ROS via the ascorbate–glutathione cycle or as an enzymatic cofactor81. According to our findings, plants under Cd stress have higher ascorbic contents. They adversely affect the size and density of stomata in epidermal cells, which slows transpiration and increases the amount of CO2 present between cells in leaves36,82.

Selenium nanoparticles mitigate the effect of Cd and reduce its uptake by plants, creating a high metal tolerance index (MTI), shown in Table 7; this might affect the ATPase or cation diffusion facilitators83. Seed primed with SeNPs-2 showed a reduced accumulation coefficient suggesting reduced uptake of Cd stress in seedlings. Furthermore, the application of selenium nanoparticles caused increased root vacuoles which reduced the translocation of Cd content in shoots from root cells78. Our findings coincide with a study done on bamboo plants to increase the tolerance of heavy metal stress84.

Conclusion

The current study indicated that seed priming with selenium nanoparticles improved the overall growth and development of Raphanus sativus L. by alleviating the adverse effects of cadmium (Cd) stress. The mitigation of Cd induced phytotoxicity was reduced by selenium nanoparticles, especially at a concentration of 30 mg/L, as compared to other concentrations (15 mg/L and 45 mg/L ). The morpho-physio-biochemical attributes of radish were enhanced by application of SeNPs against Cd-stress. In the current study, a decrease in these parameters was observed when SeNPs were applied at a higher concentration (45 mg L− 1). The results obtained suggest that seed priming with SeNPs is an expedient strategy for agricultural production as it reduces oxidative damage, boosts antioxidative systems, and promotes plant development in response to heavy metal (Cd) stress. The great potential of SeNPs for achieving sustainable crop production in cadmium-stressed soil was demonstrated in the current study. However, in order to achieve safe intake of these vegetables, future research is required to assess and screen other edible root and leafy vegetables for the presence of heavy metals.
