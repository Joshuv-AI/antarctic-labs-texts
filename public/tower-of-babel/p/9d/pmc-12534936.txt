Modeling the incidence rate of hospital re-admissions within 30 days and associated factors among patients with advanced HIV disease in Uganda: comparison of the performance of binary logistic and count regression models
Authors: Mary Mbuliro, Barbara Eva Kirunda, Douglas Joseph Musimbaggo, Nazarius Mbona Tumwesigye, David Guwatudde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdvanced HIV Disease (AHD) remains a major driver of HIV-related morbidity, readmissions, and mortality, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa. AHD affects People Living with HIV (PLHIV) with CD4 < 200 cells/mm³ and/or WHO stage 3 or 4, or all children under five years living with HIV. Globally, approximately 18% of AHD patients are readmitted within 30 days post-discharge; rates in Sub-Saharan Africa have been found to reach up to 52.9%. Understanding the incidence rate of 30-day readmissions is critical to improving quality of care and reducing system burden.MethodsThe study used retrospective cohort study design using secondary data of AHD patients aged ≥ 12 years admitted at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital (KNRH) between March 2020 to July 2022. Patients discharged alive were included, excluding in-hospital deaths. These associations remained consistent across the entire sample of AHD patients.ConclusionYounger age (12–24 years), candidiasis, and ≥ 3 comorbidities were associated with higher risk of 30-day readmission, among AHD patients active on ART and the entire sample of AHD patients. Introduction

Globally, the management of Advanced HIV Disease (AHD) remains a significant public health challenge. AHD is defined by Persons living with HIV (PLHIV) with CD4 cell count of less than 200 cells/mm³ and/or World Health Organization (WHO) stages 3 or 4 in adults and adolescents, as well as all children under five years old living with HIV [1]. AHD is also known to be associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and hospital readmissions [1–3]. Despite significant progress in HIV treatment, including widespread use of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and global initiatives like the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), AHD continues to present challenges, with substantial hospital readmission rates reported worldwide [4–6].

Studies have shown that globally, approximately 18% of AHD patients experience readmissions after hospital discharge [7]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, AHD-associated hospital readmissions persist at alarmingly high rates, with some studies reporting rates as high as 52.9% [7]. In Uganda, AHD remains prevalent, with reports indicating that 20% of newly enrolled HIV patients present with AHD, and 20% of unsuppressed PLHIV return to care with AHD [8]. Furthermore, 40% of newly diagnosed HIV patients in Uganda are found to have AHD [8]. To address these concerns, Uganda introduced the AHD Package in 2020, which includes measures such as point-of-care CD4 tests, healthcare provider training, and community engagement initiatives aimed at improving AHD management and reducing readmissions [8]. However, existing studies in Uganda have focused primarily on post-discharge mortality among AHD patients [9–11], leaving a gap in understanding the incidence rates and causes of readmissions, particularly among PLHIV with AHD. Despite the burden of readmissions on the healthcare system, there is limited data on the incidence of 30-day hospital readmissions for AHD patients in Uganda [9–11]. The 30-day post-discharge period is crucial for assessing healthcare quality and addressing complications that require immediate attention [12, 13]. Additionally, the 30-day period was selected since findings from a systematic review reported that the risk of readmission was higher within the 30-day time frame compared to other follow up time points, for people living with HIV especially those who had been discharged against medical advice from one of the studies [7].

Statistical modeling plays an essential role in identifying factors that predict readmissions [14]. Binary logistic regression is commonly used in existing studies to evaluate the relationship between predictor variables (either categorical or continuous) and an outcome which is binary (dichotomous) [14–16]. The dichotomous categorization of outcome variables using binary logistic regression overlooks the continuous nature of incidence rates, neglecting the magnitude and subtle variations in the intensity or severity of events. Consequently, the analysis may lose granularity and precision [17]. In contrast count regression models such as Poisson and negative binomial offer advantages in handling over-dispersed data and variations in observation time [18, 19]. Count regression models predict the relationship between explanatory variables and count outcomes while incorporating an offset variable to adjust for varying lengths of exposure or observation time and thus capturing differences in event intensity in terms of counts and exposure time [18]. The binary logistic regression and count regression models (with an offset variable) were applied to assess their relative performance in identifying factors associated with 30-day readmissions.

This study aimed to determine the incidence rate of hospital readmissions within 30 days, identify associated risk factors among AHD patients in Uganda, and compare the performance of binary logistic and count regression models to model the data.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study design was used to determine the 30-day hospital readmission rates among AHD patients, stratified by antiretroviral therapy (ART) status. This design was appropriate and cost effective for estimating incidence rates given that secondary data was utilized.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital (KNRH), a government-funded, public tertiary hospital located in Makindye division, Kampala, Uganda. With a 200-bed capacity, KNRH provides comprehensive medical and surgical services, including HIV care and treatment, for individuals aged 12 years and older [20]. The hospital handles approximately 30,000 outpatient visits and 10,000 inpatient admissions annually. As a national referral facility, it offers specialized services such as internal medicine, laboratory and diagnostic services, general surgery, and renal replacement therapy. KNRH also functions as a teaching and research center [20]. In January 2020, KNRH established an AHD program that provides specialized ART care, treatment for opportunistic infections and post-discharge follow-up for AHD patients [8, 20].

Study population and sample size determination

The study population included HIV-positive patients aged 12 years and above, with a confirmed AHD diagnosis, defined by a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm³ and/or WHO stage 3 or 4, admitted at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital between March 2020 and July 2022. Only patients who were enrolled in the AHD program and discharged alive were included. Those who died during hospitalization were excluded from the study.

In this study, the sample was stratified based on ART status categorized as ART naive, ART active, and ART defaulter. ART naive individuals were defined as HIV positive patients who had never initiated antiretroviral therapy at the time of admission [21, 22]. ART defaulters were defined as patients who returned for their clinical visit or medication pick up after more than 30 days from the date of their last appointment, without documentation of death or transfer [22]. ART active patients were those retained in care and did not miss their scheduled appointment by greater than 30 days, nor recorded as deceased or transferred to other health facility [22]. This stratification was performed because previous research has demonstrated that ART status significantly impacts hospitalization rates. Specifically, ART-active patients have previously been reported to be less likely to experience readmissions, whereas ART-defaulters are at higher risk for hospitalizations [23]. These strata were anticipated to have differential risks for readmission within 30 days post-discharge [18, 24].

Data source and collection

Secondary data was extracted from medical records of patients admitted with AHD at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital (KNRH) between March 2020 and July 2022. Data was extracted from electronic medical records and ward registers by trained research staff using a data abstraction tool.

Measurement of variables

The outcome variable in this study was hospital readmission within 30 days post-discharge among patients with advanced HIV disease (AHD). This outcome was recorded in two ways based on the regression model to be applied.

i)Binary Logistic Regression Model: The outcome was recorded as a binary variable. Readmission within 30 days post-discharge for each patient was coded as 0 (“No”), if the patient was not readmitted within 30 days after hospital discharge or 1 (“Yes”), if the patient was readmitted at least once within 30 days after hospital discharge.

Binary Logistic Regression Model: The outcome was recorded as a binary variable. Readmission within 30 days post-discharge for each patient was coded as 0 (“No”), if the patient was not readmitted within 30 days after hospital discharge or 1 (“Yes”), if the patient was readmitted at least once within 30 days after hospital discharge.

ii)Count Models (Poisson and Negative Binomial Regression): The outcome was recorded as a count of readmissions per patient, with a corresponding offset variable to account for varying person time of observation within the 30-day follow-up period. The numerator was the total number of readmissions observed during the 30-day follow-up period. The denominator was the total person-time of observation, summed for all patients. For each patient, person-time was calculated from the date of discharge to the date of readmission, or up to 30 days if no readmission occurred.

The incidence rate of 30-day readmission was calculated as the total number of readmissions divided by the total person-time of observation and was expressed per 100 person months of observation [29].

Additionally, the dataset comprised the following independent variables: patient demographics, where patient sex was recorded as Male or Female. Age was recorded as age in completed years. The district of residence was either Kampala District, Wakiso District, or Other District.

HIV-related clinical variables included ART status, recorded as ART naive, ART defaulter, or Active on ART. The most recent CD4 cell count was recorded as CD4 count in cells/µL. The most recent HIV viral load was recorded as HIV viral load in copies/ml.

Variables on HIV-related illnesses or opportunistic infections were recorded as binary responses: 1 = Yes, indicating presence of the specific HIV-related illness or opportunistic infection, and 0 = No, indicating absence. These included tuberculosis, cryptococcal disease, candidiasis, toxoplasmosis, and Kaposi sarcoma.

Similarly, comorbidities such as sepsis, cardiovascular disease, anemia, kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, liver disease, severe malnutrition, COVID-19, chronic lung disease, cancers, and mental illness were recorded as binary responses: 1 = Yes, indicating presence of the specific comorbidity, and 0 = No, indicating absence of the specific comorbidity.

Data management and analysis

The data was imported into Stata version 15 for data management and cleaning. Data quality checks included de-duplication using unique patient identifiers, range checks (e.g., age), and validation of inconsistencies or missing values by cross-referencing patient charts and hospital registers. Data formatting was harmonized, with string variables converted to numeric format, and variables that were dates validated. The sample was adjusted for missingness below 11%, but the CD4 cell count variable had 36% missing data due to stockouts of CD4 count cartridges on some admission days. Given the assumption of missing completely at random (MCAR) and the fact that missing data was below 40%, single imputation was applied [30].Single imputation was applied using the median CD4 values for each ART stratum. HIV viral load had high missingness of 96.6% and was dropped from the analysis [31].

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables using frequencies and proportions. To account for unequal sampling probabilities across ART status strata, sampling weights were constructed and applied for the regression analysis [24]. These weights were calculated as the inverse of the probability of selection for each ART stratum, based on the proportion of sampled versus total AHD patients within each ART category. Applying these weights ensured that sample estimates were more representative and unbiased [32].

Model performance for the binary logistic, Poisson, and negative binomial regression models was compared using the Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC). The models were all were fitted to the same clinical outcome (30-day readmission), dataset, and predictors using maximum likelihood estimation, making BIC an appropriate metric for comparison [15, 33]. Lower BIC values indicated better model fit. This approach is supported in the literature, where BIC has been used to compare non-nested models applied to the same data [15, 33]. Bi-variable analysis was conducted to determine the probable crude association between each independent variable and the outcome variable. Multicollinearity was assessed using the correlation matrix. For any pair of variables with a correlation coefficient, r > 0.4, the variable with greater biological plausibility was retained. The binary logistic multivariable regression modelling was used both for the stratified samples by ART status and the pooled dataset to identify factors associated with 30-day readmission. The final models were built using backward stepwise selection. Backward elimination was used for variable selection in the multivariate analysis as it starts with a full model and removes non-significant variables, ensuring a more parsimonious model that controls for confounding [34]. The level of significance was set at the 5% significance level and 95% confidence intervals were applied. Model diagnostics included the Pearson chi-square goodness-of-fit test, with a p-value greater than 0.05 indicating a good fit, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, where an area under the curve (AUC) value greater than 0.5 was considered indicative of model discrimination [14].

Comparison of models for factors associated with hospital re-admission among patients with AHD

Results showed that the binary logistic regression had a better performance in modelling the data, with a smaller BIC value (1725.13) compared to the negative binomial regression model (BIC = 3369.46) and the Poisson regression model (BIC = 7218.29). The data was therefore modelled using the binary logistic regression model.

Factors associated with hospital readmission within 30 days post discharge among patients with AHD and final model diagnostics

In the bi-variable analysis, among patients active on ART, the probable predictors at p-value < 0.25 included age group, candidiasis, and the number of comorbidities. For patients who defaulted on ART, the probable predictors of readmission were age group, tuberculosis and cryptococcal disease. Similarly, among patients who were ART naive, the probable predictors were age group, tuberculosis and the number of comorbidities. For all AHD patients, the probable predictors were age group, hospitalization discharge outcome, candidiasis, and the number of comorbidities.

Testing for interaction by level of hospitalization discharge outcome

The AHD patients` discharge outcome (formally discharged, discharged against medical advice, or transferred out) was conceptualized to be an effect modifier for the association between the patients’ ART status and readmission within 30 days post-discharge. The patient outcome of transferred out antagonized the effect between the relationship of the patients’ ART status and readmission within 30 days post-discharge [AOR: 0.82, 95%CI: 0.04–16.12], however, the relationship was not statistically significant, thus there was no statistically significant interaction between the patients’ outcome at discharge and the ART status. This meant that the AHD patients’ discharge outcome neither antagonized nor synergized the relationship between the patients’ ART status and incidence of readmission within 30 days post-discharge for patients with AHD, thus the model was not adjusted for any interactions.

Predictors of 30-day readmission

From Table 3, multivariable binary logistic regression analysis identified various significant predictors of 30-day readmission among patients with Advanced HIV Disease (AHD). Regarding the overall regression analysis for all AHD patients, patients aged 25–39 years were 78% less likely to be readmitted within 30 days post discharge (aOR = 0.22; 95% CI: 0.08–0.58), while patients 40–49 years had 83% lower odds of being readmitted within 30 days post discharge (aOR = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.06–0.53) compared to those aged 12–24 years of age. Similarly, patients aged 50 years and older had 71% lower odds of being readmitted within 30 days post discharge, (aOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.09–0.91) compared to those aged 12–24 years. The presence of candidiasis among patients increased the likelihood of 30-day readmission by 2.72 times compared to those without candidiasis (aOR = 2.72; 95% CI: 1.12–6.59). Additionally, patients with three or more comorbidities were 4.56 times more likely to be readmitted compared to those without comorbidities after adjusting for other variables (aOR = 4.56; 95% CI: 1.42–14.68).

Among patients who were active on ART, age was a significant factor associated with the likelihood of readmission. Specifically, patients aged 25–39 years had 88% lower odds of readmission (aOR = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.03–0.43), those aged 40–49 years had 92% lower odds (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.02–0.37), and those aged 50 years and older had 86% lower odds of being readmitted (aOR = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.03–0.63), compared to those aged 12–24 years.

The presence of candidiasis was associated with 3.65 times increase in the odds of readmission (aOR = 3.65; 95% CI: 1.02–13.04), compared to patients without candidiasis. Patients with three or more comorbidities had 7.02 times odds of being readmitted (aOR = 7.02; 95% CI: 1.32–37.48), compared to those without comorbidities.

For patients who had defaulted on ART or were ART-naive, none of the assessed variables were statistically significant predictors of readmission, based on 95% confidence intervals.

Discussion

This study assessed 30-day hospital readmission among patients with Advanced HIV Disease (AHD) at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital (KNRH), stratified by ART status. The overall incidence of readmission was relatively low, at 6.8 per 100 person-months of observation, with the highest rate among patients active on ART i.e., 7.3 (95% C.I: 7.2–7.5) per 100 months of observation. The binary logistic regression model demonstrated a better fit than count regression models, based on lower BIC values, likely due to the rarity of the outcome. Stratified analysis showed that among ART-active patients, factors significantly associated with readmission included younger age (12–24 years), presence of candidiasis, and ≥ 3 comorbidities which were consistent with the analysis of all AHD patients. However, no significant factors were identified for the patients in the ART defaulter or ART naive strata.

The incidence of 30-day readmission among AHD patients was 6.8 (95% CI: 6.7–6.9) per 100 person-months, with the highest rate observed in ART-active patients (7.3 per 100 person-months). The incidence rates reported in this study could be attributed to partnerships between KNRH and the Non-Government organization which provides enhanced in-patient care and post discharge follow-up such as screening and treatment of opportunistic infections. These services may have improved care continuity and reduced the likelihood of early readmission. Notably, the higher readmission rate among ART-active patients may be attributed to a higher prevalence of comorbidities (81%) reported in this group. While ART defaulters and ART-naive patients had lower readmission rates (5.9 and 5.7 per 100 person-months, respectively), this could reflect delayed care-seeking or underdiagnosis of complications.

The overall incidence rate reported is lower than the readmission rates reported in studies from Brazil (14%) [35], South Africa (53%) [16]and Zambia (23.8%) [27], which were conducted in similar public hospital settings. Similar findings were reported elsewhere [35], that showed increased readmission risk among long-term ART users due to drug resistance and poor adherence. In contrast, another study [27] reported higher readmission rates among ART-naive patients, a finding that may be confounded by misclassification due to unrecorded ART use in that study. These findings underscore the importance of post-discharge care and differentiated service delivery models tailored to ART-experienced patients with high comorbidity burden.

Additionally, the binary logistic regression model outperformed negative binomial and Poisson models, as indicated by lower Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values. The better performance of the binary logistic regression model is attributed to the rarity of the outcome (incidence < 10%) as was the case in this study, for which binary logistic regression provides more stable and interpretable estimates [27].

The binary logistic model converged completely, offered interpretable odds ratios, and had the lowest BIC reflecting parsimony and stability. While the pooled dataset for the Negative Binomial model failed to converge for some of the estimates. The Negative Binomial and Poisson regression models also produced extremely small incidence rate ratios, likely due to sparse event counts. These issues compromised the credibility of count models as primary results (Results in supplementary Tables 4 and 5). This finding contrasts with results from another study [15], that analyzed adverse drug reaction data and found that the zero-inflated negative binomial regression model outperformed binary logistic regression due to the high incidence of events (71%). In such scenarios, odds ratios may exaggerate the relative risk, making count models more appropriate. Thus, model selection should be context-specific and based on outcome distribution and data sparsity.

Regarding factors associated with readmission, overall, for the pooled dataset of all AHD patients and those active on ART, those aged 12–24 years had higher odds of readmission compared to older age groups. This could be due to stigma-related barriers to adherence, low health literacy, and inadequate transition support from pediatric to adult care. These results align with a U.S.-based study [36]which found a higher readmission risk among adolescents and young adults. However, studies from Malawi and Zambia found no significant age differences in readmission risk [27, 37].Public health strategies should include adolescent-friendly services, including tailored education, psychosocial support, and structured transition programs, to improve outcomes in this vulnerable group.

Additionally, candidiasis was significantly associated with increased odds of readmission among ART-active patients and all AHD patients overall. This opportunistic infection likely reflects poor immune reconstitution, adherence challenges, or treatment failure. Although some studies [38], did not find candidiasis to be a statistically significant predictor of readmission, our stratified analysis provided a more focused assessment by ART status which the other studies did not stratify by. This finding suggests that improved screening and management of opportunistic infections at discharge may reduce avoidable readmissions and improve quality of care.

The presence of three or more comorbidities was a strong predictor of readmission among all AHD patients and those active on ART. HIV-positive individuals with coexisting chronic conditions such as renal, hepatic, or pulmonary diseases face complex treatment needs and increased vulnerability to complications which may require readmission. This aligns with findings from studies in Italy [39], Portugal [40]and the United States of America [36], where comorbidity burden was associated with higher hospital admission among PLHIV. Multidisciplinary management and individualized care planning should be prioritized for AHD patients with multiple comorbidities to reduce readmission risk.

The study however had some limitations. CD4 cell count data were missing for 36% of participants, primarily due to hospital cartridge stockouts. To address this, single imputation using the median CD4 value within ART strata was performed under the assumption of data being missing completely at random (MCAR). This approach preserved statistical power and reduced potential bias, allowing inclusion of a clinically important variable without excluding a large portion of the sample. In contrast, viral load data were missing in over 96% of cases; including it would have likely introduced significant bias and reduced estimate reliability, so it was excluded from the analysis. Additionally, the secondary dataset lacked key socio-demographic variables such as education level, income, and marital status, which could not be adjusted for. However, the analysis controlled key clinical and demographic predictors to mitigate confounding. To enhance comparability, the study adopted a 30-day post-discharge readmission period, consistent with several prior studies.

Conclusion

The study revealed the differences in readmission rates among patients with Advanced HIV Disease (AHD) by their ART status. Additionally, the binary logistic regression performed better than the count models in modeling the data. The AHD patients of younger age groups (12–24 years), AHD patients diagnosed with candidiasis, and/or 3 or more comorbidities were associated with higher odds of readmission within 30 days post-discharge, among all the AHD patients and similarly, among those active on ART.

Recommendations

The findings suggest several key actions to reduce 30-day readmissions in patients with Advanced HIV Disease (AHD). Younger patients (12–24 years) were at a higher risk of readmission, highlighting the need for targeted support from adolescent-friendly HIV care services, including age-appropriate discharge planning and adherence counseling. Candidiasis was strongly linked to readmission, so infectious disease teams should ensure routine screening and treatment of this condition at discharge. For patients with three or more comorbidities, a more integrated approach to managing these conditions during hospitalization and follow-up would involve multidisciplinary teams (including clinicians, nutritionists, and social workers) to improve long-term outcomes. Lastly, hospitals like KNRH could benefit from using predictive models to identify high-risk patients, with the hospital’s data enabling implementation and tailoring of follow-up care accordingly.
