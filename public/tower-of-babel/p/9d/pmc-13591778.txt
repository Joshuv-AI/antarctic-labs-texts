Personalizing post-stroke symptom management: integrating network analysis and in silico intervention to identify subgroup-specific targets
Authors: Yiqing Zhang, Aizhen Wang, Qun Fang, Jingjing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide. Survivors often experience multiple co-occurring symptoms that interact in complex ways. These interactions create significant symptom burden and reduce quality of life. However, substantial heterogeneity exists in how patients experience post-stroke symptoms. Traditional approaches often treat patients as a homogeneous profile. They also focus on individual symptoms in isolation. This limits the development of tailored symptom management strategies.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify distinct symptom burden based on patients’ reported symptom experiences, compare their symptom network structures, and simulate potential intervention targets using computational modeling.MethodsA total of 451 stroke survivors were recruited from three hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China. Symptoms were assessed using the Stroke Symptom Experience Scale. Exploratory factor analysis was used to identify symptom domains. Latent profile analysis was applied to categorize patients based on these domains. Network analysis with EBICglasso regularization was employed to estimate symptom networks for the full sample and each identified profile. The stability of centrality indices was assessed via the case-dropping bootstrap, and network structures were compared using a Network Comparison Test. Finally, the NodeIdentifyR algorithm was used to simulate the potential impact of aggravating and alleviating interventions on each symptom. The goal was to generate hypotheses about potential high-risk and high-benefit targets for future study.ResultsFour symptom domains were identified: Motor Dysfunction, Emotional Disorders, Cognitive and Language Disorders, and Pain and Foot Morphological Abnormalities. Latent profile analysis revealed two distinct patient profiles based on symptom experience: a low symptom burden profile (Profile 1, 65.4%) and a high symptom burden profile (Profile 2, 34.6%). In the overall network, limited limb mobility (S1), impaired memory (S9), and impaired attention (S10) showed the highest centrality. The two profiles exhibited significantly different network structures (M = 0.285, p = 0.026). Core symptoms shifted from motor and self-care deficits (S1, S19) in Profile 1 to cognitive symptoms (S9, S10) in Profile 2.In simulated interventions, targeting central symptoms such as S1 was associated with the greatest predicted alleviating impact. This intervention corresponded to a reduction in overall burden by 11.8% in the model. However, the simulations also suggested a dissociation between centrality and risk. In Profile 1, worsening a non-central symptom (foot varus, S6) was predicted to be associated with an elevation in total burden by 26.2%. This effect size was larger than that of most central symptoms.ConclusionIn this cohort of 451 stroke survivors, symptom experience was not homogeneous. Patients could be grouped into low and high symptom burden profiles with different network structures. Simulation analyses showed that targeting high-centrality symptoms was associated with the largest predicted burden reductions, while worsening a non-central symptom (foot varus, S6) in the low-burden subgroup was predicted to increase burden by 26.2%. A sensitivity analysis confirmed that motor, cognitive, and emotional symptom domains play important roles, though the precise ranking of individual symptoms should be interpreted with caution. We introduce a benefit–risk framework as a hypothesis-generating tool for symptom management. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these predictions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05119-6.Keywords: Stroke, Symptom Network, Latent Profile Analysis, Symptom Experience Heterogeneity, Network Intervention, Patient-Reported Outcomes

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. According to statistics, globally, there are approximately 12.2 million new stroke cases each year, with the total number of stroke survivors reaching 101 million [1]. China bears a significant portion of this burden, reporting 3.94 million new stroke cases annually, which accounts for nearly one-third of the global total [2]. Survivors often face a complex symptom burden, including motor dysfunction, sensory abnormalities, cognitive deficits, emotional disorders, fatigue, and pain [3, 4]. Effectively managing this symptom burden is paramount to improving long-term functional recovery and quality of life.

Although heterogeneity in post-stroke symptom profile is well-documented, traditional classification methods often fail to identify clinically meaningful subgroups that share distinct symptom patterns and intervention needs [3, 5]. Conventional research frequently treats patients as a homogeneous population or focuses on isolated symptoms, potentially overlooking the synergistic and cascading effects within symptom networks [6]. Although network analysis has emerged as a powerful tool to map the interrelationships among symptoms and identify central “hub” symptoms [7, 8], a critical gap remains. Most existing network studies rely on group-average analyses or assume that a single network structure applies across all patients, thereby lacking the granularity to inform personalized management strategies for specific patient subgroups.

Crucially, there is a need to translate static network topology into dynamic, testable intervention hypotheses. Computational simulation serves as a vital theoretical bridge from static network description to clinical decision-making, enabling the a priori comparison of distinct intervention strategies before resource-intensive trials are launched. It allows researchers to explore hypothetical perturbations within an associative network, predicting cascading effects following symptom alleviation or aggravation [9]. The NodeIdentifyR algorithm advances this field by systematically simulating node state changes, offering a novel lens to evaluate the “benefit-risk” profile of potential interventions [10, 11].

To address the aforementioned gaps, this study aims to shift the focus from merely identifying heterogeneity to defining the distinct “intervention logic” based on patient-reported symptom experiences. We integrate exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify homogeneous subgroups. Following this, we employ EBICglasso regularization to estimate stable network structures for each subgroup. Finally, we apply the NodeIdentifyR algorithm to conduct in silico simulations, evaluating how perturbations in specific symptoms may influence the network and relate to changes in overall symptom burden.

Methods

Study design and sample

This study adopted a cross-sectional design focusing specifically on the heterogeneity of patient-reported symptom experiences. We examined the variability in how stroke survivors subjectively perceive and report their post-stroke symptoms, independent of neuroanatomical lesion patterns. Within this framework, the study employed a multi-center design utilizing a non-probability convenience sampling strategy. The study setting encompassed the rehabilitation departments of three hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China. To enhance sample diversity, the sites were systematically selected to represent different healthcare service models and patient populations: two tertiary public hospitals (one provincial and one municipal) serving broad urban and rural catchment areas, and one private rehabilitation hospital. Data collection was conducted over a 14-month period from October 20, 2024, to December 1, 2025. The final analytic sample comprised 451 hospitalized stroke survivors.

According to methodological recommendations for network analysis, stable estimation of a continuous-variable network typically requires approximately 20–50 observations per node. This study included 19 symptom nodes, corresponding to a theoretical sample size range of 380–950 participants. The final analytical sample consisted of 451 stroke survivors, which falls within the recommended range and meets the requirements for network estimation. The reliability of the network was further evaluated using posterior stability analyses, including a case-dropping bootstrap (correlation stability coefficient, CS > 0.75) and bootstrapped confidence intervals for edge weights.

Participant selection protocol

Inclusion parameters

(1) Diagnostic confirmation: Clinical diagnosis of stroke according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Major Cerebrovascular Diseases (2019); (2) Age eligibility: Participants aged 18 years or older; (3) Clinical stability: Absence of intensive care transfer, emergency interventions (e.g., reintubation or vasopressor support), and observed neurological deterioration within 72 h prior to enrollment; (4) Informed consent: Voluntarily provided written informed consent.

Exclusion parameters

(1) Severe comorbidity: Diagnosis of severe systemic diseases (e.g., metastatic cancer); (2) Impaired consciousness: Altered consciousness at the time of assessment; (3) Consent withdrawal: Voluntary withdrawal of consent during the study period.

Participant enrollment and data collection

A standardized implementation framework was established across the three participating hospitals. Site-specific research personnel completed a unified training program covering study protocols, ethical guidelines, and standardized administration procedures.

Data were collected through structured paper-based questionnaires administered face-to-face during hospitalization, preceded by standardized explanation of study objectives and written informed consent. A multi-stage quality assurance protocol was implemented: (1) immediate on-site verification for completeness and clarity; (2) dual independent data entry with cross-verification against original questionnaires; and (3) random audit of 10% of records by a senior researcher. Prior to formal use, the questionnaire was piloted with 30 participants and refined for improved clarity and feasibility.

Instrument

General Information Questionnaire: A self-designed General Information Questionnaire was used to collect basic demographic and clinical characteristics. It comprised two sections: (1) demographic background, including age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, occupation, monthly household income, and the primary payment method for medical costs; (2) disease-related information, such as stroke type, time since diagnosis. As this questionnaire was developed specifically for the present study, a copy is provided as Supplementary File 1.

In addition, clinical indicators were extracted from medical records: stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [12], and functional dependency was classified based on Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scores using the Barthel Index [13].

The Stroke Symptom Experience Scale (SSES), developed by Shi et al. [14], was used to assess symptom burden in stroke survivors. The scale comprises 19 symptoms (coded as S1 to S19), covering physical, psychological, and functional dimensions: limited limb mobility (S1), limb weakness (S2), limb pain (S3), shoulder pain (S4), foot drop (S5), foot varus (S6), poor limb coordination (S7), loss of body balance (S8), impaired memory (S9), impaired attention (S10), delayed responses (S11), slurred speech (S12), easily agitated (S13), feeling down (S14), hopelessness about the future (S15), loss of interest in surroundings (S16), distress over inability to do desired activities (S17), fatigue (S18), and decline in self-care ability (S19).

The SSES employs a two-stage assessment structure. First, participants indicate whether each symptom is present (yes/no). For each symptom reported as present, three dimensions are subsequently rated: (1) frequency (1 = rarely to 4 = almost constantly), (2) intensity (1 = mild to 4 = very severe), and (3) distress (0 = not at all to 4 = very much). Frequency and intensity are scored on a 4-point Likert scale, while distress uses a 5-point scale; higher scores indicate greater symptom burden. These Likert scales are part of the original, published SSES [14]. The original scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.810 [14]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.952, indicating excellent reliability.

Statistical analysis

Overview of the analytical pipeline

The analytical strategy employed in this study followed a sequential, hypothesis-generating pipeline designed to capture the complexity of symptom interactions from multiple levels. As suggested by recent methodological scholarship [15, 16], integrating variable-centered (EFA, Network) and person-centered (LPA) approaches allows for a more comprehensive understanding of symptomatology, addressing both the underlying latent structure and population heterogeneity. The overall analytical framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis roadmap

Data preparation and descriptive statistics

All variables involved in this study were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population were presented as categorical variables in the form of percentages. The severity scores of 19 symptoms were treated as continuous variables and described using means (standard deviations). To visually display the occurrence frequency of each symptom, we used a rose diagram.

Symptom dimension extraction and latent profile analysis

To address the issue of multicollinearity among highly correlated symptoms and to distinguish core latent traits from peripheral manifestations, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was initially conducted. EFA was chosen over Principal Component Analysis because the objective was to uncover latent constructs underlying the correlations among symptoms, rather than merely reducing data dimensionality [17].

A Promax oblique rotation method was employed, which allows for correlations among the extracted factors, aligning more realistically with the nature of psychological constructs, where dimensions are often interrelated [18]. The optimal number of factors was determined through a convergent approach, combining the results from parallel analysis. This comprehensive strategy ensured that the selected factor structure was both statistically sound and consistent with existing theoretical frameworks. The resulting symptom factor scores, derived from the EFA, represented the core latent dimensions of symptoms.

Subsequently, to account for population heterogeneity, these factor scores were utilized as input variables for latent profile analysis (LPA). LPA is methodologically necessary to identify unobserved subgroups of stroke survivors exhibiting distinct patterns of symptom characteristics. The optimal number of profiles was determined by systematically comparing goodness-of-fit indices across models with varying numbers of classes. These indices included the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-adjusted BIC (SABIC), and entropy. Additionally, likelihood ratio tests, such as the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and the Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), were considered. The final model selection was based on a holistic evaluation encompassing statistical fit, model parsimony, and the clinical and theoretical meaningfulness of the identified profile. A higher entropy value (typically > 0.8) indicates well-separated and distinct classes. The two-class model, which divided participants into two profiles (profile 1, n = 295; profile 2, n = 156), was determined to be optimal based on the aforementioned statistical criteria, clinical interpretability, and profile membership probabilities.

Network estimation

While factor analysis identifies latent constructs, it does not elucidate the specific pairwise interactions between symptoms. To address this, symptom networks were estimated to visualize and quantify the complex interplay of symptoms at a granular level. Networks were estimated using the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) with graphical lasso regularization (EBICglasso). This method, applied to Spearman’s rank correlation matrices, is robust to non-normal data distributions, which are common in symptom severity ratings. Regularization was applied to shrink small partial correlations, enhancing the interpretability and stability of the network structures by differentiating genuine relationships from those attributable to sampling noise.

Sensitivity analysis for variable redundancy

To assess the robustness of network findings to potential variable redundancy, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by removing highly correlated variable pairs (|r| > 0.8) and re-estimating the network. The results are reported in the Results section, with full details provided in the Supplementary File 2.

Network description and central node identification

Network density, defined as the ratio of actual edges to possible edges, was measured overall symptom connectivity [19]. We used two key centrality metrics to identify central symptoms. Strength centrality summed the absolute edge weights of a node, showing its total connectivity [20]. Expected influence summed the signed edge weights, capturing the node’s net effect on the network. Higher expected influence suggests greater potential to spread effects across the network [21]. The stability of centrality rankings was rigorously evaluated through case-dropping bootstraps (1000 iterations) to assess the robustness of centrality indices to sample variations. A CS-coefficient ≥ 0.50 indicates acceptable stability, and ≥ 0.70 indicates high stability, thereby ensuring the reliability of the identified central nodes.

Network comparison analysis

To statistically test whether the network structures differ across the identified profiles (profile 1 and profile 2), we performed a Network Comparison Test (NCT) using the NetworkComparisonTest package in R [22]. This test is a key step in examining whether network structures differ across subgroups. NCT is a permutation-based approach that tests whether two independent networks estimated from different profiles are significantly different in terms of global structure and edge-specific connectivity.

For each comparison, we conducted 1,000 permutation iterations to generate empirical sampling distributions under the null hypothesis of network invariance. Three complementary indices were examined: (1) network structure invariance (M), which quantifies the maximum difference in edge weights between the two networks; (2) global strength invariance (S), which compares the overall connectivity levels (i.e., the sum of absolute edge weights) between profiles; and (3) edge invariance, which identifies specific connections that differ significantly between the two networks at the individual edge level [22].

Intervention simulation and total score calculation

Finally, to move beyond description toward hypothesis generation regarding clinical targets, we performed in silico interventions. While observational data cannot prove causality, simulation algorithms provide a methodological boundary to estimate the potential impact of symptom alteration. We used the NodeIdentifyR (NIRA) algorithm, which simulates the system-wide effects of altering individual symptom states within a network [11].

For each symptom, we simulated two opposing interventions: (a) relief, by setting the symptom to its minimum observed value; and (b) aggravation, by setting it to its maximum. The simulation assumed that changes would spread through the network based on edge weights, using a propagation factor of 0.5. The impact of each intervention was measured as the change in total symptom burden (sum of all 19 symptom scores), expressed as a percentage change from baseline.

Symptoms sensitivity was ranked according to three metrics: (1) relief effect (burden decrease), (2) aggravation effect (burden increase), and (3) net effect (the absolute difference between relief and aggravation effects), reflecting overall intervention sensitivity. Simulations were conducted for the full sample and separately within each clinically derived profile (profile 1 and profile 2), enabling the identification of personalized intervention targets.

To quantify each symptom’s overall clinical importance regardless of intervention direction, we calculated a Total Score as the sum of absolute relief and aggravation effects: Total Score = |Relief Effect| + |Aggravation Effect|. Higher total scores indicate greater overall symptom influence irrespective of whether an intervention aims to relieve or worsen the symptom. Standard errors for the Total Score were derived via error propagation (SE_total = √(SE_relief² + SE_aggravation²)), and 95% confidence intervals were calculated accordingly. Symptoms were ranked in descending order of Total Score based on the overall sample. Forest plots were generated with point size proportional to subgroup sample size to reflect estimation precision.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.0). Network estimation and centrality analysis utilized the qgraph, bootnet, and igraph packages; network comparison tests were conducted using the NetworkComparisonTest package; and simulation interventions and effect size calculations were based on custom scripts built on the mgm package. Network evaluation employed the EBICglasso model (via the qgraph package), calculating centrality metrics (strength, expected influence) and assessing their stability through case-dropping bootstrapping (CS coefficient reported). The NodeIdentifyR algorithm was implemented to simulate interventions and predict the theoretical impact of symptom-specific interventions on overall network activity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Symptom burden network

Figure 4B presents the overall network structure for the study cohort (n = 451). Nodes represent the 19 symptoms (S1–S19), and edges reflect regularized partial correlations estimated via EBICglasso, with edge thickness proportional to association strength. The network density was 0.193, indicating a relatively sparse structure with specific, non-random associations. Green edges represent positive partial correlations and red edges represent negative partial correlations. The network contained both positive and negative associations. Positive edges reflect symptom co-occurrence, while negative edges reflect inverse conditional relationships. For instance, strong edges were observed between limited limb mobility (S1) and limb weakness (S2), poor limb coordination (S7) and loss of body balance (S8), and impaired memory (S9) and impaired attention (S10), implying that these symptoms may cluster together. Core symptoms identified based on expected influence and strength centrality included limited limb mobility (S1, EI = 1.077), impaired memory (S9, EI = 1.065), and impaired attention (S10, EI = 1.032) (Fig. 4A). The stability of centrality indices was assessed using case‑dropping bootstrap. The correlation stability (CS) coefficients for expected influence (0.672) and strength (0.594) remained above 0.5 when up to 70% of the sample was omitted(Fig. 4C), confirming that the identified core nodes were reliably estimated.

Sensitivity analysis for variable redundancy

To assess the potential impact of variable redundancy on network estimation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. We identified 9 pairs of variables with |r| > 0.8, including limited limb mobility (S1) and limb weakness (S2, r = 0.893), impaired memory (S9) and impaired attention (S10, r = 0.921), and hopelessness about the future (S15) and loss of interest in surroundings (S16, r = 0.853). The complete list of all 9 pairs is provided in the Supplementary File 2 (Table S1).

We re-estimated the network after removing one variable from each redundant pair (retaining S1, S5, S7, S9, and S15). After removing redundant variables, S19 (Decline in self-care ability, Strength = 1.08) emerged as the top node, whereas S1 (Limited limb mobility, Strength = 1.14) was the most central in the original network. Full results are provided in the Supplementary File 2 (Tables S1–S3, Figure S1).

Network comparison between symptom burden profile

We performed a NCT based on 1,000 permutations (Table 4). The overall network structure differed significantly between the two profiles (M = 0.285, p = 0.026), whereas global strength was comparable (Profile 1: 2.691, Profile 2: 2.572; S = 0.119, p = 0.112). Edge-wise comparisons identified three connections involving fatigue (S18) that differed significantly between profiles (all p < 0.05; Table 5). In Profile 2, fatigue showed stronger positive associations with limb weakness (S2–S18: 0.227 vs. 0.000 in Profile 1, p = 0.002) and loss of balance (S8–S18: 0.106 vs. − 0.065 in Profile 1, p = 0.019). The connection between fatigue and decline in self-care ability (S18–S19) was stronger in Profile 1 (0.519 vs. 0.234 in Profile 2, p = 0.005). In the overall network, alleviating interventions most effectively reduced total symptom burden through limited limb mobility (S1), decline in self-care ability (S19), and limb weakness (S2) (Fig. 8A), with corresponding reductions of 11.8%, 11.1%, and 10.2%, respectively (Fig. 8C). Conversely, aggravating interventions increased burden most substantially through foot varus (S6), foot drop (S5), and hopelessness about the future (S15) (Fig. 8B), elevating total burden by 14.6%, 14.6%, and 14.4% (Fig. 8C). We also calculated a net effect as the absolute difference between aggravating and alleviating effect sizes. Limited limb mobility (S1), decline in self-care ability (S19), and limb weakness (S2) had the highest net effects. This indicates these symptoms were especially sensitive to both types of interventions.

Discussion

This study used multiple methods to analyze 451 stroke survivors. It is crucial to define the scope of our findings at the outset. The symptom clusters and latent profiles identified in this study should be interpreted as patterns of symptom experience (a phenomenological construct), rather than distinct clinical syndromes with independent pathophysiological bases. By strictly framing our findings within the realm of symptom experience, we aim to avoid overinterpreting statistical associations as clinical diagnoses. This distinction not only underscores the methodological rigor of our study but also highlights its specific contribution to nursing science: providing a basis for precision symptom management interventions that prioritize the alleviation of patients’ subjective distress, independent of the underlying disease pathology.

Heterogeneity of post-stroke symptoms and identification of latent profiles

The present study delineates the complex landscape of post-stroke symptomatology. We categorized symptoms into four core domains: motor dysfunction, emotional disorders, cognitive and language deficits, and pain with foot deformity. This taxonomic structure aligns with established clinical frameworks [5]. It is also consistent with prior factor analytic studies within stroke populations [6]. Our findings reinforce the notion that symptoms do not occur in isolation. They present as interconnected clusters [23].

Through latent profile analysis (LPA), we identified two distinct symptom patterns. One profile was characterized by low symptom burden, and the other profile was characterized by high symptom burden. This finding moves the analytical focus beyond simple profile averages [24, 25]. It suggests that patient heterogeneity is not merely a continuum of severity; it may reflect qualitative differences in symptom presentation. Similar patterns of heterogeneity have been observed in research on mild traumatic brain injury [26]. This suggests that symptom expression in neurological conditions may not be fully explained by a single, uniform disease process [27].

Characteristics of symptom network structures

Network analysis is an emerging methodology widely utilized in psychopathology and chronic disease management [28–30]. It allowed us to view symptoms as an interconnected system rather than isolated entities. In the full sample, three symptoms exhibited the highest centrality: limited limb mobility (S1), decline in self-care ability (S19), and limb weakness (S2). These symptoms may act as hubs that stabilize the symptom network. This finding is consistent with the centrality hypothesis [31, 32], which proposes that some symptoms exert greater influence within a network.

The comparison between the two latent profiles revealed qualitative differences beyond severity. Their network structures and core symptoms were distinct. In the low burden profile, motor symptoms (S1 and S19) remained central. In the high burden profile, the network reorganized. Cognitive symptoms became central, specifically impaired memory (S9) and impaired attention (S10). This shift suggests the involvement of different underlying organizational patterns [33, 34]. In the low burden profile, motor symptoms may be associated with wider cascading effects. In the high burden profile, emotional or cognitive symptoms may play a more central role. This level of analysis addresses a key limitation of previous studies, which often assumed patient homogeneity.

We also observed strong associations between specific symptom pairs. Examples include limited limb mobility (S1) and limb weakness (S2), representing upper and lower limb motor deficits, as well as hopelessness about the future (S15) and loss of interest in surroundings (S16). These strong associations may reflect shared neurobiological substrates [35]. They may also reflect synergistic effects in daily functioning. For instance, limb dysfunction often results from damage to proximal brain regions. The loss of one function may increase the compensatory burden on another. Similarly, anxiety and insomnia may form a self reinforcing pattern. Anxiety can disrupt sleep, and poor sleep may in turn worsen anxiety. This observation aligns with comorbidity network theory [36], which posits that co-occurring conditions may be maintained by shared mechanisms or mutual reinforcement.

Network reorganization across symptom burden profile

The network comparison test revealed differences in symptom organization between the two profiles. Overall network structure differed significantly. Global strength was similar. This indicates that the two profiles are distinguished by how symptoms interconnect, not by overall connectivity strength.

NCT revealed significant differences in the global structure of symptom organization between the two profiles. Global strength was similar across profile. This indicates that the profile is distinguished by how symptoms interconnect rather than by the overall density of connectivity. Notably, all three significantly different edges involved fatigue (S18).

In the high burden profile (Profile 2), fatigue showed stronger positive associations with limb weakness (S2) and loss of balance (S8). This finding suggests that in patients with a high symptom burden, fatigue may be more closely linked to physical impairments. The association between fatigue and loss of balance (S8–S18) showed a directional shift across profile. It was negative in Profile 1 and positive in Profile 2. The association between fatigue and a decline in self-care ability (S18–S19) was stronger in the low burden profile 1.

Collectively, these findings imply that fatigue may play distinct roles across different patient subgroups. This pattern aligns with recent research demonstrating that post-stroke fatigue comprises distinct phenotypes with varying relationships to motor characteristics [37]. Stroke rehabilitation programs may consider these distinct fatigue patterns when designing individualized interventions. Tailoring fatigue management to a patient’s symptom burden profile could be explored in future studies.

Clinical implications of simulated interventions

The simulation results generated by the NodeIdentifyR algorithm provided insights that extend beyond static network analysis. First, the simulations were consistent with traditional centrality measures. In the full sample and low burden profile, targeting limited limb mobility (S1) was associated with the largest predicted reduction in total symptom burden. This reduction was 11.8% in the model. This finding supports the potential utility of centrality indices for screening initial intervention targets [24, 38].

However, the simulations also suggested a dissociation between high-centrality nodes and high-risk nodes. A notable example is symptom foot varus (S6). In Profile 1 (low burden profile), foot varus (S6) exhibited low centrality and was not a network hub. Yet its worsening was predicted to be associated with a 26.2% increase in total symptom burden. This predicted effect size was larger than that of many more central symptoms. In contrast, the same predicted worsening effect in Profile 2 was 7.06%.

This model based finding has potential clinical implications. For patients in Profile 1, focusing solely on core (central) symptoms may be insufficient. Clinicians may also need to identify high risk symptoms. These nodes are not central, but their deterioration may be associated with larger system wide changes [39]. The marked difference in predicted effect sizes between the two profiles (26.2% vs. 7.06%) highlights the importance of subgroup specific risk assessment.

Based on these findings, we propose a benefit–risk dual-dimension framework for clinical decision making. Clinical practice may need to address two questions. First, where are the network hubs? This aims to maximize potential benefit. Second, where are the sensitive switches? This aims to minimize potential risk. This network sensitivity strategy may be more nuanced and potentially more effective than focusing on symptom relief based solely on centrality. A node may have few connections. But it may lie on a critical pathway or within a sensitive feedback loop. Changes in such a node may be associated with disproportionate system wide effects. The NodeIdentifyR algorithm can identify this dynamic sensitivity in silico.

At an operational level, this framework suggests a three step clinical workflow. First, use rapid assessment to assign patients to a specific symptom profile with brief screening tools. Second, identify candidate core symptoms (hubs) and high risk symptoms (switches) specific to that profile. Third, educate patients to recognize their specific high risk symptoms and improve self management skills to prevent potential cascading deterioration. In summary, these data driven simulations may help bridge the gap between theoretical network models and clinical action. They provide testable hypotheses and may guide target selection tailored to each patient’s symptom profile. This lays the groundwork for more effective interventions prior to the initiation of costly clinical trials.

Total impact across intervention directions

Total Score analysis revealed that limited limb mobility (S1) and decline in self-care ability (S19) had the highest overall impact across intervention directions (Fig. 12). While fatigue (S18) showed differential edge connections between profiles (Table 5), its Total Score ranked lower, suggesting that motor symptoms and self-care ability serve as foundational drivers of network stability, whereas fatigue acts as a context-dependent modulator whose role varies by subgroup. Profile 2 demonstrated consistently higher Total Scores than Profile 1, particularly for limited limb mobility (S1), decline in self-care ability (S19), indicating that patients with high symptom burden may be more sensitive to changes in these core symptoms. These findings suggest that S1 and S19 warrant priority in general intervention strategies, while fatigue requires subgroup-specific tailoring.

Implications of the sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis revealed that after removing redundant symptom pairs, decline in self-care ability (S19) emerged as the most central node, replacing limited limb mobility (S1) from the original analysis. This shift has important clinical implications. It suggests that while individual motor impairments such as limb mobility may be clinically salient when assessed separately, patients’ subjective experience of declining self-care ability may be a more fundamental driver of the overall symptom network.

From a clinical perspective, this finding indicates that interventions targeting functional decline in daily living activities could have broader effects on the symptom network than focusing solely on isolated physical symptoms. For example, helping patients maintain or regain the ability to perform self-care tasks (e.g., bathing, dressing, eating) might reduce not only the direct burden of functional impairment but also secondary symptoms such as hopelessness, fatigue, and social withdrawal.

It is important to note, however, that this interpretation is hypothesis-generating. The shift in centrality rankings was influenced by the presence of highly correlated symptom pairs. Therefore, while the pattern that functional decline appears to play a central role is notable, the precise ranking of individual symptoms should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. The symptom networks represent conditional dependencies, not causal pathways. The simulation predictions are based on these associations and assume that the estimated network structure approximates the system’s dynamics. This assumption requires testing in longitudinal or experimental studies.

Second, the study focused on symptom experience heterogeneity, not neuroanatomical heterogeneity. We did not collect data on stroke lesion location, side, or subtype. Therefore, we could not examine whether symptom network structures differ by specific stroke syndromes. Future studies should integrate neuroanatomical variables with patient-reported outcomes.

Third, the sample was recruited from three hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China, using convenience sampling. This may limit generalizability to other populations or healthcare settings. The sample size (n = 451) was adequate for network analysis, as all CS-coefficients exceeded 0.59. However, replication in independent and more diverse samples is needed.

Fourth, symptom assessment relied on a single self-report scale (SSES). The SSES does not capture some clinically important stroke symptoms, such as visual field loss, facial droop, sensory changes, specific aphasias, neglect, or dysphagia. Recall bias may also influence self-reported ratings.

Fifth, the two-profile solution (low versus high burden) is a data-driven simplification. While we acknowledge that alternative solutions (e.g., three or four profiles) showed lower AIC and BIC values, they yielded very small subgroups (e.g., < 7% of the sample) that are unlikely to represent stable, reproducible symptom phenotypes. Therefore, we prioritized clinical interpretability and model parsimony over marginal improvements in statistical fit. However, we recognize that the current two-profile solution may limit the granularity of the findings. To address this in future research, we aim to explore more advanced analytical techniques. Specifically, while we utilized Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to align with traditional latent variable frameworks, future investigations would benefit from applying walktrap-based network clustering to self-report data [15, 40]. This could help identify robust symptom communities and explore whether the structure of symptom experiences differs when analyzed through a network framework, potentially yielding more granular and clinically actionable subgroups. Additionally, we plan to explore second-order unsupervised clustering to further bridge the gap between statistical phenotypes and personalized symptom management.

Sixth, regarding variable redundancy. We detected nine pairs of highly correlated symptoms (r > 0.8). Our sensitivity analysis showed that removing redundant variables changed the ranking of central nodes. This suggests that variable redundancy may have influenced the centrality estimates. Future studies should perform formal Unique Variable Analysis prior to network construction.

Finally, the in-silico findings are hypothesis-generating. The NodeIdentifyR algorithm identifies potential high-risk nodes and high-benefit targets based on empirical data, but these predictions remain theoretical. The proposed benefit–risk framework requires validation in randomized controlled trials before clinical adoption.

Conclusion

In this cohort of 451 stroke survivors, we observed that symptom experience is not homogeneous. Patients could be grouped into low and high symptom burden profiles based on their reported symptoms. These two profiles showed different network structures. This observation suggests that symptom management strategies may need to be tailored to a patient’s overall burden level, although this hypothesis requires testing in independent samples.

The simulation analyses generated two hypothesis-driven insights. First, targeting high-centrality symptoms (e.g., limited limb mobility) was associated with the largest predicted reductions in overall symptom burden within the model. Second, we observed a dissociation between centrality and risk in the low-burden subgroup. In that subgroup, worsening a non-central symptom (foot varus, S6) was predicted to be associated with a 26.2% increase in total burden. This predicted effect size was larger than that of most central symptoms. This model-based finding suggests that relying solely on centrality may be insufficient for identifying high-risk targets.

A sensitivity analysis revealed that the presence of highly correlated symptom pairs influenced the ranking of central nodes. After removing redundant variables, decline in self-care ability (S19) was the most central node. This finding suggests that functional decline in daily living activities may be more closely associated with the symptom network than isolated motor impairments. However, due to variable redundancy, the specific ordering of individual symptoms should be interpreted with caution, while the general pattern that motor, cognitive, and emotional symptom domains play important roles remained consistent across analytic strategies.

We introduce a benefit–risk dual-dimension framework as a hypothesis-generating tool for symptom management. The NodeIdentifyR simulation may help prioritize testable intervention hypotheses before costly clinical trials begin. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to validate these model-based predictions.
