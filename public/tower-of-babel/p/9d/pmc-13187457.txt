Optimizing fatigue resistance and lifetime of MEMS scanning mirrors with a novel coupled parameter distribution structural framework
Authors: Songtao Liu, Gaofei Zhang, Zhuohang Zhang, Zheng You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a coupled parameter distribution structural optimization framework that enhances the fatigue resistance and extends the lifetime of micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) scanning mirrors. Simultaneous refinement of the number, distribution, and local width of control points reduces maximum stress range amplitude (SRA) by 44.5% and lowers angular creep rates by 36.2%. Under 50 °C accelerated aging, lifetime increases from 266.0 h to over 690.7 h, projecting to 6658.3 h–9780.3 h at room temperature. Key innovations include: (1) parameter distribution optimization to minimize the maximum SRA in the torsion beam while preserving optical performance; (2) control point distribution strategies ensuring robust convergence; and (3) cubic spline interpolation for smooth, fabrication-ready geometries. This framework enables the determination of locally optimal structural configurations within the explored design space, providing a versatile route to extend MEMS scanning mirror lifetimes and manage fatigue in MEMS actuators.

Introduction

Light detection and ranging (LiDAR) has advanced rapidly in recent years, becoming essential for applications such as autonomous driving, aerial robotics, intelligent surveillance, and smart city infrastructure1–3. Central to these systems is the micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) scanning mirror—an optical actuator that precisely steers laser beams and defines angular resolution, scanning efficiency, and spatial coverage4–6.

Traditionally, silicon-based MEMS scanning mirrors have been favored for their fabrication compatibility. However, silicon’s intrinsic brittleness makes these devices prone to failure under mechanical shocks or sustained vibrations7–12. To address this limitation, researchers have explored non-silicon alternatives, particularly alloy-based mirrors, offering superior elasticity and shock resilience13–18. Although these materials reduce fracture risks in harsh environments, they often experience accelerated fatigue under cyclic loading, resulting in shorter operational lifetimes than silicon counterparts19–22. This trade-off between impact resistance and fatigue resistance highlights that material substitution alone cannot fully ensure reliable operation in MEMS scanning mirrors. Overcoming this challenge demands structural optimization strategies capable of redistributing stress and suppressing fatigue accumulation, independent of material choice.

Various structural optimization methods have been proposed to enhance fatigue resistance in MEMS devices23–34. Parameterized scanning–based optimization is widely used in engineering practice due to its low dimensionality and high computational efficiency, where a small number of global geometric parameters—such as beam width, thickness, or length—are systematically varied to identify improved designs23. However, such approaches are inherently restricted to predefined and typically uniform structural patterns, limiting their ability to resolve localized stress concentrations along torsion beams. Beyond parameterized scanning, shape optimization24–29 and topology optimization methods30–34 have incorporated fatigue-related criteria—including accumulated damage24,25,30, localized stress concentrations26,27, fatigue reserve factors31, and strain energy-based fracture resistance32—into node-based or density-based formulations. While shape28,29 and topology optimization methods33,34 provide strong theoretical links between geometry and fatigue resistance, they typically rely on mesh-level, non-parametric representations involving thousands of design variables. This often results in high computational cost, sensitivity to initialization, and limited controllability over local geometric features, making it difficult to realize fabrication-ready, locally optimal stress-minimizing structures.

These limitations reveal a critical gap across existing optimization methods: parameterized scanning approaches are computationally efficient but lack geometric flexibility, whereas mesh-based shape and topology optimization methods offer high adaptability but suffer from heavy computational cost and slow convergence. What is currently missing is an optimization strategy that simultaneously provides spatial flexibility, strong optimization performance, and low computational demand.

From a fatigue-mechanics standpoint, selecting an appropriate optimization metric is essential for linking geometry to fatigue resistance. Although approaches such as fatigue reserve factors31, Miner’s rule35, and critical-plane criteria36 are valuable for post-design lifetime assessment, they are difficult to use as optimization objectives in MEMS torsion beams due to complex multiaxial stresses, the lack of reliable microscale multiaxial S–N data, and the strong nonlinearity these metrics introduce. In contrast, the stress range amplitude (SRA) offers a physically grounded and numerically stable surrogate: in high-cycle fatigue, crack initiation and growth are primarily governed by the stress intensity factor range, which is directly driven by the SRA37. Minimizing the SRA therefore provides a practical and robust pathway to suppress fatigue degradation through geometry optimization.

In this work, we propose a coupled parameter distribution optimization framework that provides flexible, spatially adaptive design freedom while maintaining low computational cost for fatigue-critical MEMS scanning mirrors. The method jointly optimizes the number of control points, their spatial distribution, and their local beam widths, with smooth manufacturable geometries reconstructed via spline interpolation. A key innovation is elevating the control-point distribution itself to an optimization variable, enabling uniform, random, and SRA-peak-guided initialization and progressive refinement. This formulation allows the optimizer to autonomously identify fatigue-critical geometric regions and reshape the SRA field into a balanced multi-peak pattern. The proposed framework achieves the lowest maximum SRA among parameterized scanning, shape optimization, and topology optimization, while requiring only a fraction of the computational effort of mesh-based approaches. Experimentally, the optimized torsion beam reduces maximum SRA by 44.5%, lowers average angular creep rates by 36.2%, and extends the mean fatigue lifetime from 266.0 h to 690.7 h under 50 °C accelerated testing, confirming both the performance gains and the robustness of the method.

Method

To address fatigue-induced degradation in MEMS scanning mirrors, we develop a geometry-driven, coupled parameter distribution optimization framework. The proposed methodology integrates structural optimization and finite element simulations to systematically suppress stress accumulation and extend fatigue lifetime. A graphical summary of the overall approach is first provided to illustrate the core concepts and workflow. The subsequent sections describe the definition of structural control parameters, the implementation of the optimization process, and the settings for finite element stress range amplitude (SRA) analysis. The validation of this framework is presented separately in Section Experiments and Results.

Figure 2 outlines the schematic of the coupled parameter distribution optimization framework. The framework consists of four steps: (1) identifying the number and spatial distribution of control points based on initial SRA simulation, which determines the resolution and placement of structural control variables; (2) generating n + 1 initial width distributions as the starting simplex for the Nelder–Mead optimization and constructing smooth profiles via cubic spline interpolation; (3) iteratively updating the width distribution through SRA-driven search and refinement until convergence is achieved; and (4) outputting the optimized width profile and minimized MSRA

Step 2 prepares the initial design inputs for the Nelder–Mead (NM) optimization by generating n + 1 width distributions di,n+1(Li), which form the starting simplex38. The first distribution, di,1(Li) corresponds to the constant-width torsion beam and serves as a baseline reference. Each of the remaining n distributions is generated by individually assigning the predefined minimum width to one control point while keeping all others at the baseline value. To ensure geometric continuity and manufacturability, each discrete width distribution di,j(Li) is transformed into a smooth width profile Di,j(Li) using cubic spline interpolation. Finite element simulations are then performed on these interpolated profiles to compute the corresponding MSRA values Pmax,i,1 through Pmax,i,n+1. This initialization process is applied identically across all distribution strategies, ensuring consistency in optimization across different control point configurations.

To systematically investigate the impact of initial control point configurations on the optimization results, three different control point distribution strategies are explored, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The first strategy, uniform distribution, places control points l1,1, l1,2, …, l1,n evenly along the torsion beam (Fig. 3a). This ensures that all beam segments are equally weighted during optimization, providing a spatially uniform and geometrically balanced initial condition. The second strategy, random distribution, assigns control points l2,1, l2,2, …, l2,n to randomly selected positions along the beam (Fig. 3b). Compared to the uniform configuration, this strategy introduces greater variability into the initial width profiles and expands the effective design space. It is particularly suitable for beams with irregular or non-uniform initial stress patterns. The third strategy, SRA-peak-based distribution, concentrates control points near regions of high initial SRA (Fig. 3c). Specific points such as l3,3 and l3,n-2 are intentionally placed at SRA peaks identified from the baseline simulation, while the remaining points are evenly distributed in between. Experiments and results

To experimentally validate the effectiveness of the proposed structural optimization framework, we performed a series of fatigue and lifetime tests on MEMS scanning mirrors fabricated with two different torsion beam designs. Devices incorporating modulated-width torsion beams and constant-width beams were fabricated and tested under identical conditions. These experiments aimed to quantitatively evaluate enhancements in fatigue resistance and lifetime, specifically in terms of creep rate and fracture time. The remainder of this section outlines the experimental setup and describes the testing methodologies for fatigue assessment under standard conditions and lifetime evaluation under accelerated thermal aging.

Lifetime testing configuration

Figure 8 shows the accelerated lifetime testing setup used to evaluate the long-term durability of the MEMS scanning mirrors. Fatigue resistance testing and results

Fatigue resistance was quantitatively evaluated by monitoring changes in the slow-axis optical scanning angle. To ensure a controlled and comparable environment, both types of scanning mirrors operated under identical initial conditions: the fast axis at its resonant frequency and the slow axis at 10 Hz. The fast-axis optical scanning angle was actively maintained at its initial value by dynamically adjusting the drive voltage through closed-loop control, ensuring that the mechanical loading from fast-axis motion remained constant. In contrast, the slow-axis drive voltage was held constant without adjustment. As fatigue accumulated, the slow-axis optical scanning angle exhibited progressive angular creep.

Figure 9a illustrates the angular creep behavior over a 72 h test period. The constant-width design exhibited an angular creep of DR = 1.45°, whereas the modulated-width structure showed a reduced creep of DG = 0.98°, corresponding to a 32.4% improvement. These results confirm that geometric optimization suppresses fatigue-induced angular creep and enhances structural durability under cyclic operation. Figure 9b presents replicated test results for each design, showing consistent total angular creep across independent samples. The modulated-width structures yielded creep values of 0.98° and 0.81° (average: 0.90°), while the constant-width beams reached 1.45° and 1.37° (average: 1.41°), confirming a 36.2% reduction in long-term angular creep. Discussion and conclusions

Comparative performance of different optimization strategies

Figure 12 provides a unified quantitative comparison of four optimization strategies—namely the proposed coupled parameter distribution framework, topology optimization, shape optimization, and parameterized scanning optimization—evaluated under identical objectives, initial structures, and constraints38–41. The two-dimensional performance map plots MSRA on the horizontal axis as the decisive indicator of fatigue-critical optimization effectiveness, and optimization time on the vertical axis as a secondary measure of computational efficiency. Among all evaluated methods, the proposed coupled parameter distribution framework solved via the Nelder–Mead algorithm achieves the lowest MSRA (137.0 MPa) with a comparatively short optimization time, demonstrating the best balance between SRA-reduction capability and computational cost. Topology optimization and shape optimization offer higher geometric flexibility but incur substantially longer computation times and fail to achieve the same level of SRA suppression for this problem setting. Parameterized scanning optimization delivers the shortest computation time due to its low-dimensional design space, but its limited structural adaptivity results in significantly higher SRA. Overall, the comparison confirms that the coupled parameter distribution approach provides the most effective SRA-balanced design with practical computational efficiency, justifying its selection as the final optimal solution.Fig. 12Comparative performance of different optimization strategies in terms of optimization effectiveness and efficiency.The horizontal axis represents MSRA, while the vertical axis represents the optimization time. Applicability of the proposed framework to different MEMS materials and device types

Although the experimental validation in this work focuses on titanium-alloy MEMS scanning mirrors, the proposed coupled parameter distribution optimization framework is not material-specific9,37,44. From a failure-mechanics perspective, both metallic and silicon-based torsion-beam mirrors operate under high-cycle resonant loads, and fatigue failure is dominated by cyclic stress concentrations regardless of differences in microscopic damage mechanisms. Methodologically, the framework is formulated at the geometric and structural level: the optimization objective, design variables, and constraints are independent of constitutive behavior, making the workflow directly applicable to silicon and other MEMS devices with torsion-beam-dominated architectures. Material properties enter only through finite-element SRA evaluation, and updating Young’s modulus, density, or Poisson’s ratio adapts the model without altering the optimization formulation. Thus, the framework is material-agnostic in structure, but material-aware in parameterization, and the titanium-alloy case presented here should be viewed as a representative validation rather than a limitation of applicability.

Conclusions

We present a comprehensive structural optimization framework to enhance the fatigue resistance and extend the lifetime of MEMS scanning mirrors. The proposed coupled parameter distribution optimization framework simultaneously adjusts the number, spatial distribution, and local width of control points along the torsion beam. As a result, simulations showed a 44.5% reduction in MSRA, from 247 MPa to 137 MPa. Experimentally, the average angular creep rate decreased by 36.2%, from 1.41°/72 h to 0.90°/72 h.

In accelerated fatigue testing at 50 °C, the optimized modulated-width torsion beam achieved lifetimes of 660 h, 688 h, and 724 h (mean 690.7 h), compared with 248 h, 267 h, and 283 h (mean 266.0 h) for the constant-width design. After frequency-corrected Arrhenius extrapolation, the room-temperature lifetime lies within 6658.3 h–9780.3 h. The optimized structure also reduces slow-axis stiffness, enabling lower drive voltage and reduced energy consumption.

Although the experimental validation in this work focuses on titanium-alloy MEMS scanning mirrors, the proposed coupled parameter distribution framework is broadly applicable to silicon-based mirrors and other fatigue-critical MEMS devices, as its formulation is geometric rather than material-specific. At the same time, the optimization objective—minimizing the MSRA—serves as a practical and robust surrogate for fatigue-critical behavior, but does not capture secondary factors such as crack-length evolution, geometric correction factors, or material-dependent fracture mechanisms. Thus, while the framework effectively suppresses stress concentrations and demonstrably enhances fatigue resistance, it is not a fully crack-resolved fatigue life model. Future extensions may incorporate multiaxial fatigue criteria or crack-propagation-based formulations to further improve quantitative lifetime prediction without compromising the optimization efficiency achieved in this study.
