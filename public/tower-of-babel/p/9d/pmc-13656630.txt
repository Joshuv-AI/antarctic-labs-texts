Early diagnostic performance of real-time PCR versus serology for murine typhus and Q fever in a public health setting
Authors: Mónica Vélez Tobarias, Ana María Torres Vega, José Antonio Pérez, Guillem Clot, Julio Morais Martín, Emma Carmelo, Carlos Ascaso Terrén
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the diagnostic performance of real-time PCR compared with conventional serology for murine typhus (MT) and Q fever (QF) in patients with undifferentiated febrile illness (UFI) in an endemic public health setting.MethodsThis secondary analysis was nested within a prospective cross-sectional cohort of UFI conducted in the Canary Islands Health Service (2019–2022). Of 146 eligible patients, 78 fulfilled predefined analytical criteria and were included (39 evaluable for QF and 39 for MT). Real-time PCR was performed 5–10 days after fever onset and prior to antibiotic administration. Serology was performed from day 10 onwards. Sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic timing, and concordance between methods were analysed.ResultsReal-time PCR demonstrated 100% sensitivity and high specificity (96.3% for QF; 75% for MT) under strictly controlled conditions. Median time to molecular diagnosis was 6–7 days, compared with 26–27 days for serology. In a small number of patients, PCR was positive despite negative or inconclusive early serology. Overall, both techniques identified comparable proportions of confirmed cases, although PCR provided earlier microbiological confirmation.ConclusionsReal-time PCR and serology demonstrated comparable diagnostic performance under strictly controlled conditions. However, PCR provided earlier microbiological confirmation, particularly when performed 5–10 days after fever onset and prior to antibiotic administration. These findings support a stage-adapted, complementary diagnostic strategy in endemic settings, whereby molecular testing may be especially useful in patients with early UFI, while serology remains essential at later stages. Larger prospective studies with standardised follow-up would help to further validate these findings.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13646-3.Keywords: Murine typhus, Q fever, PCR, Early diagnosis, Public health

Introduction

Rickettsia typhi and Coxiella burnetii are obligate intracellular bacilli responsible for murine typhus (MT) and Q fever (QF), respectively. Murine typhus and Q fever are endemic in the Canary Islands, with MT being the leading cause of undifferentiated febrile illness (UFI) and QF occurring at lower incidence; while QF is well recognized by local clinicians, MT has historically been underdiagnosed [1], ]. Both zoonoses are frequent causes of acute febrile illness (AFI) and intermediate-duration fever (IDF; fever lasting 7–28 days) [1–7]. They present with a febrile syndrome ranging from mild to severe, including hospitalisation risk, multiorgan failure, and death [1, 8]. Although they share clinical and therapeutic similarities, their taxonomic classification, transmission routes, and pathogenesis differ [9, 10].

Seroprevalence studies on rickettsioses [11] highlight an underdiagnosed disease burden, supporting their classification as neglected tropical diseases [12]. MT incidence is particularly underestimated in QF-endemic regions [1], due to overlapping clinical presentations and the efficacy of shared antibiotics like doxycycline [13]. Current diagnosis primarily relies on serology [14], with indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) as the gold standard (sensitivity: 83–100%; specificity: 91–100%) after the second week of fever. Diagnostic accuracy depends on local epidemiology, clinical suspicion, and timing of testing [13]. In endemic areas, empirical doxycycline often leads to clinical improvement before serological confirmation, further underestimating the rickettsiosis burden [15, 16].

In Spain, serology predominates, though PCR is used in acute phases or seronegative patients with suggestive symptoms, especially in tertiary centres or outbreaks [17–19]. Bolaños et al. (2017) is the only Spanish study comparing PCR and serology, indicating higher molecular sensitivity in early disease stages before antibiotic use [17]. Globally, systematic comparisons of PCR and serology in human cohorts are scarce. For QF, PCR specificity is 90–100%, but sensitivity varies (15–81%), with improvements in acute phases before seroconversion [18, 20, 21]. For MT, PCR sensitivity is low (20–36%) but highly specific (≥98%) [22]. Optimal sample selection and primer design are critical for early diagnosis [17], and improved access to molecular diagnostics like PCR is recommended [20, 22, 23].

Our objective is to demonstrate the usefulness of early molecular diagnosis for MT and QF compared to the current gold standard, serology. Considering the pros and cons of each method from the literature [17–20, 22–25], we implemented rigorous sample selection, incorporating time since symptom onset and individualized inclusion/exclusion criteria for molecular and serological studies. Conducted within a public health system as part of routine clinical practice [1], this study evaluates whether early patient selection without prior antibiotics can enhance molecular sensitivity while minimizing unnecessary antibiotic use and preserving patient quality of life.

Materials and methods

This study is a secondary analysis nested within a prospective cross-sectional investigation on UFI, conducted within the Canary Islands Health Service (SCS) from December 2019 to June 2022 on La Palma and El Hierro islands (Canary Islands, Spain). Its aim is to assess the diagnostic performance of real-time PCR versus conventional serology for Coxiella burnetii and Rickettsia typhi. During this period, 146 patients fulfilled the clinical eligibility criteria and were included in the parent UFI cohort. A previous study derived from this cohort assessed incidence [1] and included only patients diagnosed during the calendar year 2021 (n = 79). In contrast, the present study includes all eligible patients recruited throughout the entire study period (2019–2022) that fulfilled predefined analytical and technical criteria for this secondary analysis.

Design

Patients were prospectively recruited into the parent UFI cohort if they presented with fever ≥ 38 °C lasting 5–28 days and no diagnosis after standard evaluation, which included full blood count, basic biochemistry, urinalysis, and chest X-ray. Other common causes of fever were ruled out based on clinical history, physical examination, and the results of this initial diagnostic work-up. No alternative diagnosis was established at inclusion. Exclusion criteria for cohort entry comprised immunodeficiency, nosocomial infection, and positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR.

For the present secondary analysis, additional predefined analytical criteria were applied to select patients suitable for comparison of serological and molecular diagnostic methods for Q fever (QF) and murine typhus (MT). These criteria did not replace the clinical eligibility criteria of the parent cohort. Seventy-eight patients from the parent cohort fulfilled all criteria and were included in the analysis (see Supplementary Figure S1). Of these, 39 were evaluable for QF and 39 for MT according to the predefined diagnostic protocol. These groups represent patients investigated for each infection and were not defined on the basis of confirmed diagnosis. Since the subsets evaluable for Q fever (n = 39) and murine typhus (n = 39) were derived from the same parent cohort, patients with co-infection were excluded, so no one was classified as a confirmed case for both infections. Participants included in both analyses were classified independently for each pathogen. Within each subset, patients were classified as confirmed or non-confirmed according to reference diagnostic criteria. All included patients underwent serological and real-time PCR testing for the corresponding pathogen within the established timeframes. A flow diagram summarising the study population selection, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and derivation of the MT and QF subsets is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Blood samples for molecular diagnostics were processed at the Instituto Universitario de Enfermedades Tropicales y Salud Pública de Canarias–Universidad de La Laguna (IUETSPC-ULL) by staff independent from those handling serology at reference hospitals, ensuring blinded analysis.

Fever onset was defined as the first day of documented temperature ≥ 38 °C, either self-reported or recorded at presentation.

Serological techniques

Serological testing was performed by indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) using whole-cell antigens of Coxiella burnetii phase II and Rickettsia typhi. Commercial kits included Virion (Richland, USA; 1983–1989) and Vitaltech (Spain; 2004–2005) for C. burnetii, and Pasteur Laboratories (Marnes-la-Coquette, France; since 1986) and bioMérieux (France; since 1987) for R. typhi.

A patient was considered a confirmed case when the following cut-offs were met: IgM ≥ 1:64 or IgG ≥ 1:1024 for C. burnetii, and IgM ≥ 1:192 for R. typhi, according to previously published diagnostic criteria and manufacturer recommendations [10, 18, 26]. Inconclusive results required a fourfold increase in antibody titre between paired sera obtained at least 14 days apart and within 60 days of fever onset.

Real-time PCR

Total DNA was purified from 250 µL of frozen whole blood stored at − 80 °C using the E.Z.N.A.® Blood DNA Mini Kit (Omega Bio-Tek). Real-time PCR assays were performed using the LightCycler® 480 thermal cycler (Roche). Each 50-µL reaction contained 300 ng of gDNA, 1× reaction buffer (Thermo Fisher), additional MgCl₂ (depending on the amplicon), 0.2 mM dNTP mix, 0.2 µM of each primer, 0.2 µM of each probe, and 0.5 µL of Phire Hot Start II DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher). Two different uniplex molecular detection assays were used, targeting the IS1111 transposon-like sequence for Coxiella burnetii and the 23S-5S rRNA ITS region for Rickettsia spp., including R. typhi, respectively. The fluorescent probes and primers are thoroughly described in Vega-García et al., 2024. PCR thermal cycling for both assays consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 amplification cycles (98 °C for 5 s, 56 °C for 5 s, 72 °C for 10 s). Fluorescence emission was measured during the annealing step (excitation/emission 483/670 nm).

The diagnostic algorithm included four replicates per gDNA sample (1.2 µg total per patient), four negative amplification controls (molecular-grade water), and one positive control, consistent of 10,000 copies of purified reference amplicon (686 bp for C. burnetii and 301 bp for R. typhi), spectrophotometrically quantified. Samples negative for Rickettsia spp. or C. burnetii were retested in quadruplicate with a PCR inhibition control, which was assessed by spiking 300 ng of patient-derived gDNA into a positive control reaction. PCR-positive samples were subsequently identified to species level by amplicon sequencing and BLAST analysis (NCBI). The sequences were assigned to a particular species when the identity compared to reference strains using BLAST analysis was > 98% (Supplementary figure S2).

Statistical analysis

Variables included gender, age, fever onset day, and test request dates. Quantitative variables were summarised using mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values. Qualitative variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies, and percentages.

Diagnostic performance of real-time PCR was evaluated using conventional serology as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Concordance between PCR and serology was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Associations between categorical variables were tested using Fisher’s exact test. Homogeneity of proportions between the two diagnostic methods was assessed with a binomial exact test applied to discordant results (p = 0.5).

All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of 5%. Analyses were performed using JAMOVI version 3.0.2 and R version 4.4.2, with the ThresholdROC R package employed for sensitivity and specificity calculations.

Results

We analysed a total of 78 patients, 39 evaluated for QF and 39 for MT.

For QF, the cohort included 24 males and 15 females, with a mean age of 46.2 years (SD: 19.8; range: 15–83). Real-time PCR was performed at 5 days of fever in 25% of patients, between 5 and 8 days in 50%, and between 8 and 10 days in the remaining 25%. The median day of PCR testing was 6 days of fever, resulting in 26 negative and 13 positive results.

Initial serology identified 6 positive confirmed cases (15%), half of which were diagnosed before day 20. The remaining 33 patients (85%) were initially seronegative and were subsequently tested in a second serological assessment, of which 6 became positive. Overall, diagnoses were confirmed at a median of 27 days, with 75% established before day 38. Among the 12 cases, half (6) were diagnosed via initial serology and the other half via repeat testing.

Discussion

When comparing molecular diagnosis (real-time PCR) with the current gold-standard serological method, both techniques showed broadly comparable diagnostic performance, although molecular testing identified confirmed cases earlier in the disease course. Both methods identified similar proportions of confirmed cases. In our study, real-time PCR achieved 100% sensitivity and yielded diagnoses between 5 and 10 days after fever onset, whereas serological diagnoses were obtained at a median of 29 days for QF and 21 days for MT, with 75% of cases confirmed before days 38 and 40, respectively.

Our findings are consistent with those of Bolaños et al. (2017) in Gran Canaria (Canary Islands, Spain), who suggested that molecular diagnosis is most effective during the acute phase prior to seroconversion [17]. Although their sample size was comparable to ours, they reported lower sensitivity (82.9%) and specificity (85%) for MT, and lower sensitivity (26.7%) but high specificity (98%) for QF. These discrepancies may reflect differences in PCR timing (5–10 days post-fever in our study, always before antibiotic administration) and methodological rigour. Our protocol incorporated a comprehensive algorithm involving up to eight real-time PCR reactions per patient (2.4 μg of genomic DNA per patient), inhibition controls for negative results, and strict pre-antibiotic sampling. These measures likely improved the reliability of molecular detection, particularly in seronegative or inconclusive results.

The sensitivity and specificity estimated for QF in our study also exceeded those reported by Bae et al. (2019) in South Korea. Although both studies performed early molecular testing (mean 6.1 ± 2.4 days of fever), their sensitivity (81.3%) and specificity (89.5%) were lower. This may be explained by the inclusion of patients with prior antibiotic use, which could have reduced bacterial DNA load, or by methodological differences between studies.

Other international studies [18, 21] have reported considerably lower PCR sensitivities (15–26%) for QF diagnosis. These works cite delayed medical consultation as a potential factor but do not specify fever duration at testing, antibiotic history, or methodological details (e.g. PCR replicates, inhibition controls, detection limits). Such omissions limit interpretability and reproducibility [18, 21].

Watthanaworawit et al. (2013) [22] remains the only clinical study comparing real-time PCR and serology for Rickettsia spp. in endemic settings, yet its reported sensitivity (36%) was markedly lower than that observed by Bolaños et al. (2017) [17]. The remaining publications were only technical validations or isolated case reports [23]. Limitations of that study included the unquantified timing of blood sampling relative to fever onset, lack of documentation regarding prior antibiotic use, and a small MT-positive subgroup (11 serologically confirmed cases, of which only 4 were PCR-positive), which reduced statistical power. Furthermore, it was not possible to rule out that results considered false negatives by PCR may have represented past infections with persistent positive serology.

In our study, two MT patients with positive real-time PCR results presented negative and inconclusive serology before day 60 from fever onset, with no follow-up serology available. One patient had an underlying lymphoproliferative syndrome, potentially delaying or diminishing the humoral response, as previously reported in immunocompromised individuals. In the other patient, serological testing was performed on days 10 and 33, possibly before seroconversion. These patients illustrate that a negative serology result does not necessarily exclude active infection when clinical and molecular findings are consistent. They also support the possibility of late seroconversion beyond 60 days. This underscores the importance of extended serological follow-up in patients with compatible clinical presentation and favourable response to antibiotic therapy. Future studies should include larger cohorts, prolonged follow-up, and cost–benefit analyses.

A key strength of this study lies in rigorous patient selection, with strict inclusion and exclusion criteria and careful elimination of alternative fever aetiologies. Although the CDC recommends PCR during the acute phase (ideally before or soon after antibiotic initiation) [26], previous studies neither excluded pre-PCR antibiotic use (quinolones/doxycycline) nor quantified diagnostic timing variables [17, 18, 21, 22]. In contrast, our molecular diagnoses always preceded antibiotic therapy, whereas serology followed treatment. This temporal misalignment may lead to therapeutic mismanagement (e.g. inappropriate or excessive antibiotic use) and promote bacterial resistance. Since both diseases typically require 7–14 days of doxycycline, clinical improvement often precedes serological testing, resulting in diagnostic attrition among patients lost to follow-up.

The main differences between serology and real-time PCR lie in the time to diagnosis and in the number of medical consultations and diagnostic tests required. Although real-time PCR demonstrated comparable diagnostic performance to serology in our study, with high sensitivity and specificity under strictly controlled conditions, these findings should be interpreted in light of the sample size and require confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

Nevertheless, PCR offers a practical advantage due to its shorter turnaround time, which may enable earlier microbiological confirmation and help avoid empirical treatment in patients without a confirmed diagnosis. The observed differences therefore support a complementary, stage-dependent use of molecular and serological techniques in specific clinical contexts. In particular, PCR may be especially useful in patients presenting with UFI lasting 5–10 days who attend emergency departments and in whom, after comprehensive evaluation, the cause of fever remains undetermined, as serology may not yet have become positive. Conversely, serology remains the reference method in patients with longer fever duration, generally exceeding 10 days.

An important limitation of our study is that serology was performed at variable time intervals, as described in the inclusion criteria, and most patients did not undergo serial serological testing beyond 60 days. This limits the ability to detect late seroconversion or serological false negatives. Future studies should include longer and more standardised serological follow-up for all patients to construct seroconversion curves and obtain stronger evidence regarding possible serological false negatives, as highlighted in our work. Another limitation is that this study represents a secondary analysis, which required excluding patients who did not strictly meet selection criteria. Consequently, the sample size is relatively small and the statistical power to detect subtle performance differences between diagnostic methods was limited.

Taken together, our findings indicate that combining real-time PCR and serology, when applied in appropriate clinical contexts, can optimise diagnostic accuracy during the early phase of disease and in patients with UFI. While real-time PCR appears particularly valuable within 5–10 days of fever onset and before antibiotic initiation, serology remains the reference standard at later stages. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardised follow-up are needed to confirm these observations and assess their applicability in broader clinical settings.

In conclusion, our results suggest that real-time PCR may provide higher early diagnostic confirmation compared to serology for MT and QF, particularly when performed before antibiotic administration and following the described real-time PCR algorithm. Implementing early diagnostic protocols in endemic areas could improve detection of confirmed cases and epidemiological accuracy, optimise resource use, prevent overtreatment, and reduce costs associated with multiple serological tests and prolonged follow-up, particularly in individuals with a conclusive molecular diagnosis.

Overall, our study supports the use of molecular diagnostics, particularly in emergency departments in endemic areas, for patients presenting with UFI of 5–10 days’ duration that remains undiagnosed after thorough evaluation. The use of real-time PCR in these individuals can reduce repeated consultations, unnecessary diagnostic testing, and therapeutic errors.
