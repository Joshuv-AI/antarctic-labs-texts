Mechanistic insights into the Bacillus subtilis K6 solid-state fermentation-induced structural transformation and enhanced bioactivity of flaxseed meal dietary fiber
Authors: Chunpeng Hou, Jiaxun Chen, Chenxian Yang, Xiaohui Geng, Ying Xin, Fusheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Flaxseed meal (FSM), an oil-processing byproduct rich in dietary fiber (DF), was subjected to solid-state fermentation (SSF) using Bacillus subtilis K6. This study systematically examined structural modifications and in vitro bioactivities of soluble (SDF) and insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) fractions before and after fermentation. SEM and FTIR analyses showed degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose during SSF, yielding a more porous structure. XRD and TGA indicated disrupted crystalline regions in SDF and IDF, with fermented IDF (F-IDF) exhibiting enhanced thermal stability. SSF altered monosaccharide composition and reduced DF molecular weight. Transcriptomics revealed pentose and glucuronate interconversions and starch and sucrose metabolism as primary DF dissociation pathways, dominantly mediated by extracellular hydrolases. In vitro assessments demonstrated F-IDF's superior glucose adsorption capacity, whereas fermented SDF (F-SDF) exhibited greater cholesterol adsorption, pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity, nitrite adsorption capacity, and sodium cholate binding capacity. These findings provide a theoretical basis for FSM-derived DF utilization.

Introduction

Flaxseed represents a commercially significant oil and industrial crop, highly valued for its seeds and fibrous constituents. More than 10 % of worldwide flaxseed production originates from China (Dong et al., 2020). Flaxseed is extensively cultivated in the northwestern and northern regions of China and has two distinct seed coat colorations: golden yellow and reddish brown. Flaxseeds are rich in a variety of nutrients, which makes them rapidly emerging biomasses for the production of sustainable dietary ingredients. Flaxseed meal (FSM), a byproduct of cold-pressed flaxseed oil production, retains most original nutrients, including proteins, polyunsaturated fatty acids, and DF, with compositional similarities to those of soybean and other oilseed meals (Lan et al., 2020). Despite its nutritional potential, FSM has long been undervalued as a low-grade animal feed. As a plant-derived agro-industrial residue, FSM (rich in DF) is a promising candidate for functional food development. DF is a heterogeneous mixture of complex carbohydrates and derivatives and serves as a critical functional ingredient in food systems, driving increasing market demand. Thus, valorizing FSM-derived DF is essential for the sustainable utilization of flaxseed.

Dietary fiber (DF) is an indispensable component of plant-based foods and comprises structural cell wall polysaccharides and intracellular carbohydrates. Epidemiological and experimental studies have reported its multifaceted health benefits, including metabolic regulation and gut microbiota modulation (Gill et al., 2020). DF is classified into soluble dietary fiber (SDF) and insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) fractions based on aqueous solubility. SDF has greater hydration capacity, viscosity, and fermentability. It also has higher gelation capacity and enhanced emulsification potential, facilitating its technological integration into food matrices (Jia et al., 2019). Thus, SDF demonstrates superior bioactivity and processing adaptability compared with IDF. However, natural plant cell walls predominantly contain IDF (Xiong et al., 2022), with crystalline cellulose microfibrils imparting structural rigidity that limits SDF accessibility. The optimal functionality of DF requires an SDF content exceeding 10 % to ensure favorable physicochemical and bioactive properties (Chen et al., 2020). This necessitates DF modification strategies to convert IDF into SDF, thereby enhancing its functionality.

Microbial fermentation is an eco-efficient and sustainable strategy for modifying DF. During fermentation, microorganisms enzymatically hydrolyze complex polysaccharides into simpler, bioactive metabolites, thereby increasing their functional properties (Chen et al., 2023). Besides increasing SDF content, fermentation induces structural reorganization of DF matrices, including alterations in porosity, surface morphology, and polymer conformation, which collectively modulate physicochemical and functional attributes. For example, a study (Meng et al., 2025) reported that fermentation of okara by Bacillus subtilis BSNK-5 increased the SDF content by 7.51-fold, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examinations revealed a decrease in surface rigidity in IDF and an increase in porosity in SDF. Prolonged fermentation (72 h) significantly increased the antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and hypolipidemic activities of DF. A related study (Wang et al., 2025) found that Bacillus velezensis-mediated fermentation of highland barley modified monosaccharide profiles, yielding SDF and IDF with looser architectures and higher surface area-to-volume ratios. These advancements highlight microbial fermentation as a viable approach to validate agro-industrial byproducts through targeted conversion of IDF into SDF, thereby increasing nutritional and functional value. In recent years, with the widespread application of omics technologies, significant breakthroughs have been made in research on the metabolic mechanisms of microorganisms toward different substrates. Wang et al. (Wang, Zhang, Liu, et al., 2024) fermented soybean meal using B. subtilis BS12, and their study demonstrated that endo-1,4-β-xylanase, endoglucanase, and β-1,4-mannan endonuclease played crucial roles in fiber degradation during fermentation. Lin et al. (Lin et al., 2025), via proteomics, identified a total of 22 proteins associated with IDF degradation, with the key enzyme systems primarily including cellobiohydrolase, endoglucanase, and β-glucosidase. The application of omics technologies enables in-depth elucidation of the dissociation mechanism of DF during the fermentation process.

This research focused on exploring the impact of B. subtilis K6 fermentation on the structural properties and bioactive functions of SDF and IDF extracted from FSM, and further identified the key enzymes emerging during the fermentation process using transcriptomic techniques. The results offer valuable insights that could contribute to the creation of high-value DF products derived from FSM. Furthermore, this study presents novel perspectives for advancing the development of microbial-modified DF and functional ingredients in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Flaxseeds were purchased from Inner Mongolia, China. Flaxseeds were cold-pressed and pulverized, followed by defatting and air-dried to yield FSM. The alkaline protease (2 × 105 U/g) and thermostable α-amylase (4 × 104 U/g) were procured from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sodium cholate, glucose and furfural were purchased from Shanghai McLean Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). O-phthalaldehyde was purchased from Beijing Boao Toda Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.

Bacillus subtilis K6 was deposited at the China Center for Type Culture Collection (NO: M 20231096), Wuhan, China.

Solid-state fermentation (SSF) conditions

Based on previous optimization results (Hou et al., 2025), SSF was conducted under the following conditions: fermentation time of 45 h, temperature of 40.5 °C, solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:0.65 (w/v), and inoculum size of 10 % (v/w, based on dry substrate weight).

Extracting SDF and IDF from FSM before and after fermentation

The unfermented and fermented FSM samples were denoted as U-FSM and F-FSM, respectively. The specific methods can refer to the study of Hou et al. (Hou et al., 2025). Briefly, flaxseed meal samples were mixed with water at a ratio of 1:20 (w/v). The pH was first adjusted to 6.5, followed by the addition of 1 % (w/w) thermostable α-amylase for enzymatic hydrolysis at 95 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the pH was adjusted to 9.5, and 1 % (w/w) alkaline protease was added for enzymatic hydrolysis at 65 °C for 2 h, after which the enzyme was inactivated in a boiling water bath for 10 min. After centrifugation at 5000 ×g for 10 min, four volumes of 95 % ethanol were added to the supernatant. The residue (IDF) was washed twice with water, and the aforementioned ethanol addition step was repeated. Following alcohol precipitation for 12 h, centrifugation was performed at 5000 ×g for 5 min to collect the precipitate (SDF). Both SDF and IDF were then freeze-dried for subsequent use. The unfermented SDF and IDF were respectively named CK-SDF and CK-IDF. The fermented SDF and IDF were respectively named F-SDF and F-IDF.

Determination of SDF and IDF contents and chemical composition before and after fermentation

The SDF and IDF contents were quantified following the AOAC official method 991.43 (Aoac., 1996). The protein and ash contents of the SDF and IDF samples were determined using the Chinese National Standards methods(GB5009.4–2016, 2016; GB5009.5–2025, 2025).

Structural characterization of DF

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

Fermented and unfermented SDF and IDF samples were homogenously mixed with pre-dried KBr powder at a 1:100 (w/w) ratio and ground into a uniform pellet. Then, FT-IR spectra were acquired using a Nicolet IS50 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) across the wavenumber range 4000–400 cm−1. Each spectrum represented an average of 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with background correction applied.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Samples were dried to a stable weight, affixed to stubs with conductive double-sided tape. After gold spraying treatment, the surface morphologies of SDF and IDF were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS Sigma 360, Germany).

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystal structure of the samples was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). Powdered samples were compacted into smooth, flat discs and scanned across a 2θ range of 2–60° at 0.02° resolution. The operating parameters included a voltage of 30 kV and a current of 20 mA.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermal stability was assessed using a TGA 4000 instrument (PerkinElmer, USA). About 5 mg of sample was heated from 30 °C to 600 °C at 20 °C/min.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

About 5 mg of SDF and IDF samples were hydrolyzed with 2 mol/L trifluoroacetic acid at 121 °C for 2 h in a sealed tube. The sample was dried under a stream of nitrogen. Subsequently, it was washed with methanol and dried again, with this procedure repeated 3 times. The remaining residue, after dissolution in deionized water and filtration through a 0.22 μm microporous filter, was subjected to subsequent determination. Sample detection and parameter settings were performed following the procedures described in the study by Zhang et al.(Zhang et al., 2025).

Molecular weight distribution (Mw)

SDF samples were dissolved in 0.1 M NaNO3 aqueous solution containing 0.02 % NaN3 at 1 mg/mL, followed by filtration through a 0.45-μm membrane. For IDF, the samples were dispersed in DMSO with 0.5 % (w/w) lithium bromide (DMSO/LiBr) at 1 mg/mL, prior to the same filtration step. Subsequently, the Mw was determined using SEC-MALLS-RI coupled with a DAWN HELEOS-II laser photometer (Wyatt Technology, USA).

Transcriptome analysis

The B. subtilis K6 strain, subjected to two successive subcultures, was inoculated into FSM substrate. The samples were collected after 8 h of fermentation and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from the samples, followed by gene amplification via PCR and verification through agarose gel electrophoresis. Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The reference genome of B. subtilis K6 was retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for subsequent alignment analysis.

Determination of the bioactive properties of DF

Glucose adsorption capacity (GAC)

About 0.1 g of sample was mixed with 5 mL of 0.5 mg/mL glucose solution and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h under constant agitation. After centrifugation (5000 ×g, 15 min), 1 mL of the supernatant was reacted with 2 mL of DNS reagent, boiled for 5 min, and diluted to 15 mL. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm against a blank.

GAC (mg/g) =c1−c2vm.

Here, c1 and c2 represent the initial and residual glucose concentrations (mg/mL), v represents the supernatant volume (mL), and m represents the sample mass (g).

Cholesterol absorption capacity (Mattedi et al.)

0.2 g of sample was added to the emulsion prepared by mixing 1 mL of fresh egg yolk and 9 mL of water. The solution pH was adjusted to pH 2 (gastric phase) and pH 7 (intestinal phase), respectively, and reacted with shaking at 37 °C for 3 h. After sufficient reaction, the solution was centrifuged (5000 ×g, 15 min), and 1 mL of supernatant was diluted with 9 mL of glacial acetic acid. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of the diluted liquid was mixed with 0.2 mL of o-phthaldialdehyde and 4 mL of mixed acid (H₂SO₄:CH₃COOH = 1:1, v/v) for reaction. After 10 min of reaction, the absorbance was measured at 550 nm.

CAC (mg/g) =m2−m1m0

Here, m2, m1, and m0 denote the cholesterol content preadsorption, postadsorption, and sample mass (g), respectively.

Pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity (PLIC)

A mixture containing 0.25 g of sample, 5 mL of soybean oil, 25 mL of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.2), and 5 mL of lipase solution (25 U/mL) was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The enzymatic activity was stopped by boiling (5 min), and the liberated fatty acids were titrated with 0.1 M NaOH, with phenolphthalein used as an indicator.

PLIC(%)=V1−V2V1×100

Here, V1 and V2 represent the NaOH volume (mL) consumed in the control and sample groups, respectively.

Nitrite ion adsorption capacity (NIAC)

0.2 g of sample was mixed with 20 mL of 20 μg/mL NaNO₂ solution at pH 2 and pH 7, respectively, followed by shaking reaction at 37 °C for 2 h. After the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged (5000 ×g, 15 min), and 1 mL of supernatant was taken to determine the nitrite ion content using the N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride method.

NIAC (μg/g) =m2−m1m0

Here, m2, m1, and m0 represent the nitrite content preadsorption, postadsorption (μg), and sample mass (g), respectively.

Sodium cholate adsorption capacity (SCAC)

The SCAC was measured by incubating 0.2 g of sample with 20 mL of sodium cholate solution (1 mg/mL, pH 7) at 37 °C for 3 h. After centrifugation (5000 ×g, 15 min), 1 mL of supernatant was mixed with 6 mL of H2SO4 (45 %) and 1 mL of furfural (0.3 %), heated at 65 °C for 30 min, cooled, and measured at 620 nm.

SCAC (mg/g) =m2−m1m0

Here, m2, m1, and m0 denote the cholate content preadsorption, postadsorption, and sample mass (g), respectively.

Statistical analysis

The experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n ≥ 3). Origin 2024 and IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 were used for plotting and statistical analysis. p < 0.05 was considered a significance level.

Results and discussion

Analysis of SDF and IDF contents and chemical composition before and after fermentation

The changes in the contents of SDF and IDF in FSM before and after fermentation are shown in Fig. 1A. After fermentation, the content of SDF increased from 24.43 ± 0.70 % to 33.45 ± 0.24 %, with a relative increase of 36.92 %. Meanwhile, the content of IDF decreased from 40.77 ± 0.20 % to 28.27 ± 0.13 %, with a relative decrease of 30.66 %. Further compositional analysis (Fig. 1B) revealed that the protein contents in SDF and IDF decreased from 19.25 ± 0.06 % to 15.97 ± 0.14 % and from 14.40 ± 0.09 % to 13.24 ± 0.17 %, respectively. The alteration in protein content led to fluctuations in ash content, which remained at relatively low levels. For IDF, the distinct peak at 2923 cm−1 represented CH2 stretching in methylene groups, confirming the typical polysaccharide structure. Peaks at 1743–1740 cm−1 and 1605 cm−1 corresponded to free carboxyl groups or CO stretching vibrations, with variations in intensity linked to uronic acid content (Rezvani & Goli, 2023). The absorption at 1424 cm−1 corresponded to C—H vibrations in the benzene rings of lignin, whereas the peaks at 1367 cm−1 and 1260 cm−1 were characteristic of C—O stretching in cellulose and hemicellulose. The absorption peak at 1057–1045 cm−1, arising from C—O group stretching vibrations in C-O-C structures of hemicellulose sugar rings, is generally found in arabinoxylan or xylan (Wang et al., 2022). Serving as a characteristic peak for carbohydrates, variations in their absorption intensity may correlate with differences in monosaccharide composition. The weak absorption at 896 cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of β-glycosidic bonds in cellulose, which is consistent with its crystalline structural features. Compared with CK-IDF, the absorption peak intensities of fermented F-IDF at 1057.1 cm−1 were significantly decreased, which indicates that fermentation causes partial cleavage of the C—O bonds in insoluble hemicellulose.

XRD analysis

The SDF and IDF samples exhibited typical cellulose type I diffraction peaks (Fig. 3B). Characteristic diffraction peaks for CK-SDF and F-SDF were detected at a diffraction angle of 2θ = 18.82°, whereas those for CK-IDF and F-IDF were observed at 2θ = 21.52°. These findings indicated that fermentation did not alter the crystalline structure type of SDF or IDF but rather influenced their diffraction intensities (Tang et al., 2023). Within the 2θ range of 5–60°, F-SDF displayed a significantly lower diffraction intensity than CK-SDF, which was attributed to the degradation of crystalline substances. This phenomenon occurred probably due to microbial secretion of hydrolytic enzymes during SSF, which disrupted intermolecular bonds in macromolecules and converted crystalline cellulose into amorphous cellulose (Yang et al., 2024); this led to a decrease in crystallinity due to changes in cellulose crystal morphology. In contrast, F-IDF exhibited a higher diffraction intensity than CK-IDF in the 2θ range of 18.52–24.92°, which can be attributed to the decomposition of thermally unstable amorphous regions in IDF, such as hemicellulose and cellulose (Xie et al., 2021), or to an increase in intermolecular forces induced by fermentation that promoted tighter, more ordered alignment of molecular chains.

TGA analysis

The pyrolysis of the SDF and IDF samples occurred in three distinct stages (Fig. 3C). The first stage (30–180 °C) involved minimal mass loss, with similar trends for all samples: CK-SDF and F-SDF lost 10.13 % and 10.36 %, respectively, whereas CK-IDF and F-IDF lost 8.75 % and 7.67 %, respectively, of the total mass loss, primarily due to the evaporation of free and bound water in the DF matrix. The second stage (180–380 °C) resulted in pronounced mass loss: for SDF, this loss was attributed to polysaccharide chain cleavage and degradation (Huang et al., 2021), whereas for IDF, it resulted from the thermal decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose. The third stage (380–600 °C) represented the carbonization phase, where biomass decomposition near completion and mass loss stabilized as residual organic matter underwent slow pyrolysis. For the IDF samples, this stage primarily involved lignin degradation, as reported in another study (Zhao et al., 2016). The final residue yields after pyrolysis were 19.89 % (CK-SDF), 18.53 % (F-SDF), 14.28 % (CK-IDF), and 19.22 % (F-IDF).

Comparative analysis revealed that F-SDF exhibited slightly lower thermal stability than unfermented CK-SDF, probably due to structural disruptions caused by SSF, which increased susceptibility to thermal degradation. In contrast, F-IDF showed marginally greater thermal stability than CK-IDF, possibly because of the formation of new intermolecular bonds or interactions during fermentation that increased resistance to thermal decomposition—a finding that is consistent with the XRD results indicating tighter molecular packing in F-IDF.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

Polysaccharides, which are complex carbohydrates composed of 10 or more covalently linked monosaccharide units, play various roles in plants, including energy storage, structural support, and cell wall formation. Cellulose, a linear polymer of β-(1 → 4)-linked glucose, is a primary component of plant cell walls and provides mechanical strength and rigidity (Miao et al., 2022). Hemicellulose, the second most abundant polysaccharide in nature after cellulose, is derived from plants as non-cellulosic components of primary and secondary cell walls. Pectin, a structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls, consists primarily of galacturonic acid chains cross-linked with neutral monosaccharides (e.g., rhamnose, galactose, arabinose) (Zhu et al., 2019).

The HPLC chromatographic profiles of the 13 monosaccharide standards and DF samples are presented in Fig. 4. The monosaccharide compositions of SDF and IDF, as determined by ion chromatography (Table 1), revealed seven common sugars, including fucose, arabinose, rhamnose, galactose, glucose, xylose, and galacturonic acid, differing only in relative abundance. The dominant monosaccharides in CK-SDF—xylose, rhamnose, galactose, and arabinose—suggested that SDF primarily comprises hemicellulose and pectin (Alba et al., 2020). In F-SDF, xylose, glucose, galactose, and rhamnose were predominant, with significantly higher glucose and galactose levels but lower arabinose, rhamnose, and galacturonic acid levels following B. subtilis K6 fermentation. The increase in glucose, a major component of cellulose, indicated an increase in cellulose degradation in the cell wall during fermentation, whereas the increase in galactose (a hemicellulose marker) suggested the disruption of hemicellulosic structures. Reductions in arabinose, rhamnose, and galacturonic acid occurred probably due to the enzymatic breakdown of hemicellulose or pectin structures by microbial secretions, releasing these monosaccharides as carbon sources for microbial metabolism. Mw analysis

Molecular weight (Mw) is a critical parameter influencing the functional activity of SDF. Generally, higher Mw is associated with increased aggregation, reduced solubility, and elevated viscosity (Dong et al., 2022). The Mw values of the SDF and IDF samples ranged from 486.413 to 1360.590 kDa (Table 2). Following microbial fermentation, the Mw of SDF decreased from 796.643 kDa to 486.413 kDa, whereas that of IDF showed a more pronounced reduction from 1360.590 kDa to 511.657 kDa. The Mw and solubility of SDF are key determinants of its ability to promote probiotic growth. High-Mw SDF has a lower water-binding capacity, which limits its transport across cell membranes and impairs microbial utilization. In contrast, low-Mw SDF with improved solubility facilitates enhanced probiotic proliferation by overcoming diffusion barriers and providing readily accessible substrates for metabolic activity. These findings highlight the importance of molecular weight modification in optimizing the functional properties of DFs for applications in gut health.

Transcriptomics to elucidate the dissociation mechanism of DF by B. subtilis K6

To better explore the enzymes involved in DF dissociation secreted by B. subtilis K6 during the fermentation of flaxseed meal, samples fermented for 8 h were analyzed. Firstly, the average length of RNA gene sequences of B. subtilis K6 was approximately 1500 bp (Fig. S1), the proportions of correct base recognition rates at Q20 and Q30 reached over 99 % and 96 % of the sequences, respectively (Table S1, S2), indicating high sequencing accuracy. As shown in Table S3, the proportion of the gene sequences obtained in this experiment that could be mapped to the reference genome all exceeded 70 %, meeting the requirements for transcriptome sequencing and thus being applicable for subsequent analyses. Gene expression correlation analysis revealed that the Pearson correlation coefficients (r) among replicates within both the CK and F groups exceeded 0.8, indicative of high intra-group consistency (Fig. 5A). Differential gene expression between groups was determined using DESeq2 with rigorous thresholds (adjusted p-value ≤0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). The upregulated enzymes involved in DF dissociation were primarily categorized into five major classes: oxidoreductases, lyases, transferases, hydrolases, and isomerases. Among these, hydrolases emerged as the key enzymes responsible for DF degradation by B. subtilis K6. For instance, α-1,4-polygalacturonase (lplD) facilitated the structural remodeling of DF by cleaving the α-1,4-glycosidic linkages in the pectin backbone. β-Fructofuranosidase (sacA) catalyzed the hydrolysis of β-fructofuranosidic bonds, while levansucrase (levB) decomposed levans into fructooligosaccharides or monosaccharides. β-Mannanase, an endo-hemicellulase targeting β-1,4-D-mannosidic linkages, acted on galactomannans, glucomannans, and mannans to produce mannooligosaccharides and trace amounts of mannose. Additionally, α-galactosidase (melA) and β-galactosidase (yesZ) catalyzed the hydrolysis of α- and β-galactosidic bonds, respectively, yielding monosaccharides. Based on the aforementioned action sites of different enzymes and the glycosidic bonds they cleave, it can be concluded that during fermentation, B. subtilis K6 secretes enzymes including lplD (gene id: BSU07130), gmuG (gene id: BSU05880), mdxD (gene id: BSU34620), sacA (gene id: BSU38040), and levB (gene id: BSU34460), which are capable of disrupting the main structure of DF. In contrast, other enzymes secreted by B. subtilis K6, such as melA (gene id: BSU30300), yesZ (gene id: BSU07080), sacC (gene id: BSU27030), araA (gene id: BSU28800), and araD (gene id: BSU28780) can efficiently decompose the branched structures of DF, leading to the full disintegration of DF architectures. Significant changes in the microstructural properties of DF after fermentation affect the monosaccharide composition and molecular weight distribution of SDF and IDF, and play a crucial role in regulating their bioactivity and solubility (Liu, Tang, et al., 2021). Studies have demonstrated that arabinoxylans with a higher arabinose/xylose ratio typically exhibit a higher branching degree, greater solubility, and higher viscosity, resulting in a slower rate of fermentation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (Rose et al., 2009). Higher oil-holding capacity and viscosity of SDF enable it to bind more lipids and cholesterol through chelation, thereby effectively restricting bile acid circulation and exerting a hypolipidemic effect (Jia et al., 2019). Furthermore, Guo et al. (Guo et al., 2018) reported that galactose exerts a positive effect on the inhibition of lipase. Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2019) also found that the contents of galactose and arabinose influence the binding of DF to sodium cholate, with higher contents of these monosaccharides leading to stronger binding affinity.

Based on GO and KEGG metabolic pathway analyses, the process by which B. subtilis K6 degrades DF is inferred as follows (Fig. 6). First, the two-component system perceives signals from the presence of DF or its degradation products via sensor kinases, activates response regulators, and thereby regulates the expression of related genes. Subsequently, B. subtilis K6 secretes extracellular hydrolases, which play a key role extracellularly by cleaving glycosidic bonds in different components of DF, further promoting the decomposition of DF macromolecules. Thereafter, transferases mediate transmembrane transport of the hydrolytic products; simultaneously, under the synergistic effect of the Phosphotransferase System, they phosphorylate the substrates, facilitating subsequent metabolism. Both F-SDF and F-IDF exhibited slightly greater glucose adsorption capacities than their unfermented counterparts (CK-SDF and CK-IDF), indicating that microbial fermentation promotes glucose adsorption. F-IDF displayed stronger glucose adsorption than F-SDF, which may be attributed to the greater specific surface area of fermented IDF, which allows more glucose molecules to be adsorbed onto its surface. Alternatively, the higher crystallinity of F-SDF might hinder glucose penetration into its internal structure (Aguado et al., 2019), contributing to the higher GAC of F-IDF. Liu et al. CAC of DF from fermented flaxseed meal

The cholesterol adsorption capacity (Mattedi et al., 2023) is a key indicator for evaluating the in vitro hypolipidemic potential of DF. Excessive dietary cholesterol intake is detrimental to health, as its overabsorption and accumulation in the body can lead to obesity, coronary heart disease, and fatty liver. The CAC of SDF and IDF are presented in Fig. 7 (B). At pH 2, the cholesterol adsorption capacities of CK-SDF, F-SDF, CK-IDF, and F-IDF were 2.18 ± 0.18 mg/g, 6.78 ± 0.33 mg/g, 1.32 ± 0.2 mg/g, and 1.95 ± 0.26 mg/g, respectively. At pH 7, the corresponding values were 4.25 ± 0.23 mg/g, 10.76 ± 0.24 mg/g, 2.41 ± 0.18 mg/g, and 3.91 ± 0.13 mg/g, respectively. These results indicate that microbial fermentation enhances the CAC of DF, with most of the cholesterol adsorption occurring under intestinal conditions. Findings herein correspond to those of Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2011), indicating that the CAC of bran DF at pH 2.0 (2.2 mg/g) was significantly lower than the value at pH 7.0 (3.5 mg/g). Additionally, Liang et al. (Liang et al., 2023) reported CAC values for F-SDF from Penicillium expansum-fermented okara of 6.69 ± 0.09 mg/g at pH 2.0 and 7.62 ± 0.10 mg/g at pH 7.0.

Among all samples, F-SDF presented the highest cholesterol adsorption efficiency under intestinal conditions. In a neutral environment, the carboxyl groups in SDF dissociate into carboxylate anions, which bind to cholesterol, thereby improving the adsorption capacity of F-SDF (Ji et al., 2024). As the pH decreases, the partial positive charges on cholesterol and F-SDF repel hydrogen ions, weakening the adsorption forces (Gu et al., 2020). Additionally, the high water-holding capacity and swelling ability of SDF allow it to form a gel-like structure, which traps cholesterol and reduces its absorption in the human body.

PLIC of DF from fermented flaxseed meal

The hypolipidemic potential of DF was evaluated by conducting an in vitro pancreatic lipase inhibition assay, which quantifies the capacity to delay fatty acid liberation by impeding triglyceride hydrolysis into free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols, thereby suppressing the resynthesis and deposition of lipids. As shown in Fig. 7 (C), the lipase inhibitory activities of CK-SDF, F-SDF, CK-IDF, and F-IDF were 11.3 ± 0.33 %, 19.19 ± 0.32 %, 6.67 ± 0.17 %, and 9.82 ± 0.16 %, respectively. Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2024) found that mixed fermentation of navel orange peel with Trichoderma viride and Aspergillus niger conferred a PLIC of 22.08 ± 0.38 % to TASDF, whereas single-strain fermentations with T. viride and A. niger yielded TSDF and ASDF with PLIC values of 17.75 ± 0.25 % and 17.75 ± 0.25 %, respectively.

Variations in the microstructural attributes and surface functionality of DF directly modulate the efficacy of lipase inhibition. F-SDF exhibited greater inhibitory capacity, attributable to its greater specific surface area, reduced molecular weight, and porous matrix. These structural modifications improve the entrapment of lipid substrates and lipase enzymes within the polymer network, sterically hindering enzyme-substrate interactions and amplifying inhibitory effects.

NIAC of DF from fermented flaxseed meal

Dietary fiber (DF) can mitigate carcinogenic risks through the adsorption of excess nitrite ions (NO2−) in the human gastrointestinal tract. As shown in Fig. 7 (D), the sodium nitrite (NaNO2) adsorption capacities of CK-SDF, F-SDF, CK-IDF, and F-IDF at pH 2 were 156 ± 5.66, 278.5 ± 12.02, 92.68 ± 5.77, and 124.5 ± 2.12 μg/g, respectively. At pH 7, the adsorption capacities decreased to 38.1 ± 2.97 μg/g, 66.05 ± 4.17 μg/g, 19.4 ± 1.41 μg/g, and 29.35 ± 1.48 μg/g, respectively. Both SDF and IDF demonstrated greater nitrite adsorption at pH 2, which was likely mediated by active acidic groups (e.g., phenolic acids) on the DF matrix. In acidic environments, protonation of NO2− to nitrous acid (HNO2) facilitates rapid electrophilic interactions with electron-rich phenolic hydroxyl groups, enabling efficient scavenging of NO2− (Zhang et al., 2019). This pH-dependent behavior suggests a chemisorption-dominated mechanism, which operates primarily under gastric conditions. Ji et al. (Ji et al., 2024) demonstrated that after mixed culture fermentation of blue honeysuckle berries, the NIAC of CF-SDF was 3.75 ± 0.41 mg/g at pH 2 and 2.17 ± 0.26 mg/g at pH 7. Wang et al. (Wang, Zhang, Wei, et al., 2024) reported that lactic acid bacteria-fermented flaxseed meal-derived F-SDF exhibited NIAC values of 134.41 ± 0.02 μg/g at pH 2 and 43.05 ± 0.02 μg/g at pH 7.

Post-fermentation structural modifications in F-SDF, including increased porosity and the release of bound phenolic acids (e.g., ferulic acid), probably increased the accessibility of reactive sites and the intramolecular diffusion of NO2−. Concurrently, the increased specific surface area of F-IDF contributed to improved adsorption capacity. At pH 7, reduced nitrite adsorption by DF was observed, which may be attributed to the dissociation of carboxyl groups at higher pH, leading to increased negative charge density on the IDF surface and electrostatic repulsion against NO2−, thus promoting the release of nitrite (Shaikh et al., 2019).

SCAC of DF from fermented flaxseed meal

The binding of DF to sodium cholate represents an indirect pathway for cholesterol and lipid reduction, where stronger sequestration of bile salts by DF accelerates the conversion of cholesterol to bile acids, thereby enhancing the hypocholesterolemic and lipid-lowering efficacy. As shown in Fig. 7 (E), the SCAC of CK-SDF, F-SDF, CK-IDF, and F-IDF were 3.01 ± 0.27 mg/g, 9.55 ± 0.06 mg/g, 1.78 ± 0.03 mg/g, and 3.28 ± 0.14 mg/g, respectively. Ji et al. (Ji et al., 2024) revealed that the SCAC of CF-SDF after fermentation was approximately 6.8 mg/g. Meng et al. (Meng et al., 2025) reported that when okara was fermented by B. subtilis BSNK-5, the SCAC values of IDF and SDF peaked at 48 h, reaching 35.77 mg/g and 36.87 mg/g, respectively.

Previous studies have found a strong correlation between the bile salt-binding capacity of DF and its particle size distribution or microstructural characteristics (Yu et al., 2022). The significantly greater adsorption capacity of F-SDF than that of the CK-SDF and IDF fractions may be attributed to the structural loosening and an increase in surface area of SDF post-fermentation, which exposes additional binding sites for sodium cholate interactions.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the structural and functional modifications of SDF and IDF in FSM induced by B. subtilis K6 SSF. The results revealed that fermentation effectively reconfigured the molecular architecture of SDF and IDF, establishing a structural foundation for enhanced bioactivity. Monosaccharide profiling and molecular weight distribution analyses revealed the enzymatic degradation of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, accompanied by the cleavage of glycosidic bonds and reduced polymer molecular weights, further confirming the microbially driven depolymerization of DF matrices. XRD analysis confirmed the preservation of the crystalline structures in both fractions after fermentation. TGA revealed different thermal behaviors: F-SDF exhibited marginally reduced thermal stability, whereas F-IDF displayed improved heat resistance, suggesting distinct applicability in thermal food processing. Transcriptomic analysis was conducted on samples from the CK and F groups, and two pathways most relevant to DF dissociation were screened from the KEGG-enriched pathways, namely pentose and glucuronate interconversions and starch and sucrose metabolism. In-depth investigation of enzymes within these metabolic pathways revealed that extracellular hydrolases secreted by B. subtilis K6 play a dominant role in the DF dissociation process. These hydrolases can efficiently cleave glycosidic bonds within DF, converting IDF to SDF. This not only increases the content of SDF but also induces significant changes in the microstructure of both SDF and IDF after fermentation. In vitro bioactivity assessments revealed functional differences between the fermented fractions. F-SDF showed greater cholesterol-binding capacity (pH 7), pancreatic lipase inhibition, and adsorption of sodium nitrite (pH 2) and bile salts, which was attributable to its modified surface functionality and porosity. In contrast, F-IDF exhibited enhanced GAC, probably due to an increase in specific surface area, positioning it as a candidate for glycemic regulation. These findings highlight the potential of fermented SDF and IDF in modulating lipid metabolism and gut health, respectively. This study provided a novel biotechnological strategy for valorizing FSM into high-value functional ingredients and advanced the theoretical framework for microbial fermentation-mediated modification of DF. Future studies should prioritize the determination of enzyme-specific mechanisms driving structural alterations and the establishment of quantitative structure-activity relationships to accelerate the industrial translation of fermented DF in functional foods.
