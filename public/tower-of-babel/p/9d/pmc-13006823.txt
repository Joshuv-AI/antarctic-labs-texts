Plant cell surface receptors
Authors: Bruno Pok Man Ngou, Yasuhiro Kadota, Ken Shirasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPlants face constant environmental changes and must integrate external and internal cues to coordinate growth, development, reproduction, and stress responses. A major strategy is perception at the cell surface via a large, diverse network of receptors. Here, we outline how these receptors recognise extracellular signals and assemble active complexes with appropriate co‐receptors. Diverse ectodomain structures enable the recognition of peptides and proteins, glycans, lipids, phytohormones, and other small molecules, as well as changes in cell wall status. We then summarise the downstream pathways, highlighting how cytosolic kinase domains couple to receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases, MAPK modules and other signalling components, and how timing, partner choice, and cellular context confer specificity to produce distinct physiological outputs across diverse processes. Finally, we discuss the origin and evolution of cell surface receptors. Receptor‐like kinases share a single origin and significantly diversified around the emergence of land plants to support new functions. Together, this perception system repeatedly adapted to new roles and point to opportunities to reprogramme cell surface receptors for resilience and crop improvement.Keywords: Cell‐surface receptor, Evolution, Plant biology, Plant signaling

INTRODUCTION

Living organisms must sense their surroundings and translate external cues into appropriate responses. In bacteria and other prokaryotes, plasma membrane‐localised chemoreceptors and response regulators work as canonical two‐component systems that monitor and react to environmental changes (Papon & Stock, 2019). In animals, multiple receptor families, such as G protein‐coupled receptors, receptor tyrosine kinases and toll‐like receptors, detect hormones, growth factors, and microbial molecules, respectively (Kawai et al., 2024; Lemmon & Schlessinger, 2010; Zhang et al., 2024). By contrast, plant perception is dominated by cell surface receptors, mainly receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) and receptor‐like proteins (RLPs). RLKs are composed of an N‐terminal signal peptide and extracellular ectodomain, a transmembrane helix, and a cytosolic kinase domain. RLKs are a monophyletic family with a conserved kinase core and are among the largest gene families in plants (Shiu & Bleecker, 2001; Shiu & Bleecker, 2003). RLPs are receptors that lack kinase domain, and many arose from RLKs and signal through co‐receptor kinases (Ngou et al., 2024). Together, RLKs and RLPs perceive peptides, full‐length proteins, glycans, phytohormones, and other small molecules, as well as changes in cell wall status. In this review, we cover how different ectodomains perceive diverse ligands to assemble active receptor complexes. We then summarise the signalling pathways of well‐characterised cell surface receptors, highlighting how timing, choice of signalling components and cellular context confer specificity. Finally, we discuss the origin and diversification of cell surface receptors, including ectodomain diversification, receptor neo‐functionalisation, and subsequent expansion and network rewiring that enable closely related receptors to control distinct biological processes. We propose that plant cell surface receptors represent a modular and evolutionarily flexible perception system, repeatedly redeployed across immunity, development, symbiosis, and reproduction (Box 1).

PERCEPTION OF LIGAND BY CELL SURFACE RECEPTORS

Perception of ligands by cell surface receptors is dependent on their ectodomain, which can bind to distinct classes of extracellular molecules. Leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) ectodomains recognise diverse peptides, proteins and small molecules, including phytocytokines; LysM domains perceive glycans such as chitooligosaccharides (COs) and peptidoglycans; lectin (G‐ and L‐type) binds to lipids, peptides and extracellular nucleotides; malectin‐like domains can engage with both pectin and peptide ligands; galacturonan‐binding domains bind to oligogalacturonides (OGs); and DUF26 ectodomains might sense diverse molecules (Boutrot & Zipfel, 2017; Ngou, Ding, & Jones, 2022). Following ligand binding, receptors mostly form heteromeric assemblies with co‐receptors or homodimerise to induce downstream responses. Here, we summarise cell surface receptors with defined ligands across diverse biological processes (Figure 1), and compile representative structures of receptor ectodomains bound to their cognate ligands to illustrate the molecular basis of ligand perception and receptor complex assembly (Figure 2).

Perception of molecules from pathogens

Cell surface receptors that perceive molecules from pathogens are referred to as pattern‐recognition receptors (PRRs) (Zipfel, 2014). LRR–RLKs are classified into 20 subclasses based on their kinase domain. Within this group, subclasses XI and XII of the LRR receptor‐like kinases (LRR–RLKs) perceive both self‐ and non‐self‐peptides to activate immunity. In Arabidopsis, Flagellin‐Sensing 2 (FLS2; LRR‐RLK‐XII) binds the flagellin epitope flg22 via its LRR ectodomain and, upon ligand binding, recruits BAK1/SERK co‐receptors to form an active heterodimer (Chinchilla et al., 2006; Chinchilla et al., 2007; Sun, Li, et al., 2013). Similarly, elongation factor receptor (EFR; LRR‐RLK‐XII) perceives the elf18 peptide; the endogenous damage peptides (AtPeps) are sensed by AtPEPR1/2 (LRR‐RLK‐XI); and the phytocytokine SCOOP peptides are perceived by AtMIK2 (LRR‐RLK‐XII) (Hou et al., 2021; Jia et al., 2024; Rhodes et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2024; Yamaguchi et al., 2006; Yamaguchi et al., 2010; Zipfel et al., 2006). These ectodomains bind their cognate peptide ligands and form heterodimers with SERK co‐receptors (Figure 2) (Jia et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2024). Other examples of LRR‐RLK‐XII include CORE and SCORE, which perceive cold‐shock protein peptides; Xa21, which perceives the microbial sulphated peptide RaxX; and PERU, which recognises the pep13 peptide from Phytophthora species (Ngou et al., 2025; Pruitt et al., 2015; Torres Ascurra et al., 2023; Wang, Albert, et al., 2016).

LRR receptor‐like proteins (LRR‐RLPs) lack a cytosolic kinase domain and therefore depend on two co‐receptor kinases, SOBIR1 and SERKs, for activation (Liebrand et al., 2013). Most LRR‐RLPs involved in pathogen sensing contain island domains (IDs) followed by four LRR motifs (known as C3 region) to facilitate ligand perception and co‐receptor recruitment (Fritz‐Laylin et al., 2005). In tomato, Cladosporium fulvum resistance (Cf) proteins perceive apoplastic effectors from the fungal pathogen Cladosporium fulvum (Dixon et al., 1996; Jones et al., 1994; Thomas et al., 1997). In Arabidopsis, RLP23 recognises the nlp20 peptide from necrosis‐ and ethylene‐inducing proteins (NLPs) from a broad range of pathogens (Böhm et al., 2014). LRR‐RLPs, such as RLP23 and RXEG1, form a constitutive interaction with SOBIR1 via their transmembrane helices (Sun et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018). Upon ligand perception, the ID and C3 region undergo conformational changes and recruit SERKs for activation (Figure 2) (Albert et al., 2015; Snoeck et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2022).

The G‐type lectin ectodomains bind to hydrophobic microbial lipids. For example, Arabidopsis LORE detects medium‐chain 3‐hydroxy fatty acids (3‐OH‐C10:0) from Gram‐negative bacteria, whereas RDA2 recognises a 9‐methyl‐branched sphingolipid from oomycete pathogens (Kato et al., 2022; Kutschera et al., 2019; Ranf et al., 2015). LORE forms constitutive oligomers, and 3‐OH‐C10:0 perception may promote conformational changes that activate the receptor complex (Eschrig et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2020). Whether LORE and RDA2 require co‐receptors, or rely solely on ligand‐induced receptor oligomerisation for activation, remains to be determined.

L‐type lectin receptors perceive ligands of varied chemical nature. DORN1/P2K1 is the receptor for extracellular ATP (eATP); eATP binding to the lectin ectodomain activates DORN1 (Chen et al., 2017; Choi et al., 2014). LecRK‐I.8 binds eNAD+/eNADP+ and is required for NAD‐induced local and systemic immunity (Wang et al., 2017). By contrast, LecRK‐V.5 and LecRK‐V.6 are required for the recognition of a nematode trehalase–derived peptide (Iino et al., 2025). The mechanism by which ligand binding activates these receptors, and whether co‐receptors are involved, remains unclear.

Cysteine‐rich RLKs (CRKs) carry two DUF26 modules in their ectodomains and their expressions are broadly induced by biotic and oxidative stresses (Vaattovaara et al., 2019). A recent report suggests that the Arabidopsis CRK7 and CRK43 are required to perceive wall teichoic acids (WTAs) from Gram‐positive bacteria (Pierdzig et al., 2025). At present, other validated ligands for CRKs are still lacking (Figure 2).

Wall‐associated kinases possess galacturonan‐binding ectodomains and have long been proposed to act as receptors for OGs released from damaged cell wall (Decreux & Messiaen, 2005). WAK ectodomains bind pectin, and chimeric receptor experiments suggested that the WAK1 ectodomain can confer OG responsiveness (Brutus et al., 2010; Kohorn & Kohorn, 2012). However, recent genetic analyses suggest that OG‐induced immune responses are largely WAK‐independent; Other PRRs that sense OGs remain to be discovered (Herold et al., 2024). In addition to WAK‐RLKs, the LRR‐RLK‐VIII‐2 IGP1/CORK and related receptors have been shown to bind and perceive β‐glucans, such as cellotriose and mixed‐linked glucans (Fernández‐Calvo et al., 2024; Jiménez‐Sandoval et al., 2025; Martín‐Dacal et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2025). Thus, damage to the plant cell wall may be sensed by multiple classes of cell surface receptors.

Lysin‐motif (LysM) receptors perceive microbial cell wall glycans of various chain lengths. In Arabidopsis, LYK5 binds long‐chain chitooligosaccharides (CO8) and recruits CERK1, while LYK4 also contributes to receptor complex activation to initiate immune signalling (Cao et al., 2014; Mittendorf et al., 2024; Miya et al., 2007; Wan et al., 2012). In rice, OsCEBiP is a LysM‐RLP that also binds chitin and recruits OsCERK1 for signal transduction (Kaku et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2016; Shimizu et al., 2010). In parallel, bacterial peptidoglycans are perceived by LYM1/LYM3 with CERK1 (Willmann et al., 2011).

Perception of molecules from symbiotic partners

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi release short‐chain COs (CO4/CO5) into the rhizosphere, and plants also perceive these molecules with LysM receptors. As mentioned above, CO8 and longer chitin fragments are perceived by OsCEBiP and signal with OsCERK1 to activate immunity, whereas OsMYR1 (OsLYK2) perceives CO4 and forms heterodimer with OsCERK1 (He et al., 2019). This receptor complex activates the common symbiosis pathway rather than immunity. In addition, the perception of short‐chain COs dampens CO8‐induced immunity thereby facilitating AM colonisation (Feng et al., 2021; Zhang, Dong, et al., 2015; Zhang, He, et al., 2021).

Both rhizobia and AM fungi produce lipochitooligosaccharides (LCOs), whose acyl chains and terminal modifications determine their host range (Gysel et al., 2021). In legumes, the LysM‐RLKs NFR1 and NFR5 (NFP) act as LCO receptors. LCO perception promotes the heterodimerisation of NFR1 and NFR5 to initiate symbiosis signalling (Broghammer et al., 2012; Madsen et al., 2003; Radutoiu et al., 2003; Radutoiu et al., 2007; Rübsam et al., 2023). In parallel, closely related LysM receptors, such as the Solanaceaous LYK10, perceive Myc‐LCOs produced by AM fungi (Buendia et al., 2016). In summary, LysM receptors perceive COs of various chain lengths and structural modifications, enabling plants to distinguish between symbionts and pathogens. In addition to CO/LCO perception, plants also deploy LysM‐RLKs such as EPR3 from Lotus japonicus to perceive rhizobial exopolysaccharides (EPS) as a compatibility checkpoint during infection‐thread development (Kawaharada et al., 2015; Wong et al., 2020). In addition, EPR3a to sense fungal branched glucans to facilitates intracellular accommodation of AM fungi (Kelly et al., 2023).

Across angiosperms, LysM receptors have diversified to recognise either COs of different chain lengths to initiate distinct downstream responses (Gysel et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2012; Zhang, He, et al., 2021). Solved structures of chitin‐bound LysM ectodomains illustrate the molecular basis of how different GlcNAc oligomer lengths are perceived (Figure 2). Most LysM‐RLKs contain three tandem LysM ectodomains. In legumes, comparative structure–function analyses show that two motifs within the first LysM ectodomain (LysM1) are major determinants of ligand specificity and thus discriminate receptors activating immunity from those mediating symbiotic signalling (Bozsoki et al., 2020). In contrast, the CHIP‐type LysM‐RLKs utilise the second and third LysM ectodomains (LysM2/3) to perceive long‐chain COs, whereas LysM1 of CERK‐like receptors contributes to receptor assembly and signalling complex formation (Gysel et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings support a model in which LysM‐RLKs deploy different LysM domains to distinguish among COs, thereby assembling different receptor complexes that activate appropriate downstream pathways.

Perception of molecules involving growth and development

Plant cytokinin receptors are histidine kinases (HKs) that are not phylogenetically related to RLKs. In Arabidopsis, AHK2, AHK3, and AHK4 are cytokinin receptors that carry CHASE ectodomain, two transmembrane domains, and a cytosolic HK module (Higuchi et al., 2004; Inoue et al., 2001). The CHASE ectodomain binds to cytokinins with high affinity, promoting homodimerisation of AHKs (Figure 2) (Anantharaman & Aravind, 2001; Hothorn, Dabi, & Chory, 2011). Ligand binding leads to the activation of the cytosolic histidine (His)‐aspartic acid (Asp) phosphorelay module, in which a phosphate group is transferred from a conserved His residue to Asp within the receptor (Hwang & Sheen, 2001). Downstream Arabidopsis histidine phosphotransfer proteins (AHPs) are then phosphorylated by AHKs to activate downstream transcription factors (Rashotte et al., 2006). AHKs are predominantly localised to the endoplasmic reticulum, with the CHASE ectodomain facing the ER lumen (Wulfetange et al., 2011). However, a portion of AHKs also localises to the plasma membrane and has been shown to perceive extracellular cytokinin (Antoniadi et al., 2020).

In addition to cytokinin, the phytohormone auxin is also perceived extracellularly and intracellularly. Extracellular perception of auxin is mediated by LRR‐RLK‐IXs known as transmembrane kinase (TMK) receptors (Friml et al., 2022; Liu, Hu, et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2023). Auxin‐binding protein 1 (ABP1) is a secreted protein that binds to auxin at apoplastic pH. Auxin binding of ABP1 facilitates its association with TMK1 ectodomain, which leads to the activation of the receptor (Friml et al., 2022). There is also evidence of direct auxin binding by the LRR domain of TMK1, and auxin binding is enhanced by its association with ABP1 and related proteins (ABLs) (Yu et al., 2023). Regardless, TMKs and ABP1/ABLs are receptor partners involved in extracellular auxin perception.

Similar to LRR‐RLPs, the LRR‐RLK‐Xb receptor Brassinosteroid‐insensitive 1 (BRI1) utilises an ID domain to perceives the phytohormone brassinosteroids (BR) (Li & Chory, 1997). The BRI1 ID protrudes from the LRR ectodomain and forms a sterol‐binding pocket. BRs dock into this pocket and induce conformational changes within the C3 region that recruits SERK co‐receptors (Figure 2) (Hothorn, Belkhadir, et al., 2011; Santiago et al., 2013; She et al., 2011; She et al., 2013). BR‐induced heterodimerisation of BRI1 and SERKs leads to the activation of BR‐induced responses.

LRR‐RLK‐XIs are receptors for phytocytokines, a diverse group of small endogenous peptides that are involved in immunity, growth, development, and reproduction (Furumizu et al., 2021). Several phytocytokines require post‐translational modifications for high‐affinity receptor binding. For example, the RGF peptides are perceived by RGFR/RGI receptors to control lateral root formation, and both tyrosine sulphation and proline hydroxylation are required for high‐affinity binding to the RGFR ectodomain (Matsuzaki et al., 2010; Song et al., 2016). The CLE peptide family is detected by the closely related receptors CLV1 and BAM1/2/3 to govern meristem development. CLV1 binds to CLE peptides through its LRR ectodomain, while BAM receptors bind to CLE peptides and can act redundantly or antagonistically with CLV1, depending on the plant tissue (Crook et al., 2020; DeYoung et al., 2006; Hazak et al., 2017; Kondo et al., 2006; Nimchuk et al., 2015; Ogawa et al., 2008; Shinohara et al., 2012). In the endodermis, SGN3/GSO1 perceives CIF1/2 peptides to induce formation of the Casparian strip (CS) and CIF binding facilitates its association with the SERK co‐receptor (Doblas et al., 2017; Okuda, Fujita, et al., 2020). Across the LRR‐RLK‐XI subclass, peptide recognition specificity is determined by the inner LRR surface, while SERKs serve as activation co‐receptors upon ligand binding (Morita et al., 2016; Roman et al., 2022; Zhang, Lin, Han, Qu, & Chai, 2016; Zhang, Lin, Han, Wang, et al., 2016) (Figure 2).

The ERECTA family (ERECTA, ERL1, ERL2) perceives EPF/EPFL peptides that regulate stomatal patterning in leaves (Hara et al., 2007; Hunt & Gray, 2009; Lee et al., 2015; Sugano et al., 2010). EPFs are small cysteine‐rich peptides whose recognition involves the LRR ectodomains of both ERECTA and the LRR–RLP TMM. SERKs act as activating co‐receptors following ligand binding (Lin et al., 2017; Meng et al., 2015). Antagonistic EPF family members, notably EPFL9/STOMAGEN, also bind to the receptor complex and compete with other EPFs (EPF1/2) (Lin et al., 2017). In contrast, EPFL4/EPFL6 are expressed in stems and inflorescences to control organ growth. EPFL4/EPFL6 bind to ERECTA ectodomains in the absence of TMM, and the presence of TMM significantly reduces the binding affinity of EPFL4 to ERECTA (Lin et al., 2017). Taken together, TMM and ERECTA control diverse developmental processes across plant tissues through differential recognition specificities towards EPF peptides (Figure 2).

CRINKLY receptors possess an ectodomain with seven repeating β‐propeller structures (known as the WD40 domain), followed by a cysteine‐rich region. Arabidopsis CRINKLY4 (CR4) is a CRINKLY receptor that controls root stem‐cell niche and leaf epidermal patterning (Becraft et al., 1996; Tanaka et al., 2002; Watanabe et al., 2004). CR4 and CLV1 form a receptor complex to regulate root meristem development (Stahl et al., 2013). Although CLE peptides have been proposed as potential ligands for CR4, biochemical binding assays suggest that CR4 does not directly bind to CLE40 (Okuda, Hothorn, & Hothorn, 2020). The cognate ligand for CRINKLY4 therefore remains to be identified (Figure 2).

Beyond their established roles in growth and development, several hormone‐ and peptide‐receptors also contribute to symbiosis and nodulation. Cytokinin biosynthesis and perception are key regulators of nodule initiation and development (Held et al., 2014; Murray et al., 2007; Reid et al., 2017; Tirichine et al., 2007). In addition, auxin biosynthesis and transport are coordinated to control nodule organogenesis (Breakspear et al., 2014; Nadzieja et al., 2018; Schiessl et al., 2019). Finally, nodulation is further regulated by CLV1/CLV2 receptors, which limit excessive nodule formation (Krusell et al., 2002; Krusell et al., 2011; Okamoto et al., 2013; Okamoto & Kawaguchi, 2015).

Perception of molecules involving reproduction

Self‐incompatibility (SI) in Brassica is controlled by the S‐locus receptor kinases (SRKs), which belong to a subgroup of G‐type lectin RLKs (Stein et al., 1991; Takasaki et al., 2000). SRKs perceive small, cysteine‐rich pollen coat peptides known as SCR/SP11 (Murase et al., 2004; Schopfer et al., 1999). SI specificity is determined by the highly selective interaction between SRKs and SCRs, and this recognition is strictly haplotype‐specific (Shiba et al., 2001). SCR binds to the hydrophobic surfaces between the lectin and EGF domains of SRKs. In the presence of SCR, SRK forms homodimers and assembles into a 2:2 SRK–SCR receptor complex (Figure 2) (Ma et al., 2016; Murase et al., 2020). The hydrophobic pocket that binds to SCR is hyper‐variable among SRK haplotypes, thereby determining the specificity between distinct SCR and SRK pairs.

A compatible interaction allows the pollen tube to germinate, after which pollen tube guidance to the ovule is mediated by synergid‐secreted LURE peptides. These peptides are perceived by several LRR–RLKs expressed on the pollen tube surface. In Arabidopsis, PRK6 (LRR‐RLK‐III) is a LURE receptor (Okuda et al., 2009; Takeuchi & Higashiyama, 2016). The C‐terminal LRR loop of PRK6 directly binds to LURE1, and ligand perception leads to the accumulation of PRK6 at the pollen tube tip (Takeuchi & Higashiyama, 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). However, how LURE‐binding activates PRK6 kinase, and whether co‐receptors participate in this process, remains unclear (Figure 2).

In parallel, the receptors MDIS1 (LRR‐RLK‐VI‐2) and MIK1/MIK2 (LRR‐RLK‐XII) also bind to LURE1 and form receptor complexes (Wang, Liang, et al., 2016). Biochemical assays and genetics support the role of these receptors as direct LURE receptors to guide pollen tube growth. Mutations in multiple PRKs result in defects in pollen tube guidance, while mutations in MDIS1 and MIK1/2 impair the sensitivity of pollen tubes towards LURE peptides (Takeuchi & Higashiyama, 2016; Wang, Liang, et al., 2016). Thus, these receptors are likely to operate with partial redundancy.

Perception of abiotic stress

CARD1/HPCA1 is a receptor from the LRR‐RLK‐VIII‐1 subclass that senses extracellular redox cues. CARD1 is required for responses induced by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and by quinones, such as 2,6‐dimethoxy‐1,4‐benzoquinone (DMBQ) (Wu et al., 2020) (Laohavisit et al., 2020). These results indicate that the CARD1 ectodomain can perceive multiple ligands or more broadly, extracellular redox perturbations. Recently, solved structure of the CARD1 ectodomain suggests that redox perception requires a copper binding His‐triad, rather than cysteine pairs (Figure 2) (Ishihama et al., 2025). Another stress sensor KOIN a receptor from the LRR‐RLK‐III subclass is implicated in cold responses, although its cognate ligand has not yet been identified. Cold exposure promotes the assembly of KOIN with a cytoplasmic kinase partner, thereby activating cold stress signalling (Zhang, Li, et al., 2025). Whether KOIN perceives self‐derived molecules produced during cold stress or recognises other ligands remains to be determined.

The disulphated pentapeptide phytosulfokine (PSK) is perceived by PSKR1/PSKR2 (LRR‐RLK‐Xb) (Matsubayashi et al., 2002). PSK binds to both the LRR and ID of PSKRs, promoting their heterodimerisation with SERKs (Figure 2) (Wang et al., 2015). PSKRs are implicated in multiple biological processes, such as biotic stress, osmotic stress and drought tolerance (Li et al., 2023). Under drought conditions, roots produce the tyrosine‐sulphated peptide CLE25, which is transported to leaves and perceived by the LRR‐RLK‐XIs BAM1 and BAM3. This root‐to‐shoot coordination relays drought status to induce stomatal closure (Takahashi et al., 2018). Another LRR‐RLK‐XI, HSL3, binds to the phytocytokine CTNIP and regulates stomatal closure during abiotic and biotic stresses (Liu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Rhodes et al., 2022).

FERONIA (FER) is an RLK with two malectin‐like domains that sense both peptides and the physical state of the cell wall, thus regulating multiple biological processes (Cheung, 2024). The malectin‐like domain of FER binds to de‐esterified pectin (homogalacturonan) to regulate cell morphogenesis (Lin et al., 2022). In addition, FER associates with LLG/LORELEI‐LIKE GPI proteins to perceive rapid alkalinisation factor (RALF) peptides to regulate pattern‐triggered immunity (Li et al., 2015; Stegmann et al., 2017). RALFs constitute a large family of secreted cysteine‐rich peptides, and their binding induces the heterodimerisation of LLG1 and FER, activating the receptor complex (Figure 2) (Xiao et al., 2019). In addition, FER also senses cell wall context through extracellular LRR‐extensin proteins (LRXs). LRXs are glycoproteins embedded in the cell wall matrix and bind to RALF peptides with high affinity (Figure 2) (Moussu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018). Depending on tissue types and extracellular conditions, LRXs can either sequester RALFs or directly activate the FER–LLG receptor complex. Under salt stress or other wall‐perturbing conditions, changes in pectin methyl‐esterification and RALF abundance lead to FER activation (Feng et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2024; Moussu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). Recent work further suggests that RALFs and short homogalacturonan undergo phase separation to form condensates with the FER–LLG complex, enabling the receptor to integrate and respond to multiple abiotic stresses such as salinity and heat (Liu et al., 2024).

Cell surface co‐receptors and regulators

The LRR‐RLK‐II subclass acts as co‐receptors for many cell surface receptors in plants. SERKs have short LRR ectodomains that do not provide ligand specificity. Instead, ligands either act as the molecular glue to induce heterodimerisation between receptors and SERKs, or they induce conformational changes in the primary receptors to promote association with SERKs. For receptors such as BRI1, FLS2/EFR/PEPR1, PSKR1, RGFR, ERECTA family receptors, and GSO1, ligand binding markedly enhances association with SERKs (Figure 2) (Lin et al., 2017; Okuda, Fujita, et al., 2020; Santiago et al., 2013; She et al., 2013; Song et al., 2016; Sun, Li, et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015). While basal interactions between these receptors and SERKs can be detected biochemically, ligand‐induced enhancement of these interactions is required for full receptor activation. NIKs (NSP‐INTERACTING KINASES) are also LRR‐RLK‐IIs closely related to the SERKs. Like SERKs, these co‐receptors also have short LRR ectodomains and have been implicated as positive regulators in antiviral immunity (Zorzatto et al., 2015). In addition, NIK1 associates with and negatively regulates the formation of the FLS2–BAK1 complex (Li et al., 2019), suggesting that this subgroup of co‐receptors might modulate immune signalling response to diverse pathogens. The CIKs (CLAVATA3‐insensitive kinases 1–4) represent another subgroup of LRR‐RLK‐II that act as co‐receptors for peptide‐binding LRR–RLKs. CIKs function as co‐receptors for CLV1 during CLE perception, and the association between CIK and CLV1 is detectable even in the absence of ligand (Hu et al., 2022).

Members of the LRR‐RLK‐XVI subclass act as co‐receptors for RLPs. SOBIR1 forms constitutive complexes with many LRR‐RLPs, such as the Cf proteins and RLP23 (Albert et al., 2015). Upon ligand perception, SERKs are recruited to the SOBIR1–LRR–RLP complex to activate immune responses (Figure 2) (Albert et al., 2015; Bi et al., 2016; Hohmann & Hothorn, 2019; van der Burgh et al., 2019). CORYNE (CRN) is another LRR‐RLK‐XVI that forms a constitutive complex with the LRR–RLP CLV2. The CLV2–CRN receptor complex perceives CLE peptides in parallel with the CLV1/BAM‐type receptors (Hazak et al., 2017; Müller et al., 2008).

In addition to co‐receptors, some cell surface receptors function as negative regulators. The BAK1‐interacting receptor‐like kinases (BIRs; LRR‐RLK subgroup Xa) constitutively associate with SERK co‐receptors, thereby sequestering SERKs and limiting their recruitment to ligand‐binding receptors. Structural and functional studies support a model in which BIRs compete directly with other receptors for SERK binding to regulate receptor complex formation (Figure 2) (Gao et al., 2009; Halter et al., 2014; Hohmann et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2017).

DOWNSTREAM SIGNALLING PATHWAYS OF CELL SURFACE RECEPTORS

Following ligand binding, cell surface receptors activate a range of downstream signalling pathways. The specificity of these responses is mainly determined by the kinase domain and its interacting partners, such as co‐receptors and plasma membrane‐localised receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs) (Hailemariam et al., 2024). Together, these kinases phosphorylate and activate downstream components to modulate cellular outputs, such as ion fluxes, apoplastic pH changes, ROS production, cytoskeletal remodelling, and membrane trafficking. In parallel, MAPK cascades and Ca2+ influxes induce transcriptional reprograming. Some of these modules are shared across pathways; signalling specificity arises from the timing and unique combination of activated modules, as well as the cellular and tissue context. Spatial constraints, inhibitor factors, and endocytic turnover further fine‐tune receptor outputs. Thus, diversity in downstream responses reflects not only the repertoire of signalling components but also the manner in which these components are engaged. PRR signalling pathway

Most PRRs activate a broadly conserved downstream pathway. Ligand‐binding leads to the assembly of an active receptor complex, such as SERKs with LRR–RLKs, or SOBIR1 and SERKs together with the LRR‐RLPs (Chinchilla et al., 2007; Schwessinger et al., 2011; van der Burgh et al., 2019). The cytoplasmic kinase domains of these RLKs are brought into close proximity, triggering a series of auto‐ and trans‐phosphorylation events (Schulze et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2012). These activated receptor complexes then phosphorylate multiple RLCK‐VII family members (Lin et al., 2014). For example, BIK1/PBL1 are activated by LRR‐RLK‐XIIs, PBL30/31/32 by the LRR‐RLPs, and PBL27 by LysM‐RLKs (Liu, Wu, et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2010; Pruitt et al., 2020; Yamada, Yamaguchi, et al., 2016). These RLCK‐VIIs act as executors and phosphorylate multiple downstream signalling components. BIK1 directly phosphorylates the NADPH oxidase RBOHD to generate ROS production and promote cytosolic calcium (Ca2+) influx by activating multiple Ca2+ channels, such as CNGC2/4 and OSCA1.3/1.7 (Kadota et al., 2014; Li et al., 2014; Thor et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2019). Activation of BIK1 also induces rapid apoplastic alkalinisation through inhibition of H+‐ATPases (AHAs) (Zhai et al., 2025). In parallel, MAPK cascades are activated by RLCKs, typically the MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 and MEKK1‐MKK1/2‐MPK4 modules (Asai et al., 2002; Rasmussen et al., 2012). Ca2+ influxes further activate calcium‐dependent protein kinases (CPKs), and together with MAPKs, these kinases converge to phosphorylate defence‐related transcription factors, such as WRKYs and TGAs, driving transcriptional activation, phytohormone production, and metabolic reprogramming to restrict pathogen infection (Bjornson et al., 2021; Boudsocq et al., 2010; Gao et al., 2013; Mao et al., 2011; Yamada, Saijo, et al., 2016; Yi et al., 2014) (Figure 3a).

Signalling pathway to establish symbiosis

Symbiotic signals in roots are perceived at the root by LysM receptors that bind to LCOs. Ligand‐binding promotes heterodimerisation of LysM‐RLKs such as NFR1 and NFR5 in legumes (Broghammer et al., 2012). The activated receptor complex phosphorylates downstream RLCK‐VII kinases, such as NiCK4 and LICK1/2 (Wang et al., 2025; Wong et al., 2019). In parallel, the malectin–LRR‐RLK receptor SYMRK/DMI2 is also activated to suppress immunity (Abel et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2012; Feng et al., 2021; Ried et al., 2014). Together, these RLKs initiate the common symbiosis signalling pathway (CSSP). The cation channels at the inner nuclear membrane DMI1/POLLUX and CNGC15 are activated and generate nuclear Ca2+ oscillations, which are maintained by the nuclear‐pore components NUP85, NUP133, and NENA (Charpentier et al., 2008; Charpentier et al., 2016; Groth et al., 2010; Kanamori et al., 2006; Saito et al., 2007). These Ca2+ oscillations are decoded by the Ca2+ calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase CCaMK/DMI3, which phosphorylates and activates the transcriptional regulator CYCLOPS/IPD3 (Chen et al., 2008; Messinese et al., 2007; Yano et al., 2008). In rhizobia‐legume interactions, CYCLOPS cooperates with NIN and NSP1/NSP2 to drive infection‐thread formation and nodule organogenesis; while in AM symbiosis, a CYCLOPS‐DELLA module promotes RAM1 activation for arbuscule development and nutrient exchange (Jin et al., 2016; Kaló et al., 2005; Schauser et al., 1999; Singh et al., 2014; Smit et al., 2005). In either case, activation of the CSSP with suppression of PRR‐mediated immunity is the key to establish symbiosis (Figure 3b).

TMK1 signalling pathway

Extracellular auxin is perceived by TMK1 together with ABP1/ABLs (Friml et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2023). Auxin induces ultrafast phosphorylation response mediated by RAF‐like kinases, which might be directly activated by TMK1 (Kuhn et al., 2024). In addition, TMK1 directly interacts with and phosphorylates AHAs (AHA1/4) to induce cell wall acidification (Lin et al., 2021). In addition, TMK1 also influences auxin transport. Activated TMK1 associates with and phosphorylates the auxin transporter PIN2 in roots (Rodriguez et al., 2025). These events provide a positive feedback loop to induce auxin efflux and local hormone gradients. In addition, auxin promotes the cleavage of the cytoplasmic kinase domain of TMK1, which enables its nuclear localisation to phosphorylate the transcriptional repressors IAA32/34 (Cao et al., 2019; Gu et al., 2022). The RHO GTPase (ROPs) have been shown to be activated by auxin treatment via guanine nucleotide exchange factors (ROPGEFs), which regulate actin microfilaments and clathrin‐dependent endocytosis to promote PIN polarisation (Lin et al., 2012; Nagawa et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2014). Recently, the RLCK‐VIIIs RAK1/2/3/4 have been shown to directly phosphorylate ROPGEFs during auxin response (Zhang, Jiang, et al., 2025). Taken together, TMK1 directly activates multiple downstream signalling components (Figure 3c).

SGN3 signalling pathway to establish Casparian strip (CS)

Stele‐derived CIF1/2 peptides diffuse across the endodermis when the apoplastic barrier is discontinuous and are perceived by SGN3, which forms a receptor complex with SERKs in endodermis cells (Doblas et al., 2017; Okuda, Fujita, et al., 2020). The activated complex activates the apolar RLCK‐VII kinase SGN1, which is confined to the cortex‐facing cell membrane (Alassimone et al., 2016). This special restriction ensures specific activation of the SGN3 signalling pathway at sites where the barrier is leaky. SGN1 activates the NADPH oxidases RBOHD/F to produce apoplastic ROS, which in turn activates apoplastic peroxidases to oxidise monolignols to lignin, forming a continuous lignified band that seals the apoplast (Fujita et al., 2020). In parallel, MAPK cascades are activated, mainly the MKK1/2/3/6‐MPK2 and MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 modules. SGN3 outputs are controlled by the master transcription factor MYB36, which is specifically activated by the MPK2 pathway but suppressed by the MKK9 pathway induced by PRRs (Fujita et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024). To summarise, the precise localisation of signalling components and unique combinatorial activation of MPK modules allows SGN3 signalling to construct CS with high spatial specificity (Figure 3d).

Brassinosteroid signalling pathway

Brassinosteroid binding to the BRI1 ectodomain enables its heterodimerisation with SERKs (He et al., 2000). In the resting state, the membrane protein BKI1 associates with the BRI1 cytosolic domain and prevents access to downstream substrates; receptor activation triggers BKI1 phosphorylation and its release (Wang et al., 2014; Wang & Chory, 2006). Subsequently, the RLCK‐VIIs BSK/CDG1 are phosphorylated by BRI1, which then associates with and activates the BSU1‐family phosphatase (Kim et al., 2011; Tang et al., 2008). BSU1 dephosphorylates and inactivates the GSK3‐like kinase BIN2 (Kim et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2011). When BIN2 activity is low, its target transcription factors BES1 and BZR1 are dephosphorylated by the protein phosphatase 2A, enabling BES1/BZR1 nuclear accumulation and transcriptional upregulation of brassinosteroid‐responsive genes that promote cell expansion and growth (Kim et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2011; Yin et al., 2002). (Figure 3e).

ERECTA signalling pathway

Perception of EPF peptides by the ERECTA family together with TMM promotes association with SERK co‐receptors (Lin et al., 2017; Meng et al., 2015; Shpak et al., 2004; Shpak et al., 2005). This activates the YODA (MKKK4)‐MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 signalling cascade. MAPK activation reduces both the abundance and activity of the bHLH transcription factors SPCH and ICE1/SCRM, restraining entry into the stomatal lineage (Lampard et al., 2008; Lau et al., 2014). Genetic evidence supports a role for BSK1/2 as the downstream RLCKs acting under ERECTA to modulate stomatal patterning, although whether the ERECTA receptor complex directly activates BSK1/2 remains unclear (Neu et al., 2019). The polarity factor BASL recruits the YODA signalling components and restricts MAPK activity to one side of the meristemoid cell. Following asymmetric cell division, MAPK activity suppresses SPCH in the daughter cell, enforcing proper spacing between stomata (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2015). In addition, the MAPK cascade activated by PRRs (such as FLS2) can also regulate stomatal patterning during early leaf development, indicating crosstalk between developmental and immunity signalling pathways (Herrmann et al., 2025) (Figure 3f).

Pollen tube guidance signalling pathway

LURE perception activates PRK family receptors, mainly PRK6 (Takeuchi & Higashiyama, 2016). Two RLCK‐VIIs, LIP1 and LIP2, are required for LURE responsiveness and directly interact with PRK6 (Liu, Zhong, et al., 2013; Takeuchi & Higashiyama, 2016), suggesting that they function as downstream RLCKs for PRK receptors. PRK6 also directly associates with ROPGEF8/12, leading to activation and localisation of ROP1 at the pollen tip (Yu et al., 2018). ROP1 coordinates actin assembly and exocytosis, providing the mechanical force for pollen tube elongation (Luo et al., 2017). Whether the MDIS1‐MIK1/2 receptor complex activates similar physiological responses is unclear. Pollen tube Ca2+ gradient and oscillation are one of the defining features of pollen tube growth (Cárdenas et al., 2008; Yan et al., 2009), but it is not yet known whether PRK6 directly activates specific Ca2+ channel. In addition, MAPK cascades may contribute, as MPK3/6 are required for pollen tube guidance; however, whether these MPKs act directly downstream of PRK6, and whether transcriptional regulation plays a role in pollen tube growth, remain unresolved (Guan et al., 2014). It is therefore likely that multiple receptors are activated by LURE peptides to orchestrate physiological responses for precise pollen tube guidance (Figure 3g).

Signalling pathway induced by cold stress

KOIN is a polarly localised LRR‐RLK‐III required for cold‐induced growth restraint in roots (Rodriguez‐Furlan et al., 2022; Zhang, Li, et al., 2025). Its cognate ligand has not yet been identified. Upon exposure to low temperature, the RLCK‐NG CRPK1 phosphorylates KOIN, promoting its accumulation at the plasma membrane. CRPK1 and KOIN then form an activated signalling complex that phosphorylates 14–3‐3 proteins, leading to their nuclear accumulation (Zhang, Li, et al., 2025). Nuclear 14–3‐3 proteins facilitate ubiquitin‐mediated degradation of CBF transcription factors, thereby suppressing SHR expression in the root meristem (Liu et al., 2017; Nakajima et al., 2001). This results in the inhibition of primary root elongation under cold conditions. How low temperature activates the CPRK1–KOIN complex, and if additional cell surface receptors participate in cold stress response remains to be investigated (Figure 3h).

Self‐incompatible signalling pathway

The SI response in Brassicaceae is triggered when SRKs bind their cognate pollen ligands SCR/SP11 (Schopfer et al., 1999; Stein et al., 1991; Takasaki et al., 2000). Ligand perception releases the thioredoxin‐like inhibitors, THL1 and THL2, from the SRK kinase domain (Cabrillac et al., 2001). SRK then associates with the RLCK‐VII MLPK, which activates the U‐box E3 ligase ARC1 (Kakita et al., 2007; Kitashiba et al., 2011; Murase et al., 2004; Stone et al., 1999; Stone et al., 2003). ARC1 mediates the degradation of multiple compatibility factors, including the exocyst subunit Exo70A1, PLDα1, and GLO1 (Samuel et al., 2009; Sankaranarayanan et al., 2015; Scandola & Samuel, 2019). Their degradation blocks exocytosis and reduces cellular detoxification capacity, creating a cellular environment that prevents pollen hydration, germination, and pollen tube penetration. A second signalling module involves FER. Upon contact with self‐pollen, SRK recruits and associates with FER to promote ROS production through the ROP2‐RBOHD/F module (Huang et al., 2023; Zhang, Huang, et al., 2021). Elevated ROS levels stiffen the cell wall and further restrict pollen tube entry. Together, SRK and FER coordinate the self‐incompatible signalling pathway by initiating multiple cellular responses that block self‐fertilisation (Figure 3i).

ORIGIN AND EVOLUTION OF CELL SURFACE RECEPTORS IN PLANTS

Plant RLKs likely share a single evolutionary origin. The RLK/Pelle family contains either RLKs with both ectodomain and kinase or cytosolic RLCKs, which retained only the kinase (Figure 4) (Lehti‐Shiu et al., 2009; Shiu & Bleecker, 2001; Shiu & Bleecker, 2003). The kinase domain is relatively conserved across the RLK family, and much of the diversity of RLKs comes from shuffling of ectodomain modules or changes around the transmembrane or juxta‐membrane region (Figure 4) (Man et al., 2020; Man et al., 2023; Ngou et al., 2024). Most receptor classes, including those with LRR, LysM, lectin, malectin‐like, DUF26, and WAK ectodomains, were already present at or before the emergence of land plants (Figure 5a). Similarly, the major co‐receptors, such as SERKs, CIKs, and SOBIR1, are found throughout land plant lineages (Ngou et al., 2024) (Figure 5a). In other words, the modules for recognising diverse extracellular molecules were established early during land plant evolution. Consistent with this, bryophytes encode functional receptor systems, such as LysM‐RLKs, FERONIA‐like receptors, and the BIR‐SERK regulatory modules, together with downstream signalling components including PBL/RLCKs and RBOHs. Together, these observations suggest that many cell surface signalling pathways originated early and were maintained throughout land plant diversification (Chu et al., 2023; Mecchia et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025; Yotsui et al., 2023).

With the transition to land, some receptor classes expanded significantly, particularly the LRR‐RLK‐XIIs, LRR‐RLPs, LysM‐RLKs, and malectin‐RLKs, together with the RLCK‐VII subgroup (Li, Liu, et al., 2025; Ngou et al., 2024). These RLKs exhibit significant ectodomain diversification, likely due to abiotic challenges or increasing pathogen pressure in both soil and aerial environments (Figure 5a). Pathogen pressure further drove the expansion of PRRs in vascular plants and angiosperms, producing large, lineage‐specific clusters within LRR‐RLK‐XII and LRR–RLP families (Ngou et al., 2024; Ngou et al., 2025). By contrast, receptors primarily involved in development and growth, such as LRR‐RLK‐Xbs, LRR‐RLK‐XIs, have remained relatively conserved in both copy number and sequence, reflecting stabilising selection on ligand recognition (Ngou, Heal, et al., 2022) (Figure 5a). Similarly, co‐receptors tend to remain broadly conserved, providing stable activation scaffolds for newly evolved receptor families. A shared evolutionary origin explains why different cell surface receptors tend to utilise similar signalling components. These core components are therefore shared and genetically required in many different biological processes. The following section highlights several examples in which plants have evolved existing receptor frameworks to perceive new ligands and regulate distinct biological processes.

Coevolution between receptors and ligands

Plant PRRs and microbial ligands (MAMPs) are engaged in continual evolutionary arms races. Pathogens frequently modify or mask elicitor epitopes to evade recognition, while plants evolve receptor ectodomains to restore recognition. For example, the flagellin epitope flg22 acquires mutations that retain bacterial motility yet diminish immunogenicity, and the FLS2 LRR ectodomain shows extensive natural variation across species and accessions that maintains flg22 perception (Colaianni et al., 2021; Parys et al., 2021). Small changes on the inner LRR surface are often sufficient to shift recognition specificity (Li, Jarquin Bolaños, et al., 2025; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2025). Similarly, the LRR‐RLK‐XII SCORE perceives cold‐shock protein peptides (csp15). Csp15 sequences vary among diverse species, yet natural SCORE alleles exhibit corresponding polymorphism in csp15 recognition. Substitutions of a few amino acids in SCORE can modify its surface charge and recognition specificity towards csp15 (Ngou et al., 2025; Stevens et al., 2024; Trinh et al., 2023). PERU, which recognises the pep13 motif from Phytophthora, also exhibits polymorphism in pep13 recognition (Torres Ascurra et al., 2023). The precise modifications required for PERU to capture evasion variants of pep13 remain to be determined. Across PRRs, selection pressure mainly targets ectodomains, while kinase domains and co‐receptors, such as SERKs or SOBIR1, remain comparatively conserved across plant lineages (Man et al., 2023). Novel MAMP recognition capabilities often arise through gene duplication, ectodomain swapping, and diversification. This has driven rapid expansion of PRR repertoires across land plants, enabling perception of an increasingly diverse array of pathogens (Figure 5b).

Receptor–ligand arms race is a feature of plant–pathogen interactions. In contrast, endogenous peptide recognition systems mediated by LRR‐RLK‐XI receptors, such as CLE‐CLV1/BAM, RGF‐RGFR, and CIF‐SGN3, are under stabilising selection. Core motifs and post‐translational modifications in phytocytokines are mostly conserved, and most receptors within the LRR‐RLK‐XI subclass exhibit little lineage‐specific expansion (Furumizu et al., 2021). Since both phytocytokines and their receptors are encoded by the same genome and function in tightly constrained developmental contexts, the self‐peptide recognition system evolves more slowly and maintains conserved recognition mechanisms.

Neo‐functionalisation of cell surface receptors

Cell surface receptors within the same subclass can be repurposed for distinct biological processes without major changes to the receptor itself. In many cases, plants utilise physiological responses trigger by a given receptor in different tissues or developmental settings to achieve new functions. For example, CARD1 perceives redox cues, either peroxide and quinones, and couples these signals to stress responses (Laohavisit et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). In parasitic plants, CARD1 has been co‐opted to induce haustorium formation: quinone acts as host‐derived haustorium‐inducing factors and activate CARD1 to initiate haustorial organogenesis (Laohavisit et al., 2020). How CARD1 signalling is integrated into developmental programmes in parasitic plants remains to be investigated. Another clear example is the RGF‐RGFR module. In most plants, RGF peptides are perceived by RGFRs to maintain the root meristem and regulate lateral root development (Song et al., 2016). In parasitic plants, RGF peptides are sensed by RGFRs to promote haustorial development and intrusive cell differentiation (Fishman et al., 2025). While peptide perception and downstream signalling are conserved, these receptors have been neo‐functionalised for different biological processes. Similarly, the LysM receptors are utilised to regulate either immunity or symbiosis depending on the chain length and structure of COs (Zhang, Tan, & Wang, 2025). In this case, ligand identity and receptor partnerships dictate signalling output. Taken together, these examples illustrate how plants can repurpose existing receptors to execute new biological functions without evolving new receptor classes.

Pathogens and pests exploit the plasticity of cell surface receptors by secreting peptide mimics that redirect host signalling programmes to their advantage. For example, Xanthomonas and plant parasitic nematodes secrete the tyrosine‐sulphated peptide RaxX, which mimics plant PSY peptides and stimulates growth responses through PSYR; in rice, however, the PRR XA21 detects these peptide mimics as MAMPs (Luu et al., 2019; Pruitt et al., 2015; Pruitt et al., 2017). Similarly, many plant parasitic nematodes secrete CLE‐ and CEP‐like peptides that activate various LRR‐RLK‐XI receptors to manipulate vascular development and build specialised feeding sites (Eves‐Van Den Akker et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2017; Mishra et al., 2023; Nakagami et al., 2023). Several fungal pathogens, such as Fusarium species, encode RALF‐like peptides that activate FER to induce apoplast alkalisation and enhanced infection (Masachis et al., 2016; Thynne et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024). In each case, pathogens hijack existing receptor systems and divert developmental or growth‐associated signalling into virulence programmes through molecular mimicry (Figure 5c).

Diversification of cell surface receptors and downstream signalling pathways

LRR ectodomains are ancient scaffolds. In early land plants, the ID and C3 region evolved to facilitate ligand binding and SERK recruitment. From this ancestral framework, two receptor branches emerged: the LRR‐RLPs, which function primarily in immunity and have lost their cytosolic kinase domains, and the LRR‐RLK‐Xb receptors, which retained kinase domains and control developmental processes. Subsequent diversification in the transmembrane, juxta‐membrane, and cytosolic regions, together with differences in co‐receptor pairing, determines which downstream pathways are initiated (Ngou et al., 2024).

The Arabidopsis LRR–RLP RLP23 perceives the nlp20 peptide via its ectodomain, and signalling depends on interactions with two co‐receptors (Albert et al., 2015). First, the GxxxG/GxGxG motifs within transmembrane region allow constitutive interaction with SOBIR1 (Bi et al., 2016). Second, ligand‐induced conformational changes in the C3 region recruit SERKs; the negatively charged juxta‐membrane region is also required for SERK interactions (Ngou et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022). The assembled receptor complex then activates downstream immune RLCK‐VIIs. Subtle changes within the transmembrane helix, charges in the juxta‐membrane or the C3 region can shift co‐receptor pairing and alter signalling output. On the other hand, BR binding to BRI1 enables the C3 region to associate with SERKs (Santiago et al., 2013; Sun, Han, et al., 2013). On the cytosolic side, juxta‐membrane and C‐terminal tail of BRI1 specifically interacts with RLCKs such as BSKs/CDG1, activating the BSU‐BIN2‐BES1/BZR1 module (Kim & Russinova, 2020). In summary, variations in the transmembrane, juxta‐membrane, and cytosolic regions enable receptors to engage with different co‐receptors and downstream RLCKs, which specify the outputs (Figure 5d).

Most of the LRR‐RLK‐XI receptors bind to endogenous phytocytokines and control diverse biological processes, including immunity, development, and abiotic stress tolerance. This functional breadth likely reflects their ability to interact with different co‐receptors, such as SERKs and CIKs. In addition, distinct RLCK or MAPK activation patterns, cell type‐specific expression, and timing of receptor activation collectively shape diverse downstream responses. The RLCKs, particularly the RLCK‐VII family, have diversified in coordination with cell surface receptors to act as adaptor modules. Distinct RLCK clades preferentially couple to specific receptor classes, such as BIK1/PBL1 to LRR‐RLK‐XIIs, PBL27 to LysM receptors, SGN1 to SGN3, LIP1/2 to PRK6, and BSKs to BRI1 and ERECTA (Alassimone et al., 2016; Liu, Wu, et al., 2013; Liu, Zhong, et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2010; Neu et al., 2019; Takeuchi & Higashiyama, 2016; Tang et al., 2008; Yamada, Yamaguchi, et al., 2016). This network architecture, in which RLK transmembrane, juxta‐membrane, cytosolic domain, and co‐receptor choice determine RLCK partners, allows plants to flexibly repurpose receptors into diverse pathways and control distinct biological processes (Figure 4).

CONCLUSION

Plants deploy an extensive cell surface perception network that detects diverse molecules released under varying environmental conditions and integrates these inputs into appropriate physiological responses. Most cell surface receptors signal through cytosolic kinases, which relay information through multiple downstream components. RLKs constitute a monophyletic family and share a conserved kinase core with their cytosolic relatives, RLCKs. These two lineages co‐expanded and diversified during land plant evolution, enabling related receptor modules to be wired into different pathways across tissues and species, including specialised roles in processes such as symbiosis and parasitism. Despite substantial progress, several key questions remain open. Addressing these questions will clarify how a conserved perception system generates diverse outcomes and will guide efforts to reprogramme cell surface signalling for resilience and crop improvement (Table 1; Box 2).
