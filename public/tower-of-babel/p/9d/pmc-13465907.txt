Knowledge of emerging hypertension challenges among tribal populations in India: An analytical study among medical and allied health professionals
Authors: Sudip Bhattacharya, Akash Dasgupta, G. Jahnavi, Vinayagamoorthy Venugopal, Sunil Panigrahi, Soumik Ghosh, Richa, Abha Kumari, Sushil Kumar, Dibyanshu, Abhijit Boratne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and is increasingly recognized among India’s tribal populations, who are experiencing rapid epidemiological and lifestyle transitions. Despite rising prevalence, earlier age of onset, and high levels of undiagnosed and uncontrolled hypertension in tribal communities, national non-communicable disease strategies often inadequately account for their sociocultural, geographic, and health-system contexts. Health professionals play a central role in detection, counselling, and continuity of care; however, evidence regarding their knowledge of emerging hypertension trends and challenges in tribal populations remains limited. This study assessed knowledge levels among medical and allied health professionals in India, and examined factors associated with good knowledge.Methods:A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among medical and allied health professionals from academic, clinical, research, and programme settings across multiple Indian states. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling and completed a structured, pre-tested, self-administered electronic questionnaire assessing knowledge related to epidemiological trends, sociocultural determinants, health-system barriers, and governance challenges of hypertension in tribal populations. Knowledge scores were computed and categorized as good or inadequate. Descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify predictors of good knowledge.Results:A total of 247 participants were included. Overall, 55.9% demonstrated good knowledge. High awareness was observed regarding lifestyle transitions, dietary salt intake, alcohol use, seasonal migration, access barriers, and the potential role of telemedicine. However, fewer than half correctly identified early-onset hypertension in tribal populations, and knowledge regarding exclusion of traditional governance structures from non-communicable disease programme was limited. Participants aged 24–30 years had significantly higher odds of good knowledge compared with those aged 18–24 years.Discussion:While awareness of structural and behavioral determinants was relatively high, critical gaps persist in recognizing early-onset disease and governance-related challenges. Addressing these gaps through targeted education and training is essential to strengthen hypertension prevention and control strategies in tribal settings.Conclusions:Knowledge regarding hypertension in tribal populations was good, but important gaps remained in early detection and governance-related issues. Strengthening culturally sensitive training for healthcare professionals is essential for improving tribal hypertension care in India.

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most significant modifiable risk factors contributing to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide and remains a major public health concern in low- and middle-income countries.[1] In India, hypertension has rapidly increased across all populations, with tribal communities being especially vulnerable due to fast epidemiological transition without adequate health system and social protection support.[2]

Tribal populations were once considered protected from hypertension due to the traditional lifestyles, but evidence now shows rising prevalence, earlier onset, and poor control driven by lifestyle, migration, and systemic barriers.[345] Detection remains difficult due to asymptomatic disease, limited access, and uniform policies that overlook tribal contexts, leading to delayed diagnosis and poor outcomes.[678] Health professionals play a key role, yet urban-centric training limits preparedness, and evidence on their knowledge remains scarce.[91011] This study assesses knowledge and its predictors to inform targeted interventions and policy strengthening. This study may help practitioners by identifying specific knowledge gaps, enabling targeted training and better clinical decision-making in tribal hypertension care. For patients, it may lead to earlier detection, culturally appropriate counselling, and improved continuity of care, ultimately enhancing hypertension control and health outcomes.

Methods

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among medical and allied health professionals across India from academic, clinical, and research settings, including students, residents, faculty, and project staff. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling using professional networks and platforms, ensuring diversity in age, qualification, and designation. Data were collected via a structured 20-item, self-administered questionnaire assessing knowledge on tribal hypertension, including sociocultural, systemic, and governance aspects. Ethical approval was taken from IEC having code-2026-IND-11-06.

The tool was developed from literature and expert inputs, pre-tested for validity, and administered electronically with informed consent. Each correct response scored one point, and ≥75% was considered good knowledge. Data were analyzed after ensuring quality and completeness, with no personal identifiers collected.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS v20 software. Descriptive statistics were used to summaries sociodemographic characteristics and overall knowledge levels. Question-wise performance analysis was undertaken to identify specific domains of strength and knowledge gaps. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of good knowledge, adjusting for gender, age category, and professional designation. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 247 medical and allied health professionals participated in the study. The gender distribution was relatively balanced, with males constituting 55.5% of the sample. The age distribution was skewed towards younger participants, reflecting the academic structure of medical institutions. Individuals aged 18–24 years comprised 33.6% of participants, while the largest proportion belonged to the 24–30-year age group (41.7%). Representation declined with increasing age, with only 4.5% of participants aged above 42 years [Table 1].

Discussion

The overall pattern in this study 55.9% of medical and allied health professionals demonstrating good knowledge of emerging hypertension challenges in tribal populations sits in a mixed zone that is neither reassuring nor unexpected. It is encouraging that a majority show adequate insight, but the remaining 44.1% with inadequate knowledge is programmatically important because tribal hypertension control depends heavily on the “last-mile” decisions made by clinicians, residents, faculty, and project teams.[12] In many tribal districts, the health system is already constrained by workforce shortages, difficult terrain, and low preventive care uptake; a knowledge gap of this magnitude can easily translate into under-screening, weak counselling, poor community engagement, and missed follow-up opportunities, thereby sustaining the “rule of halves” dynamics that are repeatedly observed in hypertension control in low-resource settings.

When compared with the epidemiological literature, the need for high contextual competence becomes clearer. Systematic synthesis of Indian tribal studies has shown that hypertension is not rare in tribal adults, with pooled prevalence estimates around the mid-teens and substantial heterogeneity across tribes and settings, indicating that tribal India is not one uniform risk environment but a patchwork of differing transitions and vulnerabilities.[13] In parallel, community-based investigations have noted that tribal populations in different regions of India show increasing lifestyle-related morbidities, with earlier reports describing hypertension prevalence ranges that often fall within 15–30% in tribal groups, again with major variation by location and degree of transition.[14] This epidemiological background matters for interpretation because “good knowledge” in a tribal hypertension context is not simply knowing diagnostic thresholds or drug names; it requires an understanding that the risk profile is dynamic, that prevalence is already meaningful, and that prevention and continuity of care must be adapted to the sociocultural and structural realities of tribal life.[15]

A striking strength of the present results is the high correctness on items relating to structural and systemic barriers especially seasonal migration and its role in worsening hypertension control, late detection due to access constraints, and the underestimation of prevalence in official records. This aligns with what implementation and field studies repeatedly show: migration and weak continuity mechanisms are central disruptors of chronic disease management in marginalized populations.[16] Although much of the Indian tribal hypertension literature focuses on prevalence and risk factors rather than longitudinal continuity, tribal and rural Non communicable Disease (NCD) programme frequently report fragmentation of follow-up due to mobility, poor record portability, and inconsistent drug availability, which collectively make hypertension management far more fragile than in stable urban populations.[17] The fact that most participants identified these barriers correctly suggests that the clinical and public health workforce is increasingly sensitized to the “health-system determinants” of control rather than viewing hypertension as purely an individual behavioral issue.

Another strong domain in the present study is recognition of diet-related drivers, particularly traditional practices that can lead to salt overconsumption and underestimation of salt content in preserved foods. This finding is consistent with broader evidence that dietary transition including increased access to processed or preserved foods and changing taste preferences has become a key pathway through that cardiovascular risk accumulates in communities undergoing socio-economic change.[18] While the tribal hypertension literature does not always isolate salt as a single exposure with the same granularity seen in urban studies, the wider Indian and global evidence base has consistently positioned salt as a major modifiable determinant of blood pressure at the population level.[19] A practically important point here is that knowledge of “salt as a risk factor” is common, but knowledge of how salt is culturally consumed through preservation, ritual foods, or shared community meals is what makes counselling in tribal settings actionable. Our participants’ high performance on these items suggests a degree of contextual readiness that is valuable for programme delivery, provided it is translated into communication strategies that do not alienate communities or stigmatize cultural foods.

The gaps, however, are not minor; they point to conceptual blind spots that can weaken tribal NCD strategies even when general awareness is high. The most consequential gap is limited awareness of early-onset hypertension (only 45.7% answered correctly). This matters because early-onset hypertension reshapes screening logic: if clinicians implicitly associate hypertension with older age, they will under-screen younger adults, and miss the opportunity to interrupt risk trajectories before complications emerge.[20] While direct national estimates of “early-onset hypertension in tribal populations” vary by tribe and setting, several tribal prevalence studies and syntheses have shown meaningful burdens across adult age strata, and some have highlighted that hypertension can occur even among lean or underweight tribal individuals, challenging the stereotype that hypertension is primarily a disease of older, overweight, urban groups.[21] In that context, a workforce that under-recognizes early-onset patterns may inadvertently sustain late detection, thereby reinforcing the very underestimation and delayed diagnosis that the participants otherwise correctly perceived as a problem.

The second major gap limited awareness regarding exclusion of traditional governance structures from NCD programmes (55.9% correct) is equally important but often under-discussed in biomedical training. Tribal communities frequently operate with strong local institutions (formal and informal) that shape norms, health decisions, and trust. When NCD programme are designed and implemented without these institutions, community participation can become tokenistic, and risk communication may be perceived as external instruction rather than shared problem-solving.[22] Although the web literature is less standardized on “governance exclusion” than on prevalence, implementation perspectives in rural-tribal settings repeatedly stress that programme success depends on local legitimacy, culturally competent intermediaries, and trust-building. This is also reflected indirectly in work describing barriers to service utilization in vulnerable tribal communities and the need for community-influencing members in data collection and engagement.[23] The implication is that knowledge gaps in governance and community structures are not “soft” issues; they can determine whether screening campaigns reach younger men, whether women accept lifestyle counselling, and whether follow-up adherence survives festivals, migration cycles, and competing livelihood priorities.

Our regression finding that the 24–30-year age group had significantly higher odds of good knowledge than the 18–24-year group is conceptually coherent and resonates with evidence from training and workforce studies: knowledge that is contextual and applied often improves when learners transition from classroom-based education to early service roles where they encounter NCD programme realities.[24]

Similar patterns have been observed in community health worker (CHW) and peripheral health worker contexts, where training exposure, experience, and education level are associated with better NCD knowledge. For example, evaluations of NCD training initiatives for ASHAs report perceived gains in skills related to detection of hypertension and diabetes after structured training, supporting the idea that targeted learning interventions can shift competence meaningfully.[25] In addition, assessments of CHWs’ NCD knowledge in primary care settings have highlighted limited baseline understanding and improvements associated with training and education, reinforcing that knowledge is not automatically acquired through mere presence in the system; it needs structured reinforcement.[26] Interpreting our age effect through this lens suggests that the “knowledge jump” in early professional years may reflect exposure to clinical responsibility, programme work, or field postings yet the lack of a continued advantage in older age groups in our model may imply knowledge plateauing or dilution without continuing professional development.

The trend toward higher knowledge among males (P = 0.055) is worth cautious interpretation. It may reflect differential exposure to field postings, programme roles, or opportunities for external training within certain institutional hierarchies rather than intrinsic differences in competence. Importantly, the broader tribal hypertension prevalence literature has not consistently shown large sex differences in prevalence; pooled analyses suggest differences can be modest and context dependent.[27] Therefore, any knowledge difference by gender in professionals should not be used to generalize about disease distribution; rather, it should prompt institutions to ensure equitable access to tribal health training, field exposure, and leadership roles in NCD programme.

The absence of statistically significant differences by professional designation compared with students is another revealing outcome. In many contexts, faculty and residents are assumed to carry higher contextual knowledge. Our findings suggest that “designation” alone does not guarantee familiarity with tribal NCD realities, which may occur if curricula and continuing education do not systematically integrate tribal health content. This resonates with broader KAP literature where high general awareness coexists with gaps in specific domains, particularly those requiring applied and context-specific understanding. Studies assessing knowledge related to hypertension in general populations or risk groups often show partial knowledge across domains, reminding us that “hypertension knowledge” is multidimensional and does not necessarily consolidate without targeted, domain-specific education.[28] The practical message is that tribal hypertension competence cannot be assumed to “come with seniority;” it must be cultivated through structured teaching, field immersion, and periodic updates aligned with evolving transitions in tribal communities.[29]

Our participants’ strong recognition of telemedicine’s usefulness for tribal communities also aligns with the expanding evidence base that remote care can help overcome access barriers in rural settings. A recent meta-analysis of telemedicine interventions in rural hypertension management concluded that telemedicine can be as effective as usual care in managing hypertension, supporting its viability as an access-equalizing strategy when implemented thoughtfully.[30] In the Indian context, perspective and field discussions on telemedicine integration in rural-tribal settings highlight persistent barriers connectivity, digital literacy, local facilitation, and system readiness while still positioning telemedicine as an important bridge when supported by community intermediaries.[31] The key interpretive point is that high “belief” in telemedicine’s usefulness is not sufficient; what matters is whether professionals understand how to operationalize it in tribal contexts through assisted teleconsultations, scheduled follow-ups aligned to migration cycles, culturally appropriate counselling, and integration with medicine refills and local BP monitoring.

Taken together, the results suggest a workforce that is increasingly aware of macro-level barriers and programmatic challenges, but still has blind spots were tribal hypertension deviates from standard mental models especially early onset and governance/community participation. These gaps are precisely the ones that can compromise programme effectiveness even when general knowledge is “good.” If early-onset hypertension is underestimated, screening will remain skewed toward older adults, and a large reservoir of undetected younger hypertensives will persist.[20] If governance structures are overlooked, community ownership will remain weak, and adherence will fluctuate during festivals, rituals, and livelihood-related movements.[22] Both gaps can reduce the effectiveness and sustainability of National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD) strategies, even when services are available. Our findings suggest that undergraduate training alone is not enough for tribal NCD competence, as younger participants had lower knowledge than slightly older groups. Early introduction of tribal health modules, case-based learning, and programme exposure is needed. In-service training should continue this learning to avoid stagnation in later years. Evidence from Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) and CHW programme shows that structured training improves knowledge and skills, and similar models can be adapted with tribal-specific focus. Telemedicine training should also move from basic awareness to practical use.

Study limitations

However, this study has some limitations. Being cross-sectional, it shows associations but not causation. The questionnaire assessed knowledge, not actual clinical practice, or programme outcomes. Non-random sampling may limit generalizability and may include more motivated participants. Still, the pattern of stronger system knowledge and weaker areas like early onset, and governance provides useful direction for training and programme planning.

Future aspects

Future efforts should focus on integrating tribal-specific NCD training into curricula and strengthening continuous professional education with practical, field-based exposure. Additionally, implementation research is needed to translate telemedicine and community governance integration into effective, scalable models for improving hypertension control in tribal settings.
