Notch signaling-targeted biomimetic hydrogel constructs an anti-inflammatory “flood-control” system to alleviate pyroptosis for intervertebral disc degeneration treatment
Authors: Rui Zhang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Jiangbo Guo, Yilin Yang, Junjie Niu, Hao Shen, Yi Zheng, Jiaqi Wang, Suhao Wang, Li Ni, Huilin Yang, Qin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is the major pathological cause of low back pain and global disability. Its chronic and persistent inflammatory microenvironment, resembling a flowing flood to erode the ecosystems relentlessly, disrupts the intervertebral disc homeostasis. In the present study, we first verified that Notch signaling is negatively correlated with inflammation in IVDD samples. Meanwhile, we identified a natural flavonoid hesperetin (Hes) as a potential agonist of Notch1 by molecular docking predicting. Herein, we developed an EV-inspired bioactive reservoir system, in which the natural extracts hes was encapsulated into liposomes (Hes@lipo) and embedded within a gelatin methacryloyl (GM) hydrogel (GM/Hes@lipo)to achieve sustained release. By mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM)-like microenvironment of the intervertebral disc (IVD), GM/Hes@lipo provided physiological environment for nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) adhesion and growth maintenance. Furthermore, by activating Notch signaling, GM/Hes@lipo effectively restored the dynamic balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines and maintained ECM metabolic homeostasis, attenuating NOD-like receptor signaling, thereby inhibiting NPC pyroptosis. Based on these aspects, GM/Hes@lipo relieved the IVDD process, maintained annulus fibrosus integrity, and effectively alleviated discogenic pain after the platform was administrated into the intervertebral disc of the rats. Collectively, as an anti-inflammatory “flood-control” system, the Notch signaling-targeted biomimetic hydrogel presents a biomimetic–natural compound combinatorial strategy offering promising therapy for mitigating IVDD progression.

Introduction

The incidence of degenerative disorders of the musculoskeletal system has risen markedly as a result of population aging, and it is projected to affect up to one-third of the global population [1,2]. In fact, disability caused by low back pain has become the leading contributor to the global burden of disease, resulting in substantial productivity loss and significant socioeconomic impact [3]. Lumbar intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a common pathological basis underlying low back pain. Due to the lack of a unified understanding of its etiological factors, clinical management still primarily relies on palliative strategies aimed at the rapid alleviation of symptoms and temporary restoration of function [4].

Pathologically, inflammation persists throughout the entire course of IVDD within the disc microenvironment. Multiple deleterious and injury-associated factors associated with inflammation, such as aging, aberrant mechanical stress, and metabolic dysregulation, can induce functional impairment of nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) [5]. Once compromised, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) are released, resulting in inflammatory surge [6]. This sustained inflammatory “flood” erodes the structural foundation of the disc by upregulating disintegrin-like metalloproteinases and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which accelerate the degradation of key extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including aggrecan (ACAN) and type II collagen (COLII) [7]. Once the ECM framework collapses, the disc progressively loses its load-bearing capacity. Collectively, these events establish an inflammatory degenerative niche characterized by uncontrolled cytokine diffusion, matrix breakdown, and mechanical instability.

Moreover, during the progression of disc degeneration, inflammation-related pyroptotic cell death is triggered by the NLRP3 inflammasome, occurring in NPCs [8]. Specifically, upon sensing cellular damage, the adaptor protein ASC undergoes oligomerization to form inflammasomes, which is a star-shaped multiprotein complex [9]. NLRP3-mediated activation of caspase-1 (P20) processes gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate an N-terminal fragment [10]. These pores disrupt the plasma membrane, which triggers the massive release of inflammatory mediators, thus exacerbating the inflammatory response [11]. Therefore, to mitigate inflammation, effective therapeutic interventions should aim to control the “inflammatory flood”, thereby restoring disc homeostasis through the suppression of overactivated inflammatory signaling.

As an evolutionarily conserved pathway, Notch signaling is pivotal in controlling key biological processes including differentiation, proliferation and apoptosis [12]. Physiological Notch signaling is essential for promoting bone regeneration and regulating bone metabolic balance [13]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the magnetic nanocomposite hydrogels combined with static magnetic field-mediated physical stimulation can activate the Notch1/2 signaling cascade, thereby promoting the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [14]. Additionally, Notch signaling helps preserve the physiological homeostasis of intervertebral discs by downregulating the expression levels of ECM catabolic enzymes, thereby slowing matrix degradation [13,15]. Notably, Notch signaling is closely associated with inflammation regulation and exhibits a dual role. Research has indicated that the up-regulation of NICD1 and NICD2 facilitates an equilibrium between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines within macrophages, thereby exerting a suppressive influence on inflammatory responses [16]. Thus, proper regulation of Notch signaling contributes to inflammation control and ECM metabolic balance.

The increasing body of evidence indicates that natural products and traditional Chinese medicine formulations constitute a valuable reservoir for drug discovery, offering the dual advantages of biosafety and multifaceted therapeutic potential [17,18]. However, many natural extracts suffer from poor solubility, resulting in low bioavailability. It is a major bottleneck limiting their clinical translation [19]. Consequently, researchers have devoted increasing efforts to developing advanced biocarrier and functionalized delivery platforms to enhance the therapeutic potential of phytochemicals. Notably, liposomal formulations approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), provide a structurally biomimetic drug delivery platform [20]. It resembles the bilayer architecture and functional characteristics of extracellular vesicles (EVs), making them highly promising for biomedical applications [21]. Liposomes are readily engineered for targeted modification and size customization [22]. And their bilayer structure also facilitates the encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs and promotes cellular internalization through membrane compatibility, thereby enhancing drug bioavailability and stability [23]. Recently, our group demonstrated the therapeutic potential of liposome-based delivery systems by anchoring FA and 78c modified liposomes within injectable hydrogel microspheres for osteoarthritis treatment [24]. Therefore, integrating the biomimetic advantages of EVs with the pharmacological efficacy of natural compounds may serve as a viable treatment approach for addressing IVDD.

In the present study, transcriptomic profiling and histological analyses of clinical intervertebral disc samples firstly revealed an exacerbated inflammatory microenvironment and disrupted Notch signaling in degenerated discs. Subsequently, through screening of traditional medicine databases and molecular docking, hesperetin (Hes) was identified as a natural compound capable of targeting the Notch signaling pathway and reactivating inflammation-induced Notch inactivation. Therefore, inspired by the protective barrier function of dams against flowing floods, we developed a biomimetic EV-inspired delivery system with gelatin methacryloyl (GM) hydrogel as the dam, in which hes was encapsulated within liposomes (Hes@lipo) to construct a composite platform (GM/Hes@lipo). This system leveraged the liposome's biomimetic bilayer structure to enhance the solubility of hes, while the GM hydrogel simulated the native ECM environment and functioned as a reservoir for sustained drug release. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that this delivery system effectively activated Notch signaling, thereby rebalancing NPC inflammation and ECM metabolism. Herein, in the surgical IVDD rats, GM/Hes@lipo significantly alleviated both disc degeneration and associated pain by local injection. Materials and methods

Ethical certification

Human nucleus pulposus specimens and imaging records were harvested from patients undergoing surgery, with informed consent secured preoperatively. This process received ethical clearance from the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (20251246). Additionally, the animal study procedures adhered to the guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (SUDA20251030A06).

Transcriptome data acquisition and analysis

Transcriptomic data were obtained from public databases. Dataset GSE266883 (RNA-seq data from degenerated and non-degenerated human NP tissues) was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The screening for DEGs was performed based on specific thresholds: an false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and a |log2 fold change| > 1.

Isolation and handling of rat NPCs

NPCs were isolated from caudal IVDs from the male Sprague-Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old) as previously described [25]. After NPC attachment, the cells were treated with TNFα (20 ng/mL, RP01322, Abclonal, China) to induce inflammation for mimicking the NPCs degenerative condition [13]. After 48 h, cells and the culture supernatant were collected to evaluate the inflammatory effectiveness. In some experiments, NPCs were cultured on the different hydrogels and incubated with TNFα (20 ng/mL) for 3 days, and then, NPCs were collected for further use [26].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The level of IL-1β in the supernatant of NPCs with or without TNFα was detected by ELISA kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the instruction of the manufactory.

Molecular docking

Hes, a flavonoid derivative from citrus fruits, exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities [27,28]. Its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties have been demonstrated to be closely associated with human health benefits [29,30]. Recent evidence has extended the pharmacological scope of hes to encompass musculoskeletal disorders [31]. Notably, hes has been shown to mitigate oxidative stress-induced ferroptosis in NPCs by upregulating the Nrf2 signaling axis and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, thereby highlighting its therapeutic potential for IVDD [32]. The NICD1 domain sequence of Notch1 was retrieved from the UniProt database. The 3D structure was predicted with AlphaFold3 (DeepMind, UK) software and optimized via Rosetta Relax (University of Washington, USA). Protein preparation (including dewatering and hydrogen addition) was performed with PyMOL (Schrödinger, USA). The chemical structure of hes was obtained from the PubChem database. Blind docking was performed by the CB-DOCK2 online platform (Harbin Institute of Technology, China), and Autodock Vina (The Scripps Research Institute, USA) for docking. Protein–ligand interaction analyses were conducted with the PLIP online tool (University of Leipzig, Germany), and 3D conformations were visualized with PyMOL.

Preparation and characterization of the Hes@lipo

Hes@lipo was prepared via the thin-film dispersion method [33]. Hes (Aladdin, China), lecithin and cholesterol (Macklin, China) were co-dissolved in 30 mL of chloroform to obtain a homogeneous solution. The solvent was evaporated under rotation (35 °C, 100 rpm, 30 min) to form a thin lipid film. The film was hydrated with 6 mL PBS and sonicated for 1 h to ensure complete dispersion. The resulting emulsion was passed through a 0.22 μm polycarbonate membrane via extrusion to obtain Hes@lipo. The milky Hes@lipo suspension was analyzed by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Nano S, Malvern, UK) to determine hydrodynamic diameter and Polydispersity Index (PDI). Morphological characterization was conducted by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F).

Development and identification of the composite hydrogels

Different concentrations of Hes@lipo were incorporated into a GM–LAP (Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate, EFL, China) solution (final concentrations: 5% GM and 0.05% LAP) and stirred to obtain a homogeneous prepolymer solution. The prepolymer was crosslinked by 405 nm blue light exposure for 1 min to form GM/Hes@lipo composite hydrogels.

For morphological analysis, lyophilized hydrogels were gold-sputtered and examined by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, Kyoto, Japan). Swelling and degradation were assessed by incubating hydrogels in PBS (37 °C, 100 rpm). Samples were weighed hourly for the first 6 h and subsequently on day 7, 14, 21and 28, respectively. The swelling and degradation behavior was expressed as the percentage of remaining weight relative to the initial mass.

To evaluate release kinetics, hydrogels were incubated in PBS (37 °C, 80 rpm), and the released medium was collected at predetermined time points (day 1, 3, 5, 7, 14and 28, respectively). The concentration of released hes was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Biocompatibility evaluation of the composite hydrogels

NPCs were plated onto hydrogels within 24-well plates and maintained for durations of 1, 3, and 5 days. To evaluate cell proliferation, a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China) assay was performed. Specifically, cells underwent a 2-h incubation with the CCK-8 reagent, followed by absorbance monitoring at 450 nm. For the assessment of cell survival, a Calcein-AM/PI kit (Beyotime, China) was utilized, with cells incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. Regarding cytoskeletal morphology, samples were stained using rhodamine–phalloidin (Yeasen, China) for 40 min, while nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Final observations were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Rat NPCs with or without TNFα on cell culture plates or hydrogels were subjected to immunofluorescence staining. In brief, after fixation, blocking and permeabilized according to standard protocols [34], NPCs were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Notch1, NLRP3, ASC, P20, GSDMD, COLII, ACAN, ADAMTS5 or MMP13, respectively. Subsequently, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with fluorescent secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China), followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (Beyotime, China). The sources of primary antibodies are listed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Total protein lysates were harvested from NPCs, and their concentrations were determined via a BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Subsequently, protein samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electro-blotted onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Following a blocking step, the membranes were probed with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, succeeded by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China). Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence system. Detailed information regarding the primary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA extraction was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) and qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green reagents (Bio-Rad, USA) on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, USA). Primer sequences (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai) are provided in the Supplementary Table S2. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

RNA-sequencing analysis for NPCs

Rat NPCs were seeded on the GM or GM/Hes@lipo hydrogel with TNFα (20 ng/mL) for 3 days. Total RNA from rat NPCs was prepared and sequenced by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). DEGs were identified using the following criteria: |log2 fold change| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.Functional enrichment analyses, including KEGG, GO and GSEA, were performed to elucidate potential biological pathways.

Establishment of rat IVDD model

The rat caudal IVDD model was established as reported previously [35] and the rats without intervertebral space destruction were set as the Sham group. IVDD rats were randomly assigned to four groups according to the site implantation after the surgery: IVDD (PBS), GM (GM hydrogel), Hes@lipo (hes-modified liposomes) and GM/Hes@lipo (GM hydrogel loaded with hes liposome). Briefly, following sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, male Sprague–Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 200–250 g) were placed prone on a 37 °C heating pad. After iodophor disinfection, a longitudinal incision was made along the dorsal tail surface to expose the Co7–8 intervertebral space. A 21-gauge needle was inserted vertically into the NP center. Subsequently, 10 μL of PBS, Hes@lipo suspension, GM precursor solution, or GM/Hes@lipo precursor solution was injected using a 33-gauge microsyringe. Immediately thereafter, in situ photo-crosslinking was performed for 60 s to rapidly induce gelation of the precursor solution within the intervertebral space. The surgical incision was then closed layer by layer to prevent hydrogel leakage. After disinfection, rats were allowed to recover in a warm, ventilated environment. Animals were euthanized at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery, and caudal IVD tissues were collected.

X-ray and MRI

Postoperative disc height was evaluated by X-ray imaging, and the Disc height index (DHI) was calculated to assess disc degeneration. Coronal and axial MRI scans were obtained using a 1.5T MRI scanner (Magnetom Essenza, Germany).

Behavioral assessment of pain function

To evaluate pain-related behaviors, mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia were measured at day 0, 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28 post surgery by investigators blinded to group assignments [35]. For mechanical pain threshold, rats were acclimated in plastic cages for 30 min. The tail region corresponding to the surgical site was stimulated perpendicularly by von Frey filaments (starting at 2.0 g). Each stimulus was applied until the filament bent into a “C” or “S” shape (maximum duration: 8 s). A paw withdrawal or lack of response determined the next filament used. The 50% paw withdrawal threshold was calculated to reflect mechanical sensitivity.

For thermal pain threshold, the Hargreaves test was conducted by irradiating the corresponding surgical site on the tail with an infrared heat source, and withdrawal latency was automatically recorded. A cutoff of 20 s was applied to prevent tissue injury. Each rat underwent four trials per day, and the average latency was recorded as the thermal pain threshold.

Histological staining

Harvested caudal disc specimens were fixed, decalcified and subsequently embedded in paraffin. After dehydration and paraffin embedding, 6-μm-thick serial sections were prepared. For structural evaluation, sections were stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-Fast Green (S-O) [36].

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA) was utilized to conduct all statistical analyses. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from a minimum of three independent experiments. For comparisons involving two groups, Student's t-test was employed, whereas one-way or two-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Degenerated NP tissue exhibits an exacerbated inflammatory phenotype and downregulated Notch signaling

Initially, the severity of intervertebral disc degeneration was assessed via the Pfirrmann grading scale by T2-weighted MRI. Representative images of Grade II and Grade IV discs were displayed to illustrate the degenerative progression. Histological observations further demonstrated that advanced degeneration leads to marked structural derangement and a reduction in cellular density (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To investigate the changes in human NP tissue before and after degeneration, we screened the human Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and selected the GSE266883 dataset. Gene sets corresponding to "Positive regulation of inflammatory response" and "Cytokine production involved in inflammatory response" were found to be upregulated in the degenerated group according to the GSEA results (Fig. 1B). Previous studies have reported the exacerbated inflammatory microenvironment in IVDD [7], which was consistent with the ELISA results (Fig. 1C). The expression of IL-1β was increased in the rat NPCs with TNFα compared to the one without TNFα stimulation. The results suggest that the inflammatory factors have eroded the intervertebral disc microenvironment like a flowing flood. Since HEY1 and HEY2 are well-established target genes directly regulated by the Notch pathway, their expression levels directly reflect the transcriptional activity of this pathway [13].We noted that HEY1 and HEY2, the key downstream effectors of the Notch signaling pathway, were downregulated in the degenerated group (Fig. 1A). To more accurately assess this, we selected mild and severe human NP tissues based on the Pfirrmann grading system and performed immunofluorescence staining on tissue sections by detecting the intracellular domain of Notch1 (NICD1) expression as a target. The results showed that NICD1 was enriched in mildly degenerated NP tissue, whereas its expression decreased as degeneration progressed (Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, we treated rat NPCs with TNFα to establish inflammation. The experimental results showed a similar decrease in NICD1 expression (Fig. 1E). The highly conserved Notch signaling pathway plays an essential role in cellular processes. Some studies suggest its activation serves protective functions in inflammation. [38,39]. Thus, the depletion of NICD1 may be associated with the inflammatory microenvironment of intervertebral disc degeneration. In a word, our finding further supports the notion that the inflammatory microenvironment observed in degenerated intervertebral discs is closely linked to the downregulation of Notch signaling activity.

Hesperetin is identified as a targeted activator of Notch1

To identify natural-source drugs that target Notch1 signaling, preliminary screening of traditional Chinese medicine databases was conducted. From this screening, hes originated from citrus fruits was identified as potential agonist of Notch1. Notably, this finding aligns with some previous research, suggesting that Notch1 may be a candidate target for hes [40]. Molecular docking results showed that hes interacts with NICD1, and the binding energy was predicted to be −8.2 kcal/mol. The results were visualized by Pymol (Fig. 2A). Hes binds within the protein cavity with low free energy via hydrogen bond (red) and π-cation interactions (green). These findings further support hes's structural basis as a potential targeting molecule for NICD1 (Fig. 2B). Previous research has demonstrated that a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive composite nanomaterial based on hes effectively suppressed the Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent kinase II–mediated inflammatory cascade [41]. Interestingly, hes has also been identified as an inhibitor of pyroptosis. To improve the solubility and delivery efficiency of hes, the thin-film dispersion method was used to prepare hes-loaded liposomes (Hes@lipo). We characterized the physicochemical properties of Hes@lipo. The prepared Hes@lipo nanoparticles formed a milky white colloidal suspension (Fig. 2C). According to the DLS measurements, the average hydrodynamic diameter of Hes@lipo was 217.8 ± 12.3 nm, with a low polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.099, indicating a highly uniform size distribution (Fig. 2D). Both blank lipo and Hes@lipo exhibited negative surface charges, indicating that the loading of hesperetin (Hes) did not significantly alter the surface charge properties of the liposomes (Supplementary Fig. S2). Furthermore, TEM revealed that the synthesized liposomes were well-defined, spherical structures with smooth contours (Fig. 2E).

Notch signaling consists of four receptor types (Notch1 to Notch4) and five classic transmembrane ligands, namely Dll1, Dll3, Dll4, Jagged1, and Jagged2 [43]. After undergoing two cleavage events, the generated Notch intracellular domain (NICD) detaches and enters the nucleus. Inside the nucleus, the transcriptional expression of downstream targets is triggered by the interaction between NICD and the transcription factor RBP-Jκ [44].

Subsequently, we performed Western blot and immunofluorescence experiments to verify whether hes could act as an activator of Notch signal. Immunofluorescence results showed that TNFα induction downregulated NICD1 expression (Fig. 2F–H). Treatment with hes did not significantly alter this trend, likely due to its low solubility in the culture medium, which hindered its pharmacological efficacy. In contrast, stimulated by Hes@lipo restored NICD1 expression, confirming that hes can upregulate Notch1-mediated Notch signaling (Fig. 2F–H), which is consistent with previous reports [40]. The Western blot results were consistent with the immunofluorescence findings (Fig. 2G and H).

Characterization of the GM/Hes@lipo hydrogel

Hydrogels, as advanced drug delivery systems, can encapsulate therapeutic agents and form in situ depots at the target site, thereby enabling localized and sustained drug release for long-term [45]. GM hydrogel is widely applied as biomaterial, which serves not only as a cell carrier and tissue scaffold in regenerative medicine but also as a multifunctional platform for constructing controlled drug release systems [46]. In this study, we combined Hes@lipo with GM to successfully fabricate GM/Hes@lipo. Compared to the transparent appearance of GM hydrogel, the appearance of GM/Hes@lipo is opaque and milky (Fig. 3A). SEM results showed that, both GM and GM/Hes@lipo exhibited porous network structures (Fig. 3B). The incorporation of liposomes did not significantly alter the hydrogel architecture. The pore size distribution of both hydrogels was uniform, which was favorable for controlled drug release as well as cellular infiltration and migration (Fig. 3C). The favorable stability of the hydrogels under physiological IVD loading conditions were confirmed by rheological frequency sweeps. As shown in Fig. 3D, both hydrogels displayed a stable profile where the storage modulus (G′) consistently surpassed the loss modulus (G″) throughout the examined frequency spectrum. We further examined the swelling, degradation, and drug release behavior of the two hydrogels. Both hydrogels displayed rapid swelling within the first 6 h (Fig. 3E). Even after 28 days, part of the hydrogel still remained intact (Fig. 3F). The drug release kinetics in vitro of the composite hydrogel exhibited an initial burst release followed by a sustained slow-release phase, with detectable release persisting up to 28 days (Fig. 3G). To evaluate the biocompatibility of the hydrogel system, NPCs were seeded onto the hydrogel surface to simulate the physiological environment in vivo. First, hydrogels containing different concentrations of GM/Hes@lipo were prepared, and their effects on NPC viability were assessed via the CCK-8 assay. The results showed that when the concentration of GM/Hes@lipo exceeded 50 μM, cell viability began to decline, indicating potential cytotoxicity at higher concentrations (Fig. 4A). Therefore, the concentration of 50 μM was selected as the safe dosage for subsequent experiments. GM/Hes@lipo alleviates inflammation of NPCs stimulated by TNFα

To simulate the degenerative inflammatory microenvironment in vitro, rat NPCs were cultured on hydrogel surfaces and treated with TNFα. The experimental groups were set as follows: NPCs cultured on GM hydrogel (GM group), NPCs with TNFα on GM hydrogel (TNFα + GM group), NPCs with TNFα and Hes@lipo simultaneously (TNFα + Hes@lipo group), and NPCs with TNFα on GM/Hes@lipo hydrogel (TNFα + GM/Hes@lipo group).

Tissue homeostasis relies on the dynamic equilibrium between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. IL-1β and IL-6 synergistically amplify inflammatory signaling, promoting inflammatory responses and matrix degradation mediated by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [49]. Conversely, the anti-inflammatory properties of interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) are mediated through the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression. [50]. Based on this, qRT-PCR was performed to quantify the expression of these cytokines. The results revealed that both Hes@lipo and GM/Hes@lipo reversed TNFα–induced upregulation of IL-1β and IL-6 and the concomitant downregulation of IL-4 and IL-10, suggesting a significant alleviation of inflammation (Fig. 5A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) provided additional insight, showing that inflammatory pathways were generally downregulated in the GM/Hes@lipo group (Fig. 6H), confirming that GM/Hes@lipo exerts anti-inflammatory effects.

The results showed that the expression of multiple inflammatory genes, including pyroptosis-associated Nlrp3, Nod2, and Casp1, was significantly downregulated in the NPCs of the GM/Hes@lipo group (Fig. 6I).

GM/Hes@lipo mitigates IVDD-associated pain in rats

Inflammation associated with intervertebral disc degeneration can sensitize nociceptive neurons, leading to hyperalgesia in response to thermal and mechanical stimuli [51]. Previous studies have demonstrated that hes possesses anti-hyperalgesic properties [52]. Based on this, the present study evaluated mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity by the Von Frey and Hargreaves tests, respectively, at specific postoperative time points (Fig. 8A). The results showed that the rats in the IVDD group exhibited a significantly lower 50% withdrawal threshold and a corresponding shorter withdrawal latency compared to those of the sham group, indicating the presence of both mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia. At day 3 post-surgery, all groups exhibited significant pain-related behavioral responses (Fig. 8B and C). However, over the observation time, the rats of the GM/Hes@lipo group showed the most pronounced analgesic effect, as indicated by increased the 50% withdrawal threshold and latency (Fig. 8B and C). To elucidate the underlying in vivo therapeutic mechanisms, samples were collected at 4 and 8 weeks post-treatment for immunofluorescence staining. The IVDD group showed the lowest expression levels of COLII and NICD1, accompanied by the highest expression of MMP13 and NLRP3 (Fig. 10A and B). To this end, the present study provides new insights into the treatment of IVDD by means of natural compounds, but several limitations remain to be addressed. Firstly, while the study focuses on the activation of Notch signaling by hes, its effects on other signaling pathways have not been examined. In particular, apart from the Notch1 signaling pathway, it has been reported that the Notch2 signaling pathway inhibits the progression of IVDD by regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix metabolism [53]. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate the potential multi-target regulatory mechanisms of hes. Secondly, the activation effect of hes on the Notch signaling pathway still requires further validation. In subsequent studies, we will employ the γ-secretase inhibitor DAPT or the Notch1 knockout strategy to assess whether HES can still maintain its protective effect on NPCs, and validate the predicted amino acid residue binding sites through point mutation. Furthermore, although GM/Hes@lipo hydrogel has been shown to alleviate IVDD-related pain, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying analgesia have not been fully elucidated and require further exploration in future studies. Next, specific functional pyroptosis assays, such as LDH release detection and caspase-1 activity measurement, will be incorporated into subsequent studies to further strengthen and complete the research. Finally, the animal IVDD model used in this research differs to some extent from clinical conditions, and the long-term in vivo therapeutic efficacy of GM/Hes@lipo hydrogel as well as its impact on key biomechanical properties has not been systematically evaluated. Future studies should employ more clinically relevant models and comprehensive evaluation systems to further validate the clinical translational potential of this system.

Conclusion

Inspired by the strategy to control the flowing flood in nature, we developed a bioactive factor delivery system as the hydrogel dam for the suppression of inflammatory environment in IVDD. Specifically, the natural flavonoid compound hes was encapsulated within liposomes and subsequently integrated into a GM hydrogel, thereby forming a composite therapeutic platform with sustained drug release capability. Our findings demonstrate that this system effectively activates the Notch signaling, modulates inflammatory cytokine expression, and restores the dynamic balance between ECM synthesis and degradation. Mechanistically, the system was found to inhibit activation of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and downregulate pyroptosis-associated proteins. In vivo experiments further revealed that this therapeutic platform delayed intervertebral disc collapse, preserved the hydration of NP tissue, and alleviated discogenic pain behaviors in IVDD rats. In summary, a Notch signaling-targeted biomimetic hydrogel, as an anti-Inflammatory “flood-control” system, provides a promising strategy for rescue the intervertebral disc from degeneration and pain.
