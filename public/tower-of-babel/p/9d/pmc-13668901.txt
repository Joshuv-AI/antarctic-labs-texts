Perspectives on Analytical Treatment Interruptions and Prospects of an HIV Cure Amongst People Living with HIV and Healthcare Providers in Singapore
Authors: Chiaw Yee Choy, Ying Hao, Matthias Paul Han Sim Toh, Sophia Archuleta, Yii Ean Teh, Edwin Yu Sng Chong, Pei Hua Lee, Barnaby Edward Young, P. Arun Kumar, Zannatul Ferdous, Zhi Hui Tan, Tiane Le, Chern Pang Timothy Chia, Lizawati Binte Hamid, Simon Kiat Loon Lee, Andy Lee, Chen Seong Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Singapore has yet to conduct an HIV cure trial, and analytical treatment interruption (ATI) studies may face community scepticism. This study explores the perspectives of people living with HIV and healthcare providers in Singapore on participating in functional cure research involving ATI. Participants were recruited from four HIV care facilities through study advertisements. Healthcare providers were recruited via email. A self-administered survey gathered demographic data, views on ATI and HIV cure, acceptability of ATI, and tolerance for CD4 and viral load changes during ATI. Descriptive, univariate, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify barriers and motivators to trial participation. Among 200 people living with HIV, 116 (58%) expressed willingness to participate in a cure trial; 84 (42%) did not. Most (94.5%, 189/200) had never joined an HIV clinical study. No significant differences in age, gender, sexuality, or HIV duration were observed between the groups. Factors positively associated with willingness to participate included tertiary education (aOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.13–6.81), altruistic motivations (aOR 1.98, 95% CI 1.52–2.66), and financial compensation (aOR 3.55, 95% CI 1.36–9.88). Lower tolerance for viral load increases or CD4 declines was linked to reduced willingness (aOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.67–0.88). Monitoring frequency, self-testing, and home visits had no significant effect. Healthcare providers were more likely to recommend participation in cure trials if they demonstrated high tolerance for HIV viral load increases (p = 0.044), showed stronger interest in cure research (p = 0.009) and expressed greater acceptance of a functional HIV cure (p = 0.014). Willingness to participate in ATI cure trials is higher among individuals with more education, altruistic intent, and less concern about virologic or immunologic changes. Financial compensation is a strong motivating factor but should not play undue inducement in influencing decision to participate in an ATI cure trial. Community engagement and education are crucial to building support for future HIV cure research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10461-026-05097-3.

Introduction

Since the advent of effective antiretroviral therapy (ART), the morbidity and mortality associated with HIV has been reduced by 80%, making HIV a chronic but incurable disease [1, 2]. Newer ART agents are highly efficacious, with minimal side effects and available as single tablet formulations or 2-monthly injectables, greatly reducing the pill burden faced by people living with HIV [3]. However, despite these advances, lifelong treatment is still required. Without treatment, people living with HIV frequently experience viral rebound, decline in CD4 counts and poor clinical outcomes [4]. The main barrier to achieving a cure is HIV latency, defined as a state in which the HIV virus persists in dormant form within the host cells, particularly in resting T-cells [5]. These viruses can reactivate under certain conditions and complicate viral eradication efforts [5]. Analytical treatment interruption (ATI) is the temporary cessation of ART during HIV cure trials to assess the effects of the interventions in achieving remission or virological control in the absence of ART [6]. However, prolonged cessation of ART may cause a decline of CD4 counts, resulting in opportunistic infection (OI) and mortality [4]. In addition, there are ethical considerations associated with ATI. Participants of ATI studies are unlikely to receive personal benefit from these trials, yet ATI studies put them at risk of worsening health, income loss, and risk of transmission of HIV to sexual partners [7]. HIV cure studies, including those involving ATI, have not yet been conducted in Singapore, where strong emphasis is placed on ART adherence and the national medication compliance rate is high [8].This is supported by the positive prevention program for all newly diagnosed individuals, which places a strong emphasis on “undetectable = untransmittable” (U = U) to promote timely treatment initiation and adherence. In addition, under section 23(1) of Singapore’s Infectious Diseases Act, people living with HIV who engage in sexual activity without disclosing their HIV status risk prosecution unless they maintain a sustained undetectable viral load [9]. Given this context and the lack of local trial experience, ATI studies may be met with scepticism or distrust. This study aims to assess the perspectives of people living with HIV and healthcare providers (HCPs) in Singapore toward functional cure and ATI research, including the acceptability of monitoring strategies and perceived barriers or facilitators to participation. Findings will inform the design and implementation of future trials, support participant engagement, and guide researchers in aligning trial protocols with stakeholder expectations.

Methods

We designed two self-administered surveys, one for people living with HIV (52 questions) and one for HCP (29 questions). Survey questions were developed based on a review of existing literature, with items adapted from previously published studies exploring similar themes. Feedback from the local medical ethics committee was also incorporated during questionnaire development. Due to time constraints, no formal pilot testing of the questionnaires was done. Demographic characteristics of people living with HIV and HCP were collected (supplementary material). Participants were also asked about their understanding of the terms ATI, functional cure, and sterilising cure. Following their response, standardized definitions of these terms were provided within the survey. This ensured that all participants had a common baseline understanding before proceeding to subsequent questions that relied on these concepts. The survey for people living with HIV sought to identify motivating factors for participating in a cure trial and assessed acceptability of monitoring strategies such as frequency of blood tests, viral load (VL) and CD4 count threshold to restart ART. The survey for HCP sought to measure their level of support for people living with HIV enrolling in functional cure trials or trials involving ATI. Both surveys had questions addressing the potential risks associated with ATI study, including HIV transmission to a negative partner, decline in health, risk of OI and risk of developing resistant virus. The surveys were conducted independently.

People living with HIV were recruited via advertisements which were displayed at the clinics of the 4 HIV care centers in Singapore: National Centre for Infectious Diseases (NCID), Singapore General Hospital (SGH), National University Hospital (NUH) and Changi General Hospital (CGH) via convenience sampling method. The advertisements remained on display until the recruitment target was reached and included the inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with a QR code that participants could scan to access the survey. People living with HIV who were Singaporeans or Singapore permanent residents (PR), seeking care at an HIV care facility in Singapore and were 21 years and above were eligible for the survey. HCPs were recruited via email sent to individuals employed in the HIV care centres involved in the care of people living with HIV. A survey link was provided in the email which HCP can use to access the survey if they were interested. HCP were eligible if they were employed as a clinician, pharmacist, nurse or medical social worker, had direct experience in providing clinical HIV care and were 21 years old and above. For both surveys, participants were not eligible if they were below 21 years old, refused to consent and had cognitive impairment resulting in inability to consent. People living with HIV were also excluded if they were foreigners. Participants were reimbursed for their time upon completion of the survey. The surveys were hosted on the online platform, FormSg™ from March 2024 to May 2024. All survey fields were mandatory on FormSG™, ensuring complete responses with no missing data. Participant consent was obtained on the first page of the survey with the participant information sheet. Participants must consent to the survey before proceeding with the rest of the questionnaire.

Ethics approval for the study was granted by the NHG DSRB Domain F2 (2023/00529). The study was supported by the NCID Catalyst Grant.

Analysis

For both surveys of people living with HIV and HCP, we reported proportions for categorical variables and median with interquartile ranges (IQR) for age, our continuous variable. We used χ² tests and Mann-Whitney test to examine associations between variables and two key outcomes: the willingness of people living with HIV in participating in ATI studies, and HCP’s support for people living with HIV’s participation in HIV cure studies.

We assessed internal consistency within domain questions using Cronbach’s Alpha. For domains showing high consistency, we created total scores for use in multivariable models. These domains included barriers (concerns about antiviral resistance risk, general health risks, development of HIV symptoms, and risk of transmission to partners), motivating factors (benefits to others, personal benefits, and advancement of HIV research) and frequency of monitoring (clinical monitoring, CD4 count and VL monitoring). The Cronbach’s Alpha score was 0.90 for the barriers domain, 0.83 for motivating factors domain and 0.92 for frequency of monitoring, indicating high internal consistency across all three domains. Composite scores were calculated as follows: barriers domain used sum of reverse scoring (range 0–12) where higher scores indicate greater concern; motivating factors domain used sum of three items (range 0–9) where higher scores indicate greater perceived importance; and frequency of monitoring domain used reverse scoring (range 0–14) where higher scores indicate preference for more frequent monitoring.

We conducted univariate and multivariable logistic regression to analyse willingness to participate in ATI studies. The predictors included: demographic factors (age, gender, sexuality, education, and monthly income), clinical factors (years since HIV diagnosis and previous participation in HIV clinical trials or research), threshold to restart ART (VL count and CD4 count during ATI), total scores from the barriers, motivating factors, and frequency of monitoring. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test was conducted to check model quality of logistic regression.

All analyses were performed using R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024, R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing_. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. https://www.R-project.org/%3E).

Results

People Living with HIV Survey

200 people living with HIV completed the survey. All questions on the form were answered by 100% of the respondents. 184 (92%) respondents were male, 14 (7%) were female and 2 (1%) were transgender. The median age of the respondents was 43 years old (IQR: 20–75). 126 (63%) respondents self-identified as gay/lesbian, 49 (24.5%) as heterosexual, 12 (24%) as bisexual and 1 (0.5%) as others. 129 (64.5%) respondents were diagnosed with HIV for more than 5 years, of which 64 (49.6%) were diagnosed for more than 10 years. The predominant highest level of education attained within the respondents was a university degree (75, 37.5%). The majority (178, 89%) of the respondents have never participated in HIV-related research prior to this survey. 194 (97%) of the respondents were on ART and 175 (87.5%) reported having undetectable VL (HIV VL < 200 copies/ml).

Knowledge and attitudes towards HIV cure and ATI studies Among respondents who have never participated in a HIV-related trial, the most common reason for not participating was the lack of knowledge of these trials (172, 86%). Most of the respondents were also unaware of the concept of functional cure (151, 75.5%) or ATI (159, 79.5%). After providing the definition of a functional cure, 151 (75.5%) of the respondents felt that a functional cure was at least “probably acceptable”. Of the 200 respondents, 116 (58%) indicated they were likely to participate in a cure trial. Respondents most frequently identified “being no longer at risk of AIDS HIV-related morbidity” as the most important impact of HIV cure (68, 34%). A detailed breakdown of all responses regarding the possibilities and perceived impacts of an HIV cure can be found in the supplementary materials.

Frequency of monitoring Respondents were asked their preference for frequency of clinical, HIV VL and CD4 count monitoring during ATI. 6-monthly monitoring was the most common response, with 63 (31.5%) and 61 (30.5%) of respondents choosing this for HIV VL and CD4 count monitoring respectively (Fig. 1). This was followed by once-a-month monitoring, with 58 (29%) and 61 (30.5%) of respondents choosing this for HIV VL and CD4 count monitoring. Threshold to restart ART Respondents were asked the threshold they would allow their VL to rise if they remained clinically well during treatment interruption. 136 (68%) wanted their VL to remain undetectable throughout treatment interruption, while 27 (13.5%) would allow it to rise to 1000 copies/ml. 15 (7.5%) would allow the VL to rise to 5000 copies/ml and 22(11%) were not concerned on how high their VL will rise during ATI. Likewise, when asked how low they would allow their CD4 count to fall during ATI study if they remained well, 97 (48.5%) indicated their CD4 count would have to remain above 500 cells/µL, while 67 (33.5%) could accept a range of 200–500 cells/µL, 18 (9%) could accept a CD4 count less than 200 cells/µL and 18 (9%) were unconcerned about how low their CD4 count would go during study interruption.

Factors affecting willingness to participate in ATI Altruism was a strong motivating factor for participation in an ATI study, with 120 (60%) respondents indicating that the possibility of benefiting others was very important to them, and 100 (50%) respondents indicating that the possibility of advancing HIV research, even if there was no benefit to them as a very important motivating factor. 111 (55.5%) respondents also indicated that the possibility of benefiting themselves was a very important motivating factor for entering an ATI study. As the consistency of these 3 motivating factors was high, the cumulative effect of each factor was evaluated in the multivariable logistic model using a total score.

The following factors were found to reduce respondent’s willingness to participate in ATI study: risk of developing antiviral resistance during treatment interruption (78, 39%), risk to general health during treatment interruption (74, 37%) and risk of viral rebound or HIV-related illnesses during treatment interruptions (82, 41%). They were also included in the multivariable logistic model as a cumulative score to evaluate the overall effect on the probability of participation.

In the multivariate analysis, those with at least tertiary education were more likely than others to participate in an ATI study (aOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.13–6.81). In addition, respondents with motivations such as advancing research or benefiting others and oneself were more likely to participate in a study (aOR 1.98, 95% CI 1.52–2.66), where an increase in each score would almost double the odds of participation (Table 1). Conversely, respondents with the following concerns were less likely to participate: concerns about risk of transmission, concerns about general health, risk of developing OI and risk of resistance, where an increase in each score would reduce the odds of participating in a cure trial by 23% (aOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.67–0.88). No significant differences in age, gender, sexuality, or duration of HIV diagnosis were observed between those likely to participate and those unlikely to do so (Table 1). While univariate analysis suggested that financial compensation, less frequent monitoring, self-testing options, and home visits by a nurse positively influenced likelihood of participation, only financial compensation was a significant factor in the multivariate analysis (aOR 3.55, 95% CI 1.36–9.88).

Our survey revealed strong interest in HIV cure research but also highlighted a disconnect between participant expectations and the requirements of ATI trials. In particular, concerns about viraemia—especially regarding transmission risk, general health, OI, and resistance—warrant further exploration through focused engagement. Given Singapore’s legal framework on HIV disclosure, patient information sheets should clearly include the relevant provisions of the ID Act. While offering preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) to partners appeared to be a logical solution to this conundrum, most respondents indicated that providing PrEP would not influence their willingness to interrupt treatment. This could be due to the widespread promotion of U = U messaging and recent legal amendments reducing disclosure obligations for those with undetectable VL [9, 19]. As a result, participants may not routinely disclose their status to partners, limiting their ability to recommend PrEP. A similar concern regarding disclosure was also reported by a study done in Thailand, where many respondents do not disclose their status to their partners, which was identified as a barrier to transmission risk reduction [13]. Alternatively, PrEP uptake is still suboptimal in Singapore and it is possible that participants may not be familiar with the concept of PrEP [20, 21]. Lastly, as functional cure and ATI remain novel concepts to most patients, co-developing accessible educational materials—such as infographics, videos, or quizzes as part of the consent process—may improve understanding and support informed participation. Ultimately, aligning trial design with stakeholder concerns is key to the ethical and effective conduct of HIV cure studies. Healthcare Providers Survey

100 HCPs completed this survey. All questions on the form were answered by 100% of the respondents. 76% of the respondents were female and the median age of the respondents was 36 years old (IQR: 23–58). 46% have practiced more than 10 years and 48% have at least 5 years of practice in HIV clinical care. The full characteristics of the respondents can be found in the supplementary material.

Knowledge and attitudes towards HIV trials Among HCPs, 61% expressed strong interest in HIV cure studies, while 34% showed limited interest. However, 58% were unaware that treatment interruption is a component of cure trials. Although 40% had heard of the term “functional cure,” only 32% understood its meaning. Notably, 95% had never enrolled a patient in a cure-focused trial. Overall, support for HIV cure research was high: only 2% indicated they would not allow patients to enroll in ATI studies, 2% indicated they would not support patients under their care if they wished to enroll in such studies and 5% indicated they would not actively promote ATI studies to their patients. Additional findings on knowledge and attitudes towards ATI studies in HCP can be found in the supplementary material.

Threshold to restart ART Among HCPs, 10% would not permit any period of detectable viraemia during ATI, even if patients remained clinically stable. In contrast, 24% would allow 4–6 months of viraemia, 23% would allow 1–3 months, 10% would allow 7–12 months, 9% up to 1 month, and 24% would accept viraemia for as long as required, provided patients remained well and CD4 counts were stable.

Regarding CD4 count thresholds during ATI, 60% of HCP preferred levels to remain between 200 and 500 cells/µL, 19% accepted levels < 200 cells/µL, and 15% preferred levels > 500 cells/µL. 6% reported CD4 count did not matter if the patient remained well.

For eligibility to enroll in an ATI trial, 41% of HCP preferred patients to have CD4 counts > 500 cells/µL, 43% between 200 and 500 cells/µL, and 11% accepted < 200 cells/µL; 5% had no CD4 threshold concerns if patients were clinically stable.

Factors affecting willingness to participate in ATI HCPs expressed significant concerns regarding ATI studies. Transmission risk was the most cited concern (95%), followed by the risk of OI (88%), antiretroviral resistance (86%), and adverse effects on general health (86%) (Fig. 2). Univariate analysis showed HCPs were more likely to support participation in cure trials if they had higher tolerance for HIV VL increases (p = 0.044), stronger interest in cure research (p = 0.009), and greater acceptance of a functional cure (p = 0.014). Demographic and professional characteristics, such as age, gender, sexuality, profession, and years of practice, did not show significant correlation with likelihood to support cure trial participation. Discussion

This is the first qualitative survey investigating the perspective and attitudes of people living with HIVand HCP in Singapore towards a functional cure trial and ATI study. Despite the relative inexperience of the local population to these studies, most participants in both groups expressed support for such trials after the definition of ATI was shared. While this is promising, there are differing expectations between the community and trial conventions that must be addressed for a successful collaboration.

When given a choice for frequency of monitoring, most people living with HIV preferred the longest intervals for HIV viral load and CD4 count checks, aligning with current local practice of biannual follow-up for well-controlled HIV [3]. In contrast, consensus guidelines for ATI studies recommend weekly monitoring during the first 12 weeks, yet only 10% of people living with HIV selected weekly monitoring for either test (Fig. 1) [10]. Despite this preference for less frequent monitoring, frequency of monitoring did not significantly influence willingness to join a cure trial in the multivariate analysis. We postulate two reasons for this. First, unfamiliarity with ATI or HIV cure trials likely meant people living with HIV were unaware that weekly monitoring is standard. Second, the survey questions referencing “more” or “less” frequent monitoring did not specify exact frequencies (supplementary material). As a result, people living with HIV may have undervalued monitoring frequency as a deciding factor, leading to the mixed responses observed. A more in-depth qualitative approach and targeted engagements are needed to better align participant expectations with trial requirements and clarify the rationale for more intensive monitoring in ATI studies.

Both people living with HIV and HCPs expressed concern about viraemia during ATI, but people living with HIV showed significantly lower tolerance. While 68% of people living with HIV preferred to remain virologically undetectable throughout ATI, only 10% of HCPs indicated zero tolerance for any viraemia. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, all people diagnosed with HIV in Singapore undergo positive prevention counselling, which emphasizes strict ART adherence and the “undetectable = untransmissible” (U = U) message—an approach reflected in the country’s strong 95-95-95 outcomes [8]. Hence, the concept of ATI may conflict with this messaging, as many may be unfamiliar with its scientific rationale. Next, under section 23(1) of Singapore’s Infectious Diseases Act, people living with HIV risk prosecution if they engage in sexual activity without disclosing their HIV status, unless they have sustained undetectable viral load [9]. Therefore, those undergoing ATI who become viraemic would be legally obligated to disclose their status, which may explain their lower tolerance for viraemia compared to HCPs [9, 11].

However, viraemia is likely unavoidable in ATI studies, as most recent trials define viral rebound as a VL of ≥ 1000 copies/mL on two consecutive measurements before ART is resumed [10, 12]. This typically results in at least two weeks of detectable viraemia before resuming ART, which contrasts with the preferences expressed by many people living with HIV. On the other hand, HCPs are likely more familiar with the concept of ATI study design and recognise that viraemia, while inevitable, can be managed with close monitoring.

Similarly, 48.5% of people living with HIV preferred their CD4 count to remain above 500 cells/µL, whereas 60% of HCPs were comfortable with levels between 200 and 500 cells/µL. This likely reflects shared awareness of the link between low CD4 counts and increased risk of OI, as demonstrated in the SMART trial [4]. However, while HCPs may recognise that OI typically occur at CD4 count less than 200 cells/µL, people living with HIV may feel safer maintaining a higher CD4 threshold when participating in ATI study that carry additional risk, contributing to the observed difference in acceptance.

These concerns expressed by people living with HIV are consistent with findings from other settings, including a study conducted in Thailand [13, 14]. Similarly, a comparable dichotomy in tolerance between healthcare providers and people living with HIV has been reported in an Australian study, suggesting that these differences may be observed across diverse sociocultural and healthcare contexts [14].

Despite the differences in tolerance for viraemia and CD4 level seen in both groups, both groups also reported similar concerns, including risk of HIV transmission, OI and resistance during ATI trials. These shared concerns, alongside differing thresholds of acceptable risk, underscore the need for nuanced, tailored communication when engaging both people living with HIV and HCPs to support the successful conduct of ATI studies.

Altruism—specifically, the desire to benefit others and advance science—was a key motivator for participation in ATI studies, a finding consistent with previous researches [7, 13, 15–17]. As in other studies, financial compensation was also identified as a motivator. Although other studies suggest it is rarely the primary motivation, this aspect is not clearly determined in this study [15, 16]. While it is important that participants are compensated for their time and commitment, it should not play an undue inducement on a participant’s decision to join a trial [17]. Results from this study suggest that the recruitment strategy should be careful not to put a heavy emphasis on financial compensation but focus more on altruistic considerations and resource allocation instead.

This study had several limitations. First, it was conducted in English, potentially excluding non-English speakers. Second, participation required scanning a QR code, which could have limited access for older adults and those from lower-income households who may lack the necessary technology or digital literacy. Voluntary access to the surveys also introduced selection bias, favouring individuals already interested in cure research. Future studies could improve accessibility through multilingual surveys and active recruitment. In addition, attitudes toward ATI and related ethical or legal issues were self-reported and may have been influenced by social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of HIV status, transmission risk, and legal obligations. Although the survey instrument was informed by existing literature, the absence of external validation may have affected measurement precision. Future studies should consider externally validating the instrument to enhance robustness and facilitate cross-study comparisons. Lastly, the sample size was small due to funding constraints, though it remains broadly representative of Singapore’s HIV population, which is predominantly male [18]. Future qualitative or mixed-methods studies may help to explore underlying beliefs, ethical reasoning, and contextual factors in greater depth.

Conclusions

This is the first study in Singapore to assess the perspectives of people living with HIV and HCPs toward an ATI study. Key predictors of trial participation among patients included having a tertiary education and altruistic motivations, while HCP support was associated with greater tolerance for viraemia, stronger interest in cure research, and higher acceptance of a functional cure. Despite strong interest in cure trials, discrepancies emerged between respondent expectations and trial requirements, particularly regarding monitoring frequency and viraemia. Stakeholder engagement is essential to address these concerns. In Singapore, the consent process should clearly communicate the legal implications of viraemia under the ID Act. While HIV-related policies differ by country, our findings highlight the need to contextualize study materials to local contexts. Close collaboration between researchers and community partners is critical to developing accessible education materials that convey the scientific and legal dimensions of ATI studies during informed consent.
