Hearing impairment and depression in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: the potential intermediary role of sleep duration in the CHARLS cohort
Authors: Chenxi Zhang, Yan Xu, Dongcai Li, Peng Zhang, Yanyan Wang, Qing Wan, Juanjuan Li, Xianhai Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHearing impairment has been associated with depressive symptoms in older adults. Sleep duration may represent a potential contributing factor linking these conditions.MethodsWe analyzed data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2015), including 12,934 participants aged ≥ 45 years. Hearing impairment was defined by self-report, depressive symptoms by CES-D10 ≥ 10, and sleep duration as average nightly sleep hours. Logistic regression models were used to estimate associations, adjusting for sociodemographic and health-related covariates. To explore whether sleep duration contributed to the observed association, we compared effect estimates before and after adjustment for sleep duration.ResultsHearing impairment was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms (OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.69–1.97). After further adjustment for sleep duration, the association was slightly attenuated (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.63–1.91), suggesting that sleep duration may contribute modestly to the observed relationship. Stratified analyses showed similar patterns across subgroups.ConclusionsSelf-reported hearing impairment is associated with depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Sleep duration may partially contribute to this association, although causal inference cannot be established.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04215-z.Keywords: Hearing impairment, Sleep duration, Depression, CHARLS, Old adults

Introduction

Hearing impairment is one of the most prevalent chronic conditions affecting older adults worldwide. The World Health Organization estimates that by 2050, nearly 2.5 billion people will experience some degree of hearing loss, with over 700 million requiring rehabilitation [1]. In China, the prevalence of hearing impairment among individuals aged 60 years and above exceeds 30%, representing a major burden for families and healthcare systems [2]. Beyond communication difficulties, hearing impairment has been increasingly recognized as a risk factor for adverse health outcomes, including social isolation, cognitive decline, poor quality of life, and mental disorders [3].

Among these outcomes, depression stands out as a particularly important consequence. Depression is a leading cause of disability globally and is projected to become the top contributor to disease burden in the coming decades [4]. Older adults with hearing impairment often report higher rates of depressive symptoms, with odds ratios ranging from 1.4 to 2.0 in prior studies [5]. This relationship may be driven by social withdrawal, stigma, reduced participation in daily activities, and biological mechanisms affecting the central nervous system [4]. Despite growing recognition, the pathways linking hearing impairment to depression remain incompletely understood.

Sleep disturbance has emerged as a plausible pathway through which hearing impairment may contribute to depression [6]. Poor hearing is frequently accompanied by tinnitus, nocturnal arousal, and heightened stress, all of which can compromise sleep quality and duration [6]. Sleep problems, in turn, are well-established risk factors for depression in middle-aged and older adults [7]. However, the potential intermediary role of sleep in the hearing–depression relationship has rarely been tested empirically, particularly in longitudinal population-based studies. Most prior work has focused on either direct association between hearing impairment and depression or separate associations involving sleep disturbance, leaving a critical knowledge gap regarding their interconnections.

Several limitations in the existing literature warrant further investigation. First, most studies on hearing impairment and depression have been cross-sectional, limiting the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, few analyses have formally evaluated whether sleep explains part of the association between hearing impairment and depression. Third, despite the high prevalence of both conditions in China, there remains a scarcity of population-based evidence, especially using longitudinal datasets such as the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Finally, little is known about potential heterogeneity, including whether sex, age, or residential setting modifies these associations, despite well-documented disparities in hearing, sleep, and depression risk.

To address these gaps, the present study used nationally representative data from CHARLS (2011–2015) to examine the association between hearing impairment and depression and to evaluate the potential potential intermediary role of sleep duration. Specifically, our objectives were to: (1) assess the cross-sectional relationship between hearing impairment and depression at baseline (2011); (2) test whether sleep duration mediates this association; (3) conduct stratified and interaction analyses by sex, age, and rural–urban residence to explore subgroup differences; and (4) extend the analysis longitudinally to evaluate the temporal pathway from hearing impairment (2011) through sleep duration (2013) to depression (2015). We hypothesized that hearing impairment would be positively associated with depression, that shorter sleep duration would partially mediate this relationship, and that the mediation effect would be more pronounced in vulnerable subgroups such as women and older adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study combined cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). CHARLS is a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years and their spouses, initiated in 2011, with follow-up every two years. The baseline survey in 2011 included 17,708 participants across 150 counties and 450 communities. For the present analyses, we included individuals with complete data on hearing status, depressive symptoms, sleep duration, and covariates. Participants with missing values in any of these variables were excluded using a complete-case approach. The final baseline analytic sample comprised N = 12,934 individuals (mean age = 59.4 years, SD = 10.2; 47.9% female). For the longitudinal mediation analysis, we constructed a panel of respondents observed at 2011 (Wave 1), 2013 (Wave 2), and 2015 (Wave 3) with complete data across waves (N = 9,678).

Measures

Hearing status was assessed by self-report. Participants rated their hearing as excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor. We defined hearing impairment as self-rated hearing of “fair” or “poor” (coded as 1), whereas “excellent”, “very good” and “good”, were classified as no hearing impairment (coded as 0). Depression was measured using the validated 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES-D10). Scores range from 0–30, with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms. Consistent with established cutoffs, a score ≥ 10 was used to define probable depression (coded 1 vs. 0). Participants reported their average nightly hours of sleep. Responses outside the 0–16 h range were set to missing as implausible. This variable was treated as a continuous mediator. We adjusted for demographic and health-related confounders identified in prior research Age (continuous, in years), Sex (male/female), Education level (ordinal: none, primary, secondary, college or above), Residence (urban vs. rural), Body mass index (BMI), calculated from measured height and weight (kg/m2), Chronic conditions, defined as physician-diagnosed hypertension, diabetes, stroke, heart disease, lung disease, liver/kidney disease, or asthma; we summed the number of conditions to form a comorbidity count. All covariates were measured at baseline (2011).

Statistical analysis

We first summarized sample characteristics overall and by hearing status, including prevalence of hearing impairment and depression. Differences between groups were compared using χ2 tests (categorical variables) or t-tests (continuous variables). We tested whether sleep duration mediates the association between hearing impairment and depression at baseline (2011). We conducted mediation analyses using a regression-based decomposition approach. Path a: Linear regression predicting sleep hours from hearing impairment and covariates. Path b: Logistic regression predicting depression from sleep hours, adjusting for hearing impairment and covariates. Total effect (c): Logistic regression predicting depression from hearing impairment, adjusting for covariates. Direct effect (c′): Logistic regression predicting depression from both hearing impairment and sleep, adjusting for covariates. Indirect effect (a × b): Product of coefficients approach on the log-odds scale. We used delta method standard errors for indirect effects and, in sensitivity checks, performed non-parametric bootstrap resampling (2,000 replicates) to verify the robustness of mediation results. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

To explore effect heterogeneity, we repeated mediation models stratified by sex, age group (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), and residence (urban vs. rural). We additionally fit models including interaction terms (hearing × sex, hearing × age, hearing × comorbidity count) to test moderation effects. We conducted mediation analyses using a regression-based decomposition approach to estimate total, direct, and indirect associations between hearing impairment and depressive symptoms through sleep duration. These analyses were intended to decompose observed associations rather than to estimate causal effects under formal counterfactual assumptions. Additive interaction was evaluated by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion (AP). Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for smoking status and alcohol consumption.. Predictor: Hearing impairment at Wave 1 (2011), Mediator: Sleep duration at Wave 2 (2013), Outcome: Depression at Wave 3 (2015). Logistic and linear regression models were fit with the same covariate set measured at baseline. Indirect effects were calculated as above. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 (statsmodels, pandas, matplotlib). All tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample characteristics

In the analytic baseline sample (2011), 12,934 participants aged ≥ 45 years were included after complete-case restriction. The mean age was 58.9 years (SD = 10.2), and 47.9% were female. The prevalence of self-rated hearing impairment (“fair” or “poor”) was 14.9%, while the prevalence of depressive symptoms (CES-D10 ≥ 10) was 37.4%. Participants with hearing impairment exhibited a substantially higher prevalence of depression compared with those without impairment (44.7% vs. 28.1%, p < 0.001). Average self-reported sleep duration was 6.32 h (SD = 1.88). Individuals with hearing impairment reported significantly shorter sleep than their peers without impairment (6.19 vs. 6.48 h, p < 0.001). Cross-sectional mediation analysis

Table 2 summarizes the regression estimates for the total, direct, and indirect associations between hearing impairment and depression, with sleep duration modeled as a mediator. The total effect indicated that hearing impairment was significantly associated with a higher risk of depression (adjusted OR = 1.83, 95% CI [1.69, 1.97], p < 0.001). After accounting for sleep duration, the direct effect of hearing impairment remained strong (OR = 1.77, 95% CI [1.63, 1.91], p < 0.001). In addition, a significant indirect effect was observed, whereby shorter sleep duration partially explained the association (indirect OR = 1.06, 95% CI [1.04, 1.08]). This mediation pathway accounted for approximately 6% of the overall hearing–depression relationship, suggesting that while sleep plays a role, the majority of the effect reflects direct pathways.

Stratified analyses

Figure 1 displays the stratified total effects of hearing impairment on depression. Across all subgroups, hearing impairment was consistently associated with a higher risk of depression. The effect was comparable in men (adjusted OR = 1.82, 95% CI [1.62, 2.06]) and women (OR = 1.84, 95% CI [1.66, 2.04]), although women demonstrated a slightly larger indirect pathway via sleep (indirect OR = 1.09). By age group, the association was evident both in participants < 60 years (OR = 1.86, 95% CI [1.68, 2.06]) and those ≥ 60 years (OR = 1.78, 95% CI [1.58, 2.00]); in the latter, the indirect effect through sleep was also somewhat stronger (indirect OR = 1.08). Residential status analyses showed higher odds among rural (OR = 1.89, 95% CI [1.72, 2.08]) and urban residents (OR = 1.70, 95% CI [1.49, 1.94]), with urban participants exhibiting relatively greater sleep mediation (indirect OR = 1.08). Taken together, these results suggest that while the overall hearing–depression association is consistent across groups, the contribution of sleep as a mediator may be more pronounced in women, older adults, and urban residents.

Interaction analyses

Formal interaction tests did not reveal significant moderating effects of sex, age, or comorbidity burden on the association between hearing impairment and depression. Specifically, the hearing × sex interaction was nonsignificant (OR = 1.03, p = 0.737), as were the interactions with continuous age (OR = 0.999, p = 0.819) and comorbidity count (OR = 0.97, p = 0.399). These results indicate that the subgroup differences observed in stratified analyses are more likely attributable to baseline variations in risk rather than true effect modification. The overall mediation pathway is illustrated in Fig. 2, which shows the cross-sectional model linking hearing impairment to depression through sleep duration. Although the indirect effect via sleep was statistically significant, its magnitude was modest compared with the direct pathway. Exposure–mediator interaction terms between hearing impairment and sleep duration were tested and were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S2). RERI and AP estimates were close to zero, indicating no meaningful interaction on the additive scale (Supplementary Table S2).

Longitudinal Mediation (2011 → 2013 → 2015)

Using three waves of CHARLS data (2011–2015), we further tested the temporal pathway from hearing impairment to depression through sleep duration. As shown in Table 3, baseline hearing impairment in 2011 significantly predicted an increased risk of depression in 2015 (OR = 1.39, 95% CI [1.27, 1.52], p < 0.001). This effect remained robust after adjusting for sleep duration in 2013 (direct effect OR = 1.36, 95% CI [1.24, 1.49], p < 0.001). Importantly, sleep duration partially mediated this relationship, with an indirect effect corresponding to an OR of 1.03 (95% CI [1.01, 1.04]), indicating a small but statistically significant pathway. These findings are illustrated in Fig. 3, which depicts the longitudinal mediation model (2011 hearing → 2013 sleep → 2015 depression), highlighting both the direct and indirect pathways.

Discussion

Given the observational nature of the CHARLS data, mediation results should be interpreted as statistical decomposition of associations rather than evidence of causal pathways. In this nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we observed a robust association between hearing impairment and elevated risk of depressive symptoms. Cross-sectional analyses showed that individuals with self-rated hearing impairment had 83% higher odds of depression compared with their peers without hearing impairment, even after adjusting for sociodemographic and health-related covariates. Sleep duration partially mediated this relationship, though the proportion explained was modest. This finding is consistent with recent large-scale longitudinal evidence demonstrating that persistently short sleep duration is associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms in aging populations [8, 9]. Together, these findings support sleep as a relevant but non-dominant pathway linking sensory impairment and depression. Stratified analyses revealed consistent associations across sex, age, and residential subgroups, with somewhat stronger indirect effects through sleep among women, older adults, and urban residents. Interaction models did not detect significant effect modification, suggesting subgroup differences mainly reflect variations in baseline vulnerability. Importantly, longitudinal mediation analyses confirmed temporal ordering: hearing impairment in 2011 predicted reduced sleep duration in 2013, which in turn contributed to greater depression risk in 2015. Although the indirect effect was small (indirect OR ~ 1.03), it was statistically significant, highlighting sleep as a meaningful, though not exclusive, pathway linking hearing loss and depression.

Our findings align with extensive literature documenting comorbidity between hearing impairment and depression. Importantly, hearing impairment rarely occurs in isolation. Recent evidence indicates that the combined burden of hearing loss and other functional or sensory impairments—such as vision loss or mobility limitations—is associated with substantially worse mental health outcomes than single impairments alone [10]. Prior studies in China and elsewhere have observed similar magnitudes. For example, a recent CHARLS-based study reported that fair or poor hearing was associated with increased prevalence of depression (adjusted ORs ~ 1.24–1.34) after controlling for age, sex, residence, education, and chronic conditions [4]. The observed partial mediation through sleep echoes reports that hearing problems disturb sleep by increasing nocturnal arousal, tinnitus, and psychosocial distress, which are known contributors to both reduced sleep and mood disorders [11–13]. For instance, in a study of Chinese older adults, sleep duration was among the main predictors of depressive symptoms in hearing-impaired participants [14]. Moreover, global evidence shows that sensory impairment is strongly linked to depressive symptoms, as demonstrated in Western cohorts and meta-analyses [5, 15, 16].

The subgroup differences we observed are noteworthy. Women and older adults showed larger indirect effects via sleep, consistent with epidemiological evidence that these groups are more vulnerable to both sleep disturbance and depression [17, 18]. Although hearing impairment was more prevalent among rural residents, the indirect effect through sleep was relatively stronger in urban participants, possibly due to urban environmental stressors such as noise and light pollution [19–21]. Similar findings were noted in the Zhang et al. [4] study from China, where place of residence moderated the hearing-depression association [4].

Potential mechanisms

Several mechanisms may underlie the observed associations. First, hearing loss can directly increase psychosocial stress and social isolation, both established risk factors for depression [22, 23]. Second, hearing impairment may disrupt sleep via tinnitus-related arousal, reduced auditory safety perception, and altered circadian regulation, thereby indirectly contributing to depressive symptoms [8, 9, 24]. Third, biological mechanisms such as shared vascular or neurodegenerative pathways could jointly affect hearing, sleep, and mood regulation [25, 26]. The modest size of the indirect effect indicates that sleep is one of multiple pathways linking hearing and depression, but nonetheless represents a clinically relevant target given its modifiability.

Clinical and public health implications

Our results have several practical implications. First, they reinforce the importance of routine hearing screening in older adults, not only for communication health but also for mental health surveillance. Second, integrating sleep assessment and management into hearing care may offer dual benefits: mitigating sleep problems and reducing downstream depression risk. Third, targeted interventions for high-risk subgroups—particularly older women and urban residents—may yield greater mental health benefits. From a policy perspective, multidisciplinary programs combining audiology, sleep medicine, and mental health services could be particularly effective in addressing these interconnected problems in aging populations.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, hearing impairment and sleep duration were based on self-report, which may introduce measurement bias. Objective audiometric and polysomnographic assessments would provide greater precision. Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment for multiple sociodemographic and health covariates. Third, the indirect effect sizes were modest, and the mediation analysis relied on parametric assumptions and δ-method approximations rather than full bootstrap resampling due to computational constraints. Finally, depressive symptoms were assessed with CES-D10, which captures risk but does not substitute for clinical diagnosis. Although the longitudinal design with temporally ordered measures (2011–2013–2015) strengthens inference compared with cross-sectional analyses, it does not fully capture time-varying or bidirectional relationships between hearing impairment and sleep duration. Future studies using cross-lagged panel models or formal causal mediation frameworks are warranted. Measures of functional limitation (e.g., ADL/IADL) were not included due to incomplete harmonization across waves and potential over-adjustment, as they may lie on the causal pathway between hearing impairment and depressive symptoms.

Future directions

Future research should employ objective measures of hearing and sleep, explore additional mediators such as social isolation, tinnitus distress, or cognitive impairment, and test whether interventions targeting hearing loss (e.g., hearing aids, cochlear implants) or sleep problems (e.g., CBT-I) reduce depression risk. Mechanistic studies linking auditory processing, sleep architecture, and neural circuits of mood regulation will also be valuable. Given the growing aging population in China, integrating hearing, sleep, and mental health care into community-based programs is both timely and necessary. Future studies should consider incorporating both subjective and objective sleep measures (e.g., actigraphy, polysomnography) to more fully capture sleep’s potential intermediary role.

Conclusion

In conclusion, hearing impairment is strongly associated with increased risk of depression among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Sleep duration partially mediates this relationship, with stronger effects observed among women, older adults, and urban residents. Longitudinal analyses confirm that sleep represents a statistically significant, though modest, pathway linking hearing loss to later depression. These findings underscore the need for integrated prevention strategies addressing hearing health, sleep hygiene, and mental well-being in aging populations.
