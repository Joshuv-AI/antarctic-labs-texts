Genome-wide identification and expression analysis of the Glutathione S-transferase (GST) gene family in Avicennia marina under salt and auxin stress
Authors: Zhian Lin, Muhammad Azhar Hussain, Yingying Li, Jiali Zhang, Sihui Li, Pengyu Chen, Qi Zhang, Junfeng Niu, Chenjing Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlutathione S-transferases (GSTs) play crucial roles in plant stress responses, particularly in salt stress acclimation, by participating in reactive oxygen species scavenging, detoxification, and regulation of hormone signaling. Avicennia marina, a pioneer mangrove species, exhibits long-term adaptation to extreme habitats such as high salinity, yet its GST gene family has not been systematically characterized.ResultsThis study provides the first genome-wide characterization and identification of the GST gene family in A. marina, identifying 25 AmGST genes classified into five subfamilies: GSTU, GSTF, GSTT, EF1G and TCHQD. Evolutionary analysis indicated that AmGSTs have undergone strong purifying selection. Gene structure and conserved motif analyses revealed subfamily-specific sequence patterns. Promoter analysis identified numerous cis-acting elements associated with stress responses and hormone regulation. Homology modeling and molecular docking predicted that three representative AmGST proteins, AmEF1G02, AmGSTF03 and AmGSTU08, can bind to glutathione (GSH) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) through multiple hydrogen bonds, suggesting their potential role in antioxidant defense and hormone modulation. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis showed distinct expression patterns of different AmGSTs under salt treatment and exogenous auxin treatment. Notably, AmGSTF03 was consistently upregulated by salt and further enhanced by IAA, while the auxin-specific responsiveness of AmEF1G02 (strongly induced by IAA regardless of salinity) suggests its role as a potential crosstalk node between auxin signaling and detoxification pathways. AmGSTU08 exhibited early transient upregulation under salt and auxin, supporting its involvement in rapid stress responses.ConclusionThis study establishes that the A. marina GST family has been shaped by strong purifying selection and subfamily-specific structural divergence, highlighting the evolutionary conservation and functional diversity of AmGSTs. The potential dual binding ability of some protein members represented by AmEF1G02, AmGSTF03, and AmGSTU08 to GSH and IAA, as well as their different expression patterns, suggests that AmGSTs may serve as a molecular bridge connecting auxin signaling and antioxidant defense. These findings systematically reveal the composition, evolutionary features, and potential functions of the A. marina GST family in salinity stress and hormone regulation, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms of salt tolerance in mangroves and candidate gene resources for improving crop salt tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09342-8.Keywords: Mangroves plants, Abiotic stress, GST gene family, Phylogenetic analysis, Expression analysis

Introduction

Mangroves, as vital components of typical coastal wetland ecosystems, are widely distributed in tropical and subtropical intertidal zones. They play irreplaceable roles in maintaining coastal ecological balance, shoreline protection, carbon sequestration, water purification, and biodiversity conservation [1]. Mangrove trees have evolved unique salt-tolerance mechanisms to survive in extreme environments characterized by high salinity, hypoxia, and dramatic tidal fluctuations [2]. Physiologically, they exclude 80–97% of salt via ultrafiltration by roots, secrete approximately 40% of absorbed salt through leaf salt glands, and protect tissues from residual salt damage via osmoregulation, ion compartmentalization, antioxidant enzymes, and compatible solutes [3]. At the molecular level, mangroves have evolved various adaptive strategies, including ion transporters, osmolyte biosynthesis enzymes, and antioxidant systems. They use these strategies to cope with cellular water imbalance, membrane damage, and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation induced by high salinity, which may induce oxidative damage and growth inhibition [4, 5]. Avicennia marina, a pioneer mangrove species, exhibits remarkable salt tolerance, capable of normal growth at 10‰-20‰ seawater salinity and surviving extreme salinities up to 30‰-40‰ [6]. This exceptional tolerance makes A. marina an ideal model species for studying plant salt tolerance mechanisms in mangroves.

During long-term evolution, plants have developed a series of molecular mechanisms to cope with environmental stresses. The glutathione S-transferase (GST) family, a widely distributed multifunctional enzyme family predominantly located in the cytoplasm [7, 8], plays a critical role in plant stress responses. GSTs catalyze the conjugation of reduced glutathione (GSH) with various electrophilic substrates, including lipid peroxides, reactive aldehydes, and xenobiotics, participating in cellular detoxification, redox homeostasis, hormone signal transduction, and secondary metabolism [9]. Plant GSTs typically consist of two domains: an N-terminal thioredoxin-like domain (G-site) that binds GSH, and a C-terminal domain (H-site) that binds hydrophobic substrates [10]. Based on sequence and structural features, plant GSTs are classified into 14 subfamilies, of which eight are widely distributed: tau (GSTU), phi (GSTF), theta (GSTT), zeta (GSTZ), lambda (GSTL), dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR), tetrachlorohydroquinone dehalogenase (TCHQD), and the γ-subunit of eukaryotic translation elongation factor (EF1G) [11]. Tau and Phi classes are particularly abundant in plants and are extensively involved in abiotic stress responses such as salt, drought, heavy metal, and cold stress [12, 13]. GST families have been characterized in various plants including Solanum lycopersicum L. [14], Triticum aestivum [15], Populus trichocarpa [16], and Betula platyphylla [17]. Comparative analyses of GST gene numbers, types, and expression patterns across species reveal family diversity and functions. GST members in many plants respond to salt stress; for instance, SlGSTL in S. lycopersicum L., TaGSTU in T. aestivum, PtrGSTU in P. trichocarpa, and BpGSTF in B. platyphylla are significantly upregulated under salt treatment, suggesting a potential role in maintaining cellular oxidative homeostasis and enhancing salt tolerance [14–17].

The functional diversity of plant GSTs is not limited to catalytic detoxification, but also closely interacts with plant hormone signaling pathways. Auxin is a key hormone regulating various growth and developmental processes throughout the plant life cycle and plays a critical role in stress adaptation [18]. Early studies demonstrated that some GSTs can act as ligand-binding proteins in the absence of GSH, participating in auxin transport and signal transduction [19, 20]. Recent studies have further revealed that the transcriptional repressor SmIAA15 in the auxin signaling pathway of eggplant negatively regulates plant resistance to auxin herbicides by modulating the glutathione metabolism pathway and the expression of multiple GST genes [21]. These findings collectively indicate that the auxin signaling pathway and the GST/GSH system are functionally interconnected, and that this signal crosstalk plays a critical role in plant responses to abiotic stress and auxin-related chemicals.

In mangrove plants chronically exposed to high salinity and tidal fluctuations, this regulatory relationship between GSTs and auxin signaling may be of particularly adaptive significance. Previous genomic studies have shown that stress-responsive genes (including those involved in signal transduction and detoxification) in A. marina have undergone lineage-specific expansion driven by segmental duplication and selective pressure [22]. Such evolutionary processes may lead to differences in the structure and regulatory networks of certain gene families between mangroves (e.g., A. marina) and terrestrial plants, thereby supporting the unique physiological and hormonal regulation required for survival in saline intertidal habitats [23]. Under the combined stress of high salinity and auxin signaling, GSTs likely serve as a molecular bridge connecting stress responses and hormone regulation, coordinating antioxidant defense with growth adaptation.

Although the GST family has been systematically identified and functionally characterized in model organisms and crops, its genome-wide identification, evolutionary features, and expression regulation under salt stress and exogenous auxin treatment remain unclear in mangroves, particularly A. marina. Elucidating the composition, structure, evolution, and roles of the GST family in A. marina under high salinity and oxidative damage will not only help reveal the adaptation mechanisms of this species to high-salinity environments but also provide candidate genes and theoretical foundations for improving crop salt tolerance via genetic engineering. Therefore, this study conducted the first genome-wide identification and bioinformatic analysis of the GST family in A. marina and investigated its expression patterns under salt and exogenous auxin treatment, aiming to provide preliminary insights into the molecular mechanisms of salt tolerance and antioxidant defense in mangrove plants.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of AmGSTs

The whole-genome data of A. marina were obtained from the latest published genome assembly and annotation files [24], using the version released on February 17, 2022. The protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana GSTs were retrieved from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). These sequences were used as queries for a BLAST search against the A. marina protein database using TBtools-II (V2.323) software to preliminarily identify GST family members. Subsequently, the conserved GST domains GST_C (PF00043) and GST_N (PF02798), with their corresponding HMM profiles obtained from InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro), were employed for further screening using the HMMER function in TBtools-II, applying an e-value threshold of 10⁻⁵.

Phylogenetic analysis of the GST family

To construct the phylogenetic tree of the A. marina GST family, 171 GST protein sequences from A. thaliana, P. trichocarpa and Oryza sativa L. were included. The GST protein sequences of O. sativa L. were obtained from the Rice Genome Annotation Project database (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/). The GST protein sequences of P. trichocarpa were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). All GST protein sequences used in the phylogenetic analysis are listed in Table S1 (Supplementary Material 1).

Full-length protein sequences of all GSTs were aligned using MUSCLE within MEGA12 (V12.0.11). A Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA12 after removing 90% of poorly aligned regions and gap-rich positions. The LG + G protein substitution model was employed with branch support assessed by 1000 bootstrap replicates [25–27]. The AmGSTs were classified and renamed based on their sequence similarities and clustering with known subfamilies. The tree was visualized and annotated using the iTOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/).

Chromosomal distribution of AmGSTs

The chromosomal locations of AmGST genes were determined using TBtools-II and visualized with the MapGene2Chromosome (MG2C) V2.0 online tool (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.0/).

Synteny analysis of GSTs across species

Synteny analysis between A. marina GSTs and those of A. thaliana, S. lycopersicum, O. sativa L., and P. trichocarpa was performed using TBtools-II. The results were visualized using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/) and Adobe Illustrator (V25.4.1). For intra-species synteny analysis of A. marina, TBtools-II was used for analysis and visualization. Selective pressure was evaluated by calculating the ratio of non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions for each syntenic gene pair. A Ka/Ks ratio > 1 indicates positive selection, < 1 indicates purifying selection, and = 1 suggests neutral evolution [28]. The whole-genome sequences of S. lycopersicum and P. trichocarpa were retrieved from the EnsemblPlants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/).

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of AmGSTs

The coding sequences (CDS) of AmGSTs were analyzed using the NCBI Batch CD-Search tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi). Conserved motifs were identified using the Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) suite online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) with a maximum of 10 motifs. Results from both analyses were integrated and visualized using TBtools-II to identify important functional motifs. Motifs were annotated using InterPro, and domain predictions were further validated using the SMART online tool (https://smart.embl.de/).

Promoter cis-acting element analysis of AmGSTs

Promoter regions (2000 base pairs (bp) upstream of the transcription start site) of AmGSTs were analyzed for cis-acting regulatory elements using the PlantCARE online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). Results were visualized using TBtools-II.

Physicochemical property analysis and structure prediction of AmGST proteins

The physicochemical properties of AmGST proteins, including amino acid (aa) number, molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability index (II), aliphatic index (AI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were predicted using the ProtParam tool (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Subcellular localization of AmGST proteins was initially predicted using three online tools: DeepLoc-2.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/), Plant-mPLoc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/), and WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/). For some members that were tentatively predicted to localize to the nucleus, further analysis was performed using the NLS-signal-prediction online tool (https://www.novoprolabs.com/tools/nls-signal-prediction) to detect the presence of a classic nuclear localization signal. The final localization assignment for each protein was determined based on consensus of the three predictors and the NLS validation (At least two predicted results were the same).

Three-dimensional (3D) structures of AmGST proteins were predicted via homology modeling using the SWISS-MODEL workspace (https://swissmodel.expasy.org). The electrostatic potential surfaces and 3D folded structures of the models were rendered using ChimeraX (V1.5). Molecular docking simulations of three representative AmGST proteins (AmGSTF03, AmGSTU08 and AmEF1G02) with GSH and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) were performed using the Easy2MD online tool (https://easy2md.com/), a fully automated server that internally deploys AutoDock Vina. Ligand structures for GSH and IAA were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Docking results of the relatively optimal simulation, which was the lowest affinity among the corresponding 20 docking simulation results, were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (V3.0.3).

Plant materials and growth conditions.

A. marina seedlings (eight-nine months old) were purchased from the Yuehui Planting and Breeding (Raoping County) Farmers’ Professional Cooperative. Seeds originated from Xunzhou Island, Raoping County, Chaozhou City, Guangdong Province, China (117°01.7’E, 23°34.4’N). Seedlings were acclimatized for one month under normal conditions in the experimental greenhouse of the College of Life Sciences and Oceanography, Shenzhen University. Subsequently, seedlings were subjected to four treatments: control (15‰ sea salt), 25‰ sea salt, 15‰ sea salt plus 50 µM IAA, and 25‰ sea salt plus 50 µM IAA. Leaf samples were collected at 5, 10, and 15 days post-treatment, wrapped in aluminum foil, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at − 80 °C.

Quantitative Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from all samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Georgia, America). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Evo M-MLV Plus 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Accurate Biology, Guangzhou, China). qRT-PCR was performed using the 2× Polarsignal® SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Mikx, Shenzhen, China) on an ABI PRISM 7500 Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, California, America). The thermal cycling program was set according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the qPCR Mix as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 20 s. Among the 25 identified AmGST genes, eight were randomly selected for qRT-PCR analysis to provide an unbiased representation of expression patterns across the AmGST genes. Gene-specific primers for AmGSTs were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast) (Supplementary Material 2, Table S2) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The A. marina actin gene (AmActin) was used as an internal reference for normalization [29]. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V10.1.2 software. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent biological replicates. Differences among groups were analyzed by two-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test when overall significance was detected. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of the AmGST gene family

A total of 25 AmGST genes were identified in A. marina (Table 1). The encoded proteins ranged from 194 (AmGSTT03) to 516 (AmGSTT02) aa in length, with an average of 257.44. Molecular weights (MW) varied from 21.09 kDa (AmGSTT03) to 51.08 kDa (AmGSTT02), averaging 29.14 kDa. The predicted pI ranged from 4.47 (AmGSTT03) to 9.15 (AmTCHQD01), with an average of 6.23. Most proteins were acidic to neutral (pI < 7.0), except for AmGSTU01 (pI = 7.72), AmGSTU10 (pI = 8.23), AmEF1G02 (pI = 8.01), and AmTCHQD01 (pI = 9.15). Instability indices ranged from 20.76 (AmEF1G03) to 49.46 (AmTCHQD01), averaging 34.67. Approximately 76% of the proteins were predicted as stable (Instability Index < 40), with AmEF1G03 being the most stable. AmGSTF05, AmGSTF06, AmGSTT03, AmGSTT04, AmGSTU05, and AmTCHQD01 had instability indices above 40, suggesting lower stability.

Aliphatic indices were generally high, ranging from 77.68 (AmGSTT03) to 104.72 (AmGSTU03) with an average of 90.57, suggesting potential thermostability. Grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) values were negative for all proteins except AmGSTU01, indicating that most AmGSTs were hydrophilic. Subcellular localization predictions suggested that AmGSTs were primarily localized to the cytoplasm (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis of the GST family

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using 25 AmGST, 76 OsGST, 43 PtGSTs and 52 AtGST protein sequences to investigate evolutionary relationships (Fig. 1). The AmGSTs were classified into five subfamilies: GSTU (red), TCHQD (blue), GSTT (cyan), EF1G (green), and GSTF (yellow). No members belonging to DHAR, GSTL or GSTZ subfamilies were identified. The A. marina GST family comprised three AmEF1Gs, six AmGSTFs, four AmGSTTs, 11 AmGSTUs, and one AmTCHQD. AmGSTUs were the most abundant (44%), followed by AmGSTFs (24%), AmGSTTs (16%), AmEF1Gs (12%), and AmTCHQD (4%). The distribution pattern of GST subfamilies in A. marina was similar to that in P. trichocarpa, indicating high homology.

Synteny analysis of GSTs across species

Synteny analysis between A. marina and A. thaliana, S. lycopersicum, O. sativa L., and P. trichocarpa revealed extensive homologous relationships among GST genes (Fig. 3). Homologous genes were distributed across multiple chromosomes in each species, with GST genes highlighted in the figure using distinct colors. The distribution of AmGSTs across chromosomes was uneven, with certain chromosomes harboring a notably higher number of genes than others, ranging from 1 to 4 per chromosome. This pattern was consistent with the earlier chromosomal localization results. Specifically, three syntenic gene pairs were detected with O. sativa L. chromosomes Os-Chr1, Os-Chr5, and Os-Chr9; 13 pairs with S. lycopersicum chromosomes Sl-Chr1, Sl-Chr2, Sl-Chr4, Sl-Chr5, Sl-Chr6, Sl-Chr7, Sl-Chr9, Sl-Chr10, and Sl-Chr12; 15 pairs with P. trichocarpa chromosomes Pt-Chr2, Pt-Chr10, Pt-Chr13, Pt-Chr14, Pt-Chr17, and Pt-Chr19; and 13 pairs with A. thaliana chromosomes At-Chr1, At-Chr3, At-Chr4, and At-Chr5.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of AmGSTs

To understand the structural and functional correlation among A. marina GSTs, a separate phylogenetic tree of AmGST proteins was constructed alongside analyses of gene structure and conserved motifs (Fig. 5). The proteins clustered into the five previously described subfamilies. Exon-intron structure analysis showed that AmGSTs possessed varying numbers of exons 1–10. AmEF1G02 contained the highest number (10 exons), followed by AmEF1G01 and AmEF1G03 (eight each), reflecting structural divergence of the EF1G subfamily from other GST members. Nine genes (36%) had 3 exons, another nine (36%) had 2 exons, three (12%) had 1 exon, and only one (4%, AmGSTF06) had 5 exons. The gene structures were generally well-conserved, indicating significant similarity with orthologous genes from closely related species.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of the AmGST family members. Exons are shown as green boxes, introns as gray horizontal lines. Ten conserved motifs identified by sequence alignment are labeled motif 1–10 and distinguished by different colors

MEME analysis identified 10 conserved motifs (motif 1–10) among the 25 AmGST proteins, with lengths ranging from 21 to 50 aa (Fig. 5). Motifs 1 and 2 were present in all AmGSTs, and motif 4 was present in all except AmGSTT03. Motifs 3 and 7 were found exclusively (though not in all members) within the GSTU subfamily, serving as potential markers. Similarly, motif 8 was specific to the GSTF subfamily, motif 9 to the GSTT subfamily, and motif 10 to the EF1G subfamily, each present in most but not all members of the respective subfamily. Motif 5 was primarily found in GSTF and GSTU subfamilies.

Motif annotation revealed that motifs 7, 8, and 9 had no corresponding known conserved domains. Motifs 1 and 4 were annotated as the GST N-terminal domain (GST_N, IPR004045). Motif 2 corresponded to the GST C-terminal domain (GST_C, IPR010987). Motifs 3, 5, and 6 were annotated as the C-terminal domain of the glutathione S-transferase superfamily (Glutathione-S-Trfase_C_sf, IPR036282). Motif 10 was annotated as the EF-1-G C-terminal domain (IPR044628), which had been associated with GST activity in plants [30]. These results further indicated high similarity in gene structure and aa sequence among members of the same AmGST subfamily.

Promoter cis-acting element analysis of AmGSTs

Promoter analysis of the 2000 bp upstream regions identified numerous cis-acting elements involved in plant growth, defense, stress response, and hormone signaling (Fig. 6). Light-responsive elements included ACE, G-Box, GT1-motif, Sp1, 3-AF1 binding site, Box 4, and ATCT-motif. Hormone-responsive elements encompassed TGA-element (auxin), SARE (salicylic acid), TCA-element (salicylic acid), ABRE (abscisic acid), AuxRR-core (auxin), TGACG-motif/CGTCA-motif (methyl jasmonate), P-box (gibberellin), and GARE-motif (gibberellin). Elements related to growth and development included O2-site, CAT-box, GCN4_motif, HD-Zip 1, and AACA_motif. Stress-responsive elements comprised TC-rich repeats (defense/stress), ARE (anaerobic induction), MBS (drought), and LTR (low-temperature). Basic transcriptional elements and enhancers such as AT-rich element, A-box, circadian, CAAT-box, TATA-box, and CCAAT-box were also detected. The presence of stress-responsive elements like TC-rich repeats, and auxin-responsive elements like TGA-element and AuxRR-core, provided a transcriptional regulatory basis for the observed expression changes, and AmGSTs may be putatively regulated by various physiological and environmental cues, including salinity and auxin signaling.

3D protein structure modeling and molecular docking

Homology modeling and surface electrostatic potential calculations were performed for all 25 AmGST proteins (Supplementary Material 2, Table S3 and Table S4). The electrostatic potential, expressed in Coulombic values (min, mean, and max), revealed significant variation across protein surfaces, with coexisting local negative and positive charged regions. The average values were predominantly negative (ranging from − 2.71 to − 0.11), indicating an overall negative surface charge that may facilitate interactions with positively charged ligands or membranes. AmGSTT02 had the most negative average potential (− 2.71), while AmTCHQD01 was near neutral (0.08), suggesting functional divergence in electrostatic environments. Some proteins, including AmGSTF05, AmGSTF06, AmGSTT03, and several AmGSTUs, displayed two distinct electrostatic potential profiles. These profiles may correspond to differences between subunits in a homodimer or charge asymmetry between domains, which could influence interactions with substrates or cofactors.

Most models (22/25) had GMQE scores above 0.7, with over half (13/25) exceeding 0.90, indicating reliable global topology. Except for AmGSTT03 (28.73%), sequence identity with templates was above 55%, supporting model reliability. All predicted 3D structures of the AmGST proteins clearly displayed the classic GST fold, dominated by α-helices with some β-sheets. Each structure consisted of two distinct domains: an N-terminal thioredoxin-like fold domain (with a β-α-β-α-β-β topology core) and an all-α-helical C-terminal domain, forming a characteristic ‘V’-shaped cleft that likely represents the catalytic active site.

Docking results showed that GSH bound tightly within the negatively charged depressions of AmEF1G02 (Fig. 7B) and AmGSTF03 (Fig. 7G), and within a negatively charged groove of AmGSTU08 (Fig. 7L). High-resolution interaction diagrams revealed dense hydrogen-bond networks between GSH and the proteins. Specifically, in AmEF1G02, SER-288 formed a 2.9 Å hydrogen bond with GSH, LEU-292 contributed a 3.1 Å hydrogen bond, and ASP-293 participated in stabilizing the complex through a 2.9 Å hydrogen bond (Fig. 7C). In AmGSTF03, LEU-26 formed two hydrogen bonds with GSH (3.1 Å and 2.3 Å), while ASP-27 and TYR-28 further anchored GSH through hydrogen bonds of 2.6 Å and 3.3 Å, respectively (Fig. 7H). In AmGSTU08, GLN-72 formed two hydrogen bonds with GSH (3.3 Å and 3.4 Å), ASP-103 contributed a very strong 2.1 Å hydrogen bond, and ARG-96 was involved in two interactions: a 2.6 Å hydrogen bond and a longer 3.6 Å hydrogen bond, likely reflecting multiple interactions between different atoms of ARG-96 and GSH (Fig. 7M).

The binding sites for IAA in AmEF1G02 (Fig. 7E), AmGSTF03 (Fig. 7J), and AmGSTU08 (Fig. 7O) differed from those of GSH but were likewise located within negatively charged depressions or grooves on the protein surfaces. Specifically, in AmEF1G02, LYS-70 formed a strong 2.6 Å hydrogen bond with the carboxyl group of IAA, and HIS-563 contributed a 3.0 Å hydrogen bond (Fig. 7D). In AmGSTF03, GLN-39, LYS-42, and PHE-45 contributed hydrogen bonds of 2.5 Å, 2.6 Å, and 3.3 Å, respectively, to stabilize IAA binding (Fig. 7I). In AmGSTU08, SER-67 formed a 2.8 Å hydrogen bond, ASP-103 (which also appeared in GSH binding and may participate in recognizing multiple substrates) formed a very strong 2.2 Å hydrogen bond, and LYS-104 contributed a 3.3 Å hydrogen bond (Fig. 7N).

qRT-PCR analysis of AmGST expression

To investigate the responsiveness of AmGST genes to salinity, auxin signaling, and their interplay, the expression patterns of eight selected AmGSTs (AmGSTF03, AmGSTF06, AmGSTU02, AmGSTU07, AmGSTU08, AmGSTU10, AmEF1G01, and AmEF1G02) were analyzed under different salinity levels (15‰ and 25‰) and exogenous auxin (50 µM) treatment, both separately and in combination, at 5, 10, and 15 days (Fig. 8).

qRT-PCR analysis of AmGST expression in A. marina seedlings under different treatments. Expression levels of selected AmGST genes in leaves under four conditions: salt-15‰, salt-25‰, Auxin 50 µM (salt-15‰), and Auxin 50 µM (salt-25‰) at 5, 10, and 15 days. Statistical significance was determined by two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test (compared to the salt-15‰ control at each time point). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. The y-axis represents relative gene expression level, and the x-axis indicates treatment duration

AmGSTF03 expression was significantly (p < 0.001) induced by higher salinity (25‰) at 5 and 10 days. Exogenous IAA further enhanced this induction, causing significant upregulation (p < 0.001) at all time points under both salinity levels, indicating a strong and sustained response to both salt stress and auxin. In contrast, AmGSTF06 showed a transient response, with significant induction (p < 0.001) only at 5 days, suggesting a role in the early rapid response to stress.

AmGSTU07 and AmGSTU08 exhibited similar profiles, being significantly upregulated (p < 0.001) by salt and auxin at 5 days, with the effect diminishing over time. AmGSTU08 remained responsive to salt at 10 days, while AmGSTU07 lost its auxin response by then. Notably, AmGSTU02 displayed a unique suppression pattern: its expression was significantly lower under 25‰ salinity compared to the 15‰ control, and exogenous auxin further suppressed it at later stages (10 and 15 days, p < 0.05). This downregulation suggested AmGSTU02 might be under negative feedback regulation or its function was specifically modulated during stress adaptation. AmGSTU10 showed complex regulation: induced by salt at 5 days, suppressed at 10 days, and then dramatically upregulated (p < 0.001) by auxin under 15‰ salinity at 15 days, implying a potential specialized role in long-term adaptation and hormone-mediated tolerance.

AmEF1G01 and AmEF1G02, though from the same subfamily, responded differently. AmEF1G01 expression was significantly suppressed (p < 0.01) by high salinity at 5 days, but auxin treatment dramatically reversed this suppression and strongly induced its expression (p < 0.001). In contrast, AmEF1G02 expression was not significantly affected by salinity but showed high sensitivity to exogenous auxin, being significantly upregulated (p < 0.001) by auxin at all time points and salinity levels. This indicated AmEF1G02 is a primary auxin-responsive gene, potentially linking auxin signaling to the detoxification system independently of the salt response pathway.

Discussion

Genomic characteristics and evolutionary patterns of the AmGST family

GST gene numbers vary among plants, e.g., O. sativa L. (82), A. thaliana (50), T. aestivum (330), S. lycopersicum (90), and B. platyphylla (71). The identification of 25 GSTs in A. marina is fewer than in these species but similar to soybean (Glycine max, 25) [14, 16, 31–34]. This variation may relate to genome size, life history traits, and environmental adaptation strategies [35, 36].

Uneven chromosomal distribution and synteny analyses suggest that the expansion of the AmGST family primarily relied on segmental duplication events. The existence of seven duplicate pairs and cross-chromosomal syntenic relationships indicates that segmental duplication has been a major driver for gene number increase and functional diversification, a common pattern in plant gene family evolution [37–39]. All duplicate pairs had Ka/Ks ratios significantly less than 1, suggesting strong purifying selection, which has maintained the core functions (e.g., detoxification, antioxidation) of AmGSTs throughout evolution, ensuring stable physiological performance under extreme conditions.

However, purifying selection did not prevent functional differentiation within the family. Phylogenetic analysis classified AmGSTs into five subfamilies, showing significant expansion of the GSTU (11 members) and GSTF (six members) subfamilies, which together constitute 68% of the family. GSTU and GSTF are well-established as the most active subfamilies in plant abiotic stress responses [40]. Their expansion in A. marina, an obligate halophyte, may represent a genomic “pre-adaptation” strategy. The abundance of homologous genes provides genetic redundancy and potential for functional fine-tuning to cope with the complex and fluctuating stresses (high salt, high light, and periodic tidal fluctuations) of the intertidal zone [41–43].

Gene structure and motif analyses revealed that AmGSTs contain 1–10 exons, with members of the same subfamily sharing similar structures and motif compositions. The EF1G subfamily, with 8–10 exons, likely encodes more complex proteins involved in broader physiological processes [44]. The universal presence of motifs 1 and 2, corresponding to the GST_N and GST_C domains, confirms the typical family characteristics of AmGSTs. Subfamily-specific motifs (e.g., motif 3/7 for GSTU, motif 8 for GSTF) may underlie functional specialization and provide targets for future functional validation.

Structural features and functional potential of AmGST proteins

Physicochemical analysis showed that most AmGSTs are acidic, hydrophilic proteins (average pI 6.23, negative GRAVY), with 76% predicted as stable. These properties facilitate efficient catalytic function in the cytoplasm, where hydrophilicity aids interaction with water-soluble substrates like GSH, and stability ensures enzyme activity under extreme conditions like high salinity and hypoxia. Predominant cytoplasmic localization aligns with the classic role of GSTs as intracellular detoxification enzymes [45, 46].

3D modeling and docking revealed that AmGSTs possessed the typical GST fold, with the N-terminal thioredoxin and C-terminal α-helical domains forming the catalytic cleft. The surface exhibits significant electrostatic asymmetry, with the binding pockets enriched in negative charge, providing a favorable electrostatic environment for attracting polar or positively charged molecules. Although GSH is negatively charged at physiological pH, its thiol and glutamyl moieties bind via specific hydrogen bonds and polar interactions; the overall negative potential of the cleft may primarily serve to stabilize positively charged xenobiotic substrates or reaction intermediates formed after substrate binding [47, 48].

Molecular docking predicted that AmEF1G02, AmGSTF03, and AmGSTU08 could bind both the classic substrate GSH and IAA. This provides preliminary structural biology support for the hypothesis that GSTs act as “molecular bridges” integrating stress and hormone signals [49]. Based on this, this study hypothesizes that GSTs could fine-tune local auxin concentration and activity by directly binding and transporting IAA (akin to the early “Protein-Ligand Binding Models” hypothesis) or by metabolizing IAA precursors or conjugates, thereby regulating the growth-defense balance in cells under stress [50, 51]. It should be noted that the molecular docking in this study was based on homology models, whose accuracy may be limited by template selection and the modeling process. Determining the precise 3D structure of AmGSTs via X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy could further validate these interactions.

Expression patterns of AmGSTs in response to salinity and auxin

Promoter analysis revealed an abundance of cis-elements related to light, hormones (e.g., auxin, abscisic acid - ABA), and stress responses upstream of AmGSTs. This suggests that A. marina may fine-tune the expression of different GST copies. Should this occur, it could be a mechanism for allocating limited enzymatic functions (e.g., GSH binding, substrate catalysis) to different stress response pathways, achieving “multifunctionality” or “division of labor”. Similar phenomena are observed in the most plant GST family studies, where gene loss and retention coexist, and positive selection sites may influence protein structure and function to adapt to specific environments [52, 53]. The presence of abiotic stress-responsive (e.g., TC-rich repeats) and hormone-responsive (e.g., TGA-element, AuxRR-core) elements in AmGST promoters provides a transcriptional regulatory basis for their response to salinity and auxin signaling.

Notably, the qRT-PCR results delineated a complex dynamic network of AmGST responses to salt stress and exogenous auxin. These findings suggest that the response is more complex than a simple upregulation, revealing the potential multi-layered roles these genes may play in stress adaptation, despite the limitations of direct enzyme activity assays (such as ROS quantification and antioxidant enzyme activity) and physiological measurements.

The persistent upregulation of AmGSTF03 under salt stress, further enhanced by IAA, suggests this gene may function in long-term salt adaptation via the coordinated regulation of salt stress and auxin signals. The early transient induction of AmGSTF06 implies a role in the initial high salinity response, potentially for rapid ROS scavenging. Members of the GSTU subfamily showed differential regulation: AmGSTU07 and AmGSTU08 are early-responsive genes, while AmGSTU10 displayed dynamic expression, with dramatic upregulation by IAA at 15 days, hinting at involvement in establishing late adaptive mechanisms. Particularly, AmGSTU02 was suppressed by both salt and IAA, possibly maintaining cellular redox homeostasis via negative feedback to avoid detrimental overreaction [54, 55].

The two EF1G members exhibited contrasting patterns: the salt-induced suppression of AmEF1G01 was reversed by IAA, indicating auxin may regulate its expression by antagonizing salinity signals. AmEF1G02, unaffected by salinity but strongly and consistently induced by IAA, is a typical primary auxin-responsive gene, likely serving as a key node connecting auxin signaling to the detoxification system at the crossroads of growth and stress adaptation. These differential expression patterns suggest that the AmGST family forms a complex regulatory network through division of labor and collaboration at different stages of salt stress and within auxin signaling pathways to maintain physiological homeostasis in extreme environments. Future studies incorporating enzyme activity assays, ROS measurements, and transgenic functional validation will be essential to experimentally verify the roles of the candidate AmGST genes identified here and to establish causal links between their expression patterns and physiological phenotypes under salt stress.

Conclusion

This study provides the first genome-wide identification and functional analysis of the GST gene family in A. marina, revealing its potential roles in adapting to high-salinity habitats and responding to exogenous auxin, identifying 25 AmGST genes classified into five subfamilies. Family expansion primarily involved segmental duplication events, and the genes have undergone strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1), highlighting the functional conservation of this family during mangrove evolution. Conserved motif and gene structure analyses revealed subfamily-specific patterns, and promoter cis-element analysis supported the potential involvement of AmGSTs in both stress and hormone responses. Importantly, the results of homology modeling and molecular docking can provide a testable hypothesis. This study predicts that three AmGST proteins, AmEF1G02, AmGSTF03, and AmGSTU08, can bind both GSH and IAA, with detailed hydrogen-bond networks identified for each interaction. This dual-binding ability provides a preliminary hypothesis that these proteins may act as molecular bridges linking auxin signaling and detoxification pathways.

The analysis of qRT-PCR under salt and/or IAA treatments revealed that AmGSTF03 is consistently upregulated by salt and further enhanced by IAA, suggesting it may serve as a key node in stress adaptation; AmEF1G02 shows strong and specific responsiveness to auxin irrespective of salinity, positioning it as a key node in auxin–detoxification crosstalk; and AmGSTU08 exhibits early transient upregulation, suggesting its involvement in rapid stress responses. In addition, beyond the specific candidate genes identified here, functional characterization of the entire family, particularly through transgenic approaches in A. marina or heterologous systems, will be essential to fully delineate the roles of GSTs in mangrove salt tolerance. This study provides new insights into stress adaptation mechanisms in mangrove plants and offers candidate genes and theoretical references for improving crop salt tolerance via genetic engineering, holding significant theoretical value and application potential.
