A taxonomic sudoku: Resolving the long‐standing puzzle of Japanese Dictyoteae (Dictyotales, Phaeophyceae) with new records and biogeographic insights
Authors: Christophe Vieira, Shingo Akita, Masahiro Suzuki, Ryuta Terada, Takeaki Hanyuda, Satoshi Shimada, Shinya Uwai, Hiroshi Kawai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractJapanese Dictyoteae have been documented since 1838, with a historical checklist of 21 names recorded prior to 1990. Persistent misidentifications—particularly of species originally described from Europe and the Americas—have complicated the taxonomy of the group in Japan. We reevaluated historical records and revised the national checklist for Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, Dilophus, and Rugulopteryx using integrated morpho‐genetic analyses. Broad sampling from Hokkaido to Okinawa, including Japanese type localities, together with sequence data from type localities of species described elsewhere but reported in Japan, corrected persistent taxonomic ambiguities. The long‐standing misapplication of the Asian D. dichotoma as D. spathulata was resolved. The revised checklist recognizes 19 species: 17 Dictyota, Canistrocarpus cervicornis, and Rugulopteryx okamurae. Species richness peaks in Kyushu with 15 species, whereas only D. spathulata extends to Hokkaido. Eight taxa appear native to the Northwest Pacific, including D. coriacea, D. haenyeosa, D. spathulata, D. spinulosa, D. sumbisoria, D. taewakia, Dictyota churaumia sp. nov., and R. okamurae. Twelve taxa exhibit broad, transoceanic distributions. We describe Dictyota churaumia sp. nov. and formally report nine species from Japan for the first time: Dictyota acutiloba, D. ceylanica, D. haenyeosa, D. humifusa, D. liturata, D. pfaffii, D. sandvicensis, D. sumbisoria, and D. taewakia. By integrating molecular data with morphological analyses, we resolved major taxonomic misidentifications and implemented the necessary taxonomic treatments (synonymies and typifications) to provide an updated taxonomic framework for the Dictyoteae flora of Japan. The presence of unique morphologies in historical herbarium specimens, coupled with unexplored regions of Japan, indicates a potential for additional, unidentified species.Keywords: mitochondrial‐encoded cox1 and cox3, molecular phylogeny, new species and records, plastid‐encoded psbA and rbcL, taxonomy

INTRODUCTION

Established in 1809 by J.V. Lamouroux (1809), the brown macroalga genus Dictyota is a highly diverse group, currently containing 93 accepted species according to AlgaeBase (Guiry & Guiry, 2025), comparable to Lobophora (Vieira, 2020) and highlighting its importance as a subject of taxonomic research. In Japan, the first records of Dictyota can be traced to Hooker and Arnott (1838), establishing a long history of interest in this genus. However, comprehensive taxonomic investigations, particularly those leveraging molecular techniques, have been relatively scarce in recent decades. As in other parts of Asia, early Japanese taxonomists often adopted Atlantic species names for local Dictyota taxa (Guiry & Guiry, 2025), which introduced significant challenges in distinguishing Japan's unique species diversity. Although Dictyota has long been studied taxonomically, particularly in regions like the Atlantic, the advent of molecular phylogenetics has revolutionized our understanding of its global diversity. Over the past 2 decades, these techniques have revealed a far greater number of species (>100) than previously recognized (Bogaert et al., 2020; Vieira et al., 2021) while also exposing inconsistencies between traditional morphological classifications and genetic lineages. The cosmopolitan distribution once attributed to D. dichotoma, for instance, has been shown to be a result of misidentifications and introductions (Lopes‐Filho et al., 2017; Tronholm et al., 2008; Tronholm et al., 2010; likely introduced in Argentina and South Africa), with the species' true range being considerably narrower. Although the central Indo‐Pacific has been identified as a Dictyota diversity hotspot, the Northwest Pacific, including Japan, remains understudied. This region's Dictyoteae flora, despite its long history of research, has remained inadequately reassessed using modern molecular approaches, necessitating a comprehensive taxonomic re‐evaluation. Such molecular approaches have recently proven invaluable in resolving taxonomic confusion in neighboring regions, as demonstrated by the extensive revision of the Korean Dictyoteae flora (Vieira et al., 2025).

A retrospective examination of the taxonomic history of Dictyota, tracing from its early morphological classifications in 1838–1990, sheds light on the current status of Dictyota species listed in Japan. Between 1838 and 1990, a total of 21 Dictyota species were cataloged, constituting what we refer to as the historical collection (Table 1).

The initial taxonomic records were authored by European (British) botanists. The inaugural documentation of Dictyota in Japan can be traced back to the work of British botanists William J. Hooker and George A. W. Arnott in 1838, wherein they described Dictyota spinulosa, sourced from the “Loo‐Choo” region (now Okinawa Islands, Japan; Hooker & Arnott, 1838, p. 275). This species, newly discovered from Japan, was collected during an expedition led by Captain F.W. Beechey aboard HMS Blossom, with specimens collected by Lay and Collie and other expedition members. Subsequently, in 1896, British botanist Edward M. Holmes reported Glossophora coriacea [=Dictyota coriacea] from Enoura, Shizuoka Prefecture (Holmes, 1896), marking the second documented and newly discovered Dictyota species in Japan.

Following these initial two reports and the new species of Dictyota from Japan by British taxonomists during the 19th century, the trajectory of taxonomic research on Dictyota in Japan underwent a notable shift from the early 20th century onwards. The remaining 19 species forming part of the historical collection were documented by Japanese taxonomists, notably including K. Okamura (10 species), M. Noda (five species), Y. Yamada (one species), K. Yendo (one species), and S. Segawa (one species). The first record of Dictyota species in Japan by a Japanese taxonomist was made in 1902 by Okamura, who included these entries in a checklist. In his publication, Okamura (1902) reported D. dichotoma, the first European Dictyota species recorded in Japan, along with D. bartayresiana and D. patens [=D. bartayresiana] originally described from the Caribbean, although both were followed by question marks. This marked a departure from the earlier British taxonomic approach, which predominantly identified and described Dictyota species from Japan as novel. Following Okamura's work, Yendo (1909) reported another non‐Pacific species, D. naevosa, originally described from South Africa.

Although Okamura initially listed Dictyota dichotoma in a checklist in 1902, his exploration of Dictyota diversity in Japan deepened over the years. This is evident in his publications Icons of Japanese Algae (Okamura, 1913–1915), in which Okamura's detailed descriptions and illustrations highlighted the morphological variations within his interpretation of Dictyota dichotoma.It is evident today that Okamura's concept of D. dichotoma encompassed multiple species of Dictyota, an aspect that has been explored further in this study. Between 1913 and 1936, Okamura refined his understanding of Dictyota diversity, documenting nine species from Japan. Except for D. marginata [=Rugulopteryx okamurae], a newly described species by Okamura from Japan (Cape Nomo, Nagasaki Prefecture), all other Dictyota species documented by him were primarily of Atlantic origin. These species included D. patens [=D. bartayresiana], D. bartayresiana, D. dentata [=D. mertensii], D. divaricata [D. cervicornis = Canistrocarpus cervicornis] from the Caribbean, and D. linearis [=D. implexa] from Spain. The sole exception was D. maxima [=D. ciliolata], originally described from the Malaysia–Indonesia boundary (Zanardini, 1872). In his 1936 publication, Okamura also listed D. indica. However, he indicated that the distribution of D. indica was limited to the West Indies. The inclusion of D. indica in his work titled “Descriptions of Japanese Algae,” which was specifically focused on the Japanese flora, appears unusual and suggests potential ambiguity regarding its relevance to Japanese waters.

Yamada (1925) described Dictyota dilatata from Ryusensui, Hengchun, Taiwan, which was under Japanese rule at the time. Later, Segawa (1956) recorded this species from the central Pacific coasts of the Honshu region. However, D. dilatata Yamada (1925) is a later homonym of D. dilatata Poiret (1812) and is thus considered an illegitimate name under the International Code of Nomenclature for Algae, Fungi, and Plants (ICN), Art. 11.4 (Turland et al., 2018). Subsequently, Yamada (1928) described D. spathulata Yamada from Mutsu Bay, Aomori Prefecture.

From 1965 to 1971, Noda further expended the repertoire of Dictyota species in Japan (Noda, 1965, 1969, 1971). This expansion included the discovery of two species newly described from Japan itself by Noda: D. adhaerens from Sado Island, Niigata Prefecture, and D. virellus from Mastumae, Hokkaido Prefecture. Noda (1965) also made the first record of D. indica, which Okamura (1936) provisionally recorded in Japan from Sado Island, Niigata Prefecture. Additionally, three species previously unreported in Japan but originally described from North America were documented: D. binghamiae and D. flabellata from California, USA, as well as D. cervicornis [=Canistrocarpus cervicornis] from Florida, USA. The last species of the historical collection documented from Japan was D. friabilis, originally described from French Polynesia (Tahiti, Society Island). The latter species was reported by Tanaka (1990) from Tokunoshima Island, Kagoshima Prefecture.

In total, the historical species list included 21 names, which, after careful curation to verify their taxonomic acceptance status, has been narrowed down to a list of 17 currently accepted names according to Guiry and Guiry (2025). Notably, eight of these historically documented names have become synonyms, primarily comprising species documented by Okamura (eight spp.) and one by Noda (Table 1). Furthermore, two names previously identified as illegitimate, Dictyota marginata and D. dentata, have now been recognized as synonyms: The former is a substitute name for Rugulopteryx okamurae, and the latter is synonymous with D. mertensii. Taking into account the currently accepted species names, this list ultimately comprises a total of 15 species of Dictyota, one species of Rugulopteryx (R. okamurae), and one species of Canistrocarpus (C. cervicornis; Table 1).

Although Yoshida and Tanaka (1998) argued that Dictyota adhaerens and D. virellus were not validly published, since Noda (1965) and Noda (1971) did not indicate a type specimen, De Clerck (2003) took a differing stance, maintaining the validity of D. adhaerens. He argued that Noda's citation of “M. Noda, 20.xi.1955” could be interpreted as a valid citation of a type specimen, thus rendering the name validly published. According to ICN Articles 40.3 and 40.6 (Turland et al., 2018), D. adhaerens is considered valid, as it was described prior to January 1, 1990. Noda (1965) provided a single locality and date, which was sufficient to meet the requirements for typification. As noted by De Clerck (2003), the locality and date information in Noda (1965) are acceptable as an indication of the type. Similarly, the taxonomic validity of D. virellus can be upheld depending on the interpretation of the ICN. In this case, Noda (1971) also cited only one locality and date: “Matsumae, S.‐W. Hokkaido (Aug. 4, 1958 by S. Yokoyama).” This information appears to satisfy the criteria for typification, making it acceptable as an indication of the type.

Despite the extensive historical documentation, contemporary Japanese phycologists face uncertainty regarding Dictyota taxonomy (Noda, 1987; Yoshida & Tanaka, 1998). As previously noted, early Japanese taxonomists assigned European or American species names to Japanese species, despite acknowledging their imperfect fit, as noted by Noda (1987). Noda (1987) highlighted challenges in delineating species boundaries based solely on morphology, particularly concerning D. dichotoma‐like plants.

The advent of molecular‐based studies in the early 2000s marked the beginning of a new approach to assessing Dictyota diversity in Japan, although efforts have remained limited and far from comprehensive. The first study incorporating Japanese Dictyota sequences, Hwang et al. (2004), identified D. dichotoma, Pachydictyon coriaceum [=D. coriacea], and Dilophus okamurae [=Rugulopteryx okamurae]. Since then, only a handful of studies have contributed additional sequences, without systematically integrating them with historical species records. Among these, Vieira et al. (2021) included Japanese sequences in a global phylogenetic analysis, assigning them—often with cautionary “cf.” annotations—to Canistrocarpus cervicornis, D. acutiloba, D. cf. anastomosans, D. bartayresiana, D. cf. ceylanica, D. coriacea, D. cf. liturata, D. cf. pfaffii, D. cf. rigida, D. spinulosa, R. okamurae, and unidentified Dictyota species. More recently, Ni‐Ni‐Win et al. (2024) examined Dictyota specimens from multiple countries, including Japan, and identified two Japanese clades: one corresponding to D. coriacea and another representing their newly described species D. dimorphosa. Despite these efforts, molecular data remain sparse, and no study has yet attempted to reconcile these genetic findings with historical records. Of the species traditionally recorded from Japan, only six—Dictyota dichotoma, D. bartayresiana, D. coriacea, D. spinulosa, R. okamurae, and Canistrocarpus cervicornis—have had sequences generated and assigned to their names. However, the correspondence between these historical taxa and their molecularly identified counterparts remains unexamined, and the taxonomic accuracy of these historical records themselves has yet to be verified, particularly through molecular comparison with, for example, type‐locality material.

Among the 15 currently accepted species historically documented within Dictyota, six have their type locality in the Atlantic, spanning Europe (e.g., D. dichotoma from England and D. implexa from the Mediterranean Sea), North America (e.g., D. bartayresiana from the Caribbean), and South America (e.g., D. mertensii from Brazil and D. ciliolata from Venezuela). Additionally, a few species originated from other parts of the Pacific, such as D. binghamiae and D. flabellata from California. Dictyota naevosa originated from South Africa. This underscores the global distribution of Dictyota species believed to be present in Japan according to historical records, illustrating the taxonomic complexity of the region. It highlights the necessity for a critical reevaluation of the genus taxonomy and diversity in Japan.

The present study has provided a comprehensive reevaluation of the diversity, taxonomy, and distribution of the tribe Dictyoteae in Japan, incorporating molecular and morphological analyses to resolve historical ambiguities and update the regional checklist. By examining extensive samples from Hokkaido to Okinawa, we identified taxonomically significant patterns across Japanese coastal regions, which included species endemic to Asia, broadly distributed species, and potentially introduced taxa. Our objectives were to (1) assess the diversity of Dictyoteae species in Japan, clarifying misidentifications such as Dictyota dichotoma and establishing accurate records; (2) document novel species and range extensions, including the identification of previously undocumented species; and (3) characterize diversity patterns across Japan's geographic and climatic zones, with a focus on endemic versus broadly distributed species. This taxonomic revision and updated checklist are intended to enrich our understanding of the biodiversity of the Dictyoteae in Japan, laying a foundation for future studies on the ecological roles and conservation of this pivotal group in the Northwest Pacific.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study site and sampling

Sampling was conducted across Japan from 2019 to 2024 (Figure 1, Table S1). Specimens were collected from intertidal and subtidal zones, using snorkeling and diving methods, as well as while on foot in the shallow intertidal zone. Collection sites spanned from Okinawa Prefecture, Japan's southernmost region, to Hokkaido Prefecture in the north, covering diverse coastal areas. Specimens were initially identified to the genus level, and to the species level, when possible, based on morphological characteristics referenced in the local literature (e.g., Noda, 1971, 1987; Okamura, 1913–1915; Okamura, 1936; Yoshida, 1998). Specimens of Dictyota species previously described from Japan were collected from their respective type localities: D. virellus from Matsumae (Hokkaido Prefecture) and D. spathulata from Mutsu Bay (Aomori Prefecture), as well as specimens from Tassha, Sado Islands (Niigata Prefecture).

Most specimens were photographed both in situ and ex situ. Following collection, each specimen was prepared as a herbarium voucher, and small fragments were preserved in fine silica gel for molecular analysis. Herbarium vouchers were deposited in the Herbarium of Hokkaido University (SAP) in Sapporo, Japan. A total of 187 specimens from the genera Dictyota, Canistrocarpus, and Rugulopteryx were collected across Japan, encompassing all eight regions of Japan (Figure 1; Table S1).

Morphological analyses

Morphological observations encompassed both gross morphology and anatomical characteristics. Gross morphological observations included in situ appearance, color, iridescence, and measurements of maximum thallus height on herbarium specimens, taken from the thallus base to its tip. Additional observations included the color of dried specimens, maximum number of bifurcations (nodes), and the presence of reproductive structures. Anatomical observations focused on measurements of cortical and medullary thickness, as well as medullary cell width. For anatomical examination, transverse cross‐sections were taken at five distinct locations along the thallus: the upper 1 mm, upper 5 mm, middle of the second internode, middle part, and basal part (Figure S1), following De Clerck (2003), Hwang (2003), Hwang et al. (2005), and Vieira et al. (2025). These sections were made by hand using a razor blade on either fresh material or rehydrated herbarium specimens. Microscopic preparations were examined and photographed using a Leica D2000 binocular and light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), equipped with a Canon EOS 600D camera.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from tissue samples dried in silica gel using the MagPurix® Plant DNA Extraction Kit v.1.3 (ZP02014; Zinexts Life Science Corporation, New Taipei City, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Algal materials were directly processed without grinding, with PLA buffer, and incubated for 4 h at 60°C in a shaking microplate incubator (Provocell, ESCO, Singapore). The MagPurix DNA Extraction Kit was run on a MagPurix 12A automated nucleic acid extraction system (Zinexts Life Science Corporation, New Taipei City, Taiwan). The DNA extract (final volume of 100 μL) was stored at −20°C.

The chloroplast photosystem II D1 protein (psbA gene), recognized as a reference marker for the genus Dictyota (Vieira et al., 2021), was used as a barcode marker, with systematic attempts made for its amplification (although not always successfully) across all collected specimens. Subsequent to the lineage identification using the psbA gene, sequences were additionally amplified for the chloroplast ribulose‐bisphosphate carboxylase (rbcL) and the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase I (cox1) and cytochrome c oxidase III (cox3) genes from a subset of samples for each psbA gene lineage. The primer pairs for amplification and sequencing of each gene were as follows: for psbA, psbA‐F and psbA‐R1 (Yoon et al., 2002); for cox3, trnY‐P1 and cox3‐P2 (Hanyuda et al., 2008) or cox3‐44F and cox3‐739R (Silberfeld et al., 2010); for cox1, cox1F‐Dic or GazF2 and cox1‐P1.2 (Kawai et al., 2013; Saunders, 2005); and for rbcL‐5P, 68F and R708 (Bittner et al., 2008; Draisma et al., 2001) or RU1 and RU2 (Lee & Bae, 2002) or DRL2F and DRL2R (Hwang et al., 2005).

Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were carried out in tube strips in 20 μL reaction volumes using either the PCR master mix (1) MG 2X Taq PreMix (MGmed, Seoul, Korea) or (2) AccuPower® Taq PCR Premix kit (Bioneer Corp., South Korea). The PCRs were run either in a (1) Kyratec (SC300G‐R2; Kyratec, Mansfield, Australia) or (2) AllInOneCycler 96‐well PCR system (A‐2041‐1N; Bioneer Corp., South Korea), using a thermocycling profile with specific parameters for each marker. Sequencing reactions and runs were performed by Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea).

Sequences alignment, phylogenetic reconstruction, and species identification

All nucleotide sequences of relevant Dictyotaceae available on GenBank were downloaded for the psbA, rbcL, cox1, and cox3 genes. Sequence names were curated whenever possible based on the latest molecular taxonomic studies (e.g., Vieira et al., 2025). For each marker, a final dataset of GenBank sequences was compiled following Vieira et al. (2025) with a selection of representative sequences for Dictyotaceae species not observed in Japan. The nucleotide gene sequence datasets of psbA, rbcL, cox1, and cox3 from Japanese samples generated in this study were integrated into the GenBank datasets and aligned using MUSCLE v.3.5 (Edgar, 2004) within AliView v.1.28 (Larsson, 2014).

Phylogenetic reconstructions based on each gene (psbA, rbcL, cox1, and cox3) included both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian approaches (Figures S2–, S5). Maximum likelihood phylogenetic inferences were performed using W‐IQ‐TREE (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016) with auto model selection (ModelFinder; Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). Branch support was assessed using the Shimodaira–Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test (SH‐aLRT) and ultrafast bootstrap with 1000 replicates (Hoang et al., 2018). Bayesian analyses were monitored by the software MrBayes v.3.1 (Ronquist & Huelsenbeck, 2003). Bayesian analyses were conducted using four Metropolis‐coupled Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMCMC) simulations with the GTR + GAMMA + I model, using 100 million generations with two independent runs with four chains and sampling trees every 100 generations. The burn‐in period was identified graphically in Tracer v.1.7 (Rambaut et al., 2018) by tracking at which generation likelihoods reached a plateau.

Haplotype network analyses

In order to gain deeper insights into the phylogeographic patterns among species of some Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx in Japan with broad distribution, we constructed chloroplast DNA (cpDNA) haplotype networks for three species: C. cervicornis, D. ciliolata, and D. coriacea. These networks were geographically labeled by country. The cpDNA haplotype networks based on the psbA gene were calculated using the TCS method (Clement et al., 2000, 2002), with gaps and missing data excluded, and visualized using PopART v.1.7 (Leigh & Bryant, 2015).

RESULTS

Sequence data and phylogenetic results

In this study, we generated a total of 400 sequences from 178 specimens of Dictyota, Canistrocarpus, and Rugulopteryx from Japan, comprising 159 psbA, 105 rbcL, 55 cox1, and 81 cox3 gene sequences, all deposited in GenBank (Table S1). By combining these new sequences with previously published data from GenBank, our dataset included a total of 194 psbA, 338 rbcL, 220 cox1, and 222 cox3 gene sequences. Phylogenetic analyses using ML and Bayesian inference (BI), based on both newly generated and previously available sequence data, grouped the sequences into 27 distinct lineages. Each lineage was labeled by genus initials and a sequential number (e.g., “sp. #JAP”) and resolved across individual gene trees for the psbA, rbcL, cox1, and cox3 genes (Figures S2–, S5).

The phylogenetic analysis identified a single lineage each for Rugulopteryx (R. sp. 1JAP) and Canistrocarpus (C. sp. 1JAP), as well as 25 lineages for Dictyota (D. sp. 1JAP to D. sp. 25JAP). The Canistrocarpus lineage (C. sp. 1JAP) matched C. cervicornis. Although this species's type locality is Florida, our sequence data showed similarities with specimens from other Caribbean regions (e.g., Mexico), as no sequence data for Florida specimens were available (Figure S2). The Rugulopteryx lineage (R. sp. 1JAP) matched R. okamurae, including sequences previously reported from Japan in GenBank (Table S2).

Of the 27 Dictyoteae lineages identified, 21 exhibited close phylogenetic affinities with GenBank sequences associated with established species names (Figure S2). These lineages were identified as Dictyota acutiloba (D. sp. 1JAP), D. bartayresiana (D. sp. 2JAP), D. ceylanica (D. spp. 3JAP, 23JAP, 24JAP, and 25JAP), D. ciliolata (D. sp. 5JAP), D. coriacea (D. sp. 6JAP), D. dichotoma2 (D. spp. 16JAP and 21JAP), D. friabilis (D. spp. 7JAP, 22JAP, 4JAP, and 8JAP), D. haenyeosa (D. sp. 10JAP), D. humifusa (D. sp. 9JAP), D. liturata (D. sp. 11JAP), D. rigida (D. spp. 13JAP and 14JAP), D. sandvicensis (D. sp. 15JAP), D. sumbisoria (D. sp. 18JAP), D. spinulosa (D. sp. 17JAP), and D. taewakia (D. sp. 20JAP). Each identification was supported by close phylogenetic proximity to GenBank sequences from or near the type localities, as exemplified by D. acutiloba (OQ792181 [psbA], Hawaii), D. bartayresiana (MW224624 [psbA], Colombia), D. ceylanica (MW224691 [psbA], Sri Lanka), D. ciliolata (MW224800 [psbA], Colombia), D. friabilis (GU265786 [psbA], Tahiti), D. haenyeosa (PQ616799 [psbA], Korea), D. humifusa (MW225119 [psbA], Colombia), D. liturata (MW225190 [psbA], South Africa), D. rigida (GQ425217 [psbA], Tanzania), D. sandvicensis (GU265783 [psbA], Hawaii), D. sumbisoria (PQ616848 [psbA], Korea), and D. taewakia (PQ616858 [psbA], Korea). The resolution of species identities was initially complicated by instances of identical species names assigned to phylogenetically divergent GenBank sequences. However, prioritizing sequences from type localities facilitated the accurate identification of 19 Japanese species.

Specific attention was given to species complexes. The Japanese lineages Dictyota spp. 13JAP and 14JAP clustered within the D. rigida complex, which included a psbA gene sequence from the D. rigida type locality (Tanzania, QG425217). This complex exhibited a broad geographic distribution, encompassing sequences from Australia, New Caledonia, Papua New Guinea, South Africa, Glorioso Islands, and Panama. Similarly, D. spp. 3JAP and 23‐25JAP formed a clade within the D. ceylanica complex, alongside a psbA gene sequence from the Sri Lankan type locality (MW224691). Despite the polyphyletic sequences labeled D. ceylanica in GenBank, the clade containing the Sri Lankan type‐locality sequence was considered the most representative.

Phylogenetic analyses showed Dictyota spp. 4JAP and 8JAP to be related to but distinct from the D. friabilis complex. Dictyota sp. 4JAP clustered with GenBank sequences labeled “D. ceylanica6,” which were phylogenetically distant from the D. ceylanica type‐locality clade, whereas Dictyota spp. 8JAP presented no affinities to GenBank sequences.

Furthermore, sequences from Dictyota spathulata and D. virellus samples collected from their respective type localities, as well as sequences from D. dimorphosa, grouped within a lineage previously referred to as “D. dichotoma2,” representing the Asian D. dichotoma concept, later described as D. dimorphosa. This lineage was distinct from the European D. dichotoma (UK), as already noted by Tronholm et al. (2010) and more recently by Ni‐Ni‐Win et al. (2024) and Vieira et al. (2025), indicating that these two species are non‐conspecific.

Dictyota sp. 19JAP formed a distinct clade alongside lineages labeled D. spp. 12 and 34 in GenBank, with phylogenetic relationships among these three lineages changing among gene trees.

Notably, none of our specimens matched GenBank sequences labeled as Dictyota implexa (including sequences from the western Atlantic, such as Cuba), D. mertensii (including sequences from the Mediterranean, such as Croatia), or D. naevosa (including sequences from South Africa).

The lineage associated with the Asian Dictyota dichotoma concept, as introduced by Okamura (1902), included specimens from the type localities of D. spathulata (from Natsudomari, Aomori Prefecture) and D. virellus (from Matsumae, Hokkaido Prefecture) and displayed notable morphological variation. Present collections from Natsudomari (Figures 4g, 7c) closely match both Yamada's (1928) original illustration and the lectotype specimen (SAP 8017) housed in the SAP herbarium (Figure 7b). Natsudomari individuals exhibited two distinct growth forms: slender and symmetrically branched, or prostrate with small thalli. In contrast, specimens collected from, for example, Hyogo (Figures 4i and 7k) aligned with Okamura's (1913c, Pl. CXI, figure 1) concept and showed a much broader morphology, with branches that narrowed toward the base, an asymmetrical branching pattern, and prominent basal rhizoids. The branches varied in width, creating a distinctive, irregular waving pattern along the branch axes.

New collections from Matsumae (Figure 7j) exhibited characteristics that aligned with Noda's (1971, fig. 7; Figure 7h, this study) Dictyota virellus illustration, showing an intermediate morphology between the Natsudomari specimens and Okamura's (1913c; Pl. CXI.; figure 1) concept, although they are more similar to the latter. This morphological variation across regions highlights the complexity within this species and suggests possible phenotypic plasticity or regional differentiation.

A comparison of Okamura's illustration of Dictyota divaricata (Okamura, 1913b, pl. CVIII, figures, 1–8; Figure 5b, this study) with new collections of Canistrocarpus cervicornis (e.g., specimen JOK0041; Figure 5a) suggests that D. divaricata as described by Okamura (1913b) actually corresponds to C. cervicornis. Similarly, comparing Okamura's illustration of D. linearis (Okamura, 1913b, pl. CVII.; Figure 5d, this study) and specimens from the SAP herbarium (e.g., SAP 073771; Figure 5e) with new collections of D. acutiloba (e.g., JOK0056; Figure 5c) indicated that D. linearis sensu Okamura (1913b) actually corresponds to D. acutiloba. The Hawaiian D. acutiloba specimens were somewhat smaller, with broader branches (Figure 5f), which highlights regional morphological variation. Likewise, Dictyota indica sensu Noda (1965) also resembled D. acutiloba and may indeed correspond to this species.

Okamura's illustration of Dictyota patens (Okamura, 1913a, pl. CIV, figures 1, 4; Figure 6k, this study), currently considered a synonym of D. bartayresiana, closely resembled our specimens of D. sandvicensis (e.g., specimen JOK0064; Figure 6l), suggesting that his concept of D. patens may actually represent D. sandvicensis. This was further supported by the morphological dissimilarity between D. bartayresiana and both D. sandvicensis and D. patens sensu Okamura (1913a). Notably, Okamura's records of D. bartayresiana (e.g., Okamura, 1902, 1936) lacked accompanying illustrations, making it difficult to ascertain his concept of that species.

Okamura's illustration of Dictyota dichotoma (Okamura, 1913c, pl. CXII, figures 1–3) appeared to represent D. sumbisoria. This suggests that Okamura's concept of D. dichotoma encompassed multiple species of Dictyota. This interpretation could also apply to D. cervicornis sensu Noda (Noda, 1973; Figure 7), potentially clarifying the taxonomic identity of these historical records as well.

Taxonomic treatments

This study has resulted in a comprehensive taxonomic revision of the Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx genera in Japan, integrating phylogenetic analyses, morphological observations, and historical literature. In this section, we have: (1) synonymized several species based on our findings, (2) formally designated lectotypes and a holotype for nomenclatural clarity, and (3) described one new species. Details of each taxonomic treatment are provided below.

Our analyses resolve the long‐standing taxonomic confusion surrounding the Japanese Dictyota dichotoma, confirming its identity as D. spathulata. This conclusion aligns with the “D. dichotoma2” lineage identified in GenBank and corresponds to Okamura's (1902) concept of Asian D. dichotoma. Consequently, D. virellus (Noda, 1971; Figure 7h) and D. dimorphosa (Ni‐Ni‐Win et al., 2024), which our molecular analyses placed within the D. spathulata clade (Figures 2 and S2–, S5), have been recognized as heterotypic synonyms of D. spathulata (Yamada, 1928; Figure 7b) based on nomenclatural priority.

Morphological and molecular data indicated that the historical record of Dictyota dentata (Okamura, 1914) in Japan (Figure 8e), currently treated as a synonym of D. mertensii, actually corresponds to D. spinulosa. Japanese specimens matching the true D. mertensii were not observed in this study. Re‐examination of historical collections identified that D. divaricata sensu Okamura (Okamura, 1913b; Figure 5b) corresponds to Canistrocarpus cervicornis, resolving a previous misidentification. Comparisons of historical descriptions and new collections suggested that records of Dictyota linearis (Figure 5d,e) and D. indica in Japan—specifically D. linearis sensu Okamura (1913b) and D. indica sensu Noda (1965)—represent misidentifications of D. acutiloba (Figure 5c). Japanese specimens matching the true D. implexa (including its synonyms D. divaricata and D. linearis) were not observed.

To ensure nomenclatural stability and in accordance with the ICN, we formally designate a lectotype for Dictyota marginata, an illegitimate name and a replaced synonym of Dilophus okamurae, which is the basionym of Rugulopteryx okamurae, and we formally designate a holotype for Glossophora coriacea, the basionym of D. coriacea. In each of these cases, the designated specimens either were previously identified as types (although not always formally designated) or were clearly identifiable as such within existing collections, as detailed in the taxonomic notes below.

Haplotype network results

Among the Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx species confirmed from Japan, seven displayed broad distributions. We focused our haplotype network analyses on C. cervicornis, D. ciliolata, and D. coriacea using psbA gene sequences to investigate their genetic diversity and phylogeographic structure.

The haplotype networks for these species revealed predominantly star‐like topologies, indicative of recent population expansion. A single, highly frequent central haplotype was observed in Canistrocarpus cervicornis and Dictyota ciliolata, spanning broad geographic ranges including both the Atlantic and Indo‐Pacific regions. For D. coriacea, a central haplotype was shared among samples from Korea, Japan, and Mexico.

Further details on the haplotype networks, including the specific geographic distribution of derived haplotypes and mutational steps, have been provided in Appendix S2 and Figure S9.

DISCUSSION

Our study has offered critical insights into the diversity and taxonomy of the genera Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx in Japan. Addressing a longstanding debate regarding the presence and taxonomic validity (e.g., nom. illeg. and nom. inval.) of certain species within these genera, we examined historical names and clarified the statuses of Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx in Japan. Recognizing the limitations of traditional morphology‐based approaches for species identification within Dictyotales, we applied molecular tools to reliably identify a substantial portion of the diversity in these genera, contingent upon our sampling scope. This approach enabled us to reassess historical names and validate, correct, or introduce names for these genera in the national checklist. Through extensive sampling, including at type localities, and comprehensive molecular and morphological analyses, we identified 19 distinct species. Our findings significantly advance the understanding of the taxonomy and, to a lesser extent, the diversity of Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx in Japan while contributing valuable data to the global taxonomy of these genera.

Our study has highlighted the challenges of accurately cataloging Dictyota species in Japan, for which historical taxonomic records and synonymy adjustments have yielded varied estimates. Initially, 21 Dictyota names appeared in Japanese literature, although synonymization refined this to 17. This study, incorporating new specimens alongside previously published data, has revised the list to a total of 19 species: 17 species of Dictyota, one species of Canistrocarpus, and one species of Rugulopteryx (Table 2).

Despite this progress, we recognize that gaps remain due to limited sampling. Notably, regions such as the East China Sea ecoregion (northeastern Kyushu), the Ogasawara Islands, Izu Islands, and southernmost islands (e.g., Iriomote, Ishigaki, Yonaguni Islands) remain unstudied, and areas along the Sea of Japan (from Chugoku to Hokkaido Prefectures) have been only sparsely sampled. Furthermore, some herbarium specimens at SAP bear unpublished names not formally recognized in Japanese literature, warranting future investigation to deepen our understanding of Dictyota, Canistrocarpus, and Rugulopteryx diversity in Japan.

The Japanese flora of these genera displayed greater diversity than in Korea (Vieira et al., 2025), with all Korean species also observed in Japan. This indicates that the Korean Dictyota and Rugulopteryx flora represent a subset (~40%) of the more diverse Japanese flora.

Regarding intraspecific genetic diversity, two regionally widespread Japanese‐Korean species, Dictyota spathulata and D. coriacea, showed notable genetic variation, especially given their regional endemism in Japan and Korea. Although D. spathulata exhibited substantial morphological variability, phylogenetic analyses did not support genetic divergence aligning with these differences. This suggests that observed morphologies may result from environmental factors rather than genetic separation, pointing to potential ecotypes adapted to local conditions. Future studies on these species could focus on correlations between morphological and genetic diversity and environmental variables (e.g., temperature, depth, and exposure) to better understand their intraspecific variation.

Taxonomic assessment and revision

It is important to acknowledge that although our comprehensive taxonomic revision relies heavily on robust molecular data from newly collected and existing sequenced material, direct molecular sequencing of historical type specimens was not conducted. Therefore, the reinterpretation of some older names and their application to the modern Japanese flora, particularly for species lacking contemporary molecular data from their type localities, was based on a careful synthesis of historical morphological descriptions, known distribution patterns, and phylogenetic placement of DNA‐confirmed material. This integrative approach combined empirical evidence with expert taxonomic judgment to resolve ambiguities, and we highlight instances where further molecular investigation of original type material would be beneficial.

To date, no comprehensive checklist has integrated recent synonymy and nomenclatural updates to clarify the taxonomy of Dictyoteae in Japan. Our review of Japanese literature and historical records identified 21 species names historically documented; however, after assessing their current nomenclatural statuses, we refined this list to 17 valid taxa. An additional species described in 2024, Dictyota dimorphosa, identified through molecular analyses without prior synonym comparison (Ni‐Ni‐Win et al., 2024), brought this total to 18 species. This list included Canistrocarpus cervicornis (historically recorded as D. cervicornis and D. indica) and Dictyota species such as D. bartayresiana (also as D. patens), D. binghamiae, D. ciliolata (formerly D. maxima), and D. spathulata, among others. Additionally, a substantial portion of these names were American or European in origin, with a few from regions such as the west Indian Ocean (e.g., D. naevosa) and French Polynesia (D. friabilis). Only seven species names in the checklist were originally described from Japan.

Our analysis revealed significant misidentifications in Japanese Dictyota, emphasizing the need for comprehensive taxonomic revision. Of the 18 names historically recorded, only D. bartayresiana, D. coriacea, D. friabilis, D. spathulata, and D. spinulosa were confirmed as valid species in Japan. Notably, D. dichotoma, D. virellus, and D. dimorphosa were all observed to correspond to D. spathulata. Dictyota dentata was identified as D. spinulosa, whereas D. linearis and potentially D. indica were clarified as D. acutiloba. Dictyota divaricata was confirmed as Canistrocarpus cervicornis. Finally, specimens collected from Sado Island were identified either as D. spathulata, corresponding to Noda's (1987) description of D. binghamiae and D. flabellata, or as the Vaughaniella stage of Padina crassa, corresponding to Noda's (1987) description of Dilophus radicans. This has raised the question of whether Noda's D. adhaerens was also based on misidentified material, possibly of D. spathulata prostrate form, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. Molecular analyses ruled out the presence of D. implexa, D. mertensii, and D. naevosa in Japanese waters, while the record of D. dilatata remains uncertain pending further investigation.

We provide the following in‐depth discussion to substantiate the confirmation of a revised checklist for Dictyota, Canistrocarpus, and Rugulopteryx in Japan.

Canistrocarpus cervicornis—This species was not directly recorded in historical Japanese flora but was added to the national checklist through its synonymy with Dictyota cervicornis, a name that had been previously recorded in Japan (Noda, 1987). However, this inclusion was problematic, as the records attributed to D. cervicornis corresponded to a species clearly distinct from C. cervicornis. Although C. cervicornis is indeed present in Japan, the actual species corresponding to this name was originally misidentified as D. divaricata sensu Okamura (1913b), meaning that Okamura's material represents C. cervicornis but under the wrong name. Thus, C. cervicornis is present in the Japanese flora, not under the historical records of D. cervicornis but under Okamura's misapplied name D. divaricata. Further complicating the matter, the morphological concept of D. divaricata proposed by Okamura (1913b) was itself later misapplied by subsequent authors to specimens with distinct morphologies (e.g., SAP 110300, SAP 61448, SAP 9783, SAP 73753; Figure S10a–c). This case exemplifies several challenges: (1) A species name can make its way into a regional flora through synonymy, as seen with C. cervicornis and D. cervicornis, even when the original D. cervicornis records in Japan refer to a different species; and (2) the first record of a species, while foundational, can be compromised by subsequent taxonomic revisions, requiring thorough reassessment of all related records to ensure correct species identification and classification.

Dictyota acutiloba—Our study confirmed the record of Dictyota acutiloba from Japan based on recently collected and observed material, clarifying a persistent case of species misidentification. This species has been previously traced from Japan through molecular data (Vieira et al., 2021). Previously, this species had been documented under the names Dictyota linearis and possibly D. indica (now recognized as a synonym of D. implexa). Based on our morphological and molecular evidence, D. acutiloba is the correct name for the species previously identified in Japan as D. linearis or D. linearis sensu Okamura (1913b). Dictyota linearis was initially reported from Japan by Okamura (1913b); however, it is important to note that D. linearis is an Atlantic species, with its type locality in Cádiz, Spain, and D. implexa was originally described from the Mediterranean. In contrast, D. acutiloba is a Pacific species, first described from Hawaii. Its documented presence in the Mediterranean Sea, as discussed by Delva et al. (2024), suggests a case of introduction beyond its native Pacific range. This case highlights an instance in which a European species name was mistakenly applied to a Japanese species, and we now provide the corrected identification of D. acutiloba as the valid name for the Japanese records.

Dictyota bartayresiana—The first record of this species from Japan is attributed to Okamura (1902), who listed it with the locality as Kumamoto. However, Okamura expressed uncertainty by writing the name as “Dictyota Bartayresiana Lamour.?” and later cited this same entry in Okamura (1931). As such, the origin of the D. bartayresiana recorded in 1902 remains uncertain. In 1931, Okamura reported the species from Orchid Island, Taiwan (then known as “Kotosho”), and later he (Okamura, 1936) referenced his earlier 1931 work to support its occurrence in Japan—without examining any new material. It is important to recognize the historical context: From 1910–1945, the Japanese Empire included the Japanese archipelago, Karafuto, Korea, and Taiwan. Much later, Yoshida (1998) referred to D. bartayresiana (as “D. bartayresii”) in the Ryukyu Islands but did not collect any new material. Instead, he provided a brief description based on Okamura (1936), which would therefore be referencing Okamura's (1931) description, which was based on Taiwanese material. Therefore, it is likely that Yoshida (1998) did not report new records of D. bartayresiana from Japan proper. Vieira et al. (2021) generated the genetic sequences of D. bartayresiana from Japanese material, thus confirming the presence of this species from present‐day Japan. However, their study did not explicitly address whether this represented the first collection of the species from present‐day Japan. Overall, although the presence of D. bartayresiana in Japan had been confirmed, the specifics of its first record from the current geographical boundaries of Japan (excluding Taiwan) remained uncertain. The present study thus provides an explicit report with morphological and molecular observations for D. bartayresiana.

Additionally, Dictyota patens, originally described from the Caribbean (St. Thomas, Virgin Islands) and the South Pacific (Tonga) and now recognized as a synonym of D. bartayresiana, was first reported from Japan by Okamura (1902) from Hyuga (日向) in Miyazaki and later Okamura (1913c) reported it from multiple localities based on material from multiple localities, including Okinawa Island, Nagasaki, Mie, and Kanagawa. Segawa and Kamura (1960) also recorded D. patens from the Ryukyu Islands. However, Okamura himself acknowledged uncertainty in his identification, first writing in Okamura (1902) “Dictyota patens J.Ag.?” then in Okamura (1913a, p. 16) stating, “On referring our plants to the present species, I have seen neither illustrations nor reliable specimens.” This highlights the ambiguity surrounding the application of the name. Nevertheless, Okamura clearly considered his D. bartayresiana and D. patens, which he distinctly listed in his Okamura (1902) checklist and later in Okamura (1913a) and Okamura (1936), to be different species.

Although in the absence of illustrations in the original description it is challenging to directly compare Dictyota patens J. Agardh with D. patens sensu Okamura (1913a), the illustrations provided by Okamura (1913a) differed significantly from the typical morphology of D. bartayresiana, suggesting that D. patens sensu Okamura (1913a) is not conspecific with D. bartayresiana. The closest morphological match to the Dictyota species in Japan is D. sandvicensis, particularly due to features described by Okamura (1913a, p. 16) as “axils furnished with minute teeth or processes along both margins, often growing into proliferous leaflets.” However, further analysis is needed to confirm the identity of D. patens sensu Okamura (1913a), as its current synonymy with D. bartayresiana appears inaccurate based on our evaluation.

Dictyota ciliolata —This species was first recorded in Japan under the name D. maxima sensu Okamura (1936), a species originally described from Indonesia/Malaysia (Zanardini, 1872). Dictyota maxima was later synonymized with D. ciliolata, which was originally described from Venezuela (Caribbean). Our molecular analysis confirmed the presence of this species in Japan, with Japanese specimens showing identical sequences to D. ciliolata material from Venezuela.

Dictyota dilatata —This species was first described by Yamada (1925) from Ryusensui, Hengchun, Taiwan, during the period of Japanese rule over Taiwan. However, the first record attributed specifically to Japan was by Segawa (1956, p. 26), who reported D. dilatata from the middle part of the Pacific coasts of the Honshu region. On closer examination, Segawa's illustration of D. dilatata (Segawa, 1956, pl. 14., figure 120) does not align with the authentic D. dilatata described from Taiwan, suggesting that D. dilatata as interpreted by Segawa (1956) actually represents a different species. Given the lack of verified records, we currently regard reports of D. dilatata in Japan as uncertain and in need of further study to confirm their authenticity.

Dictyota friabilis —This species was first reported in Japan by Tanaka (1990) from Amami Island. Two Japanese lineages, designated here as D. spp. 7JAP and 22JAP, closely matched sequences in GenBank labeled as D. friabilis from the type locality in Tahiti, French Polynesia. Two other Japanese lineages, D. spp. 4JAP and 8JAP, consistently formed two distinct but closely related clades to D. friabilis across all four markers analyzed. Morphologically, these two lineages were also very similar to D. friabilis, although D. sp. 8JAP exhibited larger thalli and a generally erect growth habit, contrasting with the typically adpressed morphology observed in D. friabilis and D. sp. 4JAP. Adopting a conservative taxonomic approach, we have proposed considering D. spp. 4JAP and 8JAP as part of the D. friabilis complex for the present. We recommend further integrated analyses, including more detailed morphological studies and potentially reproductive characterization, to fully resolve their taxonomic statuses and determine if they warrant recognition as distinct species.

Dictyota liturata —This study has clarified a longstanding misidentification of D. liturata in Japan, confirming its presence. Previously, Japanese records identified this species as D. naevosa sensu Yendo (1909). Yendo (1909, p. 120) noted similarities between Japanese specimens and descriptions of D. naevosa, observing, “Our plant exactly answers to the definition and illustrations of the above‐mentioned species. In some of the specimens in our possession, the basal part of the frond is not attenuated but already flat and broad at the rhizine‐bearing point.” A notable finding was an individual collected at 30 m depth off Mageshima (by bottom dredging). This deep‐water specimen differed considerably from those observed in shallow waters (∼1–3 m depth). Although shallow‐water individuals are generally smaller than 3 cm, the 30 m depth individual was 8 cm tall and displayed much wider branches. For example, the second internode had a distal width of 1.8 cm (maximum) and a proximal width of 1 cm, whereas shallow‐water specimens had a distal width of 0.8 cm (maximum) and a proximal width of 0.5 cm. These differences in size and morphology are suggestive of an adaptation to the deep‐water environment, likely to optimize light capture. Both D. liturata and D. naevosa were originally described from South Africa, which likely contributed to the historical misapplication of names. Our analysis confirmed that the Japanese specimens correspond to D. liturata.

Dictyota spathulata—The long‐standing taxonomic ambiguity surrounding the Japanese D. dichotoma, first recorded by Okamura (1902), is finally being resolved. As outlined in the introduction, the taxonomic history of this species has been complex and challenging. Okamura's illustrations in Icones of Algae depicted D. dichotoma as a polymorphic concept (Okamura, 1913–1915), and for years, Japanese taxonomists have acknowledged the clear differences between the Japanese and European forms of D. dichotoma while continuing to use the same name. The polytypic nature of the Japanese populations has been well established. Our study has concluded that the Japanese D. dichotoma corresponds to D. spathulata, described by Yamada (1928) from Mutsu Bay (Aomori Prefecture). This conclusion aligns with previous findings by Vieira et al. (2025) that demonstrated the conspecificity of the Korean D. dichotoma (D. dichotoma sensu Okamura) with D. spathulata. Additionally, D. virellus, described by Noda (1971) from Matsumae (Hokkaido Prefecture), has now been synonymized with D. spathulata due to nomenclatural priority. Furthermore, our analyses resolved that D. dimorphosa (Ni‐Ni‐Win et al., 2024) corresponds to D. spathulata. The congruence of our results with those from the Korean D. dichotoma complex (Vieira et al., 2025) reinforces the broader recognition of D. spathulata in East Asia and highlights the importance of revisiting historical type material in resolving taxonomic confusion. Given the consistent misapplication of the name D. dichotoma in both Korea and Japan and the known morphological plasticity within the genus Dictyota, we suggest that the D. dichotoma records from other Asian countries (e.g., China, Taiwan, and Philippines) also warrant careful reexamination. Although further research, including molecular analyses and detailed morphological comparisons, is necessary to confirm this hypothesis, our findings raise the possibility that D. spathulata may have a wider distribution in the region than currently recognized and that the genuine D. dichotoma from Europe may be absent from Asia altogether.

Dilophus— This study, focused at the Dictyoteae tribe level, supports the absence of Dilophus in Japan (Appendix S1). Historically, Japanese records have included Dp. radicans and Dp. okamurae (now Rugulopteryx okamurae). Dilophus radicans, originally described from the Caroline Islands by Okamura (1916), was later applied by him to Japanese material (Okamura, 1936: from Awaji Island) and further reported from Sado Island by Noda (1987). However, Fan (1953) suggested Dp. radicans (a current synonym of Padina boryana) was the Vaughaniella stage of P. boyrana. Our analysis clarifies that Noda's (1987) Dp. radicans (Figure 8j) from Sado Island represents the Vaughaniella stage of Padina crassa (Figure 8i), supported by co‐occurrence and molecular data (Appendix S1). Thus, Dp. radicans sensu Noda is a misapplication. We cannot definitively determine if Okamura's (1936) Dp. radicans from Awaji Island also represents P. crassa or another Padina species; further investigation of Awaji Island specimens is required (Appendix S1).

New records for Japan

This study has expanded the known Dictyota flora of Japan, adding nine species: Dictyota humifusa, D. ceylanica, D. pfaffii, D. liturata, D. haenyeosa, D. rigida, D. sandvicensis, D. sumbisoria, and D. taewakia. Dictyota humifusa and D. pfaffii, originally described from Colombia, are recorded from Japan based on genetic comparisons with type‐locality material. Dictyota humifusa was likely misidentified as D. dichotoma by Okamura (1913c, pL.CXII, figure 4) given their shared broad basal region and rapidly tapering thallus, which forms narrow branches. The presence of D. pfaffii in Japan has been confirmed solely based on previously published genetic data (Vieira et al., 2021), as it was not collected during this study. This has been corroborated by a more recent record from neighboring Korea (Vieira et al., 2025). Dictyota sandvicensis, originally described from Hawaii (Sonder, 1845), has here been formally recorded from Japan. A sequence from a specimen from Oki Island previously deposited in GenBank (labelled as “D. divaricata”) was curated as D. sandvicensis in a global study by Vieira et al. (2021), indicating the presence of this species in Japan. Finally, three species recently described from Korea have herein been reported for Japan, with two representing new records for the country: D. sumbisoria and D. taewakia. The third species, D. haenyeosa, although formally described from Korea, was previously confirmed in Japan by Vieira et al. (2025) in a study that achieved this confirmation by reidentifying mislabeled sequences from Japanese specimens (e.g., those previously identified as D. koreana nom. inval. or D. dichotoma) available in GenBank. Dictyota sumbisoria, with its long, twisting thallus, seems to agree with Okamura's (1913) sketch of D. dichotoma in figures 1–4 of pl. CXII in his “Icones of Japanese Algae” (Vol. 3). Okamura described this form as the “torted and interwoven” form of his concept of D. dichotoma (Okamura, 1913c, pp. 39–43). Dictyota taewakia may have been confused with D. ciliolata, as they share a similar morphology despite being genetically distinct. Two distinct Japanese lineages, D. sp. 14JAP (matching GenBank sequences labeled “D. rigida6”) and D. sp. 13JAP (corresponding to Vieira et al., 2021 sequence data deposited in GenBank and labeled as “D. rigida2”), consistently clustered within the D. rigida species complex, with a psbA gene sequence from D. rigida type locality (GQ425217; Tanzania) forming a basal position within this clade. This phylogenetic placement suggests that these Japanese lineages may represent cryptic diversity within the D. rigida complex. However, given the absence of corresponding morphological data (for D. sp. 13JAP and the GQ425217 specimen) and the reliance on preexisting genetic information, we have proposed a cautious approach and have tentatively considered these two Japanese lineages as components of the D. rigida species complex, while emphasizing the imperative for comprehensive sampling and detailed morphological investigations to better characterize of this complex.

Novel Japanese species

Our molecular analyses revealed at least one distinct lineage—Dictyota churaumia (sister to GenBank sequences labeled “D. sp12” and “D. sp34”; JOK0090)—within Japanese Dictyota that correspond to novel species of Dictyota that have been described in the present study.

Our species delimitations in this study have been primarily based on analyses of relatively conserved plastid (psbA and rbcL) and mitochondrial (cox1 and cox3) marker genes. Although these markers have proven effective in resolving broad phylogenetic relationships and identifying distinct lineages within the Dictyoteae, it is important to acknowledge that their conserved nature may lead to broader species concepts. The use of more variable markers, such as the nuclear ribosomal ITS rRNA region, or more rapidly evolving intergenic spacers or even whole plastid/mitochondrial genomes for finer‐scale resolution could potentially reveal additional cryptic diversity within currently recognized species or lead to narrower species delimitations. Future research incorporating such highly variable markers alongside comprehensive morphological and ecological data will be crucial for a more granular understanding of species boundaries and the true extent of biodiversity within this tribe, particularly for broadly distributed or morphologically plastic lineages.

Morphological comparisons of Japanese Dictyoteae species

Morphological comparisons of Japanese Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx species revealed a diverse array of distinguishing characteristics, beginning with a clear distinction between size classes. Detailed morpho‐anatomical and ecological data, synthesized from historical Japanese records and our current observations, along with supporting taxonomic notes, are available in Appendix S1. Species such as D. friabilis, D. humifusa, D. pfaffii, D. rigida, and D. sandvicensis (small form) were characterized by their small thalli, typically less than 4–5 cm tall. Within this group, D. pfaffii from Korea was characteristic for its prostrate habit and crater‐like undulations (Vieira et al., 2025), and D. churaumia was distinguished by its unique brown‐to‐green coloration and low branching order. Lineages among the D. friabilis species complex shared similar blue‐green iridescent prostrate to erect forms and required molecular data to distinguish them. Taller species, including C. cervicornis, D. acutiloba, D. ciliolata, D. coriacea, D. haenyeosa, D. sandvicensis (large form), D. spinulosa, D. sumbisoria, D. taewakia, D. spathulata (erect form), and R. okamurae, generally exceeded 4–5 cm in height. Dictyota coriacea stood out with its coarse texture and thick‐walled cortical cells, and D. acutiloba was remarkable for its very thin thallus. Dictyota sumbisoria displayed unique spirally twisted branches and zebra‐like patterns, and C. cervicornis exhibited irregular branching with blue iridescence. Notably, D. spathulata demonstrated significant morphological plasticity, with prostrate, encrusting small thalli often adjacent to erect, taller thalli, as observed in locations such as Matsumae, Hokkaido. This species also showcased variations in color, including uniformly brown and iridescent forms, further highlighting the inherent variability within the genus. Morphological plasticity is a hallmark of the Dictyota genus, yet it was particularly pronounced in D. spathulata, underscoring the importance of considering multiple morphological features and environmental context in species identification. Rugulopteryx okamurae was differentiated by its unique cross‐sectional medullary cell distribution. Finally, species such as D. bartayresiana, D. ceylanica, D. liturata, and D. spinulosa each possessed subtle yet distinct features in branching, color, and texture, which when combined with size and growth habit, allowed for their identification. These morphological distinctions coupled with molecular analyses provided a comprehensive understanding of species boundaries within these Japanese brown algae. Table S6 provides a tentative dichotomous key to assist in the field identification of Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx species occurring in Japan. Although based on observable morphological traits, the key should be used with caution, as many characters show overlap or plasticity among species, and the reliability of identification may vary depending on the condition and developmental stage of the specimens. This key is not intended as a definitive tool but as a practical aid in fieldwork and preliminary sorting. Ultimately, accurate species identification should rely on molecular analyses (e.g., DNA barcoding), especially for taxa with cryptic or convergent morphologies.

Geographic distribution insights

The taxonomic revisions and species confirmations presented in this study provide a refined framework for interpreting the biogeographic patterns of Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx in Japan. Our analyses reveal a spectrum of geographic distributions, ranging from species endemic to the Northwest Pacific to those with global or widespread regional ranges.

Regional endemism and Northwest Pacific distributions

Eight species exhibit distributions largely confined to the Northwest Pacific, suggesting regional endemism. These include Dictyota coriacea, D. haenyeosa, D. spathulata, D. spinulosa, D. sumbisoria, D. taewakia, D. churaumia, and Rugulopteryx okamurae. Notably, six of these (D. haenyeosa, D. spathulata, D. spinulosa, D. sumbisoria, D. taewakia, and D. churaumia) have been exclusively documented in the Northwest Pacific based on molecular data.

Rugulopteryx okamurae, although native to the Northwest Pacific, exemplifies human‐mediated dispersal, with its well‐documented introduction to European waters. Haplotype network analyses, including those from Vieira et al. (2025), confirmed the Japanese origin of the invasive European populations.

Dictyota coriacea presents a unique case, displaying a trans‐Pacific distribution, occurring in both the Northwest (Japan and Korea) and Northeast Pacific (California and Mexico). Our haplotype network analysis indicated higher genetic diversity in the Northwest Pacific and a strong genetic connection between Japanese and Korean populations (Figure S9c). The observed genetic relationships, with Mexican populations nested within a dominant haplotype and Californian haplotypes branching from it, suggest potential recent dispersal from Asia to the Northeast Pacific. This pattern aligns with documented cases of Asian seaweed introductions to the Northeast Pacific (Miller et al., 2011) and similar trans‐North Pacific distributions observed in other Dictyotales genera such as Padina (Díaz‐Martínez et al., 2016; Vieira et al., 2024).

Widespread distributions and dispersal histories

Twelve species exhibit broader distributions, encompassing global, trans‐Pacific, or Indo‐Pacific ranges: Canistrocarpus cervicornis, Dictyota acutiloba, D. bartayresiana, D. ceylanica, D. ciliolata, D. friabilis, D. humifusa, D. liturata, D. pfaffii, D. rigida, and D. sandvicensis. Among these, a subset exhibits global distributions: C. cervicornis, D. bartayresiana, D. ciliolata, D. humifusa, D. pfaffii, and D. rigida are characterized by cosmopolitan distributions, suggesting a high dispersal potential. Other species demonstrated regional distributions. Specifically, D. acutiloba, D. sandvicensis, and D. friabilis span the Northwest to Central Pacific, while D. liturata extends from the Northwest Pacific to the western Indo‐Pacific. Dictyota ceylanica is confined to the central Indo‐Pacific region.

Dictyota pfaffii appears to have originated in the Northeast Atlantic, with a potential introduction to the Pacific, as suggested by Vieira et al. (2025). The higher haplotypic diversity in the Atlantic supports this hypothesis.

Dictyota bartayresiana, originally described from the Caribbean, likely originated in the Indo‐Pacific, with a potential introduction to the Caribbean evidenced by haplotype network analysis revealing a distinct Indo‐Pacific haplogroup (Vieira et al., 2025).

Dictyota ciliolata exhibited significant geographic differentiation, suggesting both local adaptation and gene flow. Its complex haplotype network, with a dominant central haplotype encompassing Indo‐Pacific and Atlantic populations, made it challenging to pinpoint its precise origin.

Species such as Dictyota liturata, D. friabilis, D. acutiloba, D. sandvicensis, D. ceylanica, D. rigida, and D. humifusa warrant further investigation to elucidate their origins and dispersal histories, particularly regarding potential introduction or range expansion events.

In conclusion, the biogeographic patterns observed in Canistrocarpus, Dictyota, and Rugulopteryx in Japan reflect a complex interplay of regional endemism, natural dispersal, and human‐mediated introductions, with two cases of Northwest Pacific endemic species introduced to the Atlantic (Rugulopteryx okamurae) and Northeast Pacific (Dictyota coriacea). Further research, integrating molecular phylogeography with ecological and oceanographic data, is essential to fully elucidate the evolutionary and biogeographic histories of these species.

Species diversity within Japan's Dictyoteae exhibited a clear latitudinal gradient, peaking in the southern region of Kyushu (with an observed richness reaching 15 species based on overlapping ranges) and diminishing sharply to a single species in the northernmost area of Japan, in Hokkaido Prefecture. Species‐specific distributions further illustrated these patterns. Dictyota spathulata demonstrated the broadest national distribution, ranging from Kyushu (Kagoshima Prefecture) to northern Hokkaido, indicating a remarkable tolerance to a wide range of temperatures. Similarly, D. coriacea spanned from Kanto (Chiba Prefecture) to Okinawa, and Canistrocarpus cervicornis occurred from Tohoku (Yamagata Prefecture, Sea of Japan) to Kyushu (Miyazaki Prefecture, East China Sea). Based on their distribution, species can be categorized by thermal affinities. Dictyota bartayresiana, D. friabilis, D. churaumia, and D. spinulosa are tropical species thus far restricted to Okinawa. Species considered warm‐temperate to tropical include D. ceylanica, D. ciliolata, D. liturata, D. pfaffii, D. rigida, and D. sandvicensis. Finally, D. coriacea, D. haenyeosa, D. spathulata, and D. sumbisoria can be categorized as cold to warm‐temperate. Notably, the absence of strictly cold‐temperate species aligns with the findings of Vieira et al. (2021) suggesting a tropical origin for Dictyota and subsequent dispersal into temperate zones. Dictyota spathulata stood out for its exceptional tolerance to low temperatures and significant seasonal temperature fluctuations. It thrives in northern Hokkaido's Sea of Okhotsk (in Abashiri), where winter temperatures reach a minimum of −2°C (January) and a summer maximum of 22°C (August) according to the Japan Meteorological Agency (https://www.data.jma.go.jp/kaiyou/data/db/kaikyo/series/engan/engan103.html), demonstrating a remarkable ability to withstand extreme cold. For comparison, D. falklandica, another cold‐water species, thrives in sub‐Antarctic environments, representing one of the species with the coldest confirmed habitats in the genus (Küpper et al., 2019). Its habitat, however, experiences far less extreme thermal fluctuations (5–9°C) compared to the wide seasonal range observed in Hokkaido's Abashiri region.

Lessons learned from the Japanese Dictyoteae flora taxonomic revision case study

The first record of a species in a region, as initially described by the author who documented it, often serves as the foundational concept of that species in the area. However, subsequent records of the species, which are no longer “new” findings, may diverge from the original concept due to taxonomic revisions or misidentifications. If the original record was incorrectly assigned to a species—that is, if the name applied was wrong and the specimen actually belonged to a different species—then the initial misapplied name should be corrected and the erroneous entry removed from regional or national species checklists. Furthermore, records made after the first, which were based on this misidentification but may actually correspond to the species name that was initially proposed, should be reassessed. This reevaluation is crucial for determining whether these subsequent records align with the species concept that was ultimately determined not to match the material from the first record.

CONCLUSIONS

These results represent significant contributions to the Japanese flora and highlight the importance of revisiting historical records with modern taxonomic tools. Although this work has provided a comprehensive revision of the Dictyoteae flora, a potential unknown diversity may remain, suggested by unique morphologies observed in historical herbarium specimens at SAP that were not observed in our field samples and may represent additional, yet‐unidentified species.
