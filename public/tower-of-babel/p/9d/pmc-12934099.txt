Cross-cultural validation of the Chinese version of the nurses’ ethical decision-making around end-of-life care scale: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sibo Liu, Di Xu, Shuang Wen, Xian Liu, Dandan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn end-of-life care, nurses’ ability to make sound ethical decisions is critical to safeguarding patients’ dignity and quality of life. However, China still lacks a measurement tool tailored to this specific context for assessing such competence. This study, therefore, aimed to localize the Nurses’ Ethical Decision-Making around End-of-Life Care Scale (NEDM-EOLCS) into Chinese and to examine its psychometric characteristics among Chinese nurses.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was conducted. The study was conducted between October 2024 and December 2024. The Chinese version of the NEDM-EOLCS scale was initially developed using the Brislin translation model, cross-cultural debugging, and a pre-survey tailored to Chinese linguistic and cultural contexts. 450 nurses completed the Chinese version of the NEDM-EOLCS scale. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was employed to analyse the data from Group 1 (n = 225) in order to elucidate the factor structure, whereas Group 2 data (n = 225) were subjected to confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to verify the model’s suitability; additionally, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability tests were carried out.ResultsA total of 450 nurses participated in the survey. The Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was 0.98; EFA extracted three factors and explained 61.816% of the total variance; CFA confirmed that all the goodness-of-fit indices were acceptable. The Cronbach’s alpha of the Chinese version of the NEDM-EOLCS was 0.962, and the retest reliability coefficient was 0.896. The Chinese version of NEDM-EOLCS had 55 items in 3 dimensions.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of NEDM-EOLCS is scientifically reasonable and has good reliability and validity. It can be used to investigate Chinese nurses’ ethical decision-making around end-of-life care.

Background

End-of-life care is the final stage of the health care continuum and is essential for maintaining dignity and quality of life. As life expectancy increases and epidemiological patterns shift [1], there will be a growing global need to care for people with life-threatening illnesses [2], and end-of-life care has become a significant component of the framework of modern healthcare systems in China [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that 25.7 million of the 56.8 million individuals who require palliative care each year are in their last year of life [4]. Research indicates that approximately 7.5 million people in China require end-of-life care each year [5]. When patients’ diseases progress to the end stage, they suffer from physical and psychosomatic problems, such as dyspnoea, pain, anxiety, and depression, which seriously affect their quality of life [6–8].

Nurses, as members of the multidisciplinary palliative care team, play an important role in improving patients’ quality of life at the end of life [9]. However, in end-of-life care, nurses face ethical dilemmas, including disease disclosure and informed consent, conflicts between autonomous decision-making and medical agency, over-medicalization, and ineffective medical treatment [10–12]. An integrated review revealed that the most frequently cited ethical concerns pertained to end-of-life issues [13]. End-of-life care is intrinsically laden with ethical difficulties, and nurses often encounter intricate judgments that require balancing the principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice [14, 15]. Ethical dilemmas can cause severe damage to nurses’ physical and mental health. Studies have found that nurses experience negative emotions such as guilt, pain, anger, self-doubt, and loss of self-esteem due to ethical dilemmas, which may increase the risk of depression and other health problems [16, 17]. In addition, it erodes nurses’ professional confidence and exacerbates burnout, leading to a rising rate of nursing staff turnover [18, 19]. A survey of nurses’ propensity to leave found that nearly 50% of nurses reported considering leaving due to adverse experiences with ethical dilemmas [20, 21], and 10% to 25% of medical staff have left due to ethical dilemmas [22]. Whether it is the decline in the health of individual nurses or the loss of nursing human resources, ethical dilemmas affect the quality of care at both the individual and organisational levels, undermining patient rights and safety.

Ethical decisions need to be made when faced with an ethical dilemma [23]. To be able to make such decisions, a person needs to have competence [24]. Timely ethical decision-making by nurses is crucial for delivering important services to patients [25] and can also speed patient recovery and minimize healthcare expenditures [26]. Studies have indicated that the quality of clinical care is directly affected by ethical nursing decision-making [27]. Thus, nurses and all healthcare professionals should possess adequate knowledge and skills in the ethical decision‑making process to ensure they make the most accurate decision on behalf of their patients [28]. Supplying nurses with the requisite knowledge, competencies, and backing is essential for navigating end-of-life care’s ethical challenges and intricacies [29]. According to a systematic review, conducting ongoing education and instruction regarding ethical decision-making is required to give nurses the abilities they need to deliver superior end-of-life care [30]. Therefore, accurately assessing nurses’ capacity for ethical decision‑making in end‑of‑life situations not only helps improve the quality of life of terminally ill patients but also helps understand the decision-making patterns of professional nurses in ethical dilemmas and provides a theoretical basis for developing related education and interventions.

By reviewing the literature, it is found that scholars at domestic and abroad have developed measurement tools for ethical decision-making from different perspectives. Currently, the primary ethical decision-making assessment tool used in the domestic nursing field is the Judgment About Nursing Decision (JAND) [31], which primarily assesses nurses’ ethical decision-making level from the perspectives of ethical cognition and ethical behaviour. The Chinese version of JAND was translated by a domestic scholar, Zhang, but it exhibits an imbalance in item attribution, with two items lacking validity [32]. Hu (2024) developed an instrument to evaluate nursing students’ ethical decision‑making capacity and subsequently conducted a systematic psychometric validation of its reliability and validity [23]. However, this scale focuses on evaluating nursing students’ skills in judgment, sensitivity, motivation, and action demonstrated in the ethical decision-making process. Chen (2025) developed an instrument to evaluate nurses’ ethical decision‑making competence and subsequently conducted psychometric validation of the instrument [33]. Current instruments for assessing nurses’ ethical decision‑making were developed largely for everyday clinical scenarios. Yet, end-of-life care confronts nurses with uniquely complex and emotionally charged dilemmas [34, 35]. However, existing tools may have limitations in assessing ethical decision-making in end-of-life care. Based on Rest’s theory, Sanghee Kim developed the Nurses’ Ethical Decision Making around End-of-Life-Care Scale (NEDM-EOLCS) [36]. This is designed to assess nurses’ ethical decision-making in end-of-life care, with a focus on issues such as discontinuing life support and pain management. Iranian scholars have already assessed its psychometric properties and demonstrated good internal consistency and reliability in its application [37]. This study adapts the scale for a Chinese cultural context and evaluates its reliability and validity, thereby offering a culturally appropriate instrument for assessing nurses’ ethical decision-making in end-of-life care.

Methods

Translation and cultural adaptation

We contacted Dr Sanghee Kim, the developer of the NEDM-EOLCS, and obtained her permission. Forward translation: In accordance with Brislin’s guidelines [38], the NEDM‑EOLCS was separately translated into Chinese by an English‑proficient nursing specialist and an English-proficient master’s-degree-holding translator. After the translation was finished, the two translators and the researcher met to compare the translated versions, discuss discrepancies, and reconcile differences to develop a preliminary Chinese version for subsequent back-translation. Subsequently, it was independently translated into English by a PhD in Nursing and a Master of Clinical Medicine, neither of whom had ever had access to the original scale. The discrepancies in the back-translated version were reviewed and adjusted. Eventually, the researchers submitted the back-translated version to the scale developer for review, and Professor Sanghee Kim approved our translation work.

To better apply the scale to the Chinese nursing context, we invited six experts with high academic achievement or long-term work experience in medical ethics or palliative care. The experts had work experience of 25 to 41 (33.33 ± 6.38) years. The experts evaluated each scale item independently with respect to conceptual equivalence, content relevance, and the lucidity of its presentation. Each item was evaluated independently, and any language that was ambiguous or complex was modified. Subsequently, fifteen nurses from the Intensive Care Unit and oncology departments tested the NEDM-EOLCS in Chinese until all misunderstandings were eliminated. After these procedures, the NEDM-EOLCS-C was established.

Study design and procedure

From October to December 2024, a convenience sampling method was implemented to survey nurses at two tertiary care institutions in Liaoning. The inclusion criteria include clinical nurses with a minimum of a year’s worth of work experience, experience in caring for end-of-life patients, informed consent, and willingness to participate in this study. The exclusion criterion was nurses who were unable to be on duty during the survey period due to personal or sick leave. In accordance with the principle that the sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of entries, and given the statistical requirements for exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis in this study, the sample size must be at least 300. Allowing for a potential dropout rate of 20%, a total of 471 questionnaires were distributed. To ensure the accuracy of the results, after excluding questionnaires in which the answer to each entry on the same scale was the same option or in which there was a clear regularity throughout the questionnaire, the final 450 valid surveys were returned, yielding an effective response rate of 95.5%.

Instruments

General demographic questionnaire

The general population knowledge questionnaire includes gender, age, religious beliefs, education level, department, years of work experience, job title, job satisfaction, marital status, and frequency of general ethical occurrences.

The NEDM-EOLCS

The Nurses’ Ethical Decision-Making around End-of-Life Care Scale is a self-assessment tool [36], developed and psychometrically validated by Professor Sanghee Kim, that measures nurses’ ethical decision‑making competence across end‑of‑life care scenarios. The scale comprises three dimensions with a total of 55 items: moral reasoning and moral agency (13 items), moral practice (14 items), and perceived professional accountability (28 items). A Likert scale of six points, from 1 (never/strongly disagree) to 6 (always/strongly agree), is used to score it. The scale’s total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.950, and the dimensions’ alpha coefficients range from 0.88 to 0.95, indicating that the NEDM-EOLCS has sufficient reliability. This is evident in the Iranian version of the NEDM-EOLCS. In their investigation, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranged from 0.96 to 0.98 across dimensions, with a total of 0.98.

Data collection

This study employed a paper-based questionnaire. Four trained researchers introduced the study purpose and procedures to eligible nurses. After providing written informed consent, participants completed the questionnaire independently. All questionnaires were collected on-site and immediately checked for completeness; any missing items were completed promptly, resulting in no missing data in the final dataset. After data collection, the researchers collated and reviewed the questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

The statistical software SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0 were used for data analysis. Skewness and kurtosis were calculated to evaluate the distributional characteristics of the variables. Absolute values of skewness < 2 and kurtosis < 7 were considered indicative of acceptable normality. For measurement data that approximated a normal distribution, results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Floor and ceiling effects were calculated to assess the distribution of scores and were considered significant when more than 15% of respondents achieved the lowest or highest possible scores [39].

Content validity

Six experts were invited to participate in the cultural adaptation assessment. Using a 4-point Likert scale to evaluate each item’s semantics and relevance to the dimension, drawing on professional theoretical knowledge and clinical experience. “Not relevant”, “weakly relevant”, “relatively relevant”, and “strongly relevant” are denoted by points 1–4, respectively. The scale demonstrates excellent content validity if the S-CVI is greater than 0.9 and the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) is greater than 0.78 [40].

Item analysis

Item analysis is primarily used to assess the appropriateness and reliability of scale items [41]. Using the correlation coefficient and decision value approaches provides a valid foundation for item modification or screening. Determine the Critical Ratio and test its significance by ranking the translated scale’s total scores in reverse order and utilising an independent-samples t-test to compare the upper 27% (high-score group) with the lower 27% (low-score group). If the Critical Ratio of each item is > 3.00, the item has a strong discriminant ability, and the item with P ≥ 0.05 is deleted. Quantify the correlation between the aggregate score and each scale item. An item is considered relevant and has good overall discrimination if its correlation coefficient with the total score is between 0.3 and 1.0. Items having a correlation coefficient less than 0.3 or that fall below the significance level (P ≥ 0.05) ought to be eliminated.

Construct validity

The construct validity of the NEDM-EOLCS-C was evaluated via the use of both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). EFA was conducted using principal component analysis and maximum variance orthogonal rotation analysis. Prior to analysis, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value, which is more than 0.6, and the Bartlett’s sphericity test, which is statistically significant at a level of P < 0.05, were used to determine whether or not the target scale was suitable for factor analysis. Regarding CFA, it is essential to determine if the structural model is plausible by analysing the magnitude of the fitting index. Since there are so many different types of fitting index, nonetheless, not all types of fitting indices need scrutiny. CMIN/DF (chi-square/degree of freedom), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Parsimonious comparative-fit-index (PCFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) were all measured in this study. A total of 450 samples were split into two groups at random: 225 for EFA and 225 for CFA.

Convergent validity and discriminant validity

The average variance extracted (AVE) was used to evaluate convergent validity. AVE between 0.36 and 0.50 is acceptable. Composite reliability (CR), which ranges from 0 to 1, primarily assesses the manifest indicators’ internal consistency. The discriminant validity is tested using the Fornell-Larcker criterion [42]. The square root of a factor’s AVE is compared to its correlation with any other factor to determine its discriminant validity; if the square root of the AVE is greater than the correlation coefficient, discriminant validity is accepted.

Reliability analysis

Using Cronbach’s alpha value and test-retest reliability, the scale’s reliability was evaluated. Two weeks after the initial survey, 50 nurses were randomly selected from the participants to serve as retest reliability participants, and they completed the questionnaire again independently. The scale’s retest reliability was assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. If the value of Cronbach’s alpha is more than 0.7 [43] and Pearson’s r is greater than 0.8 [44], then the reliability of the scale is considered to be satisfactory.

Ethical approval

The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University reviewed and approved this study (Approval No. KYLL2024435). Prior to data collection, all participants received an explanation of the study aims and procedures, and each provided written informed consent. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Participation was voluntary, with the right to withdraw at any time, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Confidentiality of participant information was strictly maintained, and all data were used solely for research purposes.

Results

Cross-cultural adaptation

Experts noted that, given the cultural context of filial piety in China, end-of-life decisions may not always be made solely by the patient. Family members—particularly adult children—often play a significant role in the decision-making process [45]. Therefore, replace item 3 “I try to tailor care to a patient’s individual need” with “I try to tailor care to a patient’s individual and their family need”.

Face validity and content validity

The surface reliability of NEDM-EOLCS-C was assessed by 15 nurses from the Intensive Care Unit and oncology departments. These nurses had been in the field for almost a decade and specialised in caring for patients towards the end of life. The participants indicated that the semantics of the scale were clear. The mean duration for completing the NEDM-EOLCS-C was 7 to 10 min. The scale had good content validity, evidenced by the evaluations of six experts, yielding a S-CVI of 0.976 and an I-CVI ranging from 0.8 to 1.0.

Items analysis

The decision-making method’s findings revealed that the items’ Critical Ratio varied from 8.166 to 18.507 (P < 0.001) (Table 3). The total score was positively correlated with all items, with correlation coefficients ranging between 0.420 and 0.688 (P < 0.001) (Table 4). The total score and each dimension were correlated with coefficients ranging from 0.638 to 0.857. (P < 0.001), all exceeding the acceptable threshold.

Reliability analysis

The NEDM-EOLCS-C’s overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.962, while the three dimensions of moral reasoning and moral agency, professional responsibility, and moral practice have corresponding values of 0.916, 0.969, and 0.951. NEDM-EOLCS-C has a retest reliability of 0.896.

Discussion

For the first time in China, the Nurses’ Ethical Decision-Making around End-of-Care was translated, cross-culturally adapted, and tested for reliability. The study’s findings indicated that the NEDM-EOLCS-C had robust psychometric properties and is suitable for evaluating the ethical decision-making capabilities of Chinese nurses in end-of-life care scenarios. The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the Chinese version of the scale was 0.962, and the Cronbach’s alphas for the dimensions ranged from 0.916 to 0.969, with a retest reliability of 0.896. These results indicate that the scale has excellent internal consistency, stability, and good measurement agreement, with similar or slightly higher reliability results than the original Korean (Cronbach’s alpha range 0.88–0.95) and Iranian (Cronbach’s alpha 0.96–0.98) versions of the scale, further validating the scale’s cross-cultural stability and reliability in applications. In this study, three common factors were extracted by exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which explained a total of 61.816% of the total variance, which was slightly lower than that of the Iranian version of the scale (74.51%) but significantly higher than that of the original Korean scale (46.4%). Differences in sample size, study methods, and cultural backgrounds may account for this disparity. In particular, the Iranian version’s higher proportion of variance explaining power might indicate the greater consistency of nurses’ knowledge and comprehension of ethical decision-making competence in the area, while the original Korean study’s small sample size (230 nurses) might have impacted the stability of the factor structure and its variance explaining power. Beyond reliability and factor structure, it is also important to examine the distributional characteristics of scale scores to further evaluate measurement performance. Ceiling and floor effects are important measurement properties, as pronounced score clustering at the extremes may limit a scale’s discriminative ability and sensitivity. In this study, ceiling and floor effects—defined as a substantial proportion of respondents achieving the highest or lowest possible scores—were examined to evaluate the performance of the NEDM-EOLCS-C. None of the participants achieved the minimum or maximum possible total scores, indicating the absence of significant ceiling or floor effects. The absence of extreme score clustering suggests that the NEDM-EOLCS-C provides adequate score variability across different levels of ethical decision-making ability among nurses. This finding supports the scale’s capacity to differentiate nurses with varying levels of ethical decision-making competence without saturation at the scale extremes. From a measurement perspective, the lack of pronounced ceiling and floor effects further supports the sensitivity and discriminative capacity of the NEDM-EOLCS-C when applied to Chinese nurses involved in end-of-life care.

The positive role of end-of-life care services in responding to the needs of people with terminal illnesses and their families has been valued. Among medical staff, nurses are closest to patients and guardians within 24 h, set goals for the treatment of terminally ill patients, understand their needs and wishes, and, as care providers, guide patients’ preferences [47]. As a result, they will encounter ethical decision-making situations with terminally ill patients. Nurses’ ability to make ethical decisions in end-of-life care is crucial to ensuring that patients experience a peaceful death in hospital settings [48]. Scholar Hiroko Nagae argued that nurses have much to offer and can enhance the quality of end-of-life care [49]. A Turkish study found a statistically significant positive correlation between nurses’ ethical decision-making and their professional behaviour, suggesting that both domains should be strengthened simultaneously through education and training [50]. Therefore, this study aimed to translate and validate the Chinese version of the NEDM-EOLCS to provide a reliable measure of ethical decision-making competence among nurses involved in end-of-life care across different clinical departments in China. The Chinese version of the NEDM-EOLCS, developed through cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation, demonstrates clear item wording and a well-defined structure. The scale demonstrates good comprehensibility and feasibility, with a moderate number of items that can be completed within a relatively short time, suggesting its potential applicability in research and clinical settings.

Designed around ethical situations commonly encountered in end-of-life care, the NEDM-EOLCS is intended to assess nurses’ ethical decision-making in end-of-life-related contexts. Although ethical challenges in end-of-life care may vary across disease conditions, the scale focuses on core ethical decision-making processes that are shared across end-of-life care practice. Therefore, it is not disease-specific and may be used to measure and compare ethical decision-making among nurses caring for patients with different disease backgrounds.

It should be noted that the NEDM-EOLCS is a self-report instrument, and its content is based on nurses’ ethical decision-making experiences in end-of-life care practice. For nurses who have participated in or been exposed to end-of-life or end-of-life care, the results may be more stable and interpretable. In future research and practice, this scale can be used to evaluate and compare ethical decision-making among nurses with relevant clinical backgrounds in end-of-life care settings, thereby providing empirical support for ethics education, nursing management, and ongoing improvements in the quality of end-of-life care.

Limitations

It is essential to recognise certain methodological limitations, despite the NEDM-EOLCS-C’s promising psychometric qualities. First, participants were recruited from two tertiary hospitals in Liaoning, which may limit the representativeness and generalisability of the findings; future studies should consider multi-centre sampling with broader clinical settings. Second, unmeasured confounding (e.g., department, years of experience, and education) may have influenced response patterns; future research could address this through stratified sampling and/or multivariable analyses. Third, known-group validity was not formally established because the sample was relatively homogeneous (all were clinical nurses with end-of-life care experience), which limited the definition of theoretically distinct “known groups”. Exploratory subgroup comparisons (by job title and education) did not yield statistically significant differences and were therefore treated as preliminary rather than confirmatory evidence; future studies should include more heterogeneous groups (e.g., novice vs. expert nurses, or nurses with vs. without structured end-of-life care training) to further examine discriminative validity. Finally, although the NEDM-EOLCS-C demonstrated robust internal psychometric properties, the present study did not include an external criterion measure due to copyright restrictions on the originally planned comparator instrument. Future research should address this limitation by incorporating the validated Chinese version of the Palliative Care Difficulties Scale [51] to examine external associations, thereby further supporting the clinical relevance and utility of this measure.

Conclusion

The validity and reliability of the Chinese version of the NEDM-EOLCS are good. It is useful for assessing the ethical decision-making of Chinese nurses in end-of-life care. Given the increasing prominence of ethical dilemmas in hospice care, the introduction and use of this scale may provide an objective basis for assessing and improving nurses’ ethical decision-making in end-of-life care. Additionally, it can serve as a reference for nursing managers creating focused ethical decision-making training programs. This will enhance nurses’ capacity to make ethical decisions and determine the best course of action for patients nearing the end of life, thereby improving the quality of care.
