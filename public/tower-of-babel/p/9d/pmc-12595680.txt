Associations between socio-economic factors, mental health knowledge, and psychological distress among Lebanese adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elias Assaf, Maha Nehme, Charbel Eid, Hilda Chelala, Dunya Nohra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety and depression are significant public health concerns influenced by socio-economic factors. This study investigates the associations between socio-economic variables and levels of anxiety, mental health knowledge, and depression in a sample of 425 Lebanese adults.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using validated self-reported questionnaires to assess anxiety, depression, and mental health knowledge. Bivariate analyses were performed to examine correlations between socio-economic and paramedical factors and mental health outcomes.ResultsThe mean mental health knowledge score was 51.83 ± 11.22. The average depression score was 10.0 ± 7.22), and the average anxiety score was 9.46 ± 6.06). Moderate to severe depression was observed in 41.4% of participants, and moderate to severe anxiety in 36.6%. Adverse employment changes, low income, and low education levels were significantly associated with higher levels of anxiety and depression (p < 0.001). Individuals with middle income and higher education had significantly better mental health knowledge. Interestingly, high-income individuals had lower knowledge scores.ConclusionSocio-economic status plays a crucial role in shaping mental health outcomes, with financial insecurity and unemployment emerging as key risk factors for anxiety and depression. Strengthening mental health education, particularly among socio-economically disadvantaged groups, and promoting workplace mental health awareness programs could help mitigate these effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03566-3.

Introduction

Mental health is a major public health concern worldwide. In Lebanon, a country with a recent history of armed conflicts, economic crises, and political tensions, mental health challenges are particularly serious. The unique Lebanese context, characterized by socio-economic, cultural, and political factors, exacerbates mental health disorders among adults.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately one in four adults will experience a mental disorder at some point in their lives, with alarming rates in Lebanon reflecting global trends intensified by local factors [1].

The mental health of adults in Lebanon has profound and multifaceted repercussions, influencing various aspects of individual and collective life. Mental disorders exert a multifaceted impact on individuals, compromising their emotional stability, interpersonal relationships, work productivity, and financial security.

Lebanon has endured numerous crises over the past decades, including a civil war from 1975 to 1990, intermittent conflicts, the Beirut port explosion, and a severe economic crisis since 2019. These events have left profound scars on the population, with notable increases in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety. Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic and the ongoing economic crisis have further exacerbated these issues, underscoring the urgent need for tailored mental health services [2].

A 2018 study, conducted before the onset of the current crisis, found that 19.7% of Lebanese individuals with mental disorders sought some form of treatment, with 91% receiving care within the health sector. The perceived severity of the disorders predicted help-seeking behavior, with panic disorder being the highest. The greatest barrier to seeking help was a low perceived need for treatment (73.9%), while stigma was a factor for only 5.9% of those considering treatment. 80% of Lebanese reported they would not be embarrassed if their friends knew they were seeking professional help [3].

However, mental health treatment in Lebanon faces numerous obstacles. Mental health infrastructure is inadequate, with an uneven distribution of services across the country, limiting access for many Lebanese, especially those in rural or remote areas. Additionally, social stigma surrounding mental disorders remains a significant barrier, with many individuals hesitant to seek help due to fear of judgment or discrimination. Despite these challenges, efforts are underway to improve the situation.

Awareness initiatives, training for mental health professionals, and policy reforms are necessary to address these issues holistically and effectively.

The WHO plays a crucial role in improving mental health in Lebanon. As a specialized agency of the United Nations for public health, the WHO supports initiatives to strengthen mental health systems through technical advice, training, and funding. The WHO has collaborated with Lebanon’s Ministry of Public Health to develop national mental health programs, improve health professionals’ training, and raise public awareness to reduce the stigma associated with mental disorders [4].

The medication cost for mental disorders in Lebanon represents a significant challenge for individuals and the healthcare system. Psychotropic medications, essential for treating many mental disorders such as depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia, can be expensive and inaccessible to a large portion of the population. The current economic crisis in Lebanon has worsened this situation, making treatments harder to access. In other words, medications cost is one of the main barriers to treatment adherence for patients with mental disorders. Additionally, the reimbursement of psychotropic medications by the health care insurance systems is often limited, increasing the financial burden on patients and their families [1, 5].

Given these previous data, the aim of this study is to evaluate the mental health status among Lebanese adults, and to identify the main obstacles preventing equitable access to treatments in order to propose solutions that improve mental health services in Lebanon.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a cross-sectional analytical study on the characteristics and knowledge of adults residing in Lebanon regarding mental health. This study was conducted throughout the years 2023 and 2024 in all regions of Lebanon.

The target population includes all individuals aged 18 and older residing in Lebanon. To ensure a faithful representation of Lebanon’s demographic diversity, the sampling included adults from various geographical regions, as well as from different socio-economic, educational, and professional backgrounds. A stratified random sample was used to ensure that all relevant sub-populations are adequately represented, allowing for an in-depth analysis in this specific context.

Minimal sample size

Referring to Epi-info software version 7, the minimal sample size of 393 participants were needed for a significant analysis. Taking into account a 95% confidence level with a +/- 3 as margin of error knowing that the Lebanese adult population is approximatively 4 million individuals.

Data collection

An online questionnaire was prepared in Arabic language and distributed via Google forms, an online platform that allows for efficient and accessible data collection. Questionnaire was spread using What’s App Messenger as this is the most commonly used mobile application in Lebanon for texting and communication.

The snowball sampling method, was used to recruit participants. This technique involves asking initial respondents to refer other people from their network who meet the inclusion criteria. This approach helps reach a larger and more diverse sample.

Sample size and inclusion criteria

A total of 425 valid responses were collected from adults in Lebanon. This number was determined to ensure sufficient statistical representation for meaningful analyses.

Only adults aged 18 and older, permanent residents of Lebanon, with the ability to understand and respond to the survey questions were included in this study.

Individuals under 18 years old, non-residents or temporary residents of Lebanon, unable to understand or respond to the survey questions due to language or cognitive barriers, with incomplete or invalid responses (inconsistent or missing answers) were excluded. By adhering to these criteria, the study aims to obtain a representative sample of the adult Lebanese population.

Measurement tools

The questionnaire consisted of a set of questions divided into five sections. The first section covered the sociodemographic parameters of the participants: age, gender, nationality, marital status, educational level, working status, monthly family income and health insurance. Furthermore, participants were asked about their use and source of prescription of psychiatric medication. Mental health evaluation of participants was done using three previously validated self-assessment questionnaires.

Knowledge score

The mental health literacy questionnaire – short version for adults (MHLq-SVa), which consists of 16 items scored using a 1 to 5 Likert scale (ranging from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). Within the same context, the perceived devaluation and discrimination scale (PDD) was used to assess the degree of stigmatizing attitude toward people with mental disorders. It contains 12 items and each item is rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (totally agree) to 5 (totally disagree). Items 1, 2, 3, 4, 8 and 10 required reverse scoring. Total scores ranged from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating lower levels of stigma. These previously mentioned scales, the MHLq-SVa and the PDD, added together resulted in the mental health knowledge score. The higher the score the more knowledgeable is the participant regarding mental health issues [6].

Depression score

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), which scores each of the nine Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th edition (DSM-IV) criteria of depression, consists of 9 items with each item rated on a 0-to-3-point scale ranging from “0” (not at all) to “3” (nearly every day); added together is the depression score ranging from 0 to 27 and the higher the score the higher is the severity of depression. Interpretation of the PHQ-9 score is as: no/minimal depression (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27) [7].

Anxiety score

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Screening Questionnaire (GAD-7) contains 7 items was used to measure participants’ anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks. Each item is assigned a score from “0” (not at all) to “3” (nearly every day). The total score of GAD-7 ranges from 0 to 21 and is grouped into four categories as follows: no/minimal anxiety (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (15–21) [8].

Statistical analysis

Several statistical tests were used to compare groups and evaluate relationships between variables. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare the mean knowledge scores on mental health among different categorical groups such as employment changes, salary, education level, marital status. The Student’s t-test was applied to compare means between two groups, particularly for dichotomous variables such as gender and psychiatric medication use and employment. The chi-square test was used to assess the association between categories of depression scores and independent variables. Finally, Pearson’s correlation was employed to examine the relationship between knowledge, anxiety, and depression scores. The statistical significance is considered for P-values less than 0.05.

All analyses were performed using the IBM’s Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 (IBM, Inc, Chicago, IL).

Ethical considerations

All participants received a comprehensive and understandable information about the study’s purpose, procedures involved, and kind of the questions asked; after which an informed consent was obtained from each participant.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring compliance with internationally recognized ethical standards for research involving human participants.

Ethical approval was obtained from the research committee at USF, All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Confidentiality and anonymity of participants were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Results

Descriptive analysis

The mental health of participants is assessed using three tools: the mental health knowledge score, the MHQ depression score, and the GAD-7 anxiety score.

The average mental health knowledge score is 51.83, with a standard deviation of 11.22. The minimum score is 19 and the maximum score is 72. These results suggest that mental health knowledge varies considerably among participants, with some having very limited knowledge and others having quite high knowledge.

The average depression score is 10, with a standard deviation of 7.22. Scores range from 0 to 26, indicating that some participants have no symptoms of depression, while others have high levels of depression. This variation highlights the diversity of mental health experiences among participants.

The average anxiety score is 9.46, with a standard deviation of 6.06. Scores range from 0 to 21, indicating that some participants do not suffer from anxiety, while others have high levels of anxiety. The study focuses on the mental health of adults in Lebanon and examines several independent variables, including job-related changes, salary, education level, employment, age, gender, marital status, and psychiatric medication use.

Job-related changes were classified into four categories: unfavorable change, favorable change, no change, and no job. Among the participants, 17.2% reported an unfavorable job change, 5.9% experienced a favorable change, 43.8% had no change, and 33.2% were unemployed. These figures show that almost half of the participants have job stability, while one-third are unemployed, which could have implications for their mental health.

The distribution of participants according to their salary levels shows that 37.6% have a low salary, 55.8% have an average salary, and only 6.6% have a high salary. This distribution indicates that the majority of participants fall into the low to average salary categories, which may influence their psychological well-being and access to mental health resources.

Regarding education level, 40.5% of participants have a bachelor’s degree, 4.7% have an elementary education level, 10.4% have completed high school, 38.8% have a higher education level, and 2.8% are illiterate. The high proportion of participants with a higher education level suggests that education could play an important role in the perception and management of mental health.

Employment is a key factor in this study, with 85.9% of participants being employed and 14.1% being unemployed. The high employment rate could indicate some economic stability, but it is also important to examine the quality of employment and its effects on mental health.

Participants are distributed across different age groups: 21.6% are between 18 and 20 years old, 35.5% are between 21 and 30 years old, 20% are between 31 and 40 years old, 14.4% are between 41 and 50 years old, and 8.5% are between 51 and 60 years old.

In terms of gender, 35.1% of participants are men and 64.9% are women. This unequal distribution could reflect higher participation of women in mental health studies or a higher prevalence of mental health disorders among women.

Regarding marital status, 38.4% of participants are married and 61.6% are single. Differences in marital status can have implications for social support and stress management mechanisms, thus influencing mental health. Finally, 43.8% of participants take psychiatric medications, while 56.2% do not (Table 2).

Bivariate analysis

Anxiety among adults in Lebanon

The cross-tabulation tables help understand the relationships between various independent factors (job-related changes, income, education level, employment, gender, marital status, and psychiatric medication use) and anxiety levels measured by SCORE GAD-7 classes (minimal, mild, moderate, and severe anxiety).

Participants who reported unfavorable job changes show higher levels of moderate to severe anxiety. Among them, 9.2% have minimal anxiety, 21.2% have mild anxiety, 16.2% have moderate anxiety, and 20.8% have severe anxiety. This indicates that unfavorable changes are significantly associated with higher levels of anxiety (P = < 0.001). On the other hand, participants who reported favorable job changes have lower levels of anxiety, with only 1.0% having moderate anxiety and 8.3% having severe anxiety. Participants who experienced no job changes show a varied distribution of anxiety levels, with the majority having minimal or mild anxiety. Finally, unemployed participants exhibit a high prevalence of moderate to severe anxiety, suggesting that unemployment is a significant risk factor for anxiety.

Participants with low-income present high levels of moderate (52.5%) and severe (41.7%) anxiety. Those with a middle income show a more balanced distribution of anxiety levels, though 54.2% have severe anxiety. Participants with high income have relatively low anxiety levels, with only 4.2% experiencing severe anxiety (P = 0.001).

The results indicate that participants with a higher education level show lower anxiety levels. Those with a bachelor’s degree or higher have a lower prevalence of moderate to severe anxiety. Conversely, participants with a primary education level or less exhibit higher levels of moderate to severe anxiety (P = < 0.001).

Unemployed participants show high levels of moderate (28.3%) and severe (16.7%) anxiety. In contrast, employed participants exhibit lower anxiety levels, with only 6.1% having mild anxiety and 28.3% having moderate anxiety (P = 0.01).

Participants taking psychiatric medications show higher levels of moderate (34.3%) and severe (37.5%) anxiety. Those not taking medications have lower anxiety levels, though 65.7% have moderate anxiety and 62.5% have severe anxiety (P = 0.024).

The results do not show a significant difference between genders in terms of anxiety levels, although women have a slightly higher prevalence of mild and moderate anxiety compared to men (P = 0.143).

Similarly, marital status does not appear to have a significant influence on anxiety levels. Association between independent variables and anxiety score

Mental health knowledge

Table 4 presents a bivariate analysis between various socio-economic variables and the total mental health knowledge score. The independent variables include job-related changes, salary, education level, gender, marital status, psychiatric medication use, employment status, and age. The results show a significant association between job-related changes and the mental health knowledge score (p = < 0.001). Individuals who experienced unfavorable or favorable job changes have higher knowledge scores compared to those who experienced no changes or are unemployed. Salary is also significantly associated with the mental health knowledge score (p = 0.001). Individuals with high income have the lowest scores, while those with middle income have the highest scores. Education level is strongly associated with the mental health knowledge score (p = < 0.001). Individuals with higher education levels or a high school diploma have higher scores, while those with elementary education levels have the lowest scores. Employment status is significantly associated with the mental health knowledge score (p = 0.004), with employed individuals having higher scores than those who are unemployed. Marital status shows a marginal association with the mental health knowledge score (p = 0.05), with married individuals having slightly higher scores. No significant association was found between gender and the mental health knowledge score (p = 0.674). Additionally, there is no significant association between psychiatric medication use and the mental health knowledge score (p = 0.153) (Table 4).

Depression

The results of the bivariate analysis show significant associations between depression scores and several independent variables. Individuals who experienced unfavorable job changes have higher depression scores (p = 0.002), whereas those who experienced no job changes or are unemployed have moderate to severe depression scores. Income is also a determining factor, with individuals having low or middle incomes showing higher depression scores compared to those with high incomes (p = 0.001). Education level shows a strong association with depression (p = < 0.001), with individuals having elementary education or being illiterate exhibiting higher depression scores. Employment status is also relevant, with unemployed individuals having higher depression scores compared to those who are employed (p = 0.046). Lastly, age and marital status were found to be significantly associated with depression scores, with young adults (21–30 years) and middle-aged adults (31–40 years) having higher scores compared to other age groups (p = < 0.001), and single individuals having higher scores than married individuals (p = 0.011) (Table 5).

Discussion

Anxiety

The results of this bivariate analysis reveal significant associations between various socio-economic variables and anxiety levels measured by GAD7 scores. Specifically, adverse changes related to work, low income, low education level, unemployment, and the use of psychiatric medication are strongly correlated with high levels of anxiety. These observations largely align with the existing literature on mental health determinants.

Adverse changes in employment are associated with high anxiety levels. This relationship has been extensively documented in the literature. For example, a study by Virtanen et al. (2007) showed that job insecurity is strongly linked to increased levels of anxiety disorders [9]. Additionally, job loss and professional instability are major sources of stress, contributing to increased anxiety [10]. Our results corroborate these studies by showing that participants who experienced adverse changes in their employment have higher levels of moderate and severe anxiety.

Regarding income and anxiety, participants with low incomes have higher levels of moderate and severe anxiety. This association is also well-supported by the literature. Research has shown that low-income individuals are more likely to suffer from anxiety disorders due to constant financial stress and limited resources to cope with life’s challenges [11]. A study by Zimmerman et al. (2005) found that anxiety levels were significantly higher among individuals in lower socio-economic groups [12].

Education level plays a protective role against anxiety, according to our results. Participants with higher education levels exhibit lower anxiety levels, which aligns with the findings of many researchers. For example, Bjelland et al. (2008) demonstrated that individuals with higher education levels are less likely to suffer from anxiety disorders, likely due to better career opportunities and higher incomes, which reduce financial stress [13].

Unemployment is strongly associated with high levels of anxiety, as shown by our analysis. This relationship has been well-established in the literature, where numerous studies have shown that unemployment leads to deterioration in mental health, including increased levels of anxiety and depression [14]. Unemployed individuals often suffer from financial stress and loss of social status, which contribute to increased anxiety.

Although our results did not show significant differences between genders in terms of anxiety levels, the literature often indicates that women are more likely to suffer from anxiety disorders than men [15]. Regarding marital status, our results suggest that single individuals exhibit higher levels of moderate and severe anxiety compared to married individuals. This could be due to the social and emotional support that marriage can provide [10]. Finally, the use of psychiatric medications is associated with higher levels of anxiety, which may reflect the severity of mental disorders in these individuals. However, access to mental health care remains crucial for managing anxiety [16].

Knowledge on mental health

The results show that individuals who have experienced changes in their employment, whether favorable or unfavorable, have more knowledge on mental health. This may be explained by the fact that changes in work can push individuals to seek more information on mental health to better manage stress and new challenges. The literature supports that periods of transition or change can prompt individuals to seek information on mental health to better adapt [17].

Inndividuals with the highest income were found to have the least knowledge on mental health,

which may seem counterintuitive. A possible explanation is that high-income individuals may feel less concerned about mental health issues due to their financial security. In contrast, those with a middle income seem to have the highest scores, possibly because they are stable enough to access information but still concerned with daily challenges [18].

The results show that higher levels of education are associated with better mental health knowledge. This is consistent with existing studies that show education improves health literacy, allowing individuals to better understand and manage their mental health (Jorm, 2012) [19]. No significant difference was found between men and women regarding mental health knowledge scores. However, the literature indicates that women are generally more likely to be interested in mental health issues, although this does not necessarily reflect in knowledge scores [20].

Married individuals have slightly higher knowledge score on mental health. This could be due to social support and increased communication between partners, which fosters awareness of mental health issues [21].

There is no significant difference between those taking psychiatric medications and those who are not in terms of mental health knowledge. This may indicate that taking medication is not directly related to better knowledge, but rather to symptom management [22]. Similarly, no significant correlation was found between age and mental health knowledge, which may indicate that knowledge is relatively evenly distributed among different age groups in this specific population.

Employed individuals have higher scores of knowledges on mental health than those who are unemployed. Employment may provide resources and an environment conducive to learning and awareness of mental health issues [10].

Depression

The results of this study show a significant correlation between depression scores and several socio-economic variables, which is consistent with the existing literature. Regarding changes at work, the results align with those of Kim et al. (2012), who found that negative changes at work increase the risk of depression among employees [23]. Concerning income, a study by Lorant et al. (2003) shows that low-income individuals are more likely to suffer from depression, corroborating the results of this study [24]. Additionally, the study by Bjelland et al. (2008) demonstrated an inverse association between education level and depression, highlighting that individuals with lower education levels are more likely to suffer from depression [25].

The results related to employment status are also supported by previous research, particularly by Paul and Moser (2009), who showed that unemployment is strongly linked to high levels of depression [10]. Finally, the associations found between age, marital status, and depression are supported by studies such as that of Wang et al. (2010), which indicate that young adults and single individuals are more vulnerable to depression [26].

Correlation between mental health Knowledge, Depression, and anxiety

The results indicating a weak positive correlation between knowledge of depression symptoms and depression levels are interesting and deserve to be examined in light of the existing literature. A study by Jorm et al. (2006) showed that awareness and knowledge of mental disorders can increase the recognition of personal symptoms, which could explain why individuals with better knowledge of depression report higher levels of depressive symptoms [19]. Although the observed association between mental health knowledge and depression was weak, this finding may be partly explained by cognitive biases. For instance, individuals experiencing depressive symptoms may exhibit cognitive distortions such as negative filtering or hopelessness, which could diminish the perceived utility of their knowledge and limit help-seeking behavior [19]. In contrast, the lack of a significant correlation between knowledge and anxiety may be explained by different factors. For example, anxiety could be influenced by specific contextual factors or personal variables that are not directly related to symptom knowledge. This result is consistent with the findings of Schomerus et al. (2012), who found that stigma and other psychosocial factors can play a more significant role in the experience and reporting of anxiety symptoms [27].

These observations suggest that, while improving knowledge may be beneficial for recognizing and managing depression, additional interventions may be necessary to specifically address anxiety.

Limitations and strengths

Regarding the study’s limitations, the use of self-assessments to measure depression and anxiety levels may introduce reporting biases or perception errors by the participants. Another limitation concerns the weak correlation found between knowledge and depression, suggesting that other unmeasured factors could also play a significant role. Also, the use of snowball sampling via WhatsApp may have introduced a sampling bias by disproportionately recruiting younger, more technologically connected individuals, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. The sample was skewed toward individuals with higher educational attainment, particularly those holding a bachelor’s degree or above, which may have inflated overall knowledge scores and obscured associations among participants with lower education levels. Finally, several potential confounding factors, such as participants’ comorbid health conditions, access to mental health services, and geographic disparities (urban vs. rural settings), were not assessed or controlled for in the analysis, which may have influenced both mental health outcomes and knowledge levels.

Despite these limitations, the study presents several strengths. It uses a relatively large sample of 425 participants, increasing the statistical power and reliability of the results. The rigorous methodology, including the use of appropriate correlation tests, allows for robust conclusions about the relationships between variables. One of the major strengths of this study is the use of validated and internationally recognized questionnaires to measure key variables. The GAD-7, a widely used and validated scale for assessing generalized anxiety, was employed to measure anxiety levels among participants. Similarly, a standardized and validated scale was used to assess depression levels. Additionally, the knowledge questionnaire, designed to evaluate understanding of symptoms, causes, and treatments of depression and anxiety, was also validated, ensuring the reliability and validity of the collected data. The use of these tools not only ensures the quality of the measurements but also allows for comparison of the results with those of other similar studies, thereby reinforcing the credibility and robustness of the study’s conclusions.

Conclusion

The findings reported in this study show the considerable effect of the triple-folded crisis on the mental status of the Lebanese adult population. It illustrates the extent of depression and anxiety among Lebanon adults’ residents, as well as its relationship to different sociodemographic parameters. However, what is more of a concern is the level of mental health illiteracy in the adult Lebanese population and its effect on mental health care seeking. Hence the need for interventional approaches going from educational programs to a better spread and easier access to mental health care facilities to improve the mental wellbeing of adults in a socio-politically unstable country like Lebanon.
