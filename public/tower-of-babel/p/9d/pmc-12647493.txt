A Decade of Treating ALK-Rearranged Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer in a Lower Middle-Income Country: Changing Patterns and Improving Survival
Authors: Ullas Batra, Mansi Sharma, Irfan Ahmad, Senthil Rajappa, Shrinidhi Nathany, Luca Cantini, Rashi Sachdeva, Vanshika Batra, Ritwika Choudhury, Bhumi Agarwal, Abhinav Dewan, Sabeena Choudhary, B P Amrith, L M Darlong, Kundan Singh Chufal, Preetha Umesh, Kratika Bhatia, Paulo Nunes Filho, Kamal S Saini, Anurag Mehta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionData regarding the comparative efficacy of various generations of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in lower middle-income countries (LMICs) is scarce.MethodsA comprehensive assessment of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-directed TKIs and treatment sequences was done for 249 patients (145 male, 104 female) with advanced NSCLC at a referral cancer hospital in New Delhi, India. The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS); progression-free survival on 1st-line (PFS1) therapy, 2nd-line (PFS2) therapy, and PFS1 + 2. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards and joint frailty modelling were used for progression events and OS, respectively.ResultsWith a median follow-up of 48 months, median OS, PFS1, and PFS1 + 2 were 46.6 (95% CI 36.9–67.0), 18.2 (95% CI 14.5–26.8), and 33.1 months (95% CI 28.6–41.6), respectively. Third-generation TKIs significantly improved PFS and OS over 1st-generation TKIs, whether used 1st-line or later (HR PFS1 0.08, 95% CI 0.02–0.32, p < 0.001; HR OS 0.13, 95% CI 0.02–0.94, p = 0.043; HR PFS2 0.36, 95% CI 0.19–0.71, p = 0.003). Joint frailty modelling confirmed the correlation between repeated progression events and mortality (shared frailty parameter theta = 1.78, p < 0.001). Alectinib (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.33–0.84, p = 0.007) and lorlatinib (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.31–0.77, p = 0.002) reduced progression risk. Temporal trend analyses showed fewer treatment lines and significantly improved outcomes in patients with brain metastases with 3rd-generation TKIs.ConclusionsThis relatively large LMIC real-world study shows similar outcomes to Western data, and improved survival and lower risk of intracranial progression with 2nd- and 3rd-generation ALK-directed TKIs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-025-00390-y.Keywords: Lung cancer, NSCLC, Lower middle-income country, LMIC, ALK, Lorlatinib

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?ALK-directed tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have revolutionized the treatment landscape for patients with ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).There is a scarcity of data from lower middle-income countries (LMICs) about the comparative efficacy of various generations of TKIs in this patient population.Data from a single institution in New Delhi was analysed with respect to changing treatment sequences over the past decade, as well as the prognostic factors associated with patients’ outcomes and the clinical efficacy across different generations of ALK-directed TKIs.What was learned from this study?This relatively large LMIC real-world study shows similar outcomes to those observed in white populations, and improved survival and lower risk of intracranial progression with 2nd- and 3rd-generation ALK-directed TKIs.

Introduction

Advances in molecular oncology have resulted in a paradigm shift in the management of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with a tailored therapeutic approach replacing a “one size fits all” method. Rearrangements of the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene have been reported in 3–11.4% of Indian patients [1, 2]. As per GLOBOCAN 2022, there were 81,748 new cases of lung cancer in India in 2022 [3]. Thus there are many patients who may derive benefit from ALK-directed therapy in the country. Guidelines recommend using a broad-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel to identify actionable molecular alterations; however, its applicability is limited in the real world because of cost and availability, especially in resource-constrained settings, where there is lack of reimbursement and few accredited labs. As a result, the majority of patients in lower middle-income countries (LMICs) are treated on the basis of a few individual biomarkers. Determination of ALK status by immunohistochemistry (IHC) can be utilised as a stand-alone test to initiate treatment with ALK-directed TKIs [4].

ALK-directed TKIs have revolutionized the treatment landscape for this population, resulting in significantly improved outcomes compared to traditional chemotherapies [5–10]. However, several challenges remain in determining optimal treatment sequencing and identifying prognostic biomarkers for progression and survival. Many patients who present to the clinic do not meet stringent trial criteria, and data regarding comparative efficacy between the different TKIs is scarce. The inclusion of Indian patients in global clinical trials is limited, highlighting the need for more localized research. Real-world data from multicentre studies in India have shown that ALK-directed TKIs improve progression-free survival (PFS) and overall response rates compared to chemotherapy. These studies mostly looked at outcomes of 1st-generation (gen) inhibitors (e.g. crizotinib), with fewer patients receiving 2nd-gen inhibitors (e.g. alectinib, ceritinib). Similar data is available from the Western and Asian countries and real-world data on the use of 3rd-gen inhibitors (e.g. lorlatinib), especially in the 1st-line setting is scarce [1, 11–16].

This study leverages a relatively large, single-institution dataset of patients with ALK-positive (ALKpos) metastatic NSCLC (mNSCLC) to evaluate the real-world efficacy of ALK-targeted therapies. We aimed to describe the changing treatment patterns over the past decade, and the various treatment sequences used. Our objectives were to determine the prognostic factors associated with patients’ outcomes and compare the clinical efficacy across different generations of ALK-directed TKIs.

Methods

The criteria for inclusion into this analysis were (1) patients with a diagnosis of recurrent or metastatic ALKpos NSCLC, and (2) such patients should have received ALK-directed therapy in any line of treatment.

The exclusion criteria were (1) small cell lung carcinoma either alone or coexisting with NSCLC, (2) large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the lung, (3) patients with synchronous dual primary malignancies.

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of RGCIRC (Res/SCM/44/2021/15). A waiver of informed consent was granted by the institutional ethics committee. After institutional review board approval, we interrogated our institutional database (electronic medical records) for patients with mNSCLC and ALK-positive status determined by IHC using D5F3 monoclonal antibody (Ventana XT), and identified 310 such patients between August 2013 and August 2024. Sixty-one patients were excluded as they received treatment elsewhere after initial diagnostic workup at our institute. Medical records for the remaining 249 patients were reviewed, and the dataset was finalized for analysis on November 15, 2024 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and scoring were performed on tumour blocks using the SP263 Ventana assay (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). Of the 310 patients identified in our database, 203 had undergone NGS with a 38-gene hotspot panel (Ion Torrent S5 platform, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and their baseline characteristics were used to analyse association with genomic alteration profiles.

The date of diagnosis for recurrent or mNSCLC was defined as the date of the biopsy that confirmed the diagnosis of lung cancer. The date of progression was defined as the day when patients changed the TKI for any reason, such as toxicity or disease progression. Patients who were lost to follow-up or discontinued treatment for any reason were censored on the day of last clinical visit.

No missing data was identified for the analysed variables owing to the single-institution nature of this study and full access to electronic medical records.

Statistical Methods

Summary statistics for continuous and categorical variables were presented as medians with interquartile range (IQR), and frequencies with percentages, respectively. Time-to-event outcomes were analysed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and subgroups were compared using the two-sided Mantel–Cox log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression modelling was performed using the conditional backward elimination method after accounting for multicollinearity. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and all variables satisfied this assumption. To assess the temporal change in outcomes with changing treatment patterns, patients were classified into time-periods based on diagnosis date: August 2013 to December 2017; January 2018 to December 2021, and January 2022 till August 2024 (study completion). These were chosen to coincide with the availability of newer generations of ALK-directed TKIs in our region. The Kruskal–Wallis test and subsequent Dunn’s multiple comparisons test were used to compare the number of lines of treatment across these periods.

Joint Frailty Modelling

To evaluate the association between repeated progression events and death, as well as identify variables influencing both, we performed joint frailty modelling [17]. Repeated progressions were modelled using a penalized likelihood approach with splines (knots = 10) to flexibly (kappa 1 & 2 = 2 × 105) estimate the baseline hazard function, and a gamma frailty distribution to capture unobserved heterogeneity (random effects) between patients. Death was modelled simultaneously, sharing a common frailty term linking both processes. Variables used in the model were chosen on the basis of prior literature, consensus expert opinion and effect on model performance criteria. Each line of treatment was modelled as a time-varying covariate for progression, using the gap time formulation.

Parameter estimation was performed via maximization of the penalized marginal likelihood. Statistical significance of covariates was assessed using Wald tests and reported as p values; results are presented as hazard ratios with 95% CI. Penalized marginal log-likelihood and the likelihood cross-validation (LCV) criterion were used to evaluate model fit. The frailty parameters theta and alpha indicate the level of heterogeneity among patients and the degree of association between progression and death, respectively.

Sample Size

No sample size calculations were performed as we evaluated all patients with ALKpos mNSCLC.

All analyses were performed using R v4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023, Austria) with the survival and frailty pack packages [18]. Figures were created using Prism v10 (Dotmatics, USA), ggplot2 and sunburst R packages. All tests are two-sided, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

This report is prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) cohort reporting guidelines and SAGER guidelines [19, 20]. Sex was self-reported by the patients; however, sex-disaggregated data was not collected as it was beyond the scope of this study.

Disease Characteristics

Most of the patients had adenocarcinoma histology (220/249, 88%), and 80% were thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1)-positive on IHC (199/249).

PDL1 status was available for 53% (133/249) of the patients, and 36.8% (49/133) had PDL1 expression of less than 1%.

Most of the patients had extrathoracic metastases on diagnosis (203/249, 82%). Thirty-seven percent (93/249) of patients had brain metastasis at diagnosis and a further 31% (49/156) developed one during their disease course.

Genomic Profile

Molecular profiling (including ALK variant analysis) using an NGS panel was done for 66% (163/249) of the cohort. The remaining patients underwent single-gene testing. The entire NGS profile of patients with available data and selected clinical features is shown in an Oncoplot (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Treatment and Outcomes

Most patients (217/249, 87%) received an ALK-directed TKI in the 1st-line and nearly half of the patients received newer-gen TKIs (2nd-gen 32% and 3rd-gen 17%). The number of patients opting for 3rd-gen TKI increased yearly (Fig. 1). At the time of data cut-off, 34% (85/249) patients were continuing their 1st-line treatment, including 40 of the 42 patients who were started on lorlatinib. Sixty-two percent (155/249) of patients had progression on 1st-line therapy and 13.6% (34/249) of patients did not take subsequent treatment. Similarly, of the patients who progressed on 2nd-line, 3rd-line, and 4th-line treatment, 35.4% (33/93), 55.1% (27/49), and 40% (6/15) did not take further treatment respectively (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Influence of Sequence of TKIs (1st vs 2nd vs 3rd Generation) on Outcomes

Compared to 1st-gen TKIs, newer-gen TKIs (2nd-gen and 3rd-gen) significantly improved PFS1 when given as 1st-line treatment (2nd-gen and 3rd-gen vs 1st-gen: HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.25–0.55, p < 0.001; HR 0.08, 95% CI 0.02–0.33, p < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 3), while only 3rd-gen TKIs improved PFS2 when administered as 2nd-line treatment. Combined progression-free survival on the first two lines of treatment (PFS 1 + 2) was better when 2nd-gen and 3rd-gen TKIs were administered either in 1st-line or 2nd-line treatment, compared to 1st-gen TKIs.

Sequencing strategies also influenced PFS1 + 2. Compared to the sequence of 1st-gen TKI → 2nd-gen TKI, administering a 3rd-gen TKI after progression on either a 1st-gen or 2nd-gen TKI improved PFS1 + 2 (1st-gen → 3rd-gen and 2nd-gen → 3rd-gen vs 1st-gen → 2nd-gen: HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.09–0.69, p < 0.001; HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.25–0.93, p = 0.030, respectively). Administration of newer-gen TKIs significantly improved OS when given as 1st-line treatment (2nd-gen and 3rd-gen vs 1st-gen: HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.97, p = 0.038; HR 0.13, 95% CI 0.02–0.94, p = 0.043, respectively), while only 3rd-gen TKIs improved OS when administered as 2nd-line treatment (3rd-gen vs 1st-gen: HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.19–0.73, p = 0.004). Administering a 3rd-gen TKI after progression on 1st-gen TKI also improved OS (3rd-gen vs 1st-gen: HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.19–0.73, p = 0.004), even in those who received it after progressing on two prior lines of treatment (3rd-gen vs 1st-gen: HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.19–0.73, p = 0.004) (Supplemental Table 1).

The temporal effect of changing treatment practices also significantly improved OS (Fig. 1). Median OS of patients starting treatment between 2013 and 2017 was 32.3 months and increased to 51.9 months for the patients who started treatment between 2018 and 2021. Median OS was not reached for those patients who started treatment from 2022 onwards (when 3rd-gen TKI became available for 1st-line in our region). The median lines of delivered treatments significantly reduced when 3rd-gen TKIs were used [2022–2024 vs 2013–2017: 1 (1–1) vs 2 (1–3), p < 0.001].

Brain Metastases Outcomes

None of the patients who received 3rd-gen TKIs as 1st-line treatment developed brain metastases (Fig. 3). On multivariable logistic regression (Supplemental Table 2), the risk of developing brain metastases in the 2022–2024 period was the lowest (vs 2013–2017: OR 0.03, 95% CI 0.004–0.226, p = 0.001) and the only significant variable in the model.

Multivariable Analyses

The presence of extrapulmonary metastases was detrimental to all outcomes (PFS1, PFS2, and PFS1 + 2: HR 1.63, 95% CI 1.01–2.62, p = 0.046; HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.14–3.93, p = 0.018; HR 2.63, 95% CI 1.49–4.65, p < 0.001, respectively), while TTF1 positive tumours improved outcomes (PFS2, PFS1 + 2: HR 0.23, 95% CI 0.11–0.49, p < 0.001; HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.17–0.60, p < 0.001, respectively) (Supplemental Fig. 4). Older age was associated with a mildly reduced risk of PFS1 (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–0.99, p = 0.011); however, this effect was not consistent across other outcomes. There was no association between outcomes and sex of the patients.

As expected, the choice of treatment class significantly influenced outcomes. Newer-gen TKIs significantly improved PFS1 (2nd-gen and 3rd-gen: HR 0.31, 95% CI 0.20–0.48, p < 0.001; HR 0.08, 95% CI 0.02–0.32, p < 0.001, respectively), PFS2 (3rd-gen: HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.17–0.74, p = 0.005) and PFS1 + 2 (2nd-gen and 3rd-gen: HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.26–0.81, p = 0.008; HR 0.19, 95% CI 0.09–0.38, p < 0.001, respectively) compared to 1st-gen TKIs. As a result of the diverse treatment sequences from 1st-line → 2nd-line treatments, this variable could not be included in the Cox proportional hazards model.

Joint Frailty Modelling

There was a strong association between repeated progression events and mortality, as indicated by the significant shared frailty parameter (theta = 1.78, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). With time, the baseline hazard for disease progression rises steadily, whereas the hazard for death gradually increases after an initial lag (Supplemental Fig. 5). The baseline risk of progression was also heterogeneous between patients (eta = 0.01, p < 0.001), as a result of non-observed covariates.

Results of joint frailty modelling

Amongst the variables included in our model, the presence of extrapulmonary metastases significantly worsened progression risk (HR 2.67, 95% CI 1.54–4.64, p = 0.001), while its influence on mortality (HR 1.98, 95% CI 0.99–3.98, p = 0.054) was close to significance. Patients with worse ECOG performance (2–3 vs 0–1: HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.28–3.26, p = 0.003) had a higher risk of death.

Treatments received at any line were included as a time-varying covariate in the progression model and therefore they were not included in the survival model, a choice which was justified by the strong association between the two event processes. Alectinib (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.33–0.84, p = 0.007) and lorlatinib (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.31–0.77, p = 0.002) were each associated with substantially lower risk of progression compared to crizotinib. In contrast, patients who received chemotherapy in any line exhibited a higher risk of progression (HR 2.83, 95% CI 1.92–4.18, p < 0.001).

Discussion

We analysed real-world data on the clinical outcomes of 249 patients with ALKpos mNSCLC treated at a single centre in India from August 2013 till August 2024. Incidence of ALK was found to be 8% in our cohort, which is consistent with literature from the Indian subcontinent and China [1, 2, 21], and higher than what has been reported in the Western population [22, 23]. Moreover, as a result of the variable access to ALK testing and significant costs of targeted therapies, ALKpos patients are treated with a more diversified approach compared to high-income countries, allowing an evaluation of different sequences and options of treatments in a real-world context.

The median follow-up for the entire cohort was 48 months; and we noted a median OS of 46.6 months and median PFS for the 1st- and 2nd-lines of treatment (PFS1 and PFS2) of 18.2 months and 9.1 months, respectively. The combined PFS for the first two lines of treatment (PFS1 + 2) was 33.1 months. Compared to 1st-gen TKIs, 2nd- and 3rd-gen TKIs significantly improved PFS1 when given as 1st-line treatment, with HRs of 0.37 and 0.08, respectively; and demonstrated benefit in other outcomes including OS, PFS2, and PFS1 + 2, confirming their critical role in the treatment of ALKpos mNSCLC. Third-gen TKIs also improved PFS2 when administered as a 2nd-line treatment (HR 0.36). Additionally, the TKI sequence showed an important influence on survival outcomes. Administering a 3rd-gen TKI after progression on a 1st- or 2nd-gen TKI resulted in significantly improved PFS1 + 2 and OS.

Chazan et al. analysed real-world data of a cohort of 739 patients with ALKpos NSCLC treated in the USA over a decade and reported a median OS of 37 months [14]. Their patients were older, had better PS, and 65% received TKIs (1st- and 2nd-gen). Our cohort included 87% patients who received an ALK-directed TKI in the 1st-line, included 3rd-gen TKIs, and had comparable (if not better) outcomes despite double the number of patients with an ECOG PS of 2 or more. Similar to our study, no difference in outcomes was observed on the basis of sex, and this is consistent with other literature [24]. Chiari et al. also noted the effectiveness of 2nd-gen TKI after progression on 1st-gen TKI as compared to chemotherapy [25]. This comparable literature further supports and reinforces our findings.

Our study demonstrated a higher median OS compared with other retrospective studies from India [1, 11]. These studies mostly reported outcomes of chemotherapy or 1st- or 2nd-gen ALK-directed TKIs. We included patients treated more recently, more patients received ALK-directed TKIs early in their disease course, and nearly half received 2nd- or 3rd-gen ALK-directed TKIs in the 1st-line, which may account for these differences.

The median OS reported by us is lower compared with international benchmarks (Table 2). Our study included patients with ECOG PS 2–4, and worse PS was associated with inferior survival. In addition, 2nd- and 3rd-gen drugs were not available to all patients either as a result of unavailability or unaffordability. As a result of the retrospective nature of the analysis, which included patients receiving multiple lines and various sequences of ALK-directed TKIs, a direct comparison with these landmark studies may not be feasible.

We have tried to overcome this factor by performing joint frailty modelling, which incorporates all the treatment sequences and multiple progression events to link these with OS and offers a potential solution to determine the effect of a drug irrespective of the line in which it was given. Unlike competing risk models, which focus solely on time to the ‘first’ progression event in the presence of death as a competing risk, joint frailty models permit a unified approach by capturing additional information gained from multiple progression events occurring within the same patient [18]. Patients who experience multiple progressions accumulate a higher hazard of death, as shown by the substantial correlation captured by the joint frailty model. Amongst 2nd-gen TKIs, alectinib performed better in reducing progression at any line and 3rd-gen TKIs also reduced progression risk when given at any line. This finding is consistent with the known resistance mechanisms that limit the efficacy of earlier-gen ALK-directed TKIs [26]. This model’s findings, combined with multivariable analyses of PFS1, PFS2, and PFS1 + 2, conclusively demonstrate that administering effective targeted therapies early delays progression and improves OS. A substantial number of patients in our study did not receive subsequent lines of treatment and these facts further support the hypothesis of using the most active agent upfront.

Notably, the temporal effect of changing treatment practices translated into seemingly improved OS in our study. The addition of later-gen TKIs to our armamentarium not only prolonged survival but also reduced the incidence of brain metastases, underscoring their efficacy in controlling systemic and intracranial disease [27]. Approximately 68% of our study population developed brain metastases at some point in their treatment journey, and patients who received 1st-line 2nd- or 3rd-gen TKIs had a lower incidence of such metastases. The absence of brain metastases among patients treated with 3rd-gen TKIs as 1st-line therapy is particularly notable, suggesting a potential preventive effect similar to what was seen in the CROWN study [10], and reiterates the importance of screening and surveillance for brain metastasis, and of starting drugs that cross the blood–brain barrier early in the course of the disease.

Additional findings in our study indicate that patients with adenocarcinomas and TTF1-positive tumours exhibited improved OS and PFS, consistent with prior studies [28], while the presence of extrapulmonary metastases was associated with worse outcomes [29]. Of note, high PD-L1 expression (≥ 50%) was associated with worse OS (HR 2.44). This trend was also seen in other studies of ALKpos NSCLC [30, 31]. However, literature is conflicting [32], and the interplay of various factors, including the immune system and tumour microenvironment, may also account for these differences [33, 34]. This finding warrants further investigation as NGS and molecular classification did not reveal significant associations between specific genomic alterations (v1 s v3) and outcomes, and these findings are consistent with previous reports from our group [35]. Further data with larger patient numbers is needed to confirm the prognostic effect of ALK variants in the Indian population. We emphasize the heterogeneity of progression and survival in patients with ALKpos mNSCLC [36, 37]. We used IHC for the diagnosis of ALKpos NSCLC, similar to the approach taken in the CROWN trial [10]. IHC for ALK is easily available, rapid, and interpretable even in non-academic centres. This rapid turnaround time of a few days was reflected in the short time of diagnosis to start of 1st-line treatment of 14 days in our patients.

The study’s strengths include its real-world setting, comprehensive follow-up, and robust statistical analyses, including joint frailty modelling. A more elaborate multistate model with irreversible discrete choice was also considered prior to commencement of statistical analysis, but this approach must be supported by adequate patient numbers and events. Conceptually, multistate models are attractive as they allow different predictors for each progression event but require relaxing the assumption of independence from past events. Joint frailty modelling addresses this by accounting for unobserved individual-level heterogeneity influencing future events, not just the current state, making this particularly suited for oncology data [38]. To the best of our knowledge, this statistical analysis is the first to be done in this setting of NSCLC.

The single-centre component, although a limitation, ensured high quality of data especially about treatment sequencing and lines of therapy, as well as homogeneity of genomic and biomarker data. The retrospective design, small patient numbers, and lack of comprehensive genomic or molecular data for all patients may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Our data highlights an important change in the management of ALK-rearranged NSCLC in India. With the use of sequential TKIs and increased use of later-generation drugs, survival of this subgroup has improved. Given the large number of patients who will be ALK positive in a populous country like India, the need of the hour is to make these drugs universally accessible. In a recent publication by Kapoor et al., nearly 75% of patients received 1st-line crizotinib with support from nongovernmental organizations [11], and this study, as well as our data, reflects the diversity of socio-economic profile between the Indian population, and given the chance of equity, a potential for similar outcomes to those of more developed countries.

Conclusion

This real-world data analysis of treatment patterns of ALKpos mNSCLC over 10 years revealed a median OS of almost 4 years in our cohort. Our findings underscore the significant clinical benefits of newer-generation (2nd- and 3rd-gen) ALK-directed TKIs and support their use as the preferred 1st-line treatment option, even in LMICs. We emphasize the importance of molecular testing before the initiation of systemic chemotherapy and the use of appropriate sequencing strategies with the goal of improving patient survival.
