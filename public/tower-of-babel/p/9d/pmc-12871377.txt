Experience-mediated transcriptional memory correlates with hypoxia resistance in the nervous system of the sea hare Aplysia californica
Authors: Javier A. Rodriguez-Casariego, Phillip Gillette, Michael Schmale, Mark W. Miller, Lynne A. Fieber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current therapeutics for hypoxic/ischemic brain damage can benefit from insights resulting from the study of hypoxia/anoxia-resistant organisms. Hypoxia resistance, however, is not a common feature in mammalian models. Being naturally exposed to hypoxic/anoxic conditions, the sea hare Aplysia californica could become a very useful model for the study of hypoxia resistance. Here, we experimentally exposed two cohorts of A. californica, resulting from crosses of adults with different environmental exposure histories, to daily 6 h pulses of hypoxic water conditions (<1.8 mgO2/mL) for six consecutive days. The transcriptional response to hypoxia was evaluated in the abdominal and pleural/pedal ganglia through the exposure, during rapid reoxygenation, and after >12 h of recovery. Resistance to hypoxia was observed in the offspring of wild animals, with no significant changes in growth and reflex performance, compared with unexposed controls of the same cohort. Impairments were observed however in the offspring of lab-reared individuals. Transcriptional response to hypoxia was larger in the abdominal ganglia compared with the pleural/pedal for both cohorts, and significant differences between cohorts were observed for both ganglia. Overall, wild-cross animals displayed a significant reduction in the expression of metabolic genes, and an increased expression of genes involved in stress-response and immune system functions compared with the lab-cross cohort, both under control conditions and during hypoxia exposures. The resistant group displayed similar gene-level regulation as that described to be involved in hypoxia/ischemia preconditioning (HPC/IPC) in mammalian models, including the frontloading of HIF1-a orthologs and other neuroprotective genes like VEGF and HSP70.

INTRODUCTION

Cellular effects of low oxygen levels (hypoxia) are primarily related with the impairment of the oxidative metabolism, causing reduced ATP production, calcium disequilibrium, and exacerbated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in cell death and/or damage. In neurons, with particularly high energetic demands and metabolism, hypoxia can not only have catastrophic effects that can lead to cell damage and death but also increase the risk of developing neurodegenerative disorders (1–3) and aggravate pathologies of preexisting neurodegenerative diseases (4). Beyond the direct effects of hypoxic exposure, mutations in hypoxia-responsive elements play a central role in the development of Parkinson’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (5–7), highlighting the role of hypoxia response/resistance mechanisms in neurodegenerative diseases. This makes our understanding of neurological responses to hypoxia and the cellular mechanisms involved in hypoxia resistance, key to enhance current therapeutic approaches for the prevention and treatment of hypoxic/ischemic damage and the onset/progression of neurodegenerative diseases.

Hypoxia-tolerant species are key to understanding hypoxia resistance/recovery mechanisms and to identify new potential therapeutic targets for hypoxia-related disorders (8). The mollusk Aplysia californica constitutes an ideal model for investigating transcriptomic and neurophysiological adaptations involved in the mitigation of hypoxic damage in the nervous system and has been recently proposed as a suitable model for the study of neurodegenerative diseases (9, 10). Being naturally exposed to hypoxic and/or anoxic conditions imposed by the littoral/sublittoral region they inhabit, Aplysia resistance to hypoxia has been documented, including many adaptive physiological traits like reduced rates of oxygen uptake, potential reliance on anaerobic metabolism during prolonged hypoxia, and overall metabolic and cardiorespiratory depression (11–14). However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in the responses and resistance to hypoxia are mostly unknown in Aplysia, particularly when it refers to neurons and other cells in the nervous system where only in vitro electrophysiological responses have been assessed (15–18). Given the simplicity of their nervous system and the extensive body of information about their circuitry, studying hypoxia responses in Aplysia could allow the integration of molecular, cellular, and neurobehavioral responses. Here, we aimed to characterize the neurobehavioral and transcriptional response of Aplysia to hypoxia exposure and identify mechanisms involved in the acquisition of hypoxia resistance. Using two cohorts with different environmental exposure history, we aimed to test the hypothesis that hypoxia resistance is mediated by repetitive environmental exposure (including hypoxic events), modifying transcriptional programs and phenotypic responses across generations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals, Experimental Design, Exposure, and Sample Collection

Two sibling cohorts (from a single egg mass) of the sea hare A. californica were selected from the National Aplysia Resource Facility at the University of Miami, FL. Both families (batch B60-22 and batch B71-22 from 2022) were between 5½–6 mo of age at the beginning of the exposure. Batch B71–22 had wild-collected parents (collected near Long Beach, CA by Marinus, Inc), likely exposed to hypoxia and other challenging dynamic conditions of the coastal environment (i.e., temperature, turbidity, pH, salinity) during their life (19), considered to be offspring of pre-exposed parents (wild-cross). Batch B60–22 resulted from laboratory-grown parents, reared at the National Aplysia Resource, therefore maintained in normoxia and other controlled conditions (i.e., diet, salinity, temperature), constituting the naïve to environmental exposure group (laboratory-cross).

Two parallel, flow-through systems were built, each consisting of a 200-gal covered reservoir for temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) control, automatically fed with the facility’s running sea water (~15°C and normoxic), and a trough with capacity for six plexiglass-perforated cages. Temperature was maintained around 20°C in the exposure systems using water heaters and heat exchange cassettes incorporated in the line. Water flow was set at 6 L/min divided into the six experimental cages (1 L/min per cage) by a manifold. One system was maintained normoxic (control, >95% DO, Supplemental Fig. S1), while the other was manipulated to reach hypoxic conditions by bubbling medical grade N2 gas into the reservoir. DO levels were actively controlled with an OXY-REG system (Loligo) and a solenoid valve. DO and temperature was secondarily recorded throughout the experiments using U26 Hobo Loggers (Onset).

Exposure consisting of daily pulses of 6-h hypoxia at <20% DO (<1.8 mgO2/mL) was followed by a progressive return to normoxia (Supplemental Fig. S1). Animals were fed ad libitum the algae Agardhiella subulata (Strain A1) following the same schedule as the general culturing of the facility (four feeding events per week) but after the hypoxia pulse was completed. A total of n = 48 animals (n = 24 per family) exposed to either control (n = 6) or hypoxic conditions (n = 18 per family) were collected during the experiment (Supplemental Fig. S1B). Animals (n = 3 per family) were collected after 2 h and 6 h of exposure in the first day, and immediately after 9 min of rapid reoxygenation achieved by transferring to normoxic water. Additional animals (n = 3 per family) were collected after 5 days and 6 days (anesthetized and dissected under hypoxia), and after 18-h normoxic recovery following the 6th-day hypoxic pulse. Control animals (n = 6 per family) were collected at the end of the experiment. Animals were anesthetized through injection of isotonic MgCl2 (~20–40% of body mass) and euthanized by complete removal of the nervous system. Abdominal and pleural/pedal ganglia were dissected, homogenized in ZR BashingBead Lysis Tubes containing 0.7-mL dry volume 2.0 mm ZR BashingBead lysis matrix and 1-mL DNA/RNA Shield buffer (Zymo Research). The resulting homogenate was stored at −20°C for nucleic acid extraction.

Growth and Behavior

Control and exposed animals from both families were weighed daily before starting exposure to assess growth and during the experimental set up at day 1 (no exposure performed that day). Righting reflex (TTR) and tail withdrawal reflex (TWR) were assessed (following Kempsell and Fieber, 20) daily before each exposure event (day 1 data were not collected as there was no exposure planned for that day, day 2, before first exposure, constitutes the baseline). Briefly, TTR consists of the time it takes an animal to latch and return to an upright position after being dropped in the water column. In the case of TWR, after a stimulus is applied to the tail (point depression), both the length of the contraction (amplitude) and the time until the tail is relaxed again are recorded. Stimulus was applied by pressing a blunted 18-gauge needle against the tail, depressing around 50%, and end of the reflex was ascertained when relaxation of the tail reached ~30% of initial tail length. All measurements were performed in triplicate by a blinded-to-condition investigator. Visual monitoring was maintained during the exposure, recording variables like incidence of mortality, occurrence of respiratory pumping seizures (21, 22), and other visible signs of stress.

RNA Isolation, Library Preparation, Sequencing, and Transcript Quantification

RNA from the abdominal and pleural/pedal ganglia was extracted using the Quick-DNA/RNA Microprep Plus kit (Zymo Research) following manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was assessed through spectrophotometric analysis (NanoVue GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), and quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Purified RNA (n = 84) was normalized to 750 ng in 30 μL and shipped to the Genomic Sequencing and Analysis Facility, University of Texas at Austin, for tag-based RNA-seq (tagSeq) library preparation and sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (23). RNA quality was additionally assessed using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) before library preparation. Quality trimming, mapping, and gene counts were performed following standard pipelines. Briefly, after quality assessment with FastQC (24), raw reads were quality trimmed with BBDUK (25), mapped to the A. californica genome (NCBI RefSeq assembly GCF_000002075.1) (26) with STAR (27) and quantified into gene count matrices. To ensure proper mapping of 3′ Tag-Seq reads, the genome GFF file was modified to extend the untranslated regions of each gene 1,000 bp in each direction. Specific pipeline details can be found in the manuscript repository (see DATA AVAILABILITY).

Viral Load Quantification

Aplysia Abyssovirus (AAbV) has been shown to infect both wild and hatchery-raised Aplysia, including those in the National Aplysia Resource facility (28). Given the unknown effect of viral load on the physiology of Aplysia and its potential interactions with the experiment, virus copies were quantified bioinformatically from Tag-Seq reads and confirmed through qPCR using the abdominal ganglion. For the bioinformatic quantification of viral load, we followed pipelines developed specifically for AAbV (29). Briefly, trimmed reads were mapped to the AAbV genome (30) using STAR (27), and the output was quantified for viral read number (see DATA AVAILABILITY for access to detailed pipelines). qPCR quantification was performed with the SYBRGreen qPCR kit with cDNA equivalent to 10 ng starting RNA on a Quantstudio 3 Real Time PCR system (ThermoFisher Scientific) using primers and conditions as described in Ref. 29.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were completed in R (v4.4.3; R Core Team 2025) with RStudio (v2025.05.0 + 496; R Studio Team 2025) unless stated otherwise. Analyses’ code is available on a GitHub repository and a Zenodo dataset (see DATA AVAILABILITY).

Growth and Behavioral Data Analysis

Hypoxia effect in growth across cohorts was compared using estimated marginal means using the emmeans R package with FDR-adjusted P values, following a significant interaction in a two-way ANOVA with formula growth_rate ~ treatment × cohort. To test whether hypoxia differentially affected behavioral responses depending on parental exposure history, we modeled TTR, TWR amplitude, and TWR duration using a Bayesian approach implemented in the R package brms using a three-way interaction and considering repetitive measures [~treatment × cohort × day + (1 | ID)]. To accommodate non-normal residual distributions and potential outliers in the behavioral data, we specified a Student’s t distribution (31) for the likelihood, with a log link. We used default weakly informative priors for fixed and random effects and ran the model using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) via Stan (all implemented in the brms function). Sampling was performed using four parallel chains, each with 10,000 iterations (including warm-up), for a total of 40,000 posterior draws. Model convergence and fit were confirmed through R-hat values (<1.01), by inspecting trace plots, and by using posterior predictive checks. Posterior means and 95% credible intervals were used to assess significance, with key interactions interpreted using hypothesis and marginal trends estimated via emmeans.

Differential Gene Expression Analysis

Raw count data were filtered to exclude genes with a mean count <1 across all samples and normalized using the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) implemented in DESeq2 (32). Global expression profiles were visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Manhattan distances.

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 v1.38.3 with a fully factorial model (~ treatment × cohort × tissue), allowing for the assessment of condition-specific gene regulation while accounting for baseline differences between cohorts (Lab-cross vs. Wild-cross) and ganglia (Abdominal vs. Pleural/Pedal). For each treatment versus control contrast, log2-fold changes (LFCs) were estimated and shrunk using the apeglm method (33), improving effect size accuracy for downstream comparisons. Genes with FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Pairwise correlations of log2 fold changes across conditions were computed to assess the similarity of transcriptional responses. Shrunken log2 fold changes for significantly differentially expressed genes (FDR ≤ 0.05) were extracted from DESeq2 results, and a gene-by-condition matrix was assembled. Conditions were defined by combinations of treatment, tissue, and cohort. Pearson correlations were calculated using complete cases, and the resulting correlation matrix was visualized as a clustered heatmap. DE analyses included pairwise contrasts of hypoxia treatments versus control across ganglia and cohorts, as well as comparisons between wild-cross and laboratory-cross animals within each condition. Family-specific responses were further investigated by subsetting the general model dataset by treatment and tissue and refitting a simplified model (~ cohort) to directly estimate Wild versus Lab contrasts.

To compare transcriptional responses across biological factors (ganglia and cohort), genes were classified by the presence or absence of statistically significant interaction effects between factors (as extracted from the full model). For comparisons across time points—specifically short-term (2–6 h) versus long-term (5–6 days) hypoxia exposures—where each time point was modeled independently, interaction terms were not available. Instead, shrunken LFCs and associated standard errors (LFC-SEs) from the individual contrasts were aggregated within each time group using a precision-weighted average, and the statistical significance of the difference in effect sizes between time points (ΔLFC) was tested using a Z-score: Z=ΔLFClfcSEshort2+lfcSElong2

P values were derived from the standard normal distribution and corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Clustering of Differential Expression Profiles

DEGs for all hypoxia exposure versus control contrasts were clustered according to their log2-transformed normalized expression profiles across treatments in a temporal line (Normoxia, 2 h, 6 h, reoxygenation, 5 days, 6 days, recovery) using DIANA clustering to identify coexpressed transcripts across treatments (time points) using the degPatterns R function from the DEGreport R package (34). A minimum group difference (maxLog2Fold) of 0.58 and a minimum cluster size of 20 were used for clustering.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) canonical pathways, and modules (MKEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (35). A hyper-geometric test (adjusted P value ≤ 0.05) was used to identify enriched terms for each gene group using the enrichGO, enrichKEGG, and enrichMKEGG functions. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to explore differential functional regulation between families. Genes were ranked by shrunken log2 fold change for each treatment-tissue combination and used as input for gseGO, gseKEGG, and gseMKEGG functions with 100,000 permutations and an adjusted P value ≤ 0.05. To aid in interpretation, GO enrichment was reduced using GO hierarchy and semantic similarity analyses. Enriched KEGG pathways were visualized using the pathview R package (36). Functional annotation and orthology to human models for A. californica were obtained from Refs. 37 and 38.

RESULTS

Organismal Responses to Hypoxia Exposure, Growth, and Behavior

No baseline differences were observed between controls in the reflex’s performance for TTR and TWR amplitude (PERMANOVA, P > 0.05) across cohorts. However, the duration of the TWR showed larger variability in the laboratory-cross with slightly larger duration (PERMANOVA, F = 3.8346, P = 0.001). Marked differences in the response to hypoxia were observed between laboratory- and wild-cross sea hare families. Although wild-cross Aplysia showed no significant phenotypic effects after 5 days of repeated 6-h hypoxia pulses (days 3–7), laboratory-cross animals showed sensitivity with reduced daily growth rate over the 5-day exposure period (Fig. 1A), and impaired tail withdrawal reflex, mainly across days 5–7 of the experiment (Fig. 1B) when compared with unexposed controls. In all cases, hypoxia effect differences between cohorts over time were tested on interaction terms resulting from ANOVA (for growth) or Bayesian models (for reflexes), suggesting a weaker hypoxia effect in wild-cross animals when compared with laboratory-cross (see Supplemental Table S1 for model results).

Viral Load Contribution to Phenotypic Responses

Both 3′Tag RNA sequencing data analysis and qPCR showed that laboratory-cross animals contained significantly less AAbV virus than wild-cross specimens (Supplemental Fig. S2). Viral load, however, was overestimated by the sequencing analysis method compared with the qPCR in the low load group (Supplemental Fig. S2). No treatment effects over viral loads were observed with either method (Wilcoxon, P = 0.7). As no clear deleterious effects have been reported to be caused by viral infection in juvenile Aplysia (28, 29), it is unlikely that the virus levels are causing the observed resistance to hypoxia. However, its contribution to the transcriptional response cannot be isolated from our data and will be considered in the interpretation of the results.

Transcriptional Responses to Hypoxia in Abdominal and Pleural/Pedal Ganglia

3′-Tag RNA sequencing (Tag-Seq) produced between 2 and 6 million reads per sample that mapped to the A. californica genome after trimming and quality filtering, covering 20,558 genes and showing no group-specific sequencing bias (Supplemental Fig. S3). We analyzed the transcriptional response to different hypoxia treatments (2 h, 6 h, 5 days, 6 days, 9 min reoxygenation, and > 12 h recovery) of cells in the abdominal and pleural/pedal ganglia for two cohorts of juveniles derived from parents with different exposure histories (see Supplemental Table S2 for number of DEGs). A correlation analysis of differential expression versus control for each treatment was used to summarize responses to hypoxia (Fig. 2A). We observed a tight clustering of samples by cohort and by ganglia within cohort (Fig. 2A), indicating clear and consistent responses to hypoxia treatments that differed between laboratory- and wild-cross animals and between ganglia. Perhaps surprisingly, the correlations between abdominal and pleural-pedal ganglia were less than between cohorts. There was a stronger correlation between cohorts in the abdominal ganglion than in the pleural pedal (Fig. 2A). Substantially, larger differential expression (number of DEGs) in all treatment-versus-control contrasts was observed in the abdominal ganglia compared with the pleural pedal (Supplemental Table S2). This difference in the ganglia-specific response to hypoxia was confirmed by analyzing the ganglia/treatment interaction terms. This analysis resulted in the identification of DEGs specific for each ganglion, and those shared across ganglia (no significant interaction) for both cohorts (Fig. 2B). Across all contrasts, a total of 1,409 genes were similarly differentially regulated (not significant differences in their effect sizes) in response to hypoxia in both ganglia and cohorts, while 2,192 and 32 DEGs were specific for abdominal and pleural/pedal ganglia, respectively (significant interaction, Fig. 2C). Upregulation of biosynthetic processes, transport, protein maturation and folding, and chromosome organization was observed in the universal response to hypoxia across ganglia and cohorts (no significant interaction across levels) when all treatments were collapsed (Fig. 2D, see Supplemental Fig. S4 for contrast-specific enrichments). Although the abdominal-specific DEGs were similarly enriched in protein synthesis and maturation processes, downregulated DEGs in the abdominal ganglion were enriched in functions like ionic transport, amino acid catabolism, cell cycle, and cell differentiation. Only the biological process of cytoskeleton organization was significantly enriched in pleural/pedal-specific downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2D).

In terms of the exposure-time-specific response, there was a consistent clustering across ganglia and cohorts separating early exposure (2 and 6 h), recovery and reoxygenation from late exposure (5 and 6 days) (Fig. 2A). This was more evident in the pleural/pedal ganglion where 126 out of 1,804 DEGs were differentially regulated (significant ΔLog2Fold, Padj. < 0.05) between early and late responses, while only 44 out of 13,120 DEGs were different in the abdominal ganglion (Fig. 3A, Supplemental Table S3). Direct average fold change comparisons of the specific genes differentially regulated in response to early or late hypoxia across ganglia and family (Fig. 3B) showed that not only are different gene repertoires involved in the exposure-time-specific response but also divergent regulation occurs on many shared DEGs in a ganglia- and cohort-specific way. Only three genes NPR2 (natriuretic peptide receptor 2), TUBA3D (Tubulin alpha 3 D), and CDYL2 (Chromodomain Y-like 2) were similarly differentially regulated between early and late responses for all cohorts and ganglia. TUBA3D and CDYL2 were repressed early, then neutral or mildly induced late in both cohorts and ganglia. NPR2 shows the opposite, with a strong early induction followed by late repression (Supplemental Table S3). Particularly for the laboratory-cross cohort, during the early phase a strong upregulation of translation control (EEF2K, UBE2N, CCT8), DNA replication/chromatin factors (RRM1, CENPE, CHAF1A), RNA-binding regulators (RBM4, KHDRBS2), and cytoskeleton modulators (MAPRE3, ACTR1A, RAB11B), largely resolved toward baseline during prolonged exposure for both ganglia, with a late-response activation of synaptic and extracellular matrix modulators (NRXN1, CCN3, PLOD1), transcriptional regulators (ZNF384, GPR183), and transport-related genes (SLC25A14, ITGA8) more pronounced in the pleural/pedal ganglia. In contrast, wild-cross animals exhibited only modest early changes for most genes in the abdominal ganglion, but launched a pronounced late-phase protective program, strongly inducing antioxidant/quality-control genes (PRDX6, SOD1, CSTB), ECM/remodeling factors (COL26A1, BMP1, FABP12), and metabolic modulators (CYP3A5, AOC1).

Clustering analysis of the differentially expressed genes (supervised approach) for each tissue and cohort across the exposure timeline (Fig. 3C) also supported the observed differences between the ganglia. In the wild-cross, 13 clusters of coexpressed genes were observed in the abdominal ganglion (A1-A13; 9 upregulated and 4 downregulated by hypoxia exposure), while only five were obtained for the pleural/pedal (P1-P5, 4 upregulated and 1 downregulated by hypoxia exposure). For laboratory-cross animals, 12 (6 upregulated and 6 downregulated) and five clusters (3 upregulated and 2 downregulated during early hypoxia) were observed for abdominal and pleural/pedal, respectively. For both cohorts, most abdominal clusters show consistent responses to short-and long-term hypoxia, with inflexions of the expression levels during rapid reoxygenation, and a return to near control expression levels after recovery. Pleural/pedal clusters, however, show clear differential responses to early and sustained hypoxia in coexpressed genes. Functionally, upregulated clusters in the abdominal ganglion showed enrichment of GO terms related to catabolism, Ox Phos metabolism, and regulation of protein and RNA processing for both cohorts, although the specific terms vary between cohorts (Fig. 3D). Genes in downregulated clusters, on the other hand, were mostly involved in ionic transport and cellular signaling pathways. Very similar in terms of functional enrichment, the pleural/pedal clusters upregulated during early exposure (2 and 6 h) contained genes involved in RNA metabolism, catabolism, and energetic metabolism, including Ox Phos for both cohorts. However, in the laboratory-cross, a cluster moderately downregulated during sustained hypoxia was enriched with aerobic metabolism terms too (Fig. 3D).

Divergent Transcriptional Responses to Hypoxia between Lab- and Wild-Cross Cohorts

Although in general terms the response to hypoxia in the offspring of lab and wild parents had many genes regulated in a similar fashion, substantial specific differences that could explain the differential sensitivity observed between the two families were found in addition to the cohort-specific response to early-late hypoxia described above. A more detailed direct comparison of the specific genes differentially regulated in response to hypoxia, showing significant interaction between families across ganglia and contrast (Fig. 4, A and B), showed a family-specific response characterized by larger log-fold changes (LFCs) for DEGs across contrasts in the wild-cross cohort compared with the laboratory-cross (Fig. 4A, negative ΔLFC). This was more evident in the abdominal ganglion, although the smaller number of DEGs in response to hypoxia of the pleural/pedal makes the proportional response more similar. Divergent regulation occurs on many unique and shared DEGs across the groups (Fig. 4B, Supplemental Table S4), accounting for different functional modules being divergently regulated through the exposures (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Table S5).

In more detail, a core group of genes—TREX2, SOCS2, TRIM3, UBC, RPA1, KLHL6, MAP3K9, and EEF1AKMT2—were profoundly repressed in the abdominal ganglion of laboratory-cross animals at 2 h of exposure (LFC ~ −5.8 to −8.5) but showed minimal change in wild-cross (LFC ~ −0.7 to +0.2) (Fig. 4B). This pattern was somehow maintained across 6 h, 5 days and 6 days of exposure. SLC6A5 (glycine transport) and TPPP2 (tubulin polymerization) were upregulated in both families, but to a much greater extent in wild-cross animals (LFC = +1.39, +1.49) at 2 h. GPT2 (amino acid metabolism) was modestly induced in laboratory-cross (LFC = +0.23) but downregulated in wild-cross (LFC = −1.37), and SLC46A1 (folate transport) was mildly repressed in laboratory-cross (LFC = −0.16) versus strongly repressed in wild-cross (LFC = −2.86). Notably, NLGN4X (synaptic plasticity) flipped direction entirely (LFC = −2.04 in laboratory-cross; LFC = +3.73 in wild-cross). By 6 h, synaptic adhesion and extracellular matrix-related genes (NLGN4X, COL6A6, COL12A1, FBN2, and ADAMTS18) and apoptotic regulators (BIRC7, MEGF6) remained repressed in laboratory-cross (LFC ~ −1.1 to −2.8) but were robustly induced in wild-cross (LFC ~ +1.6 to +3.5). SLC17A6 (vesicular glutamate transporter) was significantly more downregulated in laboratory-cross animals (LFCnaïve = −5.90; LFCpre-exposed = −1.77), and ACHE (acetylcholinesterase) was repressed in laboratory-cross (−1.65) yet induced in wild-cross animals (+2.81). After 5 days of repetitive hypoxia, epigenetic and trafficking regulators (PAF1, ELP5, MBD2, IFT88, CDC37, COPB1, VWA8, and PDPR) were modestly repressed in laboratory-cross (LFC ~ −0.1 to −1.45) but upregulated in wild-cross animals (LFC ~ +0.45 to +1.83). SCD (stearoyl-CoA desaturase) was induced in laboratory-cross and to a lesser extent in wild-cross (LFCnaïve = +1.16; LFCpre-exposed = +0.42). A subset—RHBDL2 (serine protease), PCIF1 (mRNA methyltransferase), and PGPEP1 (pyroglutamyl-peptidase)—showed slight induction in laboratory-cross animals (LFC ~ +0.15 to +0.49) but pronounced repression in wild-cross (LFC ~ −2.10 to −2.60). By day 6, wild-cross animals displayed strong induction of extracellular-matrix/adaptor genes (CSTB +7.03; C1QTNF4 + 5.23; NLGN4X +4.39; ADRA1A +3.87), whereas laboratory-cross animals exhibited only minimal change or moderate repression (LFC ~ −1.66 to +0.24).

During reoxygenation, cells in the abdominal ganglion of laboratory-cross animals remained repressing “core” response genes (TREX2 −8.18; UBC −7.16; TRIM3 −8.34; RPA1 −5.50; SOCS2 −6.57; KLHL6 −4.92; IRF5 −6.11), whereas wild-cross animals mounted a modest activation of these core factors (LFC~ +0.19 to +0.62) and induced repair and metabolic genes (PARP14 +0.72; SLC6A5 +0.50; ADSL +0.66; DNAJC15 +0.72). Twelve hours after hypoxia, laboratory-cross animals maintained a broad repression of “core” genes and added some regulatory genes—UBC (−6.60), TREX2 (−6.01), IRF8 (−2.83), MAP3K9 (−4.74), DDX58 (−4.65), and KLHL6 (−5.77)—whereas wild-cross specimens reinduced these factors (LFC ~ +0.21 to +2.14). Additional genes—NEDD8-MDP1 (protein neddylation and dephosphorylation), NLGN4X, TSPAN33 (immune system activation), OLAH (fatty acid metabolism), ZNF598 (E3 ubiquitin ligase), DUSP3 (dual-specificity phosphatase), and METTL24 (methyltransferase)—followed the same laboratory-down/wild-up pattern.

Unlike the abdominal ganglion—where laboratory-cross animals exhibited dramatic repression of “core” repair/inflammation genes and wild-cross animals showed robust induction—the pleural-pedal ganglion displayed more moderate differences in fold changes with higher concordance in the regulation direction between families (Supplemental Table S4). Cohort-specific divergences during early and late hypoxia in pleural-pedal were restricted to a handful of metabolic and proteostasis genes (OLAH, ACSM5, DNPEP, UBLCP1, and PPA1). The most divergent response was observed during reoxygenation (Fig. 4B), with proteostasis and chromatin factors being largely activated, with a stronger induction in the laboratory-cross [FASLG (+1.13), FNTA (+0.51), WDR5 (+0.52), OVOL1 (+1.19)]. Key divergences (LFC laboratory vs. LFC wild) during this phase included UBLCP1 (+1.18 vs. −0.74), RILPL1 (+0.62 vs. −0.08), ACSM5 (+0.31 vs. −1.57), BNC2 (+0.09 vs. −1.05), and SLC39A2 (+0.10 vs. −0.99).

Direct laboratory- versus wild-cross contrasts for each treatment are summarized in Fig. 5. Most laboratory versus wild DEGs were shared across all hypoxia treatments, or were consistent across all treatments, including control (Fig. 5A). Most of these genes were underexpressed in laboratory-cross animals and enriched in GO terms related with immune and stress responses and DNA metabolism (Fig. 5B). Gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) looking at differential expression of complete pathways between laboratory and wild-cross cohorts across all treatments (Fig. 5, C and D: KEGG/MKEGG; and S5: GO) indicate that laboratory-cross animals constitutively had a higher expression of genes involved primarily in oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial functioning, as well as other metabolic pathways, and underexpress signaling and immune response related genes either across all treatments or only during hypoxia compared with wild-cross animals. Significant enrichment of KEGG terms associated with several neurodegenerative processes, like Alzheimer’s disease (hsa05010), Huntington disease (hsa05016), and Parkinson’s disease (hsa05012), showed overexpression in laboratory-cross animals (Fig. 5C). KEGG modules with significantly different regulation between families (Fig. 5D) were almost exclusively related to metabolism, with Ox Phosmetabolism-related terms F-type ATPase (M00157) and citrate cycle (M00009) being the most represented modules across treatments with the larger term sizes. The expression of genes in all metabolism-linked modules was significantly elevated in laboratory-cross animals. Only N-glycan precursor biosynthesis was underexpressed in the group resulting from laboratory-reared parents. For the oxidative phosphorylation (hsa00190) pathway, overexpression in laboratory-cross animals was observed across the pathway components for many treatment time points, including controls (baseline overexpression) (Fig. 6), being very consistent across F-type ATPases and electron transfer complex components. Only cytochrome C oxidases (COX10, CyoE, and COX15) and one V-type ATPase were underexpressed in laboratory-cross compared with wild-cross animals during acute hypoxia (2 h and 6 h) and after recovery.

Expression Patterns of Previously Described Hypoxia-Preconditioning-Related Genes

In addition to the observed metabolic and immune response, the Aplysia CNS showed stark differential expression of hypoxia-related genes in the two animal groups, including genes involved in the HIF-1 pathway and neuroprotection, genes participating in metabolic shifts, genes involved in the response to oxidative damage, and genes related with inflammation and immune response (Fig. 7 and Supplemental Fig. S6). Consistently higher expression of HIF1 pathway genes was observed in both ganglia of wild-cross animals (Fig. 7 and Supplemental Fig. S6), with constitutively higher expression in the HIF1b ortholog gene under control conditions (frontloading), strong induction of HIF1a (ortho 1) during repetitive hypoxia, and downregulation of the HIF-1a inhibitor mainly during early hypoxia (2–6 h). Although the HIF1a (ortho 2) is expressed in low levels in these tissues, its expression seems to be moderately responsive to hypoxia in a similar way in both families. Other known neuroprotective genes like VEGF, HSP70 (ortho 2), and MAPK14 (ortho 2) also had constitutively higher expression levels in wild-cross animals (Fig. 7); only the sodium/calcium exchanger gene NCX3 had higher expression levels in the laboratory-cross. MAPK14 (ortho 1) with comparable expression levels as ortho 2, was not responsive to hypoxia nor different between families.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK1) and pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase regulate the TCA cycle enzyme, pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), which converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA. PDK1 was upregulated during hypoxia in both cohorts, while PDP1 maintained unchanged expression across exposure time points (Fig. 7 and Supplemental Fig. S6), indicating active reduction of acetyl-CoA availability for oxidative phosphorylation. Interestingly, the pattern of expression of PDK1 was different between cohorts in the abdominal ganglion, with laboratory-cross animals overexpressing this gene during early hypoxia (2–6 h) while wild-cross animals maintained similar levels across exposures. A similar overexpression pattern was observed for the hypoxia-inducible mitochondrial protein 1-a (HIGD1A) involved in maintaining mitochondrial functioning under hypoxia. Although HIGD1A is upregulated under hypoxia in both cohorts and ganglia, it was significantly overexpressed at 2–6 h hypoxia in the laboratory-cross (Fig. 7, Supplemental Tables S6–S9).

One catalase ortholog [CAT (ortho1), Fig. 7] was overexpressed almost constitutively in both ganglia of laboratory-cross individuals, suggesting that these animals relied heavily on this peroxidase before, during hypoxia and in its aftermath, in wild-cross animals this catalase was expressed in a significantly lower levels, suggesting its lesser role in potentially hypoxia-hardened animals. The other catalase ortholog annotated in Aplysia, CAT (ortho 2), was downregulated in the abdominal ganglion for both cohorts at all exposure stages, including reoxygenation and recovery (Fig. 7, S5–6). However, this was not the case in the pleural/pedal ganglia where its response to hypoxia was less pronounced and slightly different between families (Supplementary Fig. S6). Other antioxidant genes like GPx4, MnSOD, GPx5 and SOD (ortho3), were also overexpressed in both ganglia of laboratory-cross animals under hypoxia compared to pre-exposed. SOD (ortho 4) showed a strong induction in the abdominal ganglion of laboratory-cross animals, but not in pleural/pedal. Significantly higher expression levels under normoxia (control and recovery) were observed in GPx5, SOD1, SOD (ortho4), and MnSOD for the abdominal ganglion of the group descending from laboratory parents.

In terms of genes involved in inflammation, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) orthologs (1 and 3) were responsive to hypoxia in the pleural/pedal ganglia (Supplementary Fig. S6), although TNF (ortho 1) responded to early hypoxia only (2–6 h), and TNF (ortho 3) was upregulated only at long term hypoxia (5 and 6 days). Only TNF ortholog 4 showed expression levels consistently higher in wild-cross animals for both ganglia, but its responsiveness to hypoxia and overall expression level was less than other TNF orthologs in these tissues. Only in the abdominal ganglion, TNF (ortho 3) had significantly higher expression levels in laboratory-cross animals. The RAB7-GTPase-activating protein TBC1D15 (ortho 2), also involved in inflammation and mitochondrial integrity, was overexpressed in the abdominal ganglion of wild-cross animals, although its response to hypoxia was not very marked in this group.

DISCUSSION

Parental Environmental Pre-Exposure Correlates with Enhanced Hypoxia Resistant Phenotypes

Remarkable resistance to hypoxia at the phenotypic level was displayed by all the A. californica individuals in this study, as had been described before (11, 16, 17). Regardless of the exposure history, all animals showed no visual indicators of effects after repetitive hypoxia exposures (<1.8 mgO2/mL) for several days (and longer-term exposures >24 h, data not included). No mortality was observed during the experiments, and previously described behaviors linked to hypoxia, like respiratory pumping seizures (21, 22), did not occur under our exposure conditions nor during reoxygenation. However, on a deeper look, some effects were observed only in the progeny of animals grown under laboratory-controlled conditions when compared with the offspring of wild animals, regularly exposed to noncontrolled environmental conditions, including hypoxia/anoxia (39). Growth was impaired only in laboratory-cross Aplysia exposed to repetitive hypoxia in the laboratory (Fig. 1A). Growth retardation mediated by hypoxia exposure has been documented in many animals, with disturbed functioning of the GH-IGF axis as the main mechanistic cause in vertebrates (40). This has also been shown in aquatic invertebrates with rapid reductions in feeding rates followed by a reduction of somatic growth under hypoxia (41). The observed negative growth rate (weight loss) under hypoxia in this experiment has been previously documented in other aquatic invertebrates (42). However, given the short duration of the experiment, the growth changes were very small, limiting the strength of any conclusion in this regard.

Reflex outputs were impaired at some time points in the laboratory-cross, but no effects were observed in the wild-cross animals across the experiment. Both the tail withdrawal reflex (TWR) and, in a lesser extent, the time to right reflex (TTR) were affected in the laboratory-cross animals after repetitive hypoxia (Fig. 1B). The observed weakened response of the TWR, with reduced amplitude and extended duration of the reflex relative to controls, is indicative of a disruption in the neural circuitry responsible for this defensive reflex. Impairments of this reflex have been previously associated with aging (20, 43) and environmental exposure (44, 45), displaying a reduction in excitability of sensory and motor neurons located in the pleural and pedal ganglia (46).

Transcriptional Response to Hypoxia Highlights Resistance Mechanisms in Aplysia

Across cohorts, the transcriptional response to hypoxia involved massive gene expression reshaping in the abdominal ganglion, but not so much in the pleural/pedal ganglia (Fig. 2C), with coexpressed gene profiles showing remarkable plasticity in terms of induction and baseline recovery, potentially supporting the resilience/resistance observed at the phenotypic level (Fig. 3C). These ganglia were selected based on their role controlling cardiorespiratory function (abdominal) and the TWR (both) (46, 47). Although marked differences in the specific genes and temporal dynamics of the response were observed between ganglia (discussed below), a core response to hypoxia was observed.

Upregulation of genes related to protein catabolism, folding and maturation processes, mRNA metabolic processes, and several terms related to OxPhos and mitochondrial metabolism were observed across ganglia in response to hypoxia, along with the downregulation of mainly trans-membrane transport-related genes, but also some metabolic processes mainly after 5–6 days of repetitive hypoxia (Fig. 3D; Supplemental Fig. S4). Downregulation of trans-membrane transport genes is consistent with reduced ATP availability caused by hypoxia. The main ATP consumer of the cell, the Na,K-ATPase, has been shown to be removed from the membrane under hypoxia (48), resulting in disruptions of the cellular ionic balance. Dramatic but reversible changes in membrane potential have been observed in Aplysia neurons under hypoxia (16, 17), supporting the plastic transcriptional response observed in our data, and consistent with the upregulation of transporter genes during reoxygenation (e.g., KCNAB2, GLUT1, ASIC2; Supplemental Tables S6–S9). Protein catabolism and autophagy are required to support ATP production under hypoxia in hypoxia-resistant tumor and skeletal muscle cells (49, 50), and its upregulation has been linked with hypoxia responses. In addition, hypoxia-induced mRNA processing (5′ capping, splicing, 3′ end processing, and export before translation) is critical for hypoxia adaptation of both normal and tumor cells in mammalian models (51, 52). However, upregulation of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and aerobic metabolism are counter intuitive to the decrease in oxygen availability and the subsequent metabolic shift mediated by the activation of the HIF-1 pathway (53).

Such upregulation of genes involved in the oxidative phosphorylation pathways, however, has been observed before in fish, representing up to 44% of the DEGs after 96 h-hypoxia in Fundulus grandis (54). This has also been the case in high-altitude birds (55, 56) and even in some transformed mammalian hypoxia-resistant cells (57). It seems that at least in the early response to hypoxia, the upregulation of genes in oxidative pathways can increase respiratory efficiency, reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), and maximize ATP production. Such processes can be mediated in part by the activation of hypoxia-induced gene 1 (Higd1a), involved in maintaining functional electron transport chain under hypoxia, mainly by regulating the expression of specific cytochrome-C oxidases and other mitochondrial genes (58). Higd1a was upregulated during early hypoxia (2–6 h) and reoxygenation in both ganglia (Fig. 7, Supplemental Fig. S6). On the other hand, upregulation of some OxPhos genes does not necessarily involve increases in oxidative metabolism and it could co-occur with the typical hypoxic metabolic shift. One of the primary mechanisms of metabolic shift under hypoxia is the HIF1-mediated upregulation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) (59). PDK is an inhibitor of the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), which mediates the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, hence starving the TCA cycle and reducing oxidative metabolism. This is reversed by the pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase (PDP) (60), which together with PDK defines the fate of pyruvate and the metabolic mode of the cell. PDK expression was strongly induced under hypoxia in both ganglia (mainly at 2 h, 6 h, and reoxygenation; Fig. 7, Supplemental Fig. S6), while PDP expression remained mostly constant across treatments. This indicates that the highly conserved metabolic shift mediated by HIF1 is still occurring at the same time as other processes like enhanced mitochondrial efficiency and supplementing ATP production through protein catabolism, supporting the remarkable hypoxia resistance observed in these animals.

Pre-Exposure to Wild Environment Correlates with Enhanced Hypoxia Resistance through Transcriptional Programing

Regardless of a common core response, pre-exposure to wild environment (including regular hypoxia) in the parental generation calls upon a different suite, direction, and timing of expression of hypoxia-responsive genes in the offsprings than those that come into play in animals whose parents were raised in laboratory-controlled conditions. Both gene expression levels and gene regulation under hypoxia showed striking differences between the cohorts.

Expression level comparisons across all treatments identified consistently reduced expression of genes involved in OXPHOS and increased expression of immune response-associated genes in the wild-cross even in controls (Fig. 5, Supplemental Fig. S5). Adaptation to prolonged hypoxia in vertebrate neurons involves the downregulation of OXPHOS genes (61), and hypoxia-tolerant tumor cells show a decreased basal oxygen consumption rate compared with hypoxia-sensitive cells (62). Future analyses of the time course of laboratory and wild-cross-derived A. californica metabolic rates and metabolic end product levels under hypoxia will help determine whether transcriptional downregulation of OXPHOS is indeed modulating a constitutive hypometabolic state in resistant animals, allowing an improved response to hypoxia. In terms of immune response, HIF has been proposed as an important regulator of innate and adaptive immunity (63); therefore, enhanced immune response in hypoxia-resistant-HIF-overex-pressing animals can be expected. However, specific cells, genes, and mechanisms involved in these “constitutive” preconditioned phenotypes require additional exploration in the Aplysia nervous system, including the environmental factor(s) driving this preconditioning.

Cohort-specific transcriptional responses were most pronounced in the abdominal ganglion, where wild-cross animals robustly maintain or upregulate a “core” network of DNA repair, cytokine feedback, proteostasis, and synaptic stabilization, whereas laboratory-cross animals exhibit profound, sustained repression of the same factors across all phases of the experiment (Fig. 4B, Supplemental Table S4). This failure to engage repair and inflammatory-resolution hubs in laboratory-cross-derived Aplysia coincides with their inability to later induce additional ECM/synapse remodelers, antioxidant defenses, and apoptotic regulators, foreshadowing impaired recovery and vulnerability to reoxygenation stress.

Several of these genes have well-characterized roles in hypoxia adaptation, and their differential regulation between laboratory and wild-cross Aplysia likely underpins the observed differences in hypoxia tolerance. For example, PAF1, a component of the RNA polymerase II-associated factor complex, interacts directly with HIF-1α to rewire glycolytic metabolism under low-O2 conditions in pancreatic cancer (64). Although wild-cross animals sustain or induce PAF1 during hypoxia, laboratory-cross animals sharply repress it (Supplemental Table S4). With the same regulation pattern, SOCS2 has been identified as an HIF target that mediates cross talk between hypoxia and JAK-STAT signaling (65). Another gene of the “core” response repressed in laboratory-cross animals and induced in wild-cross, MAP3K9 (part of the MAPK signaling cascade), is required for HIF-1α transactivation and p300 coactivator recruitment under hypoxia (66, 67). Finally, the hypoxia-responsive gene, PARP14, was modestly induced upon reoxygenation in wild-cross, while repressed in laboratory-cross animals. PARP14 promotes HIF-1α expression and mediates metabolic shifts to glycolysis in hypoxic cells via NF-κB activation (68, 69). Together, these coordinated, phase-specific deficits in the abdominal ganglion of laboratory-cross Aplysia, including known hypoxia-responsive genes, likely underlie their impaired growth and reflex performance under chronic hypoxia.

Beyond the known hypoxia responders discussed before, our analysis uncovered multiple divergently regulated genes with functions related to epigenetic/epitranscriptomic regulation, proteostasis, translation quality control, metabolism, and synaptic-cytoskeletal remodeling (Supplemental Table S5), making them compelling novel candidates for mediating hypoxia resistance in neurons. For instance, ELP5 and EEF1AKMT2 (tRNA modification and elongation-factor methyltransferases) could enforce translational fidelity when oxygen is limiting, preventing proteotoxic stress during repeated hypoxic bouts (70–72). Likewise, ZNF598 and TTC3, which govern ribosome quality control (73) and nascent-chain ubiquitylation (74), may clear stalled ribosomes, a key tolerance mechanism under hypoxia. The ubiquitin-ligase adaptor HERC4 and MAPK-phosphatase DUSP3 could recalibrate stress-activated kinase signaling directly or indirectly (75, 76), shaping the strength and duration of preconditioning signals. Metabolic adaptations also emerge with novel candidates: GPT2, SLC46A1, and OLAH funnel substrates into alternative energy and one-carbon pathways (77–79), potentially preserving ATP and redox balance during repetitive hypoxia. TPPP2 and NLGN4X—modulators of microtubule stability and synaptic adhesion—may rewire neuronal connectivity to reduce energetic demand during hypoxia (80–82). Finally, differentially regulated extracellular-matrix and trafficking regulators like COL6A6, ADAMTS18, VWA8, and COPB1, could remodel the perineuronal niche and vesicular transport routes (83–85), promoting long-term circuit resilience. Together, these genes appear to form a multitiered network poised to sense, buffer, and adapt to repeated O2 fluctuations in wild-cross animals, making them attractive targets for functional studies of hypoxia resistance/preconditioning in the nervous system.

Regulation in pleural/pedal ganglia was generally concordant between cohorts in terms of direction, although effect sizes of many genes were significantly different between the cohorts (Fig. 4). Only ACSM5 and UBLCP1, both relevant for hypoxia response, showed cohort-specific directionality. ACSM5 (Acyl-CoA synthetase medium-chain family member 5) is a mitochondrial enzyme involved in activating medium-chain fatty acids to their CoA derivatives. Both proteomic and transcriptomic studies of hypoxia responses and adaptation have identified ACSM5 among the recurring markers (86, 87). UBLCP1 dephosphorylates the proteasome, inhibiting its activity under stress, especially within the nucleus (88). HIF-1a proteasome-mediated turnover is modulated by proteasome phosphatases like UBLCP1 (89).

Such marked difference in response between the abdominal and pleural/pedal ganglia (Figs. 2 and 4) could be explained by two hypotheses: 1) a selective sensitivity potentially based in circuit specialization, with the abdominal ganglion’s dependence on DNA-repair and proteostasis reflecting its unique physiological demands—perhaps higher metabolic or synaptic activity—whereas the pleural/pedal ganglion appears to buffer stress via membrane integrity and RNA processing; and 2) different levels of effective exposure experienced by the two tissues based on different hemolymph irrigation patterns. Although the pleural and pedal ganglia are in the circumenteric nerve ring bathed by the anterior portion of the hemocoel, the abdominal ganglion receives most of its hemolymph directly from a small caudal artery (90), potentially experiencing changes in oxygen faster. Future directions should include functional validation of these ganglion-specific modules and replicate experiments to identify additional circuit-selective regulators. Understanding how different neuronal types prioritize diverse functions under hypoxia will inform targeted neuroprotective strategies.

Similarities with Hypoxia Preconditioning Mechanisms

Hypoxia preconditioning (HPC), induced by sublethal exposure to hypoxia, has been observed before in many taxa, and results in acquired neuroprotection as was shown in mammalian models (91). In addition to a constitutive reduction and rapid regulation of metabolic demands, HPC involves enhanced activation of the HIF pathway, oxidative stress response, anti-inflammation and antiapoptotic mechanisms, and autophagy (92). Both laboratory- and wild-cross Aplysia individuals display many of these features, with the offspring of wild animals showing significant enhancements consistent with an exposure-driven HPC. Both HIF1a and HIF1b subunit orthologs were overexpressed in the wild-cross cohort compared with the laboratory-cross, along with HIF-regulated genes involved in neuroprotection like VEGF, HSP70, and MAPK14 (Fig. 7 and Supplemental Fig. S6). The role of HIF1, VEGF, HSP70, and MAPK14 mediating acquired neuroprotection and overall hypoxia/ischemia resistance has been characterized in human health models (93–95). These neuroprotective genes were constitutively overexpressed (frontloaded) in wild-cross animals in this experiment. This “frontloading” phenomenon has been previously described in anoxia-resistant animals like the freshwater turtle Trachemys scripta, displaying “constitutive preconditioning” to anoxia by maintaining high basal expression and protein levels of heat shock proteins of the HSP70 family (96–98).

A similar pattern of frontloading and enhanced induction when faced with hypoxia was observed in genes involved in inflammation. Two out of four tumor necrosis factor (TNF) orthologs in Aplysia showed constitutive overexpression in controls of the wild-cross cohort compared with the laboratory-cross, while TNF (ortho3) was consistently overexpressed in laboratory cross (Fig. 7). Upregulation of TNFs in hypoxia has been shown to both contribute to tissue damage and mediate neuroprotective mechanism in a context-dependent manner (99). TNFs can initiate cellular adaptations and modulate HPC/IPC even in the absence of hypoxia exposure (100) or can mediate the upregulation of proinflammatory genes through the NF-κB pathway (101). Finally, higher constitutive expression in the wild-cross cohort was also observed in the autophagy-related RAB7 GTPase-activating protein gene (TBC1D15). TBC1D15 has been linked with cardio- (102) and neuroprotection (103) in mammalian models.

Most of the antioxidant genes evaluated were overexpressed in laboratory-cross animals compared with wild-cross both under normoxia and hypoxia (Fig. 7 and Supplemental Fig. S6). Although this may be related to the potential reduction in metabolic rate in wild-cross animals, it seems counter to the idea of better preparing to face increased ROS stress caused by hypoxia. In mammalian brains, hypoxia generally induces antioxidant genes (104), and enhanced antioxidant response is one of the hallmarks of HPC (105). However, this can be dependent on the intensity and duration of the preconditioning. Nanduri et al. (106) showed that rats exposed to >30 days of intermittent hypoxia downregulated a large array of antioxidant genes in the carotid body, the adrenal medulla, and the brainstem. This downregulation was associated with an increase in DNA methylation in these genes that was not achieved with shorter exposure (106). Reduced expression of antioxidant pathway genes was also observed in hypoxia-resistant human mesenchymal stem cells exposed to severe hypoxia (107). These results highlight the variability of HPC mechanisms, especially in models where hypoxia-response and injury-response mechanisms cannot be isolated. In this sense, Aplysia, as other resistant models (e.g., turtles and hibernating mammals), can provide novel mechanistic understanding of hypoxia response and resistance by experimentally expanding HPC-linked gene candidates as proposed in the previous section. Overall, wild-cross animals show transcriptional programs with many similarities to HPC/IPC models. This prompts the hypothesis that the observed enhanced resistance of this group is mediated by pre-exposure to hypoxia in the parental generation, causing heritable transcriptional reprogramming that is lost or reduced when no exposure is experienced for several generations.

Abyssovirus Infection and Other Confounding Factors and Limitations

A very large nidovirus genome (~35 kb) was recently identified from A. californica transcriptomic datasets, and coined Aplysia Abyssovirus 1 (AAbV) (30). This viral relative of the notorious Coronavirus has been described to infect diverse tissues from wild and laboratory-reared Aplysia, being particularly enriched in the nervous system (28, 29). Although no pathological effects have been associated with high viral loads in adult animals (29), transcriptional changes have been associated with infection (28) that could potentially influence the transcriptional response we are associating with hypoxia resistance and preconditioning here. Viral load was significantly different between the two studied cohorts, with naïve animals displaying very low viral loads from both bioinformatic and qPCR quantifications (Supplemental Fig. S2). It is very unlikely that the impairment in growth and reflexes displayed by the naïve group had any relation with viral load, but some of the observed transcriptional differences in the pre-exposed group have been described to be caused also by the virus. Kron et al. (28) analyzed archived RNA-seq datasets from Aplysia nervous system and found a positive correlation between viral load and genes involved in immunity, along with decreased glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism. Upregulation of HSP70 and a gene for a protein containing epidermal growth factor (EGF) domains in ganglia of animals with a high viral load (28) mimics hypoxia preconditioning responses discussed before. Thus, caution needs to be exercised when assigning a specific transcriptional response to a stressor without considering the level of viral transcripts in the experimental animals. Further research is required to dissect the core transcriptional response to hypoxia and preconditioning in the context of different viral loads.

A critical confounding factor is due to experimental limitations, where the effect of cohort and exposure history cannot be clearly differentiated due to the lack of replication. Only one Aplysia family for each exposure history was included in the experiment due to limited space in the exposure system and to reduce the variance of the transcriptional responses. Although we cannot eliminate this issue, a certain level of confirmation came from additional experiments with comparable cohorts that replicated the observed phenotypic and transcriptional differences (manuscript in preparation). In addition, the clear response of genes previously described to participate in HPC/IPC, a mechanism specific to the phenomena we are attempting to describe here, provides some support to the validity of these interpretations. Caution should be exercised, however, until the results are confirmed with additional experiments including direct exposure manipulations across generations.

Conclusions

Here, we describe phenotypic and transcriptional responses to hypoxia in two Aplysia families with different environmental exposure histories. Our results highlight the remarkable resistance and resilience of this species when faced with hypoxia, relying on massive gene expression reshaping to maintain neurophysiological functions. Observed phenotypic and transcriptional differences between laboratory- and wild-cross-derived individuals hint at an intergenerationally transmitted acclimatization induced by repetitive environmental exposure. Such mechanisms, at the transcriptional level, showed large similarities to other hypoxia-resistant species and remarkably to HPC/IPC mammalian models, raising the hypothesis that hypoxia exposure experienced by wild parents is the main driver of the enhanced resistance. This could indicate a significant level of conservation in the mechanisms that participate in exposure-mediated cellular memory and presents the short-lived and very simple neural model of A. californica, as a great candidate to expand our understanding of the acquisition, maintenance, and transgenerational inheritance of hypoxia preconditioning. Together, our data support a model in which hypoxia preconditioning—both within a generation and through parental exposure—primes key neuronal modules (DNA repair, proteostasis, metabolic flexibility, and synaptic stability) that buffer against the cumulative damage of repeated hypoxia, preserving organismal growth and defensive reflexes. Future work should dissect the heritable (e.g., epigenetic) mechanisms of this preconditioning (within and across generations), including its main environmental trigger, and test whether targeted manipulation can rescue hypoxia resistance in sensitive animals.
