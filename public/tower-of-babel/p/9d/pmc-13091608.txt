Diarrhoea events in offspring exposed to tumour necrosis factor inhibitors and rotavirus vaccine: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Leah K Flatman, Marie-Eve Beauchamp, Yvan St-Pierre, Isabelle Malhamé, Anick Bérard, Sasha Bernatsky, Évelyne Vinet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPreviously, TNF inhibitor (TNFi)-exposed infants were recommended to withhold rotavirus vaccination until 6 months due to infection risk. Guidelines have recently supported earlier vaccination, although safety data remain limited. We aimed to compare diarrhoea risk (during early [1.5–6 months] and later [6–24 months] infancy) in TNFi-exposed infants who received rotavirus vaccination before 6 months vs those unvaccinated by 6 months.MethodsWe identified 3167 TNFi-exposed offspring in MarketScan commercial claims born to mothers with chronic inflammatory diseases between 2011 and 2021. Early vaccination was defined as ≥1 rotavirus dose between 1.5 and 6 months and modelled as time-varying. Diarrhoea events were identified using ICD-9/10 codes from hospitalizations, outpatient visits or emergency department visits. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI for diarrhoea events in vaccinated vs unvaccinated infants.ResultsAmong TNFi-exposed offspring, no statistically significant association was found between early vaccination and diarrhoea risk during early (101 events; HR 1.04; 95% CI 0.65, 1.68) or later infancy (283 events; HR 1.16; 95% CI 0.87, 1.55). Patterns remained consistent among infants exposed to TNFi in the third trimester (1.5–6 months: HR 1.07; 95% CI 0.62, 1.85; 6–24 months: HR 1.10; 95% CI 0.79, 1.52) and those exposed to high-placental transfer TNFi in the third trimester (1.5–6 months: HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.51, 1.70; 6–24 months: HR 1.09; 95% CI 0.77, 1.56).ConclusionEarly rotavirus vaccination in TNFi-exposed infants did not increase diarrhoea risk, even for those exposed late in pregnancy. Findings support early vaccination and reinforce updated recommendations.Keywords: pregnancy exposure, biologic therapy, TNFi, rotavirus immunization, infant health, childhood immunization, pharmacoepidemiology, administrative database, observational cohort study

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory diseases disproportionately affect women of reproductive age and often require ongoing immunosuppression, including TNF inhibitors (TNFi). Consequently, many infants are exposed to TNFi in utero [1].

Rotavirus is the leading cause of severe gastroenteritis in infants, but highly effective live-attenuated vaccines reduce rotavirus-related hospitalizations by >85% and prevent ∼45 000 infant hospitalizations annually in the USA [2–6]. In North America, rotavirus vaccines are the only live vaccines administered before 6 months of age [3, 4].

Because TNFi can be detected in infants for up to 6 months after birth [7, 8], concerns have been raised regarding the safety of live vaccines in this population. Following a 2010 case report [9] of a fatal infection after Bacillus Calmette–Guérin vaccination in a TNFi-exposed infant, guidelines recommended deferring all live vaccines, including rotavirus, until 6 months of age [10–14]. However, this delay may increase the risk of severe rotavirus disease, which primarily affects unvaccinated children aged 3–12 months [2, 15, 16].

More recent guidelines from the American College of Rheumatology and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology support administering rotavirus vaccine during the first 6 months of life, although these recommendations are based on limited evidence [17, 18]. To address this gap, we used administrative data to examine the risk of diarrhoea-associated healthcare events among TNFi-exposed infants according to rotavirus vaccine exposure during the first 6 months of life.

Methods

Data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using MarketScan commercial claims, a USA employer-provided private health insurance database including de-identified medical and drug claims for >273 million individuals [19]. Diagnoses and procedures were identified using the International Classification of Diseases 9th and/or 10th revisions (ICD-9/10) codes and American Medical Association Current Procedural Terminology procedure codes.

Study population

We identified all live-born infants (2011–2021) whose mothers, aged 15–45, had a pre-pregnancy chronic inflammatory disease (i.e. rheumatoid arthritis [RA], ankylosing spondylitis [AS], psoriasis [PsO], psoriatic arthritis [PsA], and inflammatory bowel disease [IBD], Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis) and received ≥1 TNFi prescription (i.e. infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, etanercept, certolizumab) during the pregnancy. Prescription records were identified using National Drug Codes and medical billing codes. Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Office at McGill University (A11-M107-14A). The requirement for patient-informed consent was waived due to the use of de-identified data.

The timing of TNFi exposure was determined based on prescription or infusion dates in relation to the gestational period and trimester, calculated from the onset of gestation. Term deliveries were identified through maternal or child ICD-9/10 codes using a validated algorithm for administrative databases by Margulis et al. [20]. If gestational age was unknown and no preterm code was present, the onset of gestation was estimated by subtracting 39 weeks (273 days) from the delivery date. For cases with a preterm code, 35 weeks (245 days) were subtracted from the birth date to estimate conception. When preterm birth ICD codes included a gestational age range, we used another validated algorithm for electronic health plan databases by Li et al. [21]. After each prescription, a grace period of five half-lives was applied to account for maternal drug exposure, corresponding to ∼97% drug elimination (ranging from 4.5 to 20 days for different TNFi agents) [22]. Offspring were considered exposed to TNFi in a trimester if maternal prescriptions (including grace periods) overlapped that period. Exposure status could vary across trimesters as maternal use changed.

Offspring were linked with their mothers using family identifiers and delivery dates. This method is commonly used with MarketScan data and has been shown to successfully link between 72% and 81% of live-born infants with their mothers [23–26]. Infants were excluded if they were born in the 13 USA states with a state-funded universal rotavirus vaccine program (i.e. Alaska, Idaho, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Mexico, North Dakota, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin, and Wyoming) [3]. There would be no private insurer claims for the rotavirus vaccine among these children; therefore, commercial databases would not capture them.

Exposure

Early rotavirus vaccination was defined as ≥1 procedure code for RV5 (90681) and/or RV1 (90680) administered between 1.5 months (6 weeks; the earliest age to receive these vaccines) and 6 months of age. These codes are validated in commercially insured USA infants (positive predictive values 86.7–88.5%) [27, 28]. Vaccinations before 1.5 months (<1% of infants) were excluded because the date of vaccination falls outside guideline-recommended timing and is therefore likely to reflect data-entry errors. Exposure was time-varying, where infants contributed person-time as unexposed until their first dose and as exposed thereafter. Although RV5 requires three doses and RV1 two doses for full protection, we defined exposure ≥1 dose to assess associations with diarrhoea events. RV1 provides 89.8% (95% CI 8.9, 99.8) protection after one dose [6], and RV5 provides 48% (95% CI 21.6, 66.1) efficacy after one dose [5].

Outcome

The first instance of diarrhoea-associated healthcare use was identified using ICD-9/10 codes from hospitalizations, outpatient visits, or emergency/urgent care visits. Codes included ICD-9 008.6–008.8, 001.0–005.9, 008.0–008.5, 006.0–007.9, 009.0–009.3, 558.9, 787.9 and ICD-10 A00-A09, capturing intestinal infections, diarrhoea and specifically rotavirus (ICD-9 008.61; ICD-10 A08.0). This approach has been previously used to evaluate rotavirus vaccine effects in MarketScan [3, 29].

Among TNFi-exposed infants, we assessed diarrhoea events over two periods. The first period was early infancy (1.5–6 months): this period reflects the recommended window for initiating rotavirus vaccination. Infants who received at least one rotavirus vaccine dose before 6 months were compared with those who remained unvaccinated through 6 months (i.e. comparing early vs delayed vaccination). The second period was later infancy and early childhood (6–24 months): the same exposure groups were followed to evaluate potential delayed safety outcomes after early-vaccinated infants would be expected to have completed the vaccine series, including events such as intussusception (ICD-9 560.0, ICD-10 K56.1), which may occur after the first dose but are monitored throughout infancy. Extending the follow-up to 24 months allowed for assessment of diarrhoea events beyond the vaccination period to ensure no emerging safety signals over time.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the cohort characteristics. To estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HR) and corresponding 95% CI, we estimated Cox proportional hazards models. For the primary analysis (early infancy), follow-up began at 1.5 months and continued until the first instance of diarrhoea-associated healthcare use or 6 months of age. For our later infancy analysis, follow-up started at 6 months and continued until the first event or 24 months of age. However, infants vaccinated after 6 months for the first time were censored at the time of vaccination, as the focus was on the effect of early vaccination within the first 6 months of life. Offspring who experienced a diarrhoea-associated healthcare event within the first 6 months were excluded from this extended analysis. For both analyses, follow-up was right-censored for offspring who remained event-free but reached the end of insurance eligibility, death or the end of the study period (12/2021).

The models were adjusted for potential confounders: in utero exposures to corticosteroids (i.e. methylprednisolone, prednisolone, prednisone, budesonide) and non-biologic immunomodulators (i.e. sulfasalazine, chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide, methotrexate, azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, mesalamine, tacrolimus, ciclosporin, apremilast, tofacitinib, baricitinib), sex, geographic region (northern [northeast and north central] or southern and western USA), year of birth (2011–2014, 2015–2018, 2019–2021), season of birth (October–March, April–September), and high TNFi placental transfer (adalimumab, infliximab, golimumab) at any point during pregnancy. All TNFi medications included in the study were approved and available on the market prior to the study start, with the exception of intravenous golimumab, which became available in July 2013.

We verified the proportional hazards assumption for all variables and stratified our analyses by sex, allowing the baseline hazards to differ between strata, as the relationship of the outcome with this variable violated the proportional hazards assumption [30]. We adjusted the models for both calendar year and season of birth because of the possibility that year (due to certain calendar years having higher rotavirus activity) and season (due to seasonal variations in diarrhoea-associated illness) could potentially confound the effect. We also calculated the crude incidence of diarrhoea-associated healthcare use with 95% CI, based on the Poisson distribution, stratified by TNFi exposure status and vaccination status. Exploratory analyses of intussusception were descriptive and conducted as a safety assessment. Incidence rates and proportions were summarized by study group without formal hypothesis testing.

Additional secondary analyses

We performed two additional secondary analyses of diarrhoea illnesses in the offspring, with the same model covariates, and tested if the associations were modified by in utero TNFi exposure characteristics, high vs low transfer or third trimester exposure. In the first secondary analysis, TNFi exposure was characterized by timing: exposure during the first and/or second trimesters only, vs exposure anytime during the third trimester (± other trimesters). A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was used, including an interaction term between TNFi exposure timing and rotavirus vaccination. This approach allowed us to estimate the effect of rotavirus vaccination separately for offspring exposed to TNFi in the first and/or second trimesters only and for those exposed in the third trimester (± other trimesters).

In the second secondary analysis, we included all TNFi-exposed infants with third trimester exposure, regardless of whether they were also exposed during the first and/or second trimesters. Third trimester TNFi exposure was classified based on the degree of placental transfer (high vs low transfer). Using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, we included an interaction term between TNFi placental transfer and rotavirus vaccination. This allowed us to estimate the vaccine effect separately for offspring exposed to high placental transfer TNFi and low placental transfer TNFi during the third trimester.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis in which we introduced a lag period between the vaccine administration date and the infant’s classification as exposed; this was to account for the possibility that some vaccine effects may be delayed by several days. We varied the lag period between 2 days and 14 days.

To assess the potential impact of seasonal variation on our findings, we conducted another sensitivity analysis including only infants whose observation period (starting at 1.5 months of age and continuing until 6 months of age) included at least 50% of the follow-up within a fall/winter season, defined as 1 October to 31 March of the same or following calendar year. Based on this definition, this corresponds to infants born between 8 June and 7 December, whose follow-up periods overlap substantially with the fall/winter season. We then repeated our primary analysis within this restricted cohort to evaluate whether seasonal factors influenced the association of interest. Cohort creation was done with SAS® Enterprise Guide (version 7.15; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). All analyses for this study were conducted using R (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Between 2011 and 2021, 3167 offspring were born to mothers with chronic inflammatory diseases who used TNFi during pregnancy: 981 exposed in the first and/or second trimesters only, and 2186 exposed during the third trimester (± earlier trimesters). Among those exposed in the third trimester, 1665 (76%) were exposed to TNFi with high placental transfer. Adalimumab (41%) and infliximab (30%) were the most common. Overall, 71% (n = 2240) received ≥1 rotavirus vaccine dose between 1.5 and 6 months of age (median 64 days since birth). During early infancy (1.5–6 months), 101 diarrhoea-associated events occurred; during later infancy (6–24 months), 283 events occurred among 2583 offspring. The median time to the first event after rotavirus vaccination among TNFi-exposed offspring was 62 days. Seven vaccinated infants (0.3%) experienced diarrhoea within 14 days of vaccination.

Further looking at the third-trimester TNFi exposure, among those exposed to high placental-transfer TNFi in the third trimester, we found no statistically significant association during early infancy (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.51, 1.70) or later infancy (HR 1.12; 95% CI 0.79, 1.59). As only 10 diarrhoea events (2%) were observed among the 521 offspring who were exposed to low placental transfer in the third trimester, this translated into a large effect estimate with very wide CI overlapping with the null during early infancy (HR 4.03; 95% CI 0.49, 33.20), but somewhat stabilized during later infancy (HR 1.19; 95% CI 0.46, 3.04), as there were 42 events (10%) among 411 exposed. Including a lag period (i.e. 2 days, 5 days, or 14 days) between the date of vaccination and being classified as exposed did not drastically change the effect estimates; therefore, no lag period was included in the final models (results not shown). Restricting the analysis to infants born in the fall and winter, where rotavirus activity is highest, produced similar results (HR 0.99; 95% CI 0.53, 1.85) to those observed in the full cohort (HR 1.04; 95% CI 0.65, 1.68). These findings suggest that seasonal variation during follow-up did not influence our effect estimate. Therefore, the entire cohort was used for all subsequent analyses.

In our exploratory analysis, three infants who received the rotavirus vaccine were diagnosed with intussusception (3/2240; 0.1%). All cases occurred among infants who were vaccinated before 15 weeks of age. The median age at rotavirus vaccination among these infants was 9 weeks, and the median age at intussusception diagnosis was 42 weeks. Among unvaccinated infants, zero cases of intussusception were identified.

Discussion

In our study, the largest to date, we evaluated diarrhoea-associated healthcare events among TNFi-exposed infants across two developmental periods. The early infancy window (1.5–6 months) aligns with rotavirus vaccination guidelines, while 6–24 months allows for delayed events, including intussusception, and ensures infants receiving later doses are represented. In both follow-up periods, early rotavirus vaccination was not clearly associated with an increased risk of diarrhoea-associated healthcare use compared with delayed vaccination. Results were consistent among infants exposed during the third trimester and to high placental transfer TNFi.

Our study adds to a recent systematic review by Schell et al. that assessed the safety of rotavirus vaccination in biologic-exposed infants; that review identified only 10 studies with a total of 162 TNFi-exposed infants who received the vaccine [31]. That review concluded that rotavirus vaccination was safe in all 162 infants, with a significant portion of children (64%, n = 103) drawn from a single study by Fitzpatrick et al. [32].

While prior studies offer early safety data, our research substantially expands on them. First, our cohort provides stronger evidence of early rotavirus vaccination regarding diarrhoea-related visits. Second, we examined short-term (early infancy, 1.5–6 months) and long-term (later infancy, 6–24 months) outcomes. Prior studies, like Fitzpatrick et al. [32], assessed only the immediate post-vaccination period (72 h for severe diarrhoea [defined as 3+ episodes in 24 h] and 42 days for intussusception). Our study uniquely considers TNFi exposure during pregnancy by assessing both high placental transfer and third-trimester exposure, which prior studies did not differentiate. This allowed us to explore whether in utero exposure to TNFi influences the infant’s risk of post-vaccination diarrhoea events.

Recent rheumatology recommendations now conditionally recommend rotavirus vaccination within the first 6 months for TNFi-exposed offspring [17, 18]. Our study provides new data on the long-term risks using a significantly larger sample size than previous studies to provide a more comprehensive assessment of safety. We confirm the updated recommendations by showing no excess diarrhoea risk among infants vaccinated before 6 months, compared with those who waited, and by extending safety follow-up through 24 months of age. This evidence offers important reassurance to clinicians and families regarding rotavirus vaccine administration in TNFi-exposed infants.

An earlier study by Cortes et al. [3] included children aged 3–23 months who had had in utero TNFi exposure and received at least one RV5 dose before the start of the rotavirus season. That study found a 44% reduction in diarrhoea-associated hospitalization (rate reduction of 44%; 95% CI 33, 53), also based on administrative health care ICD codes. A key difference from our study is the narrower age range and shorter time frame. Cortes et al. examined only two post-vaccine rotavirus seasons (January–June 2008–2009), while we assessed early (1.5–6 months) and later infancy (6–24 months) across 2011–2021. Seasonal patterns influence rotavirus risk, peaking in the fall and winter months [2, 33], with higher hospitalization among winter-born infants [34].

In our exploratory analyses, intussusception was rare among vaccinated and unvaccinated infants. Current vaccine guidelines recommend administering the first rotavirus dose before 15 weeks of age due to the potential risk of intussusception [35]. Among vaccinated infants, all three cases occurred in children who received their first dose before 15 weeks, with a median age at diagnosis of 42 weeks. These results are descriptive and should be interpreted with caution, as the study was not designed to evaluate causality or quantify the risk of intussusception. The age patterns observed are generally consistent with those observed in the general population. A systematic review by Clark et al. reported that in the Americas (WHO region), 23.5% of intussusception hospital admissions occurred in the first 6 months of life, increasing to 47.7% and 63.4% by 9 months and 12 months, respectively [36]. Overall, our exploratory results provide some reassurance regarding the timing of rotavirus vaccination in TNFi-exposed infants.

Our study has some potential limitations. First, as mentioned, we only assessed outcomes following the first dose of the rotavirus vaccine, while a complete series requires two (RV1) or three (RV5) doses. The Food and Drug Administration reported that the vaccine’s protective effect after one dose is 89.8% (95% CI 8.9, 99.8) [6]. Most offspring in our study should have completed the full series by the extended follow-up ending at 24 months, as the final doses are to be administered by 24 weeks (RV1) and 32 weeks (RV5) [5, 6]. However, we inferred that infants who received at least one dose before 6 months were likely to have completed the full vaccine series thereafter. As a result, the extended later infancy 6–24 months follow-up should be interpreted as a safety surveillance window rather than an evaluation of vaccine effectiveness. Another potential limitation is by using diarrhoea-associated healthcare use as our outcome, we may not capture all cases of post-vaccination diarrhoea, especially mild cases not requiring medical attention. However, our study is designed to capture the more severe cases of diarrhoea, which are most clinically significant. Prescribing practices for TNFi in pregnancy evolved over the study period following the publication of pregnancy-specific biologic guidance, which may have influenced the relative contribution of underlying diagnoses over time. Although the number of TNFi-exposed pregnancies linked to IBD dropped slightly over time, most cases were still due to rheumatic diseases. We also accounted for other immunosuppressive treatments in our analysis; therefore, these small temporal changes are unlikely to have affected our results. Finally, our dataset does not capture medications administered in hospital or emergency room settings. Therefore, if a mother was admitted during pregnancy and received a TNFi during her stay, this would not be recorded, unless the drug was administered as an infusion, for which we have access to procedure codes. This may lead to some misclassification of exposure; however, because exposure was assessed at the trimester level, it is likely that mothers receiving TNFi therapy would also have at least one outpatient prescription dispensed during the same trimester, thereby minimizing the potential impact of this limitation.

In conclusion, among in utero TNFi-exposed offspring, our findings suggest that early rotavirus vaccination before 6 months was not associated with an increased risk of diarrhoea-related healthcare use, during either early or later infancy. We further found no clear increased risk of diarrhoea-related healthcare use when comparing early (first/second trimester only) vs late (third trimester ± first/second trimesters) TNFi exposure, nor with late pregnancy high placental transfer TNFi exposure. Intussusception was rare in both vaccinated and unvaccinated groups, and the age distribution of cases aligned with background patterns reported in general infant populations. These results provide compelling evidence to support early rotavirus vaccination in TNF-exposed infants, offering additional reassurance that may help successfully disseminate and reinforce the recent recommendation changes recommending rotavirus vaccination in this population.
