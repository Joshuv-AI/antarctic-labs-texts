Metformin’s protection against blood-brain barrier disruption and neuronal dysfunction under oxygen-glucose deprivation and tobacco smoke and e-vape chemical exposure
Authors: Khondker Ayesha Akter, Sejal Sharma, Sejal Rajesh Jadhav, Yong Zhang, Thomas J. Abbruscato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccording to our previous findings, the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is affected by tobacco smoke (TSe) and electronic cigarette (ECe) exposure, and metformin (MF) can counter these detrimental effects. It is unknown, therefore, if MF protects against neuronal dysfunction after BBB damage caused by either TSe or ECe alone or combined exposure (TSe and ECe) in stroke cases. Additionally, MF’s ability to enter the ischemic brain during ischemic stroke is unknown. The purpose of this effort is to address these questions.MethodsA well-established bEnd3/astrocyte co-culture in vitro BBB model was utilized to conduct permeability studies. Normoxia and hypoxia using oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) conditions were used to mimic the in vitro stroke conditions. Western blot (WB) and immunofluorescence analysis were performed for relevant molecular targets. Additionally, mitochondrial dysfunction was assessed using Seahorse Mito-stress analysis using primary neurons. Also, tMCAO was performed in C57BL/6 J mice to create ischemic injury. To quantify MF in the mouse brain, a highly sensitive LC-MS/MS technique was used.ResultsAccording to our findings, a decrease in transendothelial electric resistance (TEER) values and increased permeability coefficient (PC) for sodium fluorescein were observed in the OGD/R condition alone or when combined with TSe, ECe, or mixed exposure compared to the control group. MF pretreatment, however, protected the BBB from losing barrier properties by increasing the TEER and decreasing PC values. Altered expression of tight junction (TJ) proteins was observed following TSe and ECe exposure paired with OGD compared to the control and OGD alone. However, MF was capable of offsetting the majority of these adverse effects by differentially upregulating ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5 expression. Altered neuronal mitochondrial dynamics and decreased OCR were observed after OGD alone or in combination with TSe or ECe, however, MF pretreatment significantly increased several indices of mitochondrial functions, especially basal respiration, ATP production, and non-mitochondrial O2 consumption.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that MF pretreatment could be an effective countermeasure following OGD exposure in conjunction with TSe and ECe exposure, which is often linked to the deterioration of the BBB and possibly mitochondrial function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-025-00719-9.

Introduction

Stroke, which is the second largest cause of mortality globally, ranks third in terms of both disability and mortality [1]. It is also the fifth most frequent cause of mortality in the United States, and ischemic strokes account for nearly 87% of all strokes [2]. The sole treatment option for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) that is currently approved is mechanical thrombectomy (MT) and intravenous thrombolysis, both of which focus on recanalizing the blocked artery. The fact that less than half of AIS patients treated with MT achieve long-term functional independence makes pursuing recanalization necessary, yet insufficient [3, 4]. MT is mostly effective in patients with large vessel occlusions and less effective in medium vessel occlusions and mild strokes. In the past two or three decades, very few investigational CNS therapeutics have advanced past phase three clinical trials [5]. As a result, repurposing a proven, safe, and successful treatment could offer a considerably quicker pharmacological transition from bench to patient since the drug’s safety profile is known and it avoids the costly and time-consuming early phases of research and preclinical testing [6].

Metformin (MF) is widely recognized as the primary medication for managing type 2 diabetes mellitus [7]. The US-FDA approved it nearly 30 years ago, reporting no significant adverse effects [8]. In addition to its glucose-regulating properties, numerous preclinical and clinical studies indicate that this drug offers neuroprotective benefits against various brain conditions, such as AIS, leading to the gradual expansion of its therapeutic applications [9–14]. However, the precise mechanism underlying the action of this pleiotropic agent remains unidentified [15].

Several comorbidities are linked to stroke risk and worsened outcome, with tobacco smoking (TS) being among the most significant modifiable risk factors [16]. Research indicates that TS exacerbates stroke outcomes by heightening oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and causing blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption [13, 17, 18]. Recently, electronic cigarettes (EC) have become popular as alternatives to traditional smoking, often viewed as safe [19]. However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that chronic EC use may also lead to cerebrovascular dysfunction and intensify stroke severity [20, 21]. Moreover, many people smoke and vape simultaneously, complicating their effects on cerebrovascular health and increasing stroke incidence, potentially due to a wider exposure to potentially toxic chemicals [22]. Previous studies have indicated that both TS and EC exposure impair BBB integrity by downregulating or dysregulating TJ proteins like claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1 [13, 14]. Prior studies from our lab have also shown that MF can alleviate stress and reduce inflammatory responses during stroke injuries [13]. This finding is crucial since it is believed that a significant portion of initial brain damage resulting from the ischemic cascade stems from free radical generation [23]. AIS produces a huge amount of ROS, and there is strong evidence that oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in mediating tissue damage in AIS [24].

MF functions as a neuroprotective agent primarily by stimulating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which subsequently activates signaling pathways like NF-кB and mTOR. This mechanism reduces oxidative stress-induced inflammation in the brain [25–27]. Moreover, MF boosts antioxidant defenses in primary neurons and astrocytes by activating the Nrf2 pathway along with its target genes, NQO-1 and HO-1. Additionally, after an ischemic stroke and exposure to tobacco smoke (TS), MF therapy elevates anti-inflammatory cytokines while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and lessens reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in neurons [17]. Moreover, MF decreases pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and NF-κB activation in brain tissue and plasma following TS exposure [17].

Typically, MF crosses the BBB through the organic cation transporter-1 (OCT-1), allowing it to get to the brain at therapeutic levels [28, 29]. While MF is known to attain effective concentrations in a healthy brain, it is unclear whether it effectively reaches the ischemic brain during a stroke [30], whereby OCT-1 expression is believed to be increased at the BBB [26]. Additionally, its possible role in safeguarding BBB integrity from OGD, along with TS and EC exposure-induced disruption, has not been well characterized.

This study had two primary objectives: (1) to assess the protective effect of MF in an in vitro BBB co-culture model of brain endothelial cells and astrocytes. For this purpose, cells were exposed to OGD/reperfusion (OGD/R) both in the presence and absence of TS and EC extracts (TSe and ECe, respectively), as well as mixed extracts, to simulate both the individual and combined effects of smoking and vaping on the worsening of ischemic stroke, and (2) to investigate MF’s permeability in the ischemic brain using a preclinical ischemic stroke model by performing middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) surgery. We also assessed neuronal health and metabolic patterns following exposure to OGD alone or in combination with TSe and ECe. We hypothesized that MF will improve mitochondrial function, preserve BBB integrity, and mitigate stroke severity by counteracting the detrimental effects of TS and EC single and dual exposures. By addressing these critical questions, this study will provide further valuable insights into MF’s potential as a therapeutic agent for cerebrovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke and comorbid conditions, such as tobacco product exposure.

Materials and methods

Tobacco smoke, e-cigarette, and mix extracts preparation

According to a previously published protocol, TSe and ECe were prepared using a single cigarette smoking machine [13, 31]. In brief, for TSe, one conventional cigarette was smoked following the FTC protocol (35 mL puff volume, 2-second puff duration, 1 puff per minute). For ECe, a disposable e-cigarette (JUUL 5%) with classic tobacco flavor was used, and puffs were drawn using a modified CORESTA protocol (27.5 mL puff volume, 3-second puff duration, 2 puffs per minute). In both cases, 32 puffs were collected into 10 mL of PBS. These puffing protocols were optimized to achieve nicotine levels (∼ 100 ng/mL) similar to those reported in the plasma level of a chronic heavy smoker or rodent model. The method involves 20 µL of calibration standard and test samples (plasma taken from mice exposed to smoke) were mixed with 160 µL of IS solution (1:8), vortexed, and then centrifuged. For LC-MS/MS analysis, the supernatant was cautiously moved into autosampler inserts. An analytical column was injected with a 4 µL sample [32]. TSe and ECe were freshly prepared and diluted in culture medium to a final concentration (v/v) of 5% before use. For the Mix group, TSe and ECe were prepared separately and then combined in a 1:1 ratio, maintaining 5% of each in the final medium.

Development of a co-culture model using bEnd.3 cells and primary astrocytes

bEnd.3 cells (passage 21–24) were cultured in high glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% non-essential amino acids, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and passaged at 70–80% confluency. Primary astrocytes were isolated from cerebral cortices of postnatal day 1 CD-1 mouse pups following the previously published method [33]. Briefly, cortices were minced, digested with 0.25% trypsin for 10–15 min at 37 °C, and the reaction was stopped using complete DMEM. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and seeded in T75 flasks. Media were replaced every 3 days until confluency (80–100%) was reached. To establish the co-culture model, 150,000 astrocytes were seeded onto the bottom (basolateral) surface of a polyester transwell insert (12-well, 0.4–1 μm pore size) by inverting the insert and allowing cells to attach for 4 h. Inserts were then placed upright and incubated for 48 h. Subsequently, 50,000 bEnd.3 cells were seeded on the apical (luminal) side of the insert. The co-culture was maintained for 8 days, with media changed every other day. Experiments were conducted between days 8 and 10.

Primary neuron isolation and culture

Primary mouse neurons were isolated and cultured with minor adjustments based on previous methods from our lab [34]. Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center (TTUHSC) in Lubbock, Texas (Protocol# 08017) approved this protocol. In summary, from E16 CD-1 mouse embryos (Charles River Laboratories, Inc., Wilmington, MA, USA; Cat# CRL: 22, RRID: IMSR_CRL: 22) cerebral cortices were collected and were dissected in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) with 250 µg/mL gentamicin (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA; Cat# MT30005CR). The meninges were meticulously separated, and the cortices were cut into small pieces and digested for 15 min at 37 °C in 0.25% trypsin, then neutralized with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Using a cell counter, cells were then counted and seeded into 24-well Seahorse plates that were pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat# P6407). The cells were cultured in Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat# 21103049) supplemented with 0.5 mM Glutamax (Thermo Fisher; Cat# 35050061), 25 µg/mL gentamicin, and 2% B27 (Thermo Fisher; Cat# 17504044), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. After incubating overnight, the whole medium was replaced with fresh Neurobasal medium. Following that, half of the medium was renewed every two days.

Exposure of tobacco smoke, e-cigarette, and mix extracts to the cells in the presence and absence of metformin treatment

On day 5, cells were treated with 5% TSe, ECe, or Mix (5% each of TSe and ECe) with or without 10 µM MF for 5 consecutive days. The treatment medium was refreshed every other day. After 5 days of treatments, co-cultured inserts were subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation followed by reoxygenation (OGD/R).

Oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R)

To mimic hypoxia-reperfusion injury, cells were incubated in glucose-free, deoxygenated Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS) containing 140 mM NaCl, 5.36 mM KCl, 1.02 mM NaH₂PO₄, 0.83 mM MgSO₄, 1.18 mM CaCl₂, and 26.19 mM NaHCO₃ (pH 7.4). The plates were placed in a hypoxia chamber and exposed to a gas mixture (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) [35] for 4 h. Following OGD/R, cells were returned to normoxic conditions, and complete media were added for 2 h (reperfusion).

Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement

TEER was measured using an EVOM voltmeter with STX2 electrodes. Values were corrected for blank inserts, multiplied by the surface area of the insert, and expressed in Ω·cm². TEER was recorded in all groups before permeability experiments to verify barrier integrity. All treatment groups were compared for TEER values, including control, treatments with and without metformin, TSe, ECe, Mix, and/or OGD/R.

Sodium fluorescein permeability assay

Sodium fluorescein (∼ 376 Da) was used to assess paracellular permeability. Following treatments and/or OGD/R, cells were washed and equilibrated with pre-warmed HBSS in both apical and basolateral chambers for 30 min at 37 °C. Then, 500 µL of sodium fluorescein (10 µg/mL) was added to the apical chamber. At 30, 60, and 120 min, 200 µL samples were collected from the basolateral chamber and replaced with fresh buffer. Fluorescence was measured (485–520 nm) using a plate reader. Membrane protein isolation

Membrane proteins were isolated from bEnd.3 cells following the manufacturer’s protocol (Mem-PER Plus kit, Part No. 89842, Thermo Scientific) with slight modifications. Briefly, 5 × 106 cells were grown in a T75 flask and treated as described previously. After the treatment period, the cells were washed with 3 ml of cell wash solution, resuspended, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was then washed with 1.5 ml of wash solution, resuspended, and centrifuged again under the same conditions, with the supernatant discarded. Next, 0.75 ml of permeabilization buffer was added, vortexed, and incubated for 10 min at 4 °C with mixing. Afterward, the sample was centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C at 16,000×g. The supernatant was collected as cytosolic proteins. Then, 0.1 ml of solubilization buffer was added, the pellet was resuspended, incubated for 30 min at 4 °C with mixing, and centrifuged again at 16,000×g at 4 °C. Finally, the supernatant was collected as membrane proteins, which were then quantified using the BCA, and samples were prepared for WB analysis.

Western blot

Control or OGD/R, TSe, and/or ECe-exposed and MF-treated bEnd.3 cells were lysed using RIPA buffer combined with phosphatase inhibitor (PI) to isolate protein lysate. By using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, protein concentration was calculated. Approximately 25-30 μg of protein from each sample was loaded and separated using a 10% Tris-glycine polyacrylamide precast gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA; Cat# 4568034). Using a Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system (BIO-RAD), protein samples were then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (BIO-RAD; Cat# 1704272). To block the nonspecific protein bands, the membranes were then incubated in blocking buffer (0.2% Tween-20 containing Tris-buffered saline (TBST) with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) for 2 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-ZO-1 antibody (1:1000 dilution (0.25 µg/ml), Thermo Fisher; Cat# 40-2200), mouse polyclonal anti-claudin-5 antibody (1:2000 dilution (0.25 µg/ml), Thermo fisher; Cat# 34-1600), rabbit polyclonal anti-occludin antibody (1:1000 dilution (0.088 µg/ml), Thermo Fisher; Cat# 40-4700), and mouse monoclonal anti-beta-actin (β-actin) antibody (1:10,000 Millipore Sigma; Cat# A5441) in TBST with 5% BSA at 4° C overnight. Next day, following 3 times washing with TBST for 15 min each cycle, membranes were incubated with anti-rabbit (Sigma Aldrich; Cat# GENA934- 1ML, RRID: AB_2722659) or anti-mouse (Sigma Aldrich; Cat# GENXA931-1ML, RRID: AB_772209) IgG-horseradish peroxidase secondary antibody (1:10,000) in TBST with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. After 3 times washing with TBST for 15 min each cycle, the protein signals were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Millipore, Sigma Aldrich; Cat# WBKLS0500), and by using the VersaDoc imaging system, the bands were visualized. ImageJ software was utilized to quantify the relative protein expression, taking β-actin as a control.

Immunofluorescence

The bEnd.3 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate, each well containing a cover glass, at a density of 1 × 105 cells and treated as described previously. After the treatment period, the media was discarded, and the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 5–10 min, then washed three times with PBS, with 5-minute intervals between washes. Next, cells were blocked with 3% BSA containing 0. 01% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature (RT). After blocking, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting ZO- 1 (1: 200; Thermo Fisher; Cat# 40-2200), occludin (1: 200; Thermo Fisher; Cat# 40-4700), and Claudin- 5 (1: 500; Thermo Fisher; Cat# 34-1600), all diluted in blocking buffer. The following day, after three washes with PBS and a 5-minute wait between each, the cells were incubated with an Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody for 90 min at RT. Then, cells were washed three times with PBS again, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (100 nM) for 10 min at RT. Finally, the cells were washed three times with PBS and mounted using the specified mounting medium. Mounted slides were kept at RT overnight. The next day, slides were examined using a Leica Stellaris SP8 Falcon microscope (Leica Microsystems). Images were captured at 93X magnification. Consistent acquisition parameters, including laser power and detector gain, pinhole size, and intensity, were maintained throughout to avoid pixel saturation when imaging all slides stained with the same primary and secondary antibodies. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the TJ proteins was calculated by selecting three random fields from each slide. The LAS X software was used to measure the MFI of the targeted protein.

Seahorse XF assay

Mitochondrial metabolic study of primary cortical neurons (cell density of 1.5 × 105 cells/cm2) was performed in 24-well assay plates (Seahorse Biosciences-# 102342-100), using an XF24 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Biosciences, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The cell medium was changed to an assay medium made of XF Base Medium supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 10 mM pyruvic acid, and 1 mM L-glutamine on day 12 (after the cells were treated with either TSe or ECe for 5 days, and then underwent OGD/R). The cells were incubated in a CO2-free incubator for one hour before the experiment. A Seahorse Bioscience XFe24 flux analyzer was then used to undertake oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Oligomycin, FCCP, and antimycin A/rotenone were prepared in an XF assay medium from a Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Seahorse Biosciences-# 103015-100) with final concentrations of 1.5 µM, 1.0 µM, and 0.5 µM, respectively, and were serially injected to measure the OCRs of cells.

Animals

C57BL/6J male mice 2–3 months old and weighing 24–28 g were procured from Charles River Laboratories. The animal studies were conducted following approval from the IACUC of Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center. All experiments were conducted in compliance with applicable guidelines and regulations.

Transient middle cerebral artery occlusion surgery with reperfusion (tMCAO)

Using a Zeiss OP pico I surgical microscope (Carl Zeiss GmbH, Jena, Germany) and previously described techniques, transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) surgery was performed on mice weighing 24–28 g [35]. Mice were sedated with 4% isoflurane and kept at 1.5% isoflurane in an N2O/O2 mixture (70/30) using a SurgiVet Vaporizer (Smith Medical North America, Waukesha, WI, USA). A laser Doppler probe (Moor Instruments, Wilmington, DE, USA) was positioned right above the left MCA region (1 mm posterior and 3 mm lateral to the Bregma) to continuously measure the blood flow. A thermostatic blanket (TC-1000 Temperature Controller, CWE, USA) was used to maintain body temperature at 37 °C. Following betadine aseptic preparation, a 1.5 cm incision was made along the midline of the neck. The left common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery (ICA) were all meticulously isolated. Following occlusion of the CCA, a micro clip was placed on the ICA, and the ECA was ligated and coagulated. A small incision was made on the ECA to introduce a 6–0 nylon microfilament with a round tip (0.20–0.25 mm), which was gradually inserted until it blocked the MCA bifurcation. A decrease in blood flow of 80% from baseline indicated successful occlusion. After 30 min of occlusion, the nylon filament was carefully removed to restore blood flow, thus inducing reperfusion. An increase in blood flow of 70% or more after occlusion was considered successful reperfusion. A subset of animals underwent TTC (2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride) staining two hours after reperfusion to visualize the infarcted area and confirm the incidence of ischemic stroke.

Metformin intraperitoneal administration and evaluation of brain concentration by LC-MS/MS

MF (250 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) was administered to both control and tMCAO mice. In case of tMCAO mice, MF was administered during the reperfusion phase more specifically 2 h after reperfusion, and transcardiac perfusion was conducted after 30 min of MF administration for vascular space correction. Briefly, 40 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4, room temperature) was infused through the left ventricle after the thorax was opened, at a flow rate of 5 mL/min using a Harvard syringe pump. Following euthanasia via cervical dislocation, the brains were collected, and the meninges were removed. The cortex was isolated by separating ischemic and non-ischemic hemispheres and then weighed.

Then, tissues were homogenized and diluted (1:10) before being stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Brain concentrations of MF were quantified using a SCIEX QTRAP 5500 mass spectrometer interfaced with a Shimadzu Nexera UHPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters XBridge BEH C18 column (50 × 3.0 mm, 3.5 μm). MF and its internal standard (metformin-d6) were detected in positive ionization mode with MRM transitions of 130.1 → 60.0 m/z and 136.1 → 60.0 m/z, respectively.

Sample preparation involved protein precipitation with ice-cold acetonitrile by vortexing and centrifugation, then evaporation, and reconstitution in 2 mM ammonium acetate. A 2 µL aliquot was injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis [30].

Statistical analyses

All data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.3. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups, where applicable. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Metformin improved TEER values of transwell inserts after OGD/R ± TSe, ECe, and mix extracts exposure

Compared to the control (vehicle), TEER values significantly decreased following OGD/R exposure, from 109.25 ± 2.2 to 66.25 ± 10.7 Ω·cm² (p < 0.0001). Similarly, co-cultures exposed to TSe, ECe, and Mix extracts in the presence of OGD/R also exhibited significantly reduced TEER values, measured at 73 ± 13 Ω·cm², 67 ± 4.7 Ω·cm², and 64.7 ± 13.2 Ω·cm², respectively (p < 0.0001 for all groups). Interestingly, MF treatment restored TEER values across all exposure groups (Fig. 1). TEER values following MF treatment were 91 ± 4.24 Ω·cm² for OGD/R + MF (p < 0.01), 91.25 ± 7.67 Ω·cm² for OGD/R + TSe + MF, 92 ± 8.28 Ω·cm² for OGD/R + ECe + MF (p < 0.01), and 91.25 ± 2.98 Ω·cm² for OGD/R + Mix + MF (p < 0.01), indicating a protective effect of MF against OGD/R and extracts-induced barrier disruption.

Metformin improved sodium fluorescein permeability in transwell inserts exposed to OGD/R ± TSe, ECe, and mix extracts

The PC of sodium fluorescein significantly increased following OGD/R exposure, as well as in OGD/R-exposed co-cultures treated with TSe, ECe, and Mix extracts, compared to the control (5.74 ± 0.96 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s). The respective PC values were 16.29 ± 2.33 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min, 16.22 ± 11.74 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min, 15.48 ± 5.80 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min, and 14.36 ± 1.49 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min (p < 0.0001 for all vs. control), indicating substantial BBB disruption. In contrast, co-cultured inserts treated with MF showed significantly lower PC values across all groups: OGD/R + MF (6.23 ± 0.39 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min), OGD/R + TSe + MF (5.70 ± 1.41 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min), OGD/R + ECe + MF (6.69 ± 2.38 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min), and OGD/R + Mix + MF (6.10 ± 0.65 × 10⁻⁶ cm/min), comparable to control levels (p < 0.01 vs. respective MF untreated groups) (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that MF confers a protective effect on paracellular barrier integrity under OGD/R and extract exposure.

Metformin modulates TJ protein expression in bEnd.3 cells exposed to OGD/R ± TSe, ECe, and mix extracts

We calculated the expression levels of ZO-1, claudin-5, and occludin by WB in the normoxic and hypoxic conditions in bEnd.3 cells, which were exposed to TSe, ECe, and mix extracts, and pretreated with or without MF. OGD/R alone or in combination with TSe and mixed exposure had a noticeable detrimental effect on ZO-1. OGD alone (p < 0.01) or combined with TSe (p < 0.0001) and mix (p < 0.01) exposed bEnd3 cells had a significantly reduced ZO-1 expression compared to the control group (Fig. 3A & D). There were no major changes in the OGD/R combined with ECe exposed group’s ZO-1 levels. However, MF was able to restore the ZO-1 expression after OGD/R alone (p < 0.05) or combined with TSe exposure (p < 0.05), but not in OGD/R combined with mix exposure. Also, no substantive change in the expression of claudin-5 was observed in the OGD/R alone or combined with ECe exposure compared to the control group. However, a significant decrease in claudin-5 expression was observed in the OGD/R combined with TSe (p < 0.01) group as well as OGD/R combined with mix group (p < 0.001) in comparison to the control group (Fig. 3B & D). Nevertheless, MF was able to increase claudin-5 expression (P < 0.05) in both conditions. Occludin expression level was also noticeably reduced in the OGD/R combined with mix group (p < 0.01) compared to both control and OGD/R combined with TSe groups. However, MF was capable of restoring the expression level (p < 0.05) in that group. No noticeable change in occludin expression was monitored in the OGD/R alone or in combination with TSe or ECe in comparison to the control group (Fig. 3C & D).

Metformin significantly increases TJ protein expression in the plasma membrane of bEnd.3 cells exposed to OGD/R

It is commonly known that 3–4 h after an ischemic stroke episode, paracellular permeability across the BBB increases [36]. Indeed, in hypoxic conditions, brain microvascular endothelial cells have reduced expression of the TJ proteins ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5, which results in paracellular leakage [37, 38]. However, when we examined TJ protein expression in the total cell lysate obtained from bEnd.3 cells following 4 h of OGD/R, we observed a significant decrease in ZO-1 but not in occludin or claudin-5 expression compared to the control group. Therefore, we were curious to examine if there were any changes in the expression of those two proteins in the crude membrane. As expected, the expression of all three TJ proteins was markedly reduced at the plasma membrane of bEnd.3 cells after OGD/R (Fig. 4A). As an extension of this experiment, to balance the WB data, we performed immunostaining targeting ZO-1, claudin-5, and occludin to check whether any localization changes occured, in addition to protein changes, these can be found. Indeed, localization changes in TJ proteins are a critical mechanism that also determines paracellular permeability. However, we were unable to find any significant localization changes of TJ proteins in the OGD/R model. Nevertheless, we identified a significant decrease (p < 0.0001) in the MFI of claudin-5 after OGD/R and a decreasing trend in ZO-1 and occludin intensity, which were abrogated with MF pretreatment (Fig. 4B-E).

Discussion

The BBB serves as a protective barrier that shields the brain from toxic material, primarily composed of endothelial cells supported by astrocytes, pericytes, and neurons [39–41]. It maintains overall brain homeostasis by regulating nutrient transport while preventing harmful or toxic substances from entering the brain. Since its inception, TEER has been the most often utilized metric to assess the BBB’s effectiveness [42] and was utilized to calculate in vivo BBB characteristics of various species. The TEER value of brain microvessels usually ranges from 1,500 to 2,000 Ω·cm², significantly higher than the TEER of peripheral microvessels, which typically falls between 3 and 30 Ω·cm² [43, 44]. To recreate in vivo TEER values in in vitro BBB models, a co-cultured setup composed of brain endothelial cells with other brain cells, such as astrocytes, subjected to different physiological stress [45], or treated with different chemicals has been introduced [46]. While TEER values of approximately 300 Ω.cm2 were obtained using primary endothelial cells from rats and have been thoroughly studied, in vitro models employing hCMEC/D.3 cells co-cultured with primary astrocytes have produced comparatively modest TEER values of 140 Ω.cm2 [47–49]. We employed an in vitro co-culture model of primary astrocytes and bEnd.3 cells in this investigation, and all tests were carried out using co-cultured transwell inserts with TEER value readings more than 90 Ω·cm². We observed a drastic reduction in TEER value and a notable elevation of PC value after OGD/R alone or in combination with TSe, ECe, or mix extract. This aligns with the hypothesis that stroke conditions can disrupt the integrity of the BBB. Although we speculated that OGD/R, along with TSe, ECe, or mixed exposure, could cause more damage to the BBB compared to the OGD/R alone, we could not find any significant differences between the groups. However, MF pretreatment significantly increased the TEER value and reduced the PC value of sodium fluorescein after OGD/R alone or combined with TSe, ECe, or mix exposure. These findings are crucial and support a previous study from our lab that has shown that MF pretreatment abrogates the harmful effect of either TSe or ECe exposure in iPSC-derived BMEC cells [14]. Our findings using a robust in vitro, co-culture model of the BBB have made us more confident about the neuroprotective role of MF.

Endothelial cells possess TJ proteins such as occludin, claudin-5, and ZO-1, which are critical for establishing a seal that can restrict the passage of substances or drugs through the BBB and selectively import nutrients and energy essential for neuronal growth [50]. While TJs at the BBB were once considered static, research over the past few decades has shown that they are highly dynamic and can become disrupted and dysfunctional at the BBB, and can lead to the development of various neurovascular disorders [51–54]. Claudins, specifically claudin-5, are thought to be the main proteins that form the backbone of TJs and are important factors that determine the paracellular “tightness” that exists between neighboring BBB endothelial cells [55, 56]. Transgenic mice with claudin-5 depletion have morphologically normal TJs at the BBB but suffer from harmful brain abnormalities and die about 10 h after birth [57]. Conversely, claudin-5 overexpression causes increased paracellular tightness in cultured brain microvessel endothelial cells, indicating a clear relationship between TJ function and claudin-5 expression levels [58]. It has also been demonstrated that occludin is a crucial regulator of TJ assembly and function. Increased paracellular permeability is a sign of BBB failure caused by the disruption of occludin oligomeric structures [59–62]. In contrast, ZO-1 plays its role by linking transmembrane proteins within the TJ to the actin cytoskeleton [63]. In this study, we observed that OGD/R alone or in combination with TSe and mixed exposure had a deteriorative effect on ZO-1, claudin-5, and occludin. An immunofluorescence study, as well as membrane protein isolation from bEnd.3 cells also revealed a similar type of detrimental effect observed under OGD/R conditions. However, MF pretreatment showed improved barrier integrity by increasing the levels of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5 after OGD/R. Since we had several comorbid conditions alongside the stroke model, including a combination with TSe/ECe/mixed exposure, we observed different patterns of increasing the levels of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5 in those conditions. This could be because when multiple stresses are applied, MF may not rescue all of those TJ proteins and upregulate their expression in a similar pattern. As we know, during ischemic stroke, BBB integrity is compromised partly due to oxidative stress-induced inflammation, and also due to the downregulation or dysregulation of TJ protein expressions, leading to increased permeability to unwanted molecules and worsening neuronal damage, but it is important that MF could partly rescue those harmful impacts on the BBB.

Even though several studies have been performed to define the beneficial mechanisms of MF during certain pathophysiology, the function of mitochondria has not received much attention [15]. Mitochondria are crucial for energy production, regulation of metabolic activity, cell signaling, and cell death. Recent research has revealed that MF provides a metabolic role by regulating mitochondria. Recent studies have also shown that MF can reduce the disequilibrium properties of mitochondria in myocardial ischemia/R injury and preserve the heart [64]. According to recent, ground-breaking research, MF works by blocking complex I in the mitochondrial respiratory chain and upregulates AMPK [65]. Mitochondrial complex I is the main source of ROS, which halts mitochondria-mediated programmed cell death, and may also play a major role as signaling molecules in various pathways [66]. The main molecular targets of MF are still the subject of much debate, but as more and more data become available, it is becoming clear that mitochondria are a critical target for MF actions.

In our previous study, we showed that MF could reduce ROS production in primary neurons that were exposed to either OGD or TSe [17]. To further investigate whether mitochondria are playing a role in this process, we have assessed the mitochondrial function and metabolic pattern using primary neurons exposed to OGD/R alone or in combination with TSe or ECe. The respiration rate measured at the beginning of the experiment is known as “basal respiration,” and it reflects the cell’s energy consumption under resting conditions. When oligomycin is introduced, it blocks mitochondrial ATP production, leading to a drop-in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) by the mitochondria. This reduction highlights the portion of OCR linked to ATP generation. The remaining OCR compensates for mitochondrial proton leakage and may also result from non-mitochondrial activities, indicating other oxygen-dependent cellular processes. Maximum OCR is reached by adding FCCP (Carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone), the uncoupler, which simulates a high-energy demand. This forces the electron transport chain to function at full scope, accelerating the breakdown of substrates like fats, sugars, and amino acids to meet the increased energy requirements [67]. We observed a noticeable increase in ATP production, basal respiration, and non-mitochondrial respiration in the MF treatment group in comparison to the control group. According to a previous study, MF can promote non-mitochondrial respiration by inhibiting mitochondrial complex I. Although it accounts for 10% of total cellular respiration, non-mitochondrial respiration sometimes plays an important role in O2 sensing, inflammation, and energy regulation. While some lactic acid can be produced through this process, lactate is not totally a waste product today. Moreover, it is regarded as a favored substrate for neuronal metabolism [68] and is also very important for the metabolic coupling between astrocytes and neurons [69, 70]. Additionally, lactate has shown promise as a potential neuroprotective agent in several diseases, like neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [71], adult stroke [72], or traumatic brain injury [73]. Lactate can also function as a messenger molecule in the CNS, triggering several important processes [74, 75]. As a result, it can influence several gene expression modifications and help regulate redox status [76–78]. MF cannot easily pass through biological membranes unless transported via carrier-mediated methods or between cellular gaps, as it is a small (MW: 129 Dalton), hydrophilic (logD − 6.13) molecule. At physiological pH, it is positively charged (pKa 12.4) [79, 80]. Therefore, the expression of organic cationic transporters, such as OCTs, the multidrug and toxin extrusion transporter (MATE), and other monoamine transporters, is essential for the drug’s biodistribution and pharmacodynamics [81, 82]. OCTs are involved in the initial cellular uptake of cationic compounds during the two-step translocation process across biological membranes in polarized cells [83]. OCT1 is a key transporter in bEnd.3 and hCMEC/D3 cells, which were detected predominantly at the luminal membrane compared to the abluminal membrane [84]. Another study also found that OCT1 and OCT2 were primarily expressed on the luminal side of human brain microvessel endothelial cells and adult rat brain endothelial cells [85]. OCTs are often paired with MATEs in polarized barrier tissues, where OCTs facilitate MF uptake into the cell, and MATEs help extrude the drug, and the substrates of MATEs are very similar to those of OCTs [86]. Recently, MATE1 and MATE2 protein expression has been seen in hCMEC/d3 cells [84]. Although the exact location of MATEs in mouse brain endothelial cells remains unclear, the Mate1 gene and protein have been found in the brain capillaries and cerebral microvasculature of male ddY mice [86, 87]. These findings are consistent with human brain microvessel immunofluorescence labeling, which confirmed MATE1 expression at the BBB [86]. Although our lab has identified the involvement of OCT-1 in the transport of MF across the BBB and also concluded that it does not interact with the efflux transporter P-GP in a P-GP overexpressing cell line [28], however involvement of MATE in CNS disposition of MF has not been studied yet by our group. High permeability of MF across the BBB could be due to transporter synergy between OCT and MATE, which we will focus on in future studies.

Additionally, preclinical stroke research has not sufficiently examined the role of transporters in CNS drug delivery, even though neuroprotective drugs like MF are substrates for endogenous BBB transporters [88]. Nevertheless, during stroke conditions, the expression pattern of OCTs may vary, and the disruption of the BBB also poses a significant challenge in treating neurological diseases, especially regarding drug safety, as the degree of increased drug penetration into the brain due to enhanced permeability remains uncertain. The maximum tolerable dose of MF is up to 2.5 g/day; however, the recommended daily dosage for humans is 0.5–1.5 g/day when taken orally [89]. Preclinical studies focused on ischemic stroke and other neurodegenerative conditions like multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease are conducted primarily on mice. Human dosages are frequently converted to mouse comparable doses in preclinical research using a dose scalability factor (12.3), which permits the use of mouse equivalent doses of MF up to 405 mg/kg/day [90]. To this point, few studies have sufficiently examined the quantitative evaluation of MF in mouse brains in connection with the wide range of treatment dosages and delivery methods used.

For our investigations, MF (250 mg/kg, IP) was administered to both control and tMCAO mice, and we aimed to determine the concentration of MF present in both the ipsilateral and contralateral regions of an ischemic brain. We found that the concentration of MF, although not statistically significantly higher compared to control brains, was higher in both hemispheres of the stroke brain, ischemic (116%) and non-ischemic (87%). Furthermore, the observed concentration of MF in the ischemic brain is estimated to be ∼ 10 µM, a concentration that activates Nrf2 [17] and is employed in our current investigation (in vitro study). MF at a dose of 10 µM activated Nrf2 and NQO-1 and preserved the BBB integrity in an in vitro model under various stress conditions [18]. We also showed that 10, 20, and 40 µM MF treatments caused concentration-dependent upregulation of Nrf2 in an AMPK-independent manner on mouse brain endothelial cells [18]. These results provide us with sufficient confidence regarding MF’s potential application for stroke and possible neuroprotection. However, since MF has a short half-life in plasma (2–6 h), resulting in a stable plasma concentration in patients of ∼ 4–15 µM (∼ 0.5–2.0 µg/ml) [91], the therapeutically suitable concentrations to determine the cellular effects of MF in preclinical models have raised concerns [92–95]. To overcome this raised issue, in the future, we will explore an infusion pump-based approach (Alzet mini-osmotic pumps implanted subcutaneously) aimed at enhancing the brain uptake of MF and understanding the molecular mechanisms associated with its brain concentration in a preclinical ischemic model.

Conclusion

Our study provides comprehensive evidence supporting MF’s protective role in maintaining BBB integrity under different diseased conditions, including in vitro stroke modelling by OGD/R coupled with exposure to TSe, ECe, and mix extracts. MF significantly reversed BBB disruption, as demonstrated by improved TEER of the co-cultured transwell inserts. Furthermore, MF restored paracellular transport of the light-weight BBB marker sodium fluorescein and positively modulated the expression of TJ proteins in OGD/R exposed TSe, ECe, and mix-treated cells. In addition, MF improved neuronal mitochondrial function by increasing ATP production, basal respiration, and non-mitochondrial respiration exposed to OGD/R alone or coupled with TSe or ECe. Most importantly, our findings show that MF reaches the brain during in vivo stroke at concentrations sufficient to activate the Nrf2 pathway, a key regulator of antioxidant defense. These findings culminate in MF’s potential role as a remedy for preserving BBB function in the context of ischemic injury and comorbidities such as tobacco smoking and electronic cigarette vaping.
