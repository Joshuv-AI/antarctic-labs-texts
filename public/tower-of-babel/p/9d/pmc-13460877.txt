Endometrial Hemangiomatous Polyp Presenting as Postmenopausal Bleeding and Mimicking Uterine Malignancy: A Rare Diagnostic Challenge
Authors: Akriri Jindal, Ankita Soni, Gargi Kapatia, Manjit Kaur Rana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVascular lesions of the uterus are rare, yet remain a potential differential diagnosis for the etiologies of postmenopausal bleeding. Hemangiomas of the uterus are benign neoplasms that can occur in any age group, with a predilection for reproductive age females. It is a benign vascular tumor and should be kept as a differential diagnosis for uterine endometrial mass lesions. Conservative management, such as local excision and laser ablation may help avoid unnecessary hysterectomy in such patients. We report the rare case of a 52-year-old postmenopausal woman who presented with a mass protruding into the vagina associated with bleeding and discharge and was suspected of having an endometrial malignancy that was finally diagnosed as a large endometrial polypoidal hemangiomatous polyp on histopathology. This case report underscores the importance of considering hemangiomatous lesions in the differential diagnosis of uterine polypoidal mass lesions for appropriate management.

INTRODUCTION

Vascular anomalies of the female genital tract are uncommon and pose significant diagnostic challenges.[12] The International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies classifies these lesions into vascular neoplasms, including hemangiomas, and vascular malformations, such as arteriovenous malformations.[1] Due to their rarity, the exact incidence of uterine vascular lesions remains unknown, with limited cases reported in the literature.

Uterine hemangiomas are benign vascular neoplasms that can occur across a wide age range, although they are more frequently described in women of reproductive age, particularly following pregnancy, cesarean section, abortion, or uterine instrumentation.[13] Clinically, they may present with abnormal uterine bleeding, anemia, infertility, or pelvic mass and may occasionally mimic malignant uterine pathology.

We report a rare case of a hemangiomatous endometrial polyp in a postmenopausal woman presenting with bleeding and a vaginally protruding mass, clinically suspected to be endometrial malignancy.

CASE REPORT

A 52-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with complaints of a vaginal mass, intermittent bleeding, and whitish discharge per vaginum. She had two previous uneventful vaginal deliveries and no significant medical or surgical history.

On per vaginal examination, the uterus was approximately 13 weeks in size, with unremarkable fornices. The cervix appeared elongated, measuring approximately 5–6 cm. Ultrasonography revealed a postmenopausal-sized uterus with a 2-cm intramural fibroid. A hypoechoic polypoidal lesion measuring 28 mm × 21 mm was noted within the cervical canal, with a vascular pedicle attached to the uterine fundus. The endometrial thickness measured 9.2 mm, which was increased for the postmenopausal age group. In view of these findings, a provisional diagnosis of endometrial carcinoma arising in a background of endometrial hyperplasia was considered.

The patient underwent excision of the polypoidal mass along with an endometrial biopsy. Gross examination revealed a reddish-brown polypoidal mass measuring 3.5 cm × 2.5 cm × 1 cm. Microscopic examination demonstrated a polypoidal lesion composed of numerous regularly distributed small-caliber vascular channels interspersed with sparse benign proliferative-phase endometrial glands within loose stroma. The surface epithelium was ulcerated, with underlying granulation tissue and inflammatory infiltrate. No cytological atypia or malignant features were identified [Figure 1a and b].

The endometrial biopsy showed simple endometrial hyperplasia without atypia. Based on these findings, a final diagnosis of hemangiomatous endometrial polyp associated with endometrial hyperplasia without atypia was rendered. The patient was managed conservatively and remained clinically stable on the follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Hemangiomas are benign vascular neoplasms most frequently encountered in the skin and mucosal surfaces, whereas uterine involvement is exceedingly rare.[4] The first documented case of uterine hemangioma was reported in 1897 as an incidental autopsy finding.[45] These lesions may be congenital or acquired, with the latter being more common. Congenital hemangiomas have been associated with hereditary conditions such as tuberous sclerosis, Maffucci syndrome, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, and Kasabach–Merritt syndrome.[4]

The etiopathogenesis of acquired uterine hemangiomas remains poorly understood. Hormonal factors, particularly estrogen-mediated angiogenesis, have been proposed to play a role. Estrogen is known to stimulate angiogenic mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor, matrix metalloproteinases, and endothelial progenitor cells, thereby promoting vascular proliferation.[1346]

Clinically, patients may present with abnormal uterine bleeding, anemia, pelvic pain, or infertility, particularly following uterine trauma or instrumentation.[14] In pregnancy, complications such as postpartum hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and fetal or maternal loss have been reported.[137] In postmenopausal women, uterine hemangiomas may closely mimic malignant lesions, leading to diagnostic confusion, as observed in the present case.

Histologically, hemangiomas are classified into capillary and cavernous types. Capillary hemangiomas primarily involve the endometrium, whereas cavernous hemangiomas may infiltrate the entire myometrial thickness.[4] Radiological investigations, including ultrasonography and color Doppler, may demonstrate a vascular polypoidal lesion; however, definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological examination.[28]

The differential diagnosis includes arteriovenous malformations, lymphangiomas, adenomatoid tumors, angiosarcoma, and malignant endometrial neoplasms.[23] Immunohistochemistry may assist in difficult cases, with hemangiomas demonstrating positivity for endothelial markers such as CD31, CD34, and von Willebrand factor and negativity for epithelial markers.[4]

Management options range from conservative procedures, including local excision, cauterization, cryotherapy, laser ablation, and uterine artery embolization, to hysterectomy in cases of refractory bleeding.[14] Conservative management is particularly desirable in postmenopausal women to prevent unnecessary extensive surgery when malignancy is excluded.

CONCLUSION

Uterine hemangiomas are rare benign vascular lesions that can present with abnormal uterine bleeding and polypoidal masses, particularly in postmenopausal women, where they may closely mimic malignant uterine neoplasms both clinically and radiologically. This diagnostic overlap often leads to extensive surgical intervention. Awareness of this rare entity, along with careful clinic-radiological correlation and thorough histopathological evaluation, is essential to establish an accurate diagnosis. Early recognition allows for appropriate conservative management, thereby preventing unnecessary radical surgery and associated morbidity.
