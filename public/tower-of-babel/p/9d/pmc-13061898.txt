Tunable colorless and transparent copoly(ester imide)s and nanocomposites derived from an optimized composition
Authors: Young Chul Choi, Yun Sang Shin, Jin-Hae Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A series of colorless and transparent copoly(ester imide)s (Co-CPEIs) were synthesized by reacting diamine p-xylylenediamine (p-XDA) with two types of dianhydrides of various molar contents, namely 2,7-dihydroxynaphthalene bis(trimellitate anhydride) (NATA) and hydroquinone bis(trimellitate anhydride) (HQTA) in N,N’-dimethylacetamide (DMAc). All the Co-CPEI films synthesized by changing the molar ratio of the two dianhydrides from 0 to 1.0, were colorless, transparent, flexible, and tough. The thermomechanical properties and optical transparencies of the Co-CPEI films were investigated according to the molar compositions of the different dianhydrides. Among the synthesized Co-CPEIs, hybrid films were created by dispersing various concentrations of organoclay into Co-CPEI with a p-XDA: NATA: HQTA molar ratio of 1.0:0.50:0.50. These hybrids were synthesized by a solution intercalation method. STN, the organoclay used for hybridization, was obtained through the organo-modification of hectorite. Various amounts of STN (5–40 w%) were incorporated into the matrix Co-CPEI to obtain Co-CPEI hybrid films. The thermomechanical properties, optical transparency, and clay dispersion morphology of the Co-CPEI hybrid films with various STN contents were investigated and compared. A TEM analysis showed that the clay was uniformly dispersed at a nanoscale until the clay content reached 10 wt%; however, when the clay content exceeded 10 wt%, most of the clay agglomerated. The physical properties of the hybrid containing a specific critical clay content exhibited the highest values, and the physical properties deteriorated when the clay content exceed the critical limit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46406-z.

Introduction

Aromatic polyimide (PI) has excellent thermal stability in electrical and electronic materials and aerospace industries; therefore, it has high potential for use in flexible substrates and high-temperature processes1–3. However, PIs are very difficult to process because they are insoluble and infusible owing to the rigidity of their aromatic structure and the strong attraction between their main chains4,5. Therefore, research on PI synthesis has focused on improving the physical properties of the completely imidized state by changing its chemical structure. That is, the thermomechanical properties and optical transparency of PIs can be improved by incorporating various functional groups such as esters, ethers, amides, and sulfone6–8.

Among them, poly(ester imide) (PEI), which contains an ester group in its main chain, is more economical than polyester or PI. Because of its excellent thermal stability, solubility, and mechanical properties, it is used in optoelectronic applications such as thin-film transistors, light-emitting diodes, solar cells, sensors, and photorefractive devices9–11. In addition, PEI is used in engineering thermoplastics, adhesives, and printed circuit boards. Furthermore, it can also be applied to membranes; continuous production is possible owing to its excellent properties and easy processing. The wide applicability of PEI is due to the advantages of both ester and imide12.

However, despite the excellent physical properties of general aromatic PEI, it is inherently dark brown in color, which presents significant challenges for its application in display fields that require colorless and transparent optical properties13,14. To synthesize colorless and transparent PEI (CPEI), a bent structure or an asymmetric substituent must be incorporated to prevent the formation of charge transfer complexes (CTCs) and thereby achieve the required colorless and transparent properties are satisfied15,16. However, these modifications often lead to the rapid degradation of their thermal properties. Therefore, to compensate for these disadvantages, many studies have been conducted on CPEI using rigid monomers. This approach avoids degradation of the overall thermal properties of CPEIs while maintaining optical transparency17–19.

Another approach for achieving the desired properties is copolymerization. Colorless and transparent copoly(ester imides)s (Co-CPEIs) generally have much lower molecular regularities than their corresponding homopolyimides. The altered intermolecular interactions, resulting from this reduced regularity, can result in polymers with new physical properties. Additionally, the copolymerization method allows for precise control over the physical properties by controlling the ratio of dianhydride and diamine monomers, facilitating the easy attainment of polymers with the desired properties20–22. 2,2′-Bis(trifluoromethyl)benzidine (TFB) has been widely used in Co-CPEI systems as an excellent monomer that compensates for disadvantageous thermal properties while maintaining high optical transparency23,24. However, alternative approaches that enhance transparency through the introduction of flexible alkyl groups have also been explored. In general, introducing a flexible monomeric structure containing an alkyl group into Co-CPEI significantly improves its optical transparency owing to the inhibition of CTC formation. For example, the structure of p-xylylenediamine (p-XDA) effectively prevents CTC formation while maintaining excellent optical transparency owing to the presence of flexible alkyl groups25.

Layered silicate clays have been widely used as nanoscale fillers in polymer nanocomposites because of their unique two-dimensional structure, high aspect ratio, and excellent thermal and mechanical stability. Among them, hectorite-based clays are particularly attractive owing to their expandable interlayer galleries, which allow polymer chains to intercalate or exfoliate between the silicate layers26,27. When the naturally hydrophilic clay surfaces are modified with organic alkyl ammonium ions, organoclays become highly compatible with aromatic polymer matrices such as polyimides and colorless polyimides, leading to improved dispersion and strong interfacial interactions. As a result, organoclays can effectively enhance mechanical strength, thermal stability, and dimensional stability by restricting polymer chain mobility and providing rigid inorganic reinforcement. In addition, the well-dispersed layered structure creates a tortuous diffusion pathway for gases and moisture, thereby improving barrier properties without significantly compromising optical transparency when the clay platelets are exfoliated at the nanoscale28.

STN is an organically modified layered silicate in which the naturally hydrophilic surface of hectorite clay is rendered organophilic by the introduction of long-chain alkyl ammonium groups27. This organic modification expands the interlayer spacing of the silicate galleries and significantly improves the compatibility of the clay with aromatic polymer matrices such as polyimides and colorless polyimides. As a result, STN can be uniformly dispersed at the nanoscale, allowing polymer chains to intercalate or exfoliate between the clay layers. Owing to its rigid inorganic silicate framework combined with organic surface functionality, STN acts as an efficient nanoreinforcing filler that enhances thermal stability, mechanical strength, and dimensional stability while maintaining optical transparency when well dispersed29,30.

To satisfy the optical transparency requirements, the main chain of Co-CPEI must have a bent structure or an asymmetric substituent; however, such modifications often lead to rapid deterioration of the thermomechanical properties. In this study, a Co-CPEI structure was designed using rigid monomers that did not degrade the thermomechanical properties of Co-CPEI, while maintaining optical transparency. Specifically, the diamine p-XDA was consistently used for its transparency, while the rigid dianhydrides 2,7-dihydroxynaphthalene bis(trimellitate anhydride) (NATA) and hydroquinone bis(trimellitate anhydride) (HQTA) were employed to enhance the thermomechanical properties of the synthesized Co-CPEI. The thermomechanical properties and optical transparencies of Co-CPEIs with different compositions, prepared with varying molar ratios of the two dianhydride monomers were measured, and the physical properties with respect to the monomer structure were investigated17,21.

Among the various Co-CPEI compositions, a p-XDA: NATA: HQTA ratio of 1.0:0.50:0.50 (mole ratio) was selected for hybrid film preparation, and organoclay was dispersed to prepare hybrid films. Hybrid films with various organoclay STN contents (5–40 wt%) were synthesized using the solution intercalation method. In addition, the dispersion state of the organoclay, as well as the thermomechanical properties and optical transparency, were investigated as a function of the organoclay content in the Co-CPEI matrix, and the results were systematically compared.

The objective of this study was to synthesize a new series of Co-CPEIs using different monomer compositions and to measure and compare their composition-dependent properties. In addition, the thermal behavior, mechanical performance, clay dispersion state, and optical transparency of the Co-CPEI and STN-based nanocomposites were systematically investigated as a function of clay content. By combining dianhydride composition control with nanofiller reinforcement, this study presents a multifunctional and tunable materials platform capable of meeting the stringent requirements of next-generation transparent and flexible electronic applications.

Methods

Materials

The diamine p-XDA was obtained from TCI (Tokyo, Japan), while the dianhydride monomers NATA and HQTA were purchased from Lumtech (Hong Kong). N,N’-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) was purified, and moisture was removed using a molecular sieve (4 Å). All reagents were used without further purification. Hectorite had a cation exchange capacity of 79–83 meq/100 g31,32. STN was purchased from Co-op Chemical Co. (Tokyo, Japan).

Preparation of Co-CPEI films

The monomer compositions of the Co-CPEIs prepared in this study are summarized in Table 1. The structure of Co-CPEI used in this study and synthesis routes are shown in Scheme 1. Because the experimental method was the same for all monomer compositions, Composition C in Table 1 (p-XDA: NATA: HQTA = 1.0:0.5:0.5 (mole ratio)) is described as an example. Co-CPEIs were synthesized using a two-step method. To synthesize poly(amic acid) (PAA), a 250 mL three-neck flask was used. Then, 1.84 g (1.36 × 10− 2 mole) of the amine monomer p-XDA and 3.45 g (6.78 × 10− 3 mole) of NATA were added to 90 mL of DMAc and completely dissolved under a nitrogen atmosphere at 0℃ for 30 min. Subsequently, 3.11 g (6.78 ✕ 10-3 mole) of the anhydride HQTA was added, and the mixture was stirred vigorously under a nitrogen atomosphere at 0℃ for 1 h, followed by stirring at room temperature for over 18 h to synthesize PAA with 10 wt% soild contents.

The obtained PAA solution was cast onto a clean glass plate and then stabilized under a nitrogen atmosphere at 50 ℃ for 1 h and 70 ℃ for 2 h. Subsequently, the solvent was removed under vacuum for 2 h at 80 ℃, followed by 30 min each at 110 and 140 ℃. The Co-CPEI film was obtained by immersing the glass plate in hot water.

Preparation of Co-CPEI hybrid films

The method used to synthesize the polymer hybrid was the same for all the clay contents. Hence, a detailed explanation is provided for the Co-CPEI hybrid with 10 wt% STN as a representative example. STN (1.0 g) and DMAc (30 mL) were added to a 100 mL three-neck flask and stirred at 25 ℃ for 2 h. The mixture was sonicated for 3 h to obtain a uniformly dispersed clay solution. In a separate beaker, a Co-CPEI film (9 g, 1.18 mol) and DMAc (50 mL) were mixed together. This mixture was then added dropwise to the STN/DMAc solution with vigorous stirring at 30 ℃ for 1 h to obtain a homogeneously dispersed system. The resulting mixture was ultrasonicated six times for 20 min each to obtain a homogeneous composite solution. This solution was poured onto a glass plate to a uniform thickness and stabilized under N2 gas at 25 ℃ for 2 h and then at 50 ℃ for 1 h. Subsequently, to remove the solvent, the solution was heat-treated under vacuum at 80 ℃ for 1 h, followed by 30 min each at 100, 130, and 160 ℃. The film was then sequentially heated at 190, 220, 250, and 280 ℃ for 30 min at each temperature. Because thermal treatments can affect the properties of the hybrid film, including its thermomechanical properties, optical characteristics, and morphology, no fixation tools were used on the glass plate during the heat treatment.

Characterization

To confirm the synthesis of the Co-CPEI structure, Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer, Spectrum Two, Llantrisant, UK) was employed. Solid-state 1H magic-angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (MAS NMR) and 13C cross-polarization (CP) MAS NMR spectroscopy (Bruker 500 MHz, Germany) was employed for a more detailed structural elucidation. The 1H and 13C MAS NMR experiments were performed at Larmor frequencies of 500.13 MHz and 125.75 MHz. The samples were packed into 4 mm CP/MAS rotors and spun at rate of 10 kHz to suppress spinning sidebands. Chemical shifts were referenced to the peak of tetramethylsilane (TMS). The three-dimensional (3-D) structure of the polymer chains was analyzed using ChemDraw Office®.

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalyticalX’ Pert PRO-MRD, Amsterdam, Netherlands) was used to investigate the aggregation and interlayer distances of the nanofillers dispersed within the Co-CPEI matrix. Cu-Kα radiation was used as the source, and measurements were conducted in the range of 2θ = 2–14° at a scanning rate of 2°/min.

The fractured surfaces of the hybrid films, obtained by breaking them in liquid nitrogen, were sputter-coated with gold to enhance surface conductivity and then examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi S-2400, Tokyo, Japan). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI, Titan G2 Cube 60–300, Oregon, USA) was used to examine the dispersion states and morphologies of the clay layers and complement the XRD results. Epoxy resin samples for TEM measurements were cured at 70 ℃ for 24 h, cut into 90 nm thick specimens using a microtome under vacuum conditions, and examined at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

A differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, NETZSCH F3, Munchen, Germany) analysis and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, TA Q500, New Castle, USA) were performed to heat the samples at a rate of 20 ℃/min under a N2 atmosphere. The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the films was measured using a macro-expansion probe (TMA-2940, Tokyo, Japan) by applying a force of 0.1 N to the film at a heating rate of 5 ℃/min within a temperature range was 50–200 ℃, and the results were obtained from the second heating cycle.

The mechanical and tensile properties of the films were measured using a universal testing machine (UTM; Shimadazu, JP/AG-50KNX, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were cut to a size of 5 mm × 50 mm, and testing was conducted at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min. Each sample was tested more than ten times, and the results were obtained by averaging the remaining values after excluding those outside an error range.

The optical transmittance and cut-off wavelength (λo) of the films at specific wavelengths were measured using a UV-visible spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-3600, Tokyo, Japan). The yellow indices (YIs) of the films were measured using a spectrophotometer (Konica Minolta CM-3600d, Tokyo, Japan).

The stretching peaks of the imide carbonyl C = O were observed at 1778 and 1704 cm⁻¹, and the peak of the C-N-C bond of the imide group appeared at 1386 cm⁻¹, confirming the successful imidization reaction33.

FT-IR spectroscopy can confirm the structure through functional groups; however, solid-state 13C-NMR spectroscopy provides a more detailed confirmation of the synthesized Co-CPEI structure through the chemical shifts of each carbon. Peak g of the p-XDA moiety was observed at 44.0 ppm. Compared with 13C-NMR chemical shifts, 1H-NMR chemical shifts are observed over a narrower chemical shift range; therefore, 1H-NMR is generally less suitable for detailed structural analysis. The structure of the synthesized Co-CPEI was confirmed through previously obtained spectroscopic analysis results. Supplementary Figures S1–S4 present the FT-IR, ¹H-NMR, and ¹³C-NMR results of compositions A and E, respectively.

Figure 3 shows the 3-D structure of a polymer chain containing various monomers. Composition A is a homopolymer composed of p-XDA/NATA, whereas composition E is composed of p-XDA/HQTA. Figure 3(A), representing composition A, includes a substituent with a 120o bend due to 2,7-naphthalene diol (NATA). Consequently, the overall chain was relatively rigid and somewhat bent. Owing to its non-crystalline structure, its thermal properties were poor. In contrast, Composition E, featuring a hydroquinone structure (HQTA) with a 180o straight substituent, included a linear and rigid benzene ring, which facilitated easier stacking. Compared with composition A, composition E exhibited significant differences in the chain structure and consequent properties. Therefore, based on its structure, composition E exhibits a higher thermal stability. This difference in the 3-D structure significantly affects the thermomechanical properties and optical transparency, which will be discussed further.

Thermal property

In general, most Co-CPEIs are amorphous polymers. Therefore, instead of observing the melting transition temperature (Tm), their glass transition temperatures (Tg) were measured to describe their thermal properties. Tg is influenced by the fluidity of the chain and free volume of substituents caused by the segmental motion of the main chain and substituents during thermal treatment, flexibility and rigidity of the main chain, and secondary bonds between chains (such as hydrogen bonds) due to interactions between the main chain and side chains35. In particular, the Tg of the composite materials obtained by incorporating fillers into matrix polymers is significantly influenced by not only the segmental motion and free volume of the polymer chains, as previously described, but also the dispersion of the fillers and structure and content of the fillers themselves36.

The thermograms of the Co-CPEI films with various monomer compositions obtained via DSC are shown in Fig. 4, and their Tg values are listed in Table 2. As the HQTA anhydride content increased relative to the NATA monomer content, the Tg values of Co-CPEI consistently increased. For example, as the HQTA content increased from 0 to 1.0 mol ratio (relative to p-XDA), the Tg of Co-CPEI gradually increased from 156 ℃ to 186 ℃. This trend was attributed to the linear structure of the HQTA anhydride compared to the bent structure of NATA, which resulted in a more rigid main-chain structure as the HQTA content increased37. These results are also predicted from Fig. 3.

The initial decomposition temperature observed at a weight loss (TDi) of 2%, and the residual weight was observed at 600 ℃ (wtR600). The results are summarized in Table 2. Similar to the results obtained at Tg, an increase in the HQTA content from 0 mol (composition A) to 1.0 mol (composition E) resulted in an increase in TDi from 307 ℃ to 357 ℃. Polymer materials expand and contract repeatedly during heating and cooling. The CTE obtained from these processes is a fundamental indicator of the change in the dimensions of these materials with temperature variations. These values are crucial for processing and applications at elevated temperatures because each polymer material has a unique CTE. For instance, materials with low CTE values exhibit excellent dimensional stability under heat-treatments, which can be a positive factor. In most polymer films, expansion primarily occurs perpendicular to the main polymer chain during heat treatments. However, compositions that contain straight and linear monomers within polymer chains demonstrate excellent dimensional stability owing to their thermal stability and heat barrier properties40.

The CTE values were measured for different compositions of NATA and HQTA with the same molar amount of p-XDA, and the results are summarized in Table 2. Although the molar amount of linear HQTA increased, both NATA and HQTA were composed of benzene, making the main chain rigid, while maintaining a linear shape. This facilitated the orientation of the molecular chains and enhanced chain-chain interactions. As a result, the CTE showed consistent values across all compositions, ranging from 50.4 to 52.3 ppm/℃, without significant differences. The TMA results for various compositions are shown in Fig. 6.

Mechanical tensile property

The mechanical and thermal properties of the Co-CPEI film vary depending on the composition and structure of the monomer. In particular, the copolymers exhibit significant differences depending on the structures of the additional monomers. For example, as the composition of linear and rigid monomers increases, the mechanical properties of the copolymer improve. In contrast, if the composition of monomers with flexible curved structures increases, their mechanical properties tend to decrease41. Overall, as the HQTA monomer content increased from 0 mol (composition A) to 1.0 mol (composition E), both the ultimate strength and initial modulus increased. Optical transparency

In Co-CPEI films, the structure and content of monomers are closely related to the light transmittance. As the composition of the rigid, linear monomers in the polymer increases, light cannot easily pass through, resulting in a decrease in the light transmittance of the copolymer film. The optical transparency of the Co-CPEI films was evaluated in terms of λo, transmittance at 500 nm (500 nmtrans), and YI.

The UV-vis. spectra of the Co-CPEI films are shown in Fig. 7, and the results are summarized in Table 4. As the composition of linear-type HQTA monomers in the Co-CPEI film increases, the λo value increases from 373 nm to 390 nm, whereas the 500 nmtrans decreases from 86% to 81%. This is because the straight HQTA structure reduces the light transmittance. Furthermore, all films exhibited YIs of 3–5, regardless of the monomer composition, indicating nearly colorless transparent films (Table 4). Dispersibilities of the Co-CPEI hybrids

XRD was used to determine the dispersion state of the clays within the hybrid and their interlayer distances. Although the results were one-dimensional, they had the advantage of allowing the observation of new crystal forms arising from the dispersion and agglomeration of clays within the polymer matrix44. Figure 9 shows the XRD patterns of the Co-CPEI hybrid films with various contents of the dispersed organoclay STN.

Fig. 9XRD of Co-CPEI hybrid film with various organoclay contents.

XRD of Co-CPEI hybrid film with various organoclay contents.

Figure 9 shows the XRD patterns of Co-CPEI hybrid films with a p-XDA: NATA: HQTA ratio of 1.0:0.50:0.50 (molar ratio) and STN amounts ranging from 0 to 40 wt%. The Na+-Hectorite shows a characteristic peak at 2θ = 7.37o (d = 11.99 Å), while the surface-modified organoclay STN displays a peak at a 2θ = 4.60°, corresponding to an interlayer distance (d) of 19.19 Å. This increase in d-spacing occurred during chemical treatment of the clay surface with long alkyl groups. In general, an increased d-spacing facilitates the insertion of polymer chains between the clay layers within the hybrid, thereby improving dispersibility45.

As the pure Co-CPEI film was amorphous, no crystal peaks were observed. Furthermore, no XRD peaks were observed in the hybrid film, even when 5 wt% STN was dispersed (Fig. 9). These results indicate that the clay formed a highly dispersed composite material at the nanoscale within the Co-CPEI matrix. However, when the STN content was increased to 10 wt%, a weak intensity peak was observed at 2θ = 5.16° (d = 17.11 Å). When the STN content reached 20 wt%, a peak with a significantly higher intensity developed at the same position. These peaks occurred because the agglomeration of the clay exceeded a certain critical limit, leading to the formation of new crystals. The intensity of this peak was almost constant even when the STN content was increased to 40 wt%.

Because the XRD results provide only one-dimensional information about the degree of dispersion of the filler in the matrix, a more detailed morphology must be confirmed using an electron microscope to obtain complementary information.

Morphologies of the Co-CPEI hybrids

The interlayer spacing (d-spacing) of clay is most commonly determined using XRD. XRD is the simplest and most widely used technique for evaluating changes in the interlayer distance of clay dispersed within nanocomposites, allowing a primary assessment of intercalation behavior and possible clay aggregation.

However, XRD provides only average structural information and does not offer detailed morphological insight into the actual dispersion state or degree of aggregation of the clay. Therefore, more precise evaluation requires electron microscopy techniques such as SEM and TEM. These analyses enable direct observation of the detailed morphology and aggregation structure of organoclays dispersed within the polymer matrix, thereby providing a clearer understanding of the clay dispersion state.

The SEM images of the fractured surfaces of Co-CPEI hybrid films containing 0–10 wt% organoclay are compared in Fig. 10. As shown in Fig. 10(b), the formation of a clay phase within the Co-CPEI hybrid films can be clearly observed. The hybrid film containing 10 wt% STN exhibited a morphology in which the clay domains were relatively well dispersed throughout the continuous Co-CPEI matrix (Fig. 10(c)). However, in the case of the hybrid film containing 20 wt% organoclay (Fig. 10(d)), deformed surfaces and voids were observed. Furthermore, the microstructure of the 40 wt% STN hybrid film (Fig. 10(e)) revealed a rougher fractured surface with a greater number of voids and deformed regions.

In other words, the degree of deformation of the fractured surfaces increased with increasing organoclay content in the hybrid films. This behavior is likely attributable to the agglomeration of clay particles. A comparison of the micrographs indicates that the fractured surfaces of the hybrid films with higher clay contents were more severely deformed than those with lower clay contents, which can be interpreted as a consequence of clay particle aggregation.

To provide direct evidence of nanoscale composite formation, ultrathin sections of the hybrid film were analyzed via TEM. For a detailed observation, specific areas of the clay (indicated by arrows) were enlarged in all photographs according to their respective magnifications. Figure 11(a) presents the TEM micrographs of the 5 wt% hybrid film at various magnifications. The hair-like black lines visible in the images represent 1 nm thick clay layers. These TEM images show the uniform dispersion of clay within the polymer matrix, although occasional agglomerations of clay exceeding ~ 10 nm in size were observed. This phenomenon facilitated the intercalation of polymer chains within the clay layers, leading to the formation of polymer-clay nanocomposites.46 In the hybrid containing 10 wt% clay, the clay particles were generally well-dispersed, despite some agglomerations with an average thickness of approximately 15 nm or less (Fig. 11(b)). However, at 20 wt% STN, the excess clay aggregated into larger clusters, approximately 20 nm or more in size (Fig. 11(c)). This indicates that below a critical clay content, the clay remains evenly dispersed within the hybrid. However, above the critical content, significant agglomeration of the clay occurs. This result is consistent with the XRD results shown in Fig. 9.

In conclusion, at a certain critical content, the Co-CPEI hybrid successfully forms a nanocomposite by dispersing the clay at a nanoscale level. As a result, the state of clay dispersion before and after the critical content is expected to have a decisive influence on the thermomechanical properties and optical transparency of the hybrid films, which will be described in the future.

Thermal properties of the Co-CPEI hybrids

The thermal properties of the hybrid films synthesized by dispersing inorganic clay in organic polymers were enhanced owing to the stable thermal characteristics of the clay. This effect can be used to improve the thermal properties of composite materials. Therefore, the thermal properties of the Co-CPEI hybrid films are closely related to the dispersed organoclay content. Table 5 summarizes the thermal properties of the Co-CPEI hybrids with varying STN contents (0 to 40 wt%). As the STN content increases to a certain critical concentration, the Tg value increases; however, beyond this critical concentration, it decreases. For example, when the STN content increased from 0 to 10 wt%, the Tg sharply increased from 172 to 276 ℃. This increase in Tg can be attributed to two main factors: (1) Significant effect of small amounts of dispersed clay layers on the free volume of polymer chains. When clay nanoparticles are inserted between polymer chains, the restricted movement of clay can decrease the free volume and increase Tg. (2) When the movement of polymer chains inserted within the clay galleries is restricted, their segmental motion is hindered. Therefore, Tg of the hybrid is sensitive to the type and content of the dispersed filler. However, Tg decreased to 259 ℃ when the STN content was increased to 40 wt%. This decrease was attributed to the aggregation of excess clay beyond the critical concentration, as confirmed by the XRD, SEM, and TEM results shown in Figs. 9, 10 and 11, respectively. Mechanical properties of Co-CPEI hybrids

The ultimate tensile strength, initial tensile modulus, and EB of the Co-CPEI hybrid films with varying STN contents were measured, and the results are summarized in Table 6. As with the Tg results, the mechanical properties of the Co-CPEI hybrid films exhibited peak values at the critical clay content, but decreased when this limit was exceeded. For example, as the STN content increased from 0 to 10 wt%, the tensile strength of the Co-CPEI hybrid increased from 121 MPa to 171 MPa, and the initial modulus increased from 3.92 GPa to 5.86 GPa. These results indicate significant increases of 41 and 50%, respectively. The improvements in the ultimate tensile strength and initial modulus of the hybrid are not only due to the uniform dispersion of the clay layers at the nanometer scale, but also because of the excellent orientation of the high-aspect-ratio clays and inherent resistance of the clay itself to external forces49.

However, when the organoclay content increased to 40 wt%, the ultimate tensile strength and initial modulus decreased to 86 MPa and 2.78 GPa, respectively (Table 6). This trend was primarily due to the uneven dispersion of the filler particles, which agglomerated when the critical content was exceeded. This phenomenon was confirmed by the XRD and TEM images shown in Figs. 9 and 10, respectively, and similar results have been reported in studies on other hybrids50,51.

The deterioration of not only thermal properties but also mechanical properties beyond the critical clay content can be attributed to a structural transition from a well-exfoliated nanostructure to an aggregated morphology. Once this threshold is exceeded, clay–clay interactions become dominant over polymer–clay interfacial interactions, resulting in restacking, stress concentration, micro-void formation, and reduced load-transfer efficiency. Consequently, the reinforcing effect of the nanofiller weakens, leading to a rapid decline in modulus, strength, and thermal stability.

The EB value of the hybrid film decreased slightly with increasing organoclay content. For example, as the STN content increased from 0 to 40 wt%, the EB decreased from 10% to 4% (Table 6). This result was attributed to the characteristics of the hybrid material reinforced with rigid plate-like inorganic clay materials.

Optical transparency of Co-CPEI hybrids

As the amount of straight, plate-shaped clay dispersed in the polymer matrix increased, the optical transmittance of the hybrid films decreased. This indicates that the transmittance is determined by the dispersed clay content. Because optical transmittance is strongly dependent on film thickness, the measured transmittance values were normalized using the Beer–Lambert relationship to eliminate thickness-dependent attenuation effects. The absorption coefficient (α) was calculated according to:

α = −1/ι ​lnT.

where ι is the film thickness (cm) and T is the transmittance at 500 nm. The normalized transmittance values at a standard thickness of 10 μm were then recalculated for reliable comparison.

The UV-vis. spectra of the hybrid films are shown in Fig. 14, and the results are summarized in Table 7. To minimize errors due to thickness variations, each hybrid film was maintained at a consistent thickness of 49–51 μm, allowing for the comparison of their optical properties under the same conditions. As the STN content in the CPI hybrid increased from 0 to 40 wt%, the λo value increased slightly from 375 nm to 383 nm. This indicates that light was transmitted before reaching the visible range of 400–800 nm, suggesting that the film exhibited good transmittance. However, the 500 nmtrans value of the hybrid films significantly decreased as the organic clay content increased. Specifically, as the STN content was increased from 0 to 40 wt%, the transmittance of the hybrid film decreased sharply from 85% to 38%.

Conclusions

Compared to a homopolymer synthesized using two monomers, a copolymer synthesized with three monomers not only exhibits new properties depending on the composition of the third monomer, but allows for the tailoring of properties by varying the monomer composition. In this study, the diamine p-XDA was used to improve the optical transparency, while the ester-containing anhydrides NATA and HQTA were used to improve the thermomechanical properties. Co-CPEI was synthesized using a constant p-XDA content and varying compositions of NATA and HQTA. The Co-CPEI films synthesized by varying the molar ratio of the two anhydrides to p-XDA (from 0 to 1.0) were colorless, transparent, and flexible. Furthermore, the thermomechanical properties improved with increasing HQTA molar ratio.

Among the synthesized Co-CPEI samples, Co-CPEI with a p-XDA: NATA: HQTA ratio of 1.0:0.50:0.50 (molar ratio) was selected, and hybrid films dispersed with various organoclay STN contents (5–40 wt%) were synthesized. The thermomechanical properties, morphology, and optical transparency of the hybrid films with varying contents of dispersed STN were investigated and compared. The thermomechanical properties of the Co-CPEI hybrid improved up to a specific critical clay content; however, exceeding this content resulted in the deterioration of these properties. In addition, the optical properties deteriorated as the clay content increased.

As demonstrated in this study, high-performance Co-CPEIs with excellent properties for use under various conditions can be synthesized by carefully selecting the monomer structures and reaction conditions. Furthermore, by perfectly dispersing the nanoscale fillers, nanocomposites with significantly enhanced properties compared with conventional composites can be created. These advanced materials have the potential to replace glass and find widespread applications in displays and electronic materials.

Future key technologies include nanoscale thin-film technologies, physicochemical sensor materials, and advanced device technologies, all of which are crucial in materials science. The development of cutting-edge materials using these technologies requires precise patterning and thorough understanding of their physicochemical properties. Therefore, the Co-CPEI and hybrid films prepared in this study offer promising directions for upgrading existing materials.
