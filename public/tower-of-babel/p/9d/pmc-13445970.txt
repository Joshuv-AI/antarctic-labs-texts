SnRK1 in stress adaptation and growth regulation: Insights from Arabidopsis to crops
Authors: Xiong Zhong, Lei Zhang, Chao Han, Mingyi Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For their survival and fitness, organisms must optimize energy utilization and metabolic adaptation in fluctuating environmental conditions. The Sucrose non-fermenting 1 (SNF1)-Related Kinase 1 (SnRK1) protein complex serves as a central regulator of plant energy metabolism, and recent studies have revealed that it is also required to maintain stem cell activity. This review summarizes the multilayered regulatory mechanisms that govern SnRK1 activity and describes recent advances in our understanding of its roles in stress resistance and growth promotion. We integrate findings from the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana and major crops, highlighting conserved mechanisms and species-specific differences. This information will facilitate the use of SnRK1 genetic resources in intelligent crop breeding.

Introduction

To survive and reproduce, organisms must use resources and energy efficiently. Evolution has enhanced oxidative phosphorylation in eukaryotic cells and given rise to an energy-sensing system centered on a conserved family of protein kinase complexes: yeast Sucrose non-fermenting 1 (SNF1), mammalian AMP-activated kinase (AMPK), and plant SNF1-related kinase 1 (SnRK1) [1]. SnRK1 is a heterotrimeric complex composed of α, β and βγ subunits, each contributing to the structure and function of SnRK1. The subunit composition of the SnRK1 complex is conserved in plants, animals, and fungi, and its composition and specific expression is important for its activity and physiological functions. In Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis), the heterotrimeric SnRK1 complex contains a catalytic α subunit (KIN10, KIN11, or KIN12), a regulatory β subunit (KINβ1, KINβ2, or KINβ3), and a βγ subunit (KINβγ).

SnRK1 plays key roles in plant responses to diverse biotic and abiotic stresses in addition to energy stress [2,3], and researchers have identified numerous plant SnRK proteins that respond to energy stress, osmotic stress, and calcium signaling [4]. Moreover, recent studies have revealed that SnRK1 also promotes meristem activity to sustain organogenesis. Thus, SnRK1 appears to exert opposing effects on growth, restricting it under stress but promoting it in meristems under non-stress conditions. This highlights its role in balancing stress tolerance and development. Harnessing SnRK1 genetic resources is therefore a promising strategy for maintaining stable, high crop yields. In this review, we summarize recent research on the roles of SnRK1 in stress resistance and growth promotion and directly compare Arabidopsis and major crops to distinguish conserved regulatory mechanisms from lineage-specific functional outputs.

Regulation of SnRK1 activity

Unlike yeast SNF1 and mammalian AMPK, plant SnRK1 is not regulated by the AMP/ATP ratio, making it an atypical AMPK family member [5]. Instead, plants have evolved unique mechanisms that regulate SnRK1s (Fig. 1). For example, SnRK1 subcellular localization controls its activity. In this mechanism, SnRK1 is active by default: its catalytic subunit KIN10 lacks an autoinhibitory domain and functionally complements a yeast mutant lacking all complex subunits [6]. The regulatory subunits KINβ1 and KINβ2 contain myristoylation sites and associate with membranes, tethering KIN10 to cytoplasmic structures. Multiple regulatory factors control the nuclear localization of KIN10. Stress-induced production of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) promotes KIN10 nuclear accumulation by disrupting the KIN10–KINβ2 interaction [7]. BIN2, a negative regulator of brassinosteroid signaling, phosphorylates KINβ2 at six different residues. The introduction of negatively charged phosphate groups into the variable N-terminal region of KINβ2 inhibits its membrane-binding capacity [8]. In epidermal meristemoids, constitutive BIN2 activation and elevated H2O2 levels cause a diffuse distribution of KINβ2 and nuclear localization of KIN10 [7]. This brassinosteroid-mediated regulation is also involved in nitrogen-induced growth [8]. Abscisic acid (ABA) treatment triggers the export of KIN10 from the nucleus, leading to inhibition of the target of rapamycin (TOR) kinase in the cytoplasm and suppression of primary root growth [9]. Interactome analysis of the SnRK1 complex revealed that class II trehalose-6-phosphate synthases (TPSIIs) directly restrict both the nuclear localization and kinase activity of KIN10 [10]. The nuclear accumulation of KIN10 induced by extended darkness is mediated by the nuclear pore component HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 1 (HOS1). This darkness-induced nuclear localization is significantly suppressed in the hos1 mutant background, accompanied by marked reductions in biomass accumulation and the expression of dark-induced genes. These findings demonstrate that HOS1 is a critical regulator of KIN10 nuclear translocation induced by energy stress [11].

This phenomenon of stress-triggered nuclear SnRK1 accumulation is conserved in crops (Table 1). In rice (Oryza sativa), sugar starvation during early seed imbibition drives nuclear localization of OsSnRK1A (also known as OsSnRK1a, OsSnRK1α, and OsSnRK1.1; we have preserved the nomenclature used in the original papers). Together with the MYBS1 transcription factor, OsSnRK1A induces α-AMYLASE expression and starch mobilization [12]. Conversely, stress-induced SnRK1A-interacting negative regulators (SKINs) promote the cytoplasmic retention of OsSnRK1A and MYBS1, thereby suppressing starch degradation [13]. In maize (Zea mays), the wall-associated kinase ZmWAK directly phosphorylates ZmSnRK1α2 at Ser334 and Ser336, disrupting its interaction with ZmSnRK1β1 and triggering nuclear translocation; this mechanism parallels the BIN2-mediated release of KIN10 from KINβ2 in Arabidopsis [14]. Regulatory mechanisms of SnRK1 activity.

Phosphorylation also plays a key role in regulating SnRK1 activity. The catalytic subunit KIN10 contains a conserved T-loop phosphorylation site (Thr175; Thr176 in KIN11) that is essential for its function, particularly for bZIP-mediated transcription [31]. Structural analyses have shown that AMPK and SnRK1 share a kinase-domain architecture with protein kinase A (PKA) [3,32]. T-loop phosphorylation establishes a hydrogen-bonding network that facilitates kinase-domain closure and proper substrate binding [32]. SnRK1-activating kinase 1 (SnAK1) and SnAK2 catalyze the phosphorylation of a conserved T-loop threonine site [15,16]. The snak1 snak2 double mutant shows impaired development and reduced Thr175 phosphorylation of KIN10 [33]. ABA signaling inhibits type 2C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs) to activate SnRK1, relieving Thr175 dephosphorylation and sustaining SnRK1 signaling during stress and beyond the relief of energy deficiency [17]. In addition, energy stress caused by a deficiency of cytochrome c in the mitochondria enhances Thr175 phosphorylation [18]. In legumes, the receptor kinase DMI2 directly phosphorylates the legume-specific MtSnRK1α4 at the equivalent Thr175 residue; this phosphorylation is essential for nodule formation [22], demonstrating that phosphorylation of the conserved T-loop residue is a universal activation switch that can be wired to different upstream kinases in different species (Table 1). Various sugar phosphates modulate Thr175 phosphorylation and KIN10 activity, with trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) playing a prominent role [19,29,34]. T6P directly binds KIN10, suppressing its catalytic activity [35]. Structural and biochemical studies have revealed that T6P associates with a positively charged region (Lys63–Leu73) of KIN10, causing Thr175 to be positioned away from the catalytic Lys137 of SnAK1 [20]. As a signaling molecule that reflects sucrose status, T6P disrupts the interaction between KIN10 and SnAK1, thereby suppressing Thr175 phosphorylation [21]. The T6P–SnRK1 regulatory module is also functionally conserved in crops. In wheat (Triticum aestivum), the T6P phosphatase TaTPP-7A promotes grain development at least in part by reducing T6P levels and thus enhancing SnRK1 activity [36].

In addition, plants regulate SnRK1 activity by regulating the abundance of complex subunits. For example, KIN10 protein abundance is dynamically modulated in response to the plant's nutritional status. KIN10 protein accumulates under nitrogen deficiency, but KIN10 and phosphorylated KIN10T175 are rapidly degraded after nitrogen resupply [37]. The E3 SUMO ligase SIZ1, which also ubiquitinates nitrate reductase under nitrate-sufficient conditions [38], catalyzes SUMOylation of the SnRK1 complex and promotes degradation of active KIN10 via the 26S proteasome, attenuating SnRK1 signaling [24]. This suggests that SIZ1 may mediate KIN10 degradation upon nitrogen resupply. The E3 ubiquitin ligase HOS1 directly ubiquitinates KIN10, providing evidence for its degradation after nitrogen treatment [25]. In addition, loss of function of myo-inositol polyphosphate 5-phosphatase (5PTase13), a component of inositol phosphate signaling, reduces KIN10 levels under low-nutrient conditions, suggesting that this pathway maintains KIN10 stability during nutrient limitation [27]. Ubiquitin-dependent degradation of SnRK1 is a recurring regulatory theme across species. In wheat, drought induces production of the TaCEP15 peptide, which binds to its receptor TaCEPRL and triggers degradation of TaSnRK1α, thereby suppressing primary root elongation [30]. In rice, the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme OsUBC12 promotes degradation of OsSnRK1.1 to restrict seed germination under cold conditions [28]. These examples show that controlled proteolysis of SnRK1 is a conserved mechanism for integrating environmental signals, although the specific E3 ligases and cues vary among species (Table 1).

Monitoring dynamic and tissue-specific SnRK1 activity in planta is crucial for understanding its regulation. To this end, a reporter system was recently developed by fusing an ACC (acetyl-CoA carboxylase, a specific AMPK substrate)-derived short peptide containing an SnRK1-specific phosphorylation site with green fluorescent protein (GFP). Phosphorylation of this ACC-GFP peptide was then used to assess endogenous SnRK1 activity [39]. Introduction of this reporter into an Arabidopsis line with an inducible TPS1 construct enabled researchers to confirm the negative effect of elevated T6P levels on SnRK1 activity. More recently, the SPARK (separation of phases-based activity reporter of kinase) technique was adapted for use in plants. Upon phosphorylation by endogenous SnRK1, the FORKHEAD-ASSOCIATED DOMAIN 1 (FHA1) protein binds the phosphorylated AMPK-specific substrate short peptide (ASP), triggering phase separation and forming visible condensates that mark SnRK1 activity in specific tissues or cell types [40]. Analysis of ASP-SPARK transgenic lines revealed that prolonged darkness rapidly induced SnRK1 activity in the root elongation zone, and nitrogen starvation maintained high SnRK1 activity in shoots and roots [40,41]. This research tool can capture dynamic, tissue-specific changes in SnRK1 activity, providing a valuable approach for future investigations.

SnRK1 acts as a central metabolic regulator under energy stress

Conditions such as extended darkness or hypoxia cause energy stress in plants. Extensive work in Arabidopsis has shown that upon activation in response to energy stress, SnRK1 orchestrates downstream responses through two mechanisms. First, it directly modulates the activities of metabolic enzymes through phosphorylation. When carbon reserves become depleted under conditions of extended darkness or reduced photosynthesis, SnRK1 maintains carbohydrate availability through multiple mechanisms. It directly phosphorylates and inhibits sucrose phosphate synthase, reducing sucrose export to preserve source tissues [44], and it suppresses expression of the sucrose transporter gene SUC1 via bZIP transcription factors [31]. SnRK1 also modulates gluconeogenesis. Work in Arabidopsis showed that it phosphorylates fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase (F2KP) at Ser306, enabling 14-3-3 binding and thereby inhibiting F2KP activity [45]. F2KP interconverts fructose-6-phosphate and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate [46], and biochemical analyses coupled with metabolite measurements have demonstrated that activation of SnRK1 by 2-deoxyglucose reduces fructose-2,6-bisphosphate levels [45]. Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate allosterically inhibits cytosolic fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (cyFBPase), which catalyzes the conversion of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate to fructose-6-phosphate [46,47]. Silencing of F2KP reduces fructose-2,6-bisphosphate levels and enhances sucrose production [48]. Thus, SnRK1 reduces the level of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate to maintain the pool of gluconeogenic intermediates. Finally, SnRK1 transcriptionally activates starch degradation genes, including β-AMYLASE 9 (BAM9) [31]. Silencing of KIN11 in the kin10 background (d1 mutant) causes excess starch accumulation, indicating that SnRK1 promotes starch degradation [31]. Although BAM9 lacks measurable hydrolase activity, the bam9 mutation exacerbates the excess starch and reduced maltose levels of the bam1 bam3 mutant [49], suggesting that SnRK1 may enhance starch mobilization by promoting BAM9 expression.

Under energy-limited conditions, SnRK1 inhibits carbon-consuming biological processes to conserve carbon resources. For example, 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGR) catalyzes the first committed step of the cytosolic isoprenoid and sterol biosynthetic pathway in plants [50], and in Arabidopsis, SnRK1 phosphorylates HMGR at Ser577, reducing its enzymatic activity [51]. Phenylpropanoid metabolism is another carbon-consuming process, channeling approximately 30–40% of photosynthetically fixed carbon toward downstream products, including the heterogeneously cross-linked biopolymer lignin. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) is the first and rate-limiting step in phenylpropanoid metabolism [52], and SnRK1 restricts carbon flow toward lignin synthesis by promoting the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of PAL [53].

To compensate for carbohydrate deficiency under energy stress, plants enhance protein degradation and the utilization of free amino acids via SnRK1. Amino acids enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to provide energy and carbon skeletons when carbohydrates are depleted. Autophagy is a conserved eukaryotic pathway for protein degradation and recycling, capable of degrading cellular components, eliminating protein aggregates and damaged organelles, and supplementing energy reserves. In Arabidopsis, the autophagy-related (ATG) proteins ATG1, ATG13, ATG11, and ATG101 mediate the initiation of autophagy [54], and KIN10 promotes constitutive autophagy and induces ATG1-mediated autophagy under carbon starvation [55]. KIN10 directly phosphorylates ATG6 to initiate autophagosome formation [56] and phosphorylates FYVE DOMAIN PROTEIN REQUIRED FOR ENDOSOMAL SORTING 1 (FREE1) to facilitate autophagosome closure [57]. Mitochondrially localized FCS-like zinc finger (FLZ) proteins interact with ATG8 and suppress the T-loop phosphorylation of KIN10. Under nutrient deprivation, FLZ proteins are selectively degraded via autophagy, thereby feedback-activating KIN10 [23].

Amino acids generated by autophagy become crucial energy sources for plants under energy stress. SnRK1 activates the catabolism of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which can serve as alternative electron donors and carbon skeletons [58]. BCAA catabolism involves a series of shared metabolic enzymes: branched-chain amino acid transaminase 2 (BCAT2), branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex (BCKDH), isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase (IVDH), and methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCCA/B), ultimately generating acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate [59]. Electron-transfer flavoprotein:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (ETFQO) then passes electrons to the ubiquinone pool of the electron transport chain [60,61]. Work in Arabidopsis showed that SnRK1 activates the expression of the genes encoding all key components in BCAA catabolism, including BCAT2, BCKDH, IVDH, and MCCA/B [6,31,58]. Through C/S1 bZIP transcription factor heterodimers, SnRK1 directly induces the expression of ETFQO, enabling plants to maintain mitochondrial energy supply during prolonged darkness and prevent premature energy depletion [58,62]. Accordingly, mutations in these metabolic enzymes and electron carriers cause accelerated etiolation under extended darkness [59], demonstrating that BCAA catabolism controlled by SnRK1 provides supplemental energy under stress.

The expression of DIN6, one of numerous downstream genes activated by SnRK1, has been used as a readout for SnRK1 activity [6,31]. DIN6 encodes asparagine synthetase 1 (ASN1), which controls asparagine synthesis [63]. In Arabidopsis, ASN1 expression is induced specifically in ovules during the early stages of pollination and is accompanied by increases in asparagine and total amino acid levels. Immunolocalization revealed that ASN1 expression was restricted to vascular cells of the silique wall and septum, as well as the outer and inner integuments, suggesting that asparagine is transported into developing embryos by post-phloem pathways. Overexpression of ASN1 in Arabidopsis increased seed protein content and enhanced tolerance to nitrogen deficiency [64]. These observations indicate that although SnRK1 suppresses sucrose synthesis and export, it may simultaneously facilitate nutrient supply to sink tissues by enhancing organic nitrogen transport. Consistent with this scenario, SnRK1 induces the expression of CAT6, which encodes a chloroplast-localized cationic amino acid transporter [31] that preferentially transports large, neutral, and cationic amino acids [65]. The SnRK1-mediated induction of CAT6 expression provides evidence for its role in promoting amino acid export and inter-organ allocation.

SnRK1-mediated transcriptional regulation under energy stress is also conserved in crop species. In rice, dark-induced energy deprivation reduces chlorophyll content and promotes the nuclear entry of OsSnRK1A. OsSnRK1A phosphorylates the histone demethylase JMJ705 at Ser76, reducing genome-wide H3K27me3 levels, including at genes encoding energy-mobilization enzymes such as amylase, cellulase, and glycosyl hydrolases [66].

SnRK1 suppresses nitrogen assimilation and signaling

Plants acquire carbon by photosynthetic CO2 fixation and nitrogen primarily through nitrate uptake from soil. Maintaining the balance between these two essential elements is critical for plant growth and development [67]. Insufficient light depletes carbon reserves and activates SnRK1, which inhibits nitrogen metabolism to maintain the carbon–nitrogen balance. (Fig. 3). Nitrate is reduced to nitrite by nitrate reductase (NR), then to ammonium by nitrite reductase (NIR), and incorporated into glutamate by glutamine synthetase (GS) [68]. This modification facilitates the binding of 14-3-3 proteins, which allosterically inhibit NR enzymatic activity, thereby restricting the primary metabolic flux from nitrate (NO3−) to amino acids. To prioritize survival under energy stress, KIN10 overrides the nitrate signaling cascade by directly interacting with and phosphorylating NLP7 at Ser125 and Ser306. This multi-site phosphorylation leads to the cytoplasmic retention and subsequent degradation of NLP7, repressing its transcriptional growth responses. Nitrogen resupply rapidly increases accumulation of the E3 ubiquitin ligase HOS1. HOS1 directly polyubiquitinates KIN10, targeting the kinase for degradation by the 26S proteasome pathway. Nitrate signaling enables plants to perceive nitrate availability and initiate transcriptional reprogramming, inducing the expression of genes required for nitrogen metabolism and nitrate-stimulated growth [69]. Nitrate signaling relies on the sensor NRT1.1 [70]; upon nitrate stimulation, NRT1.1 interacts with cyclic nucleotide-gated channel 15 (CNGC15), triggering an influx of calcium that activates calcium-dependent protein kinases (CPKs). CPKs phosphorylate NIN-LIKE PROTEIN 7 (NLP7) at Ser205 [71,72], which translocates to the nucleus and activates primary nitrate-responsive genes [69,73]. SnRK1 suppresses nitrate-stimulated plant growth and gene expression. Nitrate-induced plant growth is reduced under low light or upon treatment with the Photosystem II inhibitor 3-(3,4-dichlorophenyl)-1,1-dimethylurea (DCMU), but this effect is partially alleviated in kin10 mutants. Overexpression of KIN10 caused 81.1% of NLP7-regulated genes and 34.5% of primary nitrate-responsive genes to become unresponsive to nitrate. KIN10 directly phosphorylates NLP7 at Ser125 and Ser306, promoting its cytoplasmic retention and degradation [37], and the SnRK1–NLP7 module attenuates nitrate signaling under light-limited conditions.

Nitrogen status reciprocally regulates SnRK1 activity. Resupplying nitrogen after nitrogen starvation triggers the rapid degradation of KIN10 protein through the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy pathways [37]. Upon nitrogen treatment, the E3 ligase HOS1 directly ubiquitinates KIN10. When nitrogen is resupplied, HOS1 levels rise, which in turn promotes KIN10 degradation and NLP7 accumulation. As a result, HOS1 enhances nitrate-induced gene expression and lateral root development [25].

SnRK1 strengthens plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses

In addition to its roles during energy stress, SnRK1 also facilitates plant survival under submergence and salinity (Fig. 4). Overexpression of KIN10T175A and KIN10K48M significantly decreases plant survival rates following submergence [74]. KIN10 phosphorylates eIFiso4G, enhancing the translation of stress-responsive genes such as those encoding alcohol dehydrogenase and pyruvate decarboxylase, thereby improving post-submergence survival [75]. KIN10 also phosphorylates the spliceosome protein SRRM1L, thus regulating the alternative splicing of pre-mRNAs to generate functional stress-responsive proteins that are essential for survival under saline conditions. The KIN10–SRRM1L module promotes expression of the functional NFYA10.1 variant and suppresses that of the non-functional NFYA10.3 variant under salt stress [76]. SnRK1 enhances abiotic stress tolerance in crops (Fig. 4). For example, overexpression of TaSnRK1α improves drought survival and root growth in wheat. Drought induces production of the TaCEP15 peptide, which binds to the receptor-like kinase TaCEPRL that phosphorylates TaSnRK1α. This phosphorylation promotes the degradation of TaSnRK1α, suppresses primary root elongation, and increases seedling mortality [30]. TaSnRK1α overexpression also alleviates cold damage, whereas CRISPR knockout lines are hypersensitive to cold. TaSnRK1α phosphorylates TaPAP6L-2B and promotes its accumulation. TaPAP6L encodes a plastid-lipid-associated protein whose overexpression increases jasmonic acid content, likely by promoting α-linolenic acid synthesis, thereby enhancing cold tolerance in wheat [79]. In rice, the E3 ligase OsDSK2a promotes degradation of the gibberellic acid (GA)-inactivating enzyme ELONGATED UPPERMOST INTERNODE (EUI). Salt stress reduces OsDSK2a levels, enabling EUI accumulation, lowering GA levels, and suppressing growth [80]. OsSnRK1A phosphorylates OsDSK2a at Ser2, triggering its degradation, and a natural Ser2Gly variant is associated with reduced height and increased yield. Near-isogenic lines carrying this allele show improved salt tolerance [81].

SnRK1 also plays a critical role in plant defense against pathogens (Fig. 5). Geminiviruses, a family of single-stranded DNA viruses, infect diverse plant species and substantially reduce crop yields worldwide. AL2 proteins from Tomato golden mosaic virus (TGMV) and Beet curly top virus (BCTV), which are pathogenicity determinants, interact with the SnRK1 kinase domain and inhibit SnRK1 kinase activity, suggesting that geminiviruses may interfere with the plant defense system by blocking SnRK1 [82]. SnRK1 phosphorylates the geminivirus replication initiation protein (Rep) of TGMV at Ser97, impairing Rep binding to viral DNA and thus limiting replication. Nearly all geminivirus Reps have a corresponding serine, implying that SnRK1 may mediate broad-spectrum resistance [83]. SnRK1 also phosphorylates recombinant AL2/C2 proteins from various begomoviruses at Ser109, and the phosphomimetic S109D mutation in Cabbage Leaf Curl Virus (CaLCuV) AL2 suppresses viral DNA accumulation and symptom development in Arabidopsis [84]. Furthermore, SnRK1 contributes to plant defense against bacterial and fungal pathogens. The function of SnRK1 in pathogen defense is broadly conserved in crops (Fig. 5). The Fusarium graminearum effector Osp24 targets TaSnRK1α for degradation in wheat, and overexpression of TaSnRK1α can therefore improve Fusarium head blight resistance [86]. In barley (Hordeum vulgare), SnRK1 phosphorylates and degrades the susceptibility factor WRKY3, improving powdery mildew resistance [87]. Rice OsSnRK1A confers broad-spectrum fungal resistance by regulating salicylic acid metabolism [88], and OsUbc13 mediates K63-linked polyubiquitination of OsSnRK1A to enhance its activity against blast and bacterial blight [26]. In maize, overexpression of ZmSnRK1α2 increases resistance to head smut. After ZmWAK-dependent nuclear translocation, ZmSnRK1α2 phosphorylates and degrades ZmWRKY53 to reduce the nutrient supply to the pathogen [14]. A recent study showed that the SnRK1β1A subunit is stabilized and translocated to the nucleus by the fungal effector Gas2, inhibiting SnRK1α1 activity and promoting susceptibility. Accordingly, knockout of SnRK1β1A confers robust broad-spectrum resistance [89]. These examples demonstrate that SnRK1 operates as a unified defense hub across angiosperms, although the upstream receptors, E3 ligases, and downstream substrates have undergone species-specific diversification, reflecting the evolutionary wiring of a conserved kinase into distinct stress-response networks.

SnRK1 maintains plant stem cell activity and promotes organogenesis

Organogenesis depends on the activities of stem cells in the shoot and root apical meristems. The kin10 kin11 double mutant exhibits embryonic mortality, suggesting that SnRK1 is indispensable for plant organogenesis [31]. SnRK1 maintains meristem activity and promotes seedling establishment, stomatal formation, and delayed flowering. However, stress conditions that activate SnRK1 also suppress meristem activity and inhibit plant growth. This apparent contradiction regarding the role of SnRK1 in meristem activity may be explained by two non-mutually exclusive possibilities. First, pleiotropic effects arising from ectopic expression of different KIN10 variants under strong promoters may produce phenotypes that do not faithfully reflect the endogenous function of SnRK1. Second, SnRK1 activation inherently alters cellular metabolic homeostasis—including changes in energy status, sugar levels, and carbon/nitrogen balance—which may indirectly interfere with meristem function.

SnRK1 promotes seedling establishment (Fig. 6). The d1 mutant exhibits dwarfism and premature senescence before flowering [31], and inducible RNA interference lines in which KIN10 and KIN11 were suppressed (i-amiR-SnRK1) showed impaired seedling growth [90]. Metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed that SnRK1 drives gluconeogenesis from stored triacylglycerols and proteins to fuel seedling growth [90]. SnRK1 also phosphorylates and activates bZIP63, which increases pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase (cyPPDK) expression for protein mobilization [62,90] and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1) expression for oil mobilization [31,91]. SnRK1 is also required for skotomorphogenesis (hypocotyl elongation in the dark) and for the transition to photomorphogenesis (cotyledon opening), and SnRK1 knockdown produces phenotypes resembling those of TOR interference lines. TOR and SnRK1 regulate light- and sugar-controlled alternative splicing in a similar manner to promote photomorphogenesis [92]. The same energetic logic operates during rice germination. OsSnRK1A promotes seedling establishment via MYBS1-dependent induction of αAMY3 expression [12]. In contrast to its positive role in apical meristems, SnRK1 suppresses lateral root initiation and elongation in Arabidopsis (Fig. 7), and kin10 and kin11 mutants exhibit a higher density of lateral root initiation sites. When a CRISPR-based tissue-specific knockout system (CRISPR-TSKO) was used to disrupt KIN10 expression specifically in pericycle founder cells at the early stage of lateral root initiation, lateral root branching density increased, confirming the suppressive role of SnRK1 [95]. SnRK1 also inhibits nitrogen-induced lateral root elongation. The kin10 mutant displays greater total lateral root length under nitrogen-sufficient conditions, resulting from KIN10-mediated suppression of NLP7 protein accumulation and expression of ANR1, a primary nitrate-responsive gene that encodes a MADS-box transcription factor [37,96]. By contrast, reduced sugar content under unexpected darkness activates SnRK1, which in turn promotes lateral root initiation. bZIP63, activated by SnRK1, is highly expressed at lateral root initiation sites, where it directly binds to the ARF19 promoter and induces its expression, thereby facilitating lateral root formation under sudden dark conditions [97].

Stomatal traits, including aperture, density, and patterning, are critical determinants of photosynthetic efficiency and transpiration [98], and SnRK1 plays a role in shaping these traits (Fig. 7). Stomatal development in Arabidopsis initiates from meristemoid mother cells (MMCs), which undergo asymmetric division to produce a smaller meristemoid and a larger stomatal lineage ground cell (SLGC) [99,100]. The transcription factor SPEECHLESS (SPCH) maintains the division capacity of MMCs and meristemoids [101]. After undergoing a certain number of asymmetric divisions, meristemoids differentiate into guard mother cells and then into stomata, a process during which SPCH-mediated gene regulatory networks give way to those governed by MUTE [101,102]. KIN10 promotes stomatal production in response to exogenous sucrose by interacting with SPCH and phosphorylating four N-terminal residues, enhancing SPCH stability and stomatal differentiation [103]. During early seedling development, sugar activates TOR, which promotes SPCH expression via the TOR–E2Fa/E2Fb module, maintaining the stomatal lineage. Appropriate sugar levels also favor KIN10 accumulation, enhancing SPCH stability. However, excessive sugar increases the level of T6P, which interferes with the KIN10–SnAK1/2 interaction and suppresses T-loop phosphorylation. Consequently, high sugar contents limit stomatal differentiation while promoting continued cell division [21]. This epidermal developmental process in Arabidopsis reveals how nutrient availability affects the balance between meristematic division and differentiation. KIN10 localizes to the nucleus in meristemoids but remains in the cytoplasm of SLGCs under normal growth conditions [7,8]. The kin10 kin11 sesquimutant and myrKIN10-flag kin10 kin11 transgenic lines have a reduced stomatal index and produce more epidermal cells. These defects arise from compromised nuclear localization of KIN10, which delays meristemoid differentiation and causes extra divisions [8]. The nuclear localization of KIN10 depends on the binding of KINβ2 to membranes. In meristemoids, BIN2 localizes to the cytoplasm and phosphorylates KINβ2. By inhibiting KINβ2 membrane anchorage, this phosphorylation enables the nuclear accumulation of KIN10, which in turn promotes differentiation [8,104]. In guard cells, H2O2 disrupts the KIN10–KINβ2 interaction, allowing KIN10 to enter the nucleus. KIN10 then phosphorylates bZIP30, thus upregulating expression of guard-cell-specific β-amylase 1 (BAM1) and α-amylase 3 (AMY3), which promote starch degradation and stomatal opening [105]. TOR also enhances BAM1 stability, further promoting stomatal opening [106]. Together, these findings show that the cell-type-specific regulation of SnRK1 subcellular localization promotes both stomatal development and opening, thereby enhancing carbon acquisition.

Flowering represents a critical physiological transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. It is significantly delayed in KIN10-overexpressing plants but occurs earlier in the kin10 and kin11 single mutants [31,107] (Fig. 7). The SnRK1-mediated regulation of flowering time is closely associated with T6P signaling. T6P-defective mutants show delayed flowering, and T6P accumulates in the shoot apical meristem in proportion to sucrose, promoting flowering [108]. SnRK1 suppresses flowering by phosphorylating the transcription factor INDETERMINATE DOMAIN 8 (IDD8) at Thr98, Ser178, and Ser182, thereby suppressing its transcriptional activity [107]. IDD8 binds to the promoters of SUC4 and SUC1 to promote their expression and increase sucrose availability in meristems, facilitating flowering [109]. Genetic studies have demonstrated that nonsynonymous amino acid substitutions in the catalytic KIN10 (KIN10G162D, KIN10G178R, KIN10R259Q) and regulatory KINβγ (KINβγA481T, KINβγV449M) subunits of SnRK1 rescue the non-flowering phenotype of the tps1 GVG::TPS1 mutant, indicating that SnRK1 acts downstream of T6P signaling to suppress flowering [110]. Although the precise mechanism by which SnRK1 inhibits flowering remains unclear, TOR-kinase-mediated flowering regulation offers a plausible explanation. TOR kinase, which is negatively regulated by SnRK1, phosphorylates FERTILIZATION-INDEPENDENT ENDOSPERM (FIE) to promote H3K27me3 deposition mediated by Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2), thus silencing the floral repressor FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) [111].

SnRK1 has various effects on grain development in crops, revealing species-specific effects of this conserved kinase on yield-related traits (Fig. 6). In rice, snrk1a-1 and snrk1a-2 mutants show increased photosynthesis and reduced levels of sucrose and non-structural carbohydrates in flag leaves, indicating greater nutrient mobilization to grains; this indicates that SnRK1 negatively regulates grain filling [112]. Similarly, in maize, ZmSnRK1a1 reduces kernel length, area, weight, starch levels, and zein accumulation by phosphorylating and destabilizing Opaque2 (O2), a master bZIP transcription factor for zein and starch synthesis [113], and by degrading the E3 ligase ZmRFWD3 to suppress the nuclear localization of O2 [114]. By contrast, SnRK1 positively regulates grain development in wheat. Overexpression of TaSnRK1α1 increases grain length and pericarp-cell elongation by phosphorylating and degrading the growth repressor TabHLH489 [115], and TaTPP-7A promotes grain development through T6P catabolism, which enhances SnRK1 activity [36]. These opposite effects illustrate how the same kinase can be coupled to distinct downstream pathways to address different metabolic priorities in different crop lineages.

In legumes, SnRK1 has been co-opted for symbiotic organogenesis. For example, the legume-specific MtSnRK1α4 is induced during nodulation and positively regulates nodule formation in Medicago truncatula. The phosphorylation of MtSnRK1α4 at Thr175 mediated by DMI2 activates malate dehydrogenase MDH1, increasing malate supply to the rhizobia [22]. In soybean, the nodulation-receptor component GmNFR5 was identified as a GmSnRK1 interactor in yeast two-hybrid screens, suggesting potential SnRK1 regulation of nodulation [116,117].

Conclusions and perspectives

SnRK1 is a conserved energy regulator with two functions: promoting stress resistance and supporting growth. Under stress, it reprograms metabolism and restricts developmental factors (e.g., STM, E2Fa); under favorable conditions, it maintains meristem activity and promotes stomatal differentiation. Cross-species comparisons have revealed that the core regulatory layers, including T-loop phosphorylation, nucleocytoplasmic partitioning, and T6P-mediated inhibition, are conserved in plants, animals, and fungi, but the physiological outputs differ markedly in specific crop contexts. Tissue-specific studies will clarify the direct versus indirect roles of SnRK1 in balancing development and stress tolerance.

In crops, SnRK1 homologs generally enhance resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses, but their effects on agronomic traits differ among species (Table 2). For instance, SnRK1 suppresses grain size in maize and rice but promotes it in wheat. In addition, SnRK1 restricts nodulation in some contexts despite being essential for nodule formation in legumes. These functional disparities may arise from natural variation in the architecture of the SnRK1 complex among species or from different metabolic demands among crops. Future agricultural research should therefore focus on the structural variation among SnRK1 complexes and their substrate-recruitment specificity. This will help clarify how metabolic demands have driven natural variation in SnRK1 across species, potentially reflecting evolutionary selection for adaptation to specific environments.
