Urine Epidermal Growth Factor/Creatinine Ratio in Female Patients with Overactive Bladder and Recurrent Lower Urinary Tract Infection
Authors: Tsai-Hwa Yang, Chih-Cheng Chen, Yu-Min Chou, Jyh-Ming Liu, Kuan-Hui Huang, Fei-Chi Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:The purpose of this study was to determine if urine epidermal growth factor (EGF) levels can be used to distinguish between overactive bladder (OAB) and recurrent lower urinary tract infection (UTI).Materials and Methods:This was a prospective study conducted at a single tertiary medical center, using medical records and biospecimens collected from premenopausal women with OAB, recurrent lower UTI, and healthy controls without urinary tract conditions. Patients with OAB were treated with either mirabegron (Betmiga®) or solifenacin succinate (Vesicare®) for 3 months. Urinary EGF and creatinine levels were measured at baseline and posttreatment. Symptom changes were assessed using voiding diaries and the OAB Symptom Score (OABSS) questionnaire.Results:A total of 61 women were included in the study: 21 with OAB, 20 with recurrent lower UTI, and 20 healthy control women. There was no significant difference in the urine EGF/creatinine ratio between the OAB, recurrent lower UTI, and control groups (3212.7 [2592.2, 3909.6] and 3362.3 [2374.7, 4632.3] vs. 3402.3 [2700.1, 4568.9], P = 0.895). Both medications were effective in relieving symptoms of OAB, but solifenacin was associated with a significantly higher rate of dry mouth (60% vs. 0%, P = 0.045).Conclusion:No significant difference in urine EGF/creatinine ratio was observed between control subjects and patients with OAB or recurrent lower UTI. Thus, urine EGF is not likely a useful marker for distinguishing between OAB and recurrent lower UTI.

INTRODUCTION

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a common problem characterized by urinary urgency with or without urge incontinence, increased urinary frequency (voiding 8 or more times in a 24-h period), and nocturia (awakening 2 or more times at night to void).[1] It can cause depression and sleep disturbance, has a negative impact on physical, social, and psychological well-being, and interferes with daily activities.[1] Studies have also found that the incidence of falls and fractures is increased among the elderly with OAB.[1234] The symptoms of OAB are not only very bothersome but also have detrimental effects on many aspects of a person’s life.

Histological investigation of the bladder urothelium and suburothelium has shown that chronic inflammation is presented in most patients with OAB.[5] Overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines is found in the urine of patients with OAB, and urinary pro-inflammatory cytokine levels become higher when OAB is more severe.[678] While the pathophysiology of OAB is complicated, chronic inflammation is likely at least part of the cause.[9] Initial treatment of OAB includes lifestyle changes, behavioral modifications, bladder retraining, and medications such as anti-cholinergic drugs and beta 3-adrenergic receptor agonists.[35] However, approximately 40% of patients either do not achieve an acceptable level of therapeutic benefit or remain completely refractory to treatment.[135]

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are relatively common and are associated with inflammation of the urinary tract.[101112] Patients with UTIs sometimes present with increased urinary frequency, dysuria, urgency, voiding difficulty, or abdominal pain, and these symptoms might be mistaken for OAB. Therefore, finding a biomarker to differentiate OAB from UTI can help make a diagnosis and guide treatment. Moreover, lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) – traditionally considered a localized bladder and urethral condition – are not only a urological nuisance – they are an independent predictor of increased healthcare burden.[13] As global populations age, the prevalence of LUTS will rise in parallel with age-related comorbidities, amplifying demands on primary care, geriatrics, neurology, cardiology, and psychiatry.[13]

Epidermal growth factor (EGF) is a mitogenic peptide that stimulates the proliferation of epithelial cells and fibroblasts. Previous studies have reported significantly elevated urinary EGF levels in patients with interstitial cystitis (IC) compared to healthy controls.[14151617] In contrast, increased levels of inflammatory cytokines have also been observed in the urine of patients with OAB, particularly in those with more severe symptoms.[818] EGF has been proposed as a biomarker for IC and for monitoring treatment response in IC patients.[161819] While OAB and IC may share some overlapping symptoms, they are clinically and pathophysiologically distinct disorders based on current guidelines and consensus statements.

This study aimed to investigate whether urine EGF levels can be used as a biomarker for OAB and distinguishing between OAB from recurrent lower UTI. We also evaluated the effects of mirabegron (Betmiga®) and solifenacin succinate (Vesicare®) on treating OAB symptoms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patient selection criteria

This was a prospective study conducted at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. Clinical data and urine samples were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records and biorepository. Eligible patients were premenopausal females diagnosed with OAB or recurrent bacterial cystitis. Inclusion criteria were age 20 years or older, premenopausal status, and no use of anticholinergic medications for at least 3 months. Exclusion criteria included treatable genitourinary conditions that could cause incontinence or hematuria, obstructive uropathy or acute UTI, neurogenic bladder or stress urinary incontinence, renal pathology, a history of urothelial carcinoma or prior pelvic radiation, receipt of a botulinum toxin injection in the bladder within the past 2 weeks, and a diagnosis of IC or bladder pain syndrome (BPS). IC/BPS was defined as chronic bladder-related pain or discomfort without infection or other identifiable causes, with or without Hunner lesions, consistent with the European Society for the Study of IC criteria.[20]

The diagnosis of OAB was made based on clinical symptoms of urgency, or urgency incontinence, and further confirmed by a 3-day voiding diary. Recurrent UTI was defined as more than 2 episodes in 6 months, or more than 3 episodes in 1 year.[21] Patients in the OAB group were given the option to receive either 5 mg of solifenacin succinate or 50 mg of mirabegron for 3 months. Physicians provided guidance on drug selection based on potential side effects. Solifenacin may cause parasympathetic side effects such as constipation and dry eyes, whereas mirabegron is contraindicated in patients with poorly controlled hypertension. Patients with constipation and dry eyes were advised to take mirabegron, whereas those with uncontrolled hypertension were recommended solifenacin.

Control subjects were recruited from healthy hospital employees who had no urinary or systemic diseases, and who had no LUTS.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulations. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (approval number: 201901419B0). All patients provided written informed consent for study participation.

Urine sample collection

Clean-catch midstream urine samples were obtained from all participants prior to initiation of medical treatment. Immediately after collection, the samples were placed on ice, then centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 4000 rpm to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis. Urine aliquots of 3 mL were used for the measurement of urinary creatinine concentrations.

Luminex assays

Urinary EGF levels were measured using the Luminex™ xMAP® system (Millipore, Billerica, MA) according to the manufacture’s instruction. Urine samples were centrifuged at 4°C at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and diluted 1:1 in a bead mix and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing 2 times with wash buffer, detection antibody (MILLIPLEX® Human Cytokine/Chemokine/Growth Factor Panel A kit, HCYTA-60K, Millipore, Billerica, MA) was added, and the sample was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Streptavidin-phycoerythrin was added, and the sample was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After washing twice with wash buffer, the reading buffer was added, and the sample was shaken at room temperature for 5 min. Then, the plate of the Luminex 200 system was loaded for reading. Overall, the Luminex assay required approximately 6–7 h for the short incubation protocol and up to ~20 h when the overnight incubation option was used. The total urinary EGF level was normalized to the urinary creatine level.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was EGF/creatinine ratio before treatment. The secondary outcomes were voiding diary measures, severity of OAB symptoms before and after the 3-month treatment. The voiding diary measures included daytime frequency, nocturnal frequency, urgency, urge urinary incontinence, and voiding volume averaged over 3 consecutive days. The severity of OAB symptoms was evaluated with the OABSS and Urgency Severity Score questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as the median and interquartile range 25th–75th percentile and compared with the Kruskal–Wallis test because of a non-normal distribution. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentage and compared by Fisher’s exact test. In the OAB group, outcomes regarding bladder diary parameters at baseline and a 3-month follow-up were analyzed using the Wilcoxon sign-rank test, and the comparison between different medication use was examined using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. A 2-sided P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp. in Armonk, NY).

DISCUSSION

The present study showed no significant difference in the urine EGF/creatinine ratio between the OAB, recurrent lower UTI, and control groups. Besides, both mirabegron and solifenacin exhibited comparable effectiveness in treating OAB; however, regarding side effects, solifenacin was associated with a significantly higher rate of dry mouth.

Our results showed that the urine EGF/creatinine ratio was similar between the 3 groups. Khan et al.[22] reported no significant difference in urine EGF/creatinine ratio between 73 patients with OAB and 74 healthy controls; however, Tyagi et al.[8] reported a 5-fold increase the urine EGF/creatinine ratio in 17 patients with OAB compared to 8 health controls, a relatively small sample size may account for the difference.

UTI and OAB share many common symptoms and frequently occur together in the same patient.[23] Thus, differentiating between the 2 conditions is important as treatments are different. Symptoms of a UTI are typically acute and include dysuria and hematuria, whereas these are not seen on OAB.[23] OAB is generally a chronic condition and is characterized by urgency, frequency, and nocturia; dysuria and hematuria are not symptoms of OAB.[23] Because of the overlap of symptoms, it is typically recommended to rely on urine culture to distinguish UTI and OAB.[23] For example, Ke et al.[24] reported a difference in the urinary microbiome between women with UTI and OAB. In a somewhat similar study, Khan et al.[22] compared urine culture results between women with OAB and healthy control women. The results showed similar culture results on routine culture, but a significantly higher positive culture rate in women with OAB when enhanced culture was performed. Notably, the authors also reported no difference in the levels of 10 different cytokines, chemokines, and prostaglandins between the 2 groups.

Many attempts have been made to identify reliable biomarkers distinguishing between UTI and OAB.[2526] A pilot study by Tyagi et al.[8] reported significantly higher levels of markers including monocyte chemotactic protein-1, soluble fraction of the CD40 ligand, macrophage inflammatory protein-1β, interleukin, and EGF in the urine of women with OAB compared with healthy controls. Kim et al.[18] studied urine levels of nerve growth factor (NGF) and heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HB-EGF) in women with OAB who were treated with fesoterodine and healthy control women. At baseline, the levels of NGF and HB-EGF were significantly higher in women with OAB compared to the control women. After 16 weeks of treatment, the levels of the 2 biomarkers were significantly decreased; however, there was no correlation between the levels and symptom scores.

Similarly, attempts have been made to identify markers that can reliably diagnose UTI, as culture results can be falsely negative in many cases.[2728] Studies have suggested that serum levels of macrophage colony-stimulating factor and urinary NGF may hold value for predicting recurrent UTI; however, their clinical usefulness has yet to be shown.[28] Markers examined for diagnosis of UTI include heparin-binding protein, heat-shock protein-70, and human defensin-5, among others.[28] Again, their clinical usefulness has yet to be shown.

While UTIs are treated with antibiotics, OAB is typically treated with medications to relax the bladder muscles.[29] Drugs used include antimuscarinics (e.g., solifenacin), β3-adrenoceptor agonists (e.g., mirabegron), intradetrusor injections of onabotulinumtoxinA, and topical vaginal estrogen cream.[29] Overall, the treatments are safe with few significant adverse effects; however, it is difficult to predict which treatment will be the most effective for individual patients.[29] Wang et al.[30] performed a meta-analysis comparing the effectiveness of solifenacin and mirabegron for the treatment of OAB. Compared with solifenacin, mirabegron was associated with a lower incidence of drug-related treatment-emergent adverse events and dry mouth.[30] Hypertension and tachycardia rates did not differ significantly; however, pulse rate did.[30]

In addition, women with pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) frequently experience LUTS. PFD is a broad term encompassing pelvic organ prolapse (POP), urinary incontinence, fecal incontinence, and myofascial pelvic pain. The comprehensive review by Hong and Ding[31] summarized current treatment options for these conditions, ranging from conservative approaches to advanced surgical techniques. In contrast, the retrospective study by Setyaningrum et al.[32] focused specifically on a minimally invasive modality – fractional Er:YAG laser therapy – for vaginal relaxation syndrome (VRS), a disorder characterized by vaginal laxity and often accompanied by stress urinary incontinence. Together, these studies illustrate the continuum of care for female pelvic floor disorders.

Epidemiological evidence underscores the substantial burden of PFD. Hong and Ding[31] reported that POP affects up to 50% of women during their lifetime, while urinary incontinence carries an estimated 11%–19% lifetime risk of requiring surgical intervention. Myofascial pelvic pain, although frequently underdiagnosed, can substantially overlap with other pelvic pain conditions such as IC, further complicating diagnosis and management. In parallel, Setyaningrum et al.[32] estimated the prevalence of VRS to be 25%–63% among sexually active women, with strong associations to multiparity, prior vaginal deliveries, and reduced sexual satisfaction. Both studies highlight the multifactorial etiology, frequent coexistence, and significant quality-of-life impact of these disorders. While Hong and Ding[31] provided an extensive discussion on the safety and complications of synthetic mesh and biologic grafts, the long-term safety profile of vaginal laser therapy remains less well established, underscoring the need for further high-quality, controlled clinical trials.

Limitations

The sample size was small, which likely impacted the statistical power of the analysis. In addition, the sample size was not determined in advance before the study began. Further, patients in the OAB group were older than those in the UTI and control groups. Further study with a large sample size and matched age is needed to clarify the role of EGF in OAB and UTI. Although patients with acute UTI were excluded, information regarding the time since the last UTI episode and the frequency of UTI episodes per year was not collected, both of which may influence urinary EGF levels. In addition, urinalysis findings – which could provide insight into subclinical inflammation or residual urinary abnormalities – were not analyzed and may represent an unmeasured confounding factor. In addition, urinary EGF/creatinine levels were not measured during follow-up, limiting the ability to evaluate their utility as a biomarker for disease progression or treatment response. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal EGF measurements to clarify its clinical relevance.

CONCLUSION

No significant difference in urine EGF/creatinine ratio was observed between control subjects and patients with OAB or recurrent lower UTI. Thus, urine EGF is not likely a useful marker for distinguishing between OAB and recurrent UTI. Both mirabegron and solifenacin are safe and effective in treating OAB. Solifenacin had a higher rate of dry mouth as side effects.
