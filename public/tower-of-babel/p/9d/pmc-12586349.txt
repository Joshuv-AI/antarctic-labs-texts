Alone on an Island: Experiences and Needs of Intensive Care Unit Nurses During the COVID‐19 Pandemic: A Focused Ethnography
Authors: Teenu Xavier, Beverly M. Hittle, Liliana Rojas Guyler, Rebecca C. Lee
Published: 2025

Introduction

Intensive care unit (ICU) nurses play a crucial role in providing care to critically ill patients who require constant monitoring and support. Their job entails performing various activities, including direct and indirect care and unit‐related activities, all of which require immense physical and mental effort. ICU nurses spend more time in direct patient care than in any other specialty area of nursing, making their job even more demanding (Nasirizad Moghadam et al. 2021; Moghadam et al. 2021). A study by Abbey et al. (2012) revealed that ICU nurses perform up to 3081 different activities each day, with 43% of them being performed simultaneously. Therefore, ICU nurses experience heavy physical and mental workloads and face unique challenges in providing care to critically ill patients on a day‐to‐day basis.

The COVID‐19 pandemic further intensified the workload of ICU nurses who were at the forefront of this global health crisis, delivering essential care to patients in critical condition amidst a rapidly evolving environment. These nurses faced numerous challenges, such as caring for a high volume of critically ill patients, caring for infectious and dying patients (Alharbi and Alshehry 2019; Toscano et al. 2022) and experiencing powerlessness over high patient mortality rates (Alharbi and Alshehry 2019; Gordon et al. 2021), coping with limited resources, lack of adequate personal protective equipment (PPEs) like N95 masks and gowns, shortage of medical supplies (Gordon et al. 2021; Shanafelt et al. 2020) and facing potential threats to their health and that of their loved ones and colleagues (Toscano et al. 2022). ICU nurses were also frequently required to provide end‐of‐life care to patients infected with COVID‐19 without the presence of their families and loved ones, which was an emotionally challenging experience for all involved. Additionally, nurses often worked long and gruelling shifts for extended periods during the pandemic, leading many to suffer from severe emotional trauma (Hossain and Clatty 2021). These stressors had a significant psychological impact on nurses, with the potential to affect both their mental and physical well‐being (Kissel et al. 2023).

In‐depth qualitative studies on the distinct perspectives, encounters, stressors and needs of ICU nurses directly involved in the care of patients with COVID‐19 can provide invaluable information for developing pandemic preparedness policies and resilience‐enhancing interventions. Therefore, there is a need to comprehend the viewpoints of ICU nurses who cared for patients with COVID‐19 to improve patient safety, quality of care and the work environment of staff during future pandemics. Even though researchers have explored the interactions and perceptions of nurses caring for patients with COVID‐19 during the pandemic, most of the studies were conducted outside the United States. They were single‐centered, focused on nurses in general, or a combination of ICU professionals, including physicians, technicians and nurses. These limitations overlook the significant regional, systemic and cultural variations present within the healthcare context. Moreover, many of these studies tend to take a generalised view of the nursing profession, offering limited attention to the distinct subcultures that exist across different practice settings and specialties. To address these gaps, this study sought to provide a culturally contextualised exploration of ICU nurses' values, challenges and professional needs, with the goal of capturing their unique experiences during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Methods

Design

The research design used in this study was a qualitative, focused ethnography to explore the phenomenon through a subcultural lens. Focused ethnography specifically aims to examine the shared experiences and behaviours of a particular group who are believed to share a common cultural viewpoint, regardless of their geographic location (Cruz and Higginbottom 2013). One assumption of this study was that ICU nurses share a common culture that shapes their experiences. Focused ethnography is considered an appropriate methodology when the problem under inquiry is preselected and the researcher has some existing knowledge about the problem that is being explored; therefore, the researcher relies less on immersing themselves in the cultural practices through the long‐term fieldwork typically associated with ethnography (Cruz and Higginbottom 2013).

Theoretical Framework

Leininger's Theory of Culture Care Diversity and Universality provided the overarching theoretical framework that guided this study. This theory emphasises that nurses themselves belong to the unique culture of professional nursing, which includes learned and transmitted beliefs, practices, symbols and patterns. Within nursing, there are subcultures with distinctive values and lifeways that differ from the dominant or mainstream culture of nursing (Leininger 1991). Therefore, in this study, ICU nurses are considered a subculture with shared values, beliefs, patterns and practices.

ICU nurses are a subculture within the nursing profession that has developed a unique identity, language, values and beliefs (Leininger 1994). The results of a study by Scholtz et al. (2016) suggest the presence of a critical care nursing culture among ICU nurses. ICU nursing subculture is shaped by the challenges that they face daily, including the need to be agile, quick‐thinking, and have a high level of attentiveness due to the precarious health condition of their patients. ICU nurses often experience heightened fatigue due to the demanding mental and physical workload they undertake. Additionally, these nurses also require expert knowledge, skills and training to guide their nursing practice in a highly specialised environment where they are part of a multidisciplinary team. ICU nursing is centered around delivering advanced and standardised care that involves a combination of nursing skills, observation, interpretation and prompt action in response to any changes in a patient's condition (Urden et al. 2006). This approach is often seen as the artistic aspect of ICU nursing (Boström et al. 2012). Due to the immersive nature of their work, ICU nurses cannot be separated from the environment in which they practice.

Sampling and Recruitment

Participants for this online study were recruited through purposive sampling. The sample included nurses who are members of the American Association of Critical‐Care Nurses (AACN), with recruitment taking place from May 2022 to January 2023. A link to the study, along with relevant details, was shared on the ‘Participate in Research’ page of the AACN website. In addition to purposive sampling, snowball sampling was used to recruit additional participants. The study participants assisted in the recruitment of prospective participants by sharing information about the study with friends and colleagues who were then asked to contact the PI for enrollment.

Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria for the study included nurses who (a) worked in an adult ICU for at least 2 years pre‐pandemic and during the pandemic; (b) are employed on a full‐time, part‐time, or per diem basis in any of the adult ICUs across the United States; (c) had experience providing care to patients with COVID‐19 disease; (d) spoke English; and (e) were willing to participate in an online interview through Zoom or telephone. Saturation was achieved after the 11th interview, but one additional interview was conducted to confirm the analysis and emerging themes.

Data Collection

Nurses who were interested in participating filled in a screening form using the link posted on the AACN website, including contact details, and a demographic form via a Research electronic data capture (REDCap) survey (Harris et al. 2009, 2019). The principal investigator (PI) screened all potential participants and contacted those determined to be eligible to schedule an online video interview through an encrypted Zoom platform at the convenience of the participants. A semi‐structured interview guide was developed based on a literature review, the theoretical framework of the study, and in consultation with the research team, and guided data collection. The interview questions were open‐ended, with supplementary probing questions used to elicit more in‐depth information if needed. A single interviewer (PI) conducted the interviews after receiving training from an experienced research team member. Each participant interview lasted approximately 60–70 min and was recorded through the recording feature in Zoom. The participants were compensated for their time with a gift card. After each interview session, reflection notes and digital recordings were transcribed using a professional transcription service. The typed transcripts were then verified by the PI while listening to the playback of the original interview. All the identifiable information was removed from the transcript and pseudonyms were assigned to each participant. Once the interviews were transcribed and verified, the video/audio recordings of the interviews were deleted. The transcribed files were stored in a secure password‐protected server and were backed up daily.

Data Analysis

The demographic information gathered from the participants was analysed using JMP.pro.16.1. A systematic approach for analysing qualitative data as proposed by Roper and Shapira (2000) was followed during the analysis of the data gathered through in‐depth interviews. The data was continuously processed and reflected on by the researchers to inform ongoing data collection and to generate new insights into the data. Analytical steps included, (a) coding meaning units with descriptive labels, (b) sorting into patterns, (c) generalising with constructs and theories and (d) memoing, including reflective remarks. The analytical steps were focused on answering the specific problem‐orientated research questions. Each verified transcript was coded individually by the two members of the research team, who then met to discuss coding for each transcript and to reach consensus on the final assigned codes. After the initial level of coding, coded transcripts were uploaded to the qualitative software NVivo, which allowed the research team to sort and organise coded passages within and across transcripts in order to reveal patterns and themes. The themes were then discussed once again by the authors until consensus was reached.

Ethical Considerations

The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (IRB) before the initiation of any research activities. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants before enrollment. A waiver of documentation of consent was obtained from the IRB as the enrollment and the interviews were conducted online. During data preparation, all personal identifiers, as well as the names of healthcare organisations and their locations, were removed to support confidentiality.

Rigour and Reflexivity

Trustworthiness was established based on the criteria established by Lincoln and Guba (1985): credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability. An audit trail of the process, peer debriefing, prolonged engagement using in‐depth interviews, negative case analysis and member checking was conducted to maintain rigour. During the member checking sessions, the ICU nurses confirmed that the results accurately reflected their experiences and needs. Reflexivity was ensured through a self‐assessment of the researcher's biases and positionality before conducting interviews. The lead researcher, a former ICU nurse, brought an emic (insider) perspective that facilitated trust and rapport with participants and enabled the recognition of subtle cultural nuances. Other members of the team brought etic (outsider) perspectives, allowing for critical distance and balancing interpretation. This insider–outsider collaboration supported a richer and more nuanced analysis. Active listening and open‐mindedness were employed during data collection to allow participants to express their experiences freely. Detailed reflexive journals were maintained to document the researcher's thoughts and reflections throughout the study and were incorporated into the analysis.

Findings

A total of 22 nurses met all the inclusion criteria, and these individuals were invited by the PI for interviews. A total of 12 nurses responded to the interview request and provided informed consent for enrollment and participation in the study. Recruitment was stopped once data saturation was achieved.

All 12 participants were female and identified as non‐Hispanic or Latino (U.S. Census Bureau 2024). The average age was 41.5 ± 10.6 (range: 29–61), and the average years of ICU experience were 12.25 ± 9.00 (range: 4–30). Half of the nurses held a master's degree (n = 6). Navigating a Constantly Changing Landscape

Participants described the need to face constant changes in the ICU as they navigated the period during which the COVID‐19 pandemic emerged. Each participant reflected on the changes they had experienced caring for ICU patients during the pandemic in relation to the nurses' experiences providing care prior to the pandemic. The COVID‐19 pandemic led to significant changes in ICUs, including changes in patient care, policies and the environment. All of these changes required nurses to adapt quickly to ensure the safety of patients and staff while providing high‐quality care. These changes affected the daily routines of nurses and the way they interacted with their patients and other healthcare providers.

Patient Care

The COVID‐19 pandemic brought significant changes to patient care over time as reflected by the participants. Participants described adapting to new protocols, such as donning and doffing PPE for every patient interaction, which hindered timely responses during emergencies. PPE shortages further limited room entries, leading nurses to cluster care tasks during single visits. The participants discussed the need to adapt to a new reality of caring for patients who were in isolation, with most requiring critical care due to severe COVID‐19 complications.

This shift also impacted the quality and intensity of care. Nurses reflected on moving from a proactive, detail‐oriented approach to a more passive, survival‐driven mode, driven by fear, uncertainty and the demands of the crisis. Many admitted that this less aggressive style of care persisted even as the crisis eased. Additionally, restrictions on family visitation created emotional challenges, particularly during end‐of‐life care, as families could no longer be physically present, compounding the distress for patients, families and care providers alike.We let more things go now than we did before. We let a lot more things slide, because when it kind of got down to the bare minimum nursing, as far as – you didn't kind of go above and beyond, ‘cause we didn't know what we were dealing with at the time. And so, we weren't as aggressive then, as far as treating things. And I feel like that's carried on over. And I feel like I'm not as aggressive a nurse as I was before then. And so, I still take care of the patients, and I still do what needs to be done, but don't go over and beyond like I used to. (P9)

Policies

Participants described how rapidly evolving policies and procedures during the COVID‐19 pandemic significantly impacted their practice. These included constant changes to visitation rules, PPE protocols, patient testing and clinical management guidelines. Nurses expressed difficulty keeping up, with one stating, ‘I mean, it's like the rules were changing every day, so you don't know what you're walking into that day’ (P5).

The shortage of N95 masks at the start of the pandemic forced nurses to ration N95 masks, reuse them using makeshift storage like paper bags, or switch to less effective alternatives. Many felt that PPE policies were driven more by supply limitations than by evidence‐based practice. Other changes, like strict visitor restrictions during peak periods, created emotional strain for patients and families, and when limited visitation resumed, enforcing safety protocols added to nurses' workload. One participant stated, ‘As visitors began being added back in, my comfort level with interacting with family members was very limited, especially because much of my job became reminding people to keep their mask on, reminding people to kind of follow PPE guidelines’ (P6).

Changes to code response policies, such as limiting personnel involved, also made it more difficult to manage emergencies effectively, as fewer hands were available to deliver comprehensive care.

Environment

Each participant reflected on the changes in the ICU environment during the COVID‐19 pandemic. These changes included establishing new units and converting step‐down and medical‐surgical units to ICUs to meet the increasing demand for patient care. These makeshift units often lacked essential features like windows or proximity between nurses' stations and patient rooms, making it difficult to monitor critically ill patients. Closed doors further limited visibility and audibility, delaying recognition of alarms or patient distress. Nurses also described how the physical layout hindered their ability to assess patients without entering rooms, adding to safety concerns and clinical workload. As one participant noted, ‘You couldn't easily see into the rooms … you couldn't hear alarms easily’ (Participant 1).

These environmental changes also disrupted established communication pathways. Physicians, who were previously stationed within the ICU for real‐time collaboration, were relocated to remote offices, making timely communication and coordination of care more difficult. Within teams, a new ‘closed room culture’ emerged, limiting spontaneous interaction among staff. One participant stated, ‘The unit is much bigger. The rooms are more spaced out. The staffing was much more limited, and the doors were all closed. And the doctors are kind of down the hallway. I would stick my head out of the room, and I wouldn't necessarily see anybody’ (P10).

Facing Unique Challenges and Stressors

ICU nurses faced numerous challenges during the COVID‐19 pandemic as reflected by the participants of the study. These included shortages, additional demands, increased expectations and uncertainty. The pandemic added further stress to an already demanding job, and these nurses had to quickly adapt and find innovative solutions to overcome these challenges while maintaining high‐quality patient care.

Shortages

All the ICU nurses interviewed reflected on the significant challenges faced during the COVID‐19 pandemic due to the shortage of supplies, equipment, PPE and staffing. The shortages of essential equipment such as ventilators, dialysis machines, and supplies such as feeding tubes, suction canisters, and medications such as Propofol and Ativan during the pandemic made it difficult for ICU nurses to provide optimal care to the patients. One participant noted, ‘Equipment was a difficult thing to navigate through, because we opened so many units so fast that finding the cardiac monitoring equipment, we had to steal from other units, and figure that part out’ (P10). PPE shortages, particularly of N95 masks and gowns, forced nurses to reuse gear far beyond recommended standards, sometimes for up to a week, causing significant anxiety about personal safety and risk of infection to themselves and their families. Some reported wearing makeshift gowns made from uncomfortable, non‐medical materials like those used in airbags.

Staffing shortages further compounded these challenges. Many nurses contracted COVID‐19 or left for safer or higher‐paying travel roles, leading to chronic understaffing. Nurses often had to care for more patients than recommended, work extended hours and train newly graduated or redeployed staff unfamiliar with ICU protocols. The influx of travel and agency nurses, while necessary, introduced additional strain, as they often required orientation to unit procedures and equipment.We had travel nurses up there working… And then, the other thing is just the difference in the care. Those nurses, they're just coming in, coming out. They don't care. There was one that even told me, he was like, ‘I don't have to chart anything if I don't want to’. (P7)

We had travel nurses up there working… And then, the other thing is just the difference in the care. Those nurses, they're just coming in, coming out. They don't care. There was one that even told me, he was like, ‘I don't have to chart anything if I don't want to’. (P7)

Furthermore, these nurses had to take on the added responsibility of training new graduate nurses who were brought in to fill the gaps. Despite the decrease in the number of COVID‐19 cases, the nursing shortage continued to pose a significant problem throughout and beyond the pandemic.

While some participants acknowledged that supply and staffing issues improved over time, the early stages of the pandemic were marked by resource scarcity and operational strain that deeply impacted ICU nurses' ability to provide safe, high‐quality care.

The participants highlighted the additional patient care tasks they were expected to perform during the COVID‐19 pandemic. These tasks were often beyond the usual scope of ICU practice and required learning new skills. With physicians limiting in‐room contact, nurses' assessments became the primary basis for daily care decisions, placing immense pressure on them to be accurate and thorough.So, doctors were not going into the rooms. We would round outside of the rooms every day. And then, the plan for the day was really getting dictated off the information that I was providing. So, it was extremely stressful and a lot of pressure that my assessment needs to be accurate. (P1)

Uncertainty

The participants highlighted the significant level of uncertainty and unpredictability that they faced during the COVID‐19 pandemic, especially in the early stages. These nurses were unsure of what to expect during the pandemic. As the only personnel entering patient rooms, ICU nurses had to provide care with limited knowledge of the virus, its complications and treatment options, creating fear of infecting themselves and others, stress and mental strain. Many felt overwhelmed by the unknown nature of COVID‐19, as patients often presented with atypical symptoms and deteriorated rapidly, especially younger individuals, challenging nurses' clinical instincts and heightening emotional distress.

The participants also spoke about the need to think ‘outside the box’ and be adaptable in the face of uncertainty and rapidly evolving situations. The emergence of new COVID‐19 variants like Delta and Omicron added to this uncertainty. The management of COVID‐19 patients posed a significant challenge for ICU nurses due to the uncertainties surrounding the virus. Furthermore, the evolving understanding of the virus made it challenging for nurses to stay updated on the latest treatment protocols and guidelines.First, we all heard about COVID, we don't know what to expect, we don't know are we're gonna get sick, those kinda things. So, it's a lot of unknown situations that you are walking into a cluster of a busy, stressful shift. (P5)

Experiencing the Impact of COVID‐19

The COVID‐19 pandemic had a significant impact on the emotional and physical well‐being of ICU nurses, as noted by the study participants. Nurses reported experiencing physical exhaustion due to the extended work hours, increased physical and mental workload, and the need for extra protective measures such as wearing personal protective equipment (PPE). They also experienced a range of negative emotions as they coped with the demands and uncertainties of working during the pandemic. In addition, participants shared their self‐identified needs during this challenging time.

Physical Impact

Participants shared their experiences of enduring the physical strain of caring for COVID‐19 patients, including the prolonged use of uncomfortable personal protective equipment, coping with longer shifts and increased workloads, and the physical challenges of taking care of critically ill patients, such as repositioning and proning them. Proning is a technique in which a patient is positioned face down to improve oxygenation in patients with respiratory failure. This physical exertion often led to exhaustion, muscle strain, back pain, body aches and fatigue. Nurses also noted that the combined physical and emotional strain often disrupted their sleep patterns, causing sleeplessness.I felt exhausted at times that, you know, coming home…, I was exhausted. I felt like I was putting my body in harm's way … These patients, you know, are sedated away. Just to pick them up, you have got backache, so you're emotionally drained and your body is being – you go home with backaches. So, you're losing your own health. (P3)

I felt exhausted at times that, you know, coming home…, I was exhausted. I felt like I was putting my body in harm's way … These patients, you know, are sedated away. Just to pick them up, you have got backache, so you're emotionally drained and your body is being – you go home with backaches. So, you're losing your own health. (P3)

Emotional Responses

Participants described the emotional toll of caring for critically ill and dying COVID‐19 patients, often in isolation from their families. The high mortality rate, especially among younger patients, led to feelings of helplessness, grief and trauma. Nurses reported experiencing depression, anger, anxiety and emotional exhaustion, with some likening their reactions to Post traumatic stress disorder. Despite a decrease in cases, caring for COVID‐19 patients continued to trigger painful memories. In addition, caring for isolated COVID‐19 patients who were separated from their loved ones was particularly stressful for these nurses, as it took an emotional toll on them.As far as like the deaths, just you feel like every day you go in, and you're always with somebody that's dying. And that's kind of hard. I was sad. Just kind of sad that you just feel like the angel of death after a while. (Participant 11)

The overwhelming workload, lack of support and sense of isolation intensified their distress. Nurses felt abandoned as they spent long hours alone in patient rooms, carrying the emotional and physical burden with limited resources, which led to burnout and a diminished sense of purpose, especially when patients died despite their best efforts.At the end of the day, it was just us in the room with the patient for 12 h. Increased Needs of ICU Nurses During COVID‐19

The participants in the study reflected that the impact of COVID‐19 resulted in a range of needs that required attention and support, including physical, emotional and mental needs.

Physical Needs

According to the participants, they needed various physical needs to be met to carry out their duties effectively, including sufficient rest, sleep, hydration and access to bathroom breaks. This was particularly important given the long hours, irregular work schedules and physical demands of the job, which often resulted in physical exhaustion and burnout. Additionally, some nurses mentioned being called in for extra shifts, which added to their physical demands and reduced the time available for rest.

Some participants struggled to take breaks or have meals during their shifts due to the lack of dedicated break spaces or the high demands of the job. ‘There were shifts I didn't have time to eat or even go to the bathroom because you're just trying to care for these patients. And so, my needs, I don't think were met when I'm on shift’ (P2). While some hospitals offered small perks like free parking or donated meals, on‐campus grocery stores, these efforts fell short of meeting essential needs.

Nurses also faced shortages in staffing, supplies and PPE, and felt that hospital leadership did not adequately address these gaps. As a result, they were often left to solve problems on their own and were forced to come up with solutions to meet their needs and address the shortages.We tried our best to be innovative in solving our own problems because it didn't seem like management was that invested or interested in it. In the beginning, there was a mask shortage, you know, and you found all these masks all over in paper bags. And then people started building these helmets, and they would go into the room with these helmets that looked like a motorcycle helmet with a … with a shield, I mean, they were very creative in the unit. (P3)

Mental and Emotional Needs

The participants reported that they had emotional needs that required attention and support, including resources and opportunities to manage the emotional demands of their work. The high levels of stress, anxiety, pressure and loss of control and purpose that these nurses experienced negatively impacted their mental health. Despite their emotional struggles, they felt that their emotional needs were not heard, and they lacked opportunities to destress at work. One participant stated, ‘I think one thing I needed was to feel like I was heard, that I had a place to vent my frustration, disappointment, sadness. And so, that was something I really needed at the end of work’ (P8).

In terms of mental needs, ICU nurses needed more than just employee assistance programs. They needed to feel appreciated and involved in the decision‐making process by their management, as well as opportunities for feedback and open communication with the administrators. The psychological demands of their work required additional resources and support, including access to mental health services such as therapy and support for work‐related stress and burnout. Many participants felt that more could have been done to address their mental health needs, particularly during the pandemic, and that their voices were not being heard by management. One participant expressed, ‘Our hospital doesn't do much for the morale of the nurses. I think our CEO came down one time when it was pretty bad … But they don't stop to hear our needs’ (P3).

Discussion

This study highlights how the COVID‐19 pandemic affected ICU nurses' experiences from a temporal perspective, particularly as members of a unique subculture of nursing. While previous research has documented challenges faced by healthcare workers during the pandemic, our study uniquely centers on the shared cultural and professional experiences of ICU nurses across diverse hospital settings in the United States. By adopting both emic (insider) and etic (outsider) perspectives in the analysis, we capture a nuanced understanding of how ICU nurses collectively navigated these difficult times alongside the emotional toll and professional demands placed upon them. By viewing ICU nurses as members of a unique subculture of nursing, caring for patients during the COVID‐19 pandemic challenged not only the ICU nurses' physical and mental capacity but also challenged the shared values, beliefs, patterns and practices that previously guided their day‐to‐day lives. The findings reveal that the COVID‐19 pandemic challenged core nursing values such as holistic, patient‐centered care, therapeutic presence and compassionate end‐of‐life support. Due to infection control protocols and staffing shortages, nurses had to shift to task‐oriented, clustered care, which limited their ability to provide emotional support and maintain usual patient and family interactions.

ICU culture is characterised by its unique characteristics, such as highly specialised care, complex and intense patient management, high levels of monitoring and interprofessional collaboration. The COVID‐19 pandemic has added additional layers of complexity to this culture, as ICU nurses had to navigate constant change and adapt to changes in policies, patient care and the environment, and face unique challenges and stressors. These changes disrupted the usual routines and comfort levels that ICU nurses relied on to function effectively in their work and required them to adapt quickly to new ways of working. These findings are consistent with the observations made by Gordon et al. (2021) and Nikbakht Nasrabadi et al. (2022) who conducted qualitative studies in individual hospital settings regarding the need for ICU nurses to adapt to changing patient care guidelines and environment during COVID‐19.

Findings from this study suggest that ICU nurses faced significant challenges and stressors in relation to ICU nurse shortages, uncertainty and additional work demands, The COVID‐19 pandemic caused a level of uncertainty that was previously unheard of in the culture of ICU nurses in the U.S. Although their work has always been challenging, the pandemic added an extra layer of complexity to their already demanding roles. The findings of the present study are supported by the results of the study by Gordon et al. (2021) and Fernández‐Castillo et al. (2021) who reported that ICU nurses faced challenges related to PPE concerns (Fernández‐Castillo et al. 2021; Gordon et al. 2021), isolation of the patient, increased workload and staffing shortage (Fernández‐Castillo et al. 2021).

The findings of our study also highlight the importance of supporting ICU nurses during future public health emergencies. Hospitals and healthcare systems need to address the shortages of supplies, equipment and staffing to ensure that nurses can provide optimal care to their patients. This includes providing adequate PPE to protect nurses from infection and addressing the nursing shortage by hiring additional staff, conducting recruitment campaigns, collaborating with educational institutions and offering incentives to retain existing staff. However, it is important to acknowledge the challenges when attempting to augment staffing levels during emergencies. Therefore, it is crucial to explore alternative care delivery models that can alleviate the isolation and heavy reliance on nurses for all aspects of care. The team‐based nursing model, utilisation of nurse float pools and innovative uses of telehealth are some of the potential approaches that can help manage resources effectively and prevent nurses from becoming overwhelmed (Beckett et al. 2021; Parreira et al. 2021). By investigating and implementing such models, healthcare facilities can better manage their resources and ensure that nurses are not overwhelmed with the burden of providing care alone.

It is crucial to acknowledge that various factors, including the breakdown in the supply chain, have hindered healthcare facilities from effectively addressing supply and equipment shortages. Healthcare organisations can adopt additional strategies to mitigate the impact of supply chain breakdowns. These strategies include diversifying suppliers to reduce reliance on a single source, implementing proactive inventory management systems to optimise resource allocation, fostering collaborations and resource‐sharing networks among healthcare facilities, strengthening disaster preparedness plans and advocating for policy changes at various levels of government (National Strategy for a Resilient Public Health Supply Chain 2021). By adopting a comprehensive approach that encompasses these strategies, healthcare facilities can enhance their resilience and ability to respond effectively during times of crisis. This proactive approach not only mitigates the impact of supply chain disruptions but also supports the well‐being of ICU nurses, ensuring they can continue to provide high‐quality care to patients during public health emergencies.

Hospitals should provide additional training and support to help nurses adapt to the demands of caring for COVID‐19 patients, including learning new skills and protocols. It is important to acknowledge that the foundation for nurses' well‐being starts long before nurses enter the workplace. Therefore, it is essential to provide support throughout the entire nursing pipeline, starting from early education and continuing through the transition of new graduate nurses, in order to ensure a sustainable and healthy workforce.

It is also important to note that the COVID‐19 pandemic has magnified pre‐existing gaps in healthcare delivery. The surge of patients with a novel and highly transmissible illness served as a catalyst for exposing weaknesses within the system. Challenges such as nursing shortages, supply and equipment shortages, and high patient care demands are ongoing issues in healthcare (Bailey 2022; Fox and Abrahamson 2009; Thalbauer 2021). Addressing these issues requires a comprehensive and long‐term approach that goes beyond the pandemic.

Our study reveals that ICU nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic faced substantial unmet needs spanning physical, emotional and mental domains. Physically, nurses require adequate rest, hydration, nutrition and access to breaks to maintain their stamina and performance during prolonged and demanding shifts. This study revealed that in addition to the physical consequences, such as work overload, feelings of exhaustion and fatigue, ICU nurses experienced significant emotional and mental hardships. They experienced high levels of stress, anxiety, pressure, and a sense of loss of control and purpose, which negatively impacted their mental health. This is consistent with previous research that has identified emotional and mental challenges faced by nurses in high‐stress environments, such as the ICU (American Nurses Foundation 2021; Sun et al. 2020). Nurses were constantly concerned and stressed both during their work shifts and at home, which led to sleep disturbances and an inability to rest properly. This is in line with the findings of (Fernández‐Castillo et al. 2021; Gordon et al. 2021) and previous research studies on the impact of stress on sleep quality (Ballesio et al. 2021; Kandemir et al. 2022). The situation was worsened by limited organisational support and minimal opportunities to express concerns or receive meaningful mental health care. The lack of engagement from hospital leadership in addressing these needs contributed to diminished morale and increased burnout. This was also noted by Cadge et al. (2021) who highlighted the importance of support from nursing leadership in maintaining the morale of nurses. The importance of management support and open communication with ICU nurses cannot be overstated, as it can help to alleviate emotional and mental distress. Furthermore, the lack of emotional and mental support for ICU nurses during the pandemic highlights the need for healthcare institutions to prioritise the well‐being of their healthcare workers. This is especially important given the significant emotional and mental challenges faced by ICU nurses during the pandemic.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

The study's strength lies in its recruitment of participants from hospitals across the US, which ensured that a diverse range of perspectives and experiences was captured. The use of in‐depth, semi‐structured interviews provided a wealth of detailed data about the experiences of ICU nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic. While the study included diverse participants from different racial backgrounds (White, Black and Asian) a comparative analysis focusing on the experiences of nurses from different races was not conducted. Instead, the study adopted a comprehensive analysis approach, considering the collective experiences of all participants. It is also important to note that all participants identified as female, which may limit the transferability of findings to the wider nursing population. Additionally, the study relied on retrospective accounts collected after the peak of the pandemic, introducing the potential for recall bias. However, given the emotionally intense and traumatic nature of ICU work during this period, participants' memories were often vivid and continued to shape their personal and professional lives at the time of the interviews.

Recommendations for Further Research

Longitudinal studies that follow up with ICU nurses after the pandemic can provide insights into the long‐term impact of their experiences on their mental and emotional health. This would enable researchers to identify any lasting effects of the pandemic on ICU nurses and to develop targeted interventions to support their mental and emotional well‐being. Additionally, investigating how emotional and mental support from management and healthcare institutions can enhance the well‐being of ICU nurses can provide useful information for developing strategies to improve the quality of care during pandemics and other emergencies. Future research could explore the experiences and requirements of nurses in various specialties as subcultures, thereby offering valuable insights into the distinct variations and challenges encountered within different nursing subcultures during the pandemic. This investigation could shed light on the differing experiences across various nursing specialties and contribute to a better understanding of the unique demands, expectations and professional cultures within each specialty. By delving into these subcultures, researchers can develop targeted interventions and support systems that address the specific needs of nurses in each specialised area.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the experiences of ICU nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The findings show that the pandemic has significantly impacted the mental and emotional well‐being of ICU nurses. The nurses experienced a range of stressors, including changes in policies, patient care and the ICU environment, fear of infection, limited personal protective equipment and increased workload, which led to feelings of burnout, emotional exhaustion and anxiety.
