Agency-Level Turnover Rates of Emergency Medical Service Clinicians and Their Characteristics
Authors: Jordan D Kurth, Jonathan R Powell, Christopher B Gage, Jacob C Kamholz, Shea van den Bergh, Rebecca E Cash, Ashish R Panchal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEmergency medical service (EMS) clinician-level turnover is high and varies dramatically by agency type. There is a need to evaluate mean turnover rates, adjusted by agency characteristics, to provide standardized and comparable measures across workplace settings. Our objective was to leverage recent methods to evaluate agency-level mean turnover and describe the agency-level characteristics of those leaving the agency of record.MethodsA 2-year retrospective evaluation of EMS clinicians from 2020 to 2022 was conducted to evaluate agency-level turnover. EMS clinicians and agencies from 9 states that required National EMS Certification to obtain/maintain EMS licensure were used. Demographic and EMS-related characteristics were collected, including agency-specific attributes (agency type, service type, majority full-time employees) and state-level identifiers. The percentage of those leaving each EMS agency was calculated, and multivariable linear regression was used to calculate adjusted agency-level mean turnover rates.ResultsA total of 1137 EMS agencies with 20,881 EMS clinicians met the inclusion criteria. The adjusted agency-level mean turnover rate was 17.4% and varied dramatically based on agency type (14.3% to 23.7%), with the lowest being fire agencies and the highest being private. When we examined the EMS demographics and work-related characteristics, we discovered significant variability, which is consistent with the theory that each agency type may have different characteristics of clinicians leaving.ConclusionAdjusted agency-level mean turnover rates varied dramatically by agency type with associated variability in EMS clinician demographics and workplace characteristics. This suggests that the characteristics of agency turnover potentially vary by specific organizational culture, and identifying these variations may assist in developing interventions to enhance retention.Keywords: emergency medical services, health workforce, knowledge management, paramedicine

The Bottom Line

Emergency medical services (EMS) clinician turnover varies widely, but most prior studies report overall rates without accounting for differences between agency types, limiting meaningful comparisons. In this multistate, retrospective study of 20,881 EMS clinicians from 1137 agencies across 9 states, the adjusted mean agency-level turnover rate was 17.4%, with substantial variation by agency type. Fire-based agencies had the lowest turnover (14.3%), whereas private agencies had the highest (23.6%). Clinicians leaving also differed in age, employment status, education, and service type across agencies. Accounting for agency-level differences may help target retention strategies where the risk of turnover is highest.

Introduction

Background

The strength and stability of the emergency medical services (EMS) workforce are integral to prehospital care delivery across the United States. Despite this, there are ongoing concerns among local, state, and national stakeholders that workforce dynamics are poorly measured and current interventions are ineffective.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Estimates of clinician turnover vary annually from 6% to 30% in both regional and national samples of EMS clinicians, depending on the methodology used.10, 11, 12 However, recent studies have recognized the importance of agency culture and its association with leaving the workforce but have not evaluated leaving by agency type. The variability in EMS clinician turnover by agency characteristics is unknown.

Importance

Variability in turnover rates has driven the effort to clearly evaluate the EMS workforce using consistent, transparent, and reproducible measures.13 Recently, a population-based evaluation of workforce dynamics has allowed for a consistent and replicable method to evaluate overall turnover rates of EMS clinicians.14 However, this evaluation did not account for the large variability in workforce dynamics by EMS agency type.15 Different EMS agency types (eg, fire-based, private) may have widely variable turnover rates that may be due, in part, to the characteristics of the culture and workplace environment. Knowing the variability in agency-level turnover rates and agency characteristics may assist in developing workforce mitigation measures that are specific to the culture and characteristics of those agencies.

Goals of This Investigation

The objectives of this study were to leverage recent replicable methods to estimate EMS clinician-level turnover14 and calculate adjusted agency-level mean turnover rates. Specifically, we evaluated agency-level mean turnover and described the agency-level characteristics of those leaving their agency of record.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Population

We conducted a 2-year retrospective cross-sectional evaluation of EMS clinician turnover at their agency of record. We examined those certified at or above the emergency medical technician (EMT) level for 9 US states between 2020 and 2022. This evaluation used a previously defined methodology, following all EMS clinicians certified within a state requiring national certification and evaluating whether they maintained that certification as a proxy for the licensed state workforce.14 The American Institutes for Research Institutional Review Board approved this study and granted a waiver of consent documentation. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational cohort studies (Supplementary Appendix 1).16

For this analysis, we leveraged the National EMS Certification database from 2020 to 2022 to identify the complete population of EMS clinicians in states that require National EMS Certification for maintenance of state EMS licensure. This population selection allows for the comprehensive identification of a defined population that, if followed over time, could be used to quantify EMS clinicians who decided to stay with or leave an EMS workforce. National certification is required for initial licensure for at least 1 certification level in more than 46 states, territories, and federal agencies. In most states, continuous national certification, including biennial recertification, is voluntary; however, 9 states require National EMS Certification to maintain state EMS licensure. These “recertification states” are Alabama, the District of Columbia, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Hampshire, North Dakota, South Carolina, and Vermont. Thus, the National EMS Certification database from the National Registry of EMTs (national registry) represents the list of all EMS clinicians licensed and available to provide prehospital care in recertification states.

National EMS certifications are valid for 2-year periods with a uniform expiration date of March 31. In this study, we followed 1 cohort of these civilian EMS clinicians through a single 2-year recertification cycle. These clinicians held National EMS Certifications that expired on March 31, 2020, and, if renewed, would expire again on March 31, 2022. We included civilian EMS clinicians aged 18 to 85 (the certified workforce) at the emergency medical technician (EMT) level or higher (advanced emergency medical technician [AEMT] and paramedic). Emergency medical responders have a unique recertification process and serve distinct roles in the workforce and therefore were excluded.

Measurements

As part of the recertification process, candidates respond to agency-specific attributes, including EMS agency type (fire, hospital, tribal, government/nonfire, private, air medical, and other), service type (9-1-1, medical transport, 9-1-1 and medical transport, clinical services, mobile-integrated health care community paramedicine, and other), full-time employment, and state-level identifiers. Agencies with fewer than 5 EMS clinicians were excluded from the analysis to ensure stable turnover estimates.

Optional demographic and EMS-related profile information was collected from nationally certified EMS clinicians. Demographic information included age, sex (male or female), race/ethnicity (dichotomized to non-Hispanic White [yes and no] because of the low proportion of minority EMS clinicians), certification level (emergency medical technician [EMT], advanced EMT [AEMT], and paramedic), and education level (high school/general educational development (GED) or less, some college, associate degree, and bachelor’s degree or higher).

EMS-related occupational information included agency of record, certification level (EMT, AEMT, and paramedic), full-time employment status (yes and no), and primary EMS agency type (fire, hospital, government/nonfire, private, air medical, and other). Community size was defined as urban/suburban and rural as defined by the US Census classification, with urban areas having populations of ≥50,000, urban clusters (suburban) with populations of 2500 to 50,000, and rural areas as those areas not included as urban or suburban. The state origin of EMS clinicians was based on a self-reported mailing address.

Our primary outcome of interest was agency-level mean turnover rate. Agency-level turnover rate was calculated by determining the status of each EMS clinician (stayed at or left their agency of record in 2020) classified as follows: (1) If an EMS clinician’s agency of record was the same in the first year of the cycle (eg, cycle ending in 2020) as the second year of that cycle (eg, cycle ending in 2022), then they were determined to have “stayed”; (2) If an EMS clinician’s agency of record was not the same in the first year of the cycle (eg, cycle ending in 2020) as the second year of that cycle (eg, cycle ending in 2022), or the EMS clinicians was no longer certified in the second year of the cycle (2022), then they were determined to have “left.” To estimate the true turnover of EMS clinicians at EMS agencies, we did not exclude any EMS clinicians for the reason they left (eg, retirement).”

Analysis

Descriptive statistics, presented as frequency (%) for categoric measures and mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous measures, were calculated. Agency-level turnover was calculated as defined above. Additionally, we conducted multivariable linear regression using stepwise forward selection to analyze the association between turnover rate and key predictors of agency-level turnover, including agency type, service type, agency size, urbanicity, and state. Model fit was assessed using adjusted R-squared and root mean squared error as well as evaluating residuals for heteroskedasticity. Because the assumption of homoscedasticity was violated, robust standard errors were used. Adjusted predictions were calculated for agency-level mean turnover rates with 95% CIs. Histograms were then created showing adjusted agency-level mean turnover rates (%) by agency type, ordered from the lowest to highest turnover rates.

We also conducted a descriptive analysis of the characteristics of EMS clinicians who left their agency of record by agency type. Between-group comparisons were performed using 2-sample t tests (continuous variables) and chi-square tests (categoric variables). A significance level of α = 0.05 was used. All analyses were completed using Stata/SE version 19 (StataCorp LP).

Limitations

In this evaluation, we leveraged the states whose recertification is through the national registry to identify agency-level turnover of EMS clinicians. Thus, we are focusing this evaluation only on the 9 states identified and cannot determine the exact percentage of EMS clinicians leaving their agencies or records outside of our study population. Unfortunately, this type of analysis is not currently feasible without a national data set of all EMS clinicians. The national EMS ID,17 a 12-digit unique identifier number for the data set of the EMS compact,18 may serve in this role in the future as these systems mature. Additionally, although we note a large variability in adjusted turnover rates in EMS agencies in this study, this variability could be different across the United States, but we cannot assess this. The existing data set may be biased to an unknown degree because a small proportion of EMS clinicians may live and work in different states, and we cannot account for the number of EMS clinicians holding multiple state certifications in recertification states. Our study should account for those who work in a recertification state; however, the impact of multiple certifications across multiple recertification states is unclear. Finally, we used available data regarding agency-level factors to adjust for turnover rates. Although agency type may strongly impact turnover rates, this designation is likely a proxy for more specific and intervenable variables that could impact clinician turnover.

Discussion

In this evaluation, we calculated adjusted agency-level mean turnover rates to provide standardized and comparable measures across workplace settings. The overall adjusted agency-level mean turnover rate (17.4%) varied dramatically based on agency type (14.3% to 23.7%) with the lowest being fire agencies. When we examined the EMS demographics and work-related characteristics, we discovered differences between agency types in those who left their agencies. Recognizing this variability and the potential impact of agency-level characteristics may help facilitate evidence-based conclusions about workforce strength and stability, enabling more directed future evaluations and intervention development.

Previous work has attempted to define overall workforce turnover and has demonstrated rates ranging from 4% to 30%, although the exact rate per agency type is poorly defined.10,12,14,19 In a convenience sample of EMS agencies, the American Ambulance Association reported turnover rates per agency type (eg, hospital; private sector—for profit; and public sector—fire), but the variability by agency type was not assessed. Previously, Patterson et al11 noted variability by agency with an annual turnover rate of 7.5% (IQR: 5.2, 10.8), weighted for the total number of positions at each agency. Our study is the first to evaluate leaving rates per agency type and adjust for leaving based on this previously observed variability. Interestingly, the large variability in turnover by agency highlights the potential contribution of agency culture and satisfaction to retention and leaving EMS.15

The reason for the large variability in turnover by agency types is likely to be multifactorial and requires further assessment. Most notably, fire agencies had lower leaving rates than all other agency types, although the mechanisms for this lower rate are currently unknown.15 One potential reason is the observed lower personal and work-related burnout rates experienced among fire-agency-based clinicians compared with private agencies.20 Additionally, this may be associated with differences in organizational culture.21 However, how these concepts influence and interact with one another is unclear, and further work is needed to identify the factors behind differences in leaving rates between agency types.

This study focused on identifying agency-level mean turnover and describing the agency-level characteristics of those leaving the agency of record. Future workforce evaluations should consider adjusting agency-level turnover rates to account for this observed EMS agency variability and allow for direct comparisons. Additionally, these data suggest that characteristics for leaving may vary by EMS agency type, and defining these factors may assist in developing interventions to enhance retention. Finally, stakeholder advocacy for a centralized EMS clinician database that allows for workforce tracking, from educational program entry to licensure, should be a priority for future workforce evaluation.
