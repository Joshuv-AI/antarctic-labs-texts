HyaluHerb Pediatric Oral Gel for Recurrent Aphthous Ulcers and Plaque-Induced Gingivitis in Children: A Two-Cohort Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Samah F Mohamed-Ali, Ahmed A Jasim, Huda E Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:Recurrent aphthous ulcers (RAU) and plaque-induced gingivitis are among the prevalent pediatric oral conditions. Standard treatments such as chlorhexidine (CHX) and topical corticosteroids have limitations, including unpleasant taste, staining, and poor adherence. This study evaluated HyaluHerb, a novel alcohol-free gel containing 0.2% hyaluronic acid (HA), Aloe vera, and polyphenol-rich herbal extracts (Myrtus communis and Populus euphratica). The present study was aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of novel HyaluHerb for treating RAU and gingivitis in children.Methods:This was a single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial. Cohort A (RAU, n = 99) compared HyaluHerb, HA-only gel, and placebo gel, applied four times daily for 7 days. Cohort B (gingivitis, n = 168) used a double-dummy design to compare HyaluHerb gel, HA-only gel, placebo gel (all twice daily), and 0.12% CHX rinse (twice daily) for 14 days. Primary outcomes were time to ulcer healing and pain score (Cohort A), and change in Gingival Index [gingival index (GI), Cohort B].Results:In Cohort A, HyaluHerb significantly accelerated ulcer healing versus placebo (median 5.3 vs. 7.2 days; hazard ratio = 1.73, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.16–2.59; P = 0.007) and reduced pain by day 3 (2.5 vs. 4.0; P < 0.001). In Cohort B, HyaluHerb reduced gingival inflammation more than placebo (adjusted difference in ΔGI = −0.20; 95% CI: −0.33 to −0.07; P = 0.004). In an exploratory comparison with CHX (not powered for noninferiority), the adjusted mean difference in GI change (HyaluHerb − CHX) was +0.10, with a one-sided 95% upper confidence bound of +0.15 (prespecified margin), and a supportive per-protocol estimate of +0.14. HyaluHerb demonstrated higher acceptability (P < 0.001) and minimal staining (4.8%) compared with CHX (59.5%).Conclusion:HyaluHerb is an effective, well-tolerated, child-friendly treatment for acute pediatric RAU and plaque-induced gingivitis.

INTRODUCTION

One of the major consequences of bacterial plaque buildup on tooth surfaces throughout the juvenile period is periodontal disease, which damages the gingiva and tooth-supporting bone structures.[1] Furthermore, children are more vulnerable to plaque-induced gingivitis due to poor oral hygiene habits, a factor associated with the existence of anatomical variations in the structure of the periodontium in children compared with adults, such as the width of the attached gingiva as well as the presence of spaces between teeth that provide a favorable location for bacterial accumulation, leading to increased susceptibility to periodontal issues in children.[2,3] Recurrent aphthous ulcers (RAU) and plaque-induced gingivitis are highly prevalent in school-aged children, causing pain, functional impairment, and reduced quality of life. The most common causes of aphthous ulcers in children are idiopathic and complex, such as nutritional deficiencies, immunological factors, and systemic factors in genetically susceptible individuals.[4] The management of these conditions in pediatric populations is challenging. For RAU, topical corticosteroids, while effective, raise safety concerns and adherence issues.[5] Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) offers various treatment modalities for recurrent oral ulcers in children, with reported high cure rates and low recurrence, though more rigorous studies are needed. For gingivitis, the gold-standard chlorhexidine (CHX) mouthrinse is limited by its bitter taste and side effects, notably tooth staining and mucosal irritation, which curtail its long-term use in children.[4,6] Alternative treatments include photobiomodulation therapy, which has shown effectiveness in reducing pain and lesion size in pediatric minor RAU, with good parental acceptance.[7] Herbal products have long been valued for their antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticariogenic properties, which contribute to their therapeutic potential with minimal side effects.[8] Phytochemicals such as flavonoids, polyphenols, and terpenoids found in plants such as Chrysanthemum species, green tea, and various polyherbal formulations exhibit strong antimicrobial activity against pathogens, reduce oxidative stress, and modulate inflammatory responses.[9] Furthermore, polyherbal-based formulations that are rich in phenolic and flavonoid compounds demonstrate strong inhibition of inflammatory mediators such as inducible nitric oxide synthase and cyclooxygenase-2, supporting their use in the management of chronic inflammatory conditions.[10] The synergy in pharmacology encompasses combining agents with different mechanisms to achieve effects superior to the sum of individual actions, so as to optimize efficacy and safety of therapies.[11] Hyaluronic acid (HA) demonstrates biocompatibility and can enhance the regenerative and therapeutic effects of encapsulated cells or drugs.[12] A combination of natural extracts can deliver synergistic antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-regenerative benefits, aligning with principles of combination therapy that amplify efficacy while lessening side effects.[11] Research on HA hydrogels shows their potential to support sustained release and improve local therapeutic outcomes, which may enhance the child-friendly application of such gels.[13] HA is a mucoadhesive biopolymer that forms a protective barrier over mucosal lesions and promotes healing. HA shows promising therapeutic effects, particularly in wound healing and skin care, due to its hydrating, angiogenic, and tissue-regenerating effects.[14] Aloe vera contains acemannan and other bioactive compounds that provide antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, and skin-protective benefits, supporting its traditional use in healing and moisturizing applications.[15,16] Aloe vera flower extract and its active constituent isoorientin enhance skin barrier function by regulating proteins involved in skin moisturization and protection against UV damage.[17] Aloe vera extracts, rich in acemannan, stimulate tissue regeneration, while polyphenols from Myrtus communis and Populus euphratica possess antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. We formulated HyaluHerb, an alcohol-free gel compounding these active ingredients, in an attempt to provide a synergistic, child-friendly therapeutic option.[11] Overall, the convergence of traditional knowledge and modern research supports the use of herbal products as safe, effective alternatives or adjuncts in treating infections, inflammation, and dental caries.[18] P. euphratica, a member of the Salicaceae family, is rich in phenolic compounds and glycosides such as populin and salicin; its leaves also contain volatile oils and various identified phenolics. Chemical analysis identified 20 compounds in P. euphratica leaves, including novel compounds such as 6-O-cis-cinnamoylsalicin and 6-O-benzoylsalicortinol, as well as protopine alkaloids, which were reported for the first time in this species.[19] Traditionally, extracts of P. euphratica have been used by local populations in Iraq for pain relief in carious teeth, inflamed gums, mucous membranes, eczema, and skin conditions, reflecting its anti-inflammatory and analgesic potential. Additionally, resins from P. euphratica contain unique terpenoids with fungicidal activities, suggesting further medicinal applications. The species is also notable for its adaptation to extreme desert environments, with genomic studies revealing genes linked to stress tolerance, which may contribute to its bioactive compound production.[20] M. communis exhibits significant antimicrobial activity against several oral pathogens, including key Gram-negative bacteria involved in periodontal disease, such as Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Prevotella intermedia. Both aquatic and methanolic extracts of M. communis demonstrated antibacterial effects with minimum inhibitory concentration as low as 10 mg/mL against these bacteria, suggesting effective inhibition without the need for costly extraction methods.[3] Essential oils from M. communis also show antimicrobial activity, particularly against oral Streptococcus species, and have potential for preventing dental caries. The antimicrobial activity is attributed to bioactive compounds such as myrtenyl acetate, linalool, 1,8-cineole, and various phenolic compounds, which also exhibit anti-quorum-sensing effects that reduce virulence and biofilm formation in resistant Gram-negative bacteria.[21]

There is a lack of robust clinical evidence for a well-tolerated, child-friendly topical gel that combines HA with specific herbal extracts to treat both pediatric RAU and gingivitis. This two-cohort, randomized, double-blind, controlled trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and acceptability of a novel polyherbal-HA gel (HyaluHerb) in children with RAU (Cohort A) or plaque-induced gingivitis (Cohort B).

The specific objectives were as follows:

In Cohort A (RAU): To determine whether HyaluHerb shortens ulcer healing time and reduces pain compared to a placebo gel and an HA-only control gel.

In Cohort A (RAU): To determine whether HyaluHerb shortens ulcer healing time and reduces pain compared to a placebo gel and an HA-only control gel.

In Cohort B (gingivitis): To assess whether HyaluHerb reduces gingival inflammation more effectively than a placebo gel and to explore its effects relative to the gold-standard 0.12% CHX rinse.

In Cohort B (gingivitis): To assess whether HyaluHerb reduces gingival inflammation more effectively than a placebo gel and to explore its effects relative to the gold-standard 0.12% CHX rinse.

To evaluate the safety, tolerability, and patient-reported acceptability of HyaluHerb in both pediatric cohorts.

To evaluate the safety, tolerability, and patient-reported acceptability of HyaluHerb in both pediatric cohorts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

STUDY DESIGN AND SETTING

This was a single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo- and active-controlled trial with two parallel cohorts, conducted at the pediatric dental clinics of Al-Mustansiriya University College of Dentistry, Baghdad, Iraq, between January 2022 and November 2023. The trial protocol was approved by the ethical committee of the same institute (Internal Reg. Nr: MUPED-2O22-2-15) and conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the consolidated standards of reporting trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines for reporting randomized trials.

PARTICIPANTS

Children aged 7–12 years in good general health were recruited from the clinic’s patient pool. Written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and written assent was obtained from all participating children. Eligibility was confirmed during a screening visit.

Cohort A (RAU) included children diagnosed with minor recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), presenting with one to five ulcers (each ≤10 mm in diameter), with onset within 48 h before enrollment, and a documented history of two or more episodes per year.

Cohort A (RAU) included children diagnosed with minor recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), presenting with one to five ulcers (each ≤10 mm in diameter), with onset within 48 h before enrollment, and a documented history of two or more episodes per year.

Cohort B (plaque-induced gingivitis) included children with plaque-induced gingivitis, defined as a Löe–Silness Gingival Index (GI) score of ≥1.0 in at least two nonadjacent quadrants, and a minimum of 20 scorable permanent teeth.

Cohort B (plaque-induced gingivitis) included children with plaque-induced gingivitis, defined as a Löe–Silness Gingival Index (GI) score of ≥1.0 in at least two nonadjacent quadrants, and a minimum of 20 scorable permanent teeth.

Key exclusion criteria for both cohorts were systemic diseases known to cause oral ulceration (e.g., Behçet’s disease and Crohn’s disease), immunodeficiency, use of systemic corticosteroids, antibiotics, or immunosuppressants within the preceding 4 weeks, fixed orthodontic appliances, and known hypersensitivity to any component of the investigational products.

HyaluHerb gel: All study procedures were initiated only after full protocol review and approval by the institutional ethical committee at Al-Mustansiriya University College of Dentistry, which classified the product as a minimal-risk topical oral gel; therefore, under institutional policy, no additional national regulatory authorization was required for this investigator-initiated study. HyaluHerb was compounded and labeled exclusively for the trial by a registered pharmacist at the Medical City Pharmaceutical Laboratories (Iraqi Ministry of Health). Batch release required Certificates of Analysis for all active ingredients (sodium hyaluronate and standardized botanical extracts), high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-based chemical standardization, and microbiological safety testing (microbial limits/sterility) to confirm suitability for human use. Packaging and storage controls (and stability checks where applicable) were implemented to maintain product integrity throughout the study. The active gel contained 0.2% (w/w) pharmaceutical-grade sodium hyaluronate (molecular weight 800–1200 kDa), Aloe vera inner leaf extract standardized to contain 0.10% acemannan (as per International Aloe Science Council methods), and hydroglycolic extracts of M. communis L. and P. euphratica leaves, co-standardized to a total polyphenol content of 0.40% expressed as gallic acid equivalents. The gel base consisted of purified water, glycerin, carbomer 940 (1.0%), triethanolamine (for pH adjustment to 6.5 ± 0.2), and sodium benzoate (0.1%) as preservative. Quality control of the botanical extracts was performed using HPLC to confirm the presence and concentration of key marker compounds (e.g., myricetin in M. communis and salicin derivatives in P. euphratica), ensuring batch-to-batch consistency.

RANDOMIZATION, BLINDING, AND ALLOCATION CONCEALMENT

A computer-generated randomization sequence with permuted blocks (block size of 6), stratified by age group (7–9 and 10–12 years) and baseline GI severity (for Cohort B), was prepared by an independent statistician. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes.

In Cohort B, a double-dummy design was employed: participants received both a gel and a rinse, only one of which was active based on their assignment. This ensured blinding of participants, caregivers, clinical examiners, and outcome assessors. The success of blinding was quantitatively assessed at the end of the study using Bang’s Blinding Index (BI) for both participants and the principal examiner.

Examiner calibration

Before the study commencement, the two clinical examiners underwent a rigorous calibration exercise to ensure high inter- and intra-examiner reliability for the GI and Plaque Index (PI) scoring. Calibration involved didactic training, visual aids, and clinical examination of 10 non-study subjects. Inter- and intra-examiner agreement was assessed using the weighted Kappa statistic. A minimum acceptable Kappa value of 0.80 was established for both indices, ensuring that all clinical data collection was highly reliable.

STUDY PROCEDURES AND OUTCOME MEASURES

Cohort A (RAU)

Eligible children applied a pea-sized amount of the assigned gel directly to each ulcer four times daily (after meals and at bedtime) for 7 days or until complete healing.

Primary endpoint 1: Time to ulcer healing. Defined as the number of days from baseline until complete re-epithelialization (loss of erythematous halo and fibrin layer) confirmed by visual inspection.

Primary endpoint 2: Ulcer pain. Assessed daily using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R) (0–10), a validated tool for pediatric pain measurement.

Primary endpoint 2: Ulcer pain. Assessed daily using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R) (0–10), a validated tool for pediatric pain measurement.

Secondary outcomes: Ulcer size reduction, proportion of subjects healed by day 7, and recurrence of new ulcers within a 30-day follow-up period.

Secondary outcomes: Ulcer size reduction, proportion of subjects healed by day 7, and recurrence of new ulcers within a 30-day follow-up period.

Cohort B (gingivitis)

Participants were instructed in standardized oral hygiene. Subjects underwent supragingival scaling and polishing with a slow-speed handpiece and pumice 1 day before randomization. Using the double-dummy method, they applied the assigned gel to the gingival margins with a cotton swab and used the assigned rinse for 30 s, both twice daily for 14 days.

Primary outcome: Change in the full-mouth mean Löe–Silness GI from baseline to day 14.

Secondary outcomes: Changes in the Turesky-modified Quigley–Hein PI, the percentage of sites with bleeding on probing (BoP), and patient-reported outcomes (acceptability, taste, and staining).

SAMPLE SIZE CALCULATION

Sample size was calculated a priori for the primary superiority comparisons using PASS 15 software (NCSS, LLC.Kaysville, Utah, USA).

Cohort A: Assuming a median healing time of 7 days for the placebo group (based on Liang et al.[9]), 30 subjects per group were required to detect a clinically meaningful 2-day reduction in median healing time (hazard ratio [HR] ≈ 1.7) with 80% power and a two-sided α of 0.025 (adjusted for two co-primary endpoints). Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, 33 per group (n = 99) were recruited.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. Statistical significance was set at a P value of <0.05, except when adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Missing outcome data (<3%) were handled using mixed-effects modeling under the assumption of missing at random; no multiple imputation was required. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Cohort A: Time-to-healing data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional-hazards models. Between-group differences in pain scores over time were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. A Bonferroni correction was applied to the two co-primary endpoints, yielding a significance threshold of α = 0.025 each.

Cohort A: Time-to-healing data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional-hazards models. Between-group differences in pain scores over time were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. A Bonferroni correction was applied to the two co-primary endpoints, yielding a significance threshold of α = 0.025 each.

Cohort B: Change in GI from baseline to day 14 was analyzed using an analysis of covariance model, with treatment as a factor and baseline GI as a covariate. The noninferiority of HyaluHerb to CHX was assessed by constructing a one-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) (equivalent to a two-sided 90% CI) for the difference in adjusted mean changes (HyaluHerb – CHX). Noninferiority would be concluded if the upper bound of this CI was below the prespecified margin of +0.15.

COHORT B: SECONDARY OUTCOMES (MULTIPLICITY-CONTROLLED)

HyaluHerb improved plaque accumulation and gingival bleeding compared with placebo [Table 4]. To strictly control the family-wise Type I error rate across the four prespecified secondary outcomes in Cohort B (PI, BoP, acceptability, and staining), the Holm–Bonferroni sequential procedure was applied. Under Holm correction, PI and BoP remained statistically significant; acceptability versus placebo remained significant; staining versus placebo did not.

DISCUSSION

Periodontitis is a multifactorial chronic disease associated with biofilm accumulation on tooth surfaces, in which an imbalance of the oral ecosystem affects periodontal tissues and manifests across diverse microbiological and immunological dimensions. It is related to the interaction of dynamic infectious agents (mainly anaerobic bacteria) with host immune responses, environmental exposures, and genetic predisposition.[22]

Oral ulcers are recurrent lesions that can cause distress, leading patients to seek treatment to alleviate pain and facilitate early healing. RAS is one of the prevalent oral mucosal ailments, and its management is rather challenging with no definitive cure being accessible so far.[23]

Topical applications have long been regarded as precise methods for delivering drugs to soft tissues, such as the periodontal ligament, as well as hard structures, including the alveolar bone and cementum. The naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan HA has a high molecular weight ranging from 4,000 to 20,000,000 Da and lacks sulfur. HA is present throughout the body, especially in the extracellular matrix of connective tissues, where it plays an essential role. It can be found in synovial fluid, embryonic mesenchyme, vitreous humor, skin, and many other connective tissues. In dentistry, HA is crucial for maintaining the health of soft periodontal tissues, such as the gingiva and periodontal ligament, as well as hard tissues such as alveolar bone and cementum, contributing to various physiological functions.[24] Many studies have tested the use of HA for alleviating signs and symptoms of RAS.[23]

Herbal treatments have gained significant popularity for managing oral lesions due to their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and healing properties. Main plant-derived bioactive compounds such as curcumin, lycopene, chamomile, Aloe vera, green tea, and coffee have shown promising outcomes in treating numerous oromucosal lesions, including leukoplakia, oral lichen planus, and RAS.[25]

However, the variety of formulations may hinder the influence of chemicals and herbal agents. Discrete herbs prove modest antiseptic action, and therefore combination of various herbs may increase their antibacterial mechanisms.[26]

HA serves as an effective mucoadhesive barrier that prolongs residence time on mucosal surfaces, enhancing localized therapeutic effects. When combined with polyphenols, which exhibit potent antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, it may help modulate inflammation and promote tissue repair.[27] Polyphenols contribute to reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines, improving barrier function and cellular health in mucosal tissues.[28] HA-based hydrogels with polyphenols also demonstrate enhanced wet adhesion and controlled release, supporting sustained therapeutic action in challenging environments such as the oral cavity.[29]

This randomized, double-blind clinical trial assessed the therapeutic performance of HyaluHerb, a HA-based oral gel enhanced with Aloe vera and botanical polyphenols, in pediatric patients with RAU or plaque-induced gingivitis. The results demonstrate clinically significant effects across both conditions, together with excellent tolerability and acceptability, suggesting that this formulation may represent a beneficial non-alcoholic, child-friendly alternative to existing therapies. The interpretation of these results, however, requires appropriate consideration of effect magnitude, biological plausibility, and methodological limitations.[30]

In the RAU cohort, HyaluHerb notably hastened ulcer healing relative to placebo, reducing the average healing time by about 2 days. This improvement is meaningful from the perspective of the natural course of minor aphthous ulcers, which typically resolve within 1–2 weeks and can significantly impair eating, speaking, and daily activities in children. The HR of 1.73 reflects a robust acceleration of epithelial recovery. While the difference in healing time compared with the HA-only gel did not reach statistical significance after applying adjustments for multiple testing, the constant and superior reduction in pain scores suggests that the botanical additives may contribute primarily to symptomatic relief potential. This analgesic benefit is consistent with the known anti-inflammatory actions of acemannan and plant-derived polyphenols, which may modulate cytokine activity and reduce oxidative stress within ulcerative tissues.[31]

HyaluHerb did not influence ulcer recurrence at 30 days, which aligns with the multifactorial nature of RAU, in which genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, nutritional factors, and mucosal micro-trauma impact intermittent occurrences. A topical formulation used only during symptomatic episodes cannot sensibly be anticipated to amend these systemic determinants. Therefore, HyaluHerb should be considered an effective option for acute symptom management and ulcer healing rather than a preventive or disease-modifying therapy.[4,32]

In the gingivitis cohort, HyaluHerb delivered a statistically and clinically significant reduction in gingival inflammation compared with placebo. The adjusted difference in GI surpassed the MCID, and the parallel reductions in plaque levels and BoP support the internal validity of this finding. The magnitude of improvement is consistent with results reported for other non-antimicrobial, short-term gingivitis therapies. The exploratory comparison with CHX requires cautious interpretation. The observed effect size suggests that HyaluHerb approximates, but does not fully match, the anti-gingivitis activity of CHX under these conditions. Consequently, the adjunctive use of such herbal products alongside mechanical plaque control appears promising for managing gingivitis; however, larger trials are needed to confirm these findings and establish optimal protocols.[33,34]

HyaluHerb demonstrated higher acceptability, absence of staining, and good adherence compared with CHX. This has practical clinical implications. Tolerability issues, particularly staining and altered taste, remain the major limitations of CHX use in children and frequently compromise adherence. The absence of these adverse effects enhances the potential role of HyaluHerb and other similar treatments for short-term gingivitis management in pediatric practice. The true value of HyaluHerb lies in its palatability-to-potency ratio. By eliminating the cosmetic and sensory side effects that typically undermine long-term therapy, we transition from a treatment that “works in theory” to one that succeeds in the home. For the pediatric practitioner, this makes HyaluHerb a more viable first-line recommendation for the sustained management of aphthous ulcers and gingivitis.[35,36]

From a cost-effectiveness perspective, HyaluHerb presents a strong value proposition. Its dual effectiveness for both aphthous ulcers and gingivitis can reduce polypharmacy costs. Compared to CHX, HyaluHerb’s significantly lower staining rate (4.8% vs. 59.5%) avoids substantial downstream expenses for dental cleaning and stain management. The observed 6% higher adherence rate with HyaluHerb enhances real-world effectiveness. For practices, a single agent for multiple conditions simplifies prescribing and inventory. Faster ulcer healing also reduces pain-days and potential absenteeism. While a formal economic evaluation is needed, HyaluHerb’s clinical profile suggests superior value for patients with multiple oral conditions or those prioritizing tolerability.[37]

Important limitations must also be recognized, including a single-center setting that may restrict generalizability, a short follow-up period that limits conclusions on long-term benefits, and the absence of microbiological or biomarker analysis, which precludes definitive mechanistic conclusions regarding the formulation’s therapeutic pathways.

Future research should focus on larger, multicenter clinical trials comparing HyaluHerb with CHX, along with mechanistic studies to clarify its biological pathways and longer-term evaluations of adherence, recurrence, and cost-effectiveness to better define its role in pediatric oral healthcare.[38]

CONCLUSIONS

HyaluHerb represents a safe, effective, and child-friendly alternative therapeutic agent for acute episodes of RAU and for treating gingivitis in children. Its efficacy, paired with a noticeable tolerability compared to CHX, positions it as a beneficial adjunct for addressing adherence challenges in pediatric oral care.
