The presence of thyroid autoantibodies is not associated with anemia, hematinic deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in oral lichen planus patients
Authors: Yi-Pang Lee, Yu-Hsueh Wu, Julia Yu-Fong Chang, Yi-Ping Wang, Andy Sun, Chun-Pin Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeOur previous study found that 144 of 588 oral lichen planus (OLP) patients have thyroglobulin antibody (TGA) and/or thyroid microsomal antibodies (TMA) but do not have gastric parietal cell antibody (GPCA, designated as thyroid autoantibodies-positive OLP or TA+OLP patients). To test the role of thyroid autoantibodies in causing microcytosis, anemia, hematinic deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in OLP patients, this study assessed whether 144 TA+OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, hematinic deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than 588 healthy control subjects (HCSs) or 305 TGA/TMA/GPCA-negative (Abs−) OLP patients (designated as Abs−OLP patients).Materials and methodsThe mean corpuscular volume, blood hemoglobin (Hb), and serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine levels were measured and compared between any two of three groups of 144 TA+OLP patients, 305 Abs−OLP patients, and 588 HCSs.ResultsWe found that both 144 TA + OLP patients and 305 Abs−OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than 588 HCSs (all P-values <0.05). However, there were no significant differences in the frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia between 144 TA+OLP patients and 305 Abs−OLP patients (all P-values >0.05).ConclusionThe disease of OLP itself rather than the thyroid autoantibodies plays a pivotal role in causing the significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in TA+OLP patients.Keywords: Oral lichen planus, Thyroid autoantibodies, Anemia, Hematinic deficiencies, Hyperhomocysteinemia

Introduction

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic inflammatory, T cell–mediated oral mucosal disease that occurs more often in middle-aged and elderly women and affects 0.2–3% of the population.1,2 Six clinical types of OLP are recognized: reticular, papular, plaque-like, atrophic/erosive, ulcerative, and bullous. The OLP patients with the last three types usually experience pain, burning sensation, sensitivity to spicy or hot foods, and oral mucosal inflammation that may impair eating and chewing, leading to reduced food intake and subsequent nutrient, especially hematinic, deficiencies. Therefore, evaluation of complete blood count and serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine levels in OLP patients is important before treatment of OLP disease.1–12

Our previous study showed that among 588 OLP patients, 25.2% had anemia, and 16.8%, 10.2%, 1.2%, and 21.1% had serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia, respectively.1 In addition, 23.6%, 27.4%, and 28.4% were seropositive for gastric parietal cell antibody (GPCA), thyroglobulin antibody (TGA), and thyroid microsomal antibody (TMA; also called as anti–thyroid peroxidase antibody or anti-TPO antibody), respectively.3 GPCA destroys gastric parietal cells, causing atrophic gastritis and impairing intrinsic factor (IF) and hydrochloric acid secretion. Loss of IF prevents IF–vitamin B12 complex formation and vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. Vitamin B12 deficiency disrupts DNA synthesis during erythropoiesis, leading to macrocytosis and macrocytic anemia (especially pernicious anemia or PA).13,14 As a cofactor of methionine synthase, vitamin B12 deficiency also blocks homocysteine conversion to methionine, resulting in hyperhomocysteinemia.15,16 Meanwhile, achlorhydria reduces iron release from food and conversion of ferric (Fe3+) to absorbable ferrous (Fe2+) iron in the duodenum, causing iron deficiency and microcytic anemia, particularly iron deficiency anemia (IDA).4,13,14,17,18 Thus, GPCA in OLP patients may induce both macrocytic changes through vitamin B12 malabsorption and microcytic changes through iron malabsorption. TGA and TMA positivity may lead to hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism in approximately 19% of affected OLP patients.3 However, few studies have examined whether TGA and/or TMA positivity significantly influences the frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, hemoglobin (Hb) reduction, iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in OLP patients.19

In this study, we enrolled 144 TGA/TMA-positive but GPCA-negative OLP patients (designated as TA+OLP patients, the main study group), 305 autoantibody-negative OLP patients (designated as Abs−OLP patients, the disease control group; negative for TGA, TMA, and GPCA), and 588 healthy control subjects from our previous study.1 We compared the complete blood count and hematinic and homocysteine profiles of the main study and disease control groups with those of the healthy control subjects to determine whether the 144 TA + OLP or 305 Abs−OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the 588 healthy control subjects. Furthermore, we compared these hematologic and biochemical parameters between the 144 TA+OLP or 305 Abs−OLP patients to assess whether the presence of thyroid autoantibodies significantly influenced the frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb and serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in TA+OLP patients.

Materials and methods

Subjects

In this study, the main study group comprised 144 TA+OLP patients (16 men and 128 women; age range, 20–83 years; mean age, 53.6 ± 14.2 years). The disease control group included 305 Abs−OLP patients (74 men and 231 women; age range, 22–88 years; mean age, 55.5 ± 14.8 years), and the normal control group consisted of 588 healthy control subjects (110 men and 478 women; age range, 20–89 years; mean age, 56.0 ± 14.1 years) retrieved from our previous study.1 All TA + OLP patients, Abs−OLP patients, and healthy control subjects were consecutively examined, diagnosed, and treated at the National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH) from July 2007 to June 2023. The 144 TA+OLP and 305 Abs−OLP patients were enrolled according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria for OLP described in our previous studies.1–12 Healthy controls had no oral mucosal or systemic diseases and no more than dental caries or mild periodontal disease. None of the OLP patients had received treatment for OLP within three months prior to enrollment.

Blood samples were obtained from all participants to determine complete blood count, serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, homocysteine levels, and GPCA status. The laboratory methods for measuring blood Hb, serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, homocysteine, and GPCA have been detailed in our previous reports.1–12,17–30,31–45,46–62 Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the NTUH (202402086RINC).

Statistical analysis

The mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean blood Hb and serum iron, vitamin B12, and homocysteine levels were compared between any two of three groups of 144 TA+OLP patients, 305 Abs−OLP patients, and 588 healthy control subjects by Student’s t-test. The differences in frequencies of microcytosis (defined as MCV <80 fL),54–56 macrocytosis (defined as MCV ≥100 fL),57–59 blood Hb and serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, hyperhomocysteinemia, and serum GPCA positivity were compared between any two of three groups of 144 TA+OLP patients, 305 Abs−OLP patients, and 588 healthy control subjects by chi-square test. Moreover, the frequencies of macrocytic, normocytic or microcytic anemia between 144 TA+OLP patients and 305 Abs−OLP patients were also compared by chi-square test. The result was considered significant if the P-value was less than 0.05.

We found significantly lower MCV, mean blood Hb (for men and women), serum iron (for women only), vitamin B12, and folic acid levels in 144 TA+OLP patients than in 588 healthy control subjects (all P-values <0.01, Table 1). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, microcytosis of red blood cell (RBC) was defined as having MCV <80 fL,54–56 macrocytosis of RBC was defined as having MCV ≥100 fL,57–59 and men with Hb < 13 g/dL and women with Hb < 12 g/dL were defined as having Hb deficiency or anemia.63 Furthermore, patients with the serum iron level <60 μg/dL,64 the serum vitamin B12 level <200 pg/mL,65 or the serum folic acid level <4 ng/mL66 were defined as having serum iron, vitamin B12 or folic acid deficiency, respectively. In addition, patients with the serum homocysteine level >12.0 μM (which was the mean serum homocysteine level of healthy control subjects plus two standard deviations) were defined as having hyper-homocysteinemis. By the above-mentioned definitions, 18.1%, 0.0%, 29.2%, 19.4%, 4.2%, 1.4%, and 10.4% of 144 TA+OLP patients and 11.8%, 2.3%, 21.0%, 12.5%, 4.6%, 1.6%, and 10.8% of 305 Abs−OLP patients were diagnosed as having microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb and serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia, respectively. We found that 144 TA+OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, blood Hb and serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than 588 healthy control subjects (all P-values <0.05, Table 2). However, there were no significant differences in the frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb and serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia between 144 TA+OLP patients and 305 Abs−OLP patients (all P-values >0.05, Table 2). In contrast, 305 Abs−OLP patients still had significantly higher frequency of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb and serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than 588 healthy control subjects (all P-values <0.01, Table 2).

Discussion

To investigate the role of thyroid autoantibodies (TGA and/or TMA) in the development of microcytosis, anemia, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in OLP patients, we compared hematologic parameters between 144 TA+OLP patients and 305 Abs−OLP patients. To assess the effect of OLP itself, we also compared these hematologic parameters between the 144 TA+OLP patients or 305 Abs−OLP patients and 588 healthy control subjects. No significant differences in the frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, or hyperhomocysteinemia were found between the 144 TA+OLP and 305 Abs−OLP patients (all P-values >0.05). However, both 144 TA+OLP and 305 Abs−OLP patients showed significantly higher frequencies of these abnormalities than the 588 healthy control subjects (all P-values <0.05). These findings indicate that thyroid autoantibodies do not play a significant role, whereas the OLP itself is the major factor associated with the increased frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, hematinic deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in both 144 TA+OLP and 305 Abs−OLP patients.

Nevertheless, thyroid autoantibodies were associated with thyroid dysfunction in approximately 19% of 159 TGA/TMA-positive OLP patients with or without GPCA positivity.3 In our previous study, serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were measured in these 159 patients. We found that 18 (11.3%) had decreased TSH levels (<0.4 μIU/mL), suggestive of hyperthyroidism, and 12 (7.6%) had elevated TSH levels (>4.0 μIU/mL), suggestive of hypothyroidism.3 These 12 patients were presumed to have Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (chronic lymphocytic or chronic autoimmune thyroiditis), although thyroid biopsy was not performed to confirm lymphocytic infiltration.72,73 TGA and TMA may damage thyroid follicular cells, and lymphocytic infiltration may further induce follicular atrophy; both mechanisms can lead to hypothyroidism in TGA/TMA-positive OLP patients. 72,73 It remains unclear why TGA and TMA are also associated with hyperthyroidism in some OLP patients. One possibility is concomitant Graves’ disease or the presence of anti–TSH receptor autoantibodies,74 although these were not evaluated in our previous study. Another explanation is “Hashitoxicosis,” a transient early phase of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis in which destruction of the thyroid gland releases excess thyroxine into the bloodstream, causing temporary hyperthyroidism symptoms.75 However, this phase is usually brief, and it is difficult to determine whether our TGA/TMA-positive OLP patients were in this early stage when their TSH levels were measured.75

In this study, although 6 and 2 of the 144 TA+OLP patients had vitamin B12 and folic acid deficiencies, respectively, neither deficiency resulted in macrocytosis or PA in these OLP patients. Nevertheless, anemia was detected in 42 (29.2%) of 144 TA+OLP patients and 64 (21.0%) of 305 Abs−OLP patients. Among the 42 anemic TA+OLP patients, 54.8% had iron deficiency and 14.3% had vitamin B12 deficiency; among the 64 anemic Abs−OLP patients, 35.9% had iron deficiency and 15.6% had vitamin B12 deficiency (Table 3). These results suggest that iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies are two major contributors to anemia (but not PA) in both TA+OLP and Abs−OLP patients.

NA and IDA were the two most common types of anemia in the 42 anemic TA+OLP patients (52.4% had NA and 23.8% had IDA) and in the 64 anemic Abs−OLP patients (53.1% had NA and 20.3% had IDA) (Table 3). NA is commonly associated with chronic or inflammatory diseases, infections, bone marrow hypoplasia, reduced erythropoietin production or response, hemolytic disorders, mild but persistent gastrointestinal blood loss, and cytokine-mediated suppression of erythropoiesis.67–70 Because OLP is a chronic inflammatory, T cell-mediated autoimmune disease that predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly individuals,2 and because our previous studies demonstrated elevated blood levels of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α in OLP patients compared with healthy control subjects, it is reasonable that NA is the predominant type of anemia in both TA+OLP and Abs−OLP patients.10–12

IDA primarily results from iron deficiency.54–56,63,64 The causes of iron deficiency include inadequate dietary iron intake in the elderly; impaired iron absorption in patients with celiac disease, gastrectomy, atrophic gastritis, or Helicobacter pylori infection, or in those taking antacids, H2-receptor antagonists, or proton pump inhibitors; and chronic blood loss due to menorrhagia, hematuria, epistaxis, hemoptysis, hemodialysis, or gastrointestinal disorders.54–56,63,64 Thyroxine is a hormone that regulates the body’s metabolism. In TA+OLP patients with hypothyroidism, a reduced metabolic rate may impair digestive function, leading to decreased iron absorption in the duodenum and subsequent iron deficiency.72,73

The present study identified thalassemia trait-induced anemia in 7 (16.7%) of 42 anemic TA+OLP patients and in 9 (14.1%) of 64 anemic Abs−OLP patients. None of these 16 patients had iron, vitamin B12, or folic acid deficiencies (Table 3). Therefore, the thalassemia trait-induced anemia observed in these 7 TA+OLP and 9 Abs−OLP patients was mainly attributable to genetic mutations leading to reduced production of either the α-globin or β-globin chains of the Hb molecule.71

The findings of this study suggest that OLP patients, regardless of thyroid autoantibody status, are at increased risk of microcytosis, anemia, iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, folic acid deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia compared with healthy control subjects. Therefore, routine hematologic evaluation of complete blood count and measurement of serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine levels should be considered in the comprehensive assessment of OLP patients, particularly those presenting with symptomatic, erosive, or longstanding disease.1,2 Early identification and appropriate management of iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies in OLP patients may help prevent anemia, alleviate fatigue, enhance mucosal healing, and improve overall quality of life. In addition, because NA was the most common pattern observed and OLP is a chronic inflammatory disease, clinicians should be aware of the potential contribution of inflammation-associated anemia and manage OLP patients in a multidisciplinary context when necessary.1,2,67–70 Although thyroid autoantibodies were not significantly associated with increased frequencies of hematinic abnormalities, screening for thyroid dysfunction may still be clinically relevant in TGA/TMA-positive OLP patients.3

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional and observational design precludes determination of a causal relationship between OLP and hematinic deficiencies or anemia. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal associations and potential mechanisms. Second, potential confounding factors that may influence iron and vitamin B12 status, such as dietary habits, gastrointestinal disorders, medication use, menstrual status, and Helicobacter pylori infection, were not systematically evaluated in this study. Third, although the sample size was relatively large and all participants were consecutively recruited from a single tertiary medical center, the study population was derived from one institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Finally, additional autoimmune markers and functional assessments of vitamin B12 deficiency were not examined, which might have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathophysiology and clinical consequences.13,14,65

In conclusion, this study aimed to determine whether the presence of thyroid autoantibodies was associated with significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, hematinic deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in 144 TA+OLP patients compared with 588 healthy control subjects or 305 Abs−OLP patients. We found that both the 144 TA+OLP and 305 Abs−OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, as well as hyperhomocysteinemia, than the 588 healthy control subjects (all P-values <0.05). However, no significant differences were observed between the TA+OLP and Abs−OLP groups in these parameters (all P-values >0.05). These findings indicate that the OLP itself, rather than thyroid autoantibodies, plays a pivotal role in the increased frequencies of microcytosis, anemia, hematinic deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in TA+OLP and Abs−OLP patients.
