Joint morphological and DNA metabarcoding gut content analysis revealed that adult body size, but not biological sex, influences variance in dietary composition within and among cunner wrasse (Tautogolabrus adspersus) populations
Authors: Christopher J.D. Bender, Camden D. Moir, Elizabeth G. Boulding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAssessment of the dietary ecology of organisms is crucial for understanding community ecology. Delineation of food webs requires in-depth understanding of how predatory fish diet varies from species to species, as well as how diet varies across the life history of any single species. The cunner wrasse (Tautogolabrus adspersus) is a common neritic demersal temperate marine fish. Yet, little is known about how its diet changes as a function of ontogeny, size and sex. This study investigated the effect of size (weight, total length, body condition) and sex on dietary composition of cunner across a 1755-km stretch of the northwestern Atlantic coastline using traditional morphological and dietary DNA metabarcoding methods of gut content analysis.ResultsAnalysis of diet using ordination (PCA/PCoA) and statistical tests (PERMANOVA) revealed that size metrics like weight and length significantly influence the diet of this species. Small cunner contained more soft-bodied organisms (ascidians, anemones) whereas larger cunner guts contained more hard-shelled organisms (bivalves, crustaceans). This increase in consumption of hard-shelled organisms with size could be potentially driven by jaw gape limitations of smaller cunners. Sex was rarely found to have a significant effect on diet except in regions wherein only large cunner were present. Limited size ranges within many locales may have reduced our ability to detect size differences in diet.ConclusionsThese results demonstrate that size-based differences in cunner diets do exist, even when regional differences in size are accounted for. These results highlight the significant change in prey consumption with increasing adult size in this species; with a shift in general diet from small soft-bodied macroinvertebrates to larger invertebrates with hard body parts. This work demonstrates how secondary consumers can link different parts of marine food webs even within a single individual’s lifetime.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44479-026-00002-3.

Background

The study of the general dietary ecology of species has long been a focus of ecologists because dietary knowledge can inform food web interactions and niche partitioning, especially in species that are typically primary and/or secondary consumers [1–5]. Investigations of dietary ecology of species at lower trophic levels, and the resulting food web dynamics that arise from these studies, may beget a more holistic understanding of the biotic factors that directly influence the populations of species that are of commercial interest to humans, such as salmonids [6, 7]. This form of ecosystem-based fisheries management is gaining increasing popularity due to how this comprehensive approach to fisheries management enables predictions of population changes over time [2, 7, 8]. These methods attempt to consider all biotic and abiotic factors influencing marine animal populations, rather than relying on the limited scope of single species’ population abundance counts at any given time point [2, 6–8].

One of the most frequently investigated aspects of dietary ecology within a single species involves changes in dietary composition as a function of ontological development and size changes over the course of an individual’s lifetime [5, 6, 9, 10]. This is especially true regarding the dietary ecology of fish species, which frequently undergo large size changes throughout development from small larvae to fully grown adults [5, 6, 9, 10]. Changes in niche partitioning throughout ontological development have been posited as a potential source of food web stability, minimizing interspecific competition as different species develop and grow into different dietary preferences [6]. Beyond early ontological development, variation in the phenotypic traits of adults, including traits like size, have also been demonstrated to mediate predator-prey interactions, thereby determining food web dynamics [4, 11–15]. In certain species, dietary partitioning has been observed to occur even beyond development from larvae to adults, typically driven by size-mediated predatory limitations amongst adults [4, 12]. In these species, gape limitations and jaw muscle physiology can directly influence intraspecific dietary variation, leading to significant differences in dietary composition of adults in different size categories [4, 12]. Optimal foraging theory predicts that individuals should maximize their relative fitness by optimizing prey consumption per unit capture effort, and since phenotypic traits directly influence aspects of a species’ evolutionary ecology such as prey capture, these empirical results seem supported by ecological theory [16].

Dietary composition has been observed to also be further influenced by sex of the individual [9, 17–19]. This has typically been posited to be a result of differences in energetic needs between sexes, although this is frequently species-specific and depends on the life history and behavioural traits of the focal species, and at what stage of sexual development a given individual has reached [9, 17, 19]. For example, differences in dietary preferences are pronounced in many sexually dimorphic species, potentially driven by differential macronutrient and energetic needs, as well as by phenotypic differences leading to variance in foraging efficiencies [20–22]. In fish, a variety of physiological and life-history driven differences between males and females of the same species may drive dietary differentiation by introducing variation in energetic needs for reproduction, growth, and sexual displays amongst other factors [23–25]. Thus, it is clear that understanding food web dynamics as a function of lower trophic species’ predatory ecology, and how said ecology is modulated by phenotypic traits like size and sex, is an important facet of understanding both individual species’ dietary ecology, and marine system food web ecology as a whole.

The cunner wrasse (Tautogolabrus adspersus) is a neritic demersal marine wrasse (Labridae) species native to the Northwestern Atlantic Ocean, ranging from Chesapeake Bay in Virginia to the Strait of Labrador north of Newfoundland [26–30]. The cunner has been used experimentally as a cleaner fish species in Canadian salmonid sea cage aquaculture, motivating recent investigations into the general makeup of its diet across its Atlantic Canadian range [31]. While this study investigated general dietary composition as a function of geographic location and year, it did not investigate the potential effects that body condition, i.e. length and weight relationships, as well as sex have on dietary variation [31]. Sexually mature adult cunner exhibit a moderately wide range of sizes, with different size classes being observed to engage in different reproductive strategies (i.e. territoriality and courtship in large adults versus group spawning among small adults) [32, 33]. Cunner have been observed to show some evidence of sexual dimorphism, with males undergoing a shift in colouration during breeding seasons from a “drab” olive colour to an indigo/blue colouration along their ventral surfaces, with females remaining the “drab” colouration year-round [32]. While one study observed significant differences in dietary composition between male and female cunner collected from the same location [34] this trend has not been re-analyzed since, and there exists no study investigating the effect of adult size on dietary variation in this species. This highlights a need for investigation of these relationships, as these differences may not only influence the biology of this particular species, but also may inform the food web and trophic dynamics in these systems, given the cunner’s role as a primary and secondary consumer of benthic macroinvertebrates [31, 35, 36].

Assessment of dietary composition in the past typically relied on extensive knowledge of organismal taxonomy for visual identification of gut contents and subsequent analysis of dietary preferences [37, 38]. These traditional methods are typically prone to error, especially during investigations of predator species at lower trophic levels that frequently consume soft-bodied prey items including many invertebrates [31, 37–39]. These errors are predicted to become worse over time as the number of individuals that are highly trained in taxonomic identifications continues to dwindle. Recent developments of genetic methods for dietary assessment using DNA barcoding/metabarcoding provide a solution to this issue, by using prey DNA sequences to generate a dietary profile for a given predator species [3, 31, 39–45]. While incredibly powerful, these genetic tools have limitations including quantification of consumption of a prey species, one predator’s dietary “snapshot” at its sampling time, and the potential for sample contamination by non-prey items [46–48]. Nonetheless, combinational analyses of diets using both traditional visual identification of gut contents and modern DNA barcoding approaches is providing ecologists with some of the most powerful tools for dietary assessment in modern history.

Aside from a few studies conducted roughly 40 years ago, there is little published information pertaining to the natural dietary ecology of the cunner wrasse across its entire native range. A recent study investigated general aspects of this facet of this species ecology [31], but did not investigate the potential effects that size and sex may have on dietary composition of this species. This study also did not seek to disseminate the confounding nature of observed regional influences on diet and size/sex-based prey selection of individuals within and across different regions. The lack of separation of the effect of region on dietary composition from the effect of predator body size on the same metrics in this study may have influenced results and conclusions, presenting a need for more detailed analysis. Due to this gap in the literature, this current study investigated the effect that size and sex have on dietary composition of cunner wrasse through both traditional morphological and modern dDNA metabarcoding approaches. Based on trends in the literature surrounding dietary niche partitioning and energetic needs of different sexes, two hypotheses were tested. Size has been demonstrated to be a fundamental limiting factor regarding foraging on the basis of prey capture efficiency [4, 12]; therefore we hypothesized that size (mass in grams [g], total length in millimeters [mm], Fulton’s condition factor [Kc = 100*W/L3, where W is the total weight of the fish and L is its total length]) would significantly influence dietary composition, with smaller individuals having diets that differ significantly from larger individuals. Regarding sexual differences in dietary composition, previous studies [34], combined with our own observations of sexual dimorphism in this species, led to the hypothesis that sex-related differences in energetic needs would lead to marked differences in dietary composition of males versus females.

Methods

Sample collection, morphological identification of gut contents, and DNA extraction

Tautogolabrus adspersus specimens collected for use in this study (N = 225) were obtained in four separate calendar years (2018, 2019, 2022, and 2024), with collections being completed between late July and September of each respective year. Specimens were collected from a total of 16 unique locations across a roughly 1755-km area of the northwestern Atlantic Ocean, ranging from Bay Bulls in Eastern Newfoundland to the Mount Sinai Inlet near Port Jefferson, New York (Fig. 1, Table S1). A total of 20 samples were collected from Hermitage Bay, Newfoundland in 2019 (N = 20), with all Nova Scotian samples collected in 2018 (N = 90), the remainder of Newfoundland samples collected in 2022 (N = 95), and samples from New York (Long Island) were collected in 2024 (N = 20).

All specimens were collected through either baited single hook angling or baited trap (modified minnow traps) techniques, with fishing lines/traps being thrown off of various wharves/docks/jetties into water ranging in depths from 2 to 10 m. Visual observations showed that benthic environments around successful sampling locations typically consisted of heterogeneous substrates, comprised of rocky/cobble bottoms/refugia dispersed amongst sand/gravel patches. All bait used for capture purposes was of terrestrial origin (i.e. chicken, turkey, pork and beef) as to minimize the chance of utilizing bait that would potentially be a natural prey item of T. adspersus. This enabled the subsequent removal of visually identified bait from the morphological analyses, as well as the bait barcode sequences during DNA metabarcoding analysis.

Specimens collected for this study were exclusively adult individuals, with specimens ranging in size from 89 mm to 312 mm in total length (µ = 193.01 mm, standard error (SE) ± 3.24 mm) and between 11 and 412 g in total weight (µ = 134.71 g, SE ± 6.51 g). Following capture, individual Cunner wrasses used in this study were euthanized with a lethal dose of 400 ppm Syncaine® (MS-222 Tricaine Methanesulfonate) of ~ 3.2 g in ~ 8 L of saltwater for 5–10 min. Cunners subject to this dose remain fully unresponsive even if moved into a bucket of fresh sea water. Each individual fish was then weighed and sexed prior to/during the removal of its gastrointestinal (GI) tract. To avoid contamination with foreign DNA, GI tracts were removed by trained personnel wearing DNA-free gloves using sterile dissection equipment including a plastic cutting board treated with DNA eliminASE TM before rinsing in DNA-free water. GI tracts were individually preserved in 70% ethanol within 125 mL Nalgene bottles. These bottles were then transported in coolers with ice to maintain a low temperature then stored in local freezers until they could be placed into − 20 °C freezers for storage at the University of Guelph.

Measurements of the weight of whole GI tracts, the entirety of tract contents, and the weights of identified taxonomic groups were recorded following return to the University of Guelph using an analytical balance (± 0.01 g). Weights recorded represent the wet weight of the specific item of interest at the time of recording. Following recording of GI tract and general content weights, sample contents were morphologically analyzed, and items were identified down to the lowest taxonomic rank possible, then weighed as separate groups. Identifications were completed using a Wild dissecting microscope from Leica Microsystems [49]. Any remaining digested items that could not be reliably identified to specific taxa were categorized as miscellaneous digested matter for subsequent analysis.

Certain samples were entirely free of contents, including two samples from Hermitage Bay (2022), one sample from Pool’s Cove (2022), two samples from Cape Breton (2019), one sample from Lower Sandy Point (2019) and two samples from Port Bickerton West (2019). This left a total of 217 samples for subsequent analyses (N = 217). Two specific samples from Hermitage Bay (2022) only possessed bait, and since bait was removed from all samples wherein it was found when analyzing weight of prey items consumed, these specific samples were removed from the morphological analysis (N = 215). However, since latent DNA from other previously ingested items may still have been present on the bait recovered from these samples, they were included in the metabarcoding analysis (N = 217). Five samples from Cape Breton (Main-a-Dieu) were unable to be sexed with full confidence, and so these samples and their contents were removed from all analysis pertaining to sex. Further reductions in sample size resulted from downstream data filtration processes, the removal of rare detections in cases where ordination techniques were applied to metabarcode data (5% detection threshold as recommended by Deagle et al. [50], and the removal of outliers when comparing size metrics across regions. The summary of sample numbers per specific dataset can be found in Table S2.

Following morphological identification of gut contents, sample contents were dried at room temperature (to remove excess preservative ethanol), and samples underwent a homogenization process using liquid nitrogen and a sterile mortar and pestle. Resulting sample homogenates were then parsed into replicates weighing between 180 and 220 mg, which was determined through the use of analytical balances (± 0.001 g) [51]. A random replicate was then chosen for each sample and placed into reinforced 1.5 mL O-ring-sealed microcentrifuge tubes, which underwent further mechanical shearing through the use of liquid nitrogen, ceramic beads (roughly 1 cm in diameter) and a Thermo Savant FastPrep FP120 homogenizer [52]. For samples whose contents weighed less than this replicate cut-off, sample contents were directly placed into the reinforced tube and mechanically sheared using the homogenizer without use of the mortar and pestle to minimize potential sample loss. This homogenization process was completed following established lab protocols for extraction of DNA from gut contents [31, 45].

DNA from GI tract contents were extracted from the resulting sample homogenates using the Qiagen QIAmp Fast DNA Stool kit [53] Manufacturers protocols were followed barring two specific exceptions: (1) digestion of contents was prolonged within an increased concentration of Proteinase K solution (2–5 h within a 56 °C oven using between 100 and 160 µ of the enzyme), and (2) a smaller final elution volume of 50 mL to further concentrate purified DNA within the collection vials. All extracted DNA was held at -20 °C prior to quantification using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer [54] using Invitrogen’s dsDNA BR Assay Kit (available at https://www.thermofisher.com/order/catalog/product/Q32850). Qubit fluorometry was completed by the staff at the Advanced Analysis Centre for Genomics (AAC) at the University of Guelph [55]. Five negative controls were included during the extraction process to test for DNA contamination, with all controls returning near zero (< 0.001 ng/µL) levels of detected DNA.

PCR amplification and DNA metabarcoding of gut contents

Following DNA extractions, gut content DNA from all samples underwent PCR amplification of the COI region of the mitochondrial genome, using select primers. PCR was completed either by the Hajibabaei Lab in the Centre for Biodiversity Genomics (CBG) at the University of Guelph [56] or by CJDB following the Hajibabaei labs established PCR protocols without the plate labelling steps. Three specific published primer sets were selected for amplification of genetic content within the mitochondrial COI “barcoding” regions during PCR based on expected prey taxa [31]. These included the BR5, F230R and ml-jg primer pairs specifically, with the criteria for choosing these specific primers outlined in a previous paper [31]. Negative controls (without template DNA) were included to validate sample amplification during each round of PCR, with results being verified following PCR through the use of gel electrophoresis. The specific PCR cycling parameters and PCR master mixtures for each primer pair can be found in Bender et al. [31].

For all samples, except those collected in New York in 2024, creation of pooled indexed libraries for the Illumina MiSeq platform was completed at the Centre for Biodiversity Genomics at the University of Guelph, following manufacturers protocols. For the MiSeq-specific sample libraries, dual indexing of generated amplicons occurred through the use of Illumina’s Nextera Indexes in a third PCR process for 12 cycles in order to include sample identifiers, with quantification completed using a Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay. Sequencing of these samples was completed using the MiSeq sequencing platform following manufacturers’ protocols, and using the MiSeq v3 sequencing kit.

For the New York samples specifically, libraries were created for the Illumina NextSeq sequencing platform at the Advanced Analysis Centre at the University of Guelph, following manufacturers protocols. For NextSeq-specific sample libraries, indexing for samples was completed using Illumina’s Nextera unique dual indexes. Resulting libraries were purified with magnetic beads and quantified using Agilent TapeStation, Qubit, and qPCR. A P1 600 cycle sequencing kit designed for the NextSeq 1000 was used to sequence the New York libraries.

The complete sequencing process resulted in a total of 73.36 million total reads, averaging roughly 342,817 forward/reverse reads per sample. Following pairing, total paired-end reads equated roughly 33.43 million reads, averaging around 156,210 reads per sample. Finally, following removal of adapter-only sequences, the trimmed dataset equated a total of around 29.32 million paired-end reads, averaging 137,009 reads per sample.

Following sequencing, pooled libraries were analyzed through the use of a singularity established on the Digital research Alliance of Canada’s (DRAC) FIR servers (DRAC, 2025), on which sample sequences were processed and taxonomically identified through the use of the MetaWorks multi-marker metabarcoding pipeline v1.13 ([57] available on https://github.com/terrimporter/MetaWorks). Specific information regarding the bioinformatic process of both sequence prep (adapter trimming, pseudogene removal, etc.) as well as taxonomic assignment of sequences can be found within the supplemental methods of Bender et al. [31]. The Results and Taxonomy “Eukaryote” output files from the Metaworks pipeline [57] can be found in additional Supplementary files 3 and 4 respectively.

For multivariate analysis of dietary composition as a function of different measured variables, only Metazoan taxa were included. This focus on metazoans was chosen because previous field observations of dietary breadth published for this species that did not show consumption of other non-metazoan eukaryotes (i.e., marine macrophytes) [34]. However, here the percent frequency of occurrence of all detected eukaryotic DNA from the macrobenthos was still visualized to assess whether the chosen primers were capable of amplifying non-metazoan DNA (Fig. S1). Datasets used for metabarcoding analysis varied in sample size as a result of sample loss through the homogenization process, and as a result of sample removal during filtration for low quality sequences. Filtration was completed through application of bootstrap cutoffs below a shared based pair percentage of 30% at the genus level (≥ 0.3 gBP), assuring 95% confidence intervals for each detection. This was in accordance with recommendations by the creators of MetaWorks for accurate taxonomic identifications [57]. Beyond these basic parameters, certain other expected contaminant and bait taxa were removed. This included removal of the predator species (Cunner), bait (Clupea, Aves, Artiodactyls), and contaminants (Primates, Canids, Felids).

Statistical analyses and data visualization

A previous study that also used joint morphological and DNA metabarcoding in the assessment of cunner diets found significant differences in the diet of cunners sampled from different biogeographic regions, but did not correct for differences in the sizes of cunner sampled at each region [31]. In this current study, it became clear that these significant differences in size between specimens from different regions needed to be accounted for in order to properly analyze the effect of size/sex without the confounding variable of sample location.

Pairwise Mann-Whitney U tests conducted on measured weight, length, and condition factor between regional groups revealed significant differences in cunner sizes (weight and length) between certain pairs of regions (Fig. 2, Table S3). In an attempt to account for the effect of region on dietary composition, and because of the significant differences in weight and length of cunner from different regions, additional datasets were created from the following grouping of samples: (1) Large specimens only (Eastern Newfoundland, Southern Newfoundland [2022]), (2) Small specimens only (Cape Breton, Southcentral Nova Scotia, Southwestern Nova Scotia, and New York), and (3) Large + Small specimens only (Eastern Newfoundland, Southern Newfoundland [2022], Cape Breton, Southcentral Nova Scotia, Southwestern Nova Scotia, and New York), with omission of the one region (Southern Newfoundland [2019]) that consisted of an intermediate size class (Fig. 2). This was done for two purposes: (1) to clearly visualize differences in diet strictly as a function of size without the influence of region and size biases amongst the different regions (Fig. 2), and (2) to visualize the extent of potential differences between the two extremes of the size ranges without intermediate sizes convoluting the comparisons. These three additional data groupings were analyzed as separate datasets, with outlier samples in the small group (three Southcentral Nova Scotian samples) being removed from weight-based analyses specifically (Fig.2). The results of clustering samples in this manner resulted in 11 distinct groups to be analyzed independently. Again, specific sample numbers for each of these sample groupings (Morphological and Metabarcoding) can be found in the supplement (Table S2). Note that condition factor of measured cunner from any given region was determined to not be significantly different from all other pairwise comparisons, and thus analysis of this morphometric variable did not require additional dataset filtration (Fig. S2, Table S3).

For morphological analysis of gut contents, variation in diet as a function of a chosen variable (length, weight, sex, etc.) was visualized through the use of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on measured wet weight of identified contents, and the relationship between sample composition and different independent variables was determined through subsequent PERMANOVA analysis conducted on distance matrices generated from sample coordinates along the first two calculated principal components. Correlation plots for each major prey category were generated to visualize what prey items were frequently co-ingested, and vice versa.

For the metabarcoding data, a Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was applied instead of PCA, because of the presence/absence nature of metabarcode detections versus the abundance estimates (i.e. recorded weight of prey items) from the morphological analysis. PCoAs were generated based on distance matrices created through the use of Sørensen’s coefficient, double weighting shared presences over absences [58]. PCoA results were plotted and subsequently statistically analyzed using PERMANOVAs. In addition, percent frequency of occurrence (%FOO) of detected taxa amongst all datasets was compared to visualize differences between dietary composition of specimens across different datasets, in an attempt to identify the taxa responsible for contributing towards potential differences in diet as a result of the measured variables.

All statistical analysis performed for this study was completed using R v4.5.1 within RStudio (v2025.09.1 + 401), using a variety of R packages [59] Primary packages used for analysis included vegan, FactoMineR, and factoextra for ordination (PCA/PCoA) [60–62] as well as vegan and pairwiseAdonis for PERMANOVA and for subsequent post-hoc pairwise analyses of multivariate results [62, 63].

Results

Morphological gut contents analysis: length, weight and condition factor

Morphological identification of gut contents yielded 11 measured variables, corresponding to 10 distinct taxa and an extra “Other” category for all other unidentified contents within each GI tract. Surprisingly, in addition to the identified metazoan macrofauna, chunks of brown macroalgae (Phaeophyceae) were observed in a few cunner GI tracts, and were grouped in with the “Other” category for ordination. Various PCAs revealed differing degrees of clustering arising from different combinations of recorded variables for each cunner specimen (Fig. 3). Subsequent PERMANOVAs revealed weight/length to significantly influence diet composition in the first two principal components of several sample groups (Fig. 3, Fig. S3, Fig. S4, Table S4), while only the Southcentral Nova Scotia group revealed a significant relationship between condition factor and dietary composition (Fig. S5, Table S4). Region was found to have a significant influence on dietary composition as well, although subsequent pairwise analyses revealed that no specific region was different from all other comparisons (Fig. S6, Table S5). Based on the PERMANOVA results, weight and length were interchangeable in terms of the effect size of each variable on overall dietary composition, with weight having a slightly larger effect size across all data (Table S4). Condition factor was not significant in any morphological diet comparison beyond the Southcentral Nova Scotia region, suggesting minimal to no influence on dietary composition (Table S4).

PCAs of the morphological dataset showed that distinct taxa were large contributors to differentiation in diet between larger and smaller cunner. Soft-bodied prey organisms like ascidians and hydroids were frequently consumed by smaller cunner, whereas larger cunner consumed macroinvertebrates with tougher exoskeletons such as various crustaceans, bivalves and gastropods (Fig. 3). These trends were reverberated in multiple datasets (i.e., Southern Newfoundland [2022], All Data), suggesting an overarching pattern between preferential consumption of soft-bodied prey items amongst smallest individuals, and consumption of harder-shelled prey items in larger individuals (Fig. S4). This was not always the case though, as Southcentral Nova Scotian samples demonstrated the inverse trend with smaller individual cunners significantly preferring hard shelled organisms (barnacles: thecostracans, crustaceans: caprellid amphipods) over soft-bodied organisms (ascidians, hydroids) (Fig. S4, Fig. S5, Table S4). Small cunners clustered near the vector arrow end indicating a large number of acorn barnacle wall plates had been found in their guts. Alternatively, the inverse size trend with prey armor in the Southcentral Nova Scotian dataset may reflect a lack of variation in predator size in this region as only small and medium cunners were sampled. (Fig. 2).

Morphological gut contents analysis: sex

Comparisons of dietary composition between sexes within each of the datasets typically did not yield significant results, with only Southern Newfoundland (2022) and the “Large Only” dataset revealing significant associations between dietary composition and sex through PERMANOVA (Fig. 4a, Table S6). Unfortunately, these two datasets included only the largest cunners of those captured across the study, making separation of the effect of sex from the effect of size difficult (Fig. 2). PCAs revealed dietary differentiation in the large dataset was being driven by increased consumption of ascidians and hydroids amongst males, and by increased consumption of gastropods, various crustaceans, and bryozoans amongst females (Fig. 4a). Despite these results being similar to the size-based results in the large dataset, the lack of significant differences between the effect of sex on average size within this region suggests that both sex and size significantly influence on diet in these larger cunners. Contrarily, a substantial overlap in dietary composition of both males and females in the small cunner dataset reinforced the trend that diet does not seem to vary as a function of sex for the smallest size classes sampled (Fig. 4b). No differences in diet with sex results were found in all of the regional datasets that were composed mostly of smaller individual cunners (Fig. 2, Fig. S7).

Metabarcoding gut contents analysis: taxa detections and regional comparisons

The initial output for the MetaWorks options of (1) COI classifier and (2) all Eukaryotic DNA, detected four “kingdoms” (Metazoa, Fungi, Viridiplantae [including green seaweeds {Chlorophyta}], and “undefined Eukaryotes” from various clades [including red {Rhodophyta} and brown algae {Phaeophyceae}]), 42 phyla, 110 classes, 408 orders, 1219 families, 2247 genera, and 3079 species. Following removal of the bait/contaminants as well as filtration at the genus level to a shared base pair level greater or equal to 0.3 (gBP ≥ 0.3),produced a total of four kingdoms, 26 phyla, 60 classes, 152 orders, 296 families, 421 genera, and 583 species were detected. Of particular interest were large percent frequency of occurrence (%FOO) metrics for many non-metazoan Eukaryotes (Fig. S1).

Some of these detected non-metazoan eukaryotes, such as the diatoms Skeletonema, Melosira, and Navicula as well as the dinoflagellates Azadinium and Biecheleria are microscopic. While red macroalgae and brown macroalgae were visually frequently detected inside cunner GI tracts (Fig. S1), the common green macroalgal genera: Ulva and Prasiola (Chlorophyta) were not detected. Further, downstream multivariate analysis for determination of dietary composition, only Metazoan detections were analyzed as we were more confident that most genera would be detected. This resulted in a final comprehensive “metazoan” dataset comprised of detections from 14 phyla, 33 classes, 83 orders, 190 families, 247 genera, and 312 species.

Before investigating the effect of the measured phenotypic variables on dietary composition, the effect of sample region was visualized as well. Comparison of dietary composition across the entirety of collected metazoan data reinforced the findings of Bender et al. [31], highlighting that geographic region itself plays a large role in dictating dietary composition of this cunners (Fig. S8). PERMANOVA tests and subsequent pairwise comparisons revealed that gut sample contents could be clustered into four unique groups, corresponding to Eastern Newfoundland, Southern Newfoundland (2022), New York, and all other samples (Cape Breton, Southcentral Nova Scotia, Southwestern Nova Scotia, Southern Newfoundland [2019]) (Table S7).

Metabarcoding gut contents analysis: length, weight, and condition factor

Dietary composition in cunner wrasse as a function of the three measured quantitative traits as assessed through ordination (PCoA) and subsequent PERMANOVAs did not find significant relationships between cunner size and diet in all datasets. Most region-based datasets did not reveal any effect of weight, length or condition factor on dietary composition (Table S8). However, Southern Newfoundland (2022), the Large Only group, the Large + Small group (Fig. 5), and the All Data group showed significant effects of both weight and length on dietary composition (Table S8). Condition factor was found to be significant in the Large + Small, All Data, and Small Only groups (Table S8).

Amongst all test-dataset combinations, the effect size of weight and length on diet was largest amongst the Large + Small group (Fig. 5, Table S8), as well as the All Data group (Table S8). Visual comparisons of PCoA results for weight and length between the Large Only group (significant) and the Small Only group (non-significant) demonstrated the effect of size on dietary composition (Fig. S9). Small ONLY group individuals had virtually no discernable differences in diet with increasing size, whereas Large ONLY group individuals demonstrated a slight clustering of samples (Fig. S9) based on measured size (weight and length specifically).

The significant effect of size on diet composition was most noticeable in the Small+ Large dataset and in the very large Southern Newfoundland 2022 dataset. In contrast, the absence of significance in the Small Only dataset, directly correlates with lower measured variability in size for the Small Only dataset (Fig. 2). This highlights the importance of sampling a wide size range of predator body sizes to facilitate detection of significant size effects in diet composition.

Further, it is important to note that regional influences may interact with body size here. Certain regions (i.e., Newfoundland samples) were comprised mostly of larger individual cunners (Fig. 2), and therefore direct comparisons of the diets of large and small cunner groupings along the same generated PCoA axis may be subject to the confounding effect of region.

Percent frequency of occurrence analysis of the top 20 detected metazoan genera within three unique datasets (All Data, Large Only, Small Only) identified the taxa that contributed to differences in dietary composition amongst the largest and smallest individual cunners (Fig. 6a). The absence of certain soft-bodied taxa (Ciona, Metridium, Nicolea) within the large group, combined with the presence of hard-bodied taxa like Mytilus, and Gammarus agrees with the PCA results from the morphological data (Fig. 6a). The inverse applies to the small cunner group, with soft-bodied taxa being dominant, and hard-bodied taxa less common, or nearly absent entirely (Fig. 6a). Again, these results come with the caveat that the bulk of individuals comprising each dataset come from geographically distinct regions, and thus region may be a strong confounding factor with cunner size here. This was alleviated in part by comparison of these subgroups to all data, in which the same general trends (i.e., higher %FOO of hard bodied organisms and lower %FOO of soft bodied organisms in the large group compared to All Data; higher %FOO of soft bodied organisms and lower %FOO of hard bodied organisms in the small group compared to all data) are observable (Fig. 6a). These results cumulatively suggest that prey “armour” (i.e., exoskeletons) may limit prey capture in smaller cunner, driving dietary differentiation in size classes within the adults of this species.

Furthermore, %FOO for the 20 most frequently detected Non-metazoan Eukaryotes within three unique datasets (All Data, Large Only, Small Only) identified more potential genera that could contribute to differences in dietary composition amongst the largest and smallest cunners (Fig. 6b). The %FOO for the Small Only cunners was higher for red macroalgae genera (Neoporphyra, Polysiphonia and Scagelia), and the diatom genera (Melosira, Navicula, Nitzschia, Pinnularia) than it was for the Large Only cunners. The only Non-metazoan Eukaryote genera for which the %FOO was much higher for the Large Only cunners than for the Small Only cunners was the apicomplexan Emeria (Fig. 6b). However, a lack of published information pertaining to the ability of the cunner wrasse to properly digest (and thus obtain nutritional value) from these non-metazoan species limits any takeaways about whether their consumption of these species is deliberate or accidental.

Metabarcoding gut contents analysis: sex

The PCoA results and subsequent PERMANOVAs using the metabarcode datasets did not find a significant effect of Sex on dietary composition (Table S9). Only one (Eastern Newfoundland) of the individual regional datasets revealed significant interactions between sex and dietary composition; however, the larger conglomerative datasets (All Data, Large Only, Small Only, Large + Small) detected significant interactions (Table S9). Despite this, effect sizes within these PERMANOVA tests were considerably small, especially in comparison to the large effect of size metrics/region on dietary composition (Table S9). These results suggest sex as a minor influencing variable on dietary composition in this species, but the lack of reproducibility across datasets suggests this relationship may not be truly substantiative.

Discussion

Effect of length, weight, and condition factor on dietary composition

The results presented here support the hypothesis that body size has an effect on dietary composition of cunner wrasse. Specifically, weight and length seem to be significant determining factors for dietary makeup in this species, with smaller individuals preferentially consuming soft-bodied metazoans like ascidians and sea anemones, while larger individuals preyed upon hard bodied organisms like crustaceans and bivalves. A larger percentage of the smaller (Small Only) cunners’ guts contained red macroalgae and diatoms than did the larger (Large Only) cunners’ guts. The effect of weight and length on dietary composition is nearly interchangeable across datasets, often with large effect sizes. Fulton’s condition factor as a variable did not reliably influence the dietary composition of cunner. The relatively low variance in measured values for condition factor may result from its calculation as a ratio such that variance in the numerator is confounded with variance in the denominator. Length and weight in cunner, as in most fish species, are highly correlated and often vary in tandem with each other, which could explain why both variables were simultaneously significant when testing their effects on dietary consumption, especially considering the variation that can be present in this species [29, 35].

The influence of predator body size on prey breadth and composition is incredibly well documented across all forms of life, including many marine fish species [64–68]. Gape size and effective limitation is a well understood consequence of overall body size that dictates predator-prey interactions, limiting smaller individuals/species to prey items that can reliably fit in their mouths/have their morphological defenses overcame [64, 69–71]. Previous studies have found significant relationships not only between the size of the predator/predator gape and the size of potential prey items [66, 68], but also between prey selection by the predator as a result of gape and body size [64, 72]. This is likely driven by biomechanical limitations of jaw morphology, with jaw size eliciting a continuum in prey consumption patterns from small jaws and zooplankton (requiring limited gape size/bite force), medium sized jaws and macroinvertebrates (requiring intermediates of gape size and bite force), and finally large jaws and other fish (requiring large gape sizes for suction feeding) [72].

In the case of the cunner wrasse, it is also likely that gape limitations, and the functional biomechanical constraints they place on predatory activity, drive differences in dietary composition observed in this study. Cunner exhibit a wide range of sizes throughout their ontological development [29, 35], presenting opportunity for niche differentiation based on preferred prey items, likely driven by gape limitations and corresponding body size. Limitations in power output as a result of gape/muscle size may result in smaller cunner being unable to efficiently crush and consume larger macroinvertebrates with hard and thick exoskeletons or shells. However, due to a absence of measurements for gape size/morphometrics and of behavioural observations of prey capture, this relationship could not be investigated in the present study.

Effect of sex on dietary composition

While the results of ordination and PERMANOVAs performed on the data acquired from both gut analysis techniques (morphological and metabarcoding) revealed a significant interaction between size and dietary composition, the effect that sex has on dietary makeup is much less clear. A few sample groupings displayed significant relationships between sex and dietary composition, but the majority of datasets did not (Table S6, Table S8).

Previous findings have sporadically reported variation in dietary composition between sexes in fish species [73–75]. While these differences are occasionally driven by ecological pressures resulting from food scarcity and competition [73, 75], they may also be driven by seasonal prey availability and reproductive needs during breeding seasons [74]. Cunner in particular have been documented to engage in dualistic spawning behaviours dependent on body size, with larger individuals engaging in courtship and territorial behaviours, and smaller individuals engaging in group spawning events [33]. Interestingly, datasets that were comprised of larger individuals showed a significant relationship between sex and dietary preference. While our samples were not collected during breeding seasons, it remains a possibility that, at these larger sizes that elicit differential behaviours related to reproduction, that marked differences in behaviour may require different energetic/nutritional inputs between males and females [33].

The role of different cunner size classes in northwestern Atlantic food webs

The role of the cunner wrasse in neritic marine food webs has been demonstrated to vary with ontological development, as the bulk of prey items constituting the diet of this species may differ from the smallest size classes to the largest (Fig. 6). The significant presence of soft bodied taxa like hydroids and sea anemones alongside the absence of hard bodied taxa like large motile crustaceans within the diets of smaller cunner suggest a significant role of this species in food web coupling, as many of these prey items represent benthic taxa that primarily derive energy from the pelagic food webs through consumption of algal detritus [76]. Young cunner may shunt pelagic-derived energy sources back into the pelagic food web [76]. Small cunner also demonstrated significant consumption of the smallest genera of crustaceans (Caprella, Monocorophium), presumably because gape limitations preventing the consumption of the largest crustacean species. Interestingly, a higher percentage of the small cunner guts contained red macroalgae and diatoms.

Upon maturation to large sizes, cunner dietary composition shifts toward other large hard bodied macroinvertebrates like gammarid crustaceans and bivalves, reinforcing combinatorial food web coupling of benthic and pelagic derived energy sources [77, 78]. Benthic crustaceans like mysid shrimp and various amphipod species play significant roles in energetic transfer from the benthic to pelagic environments, and cunner predation may connect these small crustaceans to larger pelagic species at higher trophic levels that prey on cunners [77]. The significant increase in consumption of Mytilus bivalves amongst the largest cunners further integrates the cunner as a key part of energetic transfer between the pelagic and benthic communities [78].

DNA metabarcoding frequently detected non-metazoan Eukaryotes inside the gut contents of the cunners. Detection of microscopic eukaryote prey may have resulted from accidental consumption while the cunners were foraging on hard substrates, or through ingesting crustaceans that had previously consumed diatoms. However, the detection of large macroalgae such as the kelps ([Laminariales]: Agarum clathratum Laminaria digitata, Saccharina latissimi), various other brown macroalgal groups (Carpomitra, Fucus) and some red macroalgae (Bonnemaisonai, Neoporphyra, Plumaria, Polysiphonia, and Scagelia), may represent deliberate consumed by cunners, either to obtain nutrition from the marine alga itself or in the process of targeting macroalgal-associated metazoan species like caprellid amphipods and bryozoans (i.e., Membranipora species). We observed chunks of brown macroalgae (Phaeophyceae) in cunner GI tracts during our morphological gut analysis supporting the hypothesis that it was being deliberately consumed (Fig. S10, Fig. S11). Indeed, two of the frequently detected macroalgal species in cunner guts: Saccharina latissimi (sugar kelp or kombu) and the Neoporphyra (nori) are widely used by humans as food [79]. All of these genera of marine macroalgae are known to occur in shallow water along the Northeastern Atlantic coast [80].

Limitations of this study

Despite these results, a suite of caveats and confounding variables fundamentally limit the scope and certainty of takeaways of this study. Namely, the lack of large sample sizes at all locations, the limited variation in measured specimen sizes within local groups, and the considerable geographic distribution between compared samples may all greatly influence results. While certain relationships showed repeated significance regardless of dataset (thus potentially minimizing the confounding variables of locale and limited sample size), there remains a need for continued investigation of these relationships with standardized controls for region, benthic macroinvertebrate community composition, and predator size range.

Our metabarcoding frequently detected brown macroalgae (Phaeophyta) and red macroalgae (Rhodophyta), in cunner guts but rarely detected green macroalgae (Chlorophyta) species. This likely results from our three COI primer sets not amplifying most green macroalgae. Similarly, the degenerate mitochondrial primer set COI-5P with inosine substituted for the more variable bases, has been used to identify red macroalgae [81, 82], and brown macroalgae [83] and, despite a lack of historical success for COI [84], does amplify certain genera of green macroalgae (i.e., Ulva) [85]. This inability to amplify most green algae with our 3 COI primers is unlikely to be a consequence of missing taxonomic barcodes within the BOLD database given the high barcoding coverage of the eastern Canadian green seaweeds using alternative primers [86].

Another potential limitation of our study is that while our traditional morphological gut analysis is semiquantitative, the DNA metabarcoding gives presence/absence taxon data. This is not easily rectified by creating and barcoding a mock community as has been shown for insects [87]. We showed that the smaller size-class of cunners contained more soft-bodied invertebrates whereas the larger size-class contained more invertebrates with a hard exoskeleton. It is unlikely that these soft invertebrates and invertebrates with hard exoskeletons release DNA at the same rate, and there seems to be no reliable methods to compare consumption rate per day of small and large cunners.

Conclusions and future work

The results of this study deliver foundational insight into the feeding ecology of the cunner wrasse across the majority of its natural range in the northwestern Atlantic. These results provide support to the hypothesis that dietary composition of the cunner wrasse varies as a function of body size, yet only weak support for the hypothesis that sex influences dietary composition. Certain prey taxa were markedly more common within certain size ranges of cunner, which may be caused by gape limitations that restrict successful feeding on prey that are too large. This study also highlights the ecological role of the cunner in neritic Atlantic food webs, acting as a functional liaison between pelagic and benthic community assemblages.

A limitation of this study is that the results can only suggest gape size as a mechanistic explanation for dietary divergence because total body size was as a proxy. Future work should focus on dietary composition as a function of biting force, gape size, and breadth, and any allometry these traits show with total length in cunners. Future studies should also investigate the role of macroalgal species in the nutrition of different size-classes of the cunner.
