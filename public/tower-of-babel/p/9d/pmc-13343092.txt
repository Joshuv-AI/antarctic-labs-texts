Prevalence and factors associated with bullying based on the Indonesian version of revised Olweus/Bully Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ-R) among senior high school students
Authors: Della Sabrina Marta, I Gusti Ayu Trisna Windiani, I Gusti Ayu Putu Eka Pratiwi, I Gusti Lanang Sidiartha, I Made Arimbawa, Dewi Sutriani Mahalini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchool bullying among adolescents is a significant global public health concern, yet its prevalence and contributing factors in Indonesia remain largely unexplored. The revised Olweus Bully/Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ-r) is a widely recognised tool for assessing school bullying and is currently available in Bahasa. This study aims to determine the prevalence and associated factors of bullying victimisation among senior high school (SHS) students using the Indonesian version of OBVQ-r.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in two public SHS and four private SHS. Participants completed an online questionnaire, including the OBVQ-r, on their mobile phones. Subjects on routine medication or with physical disabilities were excluded.ResultsA total of 1875 high school students were included in this study and 210 were excluded. The prevalence of high school students involved in bullying at school was 10.7% (179 participants), comprising 148 (82.7%) as victims, 29 (16.2%) as perpetrators and 2 (1.1%) as both victims and perpetrators. Participants who reported having a poor relationship with their primary caregiver (adjusted OR (aOR) 2.2; 95% CI 1.5 to 3.3; p<0.001) and those who did not have close friends (aOR 2.4; 95% CI 1.3 to 4.5; p<0.001) were at a higher risk of being involved in bullying.ConclusionThe prevalence of bullying in high school students was 10.7%. Relationship with primary caregiver and number of close friends are associated with involvement in bullying at school.Keywords: Adolescent Health, Cross-Sectional Studies, School Teachers, Developing Countries, Child Health

Introduction

Adolescents attending senior high school (SHS) tend to experience bullying with adverse consequences for physical health, mental well-being, academic performance and long-term outcomes.1 Bullying may manifest as verbal, psychological or physical aggression. During this period, adolescents are in a critical stage of biopsychosocial maturation that influences future health, behaviour and social functioning. They are exposed to a range of biological, psychological and social factors that may affect the completeness of their maturation process.2 As adolescents seek increasing autonomy from parents, peer interactions become more prominent and influential within the school environment.3 Social norms and values also differ across regions due to cultural influences, further affecting patterns of adolescent interaction.

One of the negative impacts found in a study on bullying among high school adolescents in Ghana was suicidal ideation and planning. More than 50% of the study subjects were found to have suicidal ideation and plans within a 1-year period.4 High school adolescents who should have the opportunity to develop themselves and prepare for the future to become quality adults will be hampered if they are involved in bullying. The greater negative impact of bullying in adolescence can also appear in adulthood and interfere with their quality of life.5

High school adolescents who have had freedom from parental monitoring are an age group that is vulnerable to risk factors for youth bullying. Studies found that high testosterone and low cortisol levels are involved in increasing aggressive behaviour in males at puberty, but these hormone levels are not associated with aggressive behaviour in females.6 Children who live with their parents and receive positive support are associated with lower levels of bullying (p<0.05).7 Parenting, which is influenced by parents’ education level and income, is also thought to influence bullying in adolescents.2 8 There are various differences in the characteristics of public and private schools, such as teacher education patterns, teacher-to-student ratio and parental background of students in public and private schools. These differences may affect student behaviour and the incidence of bullying in both types of schools.9 10

The Global School-Based Student Health Survey in Indonesia found that 20.6% of adolescents aged 13–17 years reported experiencing bullying in the past 30 days.11 According to the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) study in 2018, 41% of 15-year-old students in Indonesia have experienced bullying at least more than once in 1 month.12 There is scarce recent data on the incidence of bullying in adolescents in Indonesia that displays a classification of bullying involvement. This study aims to determine the prevalence and associated factors of bullying victimisation among SHS students using the Indonesian version of revised Olweus Bully/Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ-r).

Methods

Study design

This study is a cross-sectional, descriptive-analytic study. Inclusion criteria are high school students aged under 18 years enrolled in private and government schools, selected randomly. Students with mental health disorders, physical disabilities or chronic illnesses are excluded from the study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using a formula for estimating prevalence13 with estimated bullying prevalence of 41% based on previous findings,14 and a margin of error of 5%, the minimum sample size was 372 participants. Considering the multistage random sampling was used, the design effect of 2.0 was applied. The calculated minimum sample size is 819 participants, with a 10% allowance for dropout.

Questionnaire

A multistage random sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, a list of high schools was compiled, consisting of 23 private and 12 public high schools. In the first step of randomisation, four private and two public high schools were selected. In the second step, students from the selected schools were randomly sampled to participate in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from all participating students.

Data were collected using the Indonesian version of the OBVQ-r. The translated version demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability, with Spearman’s correlation coefficients ranging from 0.28 to 0.56 and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.75. The questionnaire was distributed online via Google Forms with additional questions according to the study variables.

Statistical analysis

All data were collected simultaneously as primary data derived from responses to the Indonesian version of OBVQ-r. Associations between a binary outcome and a single categorical independent variable were performed using the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable analyses were performed using logistic regression. The size of the relationship between the independent variable and the dependent variable was reported as ORs and 95% CIs.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study.

Research reporting guideline

For the reporting of the study, the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement was followed.

Results

The study was conducted in six high schools, consisting of four private and two public schools. A total of 1875 students completed the questionnaire. Of these, 210 were excluded: 46 were undergoing mental health treatment, 2 had physical disabilities, 149 had chronic diseases and 13 had both chronic diseases and were undergoing mental health treatment. The final analysis included 1665 participants. The prevalence of bullying involvement among high school adolescents was 10.7% (179/1665). There were 148 participants (82.7%) as victims, 29 (16.2%) were perpetrators and 2 (1.1%) were both victims and perpetrators (figure 1). Discussion

The prevalence of high school adolescents involved in bullying was 10.7% (179 of 1665 subjects), consisting of 148 (82.7%) participants as victims, 29 (16.2%) as perpetrators and 2 (1.1%) as both victims and perpetrators. This study showed a lower prevalence of bullying involvement than other national and international studies. In 2019, the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization reported that 32% of adolescents in the world are victims of school bullying.15 However, the prevalence of bullying varies substantially across regions, ranging from 5.2%, 9.7%, 10.7% to 34.0% in countries such as India, the United Kingdom and the USA.16,19 In Indonesia, 20.6% of adolescents aged 13–17 years reported bullying in the past 30 days in 2015, and 41% of 15-year-old students experienced bullying at least once per month in 2018.11 12 Differences in the prevalence of bullying in schools may be due to differences in data collection and processing methodologies, definitions of bullying, time spans used in surveys and cultural and social characteristics of the populations studied.20 21 A global study also found that variations in bullying prevalence can be influenced by cultural context and school policies.22 23

Factors associated with bullying involvement

This study found that participants who felt they had a poor relationship with their primary caregiver had a greater odds of being involved in school bullying (aOR 2.2; 95% CI 1.5 to 3.3; p<0.001). A supportive parent–child relationship promotes a child’s willingness to share information with them and is associated with lower levels of bullying.24 A good relationship with parents or primary caregivers holds a crucial role in shaping adolescents’ personality, social cognition and self-confidence. Adolescents who feel uncomfortable or unable to share their stories with primary caregivers may grow up with lower self-confidence, receive less emotional support or lack strong role models for developing social skills and coping with challenges outside the home.24,26 In contrast, close and supportive relationships with primary caregivers allow adolescents to develop better social interaction skills, emotional intelligence and the ability to cope with negative experiences.27 28 Supportive parents are also more likely to detect early problems in their child’s friendship relationships and provide necessary guidance. This study also found that subjects who felt they did not have close friends were at higher odds of being involved in bullying (aOR 2.4; 95% CI 1.3 to 4.5; p<0.001). Adolescents who had high levels of social isolation and no close friends were at greater risk of being involved in bullying, both as perpetrators and victims, compared with those who had good social relationships at school. Previous research has found that strong friendship relationships serve as a protective factor against involvement in bullying.29 Therefore, building healthy, supportive social relationships among students is an essential component of bullying prevention efforts in schools.

Factors associated with victims and perpetrators of bullying

Consistent with previous findings, the present study showed that males have a higher odds of being bullied than females (aOR 1.5; 95% CI 1.1 to 2; p=0.02).3 22 30 Males face high societal demands related to masculinity, physical strength or success in certain activities (eg, sports or academic achievement). Male adolescents who are perceived not to meet these social expectations, such as those who are less good at sports, less tall, or less smart, are at risk of being bullied for being perceived as ‘different’ or ‘weak’ within the prevailing social norms of the peer environment.31 This study also found that students in grades X-XI had a lower odds of being bullied than those in grade XII (aOR 0.7; 95% CI 0.5 to 0.9; p=0.02). Bullying involvement may increase with age due to complex social dynamics, status hierarchies and academic pressures at higher school levels, thus increasing the potential for conflict and bullying.32 33 Meanwhile, lower-grade students often come from different backgrounds, and their social interactions are still developing. By grade XII, students tend to interact more and become more familiar. While this helps build bonds, it can also lead to conflicts or interactions that may escalate into bullying, especially as students become more aware of one another’s weaknesses or differences.34

This study shows that lower father’s education is associated with a higher odds of bullying perpetration (aOR=3.1; 95% CI 1.2 to 7.9; p=0.01), as supported by previous studies.35 36 Lower parental education may limit parenting knowledge, interpersonal communication skills and emotional regulation, leading to suboptimal supervision and guidance and thereby allowing children to develop aggressive behaviour towards peers.37 38 Low levels of parental education are often associated with family economic limitations, as the family stress model states that social and economic pressures can reduce the quality of parenting and parental control over children’s behaviour.39 A recent meta-analysis also found that family conditions with less warm parenting, low supervision and minimal parental involvement are significant risk factors for traditional bullying in schools.38 This present study supported that low paternal education is not only an indicator of socioeconomic status but also reflects parenting and communication dynamics within the family that may affect children’s ability to cope with peer pressure and avoid exposure to bullying. Participants with mothers whose income was above the minimum wage had a lower likelihood of engaging in bullying behaviour, with an aOR below 1 (aOR=0.4; 95% CI 0.2 to 0.8; p=0.004). This finding is consistent with previous research, showing that higher socioeconomic status is associated with reduced aggressive behaviour among adolescents.39 Families with higher incomes have greater access to educational resources and non-academic activities, which create a more structured and stable home environment, lower stress levels, fewer adverse childhood experiences and reduced risk of aggressive behaviour.40

Strength, limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations. First, we used retrospective, self-reported data, which may contribute to reporting bias. Particularly among younger students who may be reluctant to disclose any bullying experiences. Second, this study is a cross-sectional design, which is not optimal for assessing long-term risk factors for bullying behaviours. A key strength of this study is the clear differentiation among perpetrators, victims and perpetrator–victims of bullying, which has not been applied in previous research. This approach provides a better understanding of adolescents’ involvement in school bullying. Although the overall prevalence of bullying observed in this study was lower than in other previous studies, bullying in school settings remains a significant concern. A focused group discussion was conducted as an intervention to the findings. A follow-up procedures discussing the role of school, parents and associated peer groups were done. These findings underscore the need for early identification and targeted prevention strategies to address bullying and its potential long-term consequences. Future studies should use longitudinal designs to better assess causal pathways and reduce reporting bias. Early identification and targeted prevention strategies remain essential to address bullying and its long-term consequences.

Conclusion

The prevalence of bullying victims among high school adolescents, according to the Indonesian version of OBVQ-r, was 10.7%, and the prevalence of bullying perpetrators among high school adolescents was 1.7%. Factors associated with victims of bullying among high school adolescents were sex, grade level, relationship with primary caregiver and number of close friends. Factors associated with bullying perpetrators were the father’s education and the mother’s income.
