Progress in cancer control leads to a substantial number of cancer deaths avoided in Canada
Authors: Matthew T Warkentin, Yibing Ruan, Larry F Ellison, Jean-Michel Billette, Alain A Demers, Fei-Fei Liu, Darren R Brenner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is currently not known how many more cancer deaths would have occurred among Canadians if cancer mortality rates were unchanged following various modern human interventions. The objective of this study was to estimate the number of cancer deaths that have been avoided in Canada since the age-standardized overall cancer mortality rate peaked in 1988. We applied the age-specific overall cancer mortality rates from 1988 to the Canadian population for all subsequent years to estimate the number of expected deaths. Avoided cancer deaths were estimated as the difference between the observed and expected number of cancer deaths for each year. Since 1988, there have been 372 584 (standardized mortality ratio = 0.77) and 120 045 (standardized mortality ratio = 0.90) avoided cancer deaths in males and females, respectively (492 629 total). Nearly half a million cancer deaths have been avoided in Canada since the overall cancer mortality rate peaked, which demonstrates the exceptional progress made in modern cancer control in Canada.

Cancer is the leading cause of mortality globally and in Canada (1,2). The number of cancer deaths has been steadily increasing over time, and it is estimated there were 85 100 cancer deaths in Canada in 2022 (2). Cancer mortality has a significant impact on the Canadian population and health-care systems. Despite this seemingly grim outlook, the age-standardized overall cancer mortality rate has been in relatively steady decline since the peak more than 30 years ago.

The steady increase in cancer deaths over time can be attributed, in part, to a growing population and a shift in the distribution to older ages within which cancer is considerably more common. Significant advances in our understanding of cancer etiology and biology have enabled improvements in cancer control strategies that have reduced cancer mortality and prevented cancer deaths. It is currently not known how many more cancer deaths would have occurred among Canadians if cancer mortality rates were unchanged from modern human interventions. The objective of this study was to quantify the reduction in cancer mortality and estimate the number of potential lives saved because of the significant progress made in cancer control in Canada over the past several decades.

The data for this retrospective observational study were collected from four publicly available sources: Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) (3), Statistics Canada (4,5), the 2021 Canadian Cancer Statistics (CCS) report (6), and Brenner et al. (2). Data were downloaded from GLOBOCAN using its web-based graphical user interface. Data from Statistics Canada were obtained through data requests to the Web Data Services application programming interface. Finally, data from the CCS 2021 report and Brenner et al. were manually extracted from published tables.

Age and sex-specific population estimates were obtained from GLOBOCAN (1950-1999) and Statistics Canada (2000-2022). Age- and sex-specific mortality rates (per 100 000) were obtained from GLOBOCAN (1950-1999) and Statistics Canada (2000-2020). The overall number of observed cancer deaths was obtained from GLOBOCAN (1950-1999), Statistics Canada (2000-2020), the 2021 CCS report (2021), and Brenner et al. (2) .

All analyses described herein were performed separately for males and females, where applicable. Age groups were based on 5-year strata until age 84 years; all those aged 85 years and older formed the ≥85 age group. The age-specific number of cancer deaths was reported directly by Statistics Canada from 2000 to 2020. We derived the age-specific number of deaths for 1950 to 1999 using GLOBOCAN crude mortality rates and population counts. We estimated the age-standardized overall cancer mortality rates (per 100 000) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) (7) from 1950 to 2022 based on the 2011 Canadian standard population (8), using age-specific mortality rates and population counts. The age-standardized overall cancer mortality rate peaked in 1988, and the age-specific mortality rates from that year were used to project expected mortality for all subsequent years.

For every year after 1988, the expected number of deaths was estimated using the peak-year age-specific mortality rates and population counts for that year. The number of avoided cancer deaths was estimated as the difference between the expected number and observed number of cancer deaths over that period. We estimated standardized mortality ratios and 95% CI (9) on the basis of the ratio of the observed to expected number of cancer deaths. The relative reduction in expected deaths over time were estimated as the number of avoided deaths divided by the number of expected deaths. All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The numbers of observed, expected, and avoided cancer deaths in the period from 1989 to 2022 (since age-standardized overall cancer mortality rates peaked) are presented in Table 1 and Figure 1, B. In this period, there were 372 584 and 120 045 avoided cancer deaths in males and females, respectively (492 629 total), which corresponds to a 24% (standardized mortality ratio = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.77 to 0.77) and 10% (standardized mortality ratio = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.90 to 0.90) reduction in overall cancer mortality for males and females over that period, respectively, and an 18% reduction combined (standardized mortality ratio = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.82 to 0.82). As of 2022, there has been a 39% reduction in expected mortality for overall cancers in males but only a 21% reduction in females. This difference in the relative reductions in cancer mortality corresponds to a sex ratio (males-to-females) of 1.82 (95% CI = 1.79 to 1.85).

Nearly half a million cancer deaths have been avoided in Canada since overall cancer mortality rates peaked in 1988. We estimated the expected number of cancer deaths that would have occurred in a growing and aging population under a counterfactual scenario where cancer mortality rates had remained at their peak. The relative reductions in cancer mortality have been more significant for males than for females as of 2022.

This study is the largest and most comprehensive to date to estimate the potential impact that modern cancer control initiatives have had on avoided cancer deaths in Canada, based on more than 70 years of detailed historical mortality data. This sizeable reduction in the number of avoided cancer deaths highlights the importance of continued investment along the trajectory of cancer research, from prevention to treatment, to further reduce the impact of cancer in Canada, which unfortunately is still the leading cause of death of Canadians. These estimates provide a great deal of optimism in the often nihilistic discussions of cancer and cancer mortality. A similar study of avoided cancer deaths for overall cancer mortality was shared in the 2023 US cancer statistics report (10). An intriguing comparison is the American Cancer Society’s data of an overall 32% decline in cancer mortality from 1991 to 2019, leading to 3.5 million lives saved in the United States (11). Simple extrapolation of the US data would translate to 350 000 lives saved in Canada, yet our number of approximately 500 000 is higher, even if adjusted for our 34 versus their 28-year period of analysis.

This study has a few limitations that are worth noting. The estimation of expected deaths implicitly assumes that the at-risk pool remains proportional over time. Avoidance of cancer deaths can be primarily attributable to substantial progress in cancer control measures, but competing risks of mortality that preclude death from cancer can also have an effect on lowering cancer-specific mortality. Cancer became the leading cause of mortality in Canada during our study period, however, so it is expected that any changes in mortality for competing causes would not be a primary contributor to the reduction in expected mortality. In our study, the maximum age-standardized mortality rate for females was in achieved 1950, yet it remained fairly stable over the next several decades. We chose 1988 as the peak year for age-standardized mortality rate in females because it is the practical peak and corresponds with the overall peak to enable comparability with estimates from males, with a focus on the effects of modern cancer control initiatives on cancer mortality. The changes in cancer-specific mortality over time, especially by sex, are beyond the scope of this study, but more detailed analyses are underway to assess these cancer-specific differences and put them into historical context.

These dramatic reductions in mortality give hope to future ambitious endeavors to reduce cancer mortality, such as the US Cancer Moonshot and resulting research efforts (12). This study highlights the great strides we have made in modern cancer management in Canada and presents the substantial number of lives saved from premature cancer mortality that may be attributed to the significant progress made over the past several decades in prevention, screening, early detection, and treatment.
