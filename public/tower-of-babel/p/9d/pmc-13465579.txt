Intravascular Large B-cell Lymphoma Presenting as Progressive Cholestatic Liver Dysfunction and Multiorgan Failure Initially Mimicking Acute Calculous Cholecystitis: A Case Report
Authors: Ajinkya A Kulkarni, Aaron Niblock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) is a rare and aggressive subtype of extranodal diffuse large B-cell lymphoma characterised by selective proliferation of neoplastic lymphocytes within the lumina of small blood vessels. The disease often presents with nonspecific constitutional symptoms and can involve multiple organ systems, making diagnosis challenging and frequently delayed.We report the case of a 71-year-old man who presented with recurrent chest infections, significant unintentional weight loss, and abnormal liver function tests. Initial imaging performed as part of a malignancy workup suggested acute calculous cholecystitis, leading to surgical admission. Subsequent magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) demonstrated no imaging evidence supporting cholecystitis but revealed hepatosplenomegaly and periportal lymphadenopathy. Despite broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy and extensive investigation for infectious, autoimmune, and metabolic liver disease, the patient's condition progressively deteriorated, with worsening cholestatic liver dysfunction, hypoxemic respiratory failure, acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, and eventual multiorgan failure. Due to persistent diagnostic uncertainty and ongoing clinical decline, an image-guided liver biopsy was performed. Histopathological analysis demonstrated intrahepatic IVLBCL. Following multidisciplinary discussion, treatment with high-dose methylprednisolone was initiated with the intention of proceeding to rituximab-based chemotherapy. However, the patient's clinical condition continued to deteriorate rapidly, precluding definitive chemotherapy. Following discussions regarding goals of care, palliative management was pursued, and the patient subsequently died in intensive care.This case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with IVLBCL and emphasises the importance of considering an early biopsy, as this rare entity may rapidly progress to multiorgan failure if diagnosis is delayed.

Introduction

Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) is a rare and highly aggressive extranodal subtype of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma characterised by the selective proliferation of malignant lymphoid cells within the lumina of small- and medium-sized blood vessels. It is recognised as a distinct clinicopathological entity in both the International Consensus Classification (ICC) and the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours. Unlike conventional lymphomas, IVLBCL typically lacks significant lymphadenopathy or tumour mass formation, frequently resulting in delayed diagnosis and advanced disease at presentation. The disease has been described as a “great imitator” because of its diverse and often nonspecific clinical manifestations [1-14].

Clinical presentation is highly heterogeneous and depends largely on the organs involved. Common manifestations include constitutional symptoms, fever of unknown origin, neurological abnormalities, cutaneous lesions, respiratory symptoms, bone marrow involvement, hepatosplenomegaly, and progressive multiorgan dysfunction. Diagnostic delay is common because routine imaging studies are frequently unrevealing, and laboratory abnormalities are nonspecific [1-4].

Hepatic involvement is uncommon, and isolated intrahepatic disease is particularly rare. Patients may present with cholestatic jaundice, elevated liver enzymes, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, fever, weight loss, and inflammatory marker elevation. These features frequently mimic infectious, inflammatory, autoimmune, or hepatobiliary disorders, leading to extensive investigation before a diagnosis is established. Several reports have highlighted that hepatic IVLBCL may initially resemble acute hepatobiliary infection, cholangitis, cholecystitis, or unexplained liver failure [5-12].

Histopathological examination remains the diagnostic gold standard. Liver biopsy, bone marrow biopsy, and random skin biopsy have all been described as useful diagnostic modalities, particularly when conventional imaging fails to identify a focal lesion [5]. Early diagnosis is critical because rituximab-containing chemotherapy regimens have significantly improved outcomes compared with historical series; however, prognosis remains poor in patients presenting with advanced organ dysfunction [7].

We report a case of intrahepatic IVLBCL presenting with progressive cholestatic liver dysfunction, respiratory failure, acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, and eventual multiorgan failure. Diagnosis was ultimately achieved through image-guided liver biopsy following an extensive but initially unrevealing diagnostic workup.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old male presented to the Direct Assessment Unit with a two-month history of recurrent lower respiratory tract infections, progressive fatigue, anorexia, and unintentional weight loss of approximately one to two stones. On admission, he was noted to be jaundiced and had markedly elevated inflammatory markers and abnormal liver biochemistry.

Initial blood investigations demonstrated a significant inflammatory response, with C-reactive protein (CRP) persistently exceeding 200 mg/L. Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated at 802 U/L, while ferritin was 165 µg/L. Liver function tests showed a predominantly cholestatic pattern of injury, with progressive hyperbilirubinaemia and markedly elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Over the course of admission, total bilirubin increased from 16 μmol/L to greater than 300 μmol/L, while ALP rose progressively to over 1700 U/L. The platelet count declined progressively from 103 × 10⁹/L on admission to 34 × 10⁹/L over the subsequent week. Serum albumin also declined during admission, reflecting worsening hepatic synthetic function and systemic illness.

Given the significant weight loss and concern for occult malignancy, a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed. Initial imaging demonstrated a non-obstructing gallstone with pericholecystic fluid, reported as suspicious for acute calculous cholecystitis. Moderately enlarged periportal and peripancreatic lymph nodes were also noted. The patient was therefore admitted under the surgical team for further management.

Despite antimicrobial therapy, his clinical condition continued to deteriorate. A triple-phase CT liver subsequently demonstrated marked hepatomegaly measuring 24.8 cm, splenomegaly, numerous periportal lymph nodes up to 14 mm in diameter, early gastro-oesophageal varices, and small-volume ascites. No focal hepatic lesion, portal vein thrombosis, or hepatic vein thrombosis was identified (Figure 1). Further evaluation with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) excluded biliary obstruction and failed to demonstrate evidence of acute cholecystitis. However, persistent hepatosplenomegaly and trace ascites were confirmed. No mechanical explanation for the patient's worsening jaundice and cholestatic liver dysfunction could be identified (Figure 2).

The patient remained on broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy because of persistently elevated inflammatory markers and ongoing concerns regarding occult infection. Extensive investigations for viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, metabolic liver disorders, and other causes of hepatic dysfunction were unrevealing.

During admission, he developed progressive hypoxaemic respiratory failure requiring escalating oxygen therapy. He was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit for high-flow nasal oxygen support. His liver biochemistry continued to worsen, with rapidly rising bilirubin and ALP levels despite the absence of biliary obstruction on imaging.

His clinical course was further complicated by acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, resulting in multiorgan failure. Given the persistent diagnostic uncertainty and relentless clinical deterioration, a multidisciplinary decision was made to pursue image-guided liver biopsy despite the procedural risks.

Histological examination demonstrated large atypical lymphoid cells predominantly confined to hepatic sinusoids and vascular channels, with relative preservation of the underlying hepatic architecture (Figure 3). Immunohistochemistry revealed expression of CD20, CD79a, BCL2, MUM1, c-Myc and partial BCL6 positivity (Figure 4). The neoplastic cells were negative for CD3, CD5, CD10, cyclin D1 and CD30. The findings were consistent with an aggressive non-germinal centre B-cell lymphoma with a predominantly intravascular growth pattern, diagnostic of IVLBCL. The biopsy was reviewed by the regional specialist haematopathology service, which confirmed the diagnosis of intrahepatic IVLBCL.

Following multidisciplinary discussion involving haematology, high-dose methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily) was commenced on the same day with the intention of bridging to emergency R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisolone) chemoimmunotherapy. Unfortunately, the patient continued to deteriorate rapidly within three days of starting methylprednisolone, with worsening respiratory failure, progressive agitation and ongoing multiorgan dysfunction. Given his instability, dependence on organ support, and following discussions with the patient's family, palliative care measures were instituted. The patient died shortly thereafter in the intensive care unit.

Discussion

IVLBCL is an uncommon but highly aggressive lymphoma characterised by selective intravascular proliferation of malignant B lymphocytes. The pathophysiology is thought to involve abnormalities in adhesion molecules and homing receptors that impair the ability of neoplastic lymphocytes to extravasate from the vascular lumen into surrounding tissues. As a result, widespread organ involvement may occur despite minimal lymphadenopathy or absence of detectable tumour masses on imaging [1,2,8].

Diagnosis remains challenging because the disease presents with nonspecific clinical features and frequently masquerades as more common medical conditions. Constitutional symptoms, fever, weight loss, elevated inflammatory markers, hepatosplenomegaly, respiratory failure, neurological symptoms, and unexplained organ dysfunction have all been reported. Several studies have demonstrated that delayed diagnosis is common and is associated with significantly worse outcomes [1-4,8-10].

The present case illustrates many of the diagnostic challenges associated with hepatic IVLBCL. Initial CT imaging suggested acute calculous cholecystitis, leading to admission under the surgical team and an alternative diagnostic pathway. Subsequent MRCP excluded biliary obstruction but demonstrated hepatosplenomegaly and periportal lymphadenopathy without an obvious explanation for the patient's progressive clinical deterioration. Extensive investigations for infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and hepatobiliary disease failed to identify a unifying diagnosis.

Hepatic involvement in IVLBCL is uncommon but increasingly recognised. Published reports have described patients presenting with cholestatic jaundice, acute liver injury, unexplained hepatomegaly, elevated ALP, fever of unknown origin, and rapidly progressive liver failure. Importantly, imaging findings are often nonspecific and may reveal only hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, periportal lymphadenopathy, or no significant abnormality at all. This reflects the predominantly sinusoidal and vascular distribution of malignant lymphocytes rather than the formation of discrete hepatic masses [5,6].

Our patient subsequently developed progressive cholestatic liver dysfunction, hypoxaemic respiratory failure, acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, and eventual multiorgan failure. Similar presentations have been reported in advanced IVLBCL and are associated with poor prognosis. Respiratory manifestations may occur despite minimal radiological abnormalities and are thought to result from diffuse intravascular involvement of the pulmonary microvasculature [1,4,10].

Definitive diagnosis was ultimately established through image-guided liver biopsy. Histological examination demonstrated large atypical B cells confined predominantly to hepatic sinusoids and vascular channels, with expression of CD20, CD79a, BCL2, MUM1, c-Myc, and partial BCL6 positivity, together with absence of CD10, cyclin D1, and CD30 expression, supporting a diagnosis of IVLBCL with a non-germinal centre phenotype. Liver biopsy has repeatedly been shown to be a valuable diagnostic tool in patients with unexplained liver dysfunction and suspected IVLBCL, particularly when thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, or critical illness limit alternative diagnostic approaches [5]. Random skin biopsy has also emerged as a valuable adjunctive diagnostic modality in selected patients [11].

Current treatment strategies generally involve rituximab-containing anthracycline-based chemotherapy regimens such as R-CHOP, which have significantly improved survival compared with historical outcomes. More recent studies continue to support rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy as the standard first-line treatment for IVLBCL while recognising the persistently high risk of central nervous system (CNS) involvement and relapse. Accordingly, contemporary management strategies recommend consideration of CNS prophylaxis in selected patients as part of frontline therapy, although the optimal prophylactic approach remains an area of ongoing debate. High-dose corticosteroid pulse therapy has occasionally been used as a temporising measure in critically ill patients while definitive chemoimmunotherapy is being arranged; however, the supporting evidence is limited. Despite advances in treatment, prognosis remains poor when diagnosis is delayed until the onset of advanced organ dysfunction. In the present case, high-dose methylprednisolone was commenced following histological confirmation, with the intention of proceeding to definitive chemotherapy. Unfortunately, progressive multiorgan failure precluded further disease-directed treatment [7-15].

This case reinforces the importance of considering IVLBCL in patients presenting with unexplained cholestatic liver dysfunction, hepatosplenomegaly, constitutional symptoms, persistently elevated inflammatory markers, and progressive multiorgan failure despite extensive investigation. Earlier consideration of infiltrative haematological malignancy and earlier tissue diagnosis may provide a critical opportunity for timely treatment in this otherwise rapidly fatal disease.

Conclusions

IVLBCL is a rare and aggressive malignancy that may present with nonspecific constitutional symptoms, cholestatic liver dysfunction, hepatosplenomegaly, and progressive multiorgan failure. Diagnosis is frequently delayed because clinical and radiological findings mimic more common hepatobiliary and infectious conditions. This case demonstrates the pivotal role of liver biopsy in establishing the diagnosis and highlights the need for early consideration of IVLBCL in patients with unexplained hepatic dysfunction and systemic deterioration. Earlier recognition may facilitate timely initiation of therapy and improve clinical outcomes.
