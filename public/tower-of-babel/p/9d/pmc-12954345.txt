Haploidentical Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation With Anti‐Thymocyte Globulin in the Treatment of Older Adults With Hematological Malignancy
Authors: Xin Kong, Jingjing Han, Yin Liu, Baoquan Song, Xiaowen Tang, Shengli Xue, Miao Miao, Yue Han, Ying Wang, Suning Chen, Aining Sun, Mingming Hu, Jun Chen, Feng Chen, Jian Zhang, Hui‐Ying Qiu, Depei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesStudies on the efficacy and safety of haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo‐SCT) with anti‐thymocyte globulin (ATG) in patients aged ≥ 60 years remain limited.MethodsWe performed a study presenting data from 54 patients aged ≥ 60 years with hematologic malignancies who underwent haplo‐SCT with ATG.ResultsThe median follow‐up was 15 months. The median overall survival (OS) at 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year was 68.4%, 51.2%, and 47.6%, respectively. The cumulative incidence of non‐relapse mortality (NRM) at 1 and 3 years post haplo‐SCT was 22.9% and 34.8%, respectively. The cumulative incidence of 1‐ and 3‐years relapse rate was 11.3% and 25.6%. The 100‐day cumulative incidence of Grade II–IV acute graft‐versus‐host disease (aGvHD) was 23.1%, while the 3‐year cumulative incidence of extensive chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (cGvHD) was 10.4%. The absence of complete remission at the time of haplo‐SCT was identified as a risk factor for OS, relapse, and NRM.ConclusionsOur study suggests that haplo‐SCT with ATG is a safe and effective treatment option for patients aged ≥ 60 years. Trial Registration: The authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) has been used in patients aged ≥ 60 years for over a decade [1]. Multivariate analyses in older patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) have demonstrated improved overall survival (OS) with allo‐HSCT compared to chemotherapy [2]. Advances in graft processing and manipulation, control of bidirectional T‐cell alloreactivity, graft‐versus‐host disease (GvHD) prophylaxis, and supportive care have significantly reduced procedure‐related mortality, making haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo‐SCT) a viable option for nearly all patients [3]. Given that sibling donors for older patients are often of advanced age, studies have shown that each decade increase in donor age is associated with a 5.5% rise in the hazard ratio for overall mortality [4]. Furthermore, the one‐child policy in China has contributed to a growing number of elderly patients without sibling donors. As a result, haplo‐SCT has become increasingly prevalent, accounting for 63% of allo‐HSCT procedures in China in 2021 [5]. However, data on the efficacy and safety of haplo‐SCT with anti‐thymocyte globulin (ATG) in patients aged ≥ 60 years remain limited.

Patients and Methods

This retrospective study analyzed 54 patients aged ≥ 60 years who underwent haplo‐SCT with ATG between June 2015 and March 2023. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Eligible patients were required to have a hematologic malignancy confirmed according to WHO criteria, be aged ≥ 60 years, and have undergone haplo‐SCT with ATG. Patients were classified based on the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) risk stratification and the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS‐R) or IPSS‐Molecular (IPSS‐M). Acute GvHD (aGvHD) was graded according to the modified Seattle‐Glucksberg criteria [6] and chronic GvHD (cGvHD) according to the revised Seattle criteria [7]. Intensity of conditioning regimens was categorized according to the transplant conditioning intensity (TCI) score and classified as low (TCI ≤ 2), intermediate (TCI 2.5–3.5), or high (TCI 4–6) [8].

OS and relapse‐free survival (RFS) were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves generated in GraphPad Prism 9. Univariate comparisons of risk factors (baseline characteristics) for survival, GvHD, relapse, and non‐relapse mortality (NRM) were performed using the log‐rank test to identify independent prognostic factors. No adjustment was made for baseline characteristics. Cumulative incidences of relapse and NRM were analyzed using Fine and Gray's competing risk model.

Patient Characteristics and Clinical Results

In the haplo‐SCT cohort (Table 1), the median age at transplantation was 63 years (range, 60–75), with 40 men (74%) and 14 women (26%). The cohort included 38 patients with AML (4 intermediate‐risk and 34 high‐risk), 11 with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) (2 intermediate‐risk and 9 high‐ or very high‐risk), 3 with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), 1 with mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL), and 1 with chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML). The median Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation Comorbidity Index (HCT‐CI) was 1 (range, 0–7). Disease status at the time of transplantation was complete remission (CR) (bone marrow [BM] blasts ≤ 5%) in 32 patients, partial remission (PR) (BM blasts 6%–20%) in 12 patients, and active disease (AD) (BM blasts > 20%) in 10 patients. Forty‐one patients received a modified busulfan (BU, 9.6 mg/kg), cyclophosphamide (CY, 3.6 g/m2), and cytarabine (Ara‐C, 2–8 g/m2) conditioning regimen, while eight patients were treated with fludarabine‐busulfan or cytarabine‐based regimens. One patient underwent total body irradiation (TBI, 12 Gy) conditioning, and five patients received other reduced‐intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens. Conditioning intensity was categorized according to TCI score as low (TCI ≤ 2) in 2 patients, intermediate (TCI 2.5–3.5) in 26 patients, and high (TCI 4–6) in 26 patients. The median time from diagnosis to haplo‐SCT was 6 months (range, 2–34).

Haploidentical donors were mainly biological children, including daughters (n = 17) and sons (n = 34). One patient received haplo‐SCT from a haploidentical sibling (brother) and two from a nephew. The median donor age was 37 years (range, 28–55). GvHD prophylaxis included ATG (7.5–10 mg/kg), a calcineurin inhibitor (Cyclosporine A or tacrolimus), mycophenolate mofetil, and methotrexate. Patients were treated with the following ATG (7.5–10 mg/kg) protocol: ATG 2.5 mg/kg/d (on days −5/−4 to −2). The protocol for other immunosuppressive agents used for GvHD prophylaxis: cyclosporine A 3 mg/kg/d (from days −9/−8, intravenous infusion for 24 h, adjust the dosage according to the drug concentration) or tacrolimus 0.1 mg/kg/d (from days −9/−8, intravenous infusion for 24 h, adjust the dosage according to the drug concentration), mycophenolate mofetil 0.5 mg/12 h (from days −9/−8), methotrexate 15 mg/m2/day (on day +1) and 10 mg/m2/day (on days +3, +7, and +11).

Patients received a median of 3.83 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg (range, 2.0–14.86 × 106) from peripheral blood stem cells (PBSC) (n = 25), BM (n = 3), or combined PBSC and BM (n = 26). The median time to neutrophil and platelet engraftment was 11 days (range, 9–20) and 13 days (range, 10–185), respectively. The 100‐day cumulative incidence of platelet engraftment was 85.2% (95% CI: 73.4–92.3).

The 100‐day cumulative incidence of Grade II–IV aGvHD was 23.1% (95% CI: 13.7–36.1), while the 3‐year cumulative incidence of extensive cGvHD was 10.4% (95% CI: 4.5–22.2). No significant risk factors were associated with the development of a/cGvHD in this older cohort.

The median follow‐up was 15 months. Eleven patients experienced relapse following haplo‐SCT, with a median time from transplantation to relapse of 116 days (range, 21–1382). The 1‐ and 3‐year cumulative incidence of relapse was 11.3% (95% CI: 5.2–22.2) and 25.6% (95% CI: 14.6–41.1). Patients who were not in CR at the time of haplo‐SCT had a significantly higher incidence of relapse (HR = 13.6 [3.16–59.0], p = 0.0005), whereas high TCI scores did not influence relapse risk (HR = 0.71 [0.22–2.33], p = 0.57).

The median time from haplo‐SCT to NRM was 179 days (range, 3–1025). The cumulative incidence of NRM at 1 and 3 years post haplo‐SCT was 22.9% (95% CI: 13.3–36.5) and 34.8% (95% CI: 22.7–49.2), respectively. The absence of CR at transplantation was identified as a prognostic factor for NRM (HR = 6.06 [1.91–19.28], p = 0.0017). Neither an HCT‐CI score < 3 (HR = 0.43 [0.15–1.24], p = 0.12) nor a TCI score < 4 (HR = 0.99 [0.38–2.60], p = 0.988) significantly affected NRM.

The median OS was 24 months, with 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year OS rates of 68.4% (95% CI: 55.3–79.3), 51.2% (95% CI: 38.9–64.6), and 47.6% (95% CI: 35.4–61.1), respectively (Figure 1A). The median RFS was 20 months, with 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year RFS rates of 64.6% (95% CI: 51.2–76.2), 49.9% (95% CI: 37.1–62.9), and 46.3% (95% CI: 33.7–59.4), respectively.

A significantly higher 3‐year OS probability was observed in patients who were in CR at haplo‐SCT compared with those in PR or with AD (61.6% [95% CI: 45.3–77.1] vs. 20.5% [95% CI: 10.1–41.4]; HR = 0.24 [0.11–0.55], p = 0.0001, Figure 1B). Figure 1C illustrates three HCT‐CI risk groups, showing the lowest 3‐year OS probability in patients with high‐risk HCT‐CI (≥ 3) (14.4% [95% CI: 4.7–37.8]), followed by patients with HCT‐CI scores of 1–2 (49.6% [95% CI: 32.1–67.9]), and the highest survival probability in those with HCT‐CI scores of 0 (61.4% [95% CI: 35.4–84.8]). However, there was no statistically significant difference among these groups (HR = 0.55 [0.23–1.33], p = 0.1258). The 3‐year OS probability after haplo‐SCT was 49.8% (95% CI: 36.9–62.7) in patients with low/intermediate TCI scores (TCI ≤ 3.5) and 35.7% (95% CI: 23.8–48.5) in those with high TCI scores (TCI > 3.5), with no significant difference between the groups (HR = 0.77 [0.36–1.64], p = 0.49, Figure 1D). At the end of follow‐up, 28 patients (51.9%) had died. The causes of death included relapse (n = 11), infection (n = 8), GvHD (n = 4), hemorrhage (n = 3), graft failure (n = 1), and lung cancer (n = 1).

Discussion

Previous multicenter studies in elderly patients have reported 2‐year survival rates of up to 50% [9]. In a subgroup analysis of haplo‐SCT with post‐transplantation cyclophosphamide (PTCY) in patients aged 60–69 years, 1‐ and 3‐year OS rates were 64% and 48%, respectively. In comparison, our study demonstrated 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year OS rates of 68.4% (95% CI: 55.3–79.3), 51.2% (95% CI: 38.9–64.6), and 47.6% (95% CI: 35.4–61.1), respectively. Older patients receiving high‐dose PTCY have been reported to experience an overall incidence of Grade II–IV aGvHD of 33% [10]. In our study, ATG was used for GvHD prophylaxis, resulting in a Grade II–IV aGvHD incidence of 23.1%. Comparing with historical study, ATG may not appear to increase the risk of aGvHD in older patients undergoing haplo‐SCT.

The feasibility of RIC regimens in patients aged 60–70 years with AML has been demonstrated, with reported reductions in relapse and improvements in long‐term outcomes [11]. A recent study on elderly patients undergoing haplo‐SCT reported a 3‐year cumulative incidence of NRM of 16% [12], whereas this study observed a higher NRM incidence of 34.8%. The TCI score did not significantly affect NRM or OS. It is hypothesized that the elevated 1‐year NRM rate in our cohort may be attributed to multiple factors, including a higher burden of comorbidities in elderly patients, limited availability of effective anti‐infective therapies, and socioeconomic limitations affecting access to medical care. Infection was the most common complication, occurring in 17 patients (31.5%) post haplo‐SCT and resulting in seven deaths, including one case of bloodstream infection leading to septic shock before engraftment. Furthermore, during the COVID‐19 pandemic, two patients progressed rapidly due to COVID‐19‐related pneumonia and eventually died of respiratory failure at 18 and 34 months after transplantation, respectively. The 3‐year cumulative incidence of relapse in our study was 25.6% (95% CI: 14.6–41.1), which is considerably lower than the 46% (95% CI: 37–55) reported for older patients receiving PTCY‐based haplo‐SCT [10]. Whether our haplo‐SCT platform with ATG is comparable to PTCY is still limited by the relatively small sample size, requiring further studies for validation.

Our univariate analysis identified the presence of BM blasts > 5% at the time of haplo‐SCT as a risk factor for OS, relapse, and NRM. These findings highlight the importance of achieving CR1 as the optimal disease stage for allo‐HSCT in patients with high‐ or very high‐risk disease. In our cohort, elderly patients mainly selected haploidentical donors from their offspring, who were generally younger, which may have conferred further transplant benefits. Based on these retrospective observations, our study suggests that haplo‐SCT with ATG is a safe and effective treatment option for patients aged ≥ 60 years. However, due to the limited sample size and the absence of a matched control group with HLA‐matched donors, prospective studies are required to validate these findings further.
