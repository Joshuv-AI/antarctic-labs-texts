Bnip3lb-driven mitophagy maintains fate of the embryonic hematopoietic stem cell pool
Authors: Eleanor Meader, Morgan T Walcheck, Mindy R Leder, Patrice Delaney, Marcelo Falchetti, Ran Jing, Christopher Li, Netra K Kambli, Paul J Wrighton, Wade W Sugden, Mohamad A Najia, Isaac M Oderberg, Vivian M Taylor, Zachary C LeBlanc, Eleanor D Quenzer, Sung-Eun Lim, Thorsten Schlaeger, George Q Daley, Wolfram Goessling, Trista E North
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmbryonic hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) have the clinically valuable ability to undergo substantial proliferative expansion while maintaining multipotency, which remains difficult to replicate in culture. Here, we show that newly specified HSPCs achieve this unique state by precise spatio-temporal regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via Bnip3lb-associated developmentally-programmed mitophagy, a distinct autophagic regulatory mechanism from that of adult HSPCs. While ROS drives HSPC specification in the dorsal aorta, scRNAseq and live-imaging of mitophagy-reporter zebrafish indicate that mitophagy initiates during endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition and colonization of secondary niches. Knockdown of bnip3lb reduces mitophagy and HSPC numbers in the caudal hematopoietic tissue by promoting myeloid-biased differentiation and apoptosis, which can be rescued by antioxidant exposure. Conversely, chemical or genetic induction of mitophagy enhances embryonic HSPC and lymphoid progenitor numbers. Significantly, compound-mediated mitophagy activation improves ex vivo function of HSPCs derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells, enhancing serial-replating hematopoietic colony forming potential.Subject terms: Stem-cell niche, Leukopoiesis, Mitophagy

Introduction

Compared to adult HSCs, embryonic HSCs possess distinctive functional characteristics, including naïve immunity, which allows less rigorous matching for use in therapeutic transplantation and greater ability to proliferatively expand while maintaining full multipotent erythro-myeloid and lymphoid potential1. This notable difference implies activation of specialized mechanisms for establishing these attributes unique to early development. Here, we show that spatio-temporally programmed mitophagy initiated by Bnip3lb, a pathway not utilized by adult HSCs2, is the key to the precise regulation of metabolic state that grants embryonic HSCs their unusual maintenance properties.

In both the embryo and adult, Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), byproducts of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OX/PHOS), act as potent regulators of HSC fate. Normally contained within the mitochondrial outer membrane, overproduction or loss of membrane integrity allows for ROS leakage into the cytoplasm, causing oxidative stress, which can impact or damage cellular components3. Despite this potentially negative attribute, ROS is an essential signaling molecule that influences the balance between hematopoietic proliferation and differentiation3–6. In the adult bone marrow (BM), quiescent HSCs maintain low ROS and glycolytic metabolism4,7,8, then transition to OX/PHOS metabolism, and subsequent ROS production, when cycling and/or driving maturation9. Although this switch is necessary for the production of lineage-restricted blood progenitors, excess or unregulated ROS can lead to exhaustion of the HSC pool via unrestricted differentiation or apoptosis10–19. Conversely, if ROS production is completely inhibited, HSCs are prevented from cycling and producing downstream progenitors, resulting in an inability to fight infection or maintain hematopoietic homeostasis7,15,20,21, particularly in the context of stem cell transplantation. Thus, metabolic state and ROS levels must be exquisitely balanced to support hematopoietic demand for the organism’s lifespan11,22.

Adult HSCs regulate ROS levels using a number of mechanisms, including maintaining quiescence, localizing within hypoxic niches, utilizing glycolytic metabolism15,16,23–27, and employing high levels of mitophagy9,10,28–33. The latter, a specialized form of cellular autophagy, is initiated when proteins on the outer membrane of a mitochondrion are tagged to mark the organelle for destruction or via context-driven transcriptional regulation of mitophagy receptor expression. Autophagy machinery constructs a membrane to surround the mitochondrion, which is then fused to a lysosome, allowing contents to be digested and recycled. Broadly, there are two major pathways controlling mitophagy: the PINK1-Parkin pathway and the receptor-mediated pathway, which can each be regulated both transcriptionally and post-transcriptionally to fine-tune ROS levels within the cell34. PINK1-Parkin-directed mitophagy occurs in tissues, including adult BM HSCs12,20,35, in response to damage and oxidative stress. Loss of membrane integrity leads to PINK1 accumulation on the mitochondrial outer surface36. PINK1 then phosphorylates Parkin, which draws in the autophagy machinery36–38. In contrast, receptor-mediated mitophagy occurs when autophagy machinery is activated by mitophagy receptors, such as FUNDC1, BNIP3, and BNIP3l (formerly NIX, which has two zebrafish homologs, Bnip3la and Bnip3lb), expressed on the surface of a mitochondrion39,40. Receptor-mediated mitophagy is responsive not only to oxidative stress41 but also to hypoxia42 and other developmental cues34; for example, BNIP3l/NIX-associated mitophagy is utilized to create space for hemoglobin in red blood cells2,43, or minimize interference to light in developing corneal lens fibers44–47. While damage-responsive mitophagy is well documented in adult HSCs, no role has been described for receptor-mediated mitophagy, although there is notable heterogeneity in autophagy pathways used by differentiating progenitors of various blood lineages48.

In contrast to detrimental roles generally ascribed to elevated ROS in the adult, our lab, and others, have shown that induction of ROS-mediated transcriptional activation is necessary for specification of HSPCs in the embryo. In this context, ROS elevation, coinciding with the onset of blood flow and initiation of embryonic OX/PHOS metabolism49, stimulates Hypoxia Inducible Factor 1a (HIF1α)-associated gene targets critical for hematovascular development50–52 and sterile inflammatory signaling via the NLRP3-inflammasome49. To explain this apparent shift in the regulatory impact of ROS on HSCs during early embryogenesis versus their mature state, it’s necessary to understand their developmental journey. After arising de novo from hemogenic endothelium (HE) in the ventral dorsal aorta (VDA), via an energetically demanding, metabolically active process termed endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition (EHT)53, HSCs immediately migrate to secondary niches: fetal liver (FL) sinusoids in mammals, or a vascular plexus in the tail called the caudal hematopoietic tissue (CHT) in zebrafish54. Following seeding, cells undergo proliferative expansion to produce sufficient HSCs to fill the terminal niche and aid in populating subsequent waves of myeloid, lymphoid, and erythroid lineages. Notably, during this expansion phase, FL HSCs utilize OX/PHOS metabolism more so than in the adult55. Following this window of exponential growth and differentiation, HSCs migrate to colonize the mammalian BM, or kidney marrow (KM) in fish, where they take on adult characteristics, such as quiescence56,57. The distinct environmental conditions of each niche, and unique activities undertaken by HSCs within them, presumably necessitate differential regulatory mechanisms to balance ROS, although little is known about the use of mitophagy prior to BM colonization.

The work described herein documents the shifting spatiotemporal impact of ROS in the vertebrate embryo and elucidates the key regulatory role of receptor-mediated mitophagy in establishing the lifelong HSPC pool. Through scRNAseq and in vivo fluorescent mitophagy reporter analysis, we show that mitophagy is initiated in nascent HSPCs during EHT, via developmentally programmed expression of the bnip3lb receptor. Gene knockdown studies confirm that embryonic induction of mitophagy is essential for maintenance of the self-renewing HSPC population within the CHT. Furthermore, antioxidant epistasis experiments indicate that mitophagy functions to protect the embryonic HSPC population by regulating ROS levels to balance maintenance of multipotency while allowing for proliferative expansion in the absence of apoptosis. Finally, we reveal that activation of mitophagy via genetic or chemical stimulation, such as via Nicotinamide Riboside (NR), can significantly enhance the numbers of HSPCs in vivo in the zebrafish embryo, as well as promote ex vivo maintenance of long-lived multipotent hematopoietic progenitors from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs).

Results

ROS is essential for HSPC specification, but detrimental as HSPCs leave the VDA niche

We previously reported that elevation in ROS levels downstream of embryonic metabolic activation drives HSPC production in the VDA49,50. Given that sustained ROS stimulation negatively influences adult HSPC maintenance, we sought to understand at which developmental timepoint(s), and by what mechanism, the impact of ROS on embryonic HSPCs is regulated. To begin, we examined the effects of ROS manipulation on HSPCs in the specification niche (the VDA), compared to the expansion niche (the CHT), through targeted manipulation at discrete points in development (Fig. 1A). To elevate ROS levels in vivo, we microinjected morpholino oligonucleotides (MO) to knock down expression of peroxiredoxin 1 (prdx1), an enzyme that acts to reduce hydrogen peroxide and other ROS50. Conversely, incubation with the antioxidant N-acetyl cystine (NAC; 10 µM) was used to decrease ROS levels, as previously described50. In vivo alterations in ROS levels were measured via flow cytometry of disaggregated 72 hpf embryos using relative Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) values of CellROX, a vital dye which fluoresces in the presence of ROS (Fig. 1B, left); use of the Tg(−6.0itga2b:EGFP) [referred to as CD41:EGFP] HSPC-reporter line58 allowed for quantification of ROS levels within CD41lo HSPCs, with statistically significant alterations across treatment groups noted (Fig. 1B, right), as expected. Consistent with our prior report50, increased ROS mediated by prdx1 knockdown promoted expression of the conserved HSPC markers runx1 and cmyb in the VDA at 36 hpf as determined by whole mount in situ hybridization (WISH); representative images of the beads-on-a-string expression pattern of the combined probes in the VDA and a graphic summary of the qualitative phenotypic distribution (high, medium, or low expression bins compared to matched controls) of total embryos scored per condition are shown (Fig. 1C, D, top row). Notably, by 72 hpf, after HSPCs migrated to the CHT, continued ROS elevation in prdx1 morphants substantially reduced runx1/cmyb expression (Fig. 1C, D, bottom row). Similar biphasic alterations in HSPC markers were observed when increasing ROS by H2O2 or 1% glucose treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Specificity of impact on HSPCs in the CHT was confirmed by WISH for individual runx1 and cmyb probes (Supplementary Fig. 1c). In contrast, as opposed to negative effects on HSPC formation in the VDA (Fig. 1C, D, top row), exposure to NAC enhanced the appearance of HSPC markers in the CHT at 72 hpf (Fig. 1C, D, bottom row). This developmental flip was validated by in vivo imaging and further quantified by measuring the CD41:GFPlo HSPC population in the live cell population via flow cytometry58 (Fig. 1E, F; see Supplementary Fig. 1d for gating), confirming both a significant increase in the NAC-treated HSPC population and a significant decrease in the prdx1 morphant HSPC population at 72 hpf (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, the impact of ROS modulation at 48 hpf, a timepoint at which HSPCs continue to bud in the AGM, while simultaneously colonizing the CHT, is more ambiguous (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, middle rows), reflecting a transitional phase for both HSPC localization and ROS regulation.

To assess whether ROS impacts the HSPC population at later stages of development, and/or address potential effects on HSPC-dependent lineage commitment, we analyzed embryos at 120 hpf. Knockdown of prdx1 significantly reduced CD41-labeled HSPCs in the CHT and KM, as confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 1G, H); morphants also had lower expression of rag1 in the thymus (Fig. 1I, J), which was confirmed by flow cytometry of Tg(rag2:GFP) embryos59 as a significant decrease in Rag2+ lymphocytes at 120 hpf (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. 1e). Notably, prdx1 morphants showed only minor changes in expression of the neutrophil marker mpx at this timepoint by WISH or flow cytometry on Tg(mpx:GFP) embryos60 (Supplementary Fig. 1f–i), presumably reflective of primitive myeloid origin from earlier hematopoietic waves61. Interestingly, delayed initiation of NAC treatment, from 48-120 hpf, had little effect on CD41 expression in the KM (Fig. 1H), suggestive of physiological activation of ROS regulatory factor(s) as embryos mature. Together, these data indicate ROS levels must be precisely controlled in HSPCs as development progresses to properly seed terminal niches and maintain balanced differentiation capacity.

scRNAseq analysis shows bnip3lb expression increases in developing HSPCs

To understand how ROS transitions from boosting HSPC numbers in the VDA to reducing them in the CHT and KM, we performed comparative single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) analyses. Using established Tg(kdrl:Has.HRAS-mCherry)s916 vascular reporter embryos62, we microdissected and disaggregated trunk/tail regions, then collected Kdrl+ cells, including hemogenic endothelium and newly formed HSPCs, at 36 hpf and 48 hpf by FACS. Both datasets were processed using a standard Seurat workflow, including: log-normalization, selection of variable features, scaling of all genes, principal component analysis (PCA) on variable genes, construction of a shared nearest-neighbor graph, clustering, and UMAP embedding (Fig. 2A, 36 hpf; Fig. 2B, 48 hpf) using the first ten principal components63–65. Clusters were recoded into biologically interpretable labels: Endothelial Cells (EC), Hemogenic Endothelial Cells (HECs), Hematopoietic Stem Cells (HSCs), Erythroid (Ery), Macrophage (Mac), Mesenchymal Cell (MSC), Neutrophil (Neut), Neural progenitor (NP) based on differentially expressed genes and detection of established HSPC, endothelial, myeloid, and erythroid markers (e.g., cxcr4b, gata2b, itga2b, runx1, spi1b, lyz, mpx, gata1a, hbbe3, klf1, cdh5, kdrl, tie1) (Supplementary Fig. 2a–f). To quantify lineage-related programs, we computed module (activity) scores for three curated gene sets: endothelial (cdh5, kdrl, tie1), HSPC (cxcr4b, gata2b, itga2b, meis1b, mpl, myb, notch1b, runx1, spi1b), and myeloid (lyz, mpx, spi1b) (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d).

We performed differential expression analysis at 36 hpf between HSPCs vs HECs [cluster HSPC 1 vs HECs] and HSPCs vs ECs [clusters HSPC 1 vs EC 1] and at 48 hpf between HSPCs vs ECs [clusters HSPC 1, 2, and 3 merged vs EC 1] using Seurat’s FindMarkers with default parameters. Gene-level statistics were then used as ranked input for GSEA, performed with clusterProfiler’s gseGO against Gene Ontology Biological Process terms using the zebrafish annotation database (org.Dr.eg.db). To harmonize library sizes between HSPCs from the two datasets, we downsampled raw RNA counts in selected HSPC clusters using the DropletUtils downsampleMatrix to a target of ~4600 UMIs per cell. Comparisons at both timepoints revealed a distinct change in metabolic and redox stress signatures as the HECs transitioned to HSPCs (Fig. 2C, D). Compared to either ECs or HECs, HSPCs show enrichment for “Cellular Respiration”, “Oxidative phosphorylation”, and “Mitochondrial ATP Synthesis Coupled to Electron Transport”, consistent with prior reports10,27,55. Furthermore, there is a strong upregulation of ROS-associated networks, such as “Reactive Oxygen Species Metabolic Process”, “Hydrogen peroxide metabolic/catabolic process”, and “Cell Redox homeostasis” in HSPCs compared to HECs. While at 48 hpf we can no longer detect a distinct transitional HEC fraction, when comparing mature HSPCs to ECs, upregulation of mitochondria-driven processes was maintained as a distinguishing feature. Notably, GO terms related to vascular differentiation and cell adhesion were significantly downregulated from ECs to HECs to HSPCs, consistent with their established developmental trajectory66 and supporting the physical and transcriptional changes driving EHT.

As mitophagy is an established mechanism to control oxidative phosphorylation and excess ROS production42, which can impact the balance between HSC maintenance and differentiation in the adult49,52, we directly examined expression of mitophagy-/hypoxia-associated signaling components and transcriptional regulators in the embryonic HSPC cluster at both 36 hpf and 48 hpf67–69. Interestingly, in contrast to adult HSCs, receptor-mediated mitophagy genes (bnip3, bnip3la, bnip3lb, fundc1) were generally expressed at a higher level within the HSPC subcluster than damage-responsive genes (pink1, parkin) at 36 hpf (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, while expression of mitophagy genes increased overall between 36 and 48 hpf, bnip3lb and fundc1 remained more highly expressed than pink1a and parkin in HSPCs at both timepoints (Fig. 2F). Prior reports have demonstrated direct transcriptional regulation of receptor-mediated mitophagy genes, including BNIP3 and BNIP3l by HIF1a42. Expression of the zebrafish paralogs hif1aa and hif1ab was detected in the HSPC fraction, with a strong upregulation noted in the latter across developmental time (Fig. 2F), consistent with previously reported roles in developmental HSC regulation across species51 and response to ROS stimulation52. Together, these gene expression studies are supportive of a role for directed upregulation of mitophagy in HSPCs during or immediately after EHT in response to physiological shifts in embryonic metabolic state and ROS production.

Bnip3lb is expressed in the HSPCs within the CHT, coinciding with increased mitophagy

To document the spatio-temporal onset of mitophagy as it relates to definitive hematopoiesis, we utilized two recently developed fluorescent in vivo mitophagy-reporter zebrafish lines, Tg(ubi:mito-Keima) and Tg(ubi:mito-GR)70. Both tools exploit the change in pH resulting from lysosomal fusion to identify cells undergoing active mitophagy. The Mito-Keima line expresses a single pH-sensitive fluorescent protein, Keima, targeted to mitochondria71; it appears green at neutral pH in healthy mitochondria and shifts to red when exposed to the acidic pH of the lysosomes, as illustrated schematically (Fig. 3A, left). The Mito-GR line uses a fluorescent cassette72, which tandemly expresses both GFP and mCherry in the mitochondria; when undergoing mitophagy, the GFP is quenched by the low pH of the fused lysosome, leaving puncta where the pH-resistant mCherry alone is visible (Fig. 3A, right). After crossing each line to a blue Tg(kdrl:tagBFP)mu293 endothelial reporter line73, mitochondrial dynamics of EHT were examined by live whole mount confocal imaging at 36 hpf (Fig. 3B, C). As anticipated given the previously defined role of BNIP3l-mediated mitophagy in erythroid maturation2,43, mitophagy (false-colored yellow to highlight) was detected in red blood cells in circulation in the dorsal aorta and posterior cardinal vein of Mito-Keima embryos; mitophagy was also visible in discrete cells throughout the niche, including muscle, floorplate, and a subset of ECs, which based on their rounded morphology and position at the VDA/PCV junction (pink arrowhead), were likely HECs or newly specified HSPCs (Fig. 3B). Indeed, time-lapse imaging revealed mitophagy puncta emerging immediately after select Kdrl+ cells appeared to physically undergo EHT (Fig. 3B, inset). To corroborate these findings, we repeated this analysis with Mito-GR embryos (Fig. 3C); notably, by Imaris imaging analysis of the BFP+ population, we calculated a mitophagy index score (ratio of red to green in defined tissue70) which revealed significantly more mitophagy in Kdrl+ cells in the CHT compared to VDA (Fig. 3D). To further validate this observation and eliminate potential confounding analysis of non-hemogenic ECs, we created an HSC-specific Tg(6.0itga2b:EGFP:mito-GR) referred to as Tg(CD41:mito-GR), confirming high levels of mitophagy in HSPCs localized within the vascular plexus of the CHT niche (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

As the scRNAseq revealed a strong metabolic shift toward oxidative phosphorylation and ROS production during the transition from EC to HSPC state, we next sought to understand the connection between ROS levels and mitophagy in the hematovascular niche. Using an established Tg(ubb:HyPer) ROS reporter line74, ROS levels were unexpectedly found to be significantly decreased by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry within HSPCs in the CHT compared to the VDA at 48 hpf, as marked by the Tg(Runx1 + 23:mCherry) reporter75 (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). As this finding was inversely correlated with the presence of mitophagy in wild-type embryos, we next assessed the impact of ROS elevation on Mito-GR reporter activity: increased ROS production mediated by global prdx1 knockdown significantly enhanced mitophagy in Kdrl+ cells, inclusive of EC and newly specified HSPCs, in the CHT at 48 hpf (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 3b), implying that mitophagy is directly activated in response to ROS production. As our scRNAseq data indicated the presence of multiple mitophagy-associated genes during HSPC development, to further confirm and localize expression, we utilized Tg(cmyb:EGFP;kdrl:Has.HRAS-mCherry) embryos76 to perform qPCR analysis for mitophagy-related genes on FACS-purified subfractions: Kdrl-/cMyb+ (myeloid), Kdrl + /cMyb- (EC), Kdrl + /cMyb+ (HSPC), and Kdrl-/cMyb- (double negative control, inclusive of the remainder of live cells at 48 hpf). While most mitophagy genes were expressed in the cMyb+ myeloid fraction, inclusive of progenitors and primitive myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. 3e–j); only bnip3lb and pink1a were enriched in Kdrl+cMyb+ HSPCs (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 3e). Whole embryo qPCR analysis revealed that bnip3lb expression increased significantly between 36 and 72 hpf (Fig. 3G), coinciding with VDA to CHT transition, and cellular localization of bnip3lb by fluorescent WISH (FISH) showed co-expression with runx1 within the CHT vascular plexus (Supplementary Fig. 3k). Furthermore, while pink1a expression was unchanged by qPCR at 48 hpf (Fig. 3H), bnip3lb was increased in prdx1 morphants (Fig. 3I) and decreased in response to NAC treatment (10 µM, 36–48 hpf) (Fig. 3J), indicating precise regulation in response to ROS level.

Whereas many mitophagy-associated genes are regulated post transcriptionally in response to danger or stress cues, including oxidative stress, hypoxia, and/or DNA damage, our data underscore transcriptional upregulation of bnip3lb expression during embryonic hematopoiesis (Fig. 2E). BNIP3l/NIX has an established role in developmentally programmed mitophagy, regulated by metabolism-responsive transcription factors such as FOXO367 and HIF1α68. In line with our scRNAseq data, hif1aa and hif1ab are both present in the cMyb+ myeloid fraction by qPCR (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Fig. 3j), and hif1ab shows additional enrichment in the HSPC cluster, similar to bnip3lb. Importantly, hif1ab is the primary Hif1α paralog driving HSC formation in zebrafish52 and a previously established regulator of bnip3lb expression42. While MO-mediated knockdown of hif1ab77 alone was insufficient to alter bnip3lb expression by qPCR on tail isolates at 72 hpf (Supplementary Fig. 3l), indicative of redundant or alternative routes of genomic regulation, induction of Hif1α transcriptional activity via exposure to the chemical hypoxia mimetic DMOG78 (10 µM, 48–72 hpf) significantly increased expression of bnip3lb (Fig. 3L), but not that of pink1a (Fig. 3M), in the CHT. Together, this data implies that ROS-mediated Hif1α activation drives onset of Bnip3lb-associated developmentally programmed mitophagy in newly specified HSPCs.

Bnip3lb knockdown reduces embryonic HSPC and lymphoid populations post EHT

Given the developmentally synchronized induction of mitophagy and bnip3lb expression, we sought to determine if Bnip3lb-associated mitophagy is necessary for the establishment and/or maintenance of the embryonic HSPC pool. Using a previously validated splice junction-targeting MO for bnip3lb, bnip3lb-MO70, we established an effective dose to knockdown expression (Fig. 4A) with no observable effect on vascular or primitive erythrocyte development (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Knockdown of bnip3lb significantly reduced mitophagy puncta within budding Kdrl+ cells in the VDA compared to matched controls (Fig. 4B, C). Consistent with the developmental timing of bnip3lb expression and Mito-GR mitophagy reporter activity, HSPC marker expression in the VDA at 36 hpf was relatively unchanged to modestly enhanced in morphants (Fig. 4D, E); however, by 72 hpf, bnip3lb proved essential for maintaining runx1/cmyb expression in the CHT (Fig. 4F, G). A significant reduction in CD41lo HSPCs was confirmed by flow cytometry at 72 hpf (Fig. 4H) and replicated using an alternative MO targeting the ATG region of bnip3lb (Fig. 4I; Supplementary Fig. 4c). Importantly, MO-mediated targeting of pink1a, bnip3la, bnip3 or fundc1 (Supplementary Fig. 4d), had no effect on CD41lo HSPC number by FACS at 72 hpf.

To verify the impact of Bnip3lb receptor-mediated mitophagy on HSPC development, we generated a bnip3lb mutant line, as previously described70. Mutants showed an unexpected increase in HSPCs in the CHT by runx1/cmyb WISH at 72 hpf (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Notably, however, mutant embryos demonstrated significant upregulation of other mitophagy related genes (Supplementary Fig. 4g). In addition, the impact of bnip3lb loss was strongly mitigated by combinatorial injection of morpholino against the bnip3lb homolog, bnip3la (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f), suggesting that the absence of an HSPC effect in bnip3lb mutant embryos was likely due to genetic compensation, as reported for other gene families79–81. To further validate the impact of bnip3lb knockdown, we successfully generated bnip3lb crispant embryos by targeting sequences in exons 1–3 using published protocols82 and verified through Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 4h); in line with our morphant analysis, crispant embryos exhibited significant reductions in CD41lo HSPCs by FACS at 72 hpf (Fig. 4J).

Interestingly, at 48 hpf during VDA to CHT transition, runx1/cmyb expression in the aortic niche was noticeably enhanced in bnip3lb morphants (Supplementary Fig. 4i, j), in line with prior reports50, indicating that elevated ROS levels may continue to stimulate HSPCs undergoing EHT at this timepoint and/or suggesting that initiation of mitophagy may play a role in HSPC egress or migration to the CHT, consistent with the scRNAseq data. Furthermore, evaluation of hematopoietic development at later embryonic timepoints revealed that reduced mitophagy in bnip3lb morphants was associated with decreased numbers of lymphoid progenitors, as marked by WISH for rag1 in the thymus at 120 hpf and confirmed by flow cytometry using the established Tg(rag2:gfp) lymphoid reporter (Fig. 4K–M)59. A corresponding increase in myeloid fate, as determined by WISH for mpx and flow cytometry with Tg(mpx:GFP) neutrophil reporter line (Supplementary Fig. 4k–m), was also noted, suggestive of lineage skewing and/or the failure of progenitors to migrate to or effectively seed subsequent niches.

Bnip3lb protects HSPCs from ROS-driven apoptosis in the CHT

We next sought to understand the mechanism by which loss of Bnip3lb-driven mitophagy impacted HSC fate. While mitophagy is regulated by multiple pathways via complex and compensatory mechanisms, canonical pathways converge on LC3-dependent autophagosome formation and lysosomal fusion for degradation of targeted components39. Chemical inhibition of the final stage of mitophagy, via blocking lysosomal acidification with bafilomycin (50 µM) treatment, mimicked the effect of bnip3lb knockdown, resulting in a dramatic reduction in runx1 and cmyb expression in the CHT and KM (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d), emphasizing the importance of mitophagy in secondary hematopoietic niches. Based on reports in adult stem cells34, and our analysis of the impact of spatio-temporal metabolic flux on HSPC development, we hypothesized that Bnip3lb-associated mitophagy is responsible for regulating ROS levels to promote survival and balance developmental lineage commitment with HSPC expansion to allow for production of the lifelong HSPC pool. To begin to validate this conjecture, the impact of bnip3lb knockdown was assessed by flow cytometry for CellROX in CD41:EGFP reporter embryos: while ROS was not statistically elevated in the embryo as a whole at this timepoint (Fig. 5A, top), it was significantly increased within the CD41lo HSPC population (Fig. 5A, bottom). A similar trend for ROS in HSPCs was observed using an independent Runx1+ reporter line, Tg(Mmu.Runx1:EGFP) (Supplementary Fig. 5e)75. To determine if increased ROS was responsible for the reduction of HSPCs in the CHT of bnip3lb morphants, we conducted an epistasis analysis with or without NAC treatment (48–72 hpf). Knockdown of bnip3lb reduced HSPCs in the CHT at 72 hpf, while NAC enhanced their appearance, as expected (Fig. 5B, C); furthermore, NAC-mediated ROS reduction was sufficient to rescue HSPC gene expression after bnip3lb knockdown by WISH (Fig. 5B, C). This epistatic relationship with NAC was confirmed and quantified by flow cytometry of CD41lo HSPCs at 72 hpf using either the splice- (Fig. 5D) or ATG-targeted bnip3lb-MOs (Supplementary Fig. 5f), indicating that ROS is the primary driver of the observed HSPC phenotypes following loss of developmentally programmed mitophagy.

We next aimed to clarify the cellular mechanisms by which unregulated ROS impacts the embryonic HSPC population. ROS can promote or impede cell cycling, depending on the cellular context and dose2. Using a FACS-based EdU incorporation assay, we found that whereas the number of dividing cells in the whole embryo remained relatively constant (Fig. 5E), there was a significant reduction in the proportion of dividing CD41lo HSPCs in bnip3lb morphants (Fig. 5F). While reduced proliferation and alterations in lineage commitment (see Fig. 4) might fully explain the impact of loss of mitophagy on HSPC maintenance in the CHT, high and/or chronic ROS stimulation can also drive apoptosis51. Analysis of a panel of apoptotic genes by qPCR at 72 hpf revealed a strongly significant response to bnip3lb knockdown overall, with significantly increased expression of the pro-apoptotic gene, caspase casp2, as well as noxa1, a gene critical for mitochondria-mediated apoptosis83, in particular (Fig. 5G). To explore a potential role for cell death in HSPC depletion in bnip3lb morphants, we employed Acridine Orange, a marker for dead and dying cells, in Tg(Mmu.Runx1:NLS-mCherry)cz201 embryos. While the overall number of Runx1+ HSPCs was reduced, acridine orange staining revealed a pronounced enhancement in the fraction of dual-labeled HSPCs in the CHT of bnip3lb morphants compared to controls, indicative of increased apoptosis (Fig. 5H, I); notably, this effect was rescued by NAC exposure, resulting in less cell death bnip3lb morphants. Taken together, Bnip3lb-associated mitophagy appears to act to maintain the number of newly derived HSPCs in the developing embryo by allowing for proliferative expansion, while protecting cells from ROS-induced apoptosis and/or premature lineage commitment.

Mitophagy activation enlarges the HSPC population and promotes lymphoid development

To further validate the role of mitophagy in the establishment and/or maintenance of the multipotent HSPC population during embryonic development, we aimed to understand whether stimulation of mitophagy could further boost HPSC numbers in vivo. While Bnip3lb-mediated mitophagy appears to be the developmentally programmed pathway initiated during EHT to regulate ROS, expression of alternative mitophagy mediators was noted to increase over the VDA to CHT hematopoietic transition by scRNAseq (see Fig. 2E, F), suggesting that alternative pathways of activation may become functional in HSPCs. To this end, we exploited an established ΔOTC construct, which employs a clinically-relevant mutated form of mitochondrial matrix protein ornithine transcarbamylase to induce mitophagy via the misfolded protein response84–86. We engineered this gene to be expressed under the heat-shock promoter to form an inducible Tg(hsp70:ΔOTC) mitophagy activator line. Following heat-shock stimulation at 48 hpf, dual transgenic Tg(hsp70:ΔOTC;ubi:mito-GR) embryos showed an increase in overall mitophagy reporter expression within the CHT compared to matched controls (Fig. 6A, B). Using Tg(hsp70:ΔOTC;CD41:EGFP) embryos, ΔOTC induction (at 48 hpf) drove a significant expansion in CD41lo HSPCs by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry at 72 hpf (Fig. 6C, D). This positive outcome for mitophagy stimulation was noted in the CHT as early as 48 hpf by WISH for runx1/cmyb (Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). Furthermore, ΔOTC-stimulated mitophagy appeared to promote lymphoid lineage development, whereby a considerable increase in Rag1/2+ lymphoid cells was noted by WISH and flow cytometry at 120 hpf (Fig. 6E–G), without affecting the Mpx+ myeloid population (Supplementary Fig. 6e–g).

To confirm and extend these observations to facilitate potential translational application, we examined the impact of compound-mediated induction of mitophagy on HSPC development. Several classes of chemicals have been previously documented to stimulate mitophagy in vitro and/or in vivo via differing routes of action, including: carbonyl cyanide-trifluoromethoxyphenyl hydrazone (FCCP), which depolarizes mitochondrial membranes to engage the pink1-parkin pathway87; Nicotinamide riboside (NR), a NAD+ precursor which promotes transcriptional upregulation of mitophagy associated genes88,89; and AMPK activator CAS-84927-81-7 (referred to as CAS-849), which upregulates mitophagy via the mTOR pathway90,91. Notably, all three compounds stimulated mitophagy in vivo in Tg(ubi:mito-GR) embryos at 48 hpf (Supplementary Fig. 6h–j). In line with our spatio-temporal observations for bnip3lb loss, we saw little impact for stimulation of mitophagy on specification of HSPCs in the VDA at 36 hpf (Fig. 6H (upper), I). In contrast, runx1/cmyb expression was significantly increased in the CHT by WISH at 72 hpf following NR, FCCP or CAS-849 treatment (Fig. 6H (lower), J), which was confirmed as an increase in CD41lo HSCs by flow cytometry (Fig. 6K). Again, a positive impact for mitophagy activation was observed as early as 48 hpf, as HSPCs migrate from the VDA to CHT, by WISH (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d), suggesting that compound-driven stimulation of mitophagy may be a viable avenue to promote HSPC expansion following EHT.

NR-driven mitophagy sustains colony-forming potential in hiPSC-derived hematopoietic progenitors

As a well-established issue with production or maintenance of HSPCs in culture is limited multipotency and/or self-renewal capabilities, we next aimed to explore whether targeted stimulation of mitophagy might be functionally beneficial ex vivo. In addition to being the most consistent of the tested compounds in enhancing mitophagy in zebrafish embryos, NR is well tolerated in vivo and in vitro88,89. To confirm that the effects of NR stimulation on HSPC formation were due to mitophagy, we employed bafilomycin epistasis analysis. As anticipated, NR treatment significantly increased the CD41lo HSC population in the CHT by flow cytometry at 72 hpf; however, NR was unable to mediate this effect in the setting of simultaneous exposure to bafilomycin (Fig. 7A). To validate a stimulatory role for mitophagy, more generally, and not alternative NR-dependent processes mediated by NAD+ flux, we repeated the epistasis experiment with FCCP and found similar results (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, NR stimulation was also less effective in the setting of bnip3lb knockdown, implying that receptor-mediated mitophagy remains essential in expanding HSPCs and cannot be completely compensated for by activation of alternative pathways (Fig. 7C). Finally, sustained exposure to NR stimulation (48–120 hpf) continued to positively influence HSPCs colonizing the KM niche by WISH for cmyb (Fig. 7D, E), and significantly increased CD41lo HSPCs and the Rag2+ lymphoid progenitor population in the thymus by FACS (Fig. 7F, G). We therefore focused on the use of NR and FCCP to stimulate mitophagy for translational studies.

In our primary analysis, Human iPSCs were differentiated toward hematopoietic fate using a modified version of the established “Keller” differentiation protocol7 and treated with NR (1 mM) in the media during the EHT stage (stating on day 8) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Comparable to the lack of impact on HSPC specification in the zebrafish VDA, no significant differences were found in the number or proportions of CD34+CD45+ multipotent hematopoietic progenitors initially generated after 5 days of culture with NR (Supplementary Fig. 7b). However, NR was noted to reduce intracellular ROS levels in all cultured cells as measured by CellROX at this timepoint (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Following flow cytometric profiling, control and NR-treated cells were collected for assessment of hematopoietic progenitor potential via a standard methylcellulose colony forming (CFU) assay92,93. At day 14 of the primary CFU, there was a dramatic increase in all colony types scored for cells previously exposed to NR-stimulation compared to controls; of note, this included the presence of multipotent CFU-GEMM colonies, which were not observed in controls (Supplementary Fig. 7d). To assess potential long-term impact on HSPC production and/or function, cells were harvested and serially replated at equal doses into secondary CFU culture: at 28 days from termination of NR stimulation and initial harvest, increased production of all colony types detected was retained in NR-treated samples (Supplementary Fig. 7e), indicating that induction of mitophagy promotes the production and maintenance of de novo-derived human multipotent hematopoietic progenitors in vitro.

Given recent advances in the efficiency and reproducibility of iPSC to HSC differentiation protocols, we sought to validate and extend our findings using an alternative approach which more closely resembles phenotypic HSC production using the “Loh” protocol94. Human iPSCs were differentiated toward hematopoietic fate using a modified version of the Loh protocol95 and treated with NR (1 mM) or FCCP (5 µM) on day 8 of differentiation (Fig. 7H), which correlates with EHT. At day 11, compound activators were removed, and the floating, non-adherent cell population was collected from control, NR- and FCCP-treated samples at day 11 for assessment of HSPC frequency and CFU potential. At day 11, flow cytometry analysis showed a statistically significant increase in CD34+CD45+ HSPCs in NR-treated samples and no change in HSPC numbers for FCCP-treated samples (Fig. 7I and Supplementary Fig. 7f). As seen with the Keller protocol, at day 14 of the primary CFU assay, there was a dramatic increase in all colony types scored following prior NR- or FCCP-stimulation compared to controls, including expansion of multipotent CFU-GEMM colony number (Fig. 7J, left). To assess impact on HSC production and/or function, equal doses of cells were serially replated into secondary CFU culture: at 28 days from cessation of mitophagy stimulation and initial harvest, NR and FCCP-treated samples continued to exhibit enhanced CFU potential in vitro (Fig. 7J, right), indicating that induction of mitophagy, whether through NR or FCCP, promotes maintenance of de novo-derived human multipotent hematopoietic progenitors in vitro regardless of differentiation platform utilized.

Altogether, our data indicate that activation of developmentally programmed mitophagy via Bnip3lb function is an essential component of HSPC development in the vertebrate embryo, which regulates ROS levels to allow for expansion, controlled differentiation, and survival of the stem cell pool. Furthermore, our in vivo and in vitro data indicate that exogenous activation of mitophagy is sufficient to support HSC expansion and maintenance in culture, which has direct therapeutic implications for enhancing the efficiency and self-renewal capacity of HSCs derived de novo in vitro from human iPSCs.

Discussion

HSC transplantation is the gold standard of treatment for many otherwise incurable hematopoietic malignancies. As such, generation of patient-specific HSPCs from hiPSCs could alleviate major issues surrounding matched donor availability and GVHD. While recent advances in iPSC differentiation strategies have demonstrated significant promise in enabling stable hematopoietic engraftment96, line-to-line variation in effective dose and long-term multipotency remains to be optimized to allow for robust, predictable generation of HSCs at a scale sufficient for clinical use97. Further refinement requires a more complete understanding of the regulatory interactions governing in vivo HSC production and maturation. Whereas most studies have focused on the initial specification of HSPCs and the process of EHT, analysis of CHT/fetal liver stages of hematopoiesis provides an opportunity to model the unique developmental phenomena whereby HSPCs can divide many times while maintaining stemness. The work detailed here for developmentally programmed mitophagy in HSPCs expands our understanding of the regulatory interactions at play in these secondary expansion hematopoietic niches. The primary observations presented support conjectures from our lab, and others, that precise regulation of the metabolic and inflammatory state has continual influence on HSCs throughout developmental hematopoiesis, affecting both the production and long-term function of the nascent HSC pool49,98,99.

The impact of ROS during developmental hematopoiesis was of particular interest to us given it potently enhances HSPC specification in the VDA52, but is known to damage or destroy the HSPC population in the adult11. This implied the presence of a temporal or spatially restricted ‘switch’ in the role of ROS during development, and the necessity for a means of timed ROS regulation. In this study, we found that excess ROS, produced during the shift to embryonic OX/PHOS metabolism, can become detrimental to newly specified HSPCs immediately after EHT. This directly coincides with the initiation of bnip3lb gene expression, which is precisely coordinated as cells transition from an HEC to HSC transcriptional profile. Indeed, induction of bnip3lb-regulated mitophagy can be observed in the presumptive HSPCs as they progress through EHT in the VDA and is maintained in CD41lo cells in the CHT. Importantly, our bnip3lb knockdown studies indicate that mitophagy is essential for reducing the amount of ROS within HSPCs in the CHT, protecting them from apoptosis, while allowing both proliferation and differentiation to progress appropriately. The importance of maintaining this ROS balance is emphasized in a recent report by Wattrus et al.100, which showed HSPCs in the CHT with excessive ROS were engulfed by macrophages to maintain the quality of the HSPC pool; however, the mechanism by which ROS levels were balanced to protect the majority of HSPCs from phagocytosis was not previously characterized. Mechanisms of ROS regulation have also been explored in murine models: ablation of Fip200101, an essential component of the autophagic isolation membrane, revealed a similar loss in fetal HSCs, suggesting autophagy is likely employed across species to control metabolic state and maintain stemness in newly specified HSCs.

While we showed Bnip3lb induction is responsive to environmental cues in the niche, such as ROS and hypoxia, its expression does not appear to be uniquely dependent on these factors. While we were unable to define a singular regulatory pathway responsible for the onset of bnip3lb expression in the VDA, we speculate it may be coordinately or redundantly initiated as part of, or in response to, the implementation of a larger developmental program during the process of EHT. Such developmentally programmed regulatory control of BNIP3lb expression is consistent with the literature in both related and disparate cell types, including maturation of red blood cells and lens fibers in the developing eye2,43–47. We note that our data showed no endogenous relevance for PINK1-Parkin pathway activation in response to oxidative or other stressors in the CHT; this is in contrast to adult HSCs, which use PINK1-Parkin, but not BNIP3l/NIX11,19,34, further supportive of our presumption of directed employment of a mitophagy program specific to these developmental stages. It is important to state, however, that our transcriptional profiling does suggest de novo specified HSPCs exhibit a classical stress-response gene signature, perhaps indicative of the overlapping regulatory routes or gene networks that can converge to ensure induction and/or maintenance of Bnip3lb-associated mitophagy. Aligning with that conjecture, a recent paper by Liu et al.101 showed that Atg5-dependent autophagy is essential for EHT, highlighting the importance of autophagy in embryonic HSC production. Importantly, ATG5-dependent autophagy is not specific to mitochondria and encompasses multiple degradative processes, whereas BNIP3lb-mediated mitophagy represents a more selective, developmentally regulated form of autophagy. Developmentally programmed mitophagy is an under-studied phenomenon in general, and our discoveries contribute to a mounting body of evidence suggesting its use in embryonic tissue specification, and perhaps stem and progenitor cell biology in general, is more widespread than currently reported34.

Finally, our data imply that attempts to generate or maintain HSCs in vitro should simultaneously seek to recapitulate the induction of mitophagy to aid in preserving full self-renewal and multipotency of newly specified progenitors. Toward that aim, we identified several effective small molecule chemical mitophagy activators that enhanced HSC numbers and lymphoid progenitor production in vivo, highlighting the NAD+ precursor NR for further in vitro translational studies, given its safety profile102. During human iPSC hematopoietic differentiation using two independent platforms, NR exposure significantly improved hematopoietic colony-forming potential, particularly in the context of serial replating, reflective of improved HSPC maintenance. Of note, while similar results were observed for FCCP treatment, which acts via an independent mechanism to induce mitophagy, NR regulates cellular redox via multiple pathways and is not specific to mitophagy alone; additional work will be needed to distinguish the isolated and combinatorial impact of each potential arm of its regulatory mechanism on HSC fate. Nevertheless, our findings contribute to a burgeoning mechanistic understanding of regulatory interplay in secondary expansion niches by highlighting the essential role of developmentally programmed, not damage-dependent, mitophagy in HSPC development, and the fine spatiotemporal requirement for Bnip3lb/Nix in particular. Further, this work highlights a potential adjuvant path for in vitro generation of multipotent self-renewing HSPCs for transplant therapy and improving clinical outcomes for patients in need.

Methods

Ethics declarations and approval for animal experiments

Zebrafish were maintained and utilized in accordance with protocols approved by Boston Children’s Hospital and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (Protocol #: 0002155 (BCH) and 045-2024 (BIDMC)).

Zebrafish husbandry

Adults were maintained in a standard circulating water facility on a live brine shrimp diet supplemented with Gemma 300 pellets (Skretting). Wild-type embryos used were of the AB strain; transgenic and/or mutant lines utilized are detailed in the Key Resources Table (see Supplementary Table 1). Mutants were genotyped by PCR on tail-clip samples (see Supplementary Table 1 for primers). After screening for developmental abnormalities, healthy embryos were maintained in E3 fish medium in a 28 °C incubator until use. Zebrafish sex is indeterminate until ~1 month of age; as all samples used in this study were 5 days post fertilization or less, sex was not determined and unable to be considered as a variable in analysis.

hiPSC culture

Wild-type hiPSC line #1157.2 was generated with informed consent (Boston Children’s Hospital IRB protocol #: 09-02-0068) from PBMCs of a healthy adult donor, as previously described97. hiPSCs were cultured in Stemflex medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) in 37 °C, 20% O2, 5% CO2 incubators using matrigel-coated wells, with clump passaging (ReleSR, Stem Cell Technologies). Karyotyping and mycoplasma testing were performed as quality control measures to verify chromosomal integrity and ensure lines were free of mycoplasma contamination before use.

Chemical treatments

Embryos were exposed to N-acetyl-L-Cysteine (NAC) (10 µM), AMPK activator CAS 849727-81-7 (CAS-849) (0.25 µM), Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) (10 µM), FCCP (0.01 µM), DMOG (10 µM), H202 (0.05%), Glucose (1%) or equivalent percentage of vehicle (DMSO) for sibling controls in E3 (fish) water.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Embryos (pools of 8 embryos/sample) were dissociated in 500 μL LiberaseTM solution (75 μg/mL in 1xPBS/1 mM EDTA) at 34 °C for 45 min. Samples were dissociated by pipetting and filtered. SYTOX Red viability stain was added to a concentration of 5 nm; most figures display the proportion of GFP positive cells as a percentage of cells negative for the SYTOX red dye. All reagents can be found in the Key Resources Table (see Supplementary Table 1). Flow cytometry was performed on an LSRFortessa (BD) and analyzed by FlowJo software. For ROS detection, CellROX orange dye (1:1000 dilution) was added to samples 30 min prior to analysis and measured using 561 nm laser and PE filter. For cell sorting, embryos were bisected along the transverse plane of the trunk at the point where the yolk sac narrows, and tail sections were dissociated as above. Sorting for live cells was performed on a FACS Aria (BD).

In situ hybridization

Whole-mount in situ hybridization (WISH) was performed as described (http://zfin.org/ZFIN/Methods/ThisseProtocol.html). For each condition, three clutches of ≥20 stage-matched embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for processing. Fixed embryos were treated with 0.8%KOH/0.9%H2O2 to remove pigment, then dehydrated in methanol. Once rehydrated in PBS 0.1% Tween (PBST), embryos were permeabilized using 10 μg/mL Proteinase K, re-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then washed again in PBST. Hybridization with digoxigenin-UTP-labeled riboprobes was performed in hybridization buffer containing 50% formamide at 70 °C overnight. Published probes are shown in the Key Resources Table (see Supplementary Table 1); the bnip3lb probe was made from cDNA of the coding sequence using primers shown in the Key Resources Table. Embryos were washed in sodium citrate buffer/0.1% Tween and incubated overnight with Anti-dioxigenin-AP, Fab fragments. After washing in PBST, antibodies were visualized by incubation in Tris buffer (pH 8.5) with BCIP/NBT. The intensity of expression was qualitatively categorized as high, medium, or low relative to the median intensity of the control group for a given clutch. For runx1/cmyb expression, a score of “high” typically reflects continuous aortic signal with robust thickness, indicating numerous cell clusters; “medium” denotes moderate, yet continuous signal, with occasional budding cells; and “low” indicates spotty/discontinuous signal. At least 20 embryos from a minimum of two independent clutches were analyzed per treatment condition and/or genotype, with phenotype scoring done blinded and independently confirmed. The scoring distribution of each sample set was averaged across each independent clutch, and control and treatment groups compared and analyzed with a chi-squared test (p-value < 0.05 is considered significant). Representative images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Imager A1/Axio Cam MRC and Axiovision LE software. Fluorescent WISH was performed as described94, by a procedure comparable to conventional WISH, but utilizing Anti-dioxigenin-POD Fab fragments to stain the bnip3lb probe, Anti-Fluorescein-POD Fab fragments for the runx1 probe, and Rabbit anti-GFP TP401 and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa fluor 647 to detect Kdrl:GFP103. Fluorescent WISH images were captured using confocal microscopy as described below.

Confocal imaging

Confocal imaging was performed on a Ti2 (Nikon) scope using an iXon Life 888 EMCCD (ANDOR) camera and analyzed using Imaris software (Oxford Instruments) and Python. Embryos were treated with 0.003% PTU at 24hpf to inhibit pigmentation. For live imaging, embryos were mounted on glass-bottom dishes in 1% low-melting point agarose in E3 media containing 0.2 mg/ml Tricaine. For acridine orange experiments, 72 hpf embryos were exposed to 10 µg/mL of acridine orange in E3 media for 1 hour while protected from light, then washed in E3 three times before live imaging. See Key Resource Table (Supplementary Table 1) for more information regarding reagents.

Adult Tg(kdrl:TagBFP)mu293 and Tg(ubi:mito-Keima) or Tg(ubi:mito-GR) zebrafish were crossed, and embryos were maintained in E3 at 28.5 °C until imaging. For imaging of MitoKeima embryos, laser settings were: 405 nm (50%, 100 ms), 488 nm (50%, 70 ms), and 594 nm (50%, 70 ms), all at 540 MHz and 16-bit dual gain 1/4. For Mito-GR embryos, images were captured using 488 nm laser light and 561 nm laser light with an ET FITC/TRITC dual emission filter (Chroma) in triggered acquisition mode. For both lines, a 3-h time-lapse was acquired at 5-minute intervals (37 loops). Z-stacks were collected with a NIDAQ piezo (1.2 µM steps) spanning −30 to +30 µM (51 slices). For all quantitative experiments, images from all samples were collected with identical laser power, camera gain and exposure time.

Images were de-noised with the bm3d algorithm where indicated and are otherwise unaltered. Raw images were collected in Nikon Elements (.nd2) format and a ratiometric image representing mitophagy (visualized as yellow in images) was generated in Python by converting the image into 64-bit and applying the following equation: (mCherry channel)/(GFP channel+150). The 150 constant is higher than background signal ensuring that areas of low signal from both mCherry and GFP channels are excluded70. Images were exported in .tiff format and converted into .imsfiles format using the Imaris file converter software for display. HyPer relative fluorescence was quantified by masking on mCherry and taking the average values of pixels in the HyPer channel. For quantitative purposes, mitophagy puncta were defined by applying a threshold (identical across all samples) to the radiometric mitophagy channel. For experiments where it is indicated that the mitophagy index was calculated within Kdrl:BFP+ cells, a threshold (identical across all samples) was applied to mask only the BFP positive pixels. The mitophagy index was calculated by dividing the sum of all mitophagy puncta volumes by the total tissue volume. The results were averaged to give final values reported, with statistical significance determined by Students t test.

qPCR

RNA was extracted from mechanically homogenized pooled embryos (n = 20/condition) using RNAEasy Micro Kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription of 1µg of RNA was performed using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SYBR Green PCR Master Mix was used for qPCR with an ABI7900 (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative expression was calculated using the ΔΔCT method104 See Key Resources Table (Supplementary Table 1) for qPCR primer sequences.

scRNAseq

Two replicate samples of pooled dissected tail preps of 48 hpf Tg(kdrl:Has.HRAS-mCherry)s916 embryos (300 embryos/condition) and two replicate samples of pooled whole 36 hpf Kdrl+ embryos (300 embryos/condition) were isolated by FACS as described above. Samples were processed on a Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 and sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 system (Illumina).

Downstream analysis was performed in R using Seurat (v4.4)63. After filtering for low-quality cells, datasets were log-normalized, variable features were identified, data were scaled, and PCA was computed on variable genes. A shared-nearest-neighbor graph was constructed for clustering, and UMAP embeddings were generated using the first 10 principal components. Clusters were annotated based on differentially expressed genes and canonical hematovascular markers.

Differential expression analysis between HECs, ECs, and HSPCs was performed using Seurat’s Wilcoxon test on SCT-normalized data. Ranked gene lists were used as input for clusterProfiler (v4.0)64 for Gene Ontology Biological Process enrichment (org.Dr.eg.db). Differential Expression Analysis of genes detected in the RNAseq dataset generated in this manuscript from FACS-isolated 36 hpf Tg(kdrl:Has.HRAS-mCherry)s916 embryos, comparing HSPCs vs ECs and HSPCs vs HECs, is included in Supplementary Data File 1 and Supplementary Data File 2, respectively. Differential Expression Analysis of genes detected in the RNAseq dataset generated in this manuscript from FACS-isolated 48 hpf Tg(kdrl:Has.HRAS-mCherry)s916 embryos comparing HSPCs vs ECs is indicated in Supplementary Data File 3. Fold increase, p-adjusted value, and normalized reads are denoted for each gene. For comparisons between 36 hpf and 48 hpf datasets, HSPC libraries were harmonized via UMI downsampling using DropletUtils65.

Morphants

To create morphants, 1–3 nl of a given morpholino oligonucleotide was injected into the yolk ball of embryos at the 1-cell stage at concentrations shown in the Key Resources Table (see Supplementary Table 1) using a microinjector (Narishige) and pulled glass needle. Uninjected sibling embryos were used as matched controls.

Transgenesis

Tg(6.0itga2b:EGFP:mito-GR), referred to as Tg(CD41:mito-GR), was generated using tol2 transgenesis as described by Wrighton et al.5. Briefly, gateway cloning using LR Clonase II was used to make a pTol2-Cd41:mitoGR;cryaa:cerulean vector, from p5E-CD41, pME-mitoGR, p3E-polyA and pDestTol2pA2 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This vector along with tol2 transposase mRNA, made using the mMessage mMachine SP6 in vitro transcription kit, was injected directly into fertilized embryos at the 1-cell stage. F0 embryos were screened for cerulean fluorescent lenses and outcrossed as adults. Potential founders were screened for brightness, stable integration and copy number. Tg(hsp70:ΔOTC) was generated as above using p5E-hsp70, pME-ΔOTC, p3E-polyA and pDestTol2pA2 as shown in Key Resources table (see Supplementary Table 1).

Mutants and crispants

For production of mutant and crispant embryos, dgRNPs were designed against the coding sequence of bnip3lb, using the CHOPCHOP crispr design tool99, mixed and complexed with tracrRNA and Cas9 protein (IDT DNA), then injected into the cytoplasm of 1-cell stage embryos according to the methods laid out by Quick et al.78. Efficacy was validated by gel electrophoresis of PCR products of genomic DNA surrounding the sites, with visible recombination in >70% of tested embryos and confirmed by Sanger sequencing (see Supplementary Fig. 6h). crRNA sequences and genotyping primers are shown in the Key Resources Table (see Supplementary Table 1). For the creation of bnip3lb mutants, we targeted sequences in exon 1 (CGGTGGCACTGAAGCGCTGGAGG) and exon 2 (ATCCATTCACAATGGCGACATGG). For crispants, an additional sequence in exon 3 was targeted (TAAGAGTCACTGAACGCCGTGGG).

EdU assay

Larvae were exposed to 500 μM EdU in E3 water (10% DMSO) for 1 hour at 4 °C. Pooled larvae were dissociated and filtered as above, then cells were fixed, permeabilized and stained as per the Click-iT™ EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit. Flow cytometric data were collected on LSRFortessa (BD) and analyzed using FlowJo.

hiPSC differentiation

Loh Protocol: Human 1157.2 iPSCs97 were differentiated into hematopoietic progenitors using a 2D adherent culture, where each step of differentiation is defined. Differentiation was performed based on previously published protocols95,96 with slight modifications, including an extra day of culture, extending the differentiation from 10 to 11 days, and the addition of hematopoietic cytokines SCF (100 ng/mL), TPO (30 ng/mL), and FLT3L (10 ng/mL) from day 8-11 of differentiation. An additional modification to the published protocol was the inclusion of retinyl acetate (RETA) at 50 nM from days 1 to 2, 2 µM from days 2 to 4, and 100 nM from days 4 to 11. In brief and per Zheng et al.95, STAR Protocols, iPSCs were dissociated with Accutase, counted, and seeded at single-cell density on day −1 in the presence of Thiazovivin. On day 0, cells were washed with DMEM/F12, and a chemically defined medium was added to begin differentiation. Specific media conditions driving stepwise differentiation are described in Fowler et al.96. On day 11, non-adherent hematopoietic progenitors were harvested, washed, counted, and plated for CFU assays.

Keller Protocol: Human 1157.2 iPSCs97 were maintained on Matrigel matrix using Stemflex media and differentiated into CD34+45+ HSPCs cells using a previously published protocol105. Briefly, iPSC colonies were scraped, then cultured in ultra-low attachment plates with aggregation media containing BMP4 (10 ng/ml, days 0–2), bFGF (5 ng/ml, days 1–2), CHIR99021 (3 μM, day 2), and SB431542 (6 μM, day 2), VEGF (15 ng/ml, days 3–8), bFGF (5 ng/ml, days 3–8), SCF (50 ng/ml, days 6–8), EPO (days 6–8), IL-6 (days 6–8), IL-11 (days 6–8), and IGF-1 (days 6–8) to allow the formation of embryoid bodies (EBs). On day 8, EBs were dissociated using an EB Dissociation kit (Miltenyi), and CD34+ hemogenic endothelium (HE) cells were isolated by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) using the human CD34 Microbead Kit (Miltenyi). CD34+ HE cells were treated with DMSO or 1 mM Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) and cultured for 5 days in StemPro-34 media (Invitrogen) supplemented with BMP4, bFGF, IL3, IL6, IL11, IGF-1, VEGF, SCF, EPO, TPO, FLT-3L, and SHH according to standard protocols100 to allow for the formation of non-adherent CD34+45+ HSPCs.

Hematopoietic colony-forming unit (CFU) assay

CFU assays were performed using MethoCult H4636 methylcellulose media following the manufacturer’s instructions (see Supplementary Table 1). 5k cells were plated for the primary CFU assay. Colonies were counted after 14 days of culture; cells were then collected, washed with PBS and replated at 10 K cells/well for the Loh protocol and 30k cells/well for the Keller protocol in secondary CFU assays.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and Excel. Statistical significance was determined by a two-tailed unpaired t test with Welch’s correction, by Pearson’s chi-square (two-sided), or a two-tailed ANOVA calculation. Error bars indicate the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). P value < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical significance was calculated to the 4th decimal place. Values of greater significance are reported as P < 0.0001.
