Biocompatible injectable glycyrrhizic acid-based growth factor hydrogel for synergistic endometrial regeneration
Authors: Hongqiao Cai, Jing Wang, Yanan Zheng, Jiaqi Wen, Hongru Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegenerative therapies for endometrial repair are crucial for treating intrauterine adhesions (IUA) and restoring fertility. In this study, we present a biocompatible injectable GHD hydrogel based on glycyrrhizic acid (GA) and dopamine-modified hyaluronic acid (HA-DA) for synergistic endometrial regeneration. The hydrogel demonstrated shear-thinning behavior and rapid gelation, making it suitable for minimally invasive delivery. The BB/OA/GHD with the dual incorporation of Oroxylin A (OA) and platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB), exhibited sustained release of OA and PDGF-BB, and favorable degradation over 8 days. In an IUA rat model, the BB/OA/GHD significantly restored endometrial thickness, gland density, and stromal organization. Mechanistically, the BB/OA/GHD reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and collagen deposition, while enhancing VEGFA-mediated angiogenesis. The BB/OA/GHD group demonstrated superior fertility restoration, with increased embryo and fetus numbers and improved live birth rates compared to other treatments. These results highlight the potential of the BB/OA/GHD as a therapeutic platform for endometrial regeneration and fertility restoration, offering a novel and effective strategy for the treatment of endometrial dysfunction and infertility.Keywords: Glycyrrhizic acid, Hydrogel, Growth factor, Endometrial regeneration, Synergistic

Introduction

Intrauterine adhesions (IUA), often resulting from mechanical injury, infection, or surgical interventions, represent a major clinical challenge in reproductive medicine [1,2]. The pathological hallmark of IUA is fibrotic replacement of the endometrial lining, which compromises its regenerative capacity and functional receptivity [3,4]. Clinically, IUA is associated with menstrual abnormalities, recurrent pregnancy loss, and infertility, severely affecting women's reproductive health and quality of life [5,6]. Despite the availability of hysteroscopic adhesiolysis and adjunctive hormonal therapy, current clinical strategies offer limited efficacy in severe cases and are frequently accompanied by high recurrence rates [7]. Thus, the development of bioactive, minimally invasive therapeutic platforms that promote endogenous regeneration is of paramount importance [8,9]. Injectable hydrogels have attracted considerable attention in tissue engineering due to their ability to fill irregular defects, encapsulate therapeutic agents, and mimic the viscoelasticity of native extracellular matrices [10,11]. For endometrial regeneration, hydrogels offer unique advantages including acting as physical barriers to prevent adhesion recurrence, serving as scaffolds for cell proliferation, and delivering bioactive molecules in a controlled and sustained manner [12]. However, existing hydrogel systems often lack multifunctionality since they either provide mechanical support without bioactivity or deliver drugs without appropriate degradation and biocompatibility profiles [13]. Moreover, very few hydrogels are designed with a mechanistic focus on modulating the inflammatory and oxidative microenvironment that accompanies endometrial injury [14].

Oroxylin A (OA) is a flavonoid known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, as well as its ability to promote angiogenesis [15]. OA has been shown to modulate various biological processes, and exerts its effects by regulating the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and modulating signaling pathways involved in inflammation and cell survival. Previous studies have demonstrated that OA has therapeutic potential in various inflammatory and fibrotic conditions, such as liver fibrosis [16]. Notably, OA has also been shown to alleviate uterine fibrosis by inhibiting macrophage pyroptosis through the reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathway [17]. These findings highlight the potential of OA as a promising therapeutic agent for the treatment of IUA, with a particular relevance to endometrial regeneration. Growth factors are essential for regulating biological processes and are widely used in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. However, their short half-lives and potential side effects limit their effectiveness. Growth factor-loaded hydrogels have been developed to address these challenges by protecting growth factors from rapid degradation and sustaining their bioactivity, thus enhancing their therapeutic potential. These hydrogels achieve controlled release, reducing rapid proteolysis and burst release, thus enhancing therapeutic outcomes [18]. Platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) is a potent growth factor involved in endometrial tissue remodeling and neovascularization, which has been found to be reduced in endometrial endothelial cells of women with endometrial disorder [19,20].

Herein, we have developed a biocompatible, self-assembling injectable GHD hydrogel based on glycyrrhizic acid (GA) and dopamine-modified hyaluronic acid (HA-DA) (Fig. 1A). GA, a natural small molecule with clinically recognized anti-inflammatory activity, serves as both a therapeutic agent and a hydrogelator, promoting supramolecular assembly through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [21,22]. HA-DA further enhances mechanical stability and tissue adhesion through catechol-mediated crosslinking, inspired by mussel adhesion chemistry [23,24]. The hydrogel facilitates the dual incorporation of OA and PDGF-BB. This dual-drug system mimics the natural regenerative cascade and achieves spatiotemporal control of microenvironment modulation. Instead of serving as a passive scaffold, the OA and PDGF-BB-loaded GHD hydrogel (BB/OA/GHD) actively mitigates oxidative stress, curbs excessive inflammation, and enhances angiogenic signaling. The three critical mechanisms for successful endometrial regeneration are rarely integrated into a single platform. Importantly, the injectable nature of the hydrogel allows for catheter-based administration in clinical settings, aligning with current intrauterine treatment protocols while minimizing surgical risks. This hydrogel platform integrates several key innovations including multifunctional molecular design, co-delivery of synergistic bioactive agents, mechanism-oriented therapeutic strategy, and translational potential with minimally invasive delivery. In this study, we systematically characterize the physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, and degradation behavior of the hydrogel. We further assess its therapeutic efficacy in a rat model of endometrial injury. Our findings show that the BB/OA/GHD not only promotes endometrial thickness and restores glandular structure but also exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, while enhancing VEGFA-mediated angiogenesis (Fig. 1B). Collectively, this work introduces a novel, multifunctional, and mechanism-driven hydrogel system that holds significant promise for endometrial regeneration. By integrating biomimetic design, drug co-delivery, and regenerative engineering, our approach lays a solid foundation for future translational applications in reproductive medicine and may offer insights into designing hydrogel therapies for other soft tissue injuries.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of GHD hydrogel

Dissolve 1 g of sodium hyaluronate (S0780000, Sigma-Aldrich, China) in 100 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 5.0), followed by the addition of 0.5 g of dopamine hydrochloride (H8502, Sigma-Aldrich, China). The reaction mixture is stirred at 25 °C for 10 h. Afterward, the solution is dialyzed against ultrapure water for 2 days and freeze-dried to yield HA-DA. The GA (PHR1516, Sigma-Aldrich, China) powder is dispersed in physiological PBS (10 mM, pH = 7.4) in a test tube, followed by sonication for 2 min to ensure uniform dispersion. The solution is then heated in an 80 °C oil bath until a clear solution is obtained. To prepare the hydrogel, a 0.5 % GA precursor solution containing Oroxylin A (OA; HY-N0560, MedChemExpress, China) and platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB; #8912, Cell Signaling Technology, China) is mixed with 5 % HA-DA precursor solution. Upon cooling, the BB/OA/GHD is formed by self-assembly. The same method was used for synthesis of GHD hydrogel and OA/GHD hydrogel by using a 0.5 % GA solution without or with OA.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy

The conjugation of dopamine to HA was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy, conducted on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz spectrometer. HA-DA was dissolved in D2O, and the proton peaks were analyzed, particularly at 2.86 ppm corresponding to the catechol group of dopamine.

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy

UV–Vis spectra were collected using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Evolution 201 spectrophotometer. The formation of HA-DA was confirmed by a characteristic absorption peak at 285 nm, attributed to the aromatic ring of dopamine.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectra were obtained on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 spectrometer to assess the chemical structure of HA-DA and the GHD hydrogel. Key shifts and new peaks in the FTIR spectra indicated the successful crosslinking and network formation of the hydrogel.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The internal morphology of the hydrogel was examined using SEM (JEOL JSM-6010LA) at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV. The hydrogel was freeze-dried prior to imaging to preserve its structure.

Sol–gel transition and gelation time

The sol–gel transition was evaluated using the vial inversion method. The hydrogel was prepared by mixing the precursor solution and then inverting the vial at 37 °C. Gelation time was recorded when the gel remained stable at the bottom of the vial, which occurred within approximately 1 min under physiological conditions.

Sustained release of OA and PDGF-BB

As for OA release, the UPLC (Waters Corporation, USA) analysis was used [25]. Briefly, the chromatographic analysis was performed using a BEH300C18 1.7 μm 2.1 × 50 mm column. The mobile phases contained two solvents [A: water with 0.1 % formic acid, B: acetonitrile, (48:52)]. The injection volume was 3 μL with the flow rate at 0.2 mL/min, and samples were quantified by UV detection using the peak area at 270 nm referred to the plotted standard curve (Figure S3). As for PDGF-BB release, the hydrogel was dispersed into test tubes containing 3 mL Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) solution (Gibco BRL, Eggenstein, Germany) [26]. The suspension was gently stirred at room temperature in a release chamber. DPBS solutions in the tubes were collected at specific time points for 7 days. The release profiles of PDGF-BB from the hydrogel were assessed using ELISA (ab224879, Abcam, UK).

In vitro degradation

The degradation profile of the hydrogel was assessed by incubating 1 g of hydrogel in 10 mL PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. The hydrogel was weighed during 24 days, and the percentage of weight loss was calculated.

Rheological Characterization

Rheological properties of the hydrogel were analyzed using a rotational rheometer (TA Instruments). A Pamir steel plate with a diameter of 25 mm was used, and measurements were performed under oscillatory mode at 37 °C with a gap distance of 1 mm. Shear-thinning behavior, storage modulus (G′), and loss modulus (G″) were measured over a range of strain and angular frequencies. The strain sweep was conducted over a range of 0.1 %–1000 %, with a constant frequency of 10 rad/s to assess the linear viscoelastic region. Frequency sweep tests were conducted from 0.1 to 10 rad/s at 37 °C, with a constant strain of 1 %, to evaluate the hydrogel's mechanical properties.

Cell culture

Human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs) and RAW264.7 macrophages were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HESCs were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 % L-glutamine [27]. RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 % L-glutamine. These cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere. Both cell lines were passaged every 2–3 days when they reached approximately 80 % confluence.

Cell proliferation assay

HESCs were treated with PBS, GHD, OA/GHD, or BB/OA/GHD. Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CK04-13, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan) assay. Cells were incubated with CCK-8 solution for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Live cell staining was performed using calcein-AM (C1359, Sigma-Aldrich, China) with green fluorescence, and visualized under a fluorescence microscope [28]. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. As for colony formation assay, HESCs were stained with Giemsa Stain (32884, Sigma-Aldrich, China) after the same treatments.

Inflammatory cytokine measurement

To assess the anti-inflammatory potential of the hydrogel, RAW264.7 macrophages were stimulated with Lipopolysaccharides (LPS; 1 μg/mL; HY-D1056, MedChemExpress, China) for 18 h [29]. The culture supernatant was collected, and the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were quantified using ELISA (R&D Systems). The levels of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 were also measured by ELISA. qRT-PCR was used to measure the mRNA expression of these cytokines in the LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. The serum analysis of systemic inflammatory markers IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10 were also measured by ELISA.

Flow cytometry for ROS-positive cells

HESCs were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS. The cells were incubated with ROS tube (DCFH-DA) for 30 min at 37 °C, protected from light [30]. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess probe. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCant II, BD company, USA). Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters were used to gate on live cells, and the fluorescence signal corresponding to ROS production was detected. Cells with fluorescence intensity above the threshold established by control samples were considered ROS-positive. Data were analyzed using FlowJo (v10.9, BD company, USA).

VEGFA secretion and mRNA expression

ELISA was used to quantify VEGFA secretion from HESCs after treatment with GHD, OA/GHD, or BB/OA/GHD, and uterine tissues after treatment with PBS, etoh, GHD, OA/GHD, or BB/OA/GHD [31]. qRT-PCR was performed to assess VEGFA mRNA expression levels in HESCs and uterine tissues. The relative expression of VEGFA was normalized to GAPDH. The primers are as follows:

Rat uterine tissues VEGFA: Forward Primer 5′- GGG CCG CCG CAA TGA AGT -3'; Reverse Primer: 5′- CAG GGT CCG GAG CCT TGG -3'.

IUA model establishment

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200–220 g, 8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. As previously reported, the IUA rat models were established by injecting 100 μL 95 % ethanol into the uterus [32]. The animal experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Jilin University First Hospital. Rats were randomly divided into groups (N = 5). The control group (sham-operated rats) did not undergo uterine injury, with only 100 μL of PBS injected into the uterine horns. The other four groups were treated with 100 μL of PBS (ETOH group), GHD, OA/GHD, or BB/OA/GHD formulations. Treatment was administered intrauterinely for two weeks after injury. For embryos and live birth tests, male Sprague-Dawley rats were paired with the female rats in a 1:1 ratio, and successful mating was confirmed via vaginal smear. The uteri were collected for analysis of implanted embryo numbers 14 days post-coitum in 5 rats per group. The number of live births was observed approximately 21 days into gestation in another 5 rats per group. The live birth rate was calculated: live birth rate = number of live-born pups/number of embryos × 100 %. The corresponding data of live birth rate calculation was shown in Table S1.

Histological analysis of endometrial regeneration

Endometrial regeneration was assessed through histological examination of uterine tissue samples collected at predetermined time points. The tissues were first fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning into 5-μm thick slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was then performed to evaluate the histological features, including endometrial thickness, glandular structure, and cellular arrangement. This provided insights into the morphological changes in the endometrial tissue during the regeneration process. Besides, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) was performed for the systemic biocompatibility evaluation of the hydrogel. In addition to H&E staining, Masson's trichrome staining was conducted to assess the distribution and deposition of collagen fibers, which are crucial for understanding the remodeling of the extracellular matrix during endometrial regeneration. Quantitative analysis of endometrial thickness and the number of glands was performed using ImageJ software. For endometrial thickness measurement, the distance between the basal membrane and the luminal surface of the epithelium was measured at multiple points along the length of the uterine sections. The number of glands was quantified by counting distinct glandular structures across several regions of interest within the endometrial tissue.

In vivo biodegradability

To evaluate the biodegradability of the hydrogel, SD rats (200–220 g, 8 weeks old) were subcutaneously injected with 500 μL of the hydrogel on the dorsal skin. The degradation profile of the hydrogel was monitored at days 2, 4, 8, and 12 post-injection. At each time point, rats were euthanized, and tissue samples surrounding the injection site were harvested. The tissue samples were then processed for histological analysis to evaluate the degradation process of the hydrogel in vivo. Tissue samples were fixed in 10 % formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess the tissue response around the injection site, including inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue regeneration. The inflammatory response was graded based on the presence and extent of inflammatory cells such as macrophages and neutrophils. Inflammation scoring was performed on a scale from 0 (no inflammation) to 4 (severe inflammation).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0. IBM company, USA). For normally distributed data, comparisons between groups were performed using a two-tailed t-test for two groups or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple group comparisons. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SD).

Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterization and injectability of the GHD hydrogel

The successful conjugation of dopamine (DA) to hyaluronic acid (HA) was confirmed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2A, a characteristic proton peak corresponding to the catechol group of dopamine appeared at approximately 2.86 ppm, indicating the effective grafting of DA onto the HA backbone. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy further supported the formation of HA-DA by revealing a new absorption peak at 285 nm, which is attributed to the aromatic ring of dopamine (Fig. 2B). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was then employed to characterize the chemical structure of GHD and BB/OA/GHD (Fig. 2C). The spectra exhibited characteristic shifts and the emergence of new peaks, confirming the successful crosslinking and formation of the hydrogel network. Specifically, the incorporation of OA into the hydrogel causes a redshift in the O-H stretching vibration peak from 3407 cm−1 to 3440 cm−1, indicating that OA interacts with the hydrogel via hydrogen bonding. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that the GHD hydrogel possessed a highly porous and interconnected internal architecture, with average pore diameters of approximately 10 μm (Fig. 2D). This microstructure is favorable for nutrient exchange and metabolic waste removal, which are essential for tissue regeneration applications. The sol–gel transition of the GHD hydrogel was visually evaluated using the vial inversion method. The hydrogel rapidly formed a stable and transparent gel that remained at the bottom of the vial within approximately 1 min under physiological conditions (Fig. 2E), confirming its rapid gelation behavior. A balanced gelation time is critical since excessively rapid gelation may result in uneven crosslinking and compromised mechanical integrity, whereas slow gelation may lead to poor retention at the injury site and loss of bioactive cargo such as cells or therapeutic agents. The sustained release of OA and PDGF-BB from the hydrogel were evaluated. The cumulative release profile of OA (Fig. 2F) showed a gradual release over 7 days, with approximately 95 % of the drug released by the end of the period. As PDGF-BB is physically encapsulated within the hydrogel network, its sustained release properties have been validated through release studies as previously reported [26], confirming its continuous bioactive release over time (Fig. S1). In vitro degradation experiments demonstrated that the hydrogel gradually degraded over time in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), with complete degradation occurring within approximately 24 days (Fig. 2G), a time frame compatible with the endometrial regeneration process.

Injectability and self-healing are essential properties for hydrogels intended for endometrial repair. Injectable hydrogels are capable of conforming to and filling irregularly shaped injury sites, ensuring uniform coverage. Meanwhile, self-healing ability enhances the hydrogel's longevity by allowing it to recover from external mechanical forces, thereby extending its functional lifespan. Rheological assessments provided further insights into the gel's mechanical and flow properties (Fig. 2H). The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) decreased with increasing strain, and a crossover occurred when strain exceeded 80 %, indicating a transition from solid-like to sol-like behavior (Fig. 2I). The storage modulus plays a crucial role in the hydrogel's ability to resist deformation and maintain structural integrity within tissues [33]. A higher storage modulus could improve the hydrogel's stability and resistance to mechanical stresses, but may hinder its integration with surrounding tissue if it becomes too rigid. Conversely, a lower modulus might compromise the hydrogel's ability to support tissue regeneration. In addition, the hydrogel exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior, as evidenced by a decline in apparent viscosity with increasing shear rate (Fig. 2J), which is favorable for injectable delivery. Frequency sweep analysis confirmed that G′ remained higher than G″ across a wide range of angular frequencies (Fig. 2K), further supporting its solid-like behavior under physiological conditions. The hydrogel could be smoothly extruded through a 23-gauge needle to form continuous and coherent patterns (Fig. S2). Zheng et al. also developed an injectable antioxidant hyaluronan/chitosan hydrogel designed as a carrier for platelet-rich plasma and stem cells to promote endometrial regeneration and restore fertility [34]. This hydrogel can be injected through a needle and forms the gelation structure upon continuous injection. Future studies should focus on these aspects to optimize the hydrogel's mechanical properties and ensure its effectiveness in regenerative therapies [35].

Biodegradability, biocompatibility, and anti-inflammation evaluation of hydrogel

The biodegradability and biocompatibility of the GHD hydrogel were critical factors in assessing its suitability for in vivo tissue repair applications [36]. To evaluate the biodegradability and biocompatibility of the hydrogel in vivo, we subcutaneously injected the GHD hydrogel into the dorsal skin of SD rats and monitored its degradation profile. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, the GHD hydrogel exhibited a sustained degradation over approximately 8 days, providing a favorable degradation window that avoids complications associated with either overly rapid or excessively delayed degradation, thus supporting effective tissue repair. The biocompatibility of the hydrogel was further assessed via histological analysis of the subcutaneous tissue surrounding the injection site. At days 4 and 8 post-injection, mild inflammatory cell infiltration and localized inflammatory responses were observed. However, by day 12, the tissue architecture largely returned to normal (Fig. 3C). Quantitative histological inflammation scoring (Fig. 3D) confirmed a time-dependent decrease in inflammatory response, supporting the hydrogel's good biocompatibility in vivo. Biodegradability ensures that the material breaks down naturally in the body without causing long-term complications such as chronic inflammation or tissue damage. In this study, the GHD hydrogel demonstrated a sustained degradation over approximately 8 days, providing an ideal window that avoids issues associated with both overly rapid and delayed degradation, thus supporting effective tissue repair. Furthermore, the biocompatibility of the hydrogel was evaluated through histological analysis, revealing mild inflammatory responses at early time points (days 4 and 8) that resolved by day 12, with tissue architecture largely returning to normal. These findings underscore the hydrogel's potential for safe, temporary use in biomedical applications, highlighting its promising role in supporting tissue regeneration while minimizing adverse reactions. The ability of the GHD hydrogel to degrade in alignment with tissue healing and exhibit good biocompatibility makes it a valuable candidate for future regenerative medicine and tissue engineering applications.

To further illustrate the anti-inflammatory mechanism, key inflammatory cytokines were measured via ELISA in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 3E). Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), and Interleukin-10 (IL-10) are key cytokines involved in the regulation of inflammation. Compared with the control group, GHD treatment significantly (P < 0.05) reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β (Fig. 3F), IL-6 (Fig. 3G), and TNF-α (Fig. 3H). Notably, OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD treatments exhibited no significant difference, suggesting that OA is the major contributor to the anti-inflammatory effect. Conversely, levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 (Fig. 3I) showed an upward trend, further supporting the immunomodulatory function of the composite hydrogel. IL-1β is a pro-inflammatory cytokine primarily produced by activated macrophages and plays a critical role in initiating and sustaining the inflammatory response by promoting the release of other inflammatory mediators [37]. Similarly, IL-6, produced by various immune cells, is involved in immune regulation and tissue repair [38]. TNF-α, another pro-inflammatory cytokine produced mainly by macrophages, mediates acute inflammatory responses and can induce chronic inflammation, contributing to tissue damage in various diseases [39]. In contrast, IL-10 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine that counteracts excessive inflammation by inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, thereby promoting immune resolution and maintaining immune homeostasis [40]. These cytokines work together to balance the immune response, and their dysregulation can lead to either chronic inflammation or insufficient immune defense.

Evaluation of human endometrial stromal cell proliferation, ROS level, and VEGFA expression

Human endometrial stromal cells (ESCs) were used to evaluate the effects of GHD, OA/GHD, and BB/OA/GHD on cell proliferation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and VEGFA expression. The CCK-8 assay revealed no significant differences between the control, GHD and OA/GHD groups (Fig. 4A), suggesting that GHD alone or in combination with OA did not markedly affect cell proliferation under the experimental conditions. However, in the BB/OA/GHD group, a significant increase (P < 0.05) in cell proliferation was observed, indicating that the addition of PDGF-BB to the OA/GHD formulation promoted cell growth. This trend was further confirmed by live cell staining (Fig. 4B and C), where no significant changes were found in the number of viable cells in the GHD and OA/GHD groups compared to the control. In contrast, the BB/OA/GHD treatment showed a marked increase in viable cells. BB/OA/GHD significantly (P < 0.05) enhanced colony formation (Fig. 4D), further supporting its proliferation-promoting effect. No significant change was observed in ROS levels for the GHD treatment compared to the control group (Fig. 4E–F, Fig. S4). However, both OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD treatments resulted in a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in ROS levels. Interestingly, there was no significant difference between the OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD groups, suggesting that PDGF-BB did not significantly alter the oxidative stress response when combined with OA/GHD. ELISA results demonstrated that VEGFA secretion was significantly elevated (P < 0.05) in the OA/GHD group compared to the control, with BB/OA/GHD showing an even further significant increase (P < 0.05) in VEGFA levels (Fig. 4G). These results highlight that OA/GHD treatment upregulates VEGFA production in ESCs, and the addition of PDGF-BB (in BB/OA/GHD) further enhances this effect, due to PDGF-BB's role in promoting angiogenesis. Similarly, VEGFA mRNA expression was also significantly upregulated (P < 0.05) in the OA/GHD group compared to the control (Fig. 4H). The BB/OA/GHD treatment further increased VEGFA mRNA expression (P < 0.05), supporting the ELISA data and suggesting that the combination of OA and PDGF-BB synergistically enhances VEGFA transcription in ESCs. The upregulation of VEGFA at both the protein and mRNA levels are necessary for angiogenesis through VEGFA signaling pathways (Fig. 4I).

Therapeutic effect of hydrogel formulations in the IUA rat model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the hydrogel formulations, an intrauterine adhesion (IUA) rat model was established by inducing endometrial injury with ethanol. The control group was injected with PBS to simulate the surgical procedure. Following injury, rats received intrauterine administration of PBS (Etoh group), GEL, GHD, OA/GHD, or BB/OA/GHD for two weeks (Fig. 5A). Representative gross (Fig. 5B) and histological images (Fig. 5C) are exhibited. Compared to the uninjured control group, the Etoh group exhibited severe endometrial damage, characterized by a significant reduction (P < 0.01) in endometrial thickness and a marked loss of endometrial glands. In contrast, treatment with the GHD hydrogel partially restored glandular structures, although the improvements were limited. Notably, the OA/GHD-treated group demonstrated a more substantial regenerative response, with an increased number of glands and improved stromal organization. The most pronounced therapeutic effect was observed in the BB/OA/GHD group. This group exhibited well-organized stromal and epithelial structures, along with significant increases (P < 0.05) in both endometrial thickness and gland density (Fig. 5D and E). Besides, BB/OA/GHD showed biocompatibility by histopathological analysis (H&E staining) of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) (Fig. S5). These results suggest that the combined delivery of OA and BB via the GHD hydrogel synergistically promotes endometrial regeneration. Zhang et al. employed the sequential delivery of synergistic growth factors IGF-1 and VEGF to facilitate both structural and functional endometrial regeneration. Their study demonstrated that the hydrogel system effectively promotes the regeneration of endometrial structure through mechanisms such as vascular remodeling, glandular regeneration, and the proliferation of endometrial cells. Moreover, it concurrently enhances the rate of embryo implantation and live birth [41].

The in vivo anti-inflammatory effects on endometrial regeneration

The anti-inflammatory effects of gel treatment on endometrial regeneration were investigated (Fig. 6A). The results demonstrated a significant reduction (P < 0.05) in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the GHD-treated group compared to the ethanol (etoh) group. Specifically, the levels of IL-1β (Fig. 6B), IL-6 (Fig. 6C), and TNF-α (Fig. 6D) were markedly decreased following GHD treatment, indicating an anti-inflammatory effect. Notably, the combination treatments, OA/GHD and OA/PDGF-BB/GHD, showed even further significant reductions (P < 0.05) in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, although no significant difference was observed between these two combination treatments. This indicates that while both OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD are effective in reducing inflammation, the addition of PDGF-BB, which is typically known for its angiogenic properties, did not result in a synergistic effect. These cytokines are key mediators of inflammation and have been implicated in various pathologies, including endometrial dysfunction. The reduction of these pro-inflammatory markers suggests that BB/OA/GHD treatment might help mitigate inflammation in the endometrial tissue, promoting a more favorable environment for regeneration. On the other hand, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 6E) was significantly increased (P < 0.05) in the GHD-treated group, with even greater increases observed in both the OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD groups. IL-10 plays a critical role in regulating the immune response by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and promoting tissue repair. The increased expression of IL-10 following GHD treatment further supports the hypothesis that GHD not only reduces inflammation but also contributes to a more pro-regenerative environment, potentially facilitating endometrial tissue regeneration. Moreover, the mRNA expression results obtained through PCR analysis (Fig. 6F–I) were consistent with the protein-level findings, further validating the observed trends. Moreover, serum analysis of these systemic inflammatory markers also showed consistent trend (Fig. S6). Collectively, BB/OA/GHD integrating GHD gel and OA significantly modulates both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, creating an optimal microenvironment for endometrial regeneration.

The in vivo antioxidant activity and VEGFA-mediated angiogenesis on endometrial regeneration

The antioxidant activity and VEGFA-mediated angiogenesis in endometrial regeneration were evaluated (Fig. 7A). Collagen is a key extracellular matrix component, and its excessive accumulation can lead to fibrosis, which is often associated with impaired tissue regeneration. To study the collagen deposition in the endometrial tissue, Masson's staining was performed (Fig. 7B and C). The Etoh group demonstrated a significant increase in collagen deposition, indicating that ethanol exposure led to fibrotic changes in the endometrial tissue, which could impede regeneration. The GHD group exhibited a decrease in collagen deposition compared to the ETOH group, suggesting that GHD treatment may have had a protective effect on collagen synthesis, potentially through the modulation of inflammatory pathways. Interestingly, the OA/GHD group showed further significant relief in collagen deposition, indicating that the combination of OA and GHD led to more effective regulation of collagen synthesis. The BB/OA/GHD group showed the most pronounced effect, with the lowest levels of collagen deposition. This suggests that the combination therapy with PDGF-BB further enhanced the efficacy of OA/GHD, providing superior protection against collagen accumulation. The assessment of oxidative stress is critical in understanding tissue damage and regeneration, as ROS play a central role in both pathological and regenerative processes. The ROS fluorescence intensity was measured in the endometrial tissue (Fig. 7D). The Etoh group exhibited a marked increase in ROS levels, which is consistent with the well-known effects of ethanol-induced oxidative stress. Increased ROS production can lead to cellular damage, inflammation, and fibrosis, further impairing tissue regeneration. In contrast, the GHD group showed a slight decrease in ROS levels, though this change was not statistically significant, suggesting the limited impact. However, both the OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD groups demonstrated a significant reduction (P < 0.05) in ROS levels, indicating that the combined therapies with OA were more effective in reducing oxidative stress. Notably, there was no significant difference between the OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD groups, which suggests that the addition of PDGF-BB to the OA/GHD treatment did not further enhance the antioxidative effects.

VEGFA is a critical regulator of angiogenesis, the process by which new blood vessels are formed from pre-existing ones. This is essential for tissue regeneration, including endometrial healing. The protein levels and mRNA levels of VEGFA were measured by ELISA (Fig. 7E) and PCR (Fig. 7F) in rat uterine tissues, respectively. In the control, Etoh, and GHD groups, there were no significant differences in VEGFA expression, suggesting that these treatments did not substantially affect the angiogenic response in the endometrial tissue. However, the OA/GHD group showed a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in VEGFA levels, indicating that this treatment combination may have reduced the angiogenic response. Interestingly, the BB/OA/GHD group exhibited an even more significant reduction (P < 0.05) in VEGFA expression, suggesting that the addition of PDGF-BB to the OA/GHD combination further dampened the VEGFA-mediated angiogenesis. Importantly, the reduction in VEGFA levels was found to result from the synergistic effect of OA and PDGF-BB. This combined treatment likely modulated VEGFA expression through a coordinated action, reducing the excessive angiogenic activity that could otherwise disrupt proper tissue regeneration. Similarly, Lv et al. reported the treatment with IVK8-RGD hydrogel loaded with UC-MSCs significantly restored endometrial thickness, inhibited fibrosis, and facilitated angiogenesis by activating the Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway [42]. These effects were associated with a marked improvement in fertility and live birth rates in the treated rats. Both studies of Lv's and ours highlight the promising potential of hydrogels as delivery vehicles for stem cells or bioactive molecules, which can promote tissue repair and regeneration in the endometrium.

Fertility restoration and live birth outcomes

A schematic representation of gel treatment and the resulting outcomes related to embryonic development and live birth was shown in Fig. 8A. The number of embryos was consistent with number of fetuses observed in different treatment groups (Fig. 8B–D). The Etoh group had a significantly (P < 0.05) lower number of embryos and fetuses compared to the control group, indicating that ethanol exposure led to impaired fertility and reduced embryo viability. This result underscores the negative impact of ethanol on reproductive function, which is consistent with previous findings linking ethanol-induced toxicity to endometrial dysfunction. In contrast, the GHD group exhibited a significantly (P < 0.05) higher number of embryos and fetuses compared to the Etoh group. The OA/GHD and BB/OA/GHD treatments further enhanced fertility outcomes, with both groups demonstrating a significant increase in the number of embryos and fetuses compared to the Etoh group. Notably, the BB/OA/GHD group showed embryo and fetal numbers comparable to the control group, suggesting that the combination therapy was highly effective in restoring endometrial function and promoting successful pregnancy outcomes. This suggests that BB/OA/GHD treatment had a positive impact on restoring fertility, with a notable recovery in embryo and fetal development. The restoration of fertility may be attributed to the anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and regenerative properties, which likely improved the endometrial environment, allowing for better embryo implantation and development. There were no noticeable differences in fetal weight between the groups, suggesting that the treatments did not significantly affect fetal growth, indicating that the fertility restoration was not associated with adverse effects on fetal development (Fig. 8E). This observation highlights that the treatments focused on restoring fertility and endometrial function rather than impacting the normal growth patterns of the fetuses. The live birth rate in the Etoh group was significantly reduced (P < 0.01) compared to the control group, confirming that ethanol exposure severely impaired reproductive success (Fig. 8F). However, the BB/OA/GHD group exhibited the highest live birth rate, which was significantly higher than the GHD and OA/GHD groups (P < 0.05). This suggests that the combination treatment of BB/OA/GHD was the most effective in improving fertility outcomes and ensuring successful live births. The GHD and OA/GHD groups also showed improved live birth rates compared to the Etoh group, but they were not as high as the BB/OA/GHD group, indicating that BB's addition provided an additional benefit for fertility restoration.

The present study demonstrates the potential of the biocompatible injectable BB/OA/GHD for synergistic endometrial regeneration and fertility restoration. While the results are promising, it is important to acknowledge some limitations and future directions. Although the hydrogel demonstrated effective restoration of endometrial architecture and fertility outcomes, the precise signaling pathways involved in these processes remain to be explored [43]. Larger animal cohorts will provide a more reliable measure of the hydrogel's potential for fertility restoration and endometrial regeneration, offering a stronger basis for human clinical trials. The translation of this hydrogel formulation from preclinical models to human clinical use presents several challenges including scaling up synthesis, long-term stability, and human-specific physiological differences [44,45].

Conclusion

The GHD hydrogel composed of OA and PDGF-BB has demonstrated substantial potential for promoting endometrial regeneration and fertility restoration in a rat model of IUA. Through a comprehensive evaluation of physicochemical properties, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and therapeutic efficacy, it was shown that the hydrogel exhibited excellent injectability, self-healing capabilities, and sustained release profiles. These characteristics are critical for its use in tissue repair applications, ensuring uniform distribution at the injury site and prolonged therapeutic effects. In vivo studies revealed that the hydrogel formulations, especially the combination of OA and PDGF-BB, significantly enhanced endometrial regeneration by reducing inflammation, oxidative stress, and collagen deposition, while promoting cell proliferation and angiogenesis. These findings underscore the synergistic effects of OA and PDGF-BB, which not only mitigate inflammation but also support the restoration of the endometrial structure, thereby improving the likelihood of successful pregnancy outcomes. The BB/OA/GHD's ability to restore fertility, as demonstrated by the increased number of embryos and live births, further validates its potential as a novel therapeutic approach for endometrial dysfunction and infertility treatment. In summary, the BB/OA/GHD represents a promising strategy for regenerative medicine, specifically for endometrial repair and fertility restoration. Its multifunctional properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and regenerative effects, make it an ideal candidate for clinical applications aimed at restoring endometrial function and improving reproductive health.
