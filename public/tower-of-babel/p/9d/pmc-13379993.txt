Helicobacter pylori-specific genes in gastric biopsies of patients with autoimmune gastritis with and without gastric cancer
Authors: L. Belloni, G. Cozza, S. Cingolani, F. Fabretti, M. Vavallo, G. Esposito, E. Pilozzi, B. Annibale, E. Lahner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectAutoimmune gastritis(AIG), at increased gastric cancer(GC) risk, may be purely autoimmune or H.pylori-triggered. GC-risk in AIG may be H. pylori-related, but cannot be excluded in pure AIG. Distinguishing pure AIG(H.pylori-naïve) from that H.pylori-triggered is challenging. H.pylori-specific genes in AIG patients’ gastric tissue have not yet been assessed. This study aimed to assess H.pylori-specific genes on DNA extracted from gastric biopsies in AIG patients with and without GC.MethodsThis single-center study included 63 histologically-diagnosed AIG patients (median age 63 years; F88.7%) with parietal cell(100%) and/or intrinsic factor autoantibodies(42.6%) positivity, of whom ten were complicated with GC. DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded(FFPE) tissue sections of gastric biopsies and RT-PCR was performed to test for H.pylori-specific gene probes:ureA, ureC, cagA. 16sRNA and β2-microglobulin probes were used as amplification-control of bacterial and human DNA. Previous history of H.pylori infection and histopathology were negative in all 63 AIG patients.ResultsAmong AIG patients without GC, the H.pylori-specific ureC gene was detected in four(7.5%), three of whom were also ureA-positive, and one was also cagA-positive. All four ureC-positive patients had positive H.pylori serology. H.pylori-related genes were not detected in any of the ten AIG patients with GC. All available sera of AIG patients with GC were H.pylori seronegative(6/6100%). Bacterial 16 S rRNA and human-β2-microglobulin genes were similar in ureC-positive and ureC-negative AIG patients.ConclusionAssessment of H.pylori-related genes helps confirm patients with the pure H.pylori-naïve AIG type. This latter group also comprises AIG patients with GC, supporting the development of GC in AIG through H. pylori-independent mechanisms.

Introduction

Autoimmune gastritis (AIG) is a chronic inflammatory disorder in which the immune system targets and damages the gastric oxyntic glands, leading over time to mucosal atrophy, hypochlorhydria, and intrinsic factor deficiency [1,2]. AIG is typically associated with other autoimmune diseases such as autoimmune thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes and vitiligo, and, in the advanced atrophic stage, with pernicious anaemia and an increased risk of type I gastric neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) [3,4]. In contrast, the development of gastric adenocarcinoma in the context of AIG remains a debated topic [1,5].

In a subset of patients, AIG is triggered by a prior H. pylori infection [6], probably due to antigenic crossreactivity with the proton pump H+,K + -ATPase, the main autoantigen localized on the parietal cells of the corpus oxyntic mucosa. D'Elios MM et al. showed homologies between some amino acid sequences of the H+,K + -ATPase and surface proteins of H. pylori, thus enabling antigen mimicry and antigenic crossreactivity of activated autoreactive T cells and triggering gastric autoimmunity by H. pylori [7].

In AIG, assessing past H. pylori infection is challenging and often relies on anamnestic data (previous H. pylori eradication treatment or histological positivity) and/or serology documenting prior exposure to H. pylori [1,6,8]. The difficulty in assessing with certainty patients with pure AIG (H. pylori naïve) and patients with AIG triggered by previous H. pylori infection may contribute to confounding the gastric cancer estimates in patients with AIG [1,6,9], although an intrinsic gastric cancer risk in pure AIG cannot be definitely excluded. H. pylori exposure may play a role in gastric cancer risk in patients with AIG, as H. pylori has been recognized as a group 1 (definite) carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), a subordinate organization of the World Health Organization (WHO), that means that H. pylori is certainly linked to carcinogenesis in Humans [10].

Rugge et al. reported that corpus-restricted inflammation in AIG does not increase the risk of gastric cancer, and that the excess gastric cancer risk reported in patients with AIG could plausibly result from unrecognized previous or current H. pylori comorbidity [11]. This previous paper defined patients with negative H. pylori histology and serology, along with negative molecular biology testing on DNA extracted from gastric biopsies, as H. pylori naïve and thus as pure AIG [11]. In this study, a commercial kit using only one H. pylori-specific gene was applied. The presence of a panel of H. pylori-specific genes in gastric tissue from patients with AIG has not been assessed to date. This molecular biology approach might be useful for detecting patients with H. pylori exposure, thereby permitting classification into pure or H. pylori-triggered AIG. Thus, the current study aimed to assess H. pylori-related genes in DNA extracted from gastric mucosal biopsies by PCR in patients with AIG, with and without gastric cancer, along with H. pylori-positive and -negative controls.

Methods

Study population and design

This single-center study included 63 patients with histologically diagnosed AIG at the atrophic stage (age median 63, range 28-88 years; females n = 44, 88.7%) with positivity against parietal cell (100%) and/or intrinsic factor autoantibodies (42.6%), of whom ten patients were complicated with gastric cancer (in three patients gastric cancer was already present at the time of diagnosis of AIG, in seven patients gastric cancer developed at follow-up, median 96 months, range 12-156 months).

DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections of gastric mucosa biopsies. Real-time PCR was performed on DNA extracted from FFPE gastric corpus biopsies of AIG patients with and without gastric cancer to test for amplification of H. pylori-specific gene probes: ureA, ureC, and cagA. In AIG patients with gastric cancer, to further increase the sensitivity of H. pylori-specific gene amplification, digital PCR was performed on DNA extracted from FFPE tissue sections of gastric corpus biopsies, in addition to real-time PCR. To assess the reliability of H. pylori-specific gene probes, real-time PCR was performed on four positive controls (two with H. pylori-positive superficial antral gastritis and two with H. pylori-positive atrophic gastritis) and two negative controls (healthy stomach). To test the quality (goodness) of the extracted DNA, 16 S rRNA and β2-microglobulin-specific probes were used as amplification controls for bacterial and human DNA.

As further controls, four patients with gastric cancer developed in non-AIG, but extensive (antrum and corpus involving) atrophic gastritis, the typical pattern of H. pylori-related atrophic gastritis, were additionally tested for the presence of H. pylori-related genes in gastric mucosa tissue sections.

All patients enrolled in the study provided informed consent, and the local ethical committee authorized the study (6527/2021).

Diagnosis of autoimmune gastritis

During gastroscopy, five gastric biopsies were obtained according to the updated Sydney System: two from the antrum, one from the angularis incisura, and two from the corpus-fundus [12] which were sent for histopathological assessment of gastritis. Gastric biopsy samples were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined by a pathologist with high expertise in gastric pathology (EP). Each sample was evaluated for inflammation, atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and H. pylori infection according to the updated Sydney System [12]. AIG diagnosis was based on corpus-restricted glandular atrophy with oxyntic gland loss, ECL cell hyperplasia, and corpus-restricted pseudopyloric and/or intestinal metaplasia, with a spared antral mucosa representing the main histological marker of AIG [1,2,6].

Anti-parietal cell and/or anti-intrinsic factor autoantibodies were positive in all patients, as tested by luminescent immunoprecipitation system [13,14].

Status of H. pylori

Previous history of H. pylori infection and histopathology were negative for H. pylori in all 63 patients with AIG. Also, active inflammatory infiltrate (polymorphonuclear cells), an indirect marker of active H. pylori infection [15],was absent in all patients. H. pylori serology (Ig antibodies against highly specific H. pylori antigens), as assessed by ELISA or multiplex serology, was available from 59 AIG patients; from four AIG patients with gastric cancer, serology was not available. H. pylori serology was positive in 17 (28.9%), undetermined (defined as low seroreactivity not reaching the cutoff for positivity) in 19 (32.2%), and negative in 23 (38.9%) patients [15,16].

Of the four positive controls, two had H. pylori-positive superficial antral gastritis (one female aged 39 years, one male aged 68 years), and the other two had H. pylori-positive atrophic gastritis (two males aged 74 years and 69 years). The two negative controls (one female aged 39 years, one male aged 44 years) had healthy stomachs, defined as the absence of histopathological alterations in the gastric antral and corpus mucosa. The four controls with gastric cancer in non-AIG, but extensive atrophic gastritis (three females, one male, aged 71, 68, 57, and 64 years), had negative H. pylori histology and all but one had positive serology for H. pylori infection.

H. pylori DNA extraction and amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from ten 5 μm sections of FFPE tissue samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 80234), according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration was assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry, and then Real-time PCR (qPCR) assays were performed. We used TaqMan hydrolysis probes on an Applied Biosystems platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and we applied the following amplification protocol: 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 10 s. A positivity threshold was set at Ct ≤ 38. Specific probes were used to amplify Helicobacter target genes, including ureA, ureC, and cagA. The primer and probe sequences were designed based on previously published studies [17,18] and validated by BLAST analysis against the reference genome NC_018939.1. Serial dilutions of genomic DNA from H. pylori strain 26695 (purchased by ATCC) were used to generate standard curves for absolute quantification. Total bacterial DNA was additionally assessed by targeting the 16 S rRNA gene with specific probes, and amounts of genomic DNA were analyzed by qPCR targeting the human beta-2-microglobulin (B2M) gene.

To further increase the sensitivity of H. pylori-specific gene detection, DNA extracted from gastric corpus biopsies of AIG patients with gastric cancer was additionally analyzed by digital PCR (dPCR). The same primer–probe sets and thermal cycling conditions used for real-time PCR were applied. Digital PCR was performed using a Bio-Rad platform, according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were expressed as number (%) of total, mean ± SD or median (range).

Differences in the main clinical features of AIG patients with and without gastric cancer were assessed by Student's t-test for continuous variables and by Chi-square test for dichotomous variables.

Results

Of the 63 included AIG patients, 53 (84.1%) did not develop gastric cancer neither at time of AIG diagnosis nor at follow-up (median 74 months, range 12-204), while 10 (15.9%) patients developed gastric cancer, three of them at the time of AIG diagnosis, while the other seven at a median follow-up of 96 months, range 12-156 months. The overall follow-up period between the two groups was not different (p > 0.784). Also, age, sex, low serum cobalamin levels, and positivity against intrinsic factor or parietal cell autoantibodies were similar between groups. Compared to AIG patients without gastric cancer, those with gastric cancer had more frequent severe corpus atrophy (p = 0.016) and all but one had intestinal metaplasia, albeit not statistically significant (p = 0.368). In contrast, H. pylori-related genes were not detected in any of the 10 AIG patients with gastric cancer, indicating the absence of H. pylori-specific genes in gastric corpus biopsies from these patients. To investigate more thoroughly the eventual presence of H. pylori-specific genes in these patients, based on the knowledge that H. pylori infection generally starts in the antral mucosa, we extracted DNA also from FFPE antral mucosa biopsies and ran not only real-time PCR but also digital PCR in both specimens (antral and corpus DNA). We could not identify H. pylori-specific genes in any of these experiments, indicating H. pylori negativity in these patients. Of these ten AIG patients with gastric cancer, all available sera were H. pylori seronegative (6/6, 100%). By definition, the antral (pyloric) mucosa was spared in all AIG patients and atrophy and metaplasia was restricted to the corpus oxyntic mucosa.

In the four gastric cancer patients with non-AIG but extensive atrophic gastritis, only one tested ureC-positive, while the other three tested negative for H. pylori-specific genes, notwithstanding the typical H. pylori-related atrophic pattern.

Discussion

AIG may be a pure autoimmune-mediated disorder or may be triggered by H. pylori infection [1,2,6]. H. pylori exposure may increase gastric cancer risk in AIG patients due to the intrinsic carcinogenic potential of H. pylori [10],although an intrinsic gastric cancer risk in pure AIG cannot be excluded [19]. The assessment of previous H. pylori infection in AIG might be clinically relevant for the prognosis of neoplastic complications.

The current study showed that the overall detection rate of H. pylori-specific genes was relatively low (7.7%) in patients with advanced AIG at the atrophic stage, well-characterized by atrophy and metaplastic changes restricted to the corpus mucosa, a spared antrum, and gastric autoantibody positivity. This low prevalence cannot be attributed to low DNA quality or insufficient DNA abundance, two well-known challenges when DNA is extracted from FFPE tissue [[20], [21], [22]]. The 16 S rRNA gene, representative of bacterial genes, and the β2-microglobulin gene, representative of human genes, were both detectable, thus showing that the lack of amplification of H. pylori-specific genes can reasonably be attributed to their absence in the tested genetic material. One plausible explanation for the low prevalence of H. pylori-specific genes in the tested AIG patients is that a subset of them is H. pylori naïve, meaning they have never been exposed to the bacteria and thus belong to the “pure” AIG type. This may reasonably apply to those AIG patients who tested H. pylori-negative by serology (n = 23, 38.9%), but it does not explain why a high proportion of AIG patients with positive H. pylori serology (13/17, 76.5%) tested negative for H. pylori-specific genes. In this group of AIG patients with positive H. pylori serology, displaying a documented previous contact with the infection, a possible explanation for the low prevalence of retrieved H. pylori-specific genes might be that these genes persist for a shorter time in the gastric tissue compared to the persistence of the immunological memory (IgG antibodies) in the sera. In this context, it was interesting to observe that in the four patients complicated with gastric cancer on the background of non-autoimmune, extensive (antrum- and corpus-involving) atrophic gastritis but without evidence of H. pylori at histology, H. pylori genes could be retrieved in only one patient, while the other three tested negative for H. pylori-specific genes, thus supporting the poor long-term persistence of H. pylori genetic traces in the gastric tissue, even in the typical pattern of H. pylori-related atrophic gastritis. Post-eradication studies showed that, despite successful eradication as documented by negative histology or urea breath test, H. pylori genes were retrieved in low abundance up to one to two years of eradication treatment [23]. Analogously, H. pylori-specific genes were not detectable in any of the AIG patients complicated with gastric cancer. In this population, DNA extracted from gastric biopsies was tested by real-time and digital PCR to increase further the sensitivity and reliability of H. pylori-specific gene detection. Nevertheless, no H. pylori genes were retrieved, thus confirming a lack of H. pylori exposure and a pure AIG type in at least 6 patients with negative H. pylori serology (unfortunately, serology was not available for the remaining 4). This finding supports the increased gastric risk in AIG independent of previous H. pylori exposure.

At the same time, these findings confirm the relevant role of H. pylori serology in the specific clinical context of AIG as a useful marker of past infection. As previously shown in AIG and, more generally, in patients with atrophic gastritis extended to the corpus mucosa, parallel to the worsening and progression of the oxyntic atrophic damage the bacterium may progressively disappear making the diagnosis of infection challenging [16,24]. The difficulty to reliably distinguish between pure AIG and H. pylori-related atrophic gastritis, was further shown in a study reporting that in patients with extensive corpus- and antrum-involving atrophic gastritis, typically related to H. pylori infection, the antral mucosa may heal over time in about 25%, even without previous eradication treatment, thus mimicking the typical histological features of AIG [25].

Irrespective of the aetiology of atrophic gastritis, the progression of the disease along the steps of intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and finally gastric cancer has been described many years ago [26]. Several reports document that intestinal metaplasia is the pathological background in which carcinogenesis occurs [27]. This preneoplastic condition has been reported as a point of no return [28]. Moreover, many gastric cancer lesions test H. pylori negative, and gastric cancer may occur even after successful eradication treatment, thus showing that gastric cancer development may occur independently from the presence of the bacteria via H. pylori-independent pathways [29]. Possibly, the last steps leading from intestinal metaplasia to gastric cancer may occur independently of H. pylori, driven by other mechanisms, such as the accumulation of stem cell mutations [[28], [29], [30]]. In fact, all but one of the AIG patients complicated with gastric cancer had metaplastic atrophy in the corpus (Fig. 2). A previous paper conducted in Japan, compared clinic-pathological features of gastric cancer in AIG patients with and without H. pylori infection showing that H. pylori-unexposed AIG-gastric cancer patients were characterized by younger age, higher gastrin levels, and more frequent undifferentiated-type gastric cancer of the upper-middle stomach suggesting that H. pylori and AIG might play distinct roles on gastric carcinogenesis [31].Fig. 2Representative hematoxylin and eosin stained image of gastric tissue sections from a patient with H. pylori negative autoimmune gastritis (40× magnification). The population-based risk of gastric cancer in AIG is much lower than in H. pylori-related atrophy due to the much higher worldwide prevalence of the infection compared to the prevalence of AIG. However, the individual risk of gastric cancer exists in both groups, strengthening the need for surveillance, as recommended by MAPS III guidelines [32].

In conclusion, the findings of the current study show that assessing H. pylori-related genes using molecular biology techniques helps confirm patients with the pure AIG type, H. pylori-negative at histology, serology, and molecular biology, and thus never H. pylori-exposed. This latter group also comprises AIG patients complicated with gastric cancer, supporting the development of gastric cancer in AIG by H. pylori-independent mechanisms.
