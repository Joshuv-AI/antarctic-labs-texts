Two mitochondrial HMG-box proteins, Cim1 and Abf2, antagonistically regulate mtDNA copy number in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Simon Schrott, Christof Osman
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mitochondrial genome, mtDNA, is present in multiple copies in cells and encodes essential subunits of oxidative phosphorylation complexes. mtDNA levels have to change in response to metabolic demands and copy number alterations are implicated in various diseases. The mitochondrial HMG-box proteins Abf2 in yeast and TFAM in mammals are critical for mtDNA maintenance and packaging and have been linked to mtDNA copy number control. Here, we discover the previously unrecognized mitochondrial HMG-box protein Cim1 (copy number influence on mtDNA) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, which exhibits metabolic state dependent mtDNA association. Surprisingly, in contrast to Abf2’s supportive role in mtDNA maintenance, Cim1 negatively regulates mtDNA copy number. Cells lacking Cim1 display increased mtDNA levels and enhanced mitochondrial function, while Cim1 overexpression results in mtDNA loss. Intriguingly, Cim1 deletion alleviates mtDNA maintenance defects associated with loss of Abf2, while defects caused by Cim1 overexpression are mitigated by simultaneous overexpression of Abf2. Moreover, we find that the conserved LON protease Pim1 is essential to maintain low Cim1 levels, thereby preventing its accumulation and concomitant repressive effects on mtDNA. We propose a model in which the protein ratio of antagonistically acting Cim1 and Abf2 determines mtDNA copy number.

Introduction

The genome of mitochondria, referred to as mtDNA, encodes subunits required for the respiratory chain complexes. Multiple copies of mtDNA are present in cells, and the number of copies can vary between species and different cell types within the same organism. The budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae contains approximately 20–40 copies of mtDNA (1). In humans mtDNA copy numbers can range from 500 in lungs, liver and kidney (2) to 100 000 in oocytes (3). Moreover, mtDNA copy number (mtDNA CN) adapts in response to metabolic or physiological requirements. mtDNA content in S. cerevisiae increases approximately twofold, when cells are grown on a non-fermentable carbon source, where OXPHOS activity is required for cellular energy supply (1). In mammalian cells, changes in mtDNA levels have been observed depending on the developmental stage of cells, in a variety of different diseases and during the ageing process (4,5). Interestingly, it has been proposed that mitochondrial diseases can be ameliorated by an increase in mtDNA levels which can drive compensatory OXPHOS biogenesis (6,7). However, the mechanisms that determine mtDNA CN in cells remain incompletely understood.

mtDNA is organized into nucleoprotein structures known as nucleoids. Core packaging proteins required for normal organization of mtDNA are TFAM in mammals and its homolog Abf2 in S. cerevisiae (8–10). Both proteins possess two tandem HMG-boxes that each can bind to mtDNA and thereby induce turns in the bound DNA to facilitate packaging (11–20). Additionally, TFAM, in contrast to Abf2, contains a C-terminal extension that forms contacts with the RNA polymerase and facilitates transcriptional initiation (21–29).

TFAM and Abf2 are not only involved in mtDNA packaging, but also play a crucial role in regulating mtDNA CN. The abundance of both proteins is critical in this process. Mice containing only one TFAM allele exhibit reduced mtDNA CNs (30), while mild TFAM overexpression leads to an increase in mtDNA levels (22,31–33). Similarly, absence of Abf2 in S. cerevisiae results in mtDNA instability, while two to threefold overexpression of ABF2 leads to increased mtDNA levels (34). However, strong overexpression of ABF2 can also result in the loss of mtDNA (34). It has been proposed that increased TFAM-to-mtDNA ratios cause strong compaction, preventing transcription and replication of mtDNA in mammals (17,33,35,36).

The mitochondrial matrix LON protease has been identified as a potential regulator of Abf2 or TFAM levels. In Drosophila melanogaster, overexpression or depletion of the LON protease leads to a decrease or increase in TFAM levels and a concomitant reduction or rise in mtDNA CN, respectively (37). Similarly, depletion of LON in human cells with severe mtDNA deficits results in increased TFAM and mtDNA levels (38). In S. cerevisiae, a role of the LON protease, Pim1, in the degradation of Abf2 has so far only been demonstrated in vitro (39). It has been suggested that LON may not efficiently degrade DNA-bound forms of Abf2 or TFAM. DNA binding was proposed to be negatively affected by phosphorylation of Abf2 and TFAM, which thereby facilitates their degradation (38,40).

Here we identify a previously unrecognized HMG-box protein present in yeast mitochondria, which is encoded by the open reading frame YOR114W. Remarkably, we find that Yor114w limits mtDNA CN and antagonizes Abf2. In the following, YOR114W will be referred to as CIM1 based on its copy number influence on mtDNA.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth conditions

Yeast strains and plasmids used in this study are described in Supplementary Table S1. Mutants were generated from the W303 (leu2-3,112 can1-100 ura3-1 his3-11,15) WT backgrounds yCO380 or yCO381 (41). Liquid cultures were grown in yeast complete (YP) medium (1% yeast extract, 2% bacto-peptone, 0.004% adenine (all w/v), pH 5.5) containing either 2% (w/v) glucose (YPD) or 3% (v/v) glycerol (YPG) as carbon source with an agitation of 160 rpm at either 30 or 37°C. For Supplementary Figure S4G YP medium was supplemented with 2.42% (w/v) lactate (YPLac) or 2% (w/v) galactose (YPGal). For plates, medium was supplemented with 2% (w/v) bacto-agar and antibiotics were added for selection of transformants in the following concentrations: 300 μg/ml hygromycin, 300 μg/ml G418, 100 μg/ml nourseothricin. YPG plates containing additionally 0.1% glucose (indicator plates) were used for determination of petite frequencies and tetrad dissections. Synthetic defined (SD) selection plates without uracil of pH 5.5 contained 0.67% (w/v) yeast nitrogen base, 0.192% (w/v) drop-out mix minus uracil (US Biological D9535), 2% (w/v) glucose and 2% (w/v) bacto-agar. For synthetic complete selection plates additionally 0.1% (w/v) 5-fluoroorotic acid monohydrate (5-FOA) and 0.005% (w/v) uracil were added. Sporulation plates contained 0.3% (w/v) potassium acetate, 0.048% (w/v) drop-out mix minus uracil, 0.00125% (w/v) uracil and 2% (w/v) bacto-agar.

Generation of yeast strains

Strains were generated through stable integration of constructs into the haploid yeast genome based on homologous recombination. Log phase cells were transformed with linearized plasmids or as previously described PCR amplified fragments (42), successful integration into the desired locus was confirmed by colony PCR or screening for fluorescent signals. Deletion and correct tagging of proteins was confirmed by western blotting and the integrity of tags and point mutations was verified by sequencing. For metabolic comparability, antibiotics resistance markers instead of prototrophies were used. Only for ratiometric imaging of redox potential and pH all strains contained an additional URA3 marker. The generation of marker-less yeast mutants at the genomic loci of ABF2 and CIM1 was based on a two-step selection process described previously (43,44). Briefly, the respective ORF was replaced with the COunterselectable REporter (CORE) cassette of pCORE-UH (Kluyveromyces lactis URA3 gene and hphNT1 encoded resistance to hygromycin) by standard homologous recombination-mediated integration. In the second step, amplified PCR products of mutant alleles (generated either by direct PCR-fusion or NEBuilder assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich)) were transformed into the CORE-UH containing strains. To ensure maintenance of mtDNA in the Δabf2 strain, cells were cultivated in YPG medium before further selection processes were performed in the presence of glucose. Transformants originating from 4.5 OD cells were recovered overnight on two separate YPD plates and clones that had lost uracil prototrophy were selected by multiple rounds of replica plating on 5-FOA. Positive clones were further checked based on the absence of resistance against hygromycin and confirmed by sequencing of the respective locus. Mutants were backcrossed as described in tetrad dissection analysis to reintroduce mtDNA.

Drop dilution growth analysis

Starting concentrations of 3.2 × 106 cells per ml of logarithmically growing cells were diluted in 1:5 series and a volume of 3.5 μl was spotted on YPD or YPG. Plates were incubated for 48 h before images were acquired with a Vilber Fusion FX imaging system. For reasons of space in most of the figures the first three dilutions are depicted. To better discern subtle growth defects, dilutions 3–5 are shown in Supplementary Figure S3G.

Growth curve and rate determined by backscatter analysis

Log phase grown overnight cultures were adjusted to same densities and grown for further 1–2 duplications to an OD660 between 0.4 and 0.6. Cells were diluted to 0.08 × 107 cells in a volume of 25 ml medium and cultivated at 160 rpm agitation in 250 ml culture flasks with metal caps. Due to the higher sensitivity of the system in higher density ranges, for acute overexpression in Figure 6A, 0.3 × 107 cells in a volume of 25 ml were equilibrated in the system for 3 h before estradiol to a f.c. of 200 nM was added to the culture. Growth was monitored and processed by the CGQuant software. Based on the first 20 min of measurement the offset of the backscatter signal was adjusted to 100, backscatter signals were denoised to an intermediate level and growth rates smoothed by 2 degrees. Growth competition assay

Log phase overnight cultures of WT (yCO380) and Δcim1 (ySS734a) in YPG medium cultivated at 30 or 37°C were adjusted to same densities and cultivated for at least two further duplications. 4 × 107 cells were harvested for 4 min at 3146 g, resuspended in 15 ml of fresh medium and optical densities were determined again. Cultures of WT and Δcim1 were then mixed by combining 3 × 107 cells from each strain in a volume of 30 ml. 15 ml of the resulting culture were harvested for the starting time point. To ensure largely unaltered supply with nutrients, the remaining cultures were continuously diluted in a total volume of 15 ml. During day (∼10 h), cultures were maintained between an on average minimal OD660 of ∼0.123 ± 0.080 up to a maximal OD660 of ∼0.449 ± 0.164. Over night (∼14 h) cultures were maintained between an on average minimal OD660 of ∼0.022 ± 0.015 up to a maximal OD660 of ∼0.940 ± 0.730. 3 × 107 cells were harvested for determination of COX1, CIM1 and TAF10 abundance were in mid log phase in the middle of the day.

Analysis of petite frequency

Overnight cultures were diluted and cultivated for at least 2–3 duplications to mid-log phase. Cells were then diluted 1:10 in H2O in a volume of 2 ml. Cell concentration was estimated by measurement of the OD660. The sample was further diluted 1:100 with H2O and adjusted to 4000 cells/ml in a volume of 1 ml. 200 cells in a volume of 50 μl were plated on 2 indicator plates (YPG + 0.1% glucose) for each biological replicate and cultivated for 48 h at 30°C. Petite frequencies of each biological replicate were determined by counting the amount of large (ρ+) and small (ρ- and ρ0) cells with the help of a custom-built thresholding-based semi-automated and supervised macro in Fiji/ImageJ (46).

Isolation of genomic DNA from total yeast samples

Overnight cultures were diluted and cultivated for at least 2-3 duplications to mid-log phase. Based on OD660 measurements, 3 × 107 cells were collected at 3146 g for 4 min, washed in 1 ml of H2O, centrifuged 2 min at 11 000 g at 25°C and stored as pellets at –80°C. Nucleic acids were extracted as previously described by bead breaking phenol chloroform method (47). RNA was degraded for 5 min at 37°C by addition of 30 μl of 1 mg/ml Ribonuclease A (RNaseA) (Carl Roth 7156.3). DNA was precipitated with ammonium acetate and EtOH at 20 000 g and 25°C and the dried pellet dissolved in 100 μl of nuclease-free H2O. DNA concentrations were determined with a NanoPhotometer® (Implen N60-Touch) and all samples were adjusted to a final concentration of 1 ng/μl.

Efficiency-corrected quantitative PCR (qPCR)

2 μl of each DNA sample were mixed with 18 μl of reaction mix containing 0.8 μl of each primer, 6.4 μl of nuclease-free H2O and 10 μl of iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Biorad, 1725124). After initial 30 sec denaturation at 95°C samples were run with 39 cycles of 5 s at 95°C, followed by 25 s at 60°C and signal detection. A melting curve between 65 and 95°C was determined with 0.5°C increments in 5 sec intervals followed by signal readout. Thresholds for determination of the respective CT values were automatically determined with the Biorad CFX 3.1 software. Efficiency-corrected abundance of the analyzed DNA relative to the respective reference gene was calculated from the mean CT-value of at least two technical PCR replicates for each independent biological replicate with the following equation (48). PCR efficiency values (E) were determined from 1:2 dilution series of mixed template DNAs covering the range of the finally detected DNA amounts. Primers and respective E value are listed in Supplementary Table S2. If normalization to a control was performed, DNA abundances of all individual biological replicates were divided by the averaged DNA abundance from all biological replicates of the respective control.

Tetrad dissection analysis

Tetrad dissection analysis was performed to on the one hand generate mutants, where based on the segregation pattern of phenotypes, potential off targets from genetic manipulation could be excluded, and additionally unaltered mtDNA was reintroduced. Furthermore, respiration linked growth defects in haploid mutants could be detected on a single cell level after meiosis of the heterozygous diploid cells. Log phase haploid cells of opposing mating types were mixed in equal amounts, pelleted and mated on plates for at least 2 h at 30°C. Diploids were selected either by selection on medium containing two antibiotics, against which each mating partner encoded a resistance cassette, or by individual picking of morphological characteristic zygotes with the help of a SporePlay+ (Singer Instruments) dissection microscope. When zygotes were picked manually, at least 8–12 zygotes were mixed to avoid potential genetic bottlenecks. Diploid cells were cultivated for 2 days at 30°C on YPD or indicator plates and streaked in low density on sporulation plates at 25°C for at least 5 days. Ascus walls were digested for 10 min at 25°C after gentle suspension in 20 μl of 5 mg/ml Zymolyase (R) 20T (Amsbio 120491-1) in 1.2 M sorbitol, followed by dilution with 180 μl of 1.2 M sorbitol. Spores were separated with the dissection microscope and grown for 72 h at 30°C until colonies were imaged with a Vilber Fusion FX imaging system. Tetrads were further checked for a 2:2 segregation of the mating types Mat a or Mat alpha and antibiotics resistance cassettes that were present in a heterozygous state.

To clearly identify haploid spores with marker-less alleles of CIM1 or ABF2, we predominantly generated diploid heterozygous states with control alleles, in which ABF2 was replaced by a hphNT1 resistance cassette or CIM1 was replaced by a natNT2 resistance cassette. Therefore, we crossed the marker-less mutants with mating type Mat a against one of the mating type Mat alpha strains ySS060 (Δabf2::hphNT1), ySS058 (Δcim1::natNT2) or the double mutant ySS052 (Δabf2::hphNT1 Δcim1::natNT2). In order to prevent the known rapid loss of mtDNA, in strains where the ABF2 locus was modified, we cultivated and mated these strains on either YPG or indicator plates. Only strains with the Δabf2::CIM1 allele, which could not be cultivated in respiratory medium at 30°C under any circumstances, most likely due to a very rapid loss of mtDNA, were crossed with WT ABF2 or Δcim1 mating partners on YPD medium. Mutants relating to the CIM1 locus were cultivated and mated on medium containing glucose. For quantification of colony sizes of the spores after 72 h of growth on indicator plates, images of tetrads were analyzed with a custom-built Fiji/ImageJ (46) macro. Colonies were masked with the thresholding command and auto parameters on the lower and upper intensity limits.

Estradiol regulated protein expression

The previously reported plasmids encoded regulatory elements of an established yeast estradiol expression system FPR718 and FPR792 (49) were combined in the HO-locus integratable plasmid (pCO450) (1). The coding sequence of Abf2 was cloned in the SphI/NheI cloning site of plasmid pCO450 resulting in plasmid pSS001, which after integration enabled estradiol induced expression of ABF2 from the HO-locus on top of WT Abf2 levels. For the modulation of CIM1 or PIM1 expression directly at the endogenous gene locus, the system was modified into a two component system that could be stabily integrated into the genome. The plasmid pSS037 enabled stable integration into the yeast LEU2 locus of the estradiol responsive transcriptional activation element consisting of a fusion of the LexA protein with the estrogen receptor ligand binding domain as well as the B42 transcriptional activator under the control of an ACT1 promoter and CYC1 terminator. Furthermore, plasmid pSS046 encoded a construct of a natNT2 selection cassette followed by a DEG1 insulator, two LexA binding sites and a minimal CYC1 promoter. Flanking Janke S1 as well as S4 primer binding sites (42) enabled standard genomic integration of the construct at the 5’ side before the start codon. To maintain mtDNA integrity during the construction of PIM1 estradiol modulatable strains, β-estradiol (Alfa Aesar L03801) was already added during recovery and selection of transformants. Furthermore the clones were backcrossed against a WT PIM1 background and tetrads were dissected on indicator plates and 30 nM estradiol. In order to prevent loss of mtDNA and secondary effects by Pim1 level alterations, Pim1 estradiol modulatable strains were after backcrossings exclusively maintained as cryo stocks and only prior to experiment cultivated on respiratory medium containing 10 nM estradiol (WT-like Pim1 protein levels in YPG). Cells were inoculated over day in 5 ml YPG medium containing 10 nM estradiol until log phase was reached. Cells were diluted overnight to 0.007 × 107 cells in 20 ml YPG with 10 nM estradiol. The next morning 4 × 107 cells of these log phase cultures were harvested, washed and resuspended in 10 ml of YPD with 20 nM estradiol (WT-like Pim1 protein levels in YPD). Cells were further cultivated in log phase for 2 h 45 min, harvested, washed with YPD without estradiol and finally resuspended in a volume of 1 ml YPD. Strains were either inoculated to 0.05 × 107 cells (controls without modified Pim1 promoter) or 0.07 × 107 (PEstr.-Pim1 strains) in a volume of 20 ml YPD medium containing either no (acute depletion), 20 nM (WT-level) or 100 nM (overexpression) estradiol. Cells were maintained for ∼6 h under these conditions and 7 × 107 cells were finally washed with H2O and pellets were stored for protein analysis at –80°C.

Live-cell microscopy and image processing

Live-cell microscopy was performed from mid-log phase cultures. Cell amounts corresponding to OD600 = 0.4 were resuspended in 300 μl of synthetic medium without a carbon source and attached to Concanavalin A coated 8-well chambered μ-slide coverslips with glass bottom (ibidi 80827: 1.5H (170 μm ± 5 μm) D 263 M Schott glass, sterilized) by 2 min centrifugation at 800 g and 25°C. After two washing steps chambers were filled with 400 μl of sterile filtered and non-autoclaved synthetic medium containing 2% glucose. Images were acquired at 30°C as Z-stacks with 41 slices and steps of 200 nm with a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2-E microscope (Nikon MEA54000) (manual magnification changer tubus lense was set to 1.5x) equipped with a CFI Apochromat TIRF 100XC oil objective with a numerical aperture of 1.49 and a working distance of 0.12 mm (Nikon MRD01991) and a Photometrics Prime 95B 25-mm camera (PH-P95B-25). Details on dichroic and optical filter combinations and respective excitation intensities and exposure times can be made available upon request. For co-localization microscopy with DAPI staining (Figure 4H and Supplementary Figure S4P) the F68-400 F67-401 filterset (AHF Analysetechnik) combination was used for acquisition of DAPI and yeGFP/mGreenLantern emission signals in triggered mode with alternating excitation at 475 and 390 nm for each Z-layer. Matrix mKate2 signals were aquired afterwards with short exposure times as a separate Z-stack with 575 nm excitation and the F48-600, F76-594 combination. ND2 files from the NIS-ELEMENTS AR software (Nikon MQS31000) were converted to OME-TIFF format by custom-built Python 3 or Fiji/ImageJ (46) scripts and deconvolved by Huygens Essential v 18.10 (Scientific Volume Imaging) software (settings available on request). For better visualization in figures, histograms of maximum intensity Z-projected images were slightly adjusted by setting the upper and lower borders of depicted pixel intensities in Fiji/ImageJ (46) and respective color lookup tables were applied for depiction of merged images.

Ratiometric imaging of redox potential and pH

Constructs expressing ratiometric sensors under the control of a TDH3 promoter were stably integrated into the LEU2 locus by transformation of the respective strains with either NotI digested pSS099 (TDH3-Su9-roGFP1) or pSS114 (TDH3-Tim21-pHluorin2). roGFP1 was targeted to the mitochondrial matrix by N-terminal fusion with a Su9 mitochondrial targeting sequence and pHluorin2 was exposed in the IMS by fusing it to the C-terminus of full-length Tim21. Cells were cultivated in mid-log phase and microscopy and image processing was performed as described in the previous paragraph with the only exception that YPG cultivated cells in Supplementary Figure S1J were washed and imaged in synthetic medium containing 3% (v/v) glycerol instead of 2% (w/v) glucose. It was critical that images were acquired in triggered mode within a Z-stack of 8 μm and alternating excitation with either 390/22 or 475/28 nm for the same exposure times with F48-495 beamsplitter (reflection: <495), F47-525 bandpass (transmission: 525/50) filters ((AHF Analysetechnik). For per cell analysis, individual actively budding mother cells of each strain were cropped, without prior consideration of the fluorescent channels, by drawing of outlines in the bright-field image with a custom-built Fiji/ImageJ (46) macro. Further data analysis was performed through custom-built scripts in Python 3. Point centered coordinates of the mitochondrial network were determined by MitoGraph v3.0 (50) analysis of the deconvolved 475 nm excitation channel. On each point of the MitoGraph derived network, a cube of 3x3x3 pixels was centered and all 27 intensities within the cube were averaged. From the averaged cube intensities the ratio between the emissions obtained either after excitation with 395 nm or 475 nm was determined individually for each position on the mitochondrial network. All individual 395/475 nm ratios within the network were then averaged for each cell.

DAPI staining of yeast cells

For live cell staining of yeast cells, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich D8417) was freshly diluted from a 5 mg/ml DMSO stock solution stored at -20°C to a final concentration of 10 μg/ml in sterile-filtered and non-autoclaved synthetic medium. Yeast cells were diluted from an overnight culture and cultivated in YPD medium at 30°C for at least 1–2 doubling times to mid-log phase and cell amounts equivalent to OD600 = 0.4 were harvested for 2 min at 3000 g in 2 ml reaction tubes. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of DAPI solution and rotated over head at 50 rpm (IKA TRAYSTER digital 0004006000) and 30°C for 20 min. Cells were harvested 2 min at 3000 g and immediately prepared for live-cell microscopy imaging.

Analysis of nucleoid morphology of DAPI stained cells

Cells were cultivated in mid-log phase. Staining of nucleoids, microscopy and image processing was performed as described in ‘DAPI staining of yeast cells’ and ‘live-cell microscopy and image processing’. Cells with a projected area between 10 and 22 μm2 were automatically selected from the bright-field images by implementation of the YeastMate (51) plugin in the custom-built Fiji/ImageJ (46) macro. Nucleoid peaks per cell were determined within the individual cell regions of interest (ROIs) by the ‘Find Maxima’ count process with the prominence set to 170. For analysis of nucleoid areas, additional binary masks were generated from maximum intensity Z-projected DAPI images with the thresholding command and the lower and upper intensity limit set to 1100 and 65 535, respectively. The ‘Analyze Particle’ command was further used to determine the area and the mean fluorescent intensity, within Z-projected DAPI images, of each individual nucleoid particle per cells ROIs. The number of nucleoid areas per cell was then extracted by counting of the individual Fiji measurement CSV files with Python 3 scripts.

Quantification of mitochondrial network morphology

For visualization of the mitochondrial network, a construct expressing matrix-targeted mKate2 via N-terminal fusion to a Su9 targeting sequence under the control of a PGK1 promoter was stably integrated into the LEU2 locus of the respective strains by transformation with NotI linearized plasmid pSS036. The additional encoded, on both sites HisG-flanked, URA3 gene from Kluyveromyces lactis enabeling the selectable uracil prototrophy was further removed by a second selection step on 5-FOA containing medium, where only markerless clones, where both HisG sites had recombined, survived. Cells were cultivated in mid-log phase and microscopy and image processing was performed as described in ‘live-cell microscopy and image processing’. For per cell analysis, individual actively budding mother cells of each strain were cropped, prior to inspection of the fluorescent signal, by drawing of outlines in the bright-field image with a custom-built Fiji/ImageJ (46) macro. Further data analysis was performed by custom-built scripts in Python 3. Parameters of the mitochondrial network for each cell were determined by MitoGraph v3.0 (50) analysis.

Determination of mitochondrial and nuclear transcript levels

Total RNA was isolated from 30 OD660 log phase grown cells that were harvested for 5 min at 3146 g and 25°C and the supernatant was removed entirely by short centrifugation. Cells were placed on ice, washed with 1 ml of cold H2O, harvested for 2 min at 11 000 g. Cell pellets were immediately stored at –80°C. Quickly thawed pellets were mixed with 700 μl of 4°C cold TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen 15596026) and ∼0.2 ml of 0.1 mm zirconium/glass-beads (Carl Roth N033.1) on ice and vortexed in a multi-tube vortexer (IKA VORTEX 3 with VG 3.31) at maximum speed for 3x 60 sec at 25°C. Samples were incubated for 5 min on ice and centrifuged for 5 min at 12 000 g and 4°C. The supernatant was mixed by vortexing with 140 μl chloroform, placed on ice and vortexed for another 30 s. Samples were then incubated for 10 min at 25°C and centrifuged for 10 min at 12 000 g and 4°C. The aqueous phase was further cleaned by 30 s vortexing with an equal volume (400 μl) of ROTI®Aqua-P/C/I (Carl Roth X985.1) and 5 min centrifugation at 16 000 g and 4°C. The aqueous phase (∼300 μl) was mixed with an equal amount of isopropanol and incubated for 10 min on ice. RNA was pelleted for 10 min at 12 000 g and 4°C, washed with 1 ml of 75% EtOH at 25°C and pelleted again for 5 min at 16 000 g and 4°C. Pellets were dried and resuspended in 50 μl of nuclease-free H2O. 20 μg of this RNA in a volume of 44 μl were treated with TURBOTM DNase (2 U/μl) (Invitrogen AM2238) by adding of 5 μl of 10x buffer and 1 μl of DNAse and incubating the samples for 30 min at 37°C and 300 rpm agitation. Enzyme and buffer were removed with a Monarch® RNA Cleanup Kit (50 μg) (NEB T2040L) by 1 min 16 000 g centrifugation steps at 25°C and eluted with 30 μl nuclease-free H2O. Due to the absence of polyadenylation in yeast mtDNA transcripts, we reverse transcribed isolated total RNA additionally with specific reverse primers annealing efficiently within coding regions of mitochondrial genes with sufficient GC content and added anchored polyT primers for nuclear encoded references (see Supplementary Figure S5G). Specific reverse primers (see Supplementary Table S2 primers for RNA RT) were mixed to a final equal molarity of 1 μM from each primer. 2.5 volumes of this mixture were combined with 7 volumes the anchored poly T primer Oligo d(T)23 VN (NEB S1327S) and 0.5 volumes of nuclease-free H2O resulting in (0.25 μM of each specific primer and 35 μM of anchored polyT). When only nuclear encoded transcripts were analyzed (see Supplementary Figure S4A) a random primer mix of 35 μM hexamers, 25 μM dT23VN and 1 mM dNTPs (NEB S1330S) was used for reverse transcription. 750 ng of RNA in a volume of 6 μl nuclease-free H2O were mixed with 2 μl of the respective primer mix and denatured for 5 minutes at 65°C and immediately placed on ice. 10 μl of 2× reaction mix and 2 μl of 10× enzyme mix from a ProtoScript® II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (NEB E6560L) were added and cDNA synthesized by 5 min annealing at 25°C, 1 h incubation at 42°C and 5 min inactivation at 80°C. For qPCR analysis cDNA was diluted 1:6 and quantitative PCRs were performed with 2 μl of the sample. Due to the low GC content of mtDNA, the analysis of mitochondrial transcripts was limited to only 8 reliable regions that could be amplified with primer sets that have a PCR efficiency value higher than 1.8.

Cell lysates for immunoblot analysis

For isolation of total yeast proteins, mid-log phase grown 7 × 107 cells were washed with 1 ml of H2O and pellets stored at –80°C. Cell pellets were resuspended in 300 μl of H2O and incubated for 5 min at 25°C after 300 μl of 0.2 M NaOH were added. Cells were harvested at 3000 g and 4°C for 5 min and the pellet was resuspended in 150 μl of 1× Laemmli buffer (60 mM Tris/HCl pH 6.8, 2% w/v SDS, 8% v/v glycerol, 2.5% v/v β-mercaptoethanol, 0.0025% w/v bromophenol blue) and incubated for 5 min at 95°C. Undissolved particles were removed by 1 min centrifugation at 20 000 g before 15 μl of the sample were separated on a Tris–glycine–SDS-PAGE.

Isolation of mitochondria

Yeast cells of mid log phase were harvested at 3993 g for 5 min and washed twice with 50 ml of H2O with a 2 min centrifugation at 3146 g. Cells were incubated for 10 min at 75 rpm agitation and 30°C in 2 ml/g wet weight of alkaline solution (100 mM Tris, 10 mM DTT) and pelleted as in all further steps at 1861 g for 5 min. After washing with 6.7 ml/g wet weight in spheroplast buffer (20 mM KH2PO4 pH 7.4, 1.2 M sorbitol) cells were gently resuspended in the same buffer containing 6/6.6 mg/ml Zymolyase (R) 20T (Amsbio 120491-1) to digest cell walls for 30 min at 30°C and 75 rpm agitation. Cells were then harvested 5 min at 3146 g and 4°C and the buffer was exchanged without resuspension by pelleting cells again after addition of at least 10 ml of ice cold homogenization buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.4, 0.6 M sorbitol, 1 mM EDTA, 0.2% BSA fatty acid free, 1 mM PMSF). Cells were broken with 15 cycles of dounce homogenization (Kimble® 885300-0100, clearance 0.0005–0.0055 in.) in 6.8 ml/g wet weight homogenization buffer. Unbroken cells and nuclei were removed by 5 min centrifugation at 1861 g and 4°C. Mitochondria were collected from the supernatant for 10 min at 17 750 g and 4°C. Mitochondria were gently resuspended in SEM buffer (250 mM sucrose, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM MOPS–KOH pH 7.2) containing 1× cOmplete™, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel) and defined amounts of mitochondria were centrifuged for 10 min at 13 000 g and 4°C. The pellets were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80°C until use.

Swelling and protease accessibility assay for determination of sub-mitochondrial protein localization

Sub-mitochondrial protein localization was determined by hypotonic swelling of isolated mitochondria subsequent proteinase K (PK) treatment to assess protein accessibility as previously described (52). To 160 μg of mitochondria in 20 μl of SEM buffer (250 mM sucrose, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM MOPS–KOH pH 7.2) either 180 μl of SEM or EM buffer (1 mM EDTA, 10 mM MOPS–KOH pH 7.2) and 3 μl of ATP (0.2 M, pH 7.4) were added. To control samples, Triton X-100 was added to a f.c. of 0.5% (5 μl 20%). PK was added to assess protease accessibility to a f.c. of 10 μg/ml (2 μl of 1 mg/ml) and all samples were incubated for 15 min on ice before PMSF was added to a f.c. of 2 mM (2 μl of 0.2 M). Samples were incubated on ice for 5 min. Triton X-100 control samples were precipitated with 80 μl of 72% TCA at –20°C for at least 10 min and pelleted 20 min at 20 000 g and 4°C and washed with acetone. Non-solublized samples were pelleted for 5 min at 18 000 g and 4°C, washed once with 200 μl of SEM buffer containing 2 mM PMSF and pelleted again. All pellets were resuspended in 45 μl of 2× (TCA precipitations) or 1× Laemmli buffer containing 2 mM PMSF and incubated for 10 min at 95°C. Immunoprecipitation of Myc-tagged proteins and their interactors from mitochondrial lysates with and without prior formaldehyde cross-linking

Mitochondria were isolated until the collection step of 10 min at 17 750 g as described above from 2 l OD600 ∼ 0.5 YPD log phase cultures. Cross-linking samples were incubated in a thermomixer at 25°C and 300 rpm for 10 min before the reaction was quenched for 1 min at 25°C by adding a f.c. of 375 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0 from a 2.91 M stock solution. Cross-linked mitochondria were harvested for 5 min at 13 000 g and 4°C and washed again with 1 ml of SEM buffer containing 375 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0. A pooled amount of 10 μl per sample magnetic anti-Myc beads slurry (Thermo Scientific 88842) was washed at least twice with 1 ml pulldown buffer and finally split and magnetically separated for each individual sample. 500 μl of lysate supernatant was added to the beads and rotated over head at 10 rpm and 4°C for 1 h 15 min. The flow-through was removed and beads were washed twice with pulldown buffer. Proteins were eluted in 30 μl of 0.1 M glycine pH 2.0 for 10 min at 25°C which was neutralized with 4.5 μl of 1 M Tris–HCl pH 8.5 after removal from the beads. Eventually, beads and remaining bound proteins were incubated in 46 μl of 1× Laemmli buffer (60 mM Tris/HCl pH 6.8, 2% w/v SDS, 8% v/v glycerol, 2.5% v/v β-mercaptoethanol, 0.0025% w/v bromophenol blue) for 5 min at 95°C. 4× Laemmli buffer was added to all samples to obtain a 1× concentration and samples were incubated for 5 min at 95°C to reverse cross-links. Sucrose gradient fractionation of solubilized mitochondrial proteins

Isolated mitochondria were solubilized and fractionated on a sucrose gradient based on previous reports (Kucej et al., 2008). Mitochondrial pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (0.5% Nonidet P40, 0.5 M sucrose, 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 2 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 7 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1× Roche cOmplete™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) to a final concentration of 2.66 mg/ml and incubated for 5 min on ice. Subsequently, 1.5 ml (4 mg) were overlaid on a 11 step sucrose gradient. The gradient consisted of stepwise applied 1 ml of gradient buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 2 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 7 mM v β-mercaptoethanol, 1× Roche cOmplete™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) with sucrose concentrations ranging from 20 to 65% in 4.5% increments in a 14 × 95 mm centrifuge tube. The sample was centrifuged for 70 min at 110 000 g and 4°C (29 500 rpm with a Beckman SW 40 Ti Swinging Bucket rotor). An input sample diluted 1:5 with 20% sucrose solution was incubated on ice during the entire procedure. In total, 16 fractions of 750-800 μl were collected from top of the gradient. 300 μl of each fraction was immediately frozen at –80°C for later DNA extraction. For protein analysis, 90 μl of each fraction were mixed with 30 μl of 4× Laemmli buffer, incubated for 5 min at 95°C. 25 μl of each sample were separated on a Tris-glycine-SDS-PAGE.

Results

Deletion of CIM1 increases mtDNA copy number

In a previous systematic large-scale screen we identified the largely uncharacterized open reading frame CIM1 as a putative negative regulator of mtDNA CN (1). To confirm the importance of Cim1 for normal mtDNA levels, we generated a markerless CIM1 deletion strain in the W303 background. We determined mtDNA CN by efficiency-corrected quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis, in which abundance of mtDNA loci COX1, COX3 and ATP9 were assessed relative to the nuclear TAF10 locus. This analysis revealed a consistent ∼1.6-fold increase of mtDNA CN in Δcim1 cells compared to WT cells when grown on YPD containing a fermentable carbon source (29 versus 18 mtDNA copies per cell in Δcim1 and WT, respectively) (Figure. 1A and Supplementary Figure S1A+B). Similarly, mtDNA CN in Δcim1 was increased ∼1.5-fold when cells were grown on YPG containing non-fermentable glycerol as a carbon source (YPG). Upon cultivation at 37°C, we observed further increased mtDNA levels in Δcim1 cells (1.9-fold in YPD and 1.8-fold in YPG) (Supplementary Figure S1C). In summary, Δcim1 cells exhibit a robust mtDNA increase that appears to be largely independent metabolic state or growth conditions.

Next, we characterized growth phenotypes associated with deletion of CIM1. Surprisingly, the Δcim1 strain generated a significantly reduced amount of petite cells (cells that have lost respiratory capacity) compared to WT when grown in YPD (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S3H). To further assay, whether this slightly higher amount of respiring cells resulted in growth alterations, we used a backscatter-measurement-based system that allowed continuous assessment of culture densities (45). Overall, only minimal differences were observed in logarithmic or stationary growth phases on fermentable as well as non-fermentable medium (Figure 1C, D, Supplementary Figure S1D, E).

To rigorously assess growth alterations linked to CIM1 deletion, we analyzed competitive growth between WT and the Δcim1 cells by tracking the abundance of both strains in a mixed culture over 72 h. Cultures were inoculated with equivalent cell amounts from each strain and continuously diluted with fresh medium to maintain nutrient supply (Supplementary Figure S1F). During the experiment, we quantified the CIM1 allele abundance in the culture alongside the average mtDNA CN by qPCR. Cultivation at 30°C in respiratory medium revealed a slight growth advantage of WT cells within the population and a decrease of mtDNA CN in the whole population over time (Figure 1E). However, Δcim1 cells exhibited a competitive growth advantage at 37°C, which also resulted in an increase in mtDNA CN in the mixed culture (Figure 1F).

To assay if deletion of CIM1 affects physiological parameters within mitochondria, we genomically integrated genes encoding ratiometric sensors (75,76) into WT, Δcim1 and control strains to monitor the matrix redox potential (Su9-roGFP1) or the pH in the intermembrane space (IMS) (Tim21-pHluorin2). Fluorescent intensities emitted by these sensors upon excitation at a wavelength of 395 nm (oxidized roGFP1, deprotonated pHluorin2 at high pH) or 475 nm (reduced roGFP1, protonated pHluorin2 at low pH) depend on the redox potential or the pH for roGFP and pHluorin, respectively. In control experiments, we observed oxidizing conditions in the matrix of cells lacking a functional respiratory chain due to the deletion of the mtDNA-encoded ATP6 gene (62) or when redox homeostasis was directly affected in Δglr1 and Δgrx2 mutants (77) (Supplementary Figures S1G, H). Moreover, the IMS pH was significantly less acidic in Δatp6 cells compared to WT. By contrast, we observed a less oxidized matrix and, only in YPG, a slightly more acidic IMS in Δcim1 compared to WT (Supplementary Figures S1I, J). Immunoblot and mass spectrometric analyses did not reveal changes in the steady state levels of mtDNA and nuclear encoded components of the respiratory chain of Δcim1 cells (Supplementary Figure S1K, data not shown).

In summary, Δcim1 cells maintain physiological states within mitochondria that are indicative of slightly higher mitochondrial activity when compared to WT in addition to increased mtDNA CN and decreased petite formation. The phenotypes exhibited by Δcim1 cells suggest a function of Cim1 in limiting mtDNA CN, which is unlikely caused by compensatory mechanisms in response to decreased mitochondrial fitness.

Cim1 contains two HMG-boxes similar to the mtDNA packaging factor Abf2

To obtain insight into how Cim1 may affect mtDNA levels, we performed NCBI protein-protein BLAST (64) searches to identify homologous proteins. This analysis returned related proteins exclusively from the yeast family Saccharomycetaceae, all of which are functionally uncharacterized to date. Therefore, we extended our analysis and employed the HHPred tool (78,79), which performs structure prediction in addition to sequence database searching for identification of related proteins. Interestingly, this analysis revealed strong similarities of Cim1 to the yeast mitochondrial HMG-box-containing nucleoid packaging protein Abf2 and its mammalian homolog TFAM. These analyses indicate that Cim1 is not highly conserved on the protein sequence level but exhibits structural attributes indicative of an HMG-box protein. This result is reminiscent of Abf2, which displays clear sequence homology only to closely related yeast species (80), yet maintains functional conservation even in humans. Alignments of Cim1, Abf2 and TFAM homologs from species closely related to the respective host organisms identified strong conservation of two HMG-box motives with invariant or chemically redundant residues (Figure 2A+B and Supplementary Figure S2A, B). Furthermore, structural predictions by alphafold revealed strong similarities between the folds of Cim1’s HMG-boxes A (N27-E102) and B (P110-I258) and the HMG-boxes of Abf2 (Figure 2C, D and Supplementary Figure S2C–F). Notably, instead of helix breaking prolines that are present in Abf2, Cim1 possesses unique non-conserved disordered loop structures that establish the characteristic orientation of the first two α-helices in the HMG-box fold. Of particular interest are highly conserved tryptophan and lysine residues in both HMG-boxes of Cim (HMG-box A: W72, K80; HMG-box B: W218, K227), Abf2 (HMG-box A: W81, K89; HMG-box B: W154, K162) and TFAM (HMG-box A: W88, K96; HMG-box B: W189, K197). Based on the crystal structures of Abf2 and TFAM, both AAs are critical for interaction with DNA and the tryptophans form hydrogen bonds with the DNA backbone (14–16,18–20). The alphafold prediction suggests, that the homologous lysine and tryptophan residues in Cim1 are located at identical structural locations within its HMG-boxes. To determine the importance of these residues for Cim1 or Abf2 function, we replaced these residues by alanines at the endogenous locus. In line with previous studies, deletion of ABF2 resulted in strong growth defects on respiratory medium following prior cultivation on fermentable glucose (Figures 3A and Supplementary Figure S3A). Maintaining Δabf2 cells in a non-fermentable medium prevented loss of mtDNA integrity, resulting in WT-like growth at 30°C. However, growth of these cells at 37°C in YPG medium remains severely compromised (10).

Abf2 alanine mutants of W81, K89, W154 and K162 were strongly growth-impaired at 37°C on respiratory medium. In line, when cells pre-grown on YPG-plates were cultivated for ∼20 h in YPD at 30°C, the petite frequency in the population increased dramatically to almost 100% in deletion and tryptophan mutants, while lysine replacements in K89A and K162A caused less severe phenotypes (Figure 3B). This effect was also reflected in a mild or severe reduction of mtDNA levels, when Abf2 mutants were grown in YPG or YPD media, respectively (Supplementary Figure S3E, F). To address if phenotypes caused by Abf2 point mutations are associated with alterations of Abf2 or Cim1 protein abundance, we generated polyclonal antibodies and performed immunoblots on total cell lysates of YPG grown cells to exclude effects caused by mutation or loss of mtDNA in cells grown on fermentable carbon sources (Figures S3B–D). We did not observe alterations of the hardly detectable Cim1 signals. Abf2 was expressed to WT levels in both HMG-box A mutants and was reduced to only 40% in strains harbouring HMG-box B mutants.

To test whether the homologous residues W72, K80, W218 and K227 in Cim1 are also important for its function, we generated strains expressing alanine replacements of these AAs. In line with the absence of an obvious growth defect of cells lacking CIM1, we did not detect any growth defects associated with the expression of Cim1 mutants (Supplementary Figure S3G). Mutation of any of the residues, however, resulted in increased mtDNA levels, similar to Δcim1 cells (Figure 3C). In contrast, mtDNA CN was unaltered in cells expressing Cim1 forms with replacements of non-conserved lysines within the HMG-boxes (K50A and K212A). Compromised functionality of W72, K80, W218 and K227 Cim1 mutants was also apparent from decreased petite frequencies associated with their expression compared to WT (Supplementary Figure S3H). Expression levels of Cim1 mutants were not altered by >50% and analogous to Abf2 alanine replacements, mutants of HMG-box B were less abundant. Abf2 amounts were unaffected in all Cim1 mutant strains (Supplementary Figures S3I-K).

Our analysis so far reveal opposing phenotypes caused by HMG-box dependent impairment of Abf2 or Cim1 function. To address if locus-dependent genetic regulatory elements involved in protein expression are responsible for these phenotypes, we exchanged the ABF2 coding sequence directly at the endogenous locus with CIM1. Surprisingly, when we replaced ABF2 with CIM1, the growth defect of Δabf2 was exacerbated and cells were incapable to survive on respiratory medium (Figure 3D). To rule out clone-dependent defects due to loss of mtDNA, we reintroduced intact mtDNA from respiring Δabf2 cells by mating and performed tetrad analysis. Remarkably, diploids entirely lacking ABF2 and expressing CIM1 from the ABF2 locus failed to sporulate in tetrad dissection analysis, likely because of mtDNA loss. We therefore dissected tetrads from sporulated respiring diploids harbouring the Δabf2::CIM1 allele alongside WT ABF2 (Figure 3E). While Δabf2 spores produced smaller but respiring colonies, spores expressing CIM1 from the ABF2 locus germinated only as petites in which mtDNA was undetectable by qPCR analysis (Supplementary Figure S3N). Complete loss of mtDNA in Δabf2::CIM1 cells was further supported by failure of 64 individual colonies to restore respiratory growth of strains containing defective mtDNA harbouring deletions of COB, ATP6 or COX2 or the V276 mutation in the COX3 gene in mating complementation analyses. Furthermore, DAPI staining of cells where ABF2 was replaced with CIM1 resulted in exclusive staining of the nucleus in contrast to control cells that exhibited extra-nuclear punctate staining indicative of mtDNA (data not shown). These phenotypes were observed in Δabf2::CIM1 as well as Δcim1 Δabf2::CIM1 strains, demonstrating that Cim1 expression from the ABF2 locus caused mtDNA loss, regardless of additional expression of Cim1 from the endogenous locus. Thus, Cim1 expression is tightly controlled and its expression from the Abf2 promoter results in altered protein levels that adversely affect mtDNA maintenance. Indeed, Western Blot analysis revealed that Cim1 levels were ∼10-fold increased in Δabf2::CIM1 strains (Supplementary Figure S4H, I), confirming that ABF2 promoter and/or UTR elements cause higher expression than those of CIM1.

We further tested whether the two reported isoforms of TFAM that differ in the presence of a linker connecting both HMG-boxes (81), could fulfil Abf2 function. In line with previous replacement studies of Abf2 with full-length TFAM (82) or both mouse TFAM isoforms (83,84), upon exchange at the endogenous locus, both human TFAM isoforms partially restored Abf2 function in growth and tetrad analyses (Figure 3D and Supplementary Figure S3L+M).

In comparison to Abf2, Cim1 contains a C-terminal extension after HMG-box B similar to TFAM (Figure 2A). Previous studies revealed that the addition of the 29 residues C-terminal tail of TFAM to Abf2 was sufficient to convert Abf2 into a transcriptional activator (21). To examine the role of Cim1’s C-terminal extension as a potential determinant for opposing Abf2 and Cim1 functions, we replaced residues 179–183 of Abf2 with residues 240–294 of Cim1. This Abf2 variant remained largely functional with only a mild growth defect at 37°C on YPG and a 0.6-fold reduction in mtDNA CN (Figure 3D, F and Supplementary Figure S3L, M). However, Cim1 function was highly dependent on its C-terminus, as removal of last three lysines or replacement of AAs 240–294 with residues 179–183 from Abf2, resulted in increased mtDNA levels similar to Δcim1 cells.

Thus, Cim1 serves distinctly different roles compared to Abf2 and its function critically relies on conserved HMG-box residues and its C-terminus. Furthermore, heterologous expression of Cim1 under regulation of ABF2 genetic elements results in increased Cim1 levels and is associated with mtDNA maintenance defects, pointing towards tight regulation of Cim1’s endogenous expression.

CIM1 encodes a low abundant mitochondrial matrix protein that associates with mtDNA

To assess the processes that regulate and determine expression levels of Cim1, we first analyzed transcript abundance of CIM1 and ABF2. In line with our locus exchange experiments, ABF2 mRNA levels were eight-fold higher than those of CIM1 (Supplementary Figure S4A). To directly compare Cim1 and Abf2 protein amounts with Aco1 as a control, we generated strains expressing C-terminally 9xMyc tagged variants of these proteins from their endogenous locus to facilitate detection with the same antibody (Supplementary Figures S4B–F). Among 17 different C-terminal and further six internally tagged Cim1 variants only Cim1-9xMyc was detectable by immunoblot and remained partially functional judging from no increase in mtDNA levels. Cim1-9xMyc was markedly less abundant than Abf2-9xMyc and Aco1-9xMyc in fermentative and non-fermentative medium. Interestingly, Cim1-9xMyc levels were 1.5-fold increased, whereas Abf2-9xMyc and Aco1-9xMyc levels were decreased in fermentative compared to non-fermentative medium. We confirmed this observation in three different WT strains including galactose and lactate grown cells with a specific antibody against Cim1, that only detects the untagged C-terminal protein epitope (Supplementary Figure S4G). To quantitatively compare the protein levels of an untagged Cim1 version and Abf2, we generated an Abf2 variant where residues 179–183 had been replaced with the AAs 240–294 of Cim1 (Abf2+Cim1-C-term), the epitope recognized by the Cim1 antibody, (Supplementary Figure S4H+I). WT Cim1 was ∼80-fold less abundant than Abf2+Cim1-C-term as revealed by immunodetection of both proteins with the Cim1 antibody. However, immunodetection of Abf2+Cim1-C-term with an Abf2-specific antibody revealed that Abf2+Cim1-C-term was stabilized ∼3-fold compared to WT Abf2. Taken together, we estimate that Cim1 is ∼25-fold less abundant than Abf2 in cells grown on a fermentable carbon source. Thus, distinct protein levels of Cim1 and Abf2 appear to result from differences in mRNA abundance as well as regulation at the level of translation or protein stability.

Cim1 has been previously identified in highly purified mitochondria and was shown to be imported in a membrane potential-dependent manner into isolated mitochondria (52). To determine the submitochondrial localization of Cim1-9xMyc, we assessed its protease accessibility in intact mitochondria or mitochondria whose outer membrane had been disrupted by hypotonic swelling (Figures 4A and Supplementary Figure S4J). The outer mitochondrial membrane protein Tom70 was degraded upon treatment of intact mitochondria with Proteinase K (PK). The integral inner membrane proteins with domains in the IMS, Yme1 and Tim23, were only accessible to PK after hypotonic disruption of mitochondria. By contrast, Cim1 was protected from PK under both conditions, similar to the matrix and IM associated protein Tim44 and the soluble matrix proteins Abf2 and Pim1. As Cim1 was readily degraded after detergent-mediated solubilization of mitochondria, these results support a localization of Cim1 in the mitochondrial matrix.

We confirmed the mitochondria-targeting property of Cim1’s N-terminus by constitutively expressing fusion proteins consisting of the first 30 or 40 AAs of Cim1 and yeGFP with a PGK1 promoter at the endogenous CIM1 locus (Figure 4B). Microscopic analysis revealed that the first 30 AAs of Cim1 were sufficient to target the fusion protein into the mitochondrial matrix. When the fusion construct included the first 40 AAs of Cim1, yeGFP targeting to the mitochondrial matrix was further improved.

In agreement with the absence of a predicted transmembrane domain, Cim1 was released into the soluble fraction upon sodium carbonate treatment similar to the mitochondrial matrix proteins Abf2, Pim1 and Mam33, while the integral membrane proteins Tim23 and Yme1 remained predominantly in the pellet fraction (Figures 4C and Supplementary Figure S4K). In contrast, when mitochondria were sonicated in pH-neutral buffer, Cim1 as well as Abf2 fractionated predominantly to the mitochondrial membrane containing pellet fraction. Interestingly, the fractionation pattern of Cim1 and Abf2 correlated well with mtDNA, which similarly was released into the soluble fraction upon sodium carbonate treatment but remained membrane-bound in pH-neutral buffer.

To analyze the association between Cim1-9xMyc and mtDNA in further detail, we sub-fractionated NP40 lysed mitochondria by ultracentrifugation through a highly resolving sucrose step gradient ranging from 20 to 65%. Analysis of mitochondria isolated from yeast cells grown in YPD medium revealed two distinct peaks when mtDNA was quantified using qPCR (Figure 4D). A less abundant peak was observed in fraction 3 (∼20% sucrose), while the majority of mtDNA was concentrated in fraction 10 (∼42.5% sucrose). We observed a strong correlation between the fractionation patterns of mtDNA and those of the mtDNA packaging factor Abf2, the RNA polymerase Rpo41 and, to a minor extent but consistent with the close coordination between mtDNA and its expression machinery (85), the ribosomal Mrpl36 protein (Figure 4F). In contrast, the proteins Yme1, Atp2 or Cob, with no known mtDNA association, were exclusively observed in even lighter fractions compared to the low density mtDNA peak, resulting in low correlation scores. Most interestingly, we observed a high correlation between the fractionation patterns of Cim1-9xMyc and mtDNA, strongly supporting the conclusion that Cim1-9xMyc binds to mtDNA. The fractionation pattern drastically changed when mitochondria isolated from YPG grown cells were analyzed. The majority of mtDNA as well as Abf2 and Rpo41 were observed at lower densities (∼29% sucrose), whereas amounts in denser fractions (∼42.5% sucrose) were lower (Figure 4E, G). Cim1 peaked slightly with the high density fraction of mtDNA and migrated more gradual in lower density fractions similar to patterns of the OXPHOS components Cob and Cox2 as well as Mrpl36, which resulted in a low correlation score when compared with mtDNA.

To examine association of Cim1 and Abf2 with mtDNA on a cellular level we assayed the co-localization of predominantly functionally tagged Cim1-yeGFP and Abf2-mGreenLantern (Supplementary Figure S4L–O) with DAPI stained nucleoids by live-cell microscopy. Since the low abundance of Cim1-yeGFP expressed from its endogenous promoter prevented detection of signals by microscopy, we replaced the CIM1 promoter with an estradiol-inducible promoter and induced Cim1-yeGFP expression by estradiol addition for 4 h. In cells that displayed moderate Cim1 expression, we observed a spotty appearance of Cim1-yeGFP within the mKate2-labeled mitochondrial matrix. These Cim1 spots partially overlapped with DAPI labeled nucleoids (Figure 4H and Supplementary Figure S4P). In contrast, Abf2-mGreenLantern co-localized with all and the entire area of DAPI labeled nucleoids. Interestingly, we could not detect Cim1-yeGFP within mitochondria of ρ0 or ρ- cells upon estradiol induction, indicating that import, folding or stability of Cim1-yeGFP is compromised in the absence of intact mtDNA (Supplementary Figure S4Q–U). In contrast, Abf2-mGreenLantern could still be detected in ρ0 and, to a lesser extend in ρ- cells (Supplementary Figure S4V–Z). In line with its role in mtDNA binding, Abf2-mGreenLantern was distributed more evenly throughout the mitochondrial network in ρ0 cells.

Altogether, we find that Cim1 is targeted to the mitochondrial matrix and co-fractionates with mtDNA. In contrast to Abf2, which appears to associate with mtDNA regardless of metabolic conditions and covers all parts of the mtDNA, Cim1 only partially co-localizes to mtDNA and co-fractionation with mtDNA is strongly reduced under respiratory growth. These findings suggest that Cim1 may play a respiration-dependent role in the regulation of nucleoid subpopulations.

Loss of CIM1 can partially rescue mtDNA defects in cells lacking ABF2

While absence of Abf2 can cause loss of mtDNA, absence of Cim1 leads to increased mtDNA CN. We therefore asked what effects the simultaneous loss of both proteins would trigger. Δabf2 and Δcim1 strains were mated and subsequently subjected to sporulation and tetrad dissection. Surprisingly, colony sizes of Δabf2 spores were restored in Δabf2Δcim1 cells to sizes comparable to WT and Δcim1 (Supplementary Figure S5B, E). Moreover, Δabf2 associated respiratory growth defects at 37°C, increased petite frequencies and reduced mtDNA CN were rescued in Δabf2Δcim1 double mutants with mtDNA CN even exceeding WT levels (Figure 5A–D and Supplementary Figure S5A). We confirmed this observations by tetrad dissections where instead of a deletion, point mutations in conserved HMG-box residues were present in Cim1 (Supplementary Figure S5C). Moreover, we used tetrad dissection to demonstrate that variants of Cim1 lacking residues 2–30, containing the Abf2 C-terminus or replacements by TFAM isoforms or Abf2 did not restore Cim1 function. Reciprocally, all functionally impaired ABF2 HMG-box mutants and partially functional TFAM replacements were rescued by CIM1 deletion (Supplementary Figure S5D).

We further analyzed mitochondrial morphology in Δabf2 and Δcim1 single as well as Δabf2Δcim1 double mutants expressing matrix-targeted mKate2 by live cell microscopy (Figures 5E–G and Supplementary Figure S5F). When cells were grown for ∼20 h in YPD, the mitochondrial morphology of Δabf2 cells was dramatically altered. The length of the mitochondrial network was reduced and the average number of mitochondrial ends and segments, but not the branch points, were increased, indicative of fragmentation and decreased mitochondrial biogenesis. While mitochondrial morphology of Δcim1 cells was unaltered compared to WT, the Δabf2Δcim1 double mutant resembled the compromised phenotype of Δabf2 cells. Thus, deletion of CIM1 can rescue mtDNA maintenance defects, but not mitochondrial morphology defects associated with Δabf2 cells.

To assess morphology and numbers of mitochondrial nucleoids in WT, Δabf2, Δcim1 and Δabf2Δcim1 strains, we stained mtDNA with DAPI (Figure 5H). In line with our qPCR results (Figure 5C), microscopic analysis revealed that the number of nucleoid peaks was increased in Δcim1 cells and decreased in Δabf2 cells (Figure 5I). Furthermore, reduced nucleoid numbers of Δabf2 cells were partially restored in Δabf2Δcim1 double mutants. Interestingly, when we thresholded the signals and quantified the areas of separable nucleoid structures, we detected overall less nucleoid areas per cell (Figure 5J), which, however, were enlarged in size in Δcim1 cells (Figures 5K, L). As the mean DAPI intensity of each nucleoid was not significantly changed in Δcim1 cells compared to WT (Figures 5M+N) we conclude that the 1.6-fold increased amount of mtDNA in Δcim1 cells is reflected in enlarged nucleoid areas containing multiple mtDNA copies. By contrast, in Δabf2 cells less nucleoids were present that were also smaller in size but showed higher DAPI intensities. In Δabf2Δcim1 cells, nucleoid areas were increased compared to Δabf2, the mean intensity of nucleoids, however, was similarly increased in Δabf2Δcim1 and Δabf2 compared to WT and Δcim1. We interpret these findings to reflect a higher accessibility of nucleoids to DAPI in cells lacking Abf2. Thus, reduced nucleoid numbers and sizes in Δabf2 cells are partially restored when Cim1 is additionally absent. We currently can not explain, why deletion of CIM1 partially rescues mtDNA phenotypes but not mitochondrial morphology defects present in Δabf2 cells. We speculate that these differential effects reflect additional roles of Abf2 that go beyond regulation of mtDNA copy number and packaging, which may involve reported interactions with Mic60 (86) or Act1 (87) required for mitochondrial architecture or distribution, respectively.

TFAM has been shown to contribute in mtDNA transcription initiation (22–29). Therefore, we assessed a possible Cim1 involvement in mtDNA transcription through examining mtRNA levels by RT-qPCR in Δabf2, Δcim1 and Δabf2Δcim1 in comparison to WT (Supplementary Figure S5G). While COX1 transcripts were reduced in Δabf2 cells even in respiratory medium, Δcim1 cells exhibited a remarkable increase in mtDNA transcripts with the exception of ATP9. This increase of transcripts appears not to be necessarily a consequence of more mtDNA itself, as transcript levels in Δmrx6 cells, with a reported 1.5-fold increase in mtDNA CN (1), were barely altered. Furthermore, CIM1 absence clearly restored mtDNA transcript levels in Δabf2 cells and even caused an increase of COX3 and the 21SrRNA compared to WT, while transcript levels in Δabf2Δmrx6 cells were only partially restored.

Overall, deletion of CIM1 or ABF2 cause opposing alterations in maintenance of mtDNA CN, nucleoid morphology and mtDNA transcript levels. Remarkably, those alterations are balanced out when Abf2 and Cim1 are simultaneously absent, suggesting that both proteins have antagonistic functions.

Balanced levels of Cim1 and Abf2 are required for mtDNA maintenance

Since Cim1 levels are low abundant and absence of Cim1 results in increased mtDNA levels, we examined the effects of Cim1 overexpression. To this end, we replaced the CIM1 promoter with an estradiol inducible promoter and overexpressed Cim1 ten-fold by addition of estradiol (Supplementary Figure S6A). We then monitored growth in respiratory medium by backscatter analysis. After 3 h of estradiol induction, we observed a drastic drop in the growth rate, which slightly recovered but remained decreased for the remaining duration of the experiment (Figure 6A). More drastic effects were observed when Cim1 levels were increased ∼30-fold by replacing the endogenous CIM1 promoter with a constitutively active PGK1 promoter (Figure 6B and Supplementary Figure S6A). Petite frequencies rose to >80% and we observed a strong accumulation of the mitochondrial precursors of matrix-targeted Abf2 and Mdj1, indicative of decreased mitochondrial import capabilities and mitochondrial function (Figure 6C). In line with our previous analyses, overexpression of HMG-box independent mutations (K50A and K212A) largely phenocopied overexpression of WT Cim1 indicating functionality of these variants. In contrast, despite comparable overexpression levels of all mutant Cim1 variants, HMG-box mutants (W72A, K80A and W218A) did show decreased petite frequencies comparable to Δcim1 and no precursor accumulation. The results imply that the overexpression phenotypes are not merely an outcome of non-physiological protein levels. K227A was still partially functional with 32% petites and intermediate strong accumulation of precursor.

Given the strong opposing functions of Abf2 and Cim1, we asked whether the strong overexpression phenotype of Cim1 could be ameliorated by additionally increasing Abf2 levels. We inserted a second copy of ABF2 under the control of an estradiol inducible promoter into the HO-locus and induced expression for ∼24 h with 50 nM estradiol. Consequently, levels of mature Abf2 increased 3-fold in WT and 1.7-fold in PPGK1-CIM1 cells and were unchanged in the absence of estradiol (Figure 6F and Supplementary Figure S6D). Indeed, while increased Cim1 protein levels remained unaffected by the additional presence of Abf2 in PPGK1-CIM1 cells, petite frequencies were reduced from 81 to 50%, mtDNA CN was increased from 6 to 12 copies per cell and amounts of accumulated Mdj1 and Abf2 precursors were reduced by 30% or 45%, respectively, upon overexpression of Abf2 (Figure 6D–F and Supplementary Figure S6B–D). In line with previous work, the 3-fold increase of Abf2 in WT mildly raised mtDNA levels (34).

Thus, increased levels of Cim1 negatively affect mtDNA maintenance and cellular growth, especially on respiratory medium. These drastic overexpression effects critically depend on intact HMG-boxes of Cim1. Cim1 overexpression phenotypes can be alleviated by increased Abf2 levels, suggesting that balanced Abf2 and Cim1 levels are required to maintain a proper equilibrium of their antagonistic functions on mtDNA.

Cim1 abundance is regulated by the conserved LON protease Pim1

Given the cellular requirement for tightly controlled Abf2 and Cim1 amounts to maintain mtDNA CN and respiratory growth, we asked how levels of Abf2 and Cim1 are regulated. We focused on the conserved matrix LON protease Pim1, which has been reported to degrade Abf2 in vitro (39). Similarly, the mammalian Pim1 homolog LONP1 has been shown to be involved in TFAM degradation (38). We generated strains expressing Pim1 from an estradiol inducible promoter at its endogenous locus to modulate Pim1 expression upon addition or withdrawal of estradiol. Interestingly, in line with reports in mammals (38) and contradictory to findings in Drosophila (37), we did not observe alterations of Abf2 levels in our in vivo system upon acute up- or downregulation of Pim1 (Figure 7A, B). By contrast, we observed a strong accumulation of Cim1 or Cim1-9xMyc when Pim1 had been acutely depleted, suggesting that Pim1 is involved in Cim1 degradation. In agreement with this idea, we found that Pim1 interacted with Cim1-9xMyc in immunoprecipitation experiments (Supplementary Figures S7H–K). This interaction was only clearly observed upon cross-linking, suggesting that the interaction of Cim1-9xMyc and Pim1 is transient in nature. In contrast, we did not detect an interaction between Abf2-9xMyc and Pim1, regardless of cross-linking.

Phosphorylation of S55 in TFAM and S48 of Abf2 has been reported to prevent interaction of both proteins with mtDNA and promote their degradation through Pim1/LONP1 (38,40). We thus asked whether the stability of Cim1 could also be influenced by phosphorylation of the respective homologous position T32 or a further possible phosphorylation site S62. Phosphoablative (T32A and S62A) or phosphomimentic (T32D and S62D) Cim1 variants rescued Δabf2 cells in tetrad analyses and could be stably overexpressed without the drastic increase in petite frequencies associated with increased WT Cim1 levels, indicating that alanine and aspartic acid replacement variants are equally non-functional (Supplementary Figures S7F, G). Thus, we can not assess differences between phosphoablative or phosphomimetic states of residues T32 or S62 for Cim1 function. As we did not observe acute alterations of WT Abf2 upon modulation of Pim1 expression in our in vivo system, we asked whether phosphorylation of Abf2 would cause Pim1-dependent degradation and generated the respective S48A and S48D/S48E mutants. While phosphoablative Abf2 showed WT-like growth, phosphomimetic Abf2 exhibited a strong growth defect on respiratory medium with high petite frequencies, when cultivated for ∼20 h in a fermentable carbon source (Figure 7C and Supplementary Figure S7A, B). Protein levels of phosphomimetic mutants grown in respiratory medium at 30°C were reduced to 70% of WT levels without alterations of Cim1 amounts (Figure 7D and Supplementary Figure S7C–E). In agreement with a Pim1-mediated degradation of phosphomimetic Abf2, we observed an accumulation of Abf2 S48D and S48E when Pim1 was acutely depleted and a strong decrease when Pim1 levels were increased (Figure 7E).

We further assessed the impact of acute level-modulation of Pim1 on mtDNA CN. In previous work (1), we observed an increase of mtDNA levels after 12 h of acute Pim1 downregulation, rather than a decrease, which might be expected as a consequence of Cim1 accumulation. Here, we find that mtDNA levels remain largely unchanged in all strains upon six hour downregulation of Pim1 (Figures 7A, B, E). This result suggests that Pim1 serves multiple roles in regulating mtDNA levels, which might conceal the negative effect of Cim1 accumulation on mtDNA levels. To demonstrate, that acute Cim1 accumulation can indeed cause a decrease in mtDNA levels, we overexpressed Cim1 with the estradiol-system and measured mtDNA content 6 h after induction by qPCR. Strikingly, mtDNA levels dropped from ∼22 to ∼9 copies per cell (Supplementary Figure S7L). In contrast, acute overexpression of functionally compromised Cim1W72A or Cim1K80A mutants resulted in no or weaker reduction of mtDNA levels, respectively. Additionally, acute overexpression of Cim1, Cim1W72A or Cim1K80A did not result in alterations of the protein levels of Pim1, Tim50, Abf2, Rpo41, Mrx6 or Mam33 supporting its specific effect in negatively regulating mtDNA CN (Supplementary Figures S7M–O).

Taken together, we propose that Pim1 could balance antagonistic functions of Abf2 and Cim1 in vivo by determining their levels. Cim1 seems to be highly prone to degradation via Pim1 and induction of its accumulation results in a decrease of mtDNA levels. By contrast, degradation of Abf2 appears to be affected by the presence of a negative charge on S48, while steady state levels of the WT form are unaffected by acutely altered Pim1 levels.

Discussion

Here, we identify Cim1 as a novel player in regulation mtDNA CN. Our findings that Cim1 (i) is present in the mitochondrial matrix, (ii) co-fractionates and co-localizes with mtDNA and (iii) requires both of its HMG-boxes to fulfil its function, strongly suggest that Cim1 acts at mtDNA to impart its role on mtDNA CN regulation. Cim1’s domain organization is similar to the well-characterized mitochondrial HMG-box containing proteins Abf2 and its homolog TFAM, but its function in mitochondria appears antagonistic. While absence of Abf2 results in strong defects in mtDNA maintenance, deletion of Cim1 causes the opposite phenotype, and leads to increased mtDNA levels and a higher percentage of respiratory competent cells. The antagonistic relationship between Cim1 and Abf2 is further supported by our findings that mtDNA maintenance defects in Δabf2 can be rescued by additional CIM1 deletion and that defects associated with Cim1 overexpression can be partially restored by a simultaneous mild increase of Abf2 levels. We propose a model in which Abf2 to Cim1 ratios determine mtDNA abundance. High Abf2 to Cim1 ratios favour increased mtDNA CN and respiratory capability (Figure 7F). On the other hand, a decreased Abf2 to Cim1 ratio reduces mtDNA CN. A critical player determining the Abf2 to Cim1 ratios appears to be the conserved LON protease Pim1. While absence of Pim1 affects Abf2 levels only mildly unless a negative charge is present on S48, Cim1 levels increase dramatically upon downregulation of Pim1. Consistent with the notion of Cim1 accumulation exerting a negative impact on mtDNA CN, the deletion of Pim1 leads to mtDNA loss (88,89). Notably, however, Pim1’s acute depletion does not lead to an immediate decrease of mtDNA levels. We interpret this result to reflect Pim1’s diverse roles in the degradation of multiple targets that are involved in mtDNA biology. In particular, we have previously found that the protein Mrx6 binds to Pim1 and have proposed that Mrx6 serves as a substrate specificity factor that targets an unknown replication for Pim1-mediated degradation (1). Consequential accumulation of such a replication factor in the absence of Pim1 could overwrite the negative effect of Cim1 accumulation on mtDNA levels on a short timescale. Exploring how Pim1 coordinates its diverse functions in regulation of mtDNA CN poses an intriguing question.

It has been proposed that strong compaction of mtDNA by TFAM renders mtDNA inactive for transcription and replication (17,33,35,36). Interestingly, mild increases of Abf2 levels have been reported to promote mtDNA abundance while more than ten-fold overexpression has led to rapid loss of mtDNA (34).

How could Cim1 exert its negative effects on mtDNA CN? We find that expression levels of Cim1 are ∼25 times lower compared to Abf2. We therefore consider it unlikely, that Cim1 exerts its function by globally affecting mtDNA packaging. Moreover, our microscopic analysis shows that mildly overexpressed Cim1 only partially co-localizes with mtDNA, whereas Abf2 overlaps extensively with nucleoid structures. These findings rather support a model in which Cim1 function could be limited to specific mtDNA loci such as origins of replication, potentially competing with antagonistically acting Abf2. HMG-box containing proteins have been proposed to participate in transcriptional regulation in the nucleus either through inducing structural changes in DNA, recruitment of transcriptional repressors or activators to DNA or a combination of both (90,91). Notably, it was recently found that the nuclear HMG-box containing repressor Rox1 translocates to mitochondria upon insufficient precursor processing to promote mitochondrial transcription and translation (92). Cim1 in contrast could act to repress mtDNA replication, which has been proposed to be initiated by either RNA-priming (93–96) or recombination (97–105) in S. cerevisiae. It will be interesting to determine the specificity of Cim1-DNA binding in future studies. Indeed sequence specific binding of HMG-box containing proteins with repressive roles in transcription has been previously suggested (20,106,107). Moreover, also sequence specificity (20,23,108,109) has been demonstrated for TFAM with suggested roles in transcriptional activation (23,28) as well as repression (25).

The discovery of Cim1 as a regulator of mtDNA CN in S. cerevisiae highlights the unexpected complexity of this process, which apparently involves two antagonistically acting HMG-box proteins. Our results demonstrate that the LON protease, Pim1, plays a critical role in maintaining low levels of Cim1, thus preventing toxic effects on the cell. Moving forward, it will be fascinating to investigate the mechanisms that govern Cim1 degradation and mtDNA CN regulation in response to different cues. Notably, previous studies have revealed that active and inactive nucleoids can coexist within the same cell (36), suggesting that mtDNA regulation may be influenced not only on a per cell level, but also in spatially defined mitochondrial subdomains. Finally, while TFAM is the only known mammalian HMG-box protein involved in mtDNA regulation, it remains an open question whether mammalian counterparts of Cim1 exist that negatively regulate mtDNA expression in a similar fashion.
