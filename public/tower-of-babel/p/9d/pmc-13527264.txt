Quantifying Skin Sodium Concentration With Novel Very Rapid Biexponential T2 Relaxometry and Signal Loss Compensation Methodology
Authors: Gurparkash Singh, Christian Beaulieu, Rob Stobbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo accurately measure skin tissue sodium concentration (TSC) through development of spin‐3/2 relaxometry methodology that also considers blood sodium.Methods M xy generation during RF excitation is substantially reduced by very rapid T2f. Compensation for TSC measurement requires knowledge of both T2f and B1, but the effects of these two parameters are correlated together with a blood sodium contribution that does not exhibit very rapid T2f. The new spin‐3/2 relaxometry methodology uses 7 scans with 90° RF pulse durations ranging from 70 to 900 μs (constant TE = 2 ms), 2 scans with 60° and 120° flip‐angles, and 2 scans with TE = 6 and 10 ms to disentangle and simultaneously fit skin T2f, T2s, relative B1 and blood sodium fraction. Simulated M xy from the fitted parameters was used for image compensation to attain skin sodium concentration. A gellan gum phantom with known sodium concentration was used for validation. Calf skin of 20 healthy volunteers was scanned with a surface‐coil at 4.7T, measuring T2f, T2s, blood sodium fraction and TSC.ResultsThe new methodology measured skin T2f = 0.10 ± 0.03 ms, T2s = 13.7 ± 1.5 ms, blood sodium fraction = 0.08 ± 0.05, and ultimately TSC = 90 ± 8 mM, which was not correlated with age or sex. However, blood sodium fraction in this model positively correlated with age.ConclusionThis study yields the first skin TSC measurements consistent with atomic absorption spectroscopy. The potentially large impact of blood sodium in 23Na MRI of skin, which may also reflect other fluid‐like space, is a particular concern if single images are used for concentration measurement.Keywords: blood, relaxation, skin, sodium concentration, sodium MRI, TSC

Introduction

There is growing desire to quantify skin tissue sodium concentration (TSC), with previous studies measuring elevated 23Na MRI signal in disorders such as hypertension [1], type‐2 diabetes [2, 3], chronic kidney disease [4, 5], and multiple sclerosis [6]. However, reported skin TSC values of ∼20 mM are much lower than the expected ∼85 mM (derived from 77 ± 16 mmol/kg absorption spectroscopy measurement [7] and a skin density of 1.1 kg/L [8]) or ∼80 mM from inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry [9]. No study has measured the expected skin TSC with in vivo 23Na MRI. While sensitive surface‐coil imaging [10] can reduce the TSC underestimation and skin thickness dependence [11] associated with the large voxels (3 × 3 × 30 = 270 mm3) of the widely adopted volume‐coil based protocol of Kopp et al. [7], a remaining problem for skin TSC quantification is rapid biexponential relaxation. Previous work has measured T2f = 0.5 ms at 7T [10] and T2f = 0.4 ms at 4.7T [12], but all of the skin sodium studies listed above employed gradient‐echo acquisition with TE ∼1–2 ms. To address this, our recent study used twisted projection imaging (TPI) with TE = 0.11 ms and surface‐coil imaging at 4.7T with 0.4 × 4 × 4 = 6.4 mm3 voxels, but measured TSC ∼34 mM in the calf skin of healthy volunteers [11]. Simulation identified underestimation from the very rapid T2f point‐spread‐function (PSF) smearing of short‐TE radial acquisition, and M xy loss from non‐infinitesimally short RF excitation. Eliminating PSF smearing requires a very short readout. This in turn requires very large gradients (e.g., 90 mT/m for 1 mm resolution and 0.5 ms readout) and suffers from SNR inefficiency. Here, long TE = 2 ms acquisition is purposefully selected avoid the rapid T2f signal component and the PSF smearing issue. In this context, TSC quantification requires individual relaxation measurement for compensation of M xy loss both during and following the RF pulse. The first of the two previously published T2f studies listed above used standard multiple‐TE relaxometry [10]. However multiple‐TE relaxometry yields varying PSFs for biexponential relaxation and is problematic for thin structures like skin when T2f is very short [12]. The second study at 4.7T was largely concerned with the detection and measurement of the residual quadrupole splitting found in cartilage and investigated a B1 mapping double‐angle based experiment [12]. This second study also used a volume knee‐coil with voxels = 2.7 × 2.7 × 10.6 mm3 considerably larger than the thickness of skin. Thus, neither study approach is adequate to measure the very rapid T2f of skin for the purpose of signal loss compensation.

The purpose of this work is enable skin TSC measurement through introduction of a new spin‐3/2 specific relaxometry technique that relies on RF pulses of varying duration at a constant TE. A gellan gum phantom was chosen to validate the proposed methodology, with previously published T2f = 0.54 ms at 4.7T [13] that approximately matches skin. Monte Carlo simulation was performed to assess measurement capability in the context of noise. The signal contribution from sodium in the blood was also included in the new relaxometry method, as blood sodium exhibits much longer transverse decay. Skin T2f, T2s, relative B1, and the fraction of total sodium within the blood, or the blood sodium fraction, were determined from multi‐image regression and used to compensate signal values and attain skin TSC measurements in 20 healthy volunteers.

Methods

New Skin 23Na Relaxation and Concentration Quantification Methodology

M xy generation is independent of RF pulse duration τRF when τRF ≪ T2f. In this case, the value of M xy at TE can be calculated from the biexponential decay of Equation (1). Note that in a homogeneous environment the fast and slow fractions are F f = 0.6 and F s = 0.4 from spin‐3/2 physics [14].

Practical excitation requires finite RF pulse duration, but Equation (1) remains approximately valid, with TE measured from the center of the RF pulse, when τRF ≤ T2f and TE ≤ T2f. This is the case for many sodium MRI applications including brain with T2f > 2 ms [15]. However, this is not the case for very rapidly decaying spin‐3/2 nuclei.

In this paper, the skin tissue sodium that is not in the blood is labeled “skin sodium,” and is assumed to effectively reside in one homogeneous extracellular environment. The basis for this assumption is given in Section 4. For the skin sodium relaxation of T2f (60%) = 0.1 ms and T2s (40%) = 13 ms reported in Section 3, simulation using the tensor operators of Hancu et al. [16] yields relative M xy = 0.29 at TE = 2 ms for a short τRF = 70 μs RF pulse (Figure 1A), but M xy = 0.1 at TE = 2 ms for a longer τRF = 900 μs RF pulse (Figure 1B). Both values are smaller than the M xy = 0.51 calculated from Equation (1) at TE = 2 ms. Across this τRF = 70–900 μs range, large differences in the M xy dependence on τRF can be seen for simulated T2f values ranging from 0.05 to 0.4 ms (Figure 1C). Thus, multiple images were acquired between τRF = 70–900 μs and the M xy dependence used to enable very short T2f regression. However, if the excitation flip‐angle differs from 90°, M xy dependence on τRF will change (Figure 1D) and regression will yield inaccurate T2f values if a 90° flip‐angle is assumed in the fitting. Relative B1 (rB1), which describes the experimental flip‐angle difference from that prescribed, must be known. To measure rB1, double‐angle (60°, 120°) images are also included in the regression method, but rB1 values measured with double‐angle imaging depend on T2f as previously described [11]. For this reason, the new rapid biexponential relaxation measurement technique must fit both T2f and rB1 simultaneously. Finally, it should be noted that because this method includes a constant, relatively long TE = 2 ms, the PSF smearing is the same for each image acquired and depends only on T2s*.

Measurement dependence on RF pulse duration suggests that off‐resonance may be a confounding factor. For this reason the impact of off‐resonance is addressed in Figure 1E. Interestingly, simulation suggests that large off‐resonance results in greater M xy at TE = 2 ms for long RF pulses. This is a consequence of altered T31 generation during the RF pulse (not shown). However, of importance to this methodology is the very small and inconsequential impact on M xy across the range of RF pulse durations used when off‐resonance is within 50 Hz.

The T2f of blood sodium is an order of magnitude longer than that of skin sodium, and thus blood sodium does not exhibit τRF dependent signal loss at constant TE. Simulation yields the same relative M xy = 0.78 at TE = 2 ms for τRF = 70 μs (Figure 1A) and τRF = 900 μs (Figure 1B) as can be calculated from Equation (1) at TE = 2 ms. Figure 1F demonstrates the case where blood sodium constitutes 8% of the total TSC. Because blood sodium's contribution to total M xy is independent of RF pulse duration, it substantially changes the shape of total tissue M xy variation with τRF. This shape change enables blood sodium fraction regression within the new methodology, where “blood sodium fraction” is the fraction of the total tissue sodium that is within blood. Note that because skin sodium has much greater relaxation‐related M xy loss than blood sodium, the proportional blood sodium contribution to total M xy is much greater than its 8% contribution to tissue concentration. In this paper a blood sodium relaxation of T2f (20%) = 2 ms and T2s (80%) = 16 ms is used from the literature [17]. The 20% T2f fraction describes transverse relaxation composed of a spin‐3/2 biexponential component (Ff/Fs=0.6/0.4) and a monoexponential component (Fs=1.0), in a ratio of 33%/67%. These two components may reflect hematocrit and plasma. Note that blood sodium relaxation is addressed in Section 4.

Relative simulated M xy (as a fraction on M0) at TE defines the tissue sodium signal loss in each image for skin T2f, T2s (technically T2s* but listed in this paper as T2s), rB1, and blood sodium fraction. Thus, measured signal multiplication by (1/M xy) facilitates the ultimate goal of signal loss compensation. While the new relaxometry method described above enables T2f, rB1, and blood sodium fraction regression, additional sequences with TE = 6, 10 ms were also acquired to enable T2s measurement. T2f, T2s, rB1 and blood sodium fraction regression from the scans of Table 1 was performed with dictionary fitting, as all four parameters are slightly to moderately correlated (as will be shown) and must be fit simultaneously. The dictionary fitting approach is rapid and avoids local minimums. A dictionary was created by simulating relative Mxy at TE for each sequence (labeled Mxy(seq)) over a range of T2f (0.02–0.8 ms, 72 steps), T2s (5–30 ms, 51 steps), rB1 (0.83–1.3, 48 steps), and blood sodium fraction (0–0.25, 25 steps). These ranges were chosen such that fitting did not approach the minimum or maximum values of the dictionary. Experimental signal values from each sequence were divided by the mean across all sequences and best fit to similarly normalized dictionary values to attain T2f, T2s, rB1, and blood sodium fraction (see Figure 2 for a comprehensive flow diagram). The (non‐normalized) dictionary values associated with the best fitting parameters are labeled Fitted_Mxy(seq) and identify the signal reduction from M xy = 1 in each sequence. To compensate the acquired data, the experimental signal from each sequence was multiplied by the associated 1/Fitted_Mxy(seq) value. Experimental values were also multiplied by 1/rB1 for receive B1 correction. This correction assumes that transmit and receive rB1 are the same for the transmit/receive coils used, an assumption based on the relatively low Larmor frequency of 23Na (53 MHz at 4.7T) with gyromagnetic ratio ∼¼ that of 1H. Note that Figure 5A,B of reference [18] shows approximately equal transmit and receive B1 from 64 MHz simulation on the edge of a conductive object adjacent to the center of a surface coil, where skin signal would be measured. Finally, corrected values were averaged across all sequences.

ROI values (both skin tissue and agar) were normalized by the mean and fit to simulated M xy dictionaries similarly normalized by the means across sequences. Fitting yields skin T2f, T2s, rB1 and blood sodium fraction. The simulated M xy values of each sequence associated with the best fitting parameters, labeled Fitted_Mxy(seq), are used to compensate the acquired data from each sequence. Data values are also compensated by 1/rB1 to account for receive B1 variation. Compensated values are averaged, and the tissue/agar ratio used to calculate TSC.

Individual participant values from the methodology of Figure 2 are shown as a function of age for males and females. Mean ± standard deviation values across all 20 volunteers are (A) T2f = 0.10 ± 0.03 ms, (B) T2s = 13.7 ± 1.5 ms, (C) TSC = 90 ± 8 mM, (D) blood sodium fraction = 0.08 ± 0.05, (E) blood volume fraction = 0.08 ± 0.05, and (F) skin sodium concentration (SSC) = 90 ± 8 mM. T2f, T2s, TSC and SCC are not correlated with age or sex. However, blood sodium fraction and blood volume fraction are correlate with age with slopes of 0.002 per year (R 2 = 0.29, p = 0.014), and 0.0023 per year (R 2 = 0.35, p = 0.006), respectively.

To complete the measurement of sodium concentration, a calibration phantom must be placed within the field of view. Saline was used for the validation experiment with gellan gum and 5% agar was used for human skin experiment, for reasons described below. To use the calibration phantom as a sodium concentration reference, its measured signal intensities must also be corrected by 1/Fitted_Mxy(seq) and 1/rB1. This was accomplished in the same manner described above, but in this case the saline (T2f = T2s = 53 ms) and agar (T2f = 4 ms, T2s = 22 ms) relaxation values were premeasured using standard multi‐TE relaxometry. A “phantom” dictionary was then created using premeasured T2f and T2s for a range of rB1 values. Signal fitting was performed as described above to yield rB1 and Fitted_Mxy(seq). Note that premeasuring T2f and T2s was necessary because the much slower T2f = 4 ms, T2f = 53 ms of agar and saline (respectively) have very minimal impact on Mxy generation during the RF pulse, and thus T2f cannot be determined in the manner described above for skin. Finally, TSC was calculated from the tissue/calibration‐phantom corrected signal ratio and multiplication by the concentration of the calibration phantom (Figure 2). Note that this TSC value includes all sodium in both skin and blood.

Using a blood sodium concentration of 87 mM [19], a blood volume fraction can be calculated for each individual according to Equation (2).

Finally, the Skin Sodium Concentration (SSC), or the sodium concentration of skin tissue that is not blood, can then be calculated from Equation (3).

Imaging

All images were acquired on a Varian Inova (previously of Palo Alto, CA, USA) 4.7T whole body scanner using TPI with trajectories of 12.5 ms duration, chosen to approximately match T2s (or T2s*) of skin. For the phantom imaging experiment with the knee volume‐coil described below, 1500 trajectories fully sampled k‐space for an FoV = 160 mm3 and 1.3 × 4.0 × 4.0 mm3 voxels (sampling dwell = 34.75 μs, readout points = 345, anti‐aliasing bandwidth = 9000 Hz, maximum gradient magnitude = 10.1 mT/m). For both the thin‐sliced gellan phantom and human skin imaging experiments with a 5 cm diameter transmit‐receive surface coil, 1500 trajectories fully sampled k‐space for a FoV = 80 mm3 and 0.4 × 5.0 × 5.0 = 10 mm3 voxels (sampling dwell = 15.3 μs, readout points = 784, anti‐aliasing bandwidth = 20 000 Hz, maximum gradient magnitude = 35.3 mT/m). For both imaging protocols the gradient slew‐rate was set to 155 mT/m/ms. Sampling density compensation to a β = 2 Kaiser‐Bessel k‐space filter shape used an iterative approach [20] and images were reconstructed to an isotropic zero‐filled matrix of 320 (isotropic 0.25 mm zero‐filled voxels) with standard convolution gridding.

Phantom Validation

A validation phantom was constructed from 200 mM saline and 1% gellan gum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) within a ∼10 cm diameter and 6 cm tall container. The same 200 mM saline solution was also used as a calibration phantom for this test and was poured into a petri dish and placed on top of the gellan gum phantom in the scanner. The heating required to melt the 1% gellan gum was much less than typically required for agar, and minimal water boil‐off (or volume loss) was observed. It was also confirmed that gellan gum itself does not contain sodium by scanning another gellan gum phantom made with pure water (there was no measurable MRI signal). The first validation experiment used a knee volume coil to produce “uniform B1” images that clearly depict τRF dependent signal loss. The larger 200 mM sodium concentration was chosen for this experiment to offset the SNR inefficiency of the volume coil. A second study was performed by slicing the gellan gum ∼1 mm thick with a vegetable slicer to simulate skin and scanning with a 5 cm diameter surface‐coil as described below for skin. Regression and sodium concentration measurement consisted of the scans listed in Table 1. Note that the very short τRF = 70 and 85 μs RF pulses were not possible for volume‐coil imaging due to RF power constraint. TR = 250 ms was used to avoid T1 weighting in saline. Each gellan gum experiment was performed on two different days with different phantom and coil placements.

Human Skin Measurement

Twenty healthy individuals (aged 23–63 years; 11 females) were recruited for this study which was approved by the University of Alberta's Health Research Ethics Board, and written informed consent was received prior to imaging. The imaging and ROI methodology was the same as Zhu [11]. Left calf skin was pressed flat ∼0.5 cm above a 5 cm diameter surface‐coil, while a petri dish phantom with a 66 mM [Na] 5% agar phantom was placed ∼0.5 cm below the coil. The agar sodium concentration was determined from imaging with a saline phantom of known concentration. Agar was chosen for its short T1 ∼ 25 ms that enabled TR = 120 ms while avoiding T1‐weighting. Note that preliminary inversion recovery experiments also measured T1 < 25 ms in skin at 4.7T (data not shown). 1500 TPI trajectories yielded a 3 min scan time, or 33 min total for 11 scans. The pressed flat calf skin enabled thin (two zero‐filled voxels) 0.5 × ∼35 × ∼35 mm3 angled‐box ROIs to be centered within the skin. ROI measurements from the individual scans of Table 1 yielded apparent TSC. These values were compared between scans and tested for correlation with age and sex. The same ROI values were then applied to the methodology of Figure 2 to attain T2f, T2s, blood sodium fraction, blood volume fraction, TSC and SSC. These parameters were also tested for correlation with age and sex. One volunteer was scanned on 5 different days to assess quantification repeatability. Off‐resonance was measured from scans 3 and 10 of Table 1.

Regression Assessment and Monte Carlo Simulation

To evaluate parameter and TSC fitting, the correlation matrix was calculated from the Jacobian at the mean T2f, T2s, rB1, and blood sodium fraction values measured across the 20 volunteers. In addition, simulated signal values from the mean study parameters (for both skin and agar) were combined with simulated random noise to achieve SNR values ranging from 30 to 80 (in increments of 5). A total of 10 000 fittings of noisy simulated data were performed for each SNR value in the same manner performed for experiment, and both fitting error and standard deviation were recorded. Finally, simulated data 50 Hz off‐resonance was tested to determine the impact of the maximum off‐resonance measured on fitted values.

Results

Phantom Validation

While saline signal at TE = 2 ms remains constant over RF pulse duration increase from τRF = 100 to 900 μs, gellan gum signal decreases from 18.7 to 11.8 in arbitrary signal units (Figure 3A–E). Fitting all nine scans according to Figure 2 yields T2f = 0.26 ms, T2s = 7.8 ms and rB1 = 0.91 in gellan gum, as well as rB1 = 0.93 in saline. Note that similar rB1 values are expected in the gellan gum and saline ROIs for this volume‐coil test. Fitted_Mxy(Seq) = [0.307, 0.283, 0.256, 0.227, 0.193, 0.184, 0.110, 0.264, 0.254], and correcting each signal value by 1/Fitted_Mxy(Seq) and 1/rB1 yields values of 66.6 ± 0.4 across all nine scans (in arbitrary signal units). Similarly correcting saline yields values of 67.0 ± 0.3. A gellan gum sodium concentration of 197 mM can then be calculated from these signal means, validating the methodology of Figure 2, as a concentration of 200 mM matching saline is expected from phantom construction. This study was repeated with altered coil and gellan gum phantom placement, yielding T2f = 0.26 ms, T2s = 7.6 ms, and sodium concentration = 195 mM. Surface‐coil experiment regression yielded T2f = [0.22, 0.22] ms, T2s= [7.3, 7.3] ms, and sodium concentration = [208, 212] mM, where the values in brackets are given for two separate tests performed at different times. Note that the slicing and handling of the gellan gum phantom resulted in slight water loss. This, combined with evaporation (the phantom was sliced ∼1 week after it was made and tested with the volume coil above), may have elevated the sodium concentration relative to the expected 200 mM.

Human Skin Measurement

A representative skin sodium image of the human calf from a healthy 26‐year‐old male is shown in Figure 4A for τRF = 70 μs. 87% ± 7% (mean ± fitted standard deviation across 20 volunteers) of ROI voxels were within 20 Hz, and 99% ± 2% of voxels were within 40 Hz. Average image SNR in the skin was 11 ± 1. ROI signal averaging increased the measurement SNR to 55 ± 7 (calculation described in [21]). Sodium concentration measurement using only the τRF = 70 μs image and rB1 correction yielded an apparant value of 41 ± 4 mM over all 20 participants. Each RF pulse duration increase yielded significant (p < 0.05) apparent sodium concentration measurement decrease, even though echo‐time remained a constant TE = 2 ms (Figure 4B). Apparent sodium concentration measurements from individual scans correlated with age for all τRF durations from 70 to 900 μs (p < 0.005). Three examples are shown in Figure 4C–E.

Using all 11 scans and the methodology of Figure 2 yielded T2f = 0.10 ± 0.03 ms, T2s = 13.7 ± 1.5 ms, blood sodium fraction = 0.08 ± 0.05, blood volume fraction = 0.08 ± 0.05, TSC = 90 ± 8 mM, and ultimately SSC = 90 ± 8 mM (Figure 5). Repeated (5×) scans of a 48‐year‐old male yielded T2f = 0.10 ± 0.03 ms, T2s = 13.7 ± 1.9 ms, blood sodium fraction = 0.19 ± 0.03, blood volume = 0.19 ± 0.04, TSC = 88 ± 4 mM and SSC = 88 ± 5 mM (individual data not shown). TSC and SSC were not correlated with age or sex, nor were T2f or T2s. However, blood sodium and blood volume fractions positively correlated with age with slopes of 0.002 per year (R 2 = 0.29, p = 0.014), and 0.0023 per year (R 2 = 0.35, p = 0.006), respectively (Figure 5D,E).

Regression Assessment and Monte Carlo Simulation

The correlation matrix calculated from the Jacobian at the mean T2f, T2s, rB1, and blood sodium fraction values listed above is given in Table 2, showing moderate negative correlation between T2f and blood sodium fraction, as well as a moderate positive correlation between T2f and rB1. Monte Carlo investigation of simulated noisy data identified small noise‐related fitting errors for T2f, T2s, blood sodium fraction, and rB1 at the study measurement SNR of 55, ultimately culminating in positive TSC and SSC measurement errors of ∼2% (Figure 6). At a measurement SNR of 55, simulated noise resulted in TSC and SSC standard deviations of 5% and 6%, respectively. Simulating data at 50 Hz off‐resonance yielded only 0.6% fitting error for both TSC and SSC.

Discussion

The mean TSC and SSC values of 90 ± 8 mM measured across 20 healthy volunteers with mean age of 35 years are ∼4× the previously reported TSC measurements of ∼20 mM from single volume coil images acquired using conventional GRE imaging with TE ∼2 ms and 3 × 3 × 30 mm3 voxels [1, 2, 3, 4, 7], ∼2× the TSC measurement from single surface coil images acquired using conventional GRE imaging with TE = 2.3 ms and 0.9 × 0.9 × 30 mm3 voxels [10], ∼2.5× the TSC measurements from single surface coil images acquired using TPI with TE = 0.1 ms and 0.4 × 4 × 4 mm3 voxels [11], and are in accordance with 85 mM from atomic absorption spectroscopy = 77 mmol/kg [7], and a skin density of 1.1 kg/L [8]. Measured skin T2f = 0.10 ms is substantially shorter than previously published T2f = 0.5 ms at 7T from multi‐echo imaging [10] and T2f = 0.4 ms at 4.7T from double‐angle based volume‐coil experiment of the knee [12]. Gellan gum phantom experiment validates the relaxation measurement and compensation methodology of Figure 2, while Monte Carlo simulation identifies measurement capability for each parameter.

This is the first study to consider sodium in blood when measuring either skin sodium relaxation or concentration. A literature value T2f (20%) = 2 ms and T2s (80%) = 16 ms [17] was used to describe blood, but this value is from 7T and altered relaxation (particularly T2s, or really T2s*) is expected for other field strengths. Differences in the T2f/T2s ratio might also be expected for different hematocrit values. However, as long as τRF ≤ T2f and TE ≤ T2f, a long relaxing 23Na pool will yield an approximately constant signal value at a constant TE, independent of the RF pulse duration. The blood relaxation used in this paper yields M xy = [0.779, 0.780] for τRF = [70, 900 μs] as shown in Figure 1A,B. Simulating saline at 4.7T, or T2f = T2s = 53 ms, yields M xy = [0.964, 0.966], while fully biexponentially relaxing T2f (60%) = 2 ms and T2s (40%) = 16 ms yields M xy = [0.572, 0.562] for the same τRF = [70, 900 μs]. In these cases, relaxation changes the magnitude of the constant across τRF, but not the “shape” of the constant. The regression methodology fits a constant together with signal decay over τRF to measure the T2f of skin (Figure 1F). It is the value of T2f which is of primary importance in compensating signal to attain TSC, as T2f strongly weights acquired signal lower (Figure 1A,B). If this constant is removed from the fitting (i.e., a zero blood sodium fraction is forced), a 50% longer T2f = 0.15 ± 0.08 ms is attained along with a much larger TSC = 106 ± 29 mM that correlates with age (R 2 = 0.3, p = 0.013). However, when blood sodium is included, variation in the prescribed (long) relaxation value has very small impact on the T2f fitting and TSC. Instead, the associated blood sodium magnitude of constant M xy at TE will directly alter the blood sodium fraction. If more rapid transverse decay is prescribed for blood such that its constant M xy value is lower, a greater blood sodium fraction is required to attain the M xy value of the constant in the fitting. Fitted values for a range of prescribed relaxation values for blood are given in Table 3, including variation in the T2f/T2s ratio and T2s. Although the prescribed blood relaxation value also alters the fitted T2s values, the impact of T2s on M xy at TE = 2 ms is small, and thus the resultant impact on TSC is also small. However, future blood relaxation remeasurement will aid the absolute measurement of blood sodium fraction. Note that the T2f (20%) = 2 ms and T2s (80%) = 16 ms used in this study yielded a blood volume fraction = 8% ± 5% in the range of that measured from optical imaging (0.2%–7%) [22] and (8%–13%) [23].

A value of 87 mM was used to calculate SSC from Equations (2) and (3) however, hematocrit variation from 0.37 to 0.52 [24] results in blood sodium concentrations ranging from 76 to 95 mM for intracellular [Na+] ∼18 mM [25] and plasma [Na+] ∼140 mM [24]. If plasma [Na+] ranges from 135 to 145 mM, this blood sodium concentration range increases to 74–98 mM. Using 74 and 98 mM within Equations (2) and (3) yields mean blood volume fractions of 9.9% and 7.7% and mean SSC values of 91.4 and 88.9 mM, respectively, or a blood sodium related SSC uncertainty of ∼±1% (from extreme to extreme). However, this uncertainty is small compared to the noise related uncertainty (±6% standard deviation) at the measurement SNR = 55 (Figure 6F). Including a uniform random distribution of blood sodium values from 74 to 98 mM within the Monte Carlo simulation increases the uncertainty standard deviation in an individual measurement from ±6% (just noise) to ±6.1% (noise plus blood sodium variation), as might be expected from error propagation.

When just one of the first seven scans of Table 1 is used for sodium concentration analysis, the apparent sodium concentration measured is greatly underestimated (∼20–40 mM) and is correlated with age (Figure 4C–E). This matches previous single‐image 23Na MRI studies that have measured positive skin TSC correlation with age [1, 3, 4, 5, 10]. However, the multi‐scan regression methodology of Figure 2 does not yield TSC or SSC correlation with age, or even a trend approaching correlation (Figure 5C,F). Instead, the methodology of this paper yielded blood sodium and volume fraction correlation with age (Figure 5D,E). This is an important distinction if one is concerned with measuring sodium ions associated with glycosaminoglycans of the skin extracellular matrix. Simulation using the mean parameters from this study suggests that for constant TSC, 23Na MRI signal intensity should depend on blood sodium fraction with greater relative variation for long RF pulses, as depicted by the heavy blue lines in Figure 7. Measured signal intensity correlation with fitted blood sodium fraction closely matches simulation for both the τRF = 70 μs and τRF = 900 μs scans (Figure 7). The combination of 23Na signal intensity correlation with fitted blood sodium fraction and the correlation of fitted blood sodium fraction with age (Figure 5D) suggests that previous TSC correlations with age may actually reflect blood sodium or volume fraction dependence and not TSC changes. Recent optical coherence tomography study of skin vasculature (to a dermis depth of 300 μm) measured a micro‐vessel (diameter < 80 μm) volume density decrease with age, but a thicker vessel (> 160 μm) volume density increase with age, for a total vessel volume density decrease with age [23]. This is in disagreement with the results of our study. However, the 300 μm depth of this optical coherence study yields a focus on the smaller vessels of the papillary plexus [26, 27]. It may be that the larger, deeper vessels of the reticular or subcutaneous plexus elongate with greater tortuosity and become grossly dilated with age, as described for the “horizontal skin vessels” in reference [28]. Finally, it should be noted that the weight of the leg on the surface coil likely results in some blood outflow, as happens when a blanching pressure is applied to the skin to measure capillary refill time [29], This outflow may alter blood sodium and volume fraction values compared to the case without an applied pressure.

Skin in the adult lower leg is composed of a thin ∼0.1 mm epidermis [30, 31] and a much thicker dermis, yielding total thickness of ∼1.5 mm [32, 33]. While the epidermis contains densely arranged keratinocytes [34] with cellular volume fraction of 67% [35], the dermis is primarily an extracellular matrix with cellular volume accounting for only 0.3% of tissue [35]. Combining these values, the cellular volume is less than 5% of the total skin volume. An intracellular sodium concentration of 12 mM [36] yields an intracellular sodium contribution to skin tissue of only 0.6 mmole/L tissue, identifying that skin sodium primarily resides in the extracellular matrix. Note that highly transient interactions are expected between Na + ions and glycosaminoglycans [37, 38]. Even for DNA with more specific binding sites, residence times are < 1 ns [39]. Given the high diffusivity of the Na + ion, rapid exchange between the glycosaminoglycan interface and “free water” is expected such that over the relevant MRI time‐course, this microenvironment can be considered homogeneous with respect to 23Na spin‐dynamics. However, it is possible that two distinct pools of 23Na nuclei may exist within the extracellular dermal space [9]. If this is true, the blood sodium fraction may also reflect 23Na nuclei within a much slower relaxing extracellular dermal pool.

It is also possible that a distribution of homogeneous microenvironments exists within the skin. To investigate the impact, simulated data derived from a Gaussian weighed sum of distributed T2f values was fit to the uniform T2f model of this paper. TSC was −1.5% and −6% (lower) in error for simulated data with T2f standard deviations of 25% and 50%, respectively. Broader, or asymmetric distributions could produce substantially larger errors. Thus, if skin possesses large microenvironment heterogeneity, potentially resulting from pathology, the uniform T2f model fitting of this paper will yield lower than actual SSC values. The fitted blood sodium fraction was 8% and 30% (greater) in error for simulated data with T2f standard deviations of 25% and 50%, respectively. If skin microenvironment heterogeneity increases with age, some of the blood sodium fraction correlation with age (200% increase over 50 years in Figure 5D) could be explained by microenvironment heterogeneity. Note that possible blood sodium relaxation change with age may also influence the blood sodium fraction correlation in Figure 5D.

The quantitative skin TSC method presented purposefully implements a long TE = 2 ms to avoid acquisition of the very rapidly decaying T2f around the center of k‐space and the associated PSF smearing. Imaging simulation was performed as in Zhu [11] using T2f = 0.1 ms, T2s = 13.7 ms and TE = 2 ms. ROI intensity values of [0.81, 0.96, 0.98, 0.99, 0.98, 0.98] were attained for [0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2.0] mm thick simulated skin, where a value of 1.0 is expected. For very thin skin < 1.0 mm, TSC measurements will be weighted lower by PSF smearing. 23Na signal that smears into skin from adipose tissue (∼10 mM has been measured [40]), arterioles and venules of the lower horizontal plexus [41], or potentially even muscle if the adipose layer is very thin, is expected to possess T2f > 1 ms and thus contribute to the blood sodium fraction within the fitting. It should also be noted that while B1 reciprocity is expected from simulation, as described in the Section 2, reciprocity is not explicitly known, and presents a limitation of surface coil use, particularly at higher field strengths. Future work should investigate tissue sodium and blood volume fraction with chemical analysis from excised tissue following MRI experiment to confirm measured values. Finally, the total scanning duration for this initial investigation is long at 33 min. However, this can be shortened. A very long TR = 120 ms was chosen to rigorously avoid T1 weighting. This could be substantially reduced, given skin T1 < 25 ms from preliminary experiment. Sequence parameter optimization using the relaxation knowledge of this paper will also shorten scan time to make quantitative skin sodium measurement clinically feasible.
