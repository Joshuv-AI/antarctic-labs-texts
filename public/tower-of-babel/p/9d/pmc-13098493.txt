Are gynecologists prepared to offer anal cancer prevention services?: a qualitative study of barriers to implementation in the United States
Authors: Laura M Gaydos, Morgan NV Buchanan, Christine Conageski, Gaëlle Sabben, Patricia Jeudin, Lisa Flowers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTraining in anal cancer prevention (ACP) techniques is crucial to respond to increasing anal cancer rates in women. We explored what is necessary for gynecologists, specialists in lower genital tract and experts in HPV-related disease prevention, to incorporate ACP into routine practice.MethodsIn 2022, we interviewed thirteen gynecologists practicing across three states (Florida, Georgia, Maryland) about current prevention practices, provider perceptions, and support needed to incorporate ACP into practice. We employed thematic content analysis to understand issues of implementation and categorized these themes following the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research.ResultsKey themes included a need to demonstrate the importance of ACP to gynecologists; key barriers to ACP implementation focused on the lack of clinical guidelines and resulting uncertainty about value of these tests, need for additional training, already limited time with patients, logistical concerns related to laboratory capacity and insurance/billing and current reliance on referrals to other providers for ACP.ConclusionBuilding on gynecologists' existing training to enhance their ability to conduct ACP and extending training to other providers is important to increase early intervention and reduce rates of anal cancer among women; pairing this with efforts to address systems-based barriers can facilitate widespread ACP adoption.Keywords: Anus, Neoplasms, Human papillomaviruses, Gynecology, Early Cancer detection, Digital anal rectal exam

Introduction

Anal cancer is an HPV-related disease with rising rates in the U.S. (Siegel et al., 2020). In 2024, there were over 10,000 new cases, with women accounting for almost 70% of those. The lifetime risk of being diagnosed with anal cancer is 1 in 500 (American Cancer Society, 2024) however, incidence varies by prior anogenital disease. Among HIV-negative women with a history of vulvar, vaginal, or cervical disease, the prevalence of anal high-risk HPV ranges from 4% to 86%, approaching levels observed in women living with HIV (Stier et al., 2015). Anal cancer incidence is highest among women with prior vulvar cancer (48 per 100,000 person-years), while women with a history of cervical cancer have lower initial incidence (9 per 100,000 person-years) that increases with time since diagnosis (Clifford et al., 2021; Damgacioglu et al., 2025). Published data from the novel Anal Cancer/HSIL Outcomes (ANCHOR) study among people living with HIV has shown that for anal cancer prevention, there is a significant benefit to treating individuals presenting with high-grade intraepithelial lesions (Palefsky et al., 2022). The ANCHOR trial was modeled on the established paradigm of cervical cancer screening and treatment of cervical precancers, which has demonstrated reduced cervical cancer incidence in both HIV-negative and HIV-positive women. Leveraging the shared natural history of persistent HPV infection at the cervix and anus, ANCHOR evaluated whether a similar screening-and-treatment approach in people living with HIV would reduce anal cancer risk and demonstrated that detection and treatment of anal HSIL significantly decreased the incidence of anal cancer. More recently, since the study was completed, the International Anal Neoplasia Society (IANS) developed the first set of guidelines for anal cancer screening (Stier et al., 2024) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) followed with additional guidelines (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). The combination of new data and recent guidelines create the need to train providers in anal cancer prevention (ACP) techniques such as high-resolution anoscopy (HRA), as well as digital anal rectal exams (DARE), in an environment where systematic screening is not yet consistent practice (Plotzker et al., 2022). Gynecologists, who specialize in the care of the female reproductive system, are trained in the treatment of genital HPV-related diseases and should be targeted for training, as their patient population is at significant risk for anal cancer. Yet, previous studies found that anal cancer screening techniques were not commonly practiced by gynecologists. In one study, only 21% of gynecologist respondents reported currently performing anal cytology and 32% performing DARE (Gaydos et al., 2023). Other studies have demonstrated low numbers of gynecologists screening for ACP (Russell et al., 2025; Gore et al., 2025). Further, gynecologists overwhelmingly note a need for further training with more than one-half (53%) in one study stating they would be very likely to participate in ACP training (Gaydos et al., 2023). Given this positive viewpoint of ACP and the expressed need to increase gynecologic provider awareness of and training on ACP, we sought to better understand the barriers to incorporating ACP into standard practice among gynecologists.

Methods

Study design

In 2022, prior to publication of the IANS guidelines, we invited practicing gynecologists to respond to an online survey (see Supplementary Materials) addressing knowledge, practice, and barriers related to ACP. The survey was completed by 196 survey respondents, recruited through academic institutions, professional societies, and professional groups on social media. Respondents included members of the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, the GA OBGYN Society, gynecologists from academic institutions in Georgia and members of three private Facebook groups for gynecologists practicing in three different states (Florida, Georgia, Maryland) (Gaydos et al., 2023). Data from these 196 survey respondents are presented in a separate manuscript (Gaydos et al., 2023). Willingness to participate in in-depth interviews was assessed as part of the survey, enabling easy identification of the thirteen interview candidates whose perspectives are presented herein. All 13 survey participants who expressed interest in participating in the in-depth interviews were contacted. This study was approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board and exempted from further IRB review.

Interview guide development

The research team was comprised of a mix of practicing gynecologists (LF, CC, PJ), medical students (one resident (DB) and one student (MB)) and a social scientist with expertise in qualitative methods (LG). Following survey distribution, the team developed a semi-structured interview guide composed of open-ended questions to assess physicians' attitudes, knowledge, and perceptions of ACP and its implementation in gynecology practice. The interview guide was structured around key domains from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR): innovation, individual, inner and outer setting. CFIR is a framework that provides a structure for understanding and analyzing contextual factors related to implementation (Damschroder et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1, the innovation domain, which refers to the “thing” being implemented—ACP services—considers constructs like the innovation's source, evidence base, adaptability and complexity. The individual domain refers to roles and characteristics of individuals. Given that all respondents were practicing gynecologists (role), we focused on characteristics such as need, capability, opportunity and motivation. Inner setting refers to the specific setting- the clinics- in which the ACP innovation is being implemented. The outer setting domain refers to the setting in which the clinic inner setting exists (e.g., the hospital system, city, state) (Fig. 1). The interview guide was designed to explore contextual factors at each level of implementation to better understand barriers and facilitators to ACP implementation. Notably, CFIR also uses an implementation domain; however, because our study was conducted prior to guideline creation, this domain was not used to create our interview guide.

Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research constructs based on Damschroder et al. (2022) reflecting the framework applied to our thematic analysis of anal cancer prevention perspectives from interviews with practicing gynecologists in the United States.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by a medical resident who had substantive knowledge of ACP but did not yet have experience with real-world barriers or facilitators in the clinic setting. Prior to conducting interviews, the interviewers were trained in qualitative interview techniques by a qualitative research expert. We conducted webinar technology-based semi-structured, in-depth interviews, following the CFIR-guided interview guide described above. Participants completed a recorded verbal consent process. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The medical resident, who conducted the interviews, reviewed transcripts for accuracy. Interviews were conducted until saturation was reached as determined by the research team.

Data analysis

Analysis of interviewee demographics was conducted using SAS™ software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., 2023) and descriptive statistics generated. Qualitative data analysis was conducted jointly by all research team members. Using a content analysis framework, we developed both inductive and deductive categories and major themes for data analysis (Krippendorf, 2004). Data analysis included an iterative cycle of data collection, coding, and triangulation with previously collected survey data (Gaydos et al., 2023). Initially, an open coding approach was utilized, coding the data from the interviews separately and giving them equal consideration. Two team members coded each transcript. When coding themes diverged, differences were noted and discussed among the research team and a consensus-based approach used to make final decisions (Braun and Terry, 2014). Illustrative examples of each theme were identified and select quotes are included in this paper. All coding was completed using MAXQDA™ 2022 software (VERBI Software, 2022). During data analysis, team members met biweekly to discuss emerging themes, solve discrepancies, and determine when saturation was met. We generated descriptive statistics to report the cohort's professional characteristics.

Results

Thirteen gynecologists participated in in-depth interviews, of which the majority identified as generalist OB/GYNs (n = 10) in urban settings (n = 8) who primarily served patients identifying as cisgender women (n = 11) and transgender men (n = 9). All participants reported engagement in patient care, most commonly at a university-affiliated hospital (n = 10), and serving patients across sexualities and insurance statuses. Services rendered spanned well-person visits (n = 6), labor and delivery (n = 3), hospital gynecology (n = 3), and family planning (n = 3), with 5 participants not identifying specific services they provided and some indicating multiple. Key theme 1: there is a need to demonstrate the importance of ACP to gynecologists

Key among our findings is that most respondents did not fully accept the premise that ACP is an important procedure for gynecologists to perform. Noting the lack of clinical guidelines (at the time of the study) from professional organizations such as the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), respondents made it clear that they needed a strong rationale for and evidence supporting ACP before incorporating it into their practices. One respondent noted:

I mean, I think the base barrier's that right now is that there's no major guidelines on who to screen and when and how and what, how frequently, and what to do, and how to treat a little bit right. It's still a very new field.

Other respondents focused on the need to educate gynecologists about the value of ACP, noting that it is not about inability to perform these services, but needing to understand their value. As one participant stated:

I don't think it's hard.... they do hysterectomies. They do advanced surgery; they can also learn to do this. It's just a matter of folks determining is it important enough for them to learn to do it.

and

…I think there would be a lot of hesitation, scrutiny, questioning. Just with any change, I mean, even the ASCCP [American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology] guidelines to do pap testing every five years or three years. I mean, even that is not adopted by providers. They're still papping yearly… But I think if we saw the raw data on number needed to treat and how prevalent anal cancer really is, because I think we just don't see it.

Key theme 2: ACP training is needed

Participating gynecologists reported a general understanding of ACP but did not feel adequately prepared to conduct HRA independently without additional training. As one respondent noted:

I know what anal screening is… I know essentially who should be screened. But do I do it regularly? No. And the reason why is because I don't really have the skills or training to follow it up. So, it's something that if it's a high-risk person, I tend to just do either digital exams or to refer them out. But I don't do anal screening in my practice generally.

Similarly, another respondent noted the need for training for her entire department, saying:

Intrinsically, I know how to do it, but I don't do it because I haven't had the practical training… It's not part of our general practice.

Respondents also noted the requisite training is not only technical instruction on how to conduct HRA; it must include all of the logistics that surround ACP such as laboratory orders and insurance coverage.

I'd love to do the appropriate thing. It's not that I don't want to, it's that I don't know where to do it, how to send it off and what the costs to the patient's going to be, and things like that.

Key theme 3: practice barriers

Even with an understanding of ACP importance and appropriate training, respondents noted numerous barriers to implementing ACP within their own practices. A primary concern is that gynecologists have limited time to spend with patients and a growing list of items that need to be covered during each visit.

From one respondent's perspective:

You've got to prove it's…worth our effort…because if you tell me, I'm going to have to spend a couple minutes doing this, that was two minutes I should have spent talking to her about her nutrition and her exercise, which are way more important.

Similarly, another respondent noted the need to know how to prioritize for certain patients, stating:

You also have to remember that we now have been given about 50 things that we're supposed to be screening for in every 15-min visit. So, you're going to have to also prove to us that this is beneficial in a certain population and tell us how to quickly identify those populations.

Key theme 4: lacking system infrastructure

Respondents also highlighted infrastructure and logistical concerns with offering HRA in their practices.

One respondent noted the following concerns about laboratory capacity and insurance coverage:

So, if we collect those results in rural Indiana, I need to have a lab nearby [that] could do…the anal cytology lab and order it and insurance to cover it and the whole nine yards.

Another participant noted that despite the presence of infrastructure supporting screening of a subpopulation of patients, she had concerns about widespread implementation of ACP. As she noted:

But if this was something that became universal on every patient, I'd be a little bit concerned about pathology's ability to get us a quick enough turn around on results…they would have to hire more staff because you would essentially be doubling the number of Paps that they would have to process.

Key theme 5: reliance on referral to specialists

Additional concerns were found in the recurrent theme that gynecologists are heavily dependent on referrals for ACP. In fact, numerous respondents revealed that they refer patients with any anal cancer concerns to gastroenterology and infectious disease specialists.

If they had big concerns, then I would refer them to a colorectal surgeon. Okay, or gastroenterologist.

Notably, one respondent stated that her primary reason to be trained in ACP would be for referral purposes, saying:

…if I even learned, I think my main goal at this time or role would be to screen properly so that I can refer as needed.

Discussion

Gynecologists cited lack of proof for utility of screening and relevance to their practice, need for additional knowledge and training, and practice implementation concerns. Despite these barriers, respondents were positively inclined towards learning about ACP and believed these barriers are likely surmountable. This study offers important insights into the complexities surrounding gynecologists' engagement in ACP.

Anal cancer rates remain unacceptably high for subsets of the American population: women living with HIV over age 45 years, women with a history of vulvar cancer or precancer (45–47 SIR per 100,000 person years) and those with a history of solid organ transplant more than 10 years prior (51 SIR per 100,000 person years). Furthermore, anal cancer rates in women surpass cervical cancer rates at or around age 60 years (Clifford et al., 2021). Our study is the first to qualitatively explore gynecologist attitudes towards ACP, adding to and expanding on quantitative findings from other studies (Russell et al., 2025). The results suggest a pathway for addressing this increase in anal cancer rates and aligns with findings from a study focusing on ACP among women living with HIV, a particularly at-risk group (Higashi et al., 2022).

Gynecologists play a broad role in women's healthcare and frequently serve in a primary care capacity for women across their lifespan. When surveyed, OBGYNs estimate that more than one third of their private, non-pregnant patients rely on them for provision of routine primary care. ACOG acknowledges the role OBGYNs play in preventative care with their statement:

“Given the shifting and complex landscape of care, in which many women may not receive all the recommended preventive services, obstetrician-gynecologists have an opportunity to contribute to the overall health and well-being of women throughout the lifespan by providing recommended preventive services and counseling.” (ACOG Committee Opinion, 2018).

The findings from our study underscore the significance of gynecologists' involvement in ACP, which extends beyond their traditional focus on reproductive health. By engaging in discussions about anal cancer risks and preventive measures and receiving stronger training to ensure the implementation of systematic screening practices, gynecologists can play a pivotal role in improving patient outcomes. With targeted training, other medical providers and specialists in health promotion and disease prevention could contribute meaningfully to ACP. Future research will assess barriers and opportunities among these providers to inform training and expand access to ACP to other high-risk groups, including men who have sex with men. Training programs and certification organizations may address access barriers by allowing abbreviated pathways to HRA certification for providers already certified in colposcopy.

Limitations and strengths

Study limitations include potential selection bias in participants as those who agreed to participate may be more likely to come from academic medical centers and have greater exposure to anogenital tract disease prevention. As with many qualitative studies, we have a small sample size, which is not meant to be generalizable to the larger population of gynecologists. Rather, we focus on the richness and depth of information to help direct next steps in increasing access to ACP for women. One further limitation may be not asking about HPV vaccination practice and perspectives during the study.

Study strengths include the depth of understanding offered by the qualitative interview process, which highlights voices of the workforce we seek to initially target for ACP training and implementation.

Conclusion

Educational initiatives can equip gynecologists with the tools to navigate time constraints and incorporate preventative measures, including ACP, more effectively. To address identified barriers to gynecologists offering ACP services, future research should focus on developing and funding multifaceted interventions. Moreover, addressing concerns for lack of utility in screening requires clear evidence-based guidelines and management of abnormal results. Several provider organizations have recently addressed this clinical practice gap, including IANS, which released consensus guidelines on anal cancer screening in March 2024 (Stier et al., 2024), followed by the CDC in July 2024. Policymakers should invest in workforce development and reduce logistical barriers in anal cancer prevention to ensure adequate clinical capacity for the anticipated increase in abnormal screening results and related patient care needs. ACOG and other women's health professional societies should offer evidence-based guidance emphasizing the importance of anal cancer screening across the aging continuum.

Our study identifies key challenges gynecologists anticipate in engaging in ACP, including perceived clinical value, guideline limitations, time constraints, and logistical barriers, and contributes to the broader discussion on improving preventive care in healthcare. Overcoming barriers to ACP will require coordinated efforts across medical disciplines to advance holistic women's healthcare.
