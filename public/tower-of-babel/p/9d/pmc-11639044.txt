Review of the 8th autumn school in chemoinformatics
Authors: Johann Gasteiger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper gives an overview of the lectures and posters presented at the 8th Autumn School in Chemoinformatics held in Nara, Japan on 28th ‐ 30th November 2023. The topics ranged from the study of chemical reactions through drug design and the use of Chemical Language Models and electronic structure informatics to the modeling of materials. In addition, a brief overview of the 50 years of work in chemoinformatics by Johann Gasteiger is given with an emphasis on the essential decisions during his scientific career.

INTRODUCTION

The Autumn School in Chemoinformatics was initiated by Kimito Funatsu in 2009. In the first meeting, six scientists presented their research in lectures. From then on, the Autumn School became a biennial event. Since a few years, the event is yearly alternating with the Strasbourg Summer School in Chemoinformatics. The 8th Autumn School took place in Nara on 28th–30th November 2023. The program offered 13 plenary lectures and five oral lectures, 34 poster presentations, as well as eight software presentations from companies. In concordance with the large increase in lectures and presentations the audience has strongly increased with 160 participants from academia (30 %) and industry (70 %).

In previous meetings in this series the major focus was on drug design. Several lectures in the 2023 meeting still presented important developments in computer‐assisted drug design. However, the emphasis shifted to fields previously more neglected. Thus, a major focus of the presentations was on investigations on chemical reactions. And several lectures took up the challenge offered by material design. Furthermore, the use of new developments in artificial intelligence such as large language models were also investigated. This review presents short summaries of the plenary lectures and the oral presentations. Because of space restrictions no details of the poster presentations could be given although quite interesting new developments were presented in the posters. Many of the bold names given here refer to papers following in this Special Issue of Molecular Informatics.

YEARS WITH CHEMOINFORMATICS

Johann Gasteiger was asked by the organizer of the Autumn School, Kimito Funatsu, to present the history of chemoinformatics and the ideas that were developed in this field. He decided to present his own development in chemoinformatics over the last 50 years by putting a strong emphasis on the decisions that he made in his scientific career in order that the audience take benefit from these ideas for their own scientific development.

For more information on the history of chemoinformatics, the Chemical Information and Computer Applications Group (CICAG) of the Royal Society of Chemistry has published in their Newsletter articles by the pioneers of cheminformatics: Peter Willett in summer 2022, Henry Rzepa in Winter 2022, and Johann Gasteiger in summer 2023 (https://www.rsc.org/globalassets/03‐membership‐community/connect‐with‐others/through‐interests/interest‐groups/cicag/cicag‐newsletter‐summer‐2023.pdf). These articles provide interesting personal insights into the development of this field.

STUDYING CHEMICAL REACTIONS

It is interesting to realize that the chemoinformatics community is increasingly concerned with the understanding and modeling of chemical reactions. The 8th Autumn School reflected this interest by presenting several studies in that field.

Satoshi Maeda uses a quantum mechanical approach for the prediction of the products, the yields, and the mechanism of chemical reactions. He has developed an automatic search method called artificial force induced reaction (AFIR) for exploring possible reaction pathways. The approach can be used in a forward search starting from given reactants to predict possible products. On the other hand, in a backward mode the search starts with a given product to finds possible reactants. Full quantum mechanical calculations are presently limited to systems with up to 50 atoms. However, for larger systems semiempirical calculations or QM/MM hybrid calculations can be used. This was shown for transition metal catalysis and organocatalysis. This project is certainly quite ambitious but it has produced quite good results. It can expected to have an important future.

Kenji Hori has combined a synthesis route development method with theoretical calculations. First, synthetic routes are generated by the AIPHOS, TOSP, or KOSP system. Reaction mechanisms are calculated by quantum mechanical calculations leading to transition state motifs (TSmotif) that are stored in a database (TSDB). The TSmotifs of the generated synthetic reactions are search in the TSDB to find the most similar TSmotif. This allows one to find optimum reaction condition based on the reaction mechanism. The reaction similarity search uses the Reaction Smiles. Currently the TSDB contains 4,500 elementary TS‐motifs based on 22,700 quantum mechanical data. 13 descriptors are used for the search in the TSDB. The reactions are classified by a map obtained from a Kohonen neural network. Application of this method in the NEDO project (New Energy and industrial technology Development Organization) to design a synthesis route for branebrutinib provided a six‐step synthesis route for which the first 5 steps could be verified (albeit with low yield). For the last step side reactions were dominant.

Alexander Varnek has defined a Condensed Graph of Reaction (CGR) by superimposing reactant and product atoms of a reaction and indicating the bonds broken or made in a reaction. In the last 20 years a series of methods were developed to exploit this CGR concept. These methods comprise reaction rule extraction, database work, data analysis tasks, machine learning models, and de novo design. The methods can be applied both to retro‐ and forward search. An extensive series of studies included machine learning models for reaction kinetic and thermodynamic values, assessment of optimum reaction conditions, the prediction of catalyst enantioselectivity, and the generation of novel reaction transformations.

Pavel Sidorov has used in a collaboration with Alexandre Varnek and Benjamin List the Condensed Graph of Reaction (CGR) concept to predict highly enantioselective catalysts for hydroalkoxylation reactions. 2D descriptors comprising circular substructures were used in an SVM model.

Kazunari Yoshikawa investigated approaches to the conversion of methane to methanol He first studied the cleavage of the C−H bond of methane on metal and metal oxide surfaces through quantum mechanical DFT calculations. The catalysts investigated were FeO+, CuO+ and ScO+ which resulted either in energy downhill or uphill processes.

Swarit Jasial presented an approach to the prediction of monomer concentrations in copolymerization reactions. Infrared spectra were measured and then subjected to wavelet transformations. For the training set the concentrations were measured by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Various regression methods were used to build models. The best results were obtained by elastic net regularization. Further improvements could be achieved by employing analytical derivatives of the IR spectra.

APPLICATIONS FOR DRUG DESIGN

Studies in drug design have been the dominating theme in previous Autumn Schools. Several presentations showed new developments in this field.

Didier Rognan had to present his studies through the internet which worked quite fine. His major focus was on extending the screening of potentially drug‐like molecules to very large datasets of billions of structures which are not yet available but could be synthesized in a few steps. Several studies by independent research groups had shown that the virtual screening of very large virtual chemical spaces led to spectacular successes such as unexpected high hit rates, very high potencies and fine selectivity. Two approaches to studying billion‐size chemical spaces were presented. The first, POEM (Pocket‐Oriented Elaboration of Molecules) was a fragment‐based approach. The other, SPACEDOCK was a structure‐based approach which starts out with docking commercial reagents into the active site of interest. Then, 36 stereoselective organic reactions are used to transform the reagents to final products under 3D constraints of the target protein to provide 7 billion synthesizable compounds. In a typical study 670 million compounds were docked as antagonists into the human dopamine D3 receptor. Running a series of filters eventually provided 315 hits with 15 of them sharing the orthomethoxybenzamide scaffold. Experimental testing of these 15 compounds provided 10 hits with six of them exhibiting nanomolecular affinities.

Thierry Langer presented new developments in LigandScout and pharmacophore‐based compound modelling. Since a few years, dynamic effects of ligand protein binding are studied. In the Common Hits Approach (CHA) multiple coordinate sets are saved from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations thus providing a series of pharmacophore models. These are then rescored to generate a single hit‐list. This list is further analysed in the hierarchical graph representation of pharmacophore models (HGPM). Automatic visualization provides an intuitive view of the ensemble of pharmacophores of an MD simulation. First successes were obtained for quantitative pharmacophore models. These were then used for quantitative structure‐activity relationships in the QPhAR approach. First results were presented.

Sharon Bryant gave a brief overview of the importance of the pharmacophore concept. She then presented results from the work of InteLigand within the NeuroDeRisk Project consortium. Within this project, the IL‐Profiler contains 60 validated 3D‐neuropharmacophore models that allow one to profile and rank chemical structures for neuropharmacology and risk of neurotoxic adverse effects. This supports the decision making in the development of safer pharmaceuticals and chemicals at an early stage.

Kazufumi Ohkawa extended the Electronic‐Structure Informatics (ESI) concept of Sugimoto et al. (see below) to the treatment of chemical reactivity and enzyme inhibition. He defined Fukui Function Fingerprints (FFFP) through charge values obtained by a Mulliken population analysis with GAMESS and Fukui function values for nucleophiles and for electrophiles from the FMO code in GAMESS. A very good model was obtained with these FFFP for the binding of inhibitors in the TYK2 complex.

Yoshihiro Yamanishi included biomedical data on compounds (such as clinical phenotypes, gene expression patterns, target molecules) and diseases in his studies. First, he presented a computational approach for therapeutic target identification by a trans‐disease analysis. The similarity among diseases was taken into account which allowed the distinction of inhibitory from activatory target proteins. Next, a network‐based approach for the prediction of synergistic drug combinations was explained. Finally, an omics‐based approach for de novo drug design was presented. Molecules were generated that invert the disease‐specific gene expression patterns.

Yuki Matsukiyo used heterogenous gene expression data. First, molecules were selected based on transcriptional correlations between chemically‐induced and genetically‐perturbed gene expression profiles. Then, the substructures of these molecules were fused by a deep neural network. The performance was compared to two previous methods. For 7 out of 10 proteins the presented method outperformed the previous methods.

USE OF CHEMICAL LANGUAGE MODELS FOR DRUG DESIGN

Artificial intelligence methods have found applications in many areas of society. Whereas applications of artificial neural networks in chemistry and drug design have a long history, it needed novel developments such as deep neural networks to process huge amounts of data encountered in diverse areas.

Jürgen Bajorath has explored the use of deep neural networks originating from natural language processing for molecular design. For this purpose, an encoding of chemical structures such as by SMILES provides a chemical language. An outline of an architecture for chemical language models (CLM) including recurrent neural networks (RNN) or transformers was presented. A CLM is pretrained on a large number of molecular strings. This is followed by a fine‐tuning with smaller data sets of compounds with desired properties (such as activity against a target protein). RNN models typically consist of long short‐term memory (LSTM) cells. A Decoder is needed to produce output structures and activity probabilities. Different CLM for applications in medicinal chemistry were developed. The DeepCubist method was developed to generate new peptidomimetics. Another CLM termed DeepAS with R‐group representations was trained on 100,000 analogue series with activity against 2,000 targets. This model predicted new R‐groups and their activity.

Gisbert Schneider has also taken the challenge of exploring the use of chemical language models (CLM) for drug design. He presented four case studies differing in their set‐up of the various steps. The first study showed an approach for a molecular design‐make‐test‐analyze (DMTA) cycle by combining a chemical language model with a bench‐top microfluidic synthesis platform. This automated DMTA pipeline was put to test by designing – and synthesizing – liver X receptor agonists. Case study two involved a ligand‐based process relying on a deep learning network to process string representations of molecules such as SMILES strings. This CML involves three steps (i) pretraining with an extensive set of molecules (ii) transfer learning with a smaller dataset having the desired activity, and (iii) sampling by generating new molecules. Two CLMs were pretrained using distinct strategies on a large dataset of patented compound structures. Both CLMs underwent fine‐tuning with inhibitors of phosphoinositide 3‐kinase gamma. From the set of de novo generated molecules a compound with sub‐micromolecular activity was found. Case study three merged ligand‐ and structure‐based methodologies. Case study four aimed at facilitating the optimization of late stage hits and leads in drug discovery by predicting reaction outcomes, yields, and regioselectivity.

ELECTRONIC STRUCTURE INFORMATICS

Manabu Sugimoto has developed a comprehensive approach to the discovery of promising functional compounds (such as drugs) and materials which is accompanied by the prediction of their properties. To begin with, the approach comprises the calculation of molecular descriptor sets. As the descriptors are calculated by quantum mechanical methods by density functional theory (DFT), the models obtained by these descriptors are based on a fundamental theoretical level. Furthermore, these models and the descriptors selected can be interpreted and thus enhance our understanding of chemical phenomena. The concept of “electronic similarity” was introduced and defined either as wavefunction‐level similarity or as spectral similarity. The fundamental descriptors collected for electronic similarity are calculated by the Gaussian 16 program.

For wavefunction similarity such calculated properties as ionization potential, electron affinities, reorganization energies after ionization or anionization are used. The reorganization energies reflect the softness/harness of molecules and can be related to activation energies in chemical reactions of electron transfer processes. For spectral similarity UV/Vis spectra and IR spectra are used and compared to a reference molecule These reflect characteristics of the electronic structure and vibrational modes. In addition, similarities in the electronic, vibrational, and energy density of states are calculated and included in the descriptor set. Furthermore, values representing the molecular shape such as molecular volume and its anisotropy are considered. This set of descriptors, called ESI‐1, has been successfully used for modelling a variety of biological activities and for predicting other molecular properties.

Novel developments by his research group include the incorporation of electronic excitation energies and transition dipole moments which allow one to work without a reference molecule. It was also shown that a selection of RDKit descriptors is directly related to the ESI descriptors. This provides a fast and easy evaluation of ESI descriptors. Work is under way to calculate local ESI descriptors such as with Fukui indices allowing the modelling of chemical reactivity.

The Electronic‐Structure Informatics (ESI) descriptors open a new window to the prediction of properties of compounds and materials. Their clear theoretical basis will assist us in better understanding a variety of chemical properties and their underlying principles and laws.

MODELLING OF MATERIALS

It has been recognized that material design and development can strongly benefit from chemoinformatics methods. Accordingly, many applications of chemoinformatics to the modelling of material properties have recently appeared. A few studies were presented on data exploration of materials at the Autumn School.

Shigetaka Tomiya emphasized that increasingly sophisticated measurement devices provide data whose analysis become particularly difficult. Thus, a new field called metrology informatics is in development for the analysis of data from materials. Multiple measurement methods are often used for the analysis of materials with different combinations of spatial resolution and detection sensitivity. As an example for the application of metrology informatics the analysis of semiconductors was discussed.

Yosuke Harashima presented the data assimilation concept that integrates experimental data with simulated data. This is particularly helpful when the determination of experimental data require high costs. The assimilation method of multivariate Gaussian distribution was presented. The approach was illustrated with two models, one using computed data and one with experimental data. The data assimilation method was combined with Bayesian optimization.

POSTER PRESENTATIONS

34 posters were presented: 9 concerned chemical reactions, 5 drug design, 11 materials, 5 electronic structure informatics, 7 novel methods, and 1 chemical language model (multiple counts are due when a poster concerned more than one topic). All poster presentations demonstrated that chemoinformatics in Japan has a high scientific level.

SUMMARY

The 8th Autumn School in chemoinformatics in Nara, Japan in 2023 presented up‐to‐date research and showed a high scientific level. Particularly, the lectures and posters presented by Japanese scientists showed that chemoinformatics has reached a level that is internationally completely competitive. Novel developments of chemoinformatics are introduced that gain international recognition. Furthermore, it seems that a new young generation of chemoinformaticians has been educated that will assist the Japanese economy in its development. Surely, this is certainly also a result of the sequence of Autumn Schools organized over the last 15 years.

Biographical Information

Prof. Dr. Johann Gasteiger studied chemistry and has had positions at the University of Munich, Germany, ETH Zurich, Switzerland, University of California, Berkeley, USA, and Technical University of Munich. In 1994 he moved to the University of Erlangen‐Nuremberg, Germany where he co‐founded the “Computer‐Chemie‐Centrum”. He is one of the initiators of Chemoinformatics in Germany and has produced more than 250 scientific publications in this field. His work has been recognized by several awards, including the Gmelin–Beilstein Medal of the German Chemical Society, awards from the ACS Division of Chemical Information and of the ACS Division of Computers in Chemistry as well as of the Chemical Structure Association. In 1997 he founded Molecular Networks GmbH, a company developing and distributing software for chemical applications.
