Outcomes of Ventricular Tachycardia Catheter Ablation in Pediatric Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Salah H. Alahwany, Shunsuke Uetake, Juan Jiménez-Jáimez, Eva Cabrera-Borrego, Aarti Dalal, Prince J. Kannankeril, M. Benjamin Shoemaker, Daisuke Togashi, Yumi Katsume, Travis D. Richardson, Arvindh Kanagasundram, William G. Stevenson, Harikrishna Tandri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) of ventricular tachycardia (VT) in arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy is safe and reduces ventricular arrhythmia burden. Previous studies included adult patients, and data on pediatric patients are scarce. The objective of our study was to report on the safety and efficacy of RFCA in pediatric arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy.METHODS:Fifteen patients who fulfilled the 2010 arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy task force criteria, clinically presented before the age of 18, and underwent VT RFCA procedures at ≤21 years old were included. Baseline characteristics, genotypic and phenotypic data, and ablation outcomes were collected.RESULTS:The mean age at symptom onset was 15.5±1.6 years, and at the index procedure was 18.1±2 years, with 73% of the patients being male. Pathogenic mutation in desmosomal genes was detected in 87%. First presentation symptoms included palpitations (33%), syncope (27%), sustained VT (27%), and sudden cardiac arrest (13%). ECG repolarization abnormalities were present in 93% and 40% were reported to be athletes. In the index RFCA procedure, sustained monomorphic VT was induced in 60%. Electroanatomic mapping showed 100% basal RV epicardial substrate and 54% endocardial low voltage/scar. Repeat VT ablation was required in 80% over a mean follow-up of 16.4 months. The median number of procedures was 2 (interquartile range, 2–4), with sustained VT-free survival of 73% over a mean follow-up duration of 21.4 months. No acute periprocedural complications occurred. Bilateral cardiac sympathetic denervation due to recurrent ventricular arrhythmia was performed in 27%. Eventually, 2 patients (13%) developed advanced heart failure and underwent heart transplantation.CONCLUSIONS:Pediatric arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy RFCA is safe, but early recurrences are common, requiring repeat ablations and sometimes bilateral cardiac sympathetic denervation. The substrate is mostly epicardial with preserved endocardial voltage. VT free survival is 73% after multiple procedures. Progressive heart failure requiring transplantation occurred in 13% within 2 decades of initial presentation.

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular (RV) cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is a genetic condition that leads to ventricular arrhythmias (VA) and progressive RV failure, resulting from the underlying replacement of RV myocardium with fibrofatty tissue.1,2 Sudden death is an early and dreaded manifestation that occurs disproportionately in younger patients. Early diagnosis and arrhythmic risk stratification are crucial.3,4 The majority of patients who present with sudden cardiac arrest or with VA and meet task force criteria (TFC) for ARVC receive implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs) to prevent sudden death.5–8 Recurrent symptomatic VAs are common in early onset ARVC, resulting in ICD therapies including shocks for ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation. VT is often fast in this age group and falls into the ventricular fibrillation zone, frequently triggering ICD firing. Although most younger patients are managed with beta-blockers and antiarrhythmic agents, a significant number will need radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) due to antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) failure to mitigate VA burden. There are a limited number of centers that perform complex VT ablations in the pediatric cohort, and the experience with the epicardial approach in this cohort is even more limited. As such, there is a paucity of data on the safety and outcomes of VT ablation in this population. The purpose of our study is to describe the experience with RFCA of VT in pediatric ARVC patients referred to our centers for ablation.

Methods

Study Population

This is a 2-center retrospective observational study of pediatric patients with a definite diagnosis of ARVC who underwent VT RFCA. All patients presented with VA before the age of 18 and were ≤21 years old at the time of the first VT ablation procedure. All patients met the 2010 TFC5 and provided a written informed consent. The patients were enrolled from the Vanderbilt University Medical Center ARVC registry database and Virgen de las Nieves University Hospital. The Vanderbilt University Medical Center institutional review board approved the study protocol. Data supporting the study’s findings are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Data Collection

Data were collected from electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics, clinical/genetic/family history, noninvasive investigation reports, and medication history. Reviewed source data comprised electrocardiograms, ambulatory monitoring recordings, echocardiography, ICD data, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. Patients were prospectively followed for complications during hospitalization and through follow-up clinic visits or phone calls.

VT Catheter Ablation Procedure

All ablations were performed under general anesthesia. Both epicardial and endocardial access were obtained at the start of the procedure in 11 of 13 patients at the index (first) procedure. Anterior epicardial access was performed.9 High-density electroanatomical mapping (EAM) of the substrate was created using a multielectrode mapping catheter. VT was induced with programmed stimulation, but was unstable in all patients, requiring pace termination or cardioversion. RFCA was performed at sites of latest activation, best-pace maps, and critical isthmus using an irrigated force-sensing catheter. The procedural end point was noninducibility of VT. Epicardial ablation was performed first, followed by endocardial ablation if VT was still inducible. Postprocedure, 120 mg of intrapericardial triamcinolone acetonide was administered in the epicardial space.

RFCA data included VT inducibility, cycle length, number of morphologies, dominant morphology, RF delivery time, approach (endocardial/epicardial), regions of low voltage, fractionated late potentials, inducibility at procedure end or by noninvasive programmed stimulation (NIPS) before discharge, fluoroscopic data, procedure duration, repeat ablation data, and reasons for delayed discharge.

All patient data of the serial electrocardiograms, ambulatory monitoring reports, ICD episodes and therapies, imaging, and genotype analysis were interpreted by an expert, and all the above available data and other relevant details were obtained through the review of medical records, including the electrophysiological recording systems and EAM system.

Outcomes and Definitions

The primary outcome was the safety of the procedure, defined as any adverse event, such as the occurrence of pericardial effusion/tamponade, coronary artery injury, pneumothorax, hemothorax, or groin hematoma. Any periprocedural hemodynamic deterioration managed by vasopressor, inotropic, and mechanical support during the admission period was reported.

Secondary outcomes included acute and long-term efficacy. Acute success was defined as noninducibility of VT at the end of the procedure and noninducibility of VT by NIPS before discharge. Long-term efficacy was defined by freedom from sustained VT recurrence during follow-up. VT recurrence was defined as either appropriate ICD intervention (antitachycardia pacing or shock) or sustained VT detected through device interrogation. VT storm was defined as ≥3 episodes of sustained VT in 24 hours requiring therapeutic intervention. The need for sympathectomy to mitigate high VT burden, long-term mechanical circulatory support (Ventricular Assist Device), heart transplantation, and the occurrence of death were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR], Q1–Q3) and categorical variables as frequency (percent). Individual patient data were reconstructed from Kaplan-Meier curves using the methodology described by Liu et al.10. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to determine cumulative survival free of VT recurrence. All Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi Statistical Package (Version 2.6.23; Computer Software, Sydney, Australia) and R software, version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Fifteen patients with a definite ARVC diagnosis per 2010 TFC5 and who had a clinical presentation with VA before the age of 18 years were included. All patients underwent the index (first) VT RFCA at or before the age of 21 years (Table 1; Figure 1).

The mean age at arrhythmia onset was 15.5±1.6 years, and the age at first VT ablation procedure was 18.1±2.0 years, with 27% (4/15) being female. Presenting symptoms included palpitations (33%), syncope (27%), sustained VT (27%), and sudden cardiac arrest (13%). A history of participating in vigorous and competitive sports was reported by 40% of the patients. The family history of ARVC was confirmed in 4 patients (4/15, 27%; Table 2).

ICD therapy for sustained VT following failure of AADs was the most common indication for VT ablation at the index procedure, occurring in 60% of patients. The most frequently failed AADs were sotalol (53%) and flecainide (20%).

Other indications for VT ablation included recurrent nonsustained VT in 1 patient who was intolerant to both AADs and metoprolol, and sustained VT below the ICD detection threshold in another patient. Additionally, 2 patients underwent primary VT ablation before ICD implantation for sustained VT.

RFCA Outcomes

At the index (first) VT ablation procedure (Table 4), sustained monomorphic VT was induced in 60% of patients (mean cycle length, 235±95 ms). The median number of VT morphologies induced was 2 (IQR, 1–2.25), with half showing left bundle branch block superior axis and half left bundle branch block inferior axis. EAM data were unavailable for 2 patients. Of the 13 patients with available EAM data, an epicardial approach was performed in 85% (11/13) of the cases. During EAM, 100% of the patients (11/11) had low-voltage scars and late potentials on the epicardial surface. Of the 13 who underwent endocardial mapping, 54% (7/13) had low endocardial voltage (bipolar voltage <1.5 mV) detected mainly in the basal RV and the RV outflow tract. None of the VTs were hemodynamically tolerated. A substrate-based approach with ablation of late potentials and areas with good pace map in the scar was performed in all patients. At the end of the procedure, a sustained VT was induced in 1 patient after an endocardial-only approach (patient 3), and ventricular flutter in another via an aggressive ventricular extrastimulation protocol (patient 8).

Repeat RFCA Outcomes

A repeat ablation procedure was performed in 80% of patients with recurrent VT. Of the 12 patients who underwent a second procedure, 7 patients underwent a third procedure, 5 had 4 ablations, and 2 had 5 VT ablation procedures for VA control. At repeat ablation procedures, epicardial low voltage scar progression was noted in 11/12 (91%) of the patients when compared with the index epicardial procedure EAM (eg, Figure 3), and the RV basal lateral epicardium was affected in all patients (illustration, Figure 4). Significant epicardial adhesions hindered mapping and ablation in 1 case (Figure 5). Following repeated procedures (median of 2 [IQR, 2–4]), sustained VT-free survival was 73% over a mean follow-up duration of 21.4±20.3 months (Figure 6).

At the last follow-up, aside from the 2 patients who underwent heart transplantation, only 1 patient was off AADs. The remaining patients continued on AADs, primarily flecainide (62%) and sotalol (31%).

Safety of VT Ablation

A total of 30 ablation procedures were performed in the 15 patients aged ≤21 years old. None of the patients had acute periprocedural adverse events or procedural-related hemodynamic deterioration. At the index (first) ablation procedure, half of these procedures (15 out of 30) had a median hospital stay of 1 day, including patients who underwent epicardial procedures (Table 4).

Sympathectomy, Heart Failure, and Heart Transplantation

Due to recurrent sustained VT and ICD shocks, 27% (4/15) of the patients had bilateral cardiac sympathetic denervation (BCSD). Mildly reduced left ventricular function occurred in 1 patient and was maintained on an angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor with subsequent recovery of left ventricular function (patient 10). An orthotopic heart transplant was performed in 2 patients (mean age 36±6 years) due to underlying advanced heart failure manifestations (patient 1, 6). Patient (1) underwent a concomitant liver transplant due to associated progressive cardiac cirrhosis.

Discussion

Catheter ablation is increasingly used in the management of VAs in ARVC, and there is a wealth of literature on safety and efficacy outcomes in adult ARVC.11–15 Although ARVC can present with refractory VAs in the pediatric ARVC population, data on catheter ablation in this cohort are scarce. Our study is the first to report outcomes in a uniform cohort of patients with ARVC meeting the 2010 modified TFC,5 with the majority of the patients carrying a PKP2 pathogenic mutation. Our study has several interesting findings. First, VT ablation in the pediatric population appears to be safe and without major periprocedural complications. Second, the substrate of VA is mostly epicardial, sparing the endocardium in about half of cases. Third, the recurrence of VA following RFCA was frequent despite achieving noninducibility at the end of the procedure. Finally, long-term VT-free survival was achieved in most patients through a combination of repeat ablation procedures and BCSD.

ARVC can manifest in early adolescence, with diagnosis often challenging due to overlapping juvenile electrocardiogram patterns, such as T-wave inversions.3,6,7,16 This age group carries the highest risk of sudden cardiac arrest, especially during physical exertion.1,17–19 Once diagnosed with ARVC or if the patient survives a sudden cardiac arrest, an ICD is implanted either for primary or secondary prevention. Unfortunately, the recurrence of VA is not uncommon in this age group despite restriction of physical activity and the various AAD prescriptions, which are frequently poorly tolerated, leading to noncompliance. Recurrences necessitating ICD shocks trigger sympathetic responses, causing VT storms due to the adrenergic component of VT in early disease stages.13,20 Multiple shocks have consequences beyond the arrhythmia itself, leading to anxiety disorders and posttraumatic stress disorder in these patients, who may sometimes be unable to make decisions related to their care. Catheter ablation has been widely used in adult VA, yet ablation of VT in pediatric ARVC is underutilized. One of the main reasons is the lack of availability of data supporting the safety of the procedure and the lack of experience with managing arrhythmias with this disease process in the pediatric electrophysiology realm. The main objective of our study was to report on our safety outcomes and highlight the unique nuances of VT ablation in pediatric ARVC.

Safety Profile

The major concern of VT RFCA in pediatric patients is procedural safety. The relative safety of RFCA in PVCs and idiopathic VT in the pediatric population was shown in limited studies.21–23 Reported complications in adult ARVC ablation studies include pericardial tamponade, RV perforation, myocardial infarction, intestinal perforation, and death.13,14,20,24–27 However, none of these complications occurred in our cohort despite percutaneous epicardial access for mapping and ablation in 85% of our cases at the first VT ablation procedure. As the cohort’s mean height and weight were comparable to adult measurements, the safety of VT ablation may not be generalizable to significantly smaller or younger patients.

Experience gained from ARVC mapping and ablation in adult patients in a high-volume center is likely important for minimizing complications. The absence of any adverse events such as pericardial effusion, coronary artery injury, or hemodynamic deterioration during procedures is encouraging, especially considering the inherent risks associated with catheter ablation in pediatric patients.28,29 The median hospital stay of 1 day after the index VT ablation procedure further supports the procedure’s tolerance and safety in this cohort.

Our results confirm and extend the results of a prior single-center report of pediatric ARVC ablation that showed similar safety and efficacy.30 However, there are important differences between the 2 studies. The prior report only included patients who failed endocardial ablation, and the diagnosis of ARVC was based on the 1994 criteria. Further, none of the patients had genetic testing in the prior report. Nevertheless, the conclusions are valid, showing superiority of an epicardial approach in pediatric ARVC.

Efficacy in Pediatric Patients

Recurrence after pediatric VA ablation depends on the underlying cause, making efficacy comparisons of RFCA and VT-free survival unrealistic across different causes other than ARVC.23

In our study, acute success was 80%; however, long-term efficacy was discouragingly low (20%) after a single ablation procedure over a mean follow-up period of 16.4±19.5 months. Following multiple ablations, VT-free survival was 73% over a mean follow-up period of 21.6±21 months. A quarter of the patients needed sympathectomy in addition to the RFCA procedure to mitigate high VT burden and appropriate ICD shocks.

Pokushalov et al30 reported 70.6% VT-free survival in pediatric patients with ARVC over a mean follow-up of 26 months. All patients in this study underwent epicardial ablation following a failed endocardial procedure. Although these data appear similar to our cohort, recurrence rates were high after the first procedure in our study, even with an epicardial approach. The differences could be due to patient selection, as the majority of our patients had an advanced structural disease and met the 2010 modified TFC.5

Pediatric ARVC substrate in PKP2 mutation appears to differ from the adult substrate in several ways. First, the arrhythmic substrate can be confined to the epicardium, as a significant number of patients had no demonstrable endocardial low voltage. Second, induced VTs are faster and are often unmappable, requiring a substrate-based approach. Third, even though noninducibility was achieved acutely, short-term recurrence rates were high. Finally, the substrate seems to evolve within a short follow-up period, which is very much unlike adult ARVC.31

Disease progression in pediatric ARVC often presents differently than in adults, with younger patients potentially experiencing more aggressive forms of the disease.8 Pediatric patients often have different genetic profiles compared with adults, which may affect their clinical outcomes.32 The diagnostic criteria for ARVC were primarily developed based on adult populations, which may not accurately reflect the disease’s manifestation in children.33–35 This discrepancy can lead to different outcomes for pediatric-onset ARVC patients as compared with adults.

Although initial outcomes may be promising, long-term management remains challenging, necessitating repeated catheter ablation and BCSD, suggesting that adult patients may have a more favorable long-term response to ablation compared with their pediatric counterparts. In a large cohort of adult patients with ARVC, cumulative freedom from VT after a single procedure was 68.6% and 49.8% at 1 and 5 years, respectively, and cumulative VT-free survival after multiple procedures was 81.8% and 69.6% at 1 and 5 years, respectively.12

Eventually, heart transplantation was required in 13% of patients over a mean follow-up duration of 22±8 years in our cohort. The mean age at transplantation was 36 years, which is significantly younger than the mean age of 44 years reported for ARVC patients in the Organ Sharing (United Network of Organ Sharing) Thoracic Registry.36

Aggressive Arrhythmic Phenotype in Pediatric ARVC

This cohort predominantly reflects a classical pediatric ARVC phenotype driven by PKP2 variants (12 out of 15 patients). This genetic background likely contributes to the aggressive arrhythmic manifestations observed, which are more pronounced than those typically reported in adult cohorts, suggesting a genotype-specific disease expression.1,3,16,37–40

Most pediatric patients undergoing VT ablation had PKP2 gene mutations, supporting a PKP2-driven, arrhythmia-prone phenotype. The arrhythmic burden may have been influenced by intense physical activity, as nearly half were avid athletes, accelerating phenotypic progression.41,42

Limitations and Future Directions

The small sample size and retrospective nature of the analysis restrict generalizability and limit causal inferences. Variability in follow-up duration among patients could introduce bias in assessing long-term efficacy. Scar progression was noted but could not be quantified for longitudinal comparisons due to incomplete raw data. Future studies should include larger cohorts, multicenter collaborations, and standardized follow-up protocols to better understand the long-term implications of RFCA in pediatric ARVC.

Conclusions

RFCA appears to be a safe intervention for managing recurrent VT in pediatric patients with ARVC. Recurrences are common in the short term and can respond well to repeat ablation procedures. Some patients needed additional therapies, such as bilateral sympathectomy for arrhythmia control. Patients presenting early with arrhythmia may be more likely to have progression requiring transplantation. Larger multicenter studies are needed to better define an approach to VT ablation and heart failure management in this patient population to optimize long-term outcomes.
