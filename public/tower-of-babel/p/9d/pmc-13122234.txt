Advances in nanotechnology for the diagnosis and management of autoimmune diseases
Authors: Yongquan Zheng, Xiaoyu Cai, Lyu Zhang, Weidong Fei, Dongxu Qin, Xiaoqian Zhang, Jimin Zhu, Caihong Zheng, Yao Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune diseases (AIDs) are chronic, heterogeneous disorders that are often diagnosed after irreversible tissue damage and treated with broad immunosuppression that fails to deliver durable remission. Nanotechnology offers opportunities to sense early immune perturbations and to deliver interventions with molecular, cellular and organ-level precision. Here we synthesize advances from 2015 to 2025 in nano-enabled diagnosis and therapy for rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis, inflammatory bowel disease, type 1 diabetes, psoriasis and selected rare AIDs. On the diagnostic side, nano-optical and electrochemical biosensors, nano-enhanced imaging probes, and liquid-biopsy platforms for extracellular vesicles and cell-free nucleic acids improve sensitivity, stratification and longitudinal monitoring, while wearable and point-of-care devices extend assessment into home and community settings. We relate these technologies to concrete clinical scenarios and highlight performance metrics such as limit of detection, sample volume, and clinical sensitivity/specificity. Therapeutically, we review stimuli-responsive and ligand-targeted nanocarriers for small molecules and biologics, tolerogenic nanoparticles and exosomes, mRNA–lipid nanoparticle "inverse vaccines", and microneedles or tissue-nanotransporter systems for local gene and cytokine modulation. We summarize emerging clinical trial data and currently marketed nanomedicines for autoimmune indications, linking formulation design to efficacy and safety readouts. Across platforms, we outline design principles connecting physicochemical properties to biodistribution and immune interactions, and we discuss manufacturability, long-term safety and regulatory hurdles that govern translation. Collectively, these advances, together with emerging AI and digital twin frameworks, illustrate how nanotechnology can support earlier diagnosis, more precise and tolerogenic interventions, and progress toward durable, personalized remission in AIDs.

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases (AIDs) are chronic disorders in which the immune system mistakenly attacks self-tissues. They include rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), multiple sclerosis (MS), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), lupus nephritis (LN) and others [1,2]. More than 350 million people are affected worldwide [3]. From a diagnostic perspective, many AIDs are still identified only after substantial tissue injury, because early clinical manifestations are nonspecific, classification criteria tend to prioritize established disease, and conventional serological markers [such as rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP), or anti-dsDNA antibodies] lack adequate sensitivity or specificity in a subset of patients. On the therapeutic side, current management still relies heavily on non-specific immunosuppressants or anti-inflammatory drugs whose limited efficacy and significant adverse effects fail to meet the clinical demand for ‘early diagnosis, precision therapy and long-term remission [[4], [5], [6]]. With advances in materials science and immunology, nanotechnology has emerged as an important tool to address these gaps, owing to its controllable size, easy surface modification, and ability to integrate biological and physical signals [7,8].

In diagnostics, gold nanoparticle (AuNP) sensors based on localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), carbon nanotube arrays and quantum dot or magnetic nanocomposite probes can detect antinuclear antibodies, circulating microRNAs and cell-free DNA with very high sensitivity, reaching femtomolar levels [9]. Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) used as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents can label synovial macrophages at early micro-lesions, enabling earlier imaging assessment of RA [10]. In addition, liquid biopsy platforms that capture exosomes on nanoarrays and analyze them by Raman spectroscopy are creating new options for non-invasive disease monitoring [11,12].

On the therapeutic side, targeted drug delivery and immunomodulation are advancing in parallel. Stimuli-responsive or biomimetic nanocarriers can sense the inflammatory microenvironment and then release methotrexate (MTX), tocilizumab (TCZ) or siRNA in a controlled way, improving local efficacy in joints or the gut while reducing systemic toxicity [13,14]. A more recent approach is the "tolerogenic nano-strategy": engineered nanoparticles co-deliver self-antigens and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavengers, reprogramming dendritic cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) to induce antigen-specific immune tolerance and reducing MS relapse in the EAE (Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis) model [15]. In parallel, the “inverse vaccine” concept for several AIDs is rapidly developing; nucleoside-modified mRNA LNPs can suppress autoreactive T cells in mice and early clinical studies without inducing inflammation [16]. Systematic reviews suggest that nano- or microscale tolerogenic vaccine platforms are becoming a major direction for next-generation precision immunotherapy [17]. In the past 3 years, several high-impact reviews have further systematized the landscape of autoimmune nanomedicine. Yang et al. summarized antigen-specific nanomedicines for autoimmunity by categorizing nanoparticle platforms according to design principles and mechanisms of action, including targeted delivery of autoantigenic ligands to tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells (APCs), co-delivery of antigen with immunomodulators, and multimeric pMHC-based constructs that span outcomes from clonal deletion to bystander immunoregulation [18]. Thatte et al. provided a cross-disease overview of emerging nanomedicine strategies in autoimmunity, emphasizing how rationally engineered nanocarriers with tailored physicochemical properties and surface ligands can selectively target pathogenic immune cells and inflamed organs while outlining persistent translational barriers such as manufacturing, biodistribution and long-term safety [8]. More recently, Razavi et al. reviewed mRNA–LNP systems as a modular platform for autoimmune therapy, covering applications in antigen-specific tolerizing vaccines, in vivo engineering of regulatory or chimeric antigen receptor–modified lymphocytes, and local cytokine reprogramming [19], whereas Xiang et al. focused on nanomedicine targeting the programmed cell death protein 1/ programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) axis, highlighting how polymeric nanoparticles, LNPs, EVs and biomimetic nanocarriers can deliver checkpoint agonists with spatial precision to restore peripheral tolerance while reducing systemic immunosuppression [20].

Although nanotechnology offers an integrated paradigm that links diagnosis and therapy and modulates both disease and microenvironment, important problems remain. These include long-term safety, scalable manufacturing, clinical translation pathways and regulatory requirements [21]. In the past 5 years, progress in GMP-grade biodegradable materials, surface engineering and machine-learning-assisted pharmacokinetic prediction and monitoring systems has accelerated bench-to-bedside translation and industrial development [22,23]. Nevertheless, distinct AIDs differ markedly in pathogenesis, target expression and tissue barriers, necessitating disease- and subtype-specific optimization of nanoplatforms. Clinically important examples include seropositive versus seronegative RA, interferon (IFN)-α–high versus –low SLE, and HLA-stratified T1D, in which differences in autoantibody and cytokine patterns sit atop shared hypoxic, ROS-enriched and sometimes acidic inflammatory niches, implying that the choice of pH/ROS/protease-responsive linkers and antigen- or receptor-targeted ligands must be tailored rather than simply transferred between diseases [[24], [25], [26]]. However, different AIDs still show major differences in pathogenesis, target expression and tissue barriers, so nanoplatforms need disease-specific optimization [8]. In addition to prototypic AIDs such as RA, SLE, MS, IBD, T1D and psoriasis, we also briefly highlight emerging nanotechnology-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in selected rare AIDs (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis, ANCA-associated vasculitis) to illustrate how similar design principles can be leveraged in indications with few or no approved disease-modifying therapies.

AIDs and foundations of nanotechnology applications

AIDs are a heterogeneous group of chronic disorders in which immune tolerance breaks down, driven by genetic risk, environmental factors and abnormal activation of immune cells. Even though we understand more about their epidemiology and shared mechanisms, early diagnosis is still difficult. Because early symptoms are subtle or nonspecific, and existing treatments are often toxic and do not reliably induce long-term remission. The physicochemical properties of nanomaterials—such as size-dependent biodistribution, tunable surfaces and sensitivity to inflammatory microenvironments—create new options to address these gaps. By supporting targeted drug delivery, active immunomodulation and more precise diagnostics, nanotechnology can better connect disease mechanisms with clinical management and help develop disease-specific, personalized strategies for autoimmune disorders.

Global epidemiological landscape

Autoimmune and immune-mediated inflammatory diseases are placing an increasing burden on health systems worldwide. This rise is driven by aging populations, environmental changes, improved survival, and better case detection [1]. RA alone affected about 17.6 million people in 2020, with an age-standardized global prevalence of roughly 209 per 100,000 and a 14% increase since 1990. Women are disproportionately affected, with a female-to-male prevalence ratio close to 2.5:1 [27]. SLE also shows marked sex bias and earlier onset. A pooled analysis reported a global incidence of 5.14 per 100,000 person-years, with higher rates in women and continued excess mortality despite therapeutic progress [28]. MS illustrates how total case numbers can rise while age-standardized rates remain stable or decline slightly. Between 1990 and 2019, incident cases, deaths and DALYs all increased globally, driven largely by demographic shifts and improved survival. High-SDI regions bear the greatest rate-based burden, while lower-SDI regions are seeing rapid growth as diagnostic capacity increases and urbanization accelerates [29]. These trends underline the growing need for long-term neurological care and rehabilitation services [29]. IBD has evolved from a mainly Western disease to a global condition with staged epidemiologic patterns. A synthesis of more than 500 population-based studies across 82 regions described four stages—emergence, accelerated incidence, compounding prevalence and projected saturation—showing how countries move from low incidence to high chronic prevalence as populations age [30]. Newly industrialized regions therefore face a dual challenge: more new cases and a rapidly expanding pool of long-term survivors needing ongoing monitoring and prevention of complications [30]. Across these disorders, several themes are consistent: strong female predominance, earlier age at onset with long cumulative morbidity, and increasing absolute DALYs despite only modest changes in standardized rates [28,29]. This epidemiologic background supports the need for scalable, sensitive platforms for earlier detection, molecular subtyping, and longitudinal disease monitoring, and highlights the translational space where nano-enabled diagnostics and precision therapeutics may help change disease trajectories at both individual and population levels [1].

Shared immunopathogenic mechanisms

The core pathology of AIDs is failure of central and peripheral immune tolerance. In the thymus, defective negative selection allows autoreactive T cells to escape deletion [31]. In the periphery, B cells receive excessive co-stimulatory and cytokine signals, differentiate into plasma cells, and secrete high-affinity IgG/IgA autoantibodies [32,33]. Innate immune cells become abnormally activated through toll-like receptors (TLR) and cGAS–STING pathways, releasing interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and type I IFNs that maintain chronic inflammation [34,35]. Genetic susceptibility loci (for example HLA-DRB1×04, PTPN22), together with microbiome changes and environmental chemicals, promote the formation of neoantigens or post-translationally modified self-antigens [36,37]. This disrupts the balance of "Signal 1/Signal 2" in B and T cells and fuels a cycle of "autoantibody → complement activation → inflammation → tissue damage" [38]. Across AIDs, this loss of tolerance allows autoreactive T and B cells to survive and drive chronic inflammation and organ injury [1]. Nucleic acid–sensing pathways such as cGAS–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and endosomal TLR amplify type I IFN responses, activate APCs, and interact with inflammasome and pyroptosis pathways to sustain autoimmunity [[39], [40], [41]]. In RA, TLR and cGAS–STING activation in the synovium integrates with TNF, IL-6, IL-17 and stromal remodeling to promote pannus formation and joint destruction [42,43]. Similar innate–adaptive crosstalk underlies SLE (type I IFN–dominant signatures), MS (myelin-reactive T and B responses), and IBD (barrier damage with trained immunity), illustrating convergent pathogenic modules despite different organ targets [41,[44], [45], [46]]. These common circuits—including loss of tolerance, IFN and inflammasome amplification, and ectopic stromal–immune niches—offer mechanistic entry points for precision diagnostics and targeted delivery strategies [47].

Beyond the convergent amplification of pro-inflammatory circuits described above, failure of negative regulatory pathways is another unifying feature across AIDs. Forkhead Box P3⁺ (FOXP3⁺) Tregs normally dampen autoreactive T and B cell responses and maintain peripheral tolerance; numerical and functional Treg defects have been documented in RA, SLE, MS and other AIDs, and often correlate with disease activity [48]. In APCs, the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) pathway catabolizes tryptophan into kynurenine metabolites that suppress effector T cells and promote Treg differentiation, providing an endogenous brake on tissue-destructive inflammation [49]. The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) integrates signals from dietary and microbial metabolites to bias the Treg/T helper (Th)17 balance and restrain myeloid-cell-driven inflammation at barrier sites, and perturbations of AHR signaling have been linked to exacerbated autoimmunity in models of MS, RA, and IBD [50]. Inhibitory immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4) and PD-1 further tune T cell activation thresholds and help sustain self-tolerance; germline deficiency or therapeutic blockade of these pathways can precipitate multi-organ autoimmune syndromes, underscoring their relevance to systemic autoimmunity [51,52]. Together, these data highlight that autoimmune pathology reflects not only excessive activation of effector pathways but also erosion of multiple tolerance-maintaining systems, which should be considered when designing nanotechnologies that aim to restore immune balance rather than broadly suppress immunity.

Current diagnostic and therapeutic pain points

Here we use RA as a representative example to illustrate persistent diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that limit precision care. First, early and preclinical RA often present with mild or nonspecific symptoms. As a result, many patients are diagnosed after the "window of opportunity", when early treatment is most effective in changing disease course [[53], [54], [55]]. Second, serological heterogeneity is a major obstacle. About 20%–30% of patients are seronegative for RF and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA). In these patients, current criteria are less sensitive, and misclassification or delayed classification is common [[56], [57], [58]]. Inflammation in this subgroup can be as active as—or even more active than—in seropositive disease by clinical and ultrasound assessments, but reliable blood-based markers that reflect synovial biology are lacking [57,58]. These gaps highlight the need for composite, longitudinal biomarkers that link local tissue signals, immune phenotypes, and systemic readouts to enable earlier, serostatus-independent diagnosis [55,56].

On the treatment side, efficacy–safety trade-offs remain prominent. MTX, the conventional first-line drug, modestly increases overall infection risk in RA and requires careful risk management and individualized dosing [59]. Across biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), meta-analyses show higher serious infection risk compared with conventional agents, particularly in the first months of therapy and in patients with multiple comorbidities, complicating treat-to-target strategies [60,61]. Risk is even higher in RA with interstitial lung disease, where b/tsDMARDs are often necessary but adverse events and infections cluster in vulnerable phenotypes, calling for more refined risk stratification and monitoring [62]. Collectively, these realities expose a precision gap: current diagnostics inadequately resolve early disease biology—particularly in seronegative RA—and current therapeutics lack real-time, patient-specific safety "guardrails" that can adapt care as risk fluctuates [55,56,60]. Addressing these pain points will require technologies capable of sensitive, multiplexed detection of joint- and immune-derived signals in minimally invasive specimens, alongside dynamic risk models that fuse molecular, imaging and clinical streams to guide timely escalation or de-escalation of therapy [55,61].

Why nanotechnology is rational for AIDs

AIDs are characterized by multifocal, tissue-embedded inflammation behind endothelial barriers and dynamic microenvironments rich in ROS, proteases, acidosis, and danger-associated nucleic acids such as oxidized mitochondrial DNA in neutrophil extracellular traps, which drive type I IFN signaling [63,64]. These pathophysiologic signatures create “addresses” and triggers that nanosystems can exploit. First, inflamed tissues show enhanced vascular leakage and leukocyte accumulation. This supports passive nanoparticle accumulation through the ELVIS effect, while surface ligands can further direct delivery to pathogenic cell subsets, such as folate receptor-β (FRβ)–positive activated macrophages in synovium [65,66]. Second, stimuli-responsive carriers convert biochemical cues into on-demand drug release. ROS-, pH- or MMP-responsive designs improve joint targeting, local drug activation, and can even provide real-time readouts of treatment response [67,68]. Third, nanotechnology enables antigen-specific immune reprogramming. Tolerogenic polymeric nanoparticles that co-deliver autoantigen and rapamycin can induce durable, antigen-specific tolerance even in inflammatory settings, addressing the core limitation of broad immunosuppression [69]. Similarly, non-inflammatory mRNA–LNPs encoding disease antigens can silence encephalitogenic T cells while preserving protective immunity, offering a modular “inverse vaccine” concept that may be extended to multiple AIDs [16]. Lymph-node targeting, which is central for inducing tolerance, can be engineered by albumin “hitchhiking,” where amphiphile-linked cargos bind albumin at the injection site and are transported to draining lymph nodes to program dendritic cells [70].

Beyond therapy, nanoplatforms rationalize precision diagnostics. They enrich scarce EVs and cell-free nucleic acids that mirror organ inflammation and immune tone, providing serial, minimally invasive readouts aligned with decision-making in heterogeneous AIDs [71]. Together, these capabilities—pathology-guided targeting (ELVIS + ligands), microenvironment-coupled release/assessment, lymph-node-centric tolerization, and nano-enabled liquid biopsy—explain why nanotechnology is not merely incremental but structurally matched to the biological constraints that currently limit early detection and durable, individualized control of AID.

Design principles for nano-enabled diagnosis/therapy in AIDs

Microenvironment-responsive release is a central design lever for AID nanomedicines, with ROS-labile, pH-sensitive, or protease-cleavable linkers confining payload activation to inflamed tissues enriched in oxidative stress, acidosis and MMP activity [72]. Target specificity can be further enhanced by active ligands that recognize inflammation-enriched receptors, such as FRβ on activated macrophages, the mannose receptor (CD206), or CD44 via hyaluronan, thereby increasing cellular selectivity and reducing off-target exposure [73,74]. A balanced combination of passive, cell-mediated and ligand-guided transport, together with optimized particle size (often ∼100–200 nm for selective lesion uptake), helps reconcile lesion selectivity, circulation time, and endosomal escape for intracellular payloads [75]. Biomimetic coatings (e.g., cell-membrane or exosome cloaking) and complement-taming surface chemistries play important roles in mitigating corona-driven opsonization and infusion reactions while preserving targeting performance [76,77]. In addition, incorporating diagnostic readouts (imaging labels, liquid-biopsy capture elements or activity-sensing reporters) directly into therapeutic platforms creates theranostic systems that enable response-adaptive dosing and earlier detection of subclinical flares [78,79].

Despite the conceptual appeal of "active targeting", quantitative biodistribution studies consistently show that only a small fraction of the injected dose reaches diseased tissues. Meta-analyses of >300 preclinical nanomedicine datasets in solid tumors report mean and median delivery efficiencies of approximately 2.2% and 0.7%–0.8% of the injected dose (%ID), respectively, with values decreasing to around 0.3%–0.4%ID at 7 d after intravenous administration [80,81]. These data, although derived mainly from oncology, likely approximate the upper limit of systemic nanoparticle delivery also in inflamed autoimmune target organs, where similar vascular, stromal and phagocytic barriers exist. In most actively targeted formulations, ligand decoration improves lesion accumulation by only 1.5- to 3-fold compared with non-targeted counterparts, indicating that enhanced cellular uptake and retention rather than dramatic increases in absolute %ID are the main benefits of active targeting [81].

A key design variable is the surface ligand density. Both experimental and theoretical studies demonstrate that intermediate ligand densities maximize receptor-mediated binding and internalization, whereas very low densities provide insufficient avidity and very high densities can cause steric hindrance, receptor saturation or rapid clearance due to opsonization [[82], [83], [84]]. Systematic tuning of ligand density, together with control of size, shape, surface charge and stealth coatings, has therefore emerged as a practical strategy to improve the effective targeting efficiency of nanocarriers to diseased synovium, kidney, central nervous system (CNS) and skin in autoimmune models [85]. Integrating such design principles with quantitative pharmacokinetic modelling and organ-specific biodistribution studies in AID models will be essential to move from qualitative "active targeting" claims toward rigorously optimized formulations with measurable gains in %ID at the target site [81,85].

Nano-optical and electrochemical biosensors: capturing early immune signals

Autoimmune flares are often preceded by changes in cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ) and by the appearance or titer shifts of autoantibodies (e.g., anti-CCP, anti-dsDNA). Nano-optical platforms convert these scarce and transient signals into stable readouts with attomolar–femtomolar sensitivity and rapid response times. A state-of-the-art example is a two-dimensional semiconductor device: an asymmetric MoS₂ diode biosensor that uses built-in rectification and aptamer capture to detect TNF-α rapidly and label-free, overcoming Debye screening and delivering clinically relevant signals within minutes [86]. In parallel, RA-focused optical sensor surveys document maturing use cases across ACPA/anti-CCP, C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and IL-6, highlighting label-free LSPR, interferometry and SERS readouts that can complement or triage standard Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISAs) [87,88]. Given analytic variability in anti-dsDNA testing, nano-optical approaches offering orthogonal antigen formats and direct signal transduction could improve specificity in SLE work-ups [89].

Electrochemical aptamer platforms add mechanical flexibility and low-power operation suited to real-time use. Wearable graphene field-effect transistor sensors functionalized with cytokine aptamers maintain signal fidelity under strain and in undiluted biofluids, enabling time-resolved monitoring of TNF-α/IFN-γ during daily activity [90]. Microneedle (MN)-enabled electrochemical aptamer sensors extend this to interstitial fluid (ISF) in humans, continuously tracking small-molecule and protein dynamics with minimal lag and without reagents—an architecture directly portable to cytokines and autoantibodies relevant to AID [91]. Methodologically, aptasensor reviews synthesize advances in antifouling interfaces, redox reporters, and regeneration chemistries that stabilize baselines, compress calibration, and support multiplexing—key requirements for decentralized flare surveillance and treat-to-target feedback [92].

Existing comparative analyses also clarify the relative strengths of nano-optical versus electrochemical and transistor-based platforms when they are tailored to the same autoimmune biomarker. In RA, anti-CCP-focused biosensor surveys show that plasmonic LSPR, SPR and SERS formats generally achieve the lowest limits of detection—often down to the fg/ml range for anti-CCP or anti-MCV in microliter serum samples—but at the cost of bulkier optical setups and more demanding nanostructure fabrication, whereas electrochemical and impedimetric immunosensors typically provide ng/ml sensitivity with simpler instrumentation and easier multiplexing for routine laboratories [88,93]. For inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, aptamer-based MoS₂ diode and graphene field-effect transistor (GFET) sensors likewise deliver fM–pM detection limits with purely electrical readout; for example, an asymmetric MoS₂ diode platform detects TNF-α down to 10 fM, while an ultrathin wearable GFET aptasensor achieves limits of detection of approximately 2.75–2.89 pM for TNF-α and IFN-γ in biofluids [86,90,92]. Taken together, these data suggest that LSPR/SERS platforms are preferred when maximal analytical sensitivity and multiplexed spectral fingerprints are required in centralized diagnostic laboratories, whereas GFETs and other electrochemical aptasensors are better suited for continuous or point-of-care monitoring where compact electronics, mechanical deformability and straightforward integration with microfluidics outweigh the need for the very lowest possible detection limit [92,93].

From a translational perspective, highly sensitive nano-biosensors must maintain analytical performance in undiluted, protein-rich AID samples such as serum, synovial fluid or cerebrospinal fluid. Severe non-specific adsorption in such complex biofluids can mask low-abundance autoantibodies, cytokines or cfDNA, underscoring the need for robust antifouling surface chemistries. Recent work on zwitterionic poly(carboxybetaine) hydrogels and related ultra-low-fouling interfaces has shown that nonspecific protein adsorption from undiluted human serum can be reduced to <5 ng/cm² while simultaneously achieving high antibody loading and stable operation, providing a realistic route to clinically deployable SPR or electrochemical biosensors for autoimmune biomarker detection [94,95]. Equally important are long-term stability and inter-batch reproducibility. Zwitterionic hydrogel coatings have demonstrated preserved thickness, wettability, and antifouling performance after prolonged storage and repeated cycles of exposure to blood-derived fluids, indicating that carefully engineered crosslinked networks can support the long-term monitoring required for chronic AIDs [94]. At the same time, systematic analyses of antifouling sensing interfaces highlight that modest variations in polymer composition, surface grafting density or nanostructure geometry markedly influence fouling resistance, signal drift and assay reproducibility, emphasizing the necessity for standardized fabrication protocols, reference materials and validation in clinically relevant matrices before regulatory approval [95]. In this context, future clinical reports on autoimmune nano-biosensors should routinely disclose antifouling performance in undiluted biofluids, stability over clinically relevant time frames, and batch-to-batch variability alongside conventional analytical metrics to better benchmark their readiness for translation [95].

To make these trade-offs more explicit, we also compared the key performance characteristics of the nano-diagnostic platforms summarized in Fig. 3. Laboratory-based LSPR/SERS and other chip-based optical or electrochemical biosensors generally offer the lowest limits of detection and high analytical specificity using microliter-scale serum or plasma samples, but require well-aligned optics, trained operators and higher instrument costs, which confines their primary use to centralized laboratories and confirmatory testing [96]. In contrast, portable microfluidic devices and wearable electrochemical nanosensors accept slightly higher detection limits and more restricted multiplexing, but minimize sample preparation and sample volume and can operate with compact, low-cost readers, enabling point-of-care or at-home monitoring of cytokines and other soluble biomarkers in sweat or ISF with clinically acceptable sensitivity and specificity [97,98]. For clinicians and researchers, this means that lab-based nano-biosensors are more suitable for early diagnosis and fine-grained molecular stratification, whereas wearable nanosensors are optimized for capturing dynamic trajectories of inflammatory activity and treatment response in real-world settings.

Magnetic and multimodal imaging nanoprobes: from micro-lesions to joint panoramas

SPIO/USPIO nanoprobes translate the cellular features of synovitis—macrophage infiltration and angiogenesis—into high-contrast MRI signals, revealing micro-lesions before irreversible damage occurs. In antigen-induced arthritis and other murine autoimmune models, USPIOs cause T2/T2* signal loss within the synovium that co-localizes with iron-loaded macrophages on histology, demonstrating cellular specificity beyond conventional gadolinium-based perfusion imaging [[99], [100], [101]]. Clinically applicable iron formulations such as ferumoxytol further support translational macrophage imaging and prolonged vascular time windows for high-resolution joint mapping [102].

Moving from focal detection to "joint panoramas", fluorine-19 (^19F) MRI with perfluorocarbon-labeled nanotracers provides background-free hot-spot maps of inflammation that quantify leukocyte recruitment and monitor therapeutic response longitudinally [103]. Hybrid strategies fuse complementary contrasts—for example, nanoparticle-enabled ^19F MRI co-registered with near-infrared optical signals—yielding whole-joint coverage and molecular specificity in a single workflow [104].

Receptor-targeted radiotracers add cell-type resolution at the system level. FRβ–directed Positron Emission Tomography (PET) agents (^18F-FOL, ^68Ga-FOL) visualize activated macrophages across inflamed tissues and draining nodes, enabling pan-articular surveys that correlate with disease activity and may stratify therapy [105,106]. Finally, photoacoustic/ultrasound systems report hemoglobin content and oxygenation in small joints, capturing hyperemia–hypoxia coupling that precedes structural damage and complements MRI/PET for flare risk assessment [107]. Together, magnetic and multimodal nanoprobes progress from cell-resolved micro-lesion detection to organ-scale panoramas, creating quantitative, repeatable imaging endpoints for early diagnosis, treat-to-target titration, and objective response monitoring in autoimmune arthritis [99,[103], [104], [105],107].

Nano-enabled liquid biopsy platforms: non-invasive screening of exosomes and cfNAs

EVs and cell-free nucleic acids (cfNAs) provide minimally invasive access to inflamed tissues and immune circuits, enabling serial, organ-proximal readouts that conventional serology misses. In LN, urinary exosomal miRNAs (for example, miR-21/miR-150/miR-29c; miR-31/miR-107/miR-135b) track fibrosis programs and renal recovery, outperforming visit-based clinical indices for early risk stratification [108,109]. Complementing EVs signatures, the six-analyte Renal Activity Index for Lupus (RAIL; Neutrophil Gelatinase-Associated Lipocalin, Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1), Kidney Injury Molecule-1 (KIM-1), adiponectin, hemopexin, ceruloplasmin) consistently discriminates high histologic activity and captures treatment responses, often surpassing renal SLEDAI, positioning urine as a practical "liquid biopsy" for longitudinal control [[110], [111], [112]].

Nanotechnology strengthens EVs and cfNA analysis at the pre-analytical step. Microfluidic chips that incorporate nanostructured capture layers (e.g., graphene oxide/PDA) or combine them with immunomagnetic enrichment markedly improve exosome capture, reduce non-specific binding, and support in-situ multiplexed measurements from microliter sample volumes—features that are crucial for routine monitoring [113,114]. In parallel, magnetic nanoparticles streamline nucleic-acid extraction from complex biofluids, stabilizing yields and enabling high-throughput cfDNA/cfRNA workflows compatible with point-of-care automation [115].

Mechanistic data on cfDNA in systemic autoimmunity further support its biomarker value: oxidized mitochondrial DNA in neutrophil extracellular traps is a strong inducer of type I IFN, a central axis in SLE, linking cfDNA quantity and quality to pathogenic activity [64]. Reviews across autoimmune rheumatic diseases further document associations between cfDNA levels and activity, and outline analytical caveats that nano-enabled methods can mitigate [116]. Beyond concentration, fragmentomic and methylomic features of cfDNA report tissue-of-origin and chromatin state, opening routes to organ-specific surveillance when integrated with EVs cargo profiling [117]. Together, EVs and cfNA liquid biopsies—amplified by micro/nanostructured capture, immunomagnetics, and multimodal analysis—offer a scalable framework for earlier detection, flare prediction, and adaptive therapy in AID.

Nevertheless, because virtually every tissue sheds vesicles and nucleic acids into the circulation, lesion-derived EVs/cfNA signals are often diluted by systemically derived background, particularly in early or smoldering inflammation. Improving analytical specificity therefore requires technologies that can either enrich vesicles from the relevant tissue compartment or deconvolve their origin. Immunoaffinity and aptamer-based nano/microfluidic platforms can capture EVs subpopulations that express cell- or tissue-restricted surface markers; for example, L1CAM-immunoprecipitation has been used to enrich neuronal EVs from plasma, and chip-based capture of CD63/PTK7-positive exosomes achieves capture efficiencies on the order of 107–108 particles/mL with short processing times, illustrating the feasibility of selectively enriching rare EVs subsets from complex biofluids [[118], [119], [120]]. Building on these concepts, autoimmune-focused EVs studies increasingly stratify vesicles by surface phenotypes and cargo signatures in diseases such as SLE and primary Sjögren’s syndrome, supporting the notion that synovial, renal, or CNS lesion-derived EVs could be similarly targeted using combinations of canonical lineage markers and disease-associated adhesion molecules or chemokine receptors [121]. For cfDNA and cfRNA, combining size selection with epigenetic or fragmentomic profiling to infer tissue-of-origin offers a complementary route to suppress systemic noise and prioritize signal from inflamed organs; such frameworks, already piloted in oncology and transplantation, could be adapted to autoimmune indications to further enhance the specificity of nano-enabled liquid biopsy readouts.

Portable and wearable nano-diagnostics: extending real-time scenarios

Autoimmune activity can fluctuate over hours to days, so moving from occasional blood draws to continuous, near-patient monitoring is logical. MN patches that sample dermal ISF now support on-needle immunoassays amplified by plasmonic nanolabels, enabling ultrasensitive, longitudinal measurement of cytokines such as IL-6 directly in vivo with sufficient performance for flare detection and treatment monitoring [122]. Flexible, skin-conformal GFET nanosensors functionalized with cytokine aptamers (e.g., TNF-α, IFN-γ) maintain signal integrity under strain, allowing continuous molecular tracking during daily activities [90]. Complementarily, textile-like electrochemical fabrics built from carbon-nanotube/graphene fibers realize aptamer-based IL-6 monitoring in sweat, pointing to unobtrusive, washable form factors [123]. In human studies, a sweat-sensing wearable continuously measured TNF-α and correlated sweat with serum dynamics, demonstrating remote inflammation surveillance in chronic disease—an approach readily transferrable to AIDs [124].

Portable readers close the loop between nanosensors and decision-making. Smartphone-integrated lateral/vertical-flow platforms now quantify autoimmune serology at the point of need; for example, a duplex vertical-flow assay detects anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) and anti-dsDNA with ELISA-comparable performance, supporting home or clinic triage in SLE [125], while broader smartphone-based lymphocyte function-associated antigen (LFA) optics standardize quantitative readouts across labels and assays [126]. Engineering guidance from peripheral-biofluid science clarifies key pitfalls—partitioning and lag between blood and sweat/ISF, motion artifacts, and biofouling—and maps solutions via microfluidic sweat routing, on-skin preconcentration, antifouling coatings, and in situ calibration [127,128]. Together, MN-ISF analytics, deformable GFET textiles, and phone-read LFA/ Vertebral Fracture Assessment (VFA) establish an interoperable nano-diagnostic stack that can stream cytokines/autoantibodies to cloud algorithms, trigger just-in-time labs or imaging, and personalize steroid or DMARD titration in real world settings. The result is an actionable, continuous phenotype of immune tone that extends precision care beyond the infusion chair and clinic walls [90,122,124,125].

Despite these advances, a major translational limitation of wearable cytokine nanosensors is that the quantitative relationship between cytokine concentrations in dermal ISF or sweat and those in serum remains incompletely defined. Partitioning of proteins between blood and interstitial compartments is governed by local vascular permeability, diffusion and lymphatic clearance, and even for small metabolites such as glucose, continuous monitoring studies have revealed noticeable temporal delays between blood and ISF signals that must be accounted for in data interpretation [129,130]. Recent reviews of dermal ISF diagnostics and sweat biosensing further caution that, for most analytes, including inflammatory cytokines, sweat or ISF levels and their temporal dynamics cannot yet be assumed to directly mirror systemic concentrations, and their physiological relevance to established clinical end points remains to be rigorously validated [[131], [132], [133]]. As a result, current wearable platforms are best viewed as tools for tracking relative trends or local inflammatory changes rather than direct surrogates of disease activity, and longitudinal studies with paired blood–sweat/ISF sampling in patients with AIDs will be essential before cytokine readouts from these devices can be used for routine clinical decision-making [132,133].

Intelligent multimodal integration: future blueprint of AI-enhanced nano-diagnostics

With massive datasets from high-throughput sensor arrays and multimodal imaging, deep learning is becoming the engine for interpreting nano-diagnostic results [93]. Recent cross-domain models integrate SERS spectra from exosomes, MRI grayscale values, and serum electrochemical signals to build interpretable "nano-immuno signatures", achieving about 92% diagnostic accuracy across five AIDs in retrospective cohorts and predicting disease progression risk within 6 months [134,135]. Several recent clinical and translational studies already illustrate how such AI–nanotechnology convergence can address concrete diagnostic challenges in AIDs. An AI-assisted plasmonic surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) platform built on urchin-like gold nanoarchitectures, combined with machine-learning classifiers, can distinguish RA from osteoarthritis and stratify RA severity from human synovial fluid spectra with high clinical sensitivity and specificity [136]. In SLE, serum SERS fingerprints acquired on Au@Ag–porous silicon substrates, analysed by deep-learning models, have enabled non-invasive SLE screening with high diagnostic accuracy [134]. For primary Sjögren’s disease, Raman hyperspectroscopy of saliva integrated with machine-learning algorithms effectively distinguishes patients from healthy and radiation-treated controls, pointing to a rapid, non-invasive nano-optical screening strategy for this underdiagnosed autoimmune sicca syndrome [137]. These representative case studies, together with additional AI-augmented nano-diagnostic examples summarized in Table 3, highlight that AI-enhanced nano-diagnostics are already transitioning from conceptual frameworks to practical tools in autoimmune clinics. In the future, linking electronic medical records, multi-omics, and real-time wearable data will allow AI systems to create "digital-twin" patient models that help craft precise intervention plans [138]. Concurrently, blockchain encryption is being applied to protect nano-diagnostic data privacy, creating a trusted pathway from home devices to hospital cloud platforms [139]. In summary, multimodality plus intelligence is the key step for nano-diagnostics to evolve from a mere "tool" into a "decision-making engine" (Fig. 3).

While deep learning and other machine learning approaches can extract complex patterns from multi-omics profiles, imaging data and longitudinal clinical records, their decision processes often remain a "black box" to clinicians and regulators, which can undermine trust, accountability and legal acceptability in high-stakes settings such as immunomodulatory therapy selection for AIDs [140]. Explainable AI (XAI) frameworks have therefore emerged to increase transparency by either using inherently interpretable models or providing post hoc explanations for black-box models, and systematic reviews in healthcare and medical image analysis emphasize that XAI is central to building clinician trust, supporting shared decision-making, and satisfying ethical and regulatory expectations [[140], [141], [142]]. At the same time, critical viewpoints caution that many current explanation techniques may be unstable or misleading, and that their outputs must be rigorously evaluated for faithfulness and clinical usefulness rather than accepted at face value, especially when guiding patient-level decisions [143]. In the context of nano-enabled diagnosis and therapy for AIDs, prioritizing explainable or interpretable AI models can help identify which imaging features, immune cell subsets, cytokine signatures, or nanoparticle design parameters drive a given prediction, thereby not only enhancing clinician confidence but also generating mechanistic hypotheses about disease pathways and nanomaterial–immune system interactions [141,144]. Consequently, future AI pipelines for autoimmune nanomedicine should explicitly incorporate XAI methods, prospectively assess their fidelity and impact on user trust, and report both predictive performance and explanation quality to facilitate safe clinical translation and meaningful biological discovery [140,142].

Representative nanotechnology-based diagnostic platforms for AIDs

Beyond the general principles outlined above, several nanotechnology-based diagnostic platforms have already been implemented in specific AID settings, illustrating how these approaches can be translated into practice. In RA, a tilted-fiber Bragg grating (TFBG) optical biosensor has been fabricated by etching the fiber to 50 µm and self-assembling AuNPs and CCP layers on the surface to capture anti-CCP antibodies; this label-free device achieved a detection limit of 1 ng/ml for CCP antigen with a total measurement time of about 4 min in vitro, highlighting the potential of nanophotonic fiber sensors for rapid RA screening and disease monitoring [145]. Beyond this single platform, a recent comprehensive review summarized a growing pipeline of anti-CCP biosensors that exploit fluorescence, chemiluminescence, SERS, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), electrochemical transduction and lateral-flow formats, many of which rely on nanostructured electrodes, metal nanoparticles or nanostructured films to amplify signals and lower detection limits for RA-specific autoantibodies in clinical samples [88].

Anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) autoantibodies, a serological hallmark of SLE, have also been targeted by nano-enabled electrochemical immunosensors. Fagúndez and colleagues described a method in which electrodes are modified with immobilized dsDNA and coupled to an amperometric readout, enabling quantitative measurement of anti-dsDNA antibodies present at elevated levels in sera from AID patients; once the nanostructured electrodes and calibration curves are prepared, individual measurements in test samples can be completed in as little as 35 min using relatively inexpensive potentiostats, underscoring the feasibility of miniaturizable point-of-care devices for SLE-related autoantibody profiling [146].

In MS, ultrasmall SPIO (USPIO) nanoparticles have been evaluated as inflammatory MRI contrast agents that report cellular monocyte and macrophage infiltration rather than solely blood–brain barrier breakdown. In a phase II clinical trial of relapsing–remitting MS, administration of the dextran-coated USPIO SHU555C yielded 188 USPIO-positive lesions compared with 59 gadolinium-enhancing lesions, often revealing inflammatory activity that either preceded gadolinium enhancement or persisted after gadolinium signal had resolved, thereby providing complementary information on lesion biology and refining the detection and staging of CNS autoimmunity [147].

Natural nanovesicles can also serve as diagnostic carriers in AIDs. In LN, urinary exosomes—nanoscale vesicles released by renal epithelial cells into the urine—were isolated and characterized as biomarker reservoirs; miR-29c expression in urinary exosomes showed a strong negative correlation with the histological chronicity index and glomerular sclerosis and predicted early progression to renal fibrosis with an area under the curve of 0.946, supporting exosome-based miRNA signatures as non-invasive tools to stratify renal involvement and prognosis in SLE [148]. Together, these examples show how nanoengineered biosensors, nanoparticle-enhanced imaging agents and biologically derived nanovesicles are already being deployed across RA, SLE, MS and LN, moving nanotechnology-based diagnostics from conceptual frameworks toward clinically actionable platforms in AID management.

Clinical scenarios and comparative advantages of nano-diagnostics

To clarify their practical value, the nano-diagnostic platforms described above are best viewed as complements to, rather than replacements for, current laboratory and imaging tests. Nano-optical and electrochemical biosensors are particularly relevant in early or seronegative disease, in pre-clinical at-risk populations, and for tight treat-to-target monitoring, where their very low detection limits, multiplexing capability, and small sample volumes can detect subtle cytokine or autoantibody changes before they become apparent on standard ELISA or chemiluminescent assays. Magnetic and multimodal imaging nanoprobes are most useful when conventional ultrasound or radiography cannot fully explain persistent symptoms, or when quantitative whole-joint or organ inflammation needs to be followed over time; their incremental advantage lies in cell-specific contrast and micro-lesion detection. Nano-enabled liquid biopsy platforms (for EVs and cfDNA/cfRNA) are particularly helpful in organ-threatening AIDs, such as LN or RA-associated interstitial lung disease, where repeated tissue biopsies are impractical, because nanoscale enrichment improves sensitivity and allows more frequent, minimally invasive monitoring. Finally, wearable and point-of-care nano-diagnostics extend care to patients receiving biologics or Janus Kinase (JAK) inhibitors who require close flare and safety surveillance but live far from tertiary centres, converting episodic clinic-based measurements into continuous trajectories of inflammatory activity. Together, these clarifications specify when and why each nano-diagnostic modality is needed and highlight their concrete advantages over current practice.

Targeted drug-delivery systems: from passive leakage to active homing

Historically, most therapeutic nanomedicines for AIDs have used conventional backbones such as long-circulating liposomes and biodegradable polymeric nanoparticles to reformulate glucocorticoids or MTX. In collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) and EAE models, liposomal dexamethasone or methylprednisolone markedly increased drug accumulation in inflamed joints or CNS lesions and achieved sustained suppression of disease at lower cumulative steroid doses than free drug [[152], [153], [154]]. Similarly, Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-based MTX nanoparticles reduced arthritis scores and renal toxicity while rebalancing Th17/Treg responses compared with equivalent free MTX dosing in CIA mice [155]. These first-generation platforms demonstrate that even without sophisticated stimulus-responsiveness, nanoscale reformulation can improve the therapeutic index of standard immunosuppressants, but they also highlight residual class-specific adverse effects, complement activation by certain liposomal chemistries, and relatively simple release kinetics that may not fully match the spatial and temporal dynamics of autoimmune flares [71].

In autoimmune lesions, vascular leakiness and leukocyte trafficking create an ELVIS-like gateway that passively enriches nanoscale carriers within inflamed tissues; however, inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity of perfusion and drainage makes “leakage” alone unreliable, motivating active homing layered on top of passive deposition [65]. Active homing exploits disease-set adhesion molecules and myeloid phenotypes—for example, ligands to intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1/ vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1 on activated endothelium and FRβ on inflammatory macrophages—thereby concentrating payloads within synovium or other target organs while sparing bystander tissues [65,66]. Beyond ligand–receptor targeting, biomimetic coatings (e.g., leukocyte membranes) and albumin "hitchhiking" routes improve margination, immune evasion and lymph-node access, positioning tolerance-oriented cargos in the anatomic hubs where antigen presentation is decided [70]. Compared with these conventional nanomedicines, ligand-directed and biomimetic carriers deliberately trade higher formulation and characterization complexity for greater lesion selectivity and cell-type specificity; their clinical value will depend on whether this added design sophistication translates into meaningful gains in efficacy, safety or dosing convenience across heterogeneous patient populations [71].

Once at the site, microenvironment-responsive chemistries convert inflammatory cues into controlled release. Reactive-oxygen-species–labile backbones, pH-cleavable linkers, and matrix metalloproteinase–degradable gates sharpen spatiotemporal control, reducing systemic exposure and rescuing drugs with narrow therapeutic windows [67,68]. Such designs are particularly attractive for steroids, JAK inhibitors, and siRNA/ASO (Antisense Oligonucleotide) payloads where on-target efficacy and off-target toxicity are tightly coupled [67,68]. In parallel, antigen-specific immune re-education becomes feasible when polymeric nanoparticles co-display autoantigen and tolerogenic cues (e.g., rapamycin), yielding durable, antigen-restricted hyporesponsiveness instead of global immunosuppression [69]. The same logic extends to non-inflammatory mRNA–LNPs encoding disease antigens, which silence encephalitogenic T cells while preserving protective immunity, offering a modular “inverse-vaccine” chassis adaptable across autoimmune settings [16].

Finally, theranostic variants integrate imaging or sensor elements to quantify delivery, drug release, and response in situ, closing the loop between targeting and decision-making for treat-to-target care [65]. Collectively, stacking passive enrichment with receptor-guided homing, lymph-node routing, and stimulus-locked release reframes nanomedicine as a precision pharmacology system matched to the anatomic, cellular, and biochemical constraints of AID [[65], [66], [67],70].

Tolerogenic nanoplatforms: rebuilding self recognition

Antigen-specific nanotherapies aim to extinguish pathogenic immunity while preserving host defense by coupling three levers: (1) re-educating dendritic cells toward a tolerogenic state, (2) deleting or anergizing autoreactive B/T clones, and (3) expanding tissue-protective Tregs. A mature pillar is rapamycin-based tolerogenic nanoparticles (tNPs): biodegradable polymers co-encapsulating autoantigen and rapamycin induce durable, antigen-restricted tolerance even in the presence of strong innate adjuvants, outperforming matched free-drug regimens and progressing toward human translation [69,156]. Next-generation variants (ImmTOR) further widen the therapeutic window and synergize with Treg-selective IL-2 muteins to stabilize tolerance circuits, highlighting modularity for different autoimmune contexts [157].

Lymph-node targeting increases the probability that antigen meets decision-making APCs with minimal inflammatory co-signals. Albumin "hitchhiking" amphiphiles chaperone cargo from the injection site into draining nodes; when paired with tolerogenic cues, this architecture focuses instruction where fate decisions are made [70]. In parallel, B-cell–centric strategies directly silence autoantibody sources: Siglec-engaging tolerance-inducing antigenic liposomes (STALs) co-display antigen and CD22 ligands to drive apoptosis/anergy of cognate B cells, preventing recall responses without global suppression [158]. Apoptotic-mimicry phosphatidylserine (PS) liposomes exploit efferocytosis pathways (MerTK/Axl) to promote regulatory antigen presentation and dampen arthritis activity in vivo, illustrating a clinically intuitive route for packaging native self-antigens [159].

Finally, nucleic-acid platforms enable programmable tolerance. Non-inflammatory mRNA–LNPs that encode disease antigens are translated on splenic dendritic cells without co-stimulation, suppressing encephalitogenic T cells yet preserving protective immunity—a blueprint for individualized "inverse vaccines" across AIDs [16]. Together, these tolerogenic nanoplatforms—rapamycin tNPs, lymph-node-targeted amphiphiles, STALs, PS-mimetics, and non-inflammatory mRNA-LNPs—rebuild "self" recognition by aligning delivery microanatomy, cell-subset specificity, and context-appropriate signaling, offering durable control without the collateral costs of blanket immunosuppression [16,69,70,158]. At the same time, these platforms are constrained by the need to correctly identify dominant autoantigens in each disease, the potential for epitope spreading or antigenic evolution to erode long-term control, and the possibility that deeply tolerized responses may be more difficult to rapidly reverse in the setting of severe infection or malignancy, which argues for built-in mechanisms to tune dosing, monitor antigenic breadth and, when necessary, reawaken protective immunity [71].

Exosome- and biomembrane-coated carriers: natural barriers, precise delivery

Exosomes are nanoscale EVs enriched in lipids, proteins and nucleic acids that mediate intercellular communication and, when leveraged as drug carriers, offer inherent biocompatibility and signaling functions advantageous for treating autoimmune inflammation (Fig. 4) [160,161]. Cell membrane–coated nanoparticles (CM-NPs) cloak synthetic cores with natural plasma membranes to endow them with “self” markers, immune evasion and disease-relevant adhesion/chemotaxis, thereby improving precision delivery in inflamed tissues [162,163]. The in vivo fate of EVs is strongly influenced by their cellular origin and administration route, and these features can be exploited to optimize biodistribution and therapeutic index in autoimmune settings [164,165]. Red blood cell (RBC) membrane–camouflaged nanoparticles retain proteins such as CD47, reduce opsonization and prolong circulation, offering a stealth chassis for sustained anti-inflammatory drug exposure [162,166]. In inflammatory arthritis models, neutrophil membrane–coated nanoparticles selectively accumulate in the synovium, sequester pro-inflammatory cytokines, penetrate cartilage and alleviate joint damage, highlighting the value of leveraging innate homing cues [167]. Engineered neutrophil-derived exosomes functionalized with ultrasmall Prussian blue nanozymes home to inflamed joints, scavenge ROS, and mitigate synovitis and cartilage injury, illustrating how "living" biomembranes can be upgraded for redox-driven pathologies in RA [168]. Beyond joints, mesenchymal stromal cell–derived EVs modulate innate and adaptive immunity to ameliorate LN in preclinical studies, underscoring the potential of EVs immunoreprogramming across autoimmune organ targets [169,170]. The integrity and degree of membrane coating critically determine cellular uptake of CM-NPs, with highly coated particles internalizing individually, while partially coated ones rely on cooperative aggregation, which informs manufacturing quality controls for consistent targeting in vivo [171]. Exosome loading can be achieved via passive incubation, electroporation, sonication, and chemical conjugation, but electroporation may artifactually precipitate siRNA and overestimate loading, warranting careful methodological validation [172,173]. State-of-the-art reviews consolidate best practices for exosome cargo loading and surface functionalization, emphasizing method choice to balance loading efficiency, vesicle integrity, and targeting specificity for immune indications [173,174]. Membrane engineering with disease-relevant ligands can amplify tropism. For example, peptide-modified neutrophil-membrane nanodrugs target synovial macrophages and reprogram polarization to quell arthritis while reducing off-target toxicity [167,175]. Comparatively, native exosomes provide multifactorial biological signals (e.g., miRNAs, proteins) that can recalibrate dysregulated immune networks, while CM-NPs offer modular payload capacity and scalable manufacturing; selecting between them should reflect disease biology, dose needs, and process control [163,174]. Across platforms, adherence to MISEV2023 guidance on EVs definition, characterization (positive/negative markers), dosing metrics, and reporting is essential to ensure reproducibility and translational confidence for autoimmune nanotherapies [176].

Nevertheless, exosome- and cell membrane-coated nanocarriers also inherit the inherent heterogeneity of their biological source, leading to marked batch-to-batch variability in membrane protein, lipid, and glycan composition, which can alter opsonization, biodistribution, and immunomodulatory activity [177,178]. Recent reviews therefore emphasize defining and controlling critical quality attributes for the membrane component, including donor or cell line selection, passage number, culture conditions, membrane isolation protocol, and coat-to-core ratio, combined with systematic characterization of size distribution, particle concentration, zeta potential, and surface marker expression by orthogonal methods such as nanoparticle tracking analysis, high-resolution flow cytometry, electron microscopy, and omics profiling [[178], [179], [180]]. In the EV field, the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2018 and updated MISEV2023) guidelines have already set minimal requirements for documenting source, isolation, characterization and functional assays to improve reproducibility across laboratories, and similar principles should be adopted when engineering exosome and CM-NPs for clinical translation [178,181]. Implementing such standardized quality-control frameworks, with in-process controls for membrane coating efficiency and final product release criteria, will be essential to reduce inter-batch variability and ensure that biomimetic coatings confer consistent targeting and tolerogenic properties in AID applications [179,180]. Thus, while biomembrane-coated systems offer attractive opportunities for immune evasion and microenvironment-appropriate trafficking, they also exemplify how leveraging biological complexity inevitably introduces additional variables that must be tightly controlled for any future clinical product [71].

Nano-immunomodulation and "inverse vaccines"

Tolerogenic nanovaccines aim to reboot immune tolerance rather than blanket immunosuppression [17]. Recent work co-loaded ROS-scavenging lignin nanoparticles with myelin autoantigens, markedly reducing pathology scores and expanding FOXP3⁺ Tregs in the EAE model [182]. Another breakthrough is the 1-methylpseudouridine-modified mRNA LNP “inverse vaccine”, which presents antigen on splenic dendritic cells without activating TLR7/8 and suppresses multiple autoantigen-driven inflammatory responses; preclinical and early clinical studies confirm its safety and broad tolerogenic potential [16]. Encoding multiple antigens/epitopes in smart delivery shells may enable individualized, precisely tuned down-regulation of autoimmunity [183]. AIDs such as celiac disease, RA and T1D are chronic inflammatory disorders caused by abnormal immune responses to self-antigens. A nanoemulsion (NE) coupling system based on apoptotic red blood cell membranes (SCM), referred to as Smart Nanoemulsion (SNE), has been developed for the treatment of AIDs. This system achieves antigen delivery targeted to the liver, enhancing the negative regulatory function of hepatic APCs and effectively inducing immune tolerance. Studies have shown that the SNE system significantly increases the accumulation of antigens in hepatic APCs, suppresses the expression of costimulatory molecules on APCs, and promotes the generation of Tregs and regulatory B cells (Bregs). Although antigen-specific inverse vaccines and tNPs are conceptually designed to avoid the global immunosuppression seen with conventional therapies, their long-term safety profiles remain incompletely defined and warrant careful scrutiny, particularly with regard to susceptibility to infections and impaired tumor immunosurveillance. Preclinical inverse vaccination strategies using PLGA particles co-encapsulating autoantigens and IL-10 have shown durable disease attenuation in EAE with minimal acute toxicity over the study period, but these mouse models do not address late effects on host defence or neoplasia and are typically limited to weeks of follow-up [184]. For RNA-based inverse vaccines formulated in LNPs, emerging data indicate that LNPs can engage multiple innate immune sensors, including TLRs, Retinoic Acid-Inducible Gene I–like receptors and inflammasomes, and may theoretically contribute to autoimmune phenomena if not carefully engineered to minimize unintended immune activation [185]. In addition, clinical experience with mRNA–LNP vaccines has demonstrated that PEGylated lipids can boost pre-existing anti-PEG IgG and IgM, with higher anti-PEG antibody titers correlating with increased systemic reactogenicity and raising concerns about altered pharmacokinetics or hypersensitivity to other PEGylated biologics in sensitized individuals [186]. These observations highlight several potential risk domains for tolerogenic nano-immunotherapies, including increased vulnerability to opportunistic infections, impaired anti-tumor immunity, anti-carrier or anti-PEG antibody induction, and off-target autoantibody formation. Accordingly, future clinical development of inverse vaccines and tNPs in AIDs should incorporate extended safety follow-up and dedicated pharmacovigilance, including systematic monitoring of infection rates, malignancy incidence, responses to routine vaccines, anti-drug/anti-PEG antibodies and de novo autoantibodies, as well as evaluation of dose-sparing or transiently acting formulations that aim to achieve disease control without irreversible immune deviation [185,186].

An important but often underappreciated issue for tolerogenic nanotherapies in chronic AIDs is the reversibility of induced tolerance, particularly during acute infections or in the context of vaccination. Because most nanoparticle-based inverse vaccines couple a restricted set of disease-relevant autoantigens with tolerogenic cues, they are designed to suppress pathogenic clones while preserving global immune competence and responses to unrelated microbial antigens, in contrast to broad immunosuppressants that increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections [156,187,188]. However, preclinical and early clinical studies rarely report how quickly tolerance wanes after treatment cessation or how efficiently protective immunity can be re-engaged if over-suppression contributes to severe infection [[187], [188], [189]]. Conceptually, reversibility can be promoted by exploiting the biodegradability and finite persistence of nanocarriers, by titrating antigen load and dosing intervals, and by reserving temporary immunostimulatory interventions to override regulatory circuits only in life-threatening situations where breaking tolerance is justified [156,187,188]. Future tolerogenic nanomedicines should therefore be engineered with tunable pharmacokinetics, antigen payloads and Tregs dependency, and their development programs should explicitly evaluate durability, reversibility and "rescue" strategies to balance long-term remission with the ability to re-establish robust host defense [188,189].

Multifunctional responsive platforms: concurrent anti-inflammation, antioxidation and barrier repair

Autoimmune lesions couple cytokine storms with oxidative stress and barrier failure; single-axis therapies rarely re-establish homeostasis. Multifunctional nanoplatforms therefore combine catalytic ROS scavenging (“nanozymes”), stimulus-locked anti-inflammatory release, and tissue-repair cues to interrupt these feed-forward loops. Ceria- or Prussian-blue–based nanozymes provide self-regenerating superoxide/catalase-mimetic activity and dampen colitis pathology; Au–ceria hybrids and polyvinylpyrrolidone-coated Prussian blue reduced mucosal injury and inflammatory cytokines in vivo [190,191]. Hollow MnO₂ nanozymes add two synergies—consuming H₂O₂ while releasing O₂ to relieve hypoxia—and can co-carry budesonide, yielding superior control of IBD versus either function alone [192]. Conceptually, catalytic nanotherapy for IBD illustrates how redox homeostasis restoration amplifies anti-inflammatory efficacy [193].

On the anti-inflammatory axis, disease-microenvironment triggers are harnessed to confine drug activity. ROS-responsive cyclodextrin nanoparticles deliver dexamethasone to inflamed joints and suppress the iRhom2–TNF-α–BAFF (B-Cell Activating Factor) axis in arthritis, reducing systemic exposure while preserving potency [67]. At the gut interface, the field-defining thioketal polymer degrades in ROS-rich foci to release TNF-α siRNA after oral dosing, achieving site-specific gene silencing across inflamed intestine [194].

Repairing the barrier completes the triad. Hyaluronic-acid–based antibacterial hydrogels restore tight-junction proteins (Zonula Occludens-1, occludin/claudin-5), MUC2 and rebalance microbiota in colitis models, directly addressing epithelial leak and dysbiosis [195]. In parallel, epitheliotrophic cytokine agonism offers an orthogonal, non-immunosuppressive repair lever: IL-22–Fc (efmarodocokin alfa) shows target engagement and clinical activity signals in early trials, although larger studies indicate mixed efficacy and underscore the need for biomarker-guided stratification [196,197]. While multifunctional platforms that integrate targeting ligands, imaging agents, stimuli-responsive linkers and drug combinations offer compelling "nanoscale multifunctionality", each additional component increases synthetic complexity, characterization requirements and potential failure modes, raising the bar for robust manufacturing and regulatory approval [198]. Analyses of multifunctional nanoparticles in oncology have emphasized that the incremental clinical benefit of adding targeting or imaging functions is often modest relative to the disproportionate increase in formulation complexity, cost and regulatory scrutiny, arguing for careful cost–benefit assessment of each added function rather than maximal design sophistication [198]. More broadly, translational reviews highlight that complex nanomedicines face particular challenges in large-scale reproducible production, batch release testing, and long-term stability, and that these hurdles have contributed to the limited number of clinically approved products despite thousands of preclinical reports [199,200]. The concept of “clinical multifunctionality” further underscores that a given nanomedicine should be judged by the diversity of clinically useful roles it can realistically assume, not by the number of molecular modules attached to a single particle [201]. In the context of AIDs, prioritizing streamlined designs that add only those functions which clearly improve patient-relevant outcomes, while remaining compatible with scalable manufacturing and straightforward quality control, is likely to accelerate the real-world translation of multifunctional nano-platforms [199,201].

Preclinical translation and safety evaluation: the final mile from bench to bed

Closing the translational gap for nanotherapies in AIDs requires a dual focus on manufacturability and immunological safety. First, physicochemical identity (size/PDI, surface charge, ligand density, release kinetics) must be locked under GMP-like controls, with rigorous endotoxin assessment; importantly, nanoparticles can both carry endotoxin and interfere with limulus amebocyte lysate assays, risking false negatives/positives unless orthogonal tests are used [202]. Robust, bias-resistant animal studies following ARRIVE 2.0 (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments; randomization, blinding, a priori sample-size, preregistered outcomes) are mandatory to ensure credible effect sizes before scale-up [203,204].

Second, immune liabilities must be mapped early. Complement activation–related pseudoallergy (CARPA) and infusion reactions can emerge on first exposure; a porcine infusion model recapitulates hemodynamic and cardiopulmonary signatures, and enables mitigation testing (slow/stepwise infusion, antihistamines, C inhibitors) [205,206]. Pre-existing anti–PEG antibodies are common and can trigger both acute reactions and the “accelerated blood clearance” phenomenon on redosing, collapsing exposure; baseline anti-PEG IgG/IgM screening, alternative stealth chemistries, or non-PEG surfacing should be prospectively considered [[207], [208], [209], [210]].

Third, species differences complicate extrapolation of efficacy and safety thresholds. Cross-species transcriptomic analyses of inflammatory responses have produced conflicting conclusions, underscoring the need to triangulate across mouse strains, humanized models, and, where justified, large animals, with human-relevant biomarkers (e.g., complement split products, cytokine panels) embedded from the outset [211,212]. Finally, define go/no-go criteria that link exposure (AUC/Cmax), on-target engagement, and immune safety margins, and pair them with “first-in-human” mitigation bundles (dose fractionation, slow infusion, premedication, bedside complement/cytokine monitoring). Such programmatic discipline—standardized reporting, validated immunotoxicology, and species-aware biomarker bridges—turns promising nanosystems into credible clinical candidates for precision immunotherapy.

Marketed nanotechnology-based therapeutics for AIDs

Although most nanotechnology-based interventions for AIDs are still in preclinical or early clinical development, a few nano-enabled formulations have already reached the market and provide proof-of-concept for clinical translation. One representative example is certolizumab pegol (Cimzia®), a PEGylated Fab′ fragment of an anti–TNF-α monoclonal antibody in which a 40-kDa PEG chain is covalently attached to the antibody fragment. This polymer–protein conjugate increases plasma half-life, allows dosing every 2–4 weeks, and may enhance distribution to inflamed tissues. In the phase III FAST4WARD trial (Phase III Trial of Certolizumab Pegol Monotherapy in Rheumatoid Arthritis), certolizumab pegol monotherapy given every 4 weeks significantly improved American College of Rheumatology response rates and reduced disease activity in patients with active RA who had failed prior DMARDs, supporting its approval for RA and other immune-mediated inflammatory arthritides [213]. Another marketed nanotechnology-based product is peginterferon beta-1a (Plegridy®), in which IFN β−1a is conjugated to a large PEG moiety to extend its half-life and permit subcutaneous administration every two or 4 weeks for relapsing–remitting MS. In the Phase III Trial of PEGylated ylated IFN β−1a in Relapsing–Remitting Multiple Sclerosis (ADVANCE) study, peginterferon β−1a administered every 2 weeks significantly reduced annualized relapse rate, MRI lesion burden and risk of disability progression compared with placebo, with a safety profile similar to conventional IFNs [214]. At the mucosal surface, a cationic NE of cyclosporine A 0.1% (cyclosporine A cationic emulsion, Ikervis®) exemplifies a marketed nanotechnology-based immunomodulator for immune-mediated ocular surface disease, including dry eye associated with autoimmune disorders such as Sjögren syndrome. This oil-in-water cationic NE increases residence time and bioavailability of cyclosporine on the ocular surface; in the phase III SANSIKA trial, once-daily cyclosporine A cationic emulsion significantly improved corneal fluorescein staining scores and reduced ocular surface inflammation in patients with severe keratitis due to dry eye disease, with acceptable tolerability [215]. Collectively, these polymer–protein conjugates and cationic NEs illustrate how clinically approved nanotechnology can enhance pharmacokinetics, tissue targeting, and patient convenience in the management of autoimmune and immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, and they provide a translational framework for more sophisticated, disease-targeted nanocarriers discussed elsewhere in this review.

Nanotechnology in rare AIDs: current progress and unmet needs

Rare AIDs such as primary Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis and ANCA-associated vasculitis have a low prevalence but a disproportionately high burden of morbidity and mortality, and many still rely on broad immunosuppression rather than targeted or curative therapies. From a nanomedicine perspective, these conditions provide stringent testbeds for organ-targeted delivery, fibrosis-modifying strategies and antigen-specific tolerogenic approaches, yet the evidence base remains much sparser than for prototypic AIDs.

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome illustrates how nanocarriers can be used to localize potent immunosuppressants to exocrine glands. A rapamycin-binding protein polymer nanoparticle based on elastin-like polypeptides (FSI) significantly suppressed lymphocytic infiltration in the lacrimal glands of non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice while reducing systemic toxicity and more effectively lowering the tear biomarker cathepsin S than free rapamycin, highlighting the potential of locally acting nano-immunosuppressants in this rare disease [216]. More recently, exosomes derived from stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED-exos), used as "natural nanoparticles", have been shown to increase saliva flow, reduce lymphocytic foci and dampen inflammatory cytokine production in NOD mice with Sjögren’s syndrome. Mechanistically, SHED-exos deliver miR-29a-3p to suppress the Th1-lineage transcription factor T-bet, thereby limiting Th1 differentiation and IFN-γ/TNF-α production and ameliorating Sjögren’s syndrome–induced hyposalivation [217].

In systemic sclerosis, nanotechnology has been applied to mitigate fibrosis while sparing patients from the systemic toxicities of conventional cytotoxic drugs. An extracellular matrix-inspired dendrimer nanogel encapsulating cyclophosphamide combines polyamidoamine-based dendrimers with polyphenol-modified bovine serum albumin, endowing the nanogel with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. In a bleomycin-induced systemic sclerosis mouse model, this nanogel protected fibroblasts from oxidative stress, attenuated transforming growth factor (TGF)-β–mediated activation and reduced dermal fibrosis and collagen deposition, suggesting a strategy to decouple cyclophosphamide efficacy from off-target damage [218].

For ANCA-associated vasculitis, manganese-loaded carbon nanoparticles with intrinsic antioxidative capacity have been reported to alleviate vasculitic injury by limiting neutrophil extracellular trap–driven ferroptosis and associated endothelial damage in experimental models, thereby improving renal and systemic involvement compared with conventional treatment alone [219]. Although still at a preclinical stage, such disease-tailored nano-antioxidants exemplify how rationally designed nanomaterials could protect vulnerable vascular beds in severe, organ-threatening vasculitides.

Collectively, these proof-of-concept examples indicate that the general design strategies discussed in our sections on common AIDs—organ-targeted carriers, tolerogenic exosomes, multifunctional nanogels and redox-modulating nanoparticles—are readily adaptable to rare autoimmune indications. At the same time, the fact that no nanomedicines are yet approved for Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis, ANCA-associated vasculitis or other orphan AIDs underscores the need for disease-specific models, pharmacodynamic biomarkers and adaptive trial designs tailored to small, heterogeneous patient populations [8].

At the same time, the examples in this section are deliberately chosen to illustrate how nano-platforms are adapted to disease- and subtype-specific microenvironments rather than applied in a one-size-fits-all fashion. In RA, seropositive and seronegative joints share a hypoxic, ROS-rich synovial milieu, which supports the development of MMP-, hypoxia- and ROS-responsive imaging and drug-release systems that can function independently of autoantibody status [24]. In SLE, stratification into type I IFN–high versus –low biological subsets is linked to distinct cytokine and organ-involvement profiles, motivating nano-diagnostics that enrich IFN-driven cfDNA/EV markers and nanotherapies that focus IFN-pathway modulators on plasmacytoid dendritic cells and inflamed kidney tissue [25]. In IBD, segments with severe inflammation exhibit marked oxidative stress and abnormal luminal pH, providing a rationale for pH/ROS-triggered, mucus-penetrating nanocarriers that release biologics or small molecules only in highly inflamed colonic regions [220,221]. For T1D, strong HLA class II–linked heterogeneity in β-cell antigen recognition supports the design of antigen-specific tolerogenic nanovaccines and mRNA–LNP "inverse vaccines" whose encoded epitope pools are tuned to the dominant autoreactive T cell specificities in different genetic backgrounds [26,222]. RA: MMP-2-triggered "diagnosis–therapy–assessment" integrated probe

In 2023, Chen et al. designed an MMP-2-responsive peptide self-assembly probe (PEG-DTIPA-KGPLGVRK-MTX/Pal-GGGGHHHHD-TCZ) that is precisely cleaved by synovial MMP-2, instantly exposing an IL-6R-targeting motif and co-releasing MTX and TCZ, thereby achieving drug synergy and adaptive enrichment in the joint microenvironment [68]. In 2025, the same group upgraded the core to a polymer–metal hybrid, raising the r₁ value to 9.6 mM⁻¹ s⁻¹ and endowing the probe with MR/CT/fluorescence tri-modal imaging [223]. The system quantitatively tracks synovial thickening and bone erosion in real time and adjusts dosing accordingly [223]. A single intravenous injection of this MMP-2-responsive probe lowered the clinical arthritis score by approximately 70% compared with Phosphate-Buffered Saline-treated CIA mice and produced greater score reductions than free MTX or TCZ monotherapy, without systemic toxicity, thereby providing a closed-loop ‘dose–feedback–re-optimization’ concept for refractory RA [68]. Subsequent hypoxia and ROS dual-responsive MTX–quercetin micelles confirmed that microenvironment-triggered multi-drug synergy can also alleviate local hypoxia and oxidative stress, thereby laying a mechanistic foundation for combination nanotherapy [224,225]. Overall, enzyme-activated probes offer a feasible route toward individualized RA management.

Clinically, RA exhibits marked heterogeneity, from seropositive patients with high autoantibody titers to seronegative RA in whom joint inflammation occurs without RF/ACPA, making early diagnosis and risk stratification more challenging [226]. Extra-articular manifestations such as RA-associated interstitial lung disease further complicate management, as subclinical lung involvement is difficult to screen for and current pharmacologic options remain limited [227]. These features generate distinct needs for nanotechnology: synovium-targeted probes that remain sensitive in seronegative disease, and delivery systems that can spare lung, cardiovascular, and infection-prone comorbid organs while maintaining durable disease control.

SLE: exosome-based precision diagnosis and MSC-exos immune reprogramming

Urinary EVs mirror renal immunopathology and enable noninvasive tracking of disease biology. In LN, a urinary exosomal multimarker panel of miR-21, miR-150, and miR-29c detects early fibrosis and predicts progression, providing information that is complementary to clinicopathologic indices [108]. Exosomal miR-146a correlates with activity, proteinuria, complement consumption, and histology, and baseline levels predict future flares, supporting use in longitudinal surveillance [228]. Broader exosomal miRNA signatures predict treatment response and renal recovery, indicating utility for response-adaptive care [109]. Beyond miRNAs, urinary exosomal tRNA-derived small RNAs enhance diagnostic and prognostic performance for nephritis, underscoring the value of multimodal EVs cargo profiling [229]. Foundational work shows most urinary lupus miRNAs reside within exosomes, justifying EVs-focused assays [230].

Therapeutically, mesenchymal stem cell–derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) reprogram immune circuits without cell engraftment. In murine lupus, MSC-Exos educate macrophages toward an efferocytic, anti-inflammatory state that recruits Tregss; depletion of exosomal miR-16 or miR-21 abrogates this phenotype, demonstrating cargo-dependent mechanisms [231]. Human umbilical cord MSC-EVs ameliorate nephritis in MRL/lpr mice, lowering anti-dsDNA and proteinuria while shifting the Th1/Th17/Treg balance and suppressing renal IL-6/STAT3/IL-17 signaling, a pathway consonant with renal tissue biology [169]. Together, EVs diagnostics and MSC-Exos therapeutics form a closed-loop strategy in SLE: EVs panels stratify risk and forecast response, while MSC-Exos deliver modular, nucleic-acid-encoded instructions to restore immune homeostasis with potentially lower systemic toxicity.

SLE spans diverse organ phenotypes, from cutaneous and musculoskeletal disease to life-threatening LN and neuropsychiatric involvement, so no single biomarker or imaging modality reliably captures global disease activity or impending flares [232]. Current classification and response indices remain composite and partly subjective, and organ biopsy is still required in many patients to guide high-risk immunosuppression [233]. These realities highlight unmet needs for SLE-specific nano-diagnostics that can noninvasively resolve organ-level immunopathology and for delivery platforms that reconcile potent B/T-cell targeting with acceptable long-term safety in young, predominantly female populations.

MS: non-inflammatory mRNA-LNP "inverse vaccine"

The core design principle is to encode myelin antigens in nucleoside-modified mRNA and package them in delivery systems that avoid innate adjuvanticity, so antigen is presented by steady-state dendritic cells without co-stimulation. In EAE, a 1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ)–modified mRNA-LNP platform induced antigen-specific tolerance across disease stages, reduced Th1/Th17 effectors, expanded FOXP3⁺ Tregs, and even reversed paralysis after symptom onset—while preserving protective antiviral immunity, a hallmark of selective tolerization rather than blanket immunosuppression [16]. Mechanistically, nucleoside modification dampens TLR3/7/8 sensing and type I IFN amplification, enabling “non-inflammatory” expression of autoantigens that biases toward deletion/anergy of pathogenic T cells and reinforcement of regulatory circuits [234]. A practical caveat is that some ionizable-lipid formulations show intrinsic reactogenicity; formulation de-tuning and dose fractionation are therefore integral to maintaining a genuinely tolerogenic context [235].

Translation to heterogeneous MS biology requires breadth and anatomical precision. Because epitope spreading diversifies the myelin antigen repertoire over time, multiplexed mRNA encoding of Myelin Basic Protein (MBP)/PLP/ Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein (MOG) epitopes is rational to anticipate intramolecular and intermolecular antigen shifts [236,237]. Lymph-node targeting further improves the odds that antigen meets decision-making APCs under quiescent cues; “albumin hitchhiking” amphiphiles—which noncovalently ride endogenous albumin from the injection site to draining nodes—offer a clinically compatible add-on to focus presentation and dose-sparing delivery [70]. In a future clinical workflow, inverse-vaccine dosing could be scheduled against relapse biology (e.g., pre- or post-de-escalation of standard DMTs), with real-time safety gates (reactogenicity monitoring) and pharmacodynamic readouts (antigen-specific T-cell assays) to confirm on-target tolerization without bystander immunosuppression. The convergence of m1Ψ-engineered silence at the RNA level, tolerogenic antigen presentation in lymph nodes, and multi-epitope coding to track spreading thus outlines a programmable, disease-modifying strategy tailored to MS heterogeneity [16,70].

MS is characterized by relapsing–remitting and progressive courses driven by multifocal CNS demyelination, but even updated McDonald criteria and MRI protocols cannot fully prevent diagnostic delay or misclassification, especially in clinically isolated or atypical presentations [238]. Disease-modifying therapies reduce relapse rates yet only partially slow neurodegeneration, and many patients develop treatment-refractory progression or intolerable adverse effects despite escalating to high-efficacy agents [239]. These features create a strong demand for CNS-penetrant, antigen-specific nanotherapies and for quantitative nano-enabled biomarkers that can track smoldering inflammatory activity and tissue repair beyond conventional MRI lesions.

IBD: ROS-responsive PDA nanoparticles for barrier repair

In colitis, ROS amplify cytokine signaling and disrupt epithelial junctions, making redox-coupled drug delivery and intrinsic antioxidant activity highly desirable. Polydopamine (PDA) nanoparticles (NPs) offer catechol/quinone redox cycling for broad ROS scavenging while their bioadhesive surface prolongs residence on inflamed mucosa, increasing local exposure and minimizing systemic spillover [72,240].

Recent oral PDA constructs illustrate three complementary levers. First, PDA as an active material: ROS-responsive PDA NPs themselves quench H₂O₂/O₂⁻· and downshift TNF-α/IL-6 in DSS colitis, improving disease activity, histology, and oxidative stress indices [72]. Second, PDA as a smart coating: PDA-coated berberine nanoparticles (PDA@BBR) combine mucoadhesion with ROS-triggered release, yielding deeper colonic retention, stepwise drug liberation under peroxide, and superior anti-inflammatory efficacy versus drug or core particles alone [241]. Third, PDA as a bioadhesive shell on bioactives: a mucoadhesive PDA-coated yeast β-glucan nanocomplex (YBNs@PDA) restores tight-junction proteins (occludin/claudin-1), thickens the mucus layer (MUC2), lowers colonic cytokines, and rebalances microbiota richness toward beneficial taxa in DSS mice, with normal liver–kidney panels and organ histology [240].

PDA can also be endowed with lesion tropism and immune programming. In an inflammation-directed design, PDA NPs were conjugated to the antimicrobial peptide mCRAMP and cloaked with macrophage membrane, enhancing homing to diseased colon, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and favorably shifting gut flora—a tripartite mechanism of targeting, ROS scavenging, and ecological repair [242].

Together, ROS-responsive PDA platforms unify catalytic antioxidation, stimulus-locked delivery, and barrier/microbiome restoration—key axes for durable remission without broad immunosuppression. Their oral compatibility, mucoadhesion, and modular surface chemistry position PDA as a pragmatic chassis to pair small molecules, peptides, or prebiotic cores for precision control of epithelial injury and immune tone in IBD [72,[240], [241], [242]].

Beyond PDA, several recent high-impact studies extend these design concepts to more complex nano–bio hybrids for IBD. Cao et al. engineered artificial-enzymes-armed Bifidobacterium longum probiotics that couple nanozyme-based ROS scavenging with probiotic colonization, thereby attenuating inflammation, restoring intestinal barrier function, and rebalancing gut microbiota in murine and canine models of colitis [243]. Duan et al. reported capsulated cellular nanosponges, based on macrophage-membrane-coated nanoparticles encapsulated in enteric capsules, which selectively accumulate at inflamed colonic sites, neutralize pro-inflammatory cytokines, and promote epithelial repair in experimental IBD [244]. Min et al. developed an orally administrated inflamed-colon-targeted antioxidant nanozyme composed of mesoporous silica nanoparticles decorated with ceria and coated with poly(acrylic acid), achieving preferential adhesion to inflamed mucosa, potent ROS scavenging, and reduced immune cell infiltration in DSS-induced colitis [245]. Wang et al. further showed that plant-derived nanocrystalline cellulose can reinforce mucus and tight junctions, modulate MUC2 expression, and enrich beneficial taxa such as Akkermansia in ulcerative colitis models with an excellent safety profile [246]. Together with PDA-based systems, these platforms exemplify a new generation of IBD nanotherapeutics that integrate ROS control, biomimetic targeting, and microbiota–barrier repair into clinically relevant oral or luminally active formulations [[243], [244], [245], [246]].

In IBD, chronic relapsing inflammation affects distinct segments of the gut in Crohn’s disease versus ulcerative colitis, so a single endoscopic or biomarker snapshot rarely captures true disease burden or predicts long-term complications [247]. Mucosal healing has become a key therapeutic target, yet its assessment still depends largely on invasive, costly colonoscopy, and up to one-third of patients fail or lose response to biologics over time [248]. These complexities underscore the need for nano-diagnostics that report localized barrier damage and for oral, colon-targeted nanotherapeutics that can personalize dosing while minimizing systemic immunosuppression.

Nanomedicine strategies for local immune modulation in psoriasis

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin disease characterized by IL-23/Th17-driven inflammation, keratinocyte hyperproliferation, aberrant differentiation and dense leukocyte infiltration in the dermis and epidermis. Although biologics targeting TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-23 have revolutionized management, many patients still require long-term topical or systemic small-molecule therapies, which are limited by poor skin penetration, off-target toxicity and adherence issues. These features make psoriasis a compelling indication for nanomedicine, where local drug delivery and microenvironment-specific immune modulation are particularly attractive [249].

Topical nanocarriers, including lipid-based nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles and MN-assisted systems, have been developed to enhance drug deposition in inflamed plaques while limiting systemic exposure. Self-assembled hyaluronic acid nanoparticles used as drug-free topical therapeutics showed preferential accumulation in pro-inflammatory macrophages within psoriatic dermis, suppressing innate immune activation and restoring barrier function in imiquimod-induced models without overt systemic toxicity, highlighting the potential of “carrier-as-drug” concepts in cutaneous autoimmunity [250]. Drug-loaded platforms such as alantolactone-encapsulated chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanoparticles have demonstrated efficient penetration into psoriatic lesions, inhibition of Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) activation in keratinocytes, reduced inflammatory cytokine production and decreased T-cell infiltration, resulting in marked improvements in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI)-like scores in murine models [251]. Recent reviews further emphasize that nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), AuNPs and nucleic-acid–loaded nanoparticles can enhance the efficacy and tolerability of MTX, cyclosporine, small-molecule inhibitors and RNA therapeutics in psoriasis [249].

From a translational standpoint, psoriasis offers several opportunities for nanotechnology, including local, minimally invasive administration, direct access to lesional tissue, and the possibility of biomaterial-based patches or MNs that combine drug delivery with mechanical removal of scales [249]. However, there are also important challenges: the thickened stratum corneum and heterogeneous plaque morphology can lead to variable nanoparticle penetration; chronic use requires rigorous assessment of local and systemic immunosuppression; and scaling up complex topical systems (e.g., MN patches or combinatorial nano-gels) while maintaining batch-to-batch uniformity of particle size, drug loading, and release behavior remains non-trivial. Robust human pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies and head-to-head comparisons with existing biologics and standard topical therapies will be essential to determine whether nanomedicine can provide a meaningful clinical advantage in psoriasis [249].

Nanotechnology for targeted immune modulation in T1D

T1D is driven by T-cell-mediated destruction of pancreatic β cells, resulting in lifelong insulin dependence and increased risk of micro- and macrovascular complications. Current disease-modifying immunotherapies, such as anti-CD3 monoclonal antibodies, can delay but rarely prevent disease progression and are constrained by systemic immunosuppression, cytokine release syndrome, and narrow therapeutic windows. These limitations have spurred interest in nanotechnology-enabled strategies that deliver immune therapeutics directly to pancreatic lymph nodes and inflamed islets, or that induce antigen-specific tolerance with minimal off-target effects [252]. Recent nanoengineered bioactive hydrogels further illustrate how local diabetic lesions are sustained by intertwined oxidative stress, hypoxia, and infection, and how these axes can be corrected with multifunctional materials. A hyperthermia-enhanced immunoregulatory hydrogel was shown to simultaneously scavenge ROS and improve oxygenation in chronic diabetic foot ulcers, thereby interrupting the cycle of persistent inflammation and macrophage imbalance and markedly accelerating wound closure [253]. Dendritic cationic hydrogels with robust inherent antibacterial activity efficiently capture and kill bacteria while supporting re-epithelialization in infected wounds, providing a representative example of integrating antimicrobial and pro-healing cues in diabetic tissue repair [254]. A cuttlefish ink nanoparticle-reinforced adhesive biopolymer hydrogel with photothermal and immunomodulatory features has also been reported to significantly accelerate healing of oral ulcers in diabetic rats by dampening local inflammation, promoting re-epithelialization, and enhancing angiogenesis [255].

Compared with these diabetes-focused biomaterial platforms and prior T1D-centered nanomedicine reviews, the present article provides an integrated, cross-disease perspective in which T1D is analyzed alongside prototypical systemic AIDs such as RA, SLE, and MS. By jointly mapping nano-biosensor, imaging, and tolerogenic drug-delivery strategies onto shared immunopathological axes (e.g., antigen-specific T cell responses, macrophage polarization, and oxidative stress), we highlight convergent design principles that are not apparent when T1D is considered in isolation. In addition, we align each major class of nanotechnology with its dominant immune mechanism and translational stage (from preclinical models to marketed products), thereby complementing earlier T1D-focused reviews that mainly catalogue nanoparticle formulations or immunotherapies without systematically comparing them across autoimmune indications [256].

A recent proof-of-concept study engineered PLGA-PEG nanoparticles coated with the MECA79 antibody, which recognizes peripheral node addressin on high endothelial venules (HEVs) enriched in pancreatic lymph nodes and pancreata in NOD mice and patients with T1D [252]. Encapsulation of anti-CD3 mAb into these HEV-targeted nanoparticles significantly increased antibody accumulation in pancreatic lymph nodes and the pancreas compared with free anti-CD3, led to more durable reversal of hyperglycemia, and reduced effector T-cell frequencies and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, while attenuating systemic cytokine release [252]. This work exemplifies how nanoscale targeting to disease-relevant vascular and lymphoid niches can enhance the efficacy–safety balance of potent immune therapeutics in T1D.

Conceptually, nanomedicine offers multiple opportunities in T1D: (1) concentrating immunomodulatory antibodies or small molecules in pancreatic lymph nodes and islets to preserve residual β-cell mass; (2) co-delivering autoantigens and tolerogenic cues to promote antigen-specific regulatory T-cell induction; and (3) combining therapeutic nanoparticles with imaging agents to allow real-time monitoring of islet inflammation [252]. At the same time, T1D presents distinct translational challenges. Selective delivery to the small, anatomically complex pancreas and its draining lymph nodes in humans is substantially more difficult than in rodent models, and the autoimmune response is heterogeneous across patients in terms of HLA haplotypes, autoreactive T-cell specificities and disease stage [252]. Long-term safety is particularly critical, as early-life intervention may be required to preserve β-cell function, necessitating exhaustive evaluation of chronic nanoparticle exposure, off-target biodistribution and potential interference with physiological immune surveillance [252]. Furthermore, manufacturing and regulatory pathways for complex targeted nanocarriers must ensure consistent particle size, ligand density and drug loading, and will likely need to be integrated with stratification strategies based on genetic and immunological biomarkers to identify individuals most likely to benefit [252].

Challenges and future development directions

Autoimmune populations are uniquely vulnerable to unintended immune perturbation, so this section examines safety from first principles. It focuses on how material class (metals/oxides, lipids, polymers), surface chemistry and protein corona, payload immunogenicity, and biodistribution or clearance jointly shape complement activation, cytokine release, hypersensitivity such as anti-PEG responses, and off-target organ exposure. We also discuss practical levers to de-risk designs: degradable or cleavable backbones, stealth coatings beyond PEG, immune-inert targeting ligands, local rather than systemic delivery, dose fractionation, standardized immunotoxicology panels, and CMC/quality controls aligned with regulatory expectations; and we note how decentralized Point-of-Care Testing (POCT)/wearables and AI analytics can flag emerging safety signals across clinic-to-home pathways. From a material-class perspective, safety liabilities and mitigation strategies differ across autoimmune nanomedicine platforms. Lipid-based systems such as PEGylated liposomes and mRNA LNPs most often raise complement activation–related pseudoallergy and infusion reactions, as well as anti-PEG antibodies that can trigger accelerated blood clearance; these risks can be attenuated by slow or stepwise infusion, premedication, baseline anti-PEG screening, and the use of cleavable or zwitterionic stealth coatings in place of permanent PEGylation [206,207,210,257]. Biodegradable polymeric nanoparticles, including PLGA- and polysaccharide-based carriers, are generally regarded as safer but can still cause local inflammation, cytokine release, or unexpected immunostimulation if size, surface charge, endotoxin contamination and dose are not carefully optimized; employing clinically validated polymer backbones, minimizing cationic charge, and implementing standardized immunotoxicity panels across complement, cytokines, and hematology can mitigate these issues [258,259]. Inorganic and metal or metal-oxide platforms such as mesoporous silica, SPIOs and AuNPs pose distinct challenges of long-term organ retention, oxidative stress and fibrosis, which motivate "safe-by-design" approaches that favor ultrasmall or renal-clearable sizes, redox-tuned compositions, and long-term histopathology and biodistribution studies before first-in-human trials [259,260]. Exosome- and cell membrane-coated nanocarriers introduce additional concerns related to source cell selection, procoagulant tissue factor exposure, and batch-to-batch heterogeneity; adherence to MISEV2018/2023 guidelines, rigorous characterization of membrane markers and tissue factor activity, and definition of critical quality attributes for the biological coating are therefore central to safe clinical translation in autoimmune indications [176,181,261].

Safety and biocompatibility

In vivo toxicity and long-term immune risks remain the primary major challenge in the transition from preclinical studies to clinical applications (Fig. 7). A 2023 meta-analysis of 420 nano-formulations revealed that >30% of liposomes, polymeric micelles, and metal oxides triggered C3a/C5a bursts within 5 min in vitro [262]. When C3a peaks exceeded 600 ng/ml, the risks of hypotension and rash increased 12.4-fold [263]. Six-month ICP-MS tracing showed 8%–12% of administered SiO₂ and AuNP doses retained in liver and spleen with fibrosis and chemokine elevation [264]. “Safe-by-Design” strategies have therefore shifted upstream: (i) computationally predict protein-corona sites that bind C1q/MBL (Mannose-Binding Lectin); (ii) employ cleavable PEG or zwitterionic coatings that self-shear 30–60 min post-dose to shorten the complement-sensitive window; (iii) choose <10 nm (renal clearance) or 80–120 nm (macrophage uptake) "safe size bands", avoiding the highly immunogenic 20–50 nm zone. Ex vivo micro-dosing in human serum can cut CARPA risk by >70% [263]. In addition to acute toxicological indicators, long-term studies should incorporate endpoints for organ retention, fibrosis, and neurobehavioral effects, with human serum-based peripheral microexposure assays serving as a “gatekeeper” for complement activation risks.

Clinical-translation hurdles

Only 7% of 256 autoimmune nano-therapies (2000–2024) reached Phase II/III, largely due to human–mouse immune discrepancies [265]. For instance, the CIA model’s M2-macrophage bias exaggerates anti-inflammatory effects, requiring 3–5 × higher human doses [62]. Batch-scale drift in particle size and encapsulation triggers 40% of IND (Investigational New Drug) withdrawals. Continuous-flow microfluidics now keeps size RSD <3%, and FDA lists it as a critical quality attribute [266]. The 2024 EU/US guidelines mandate complement, cytokine and protein-corona fingerprints in early trials [265]. Most efficacy and safety data come from mouse models, which exhibit systematic differences from humans in immune networks, tissue barriers and pharmacokinetics. Significant variations also exist among different disease subtypes in terms of target expression, microenvironmental pH/ROS levels, and permeability. To reduce extrapolation errors, it is recommended to conduct parallel validation using Multiple species and models, and to incorporate human explants, organoids and microfluidic "organ-on-a-chip" systems to evaluate trans-barrier delivery and immune compatibility. At the disease level, factors such as human leukocyte antigen (HLA), microbiome and comorbidities (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis–associated interstitial lung disease) should be included in stratified randomization and subgroup analyses to avoid masking population differences with average efficacy outcomes.

Beyond conventional inbred rodent models, emerging human-relevant preclinical platforms may help de-risk translation of nanomedicines for AIDs. Humanized mouse models, in which immunodeficient strains are engrafted with human hematopoietic cells and/or engineered to express disease-associated HLA alleles, better recapitulate key aspects of human adaptive immunity and have been applied to model autoimmune pathogenesis and test biologics or cell-based therapies, including in RA [[267], [268], [269]]. For nano-immunotherapies that target human-specific pathways or epitopes, these models offer an opportunity to simultaneously monitor human immune reprogramming, nanoparticle biodistribution, and off-target toxicities in vivo, although residual differences in stromal and myeloid compartments mean that their predictive value is not absolute [267,268]. Complementary human microphysiological systems and organ-on-a-chip platforms integrate primary or iPSC-derived human cells in perfusable 3D microenvironments and can be engineered to include tissue-resident and circulating immune cells, enabling quantitative assessment of immune cell trafficking, cytokine release, and barrier integrity under controlled exposure to nanomaterials [[270], [271], [272]]. Notably, immunocompetent organ-on-chip systems have already been shown to predict on-target, off-tissue toxicities of T cell–engaging biologics more accurately than standard two-dimensional cultures or animal models, underscoring their value for safety profiling of immune-modulating modalities [273]. As such platforms are adapted to gut, joint, skin and kidney microenvironments relevant to AID, integrating humanized mouse studies with organ-on-chip and other microphysiological systems into nanomedicine development pipelines may provide more human-predictive efficacy, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic, and safety readouts before first-in-human trials [270,274].

Technology integration and intelligence trends

Theranostic platforms with built-in feedback, such as an "endogenous melanin/H₂ dual-activated" nano-agent that delivers H₂ and anti-inflammatory drugs while providing NIR-II, phosphatidic acid (PA) and MR tri-modal imaging, are achieving self-diagnosis and self-adjustment loops [275]. AI-assisted physiologically based pharmacokinetic (AI-PBPK) models trained on 288 datasets cut tissue-exposure error to <30% and output individual dose curves from 15 input parameters [276]. Digital twins even predict nano-drug-induced arrhythmia via patient-specific ion-channel kinetics [277]. The concept of the digital twin has continuously evolved, progressing from its initial association with products to becoming digital replicas of both living and non-living physical entities. Digital twins are intelligent models capable of continuously evolving through closed-loop optimization by adjusting to data and predicting future outcomes. They hold enormous potential for applications in AIDs, particularly in treatment and diagnosis.

Although AI-enhanced nano-diagnostics and "digital twin" concepts for AIDs are still at an early stage, several recent studies already illustrate how these tools can be operationalized along the nanomedicine pipeline. In RA, Yildirim et al. compiled over 2000 data points from nanoparticle-based RA formulations and used a combination of feature-selection, random-forest modelling and association-rule mining to identify nanoparticle composition, drug loading and exposure conditions as key determinants of cytotoxicity, providing a data-driven framework to rationally tune dosage and material parameters for safer RA nanotherapies [278]. In parallel, Chou et al. developed AI-PBPK model that integrates nanoparticle physicochemical descriptors with whole-body physiology to predict tissue and tumor exposure across dosing regimens, effectively functioning as a virtual "nanoparticle biodistribution twin" that can be used to screen formulations and optimize dosing strategies before in vivo testing [276]. Complementing these nanomedicine-oriented efforts, the scDrugPrio framework builds detailed network models of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (including antigen-induced arthritis, MS, Crohn’s disease and psoriatic arthritis) from single-cell transcriptomic data and computationally "treats" patient- or mouse-specific digital twins with thousands of drugs, successfully prioritizing therapies and recapitulating responder versus non-responder patterns in Crohn’s disease [279]. Together, these examples demonstrate that AI-driven nano-informatics and immune digital twins are already being deployed for toxicity profiling, dose optimization and patient selection in related contexts, and they provide transferable methodological blueprints for the future implementation of nano-theranostic digital twins in AID trials.

Personalized therapy and precision immune engineering

The next step for the m¹Ψ-mRNA-LNP inverse vaccine is to tailor four to six antigen pools to each patient’s HLA-II haplotype. MN arrays of degradable tissue-nanotransporters (TNTs) can locally release CRISPR(clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats)/Cas9 mRNA; dermal IL-23 knockout reverses psoriasiform inflammation [280]. Combining peptide hydrogels or nucleic-acid–liposome co-carriers may enable dual gene editing + immune tolerance regulation. The next step for the "inverse vaccine" is to tailor four to six antigen pools to each patient’s HLA class II haplotype, built on a non-inflammatory m¹Ψ-modified mRNA/LNP scaffold to induce antigen-specific tolerance. From a translational standpoint, however, fully individualized HLA-haplotype–matched antigen pools are likely to be a long-term goal rather than the first clinical implementation, because they require rapid sequencing, in silico epitope prediction and GMP manufacture for each patient, together with batch-specific quality control and regulatory review, which substantially increase cost and complexity [281,282]. A more feasible near-term pathway is to develop a limited panel of "off-the-shelf" inverse nano-vaccines encoding dominant autoantigen epitopes, with each construct tailored to one or several prevalent HLA class II supertypes so that a small set of vaccines can cover large patient fractions; HLA supertype classification has already been widely used in epitope-based vaccine design to collapse allelic diversity into a manageable number of specificity groups [283]. Population-coverage algorithms that integrate HLA allele frequencies and epitope-binding data have already been used to optimize epitope sets for T-cell vaccines across ethnicities, and similar tools could be repurposed to design tolerogenic antigen pools for autoimmune indications [284]. Experience with personalized neoantigen cancer vaccines suggests that such individualized platforms are initially implemented in specialized centers with tightly controlled manufacturing and regulatory workflows, which may provide a model for future semi-personalized or fully individualized HLA-tailored mRNA–LNP inverse vaccines in refractory AID [281]. For patients with multi-epitope or multi-organ involvement, staged encoding or barcoding strategies can be used to track the evolving immune repertoire. Biodegradable MN–tissue nanotransporters (TNTs) can locally deliver CRISPR/Cas mRNA or siRNA, enabling site-specific downregulation of axes such as IL-23 and JAK–STAT, thereby reducing systemic immunosuppression. Combining peptide hydrogels, nucleic-acid–lipid co-carriers, and exosome/cell-membrane cloaking may achieve a dual-channel control paradigm of “gene editing + immune tolerance”.

Conclusion

Nanotechnology, with its tunable size, facile surface engineering, and multimodal integration, is reshaping AID management at both diagnostic and therapeutic ends. LSPR/SERS ultra-sensitive biosensors have dropped detection limits for anti-CCP antibodies and circulating miRNAs to the fg/mL level [88], while magnetic nanoprobes integrated with NIR-II imaging enable precise visualization of synovial micro-lesions and bone erosion [223]. Nano-assisted exosome enrichment and digital Polymeric Chain Reaction (PCR) have improved non-invasive early screening in SLE and RA [285], and wearable nano-sensing films are moving continuous monitoring from hospital to home.

On the immunomodulatory front, smart nanocarriers using enzymatic, pH/ROS-responsive, and ligand-targeting mechanisms deliver MTX, TCZ and siRNA locally with high efficacy and low toxicity [68]. Landmark advances include antigen-specific tolerogenic platforms: the m¹Ψ-mRNA-LNP inverse vaccine (Phase I) induces FOXP3⁺ Tregs without TLR7/8 activation [16], while MSC-exosomes and biomembrane-coated nanoparticles offer dual delivery and immune-reprogramming functions [169].

Looking ahead, the immediate priorities for lab-to-clinic translation of nanotechnology in AIDs are rigorous safety evaluation and scalable manufacturing, rather than nanoformulation design alone. Standardized frameworks for long-term biodistribution, immunotoxicity, biodegradation and environmental risk assessment are particularly important for immunomodulatory nano–bio interfaces that may induce durable immune tolerance. In parallel, development of GMP-compliant, quality-by-design manufacturing platforms for polymeric, lipid and hybrid nanocarriers, informed by experience with PLGA-based nanomedicines and broader nanomedicine scale-up, is critical to bridge the persistent gap between proof-of-concept systems and robust clinical products. On this foundation, smart theranostic nanoplatforms that integrate responsive drug release with multimodal imaging and multi-pronged interventions can support “self-diagnosis/self-adjustment” treatment paradigms in inflammatory AID. Finally, data-driven modelling frameworks, such as AI-assisted physiologically based pharmacokinetic models and digital-twin technologies, should be coupled with clinical datasets to personalize nano-drug dosing, predict efficacy–safety profiles and iteratively de-risk development pipelines. By giving equal weight to safety evaluation, scalable manufacturing, intelligent nano–bio design and computational decision support, future nanotechnology-based interventions are more likely to reach mainstream rheumatology and immunology practice as reproducible, affordable and safe precision medicines for patients with AIDs.

Finally, while most current nano-diagnostic and nano-therapeutic programs naturally concentrate on prevalent AIDs, the highest unmet needs often lie in rare entities such as Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis and autoimmune vasculitis, where irreversible organ damage can occur despite empiric immunosuppression. Platform nanotechnologies optimized in common AIDs should therefore be strategically extended to these orphan indications, in parallel with the development of harmonized registries and multi-center trial networks that can support adequately powered, biomarker-driven studies in small and heterogeneous patient populations [8].
