Ensemble Docking and Molecular Dynamics Indicate Dynamic Modulation of hnRNPA1 UP1 Interdomain Behavior by Natural Products
Authors: Romen Meitei Lourembam, Nishant Raj, Gurumayum Sanathoi Sharma, Indira Devi Sarangthem, Nanaocha H. Sharma, Sunil S. Thorat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA-binding proteins exhibit substantial conformational plasticity that complicates structure-based ligand discovery and computational characterization. In this study, we applied an integrated computational approach combining high-throughput virtual screening, molecular dynamics simulations, ensemble docking, and interaction energy analysis to examine ligand binding and associated dynamic effects in the UP1 domain of hnRNPA1. Screening of 2847 phytochemicals followed by scaffold-diverse molecular dynamics evaluation identified three representative ligandsAmentoflavone, Ginkgetin, and Withanolide Dcapable of associating with the interdomain surface formed between the two RNA recognition motifs. Comparative simulations revealed that all three ligands form stable complexes within this region, with Ginkgetin exhibiting the most favorable interaction energy. Extended molecular dynamics simulations focusing on Withanolide D demonstrated persistent ligand localization at the RRM1–RRM2 interface and revealed a dominant interaction hotspot centered on SER85 and ARG86. Dynamic analyses indicated that RNA binding is the primary determinant of UP1 conformational stability, whereas ligand association induces modest conformational expansion and redistribution of dynamic variance within the RNA-bound ensemble. Ensemble docking across molecular dynamics-derived conformations further indicated that RNA binding modulates ligand-binding region accessibility without substantially altering average binding energetics. Energetic analysis of the protein–RNA interface revealed a modest reduction in interaction strength upon ligand association, suggesting that ligand binding may perturb the RNA-binding surface without disrupting the overall architecture of the complex. These results provide a system-specific computational characterization of ligand association with the UP1 interdomain region and highlight the role of conformational heterogeneity in shaping ligand-binding behavior in this protein system.

Introduction

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play central roles in RNA metabolism, including pre-mRNA splicing, exon selection, mRNA transport, and stress granule assembly. Among these, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (hnRNPA1) is a ubiquitously expressed RBP with broad regulatory functions in RNA processing. , The N-terminal UP1 fragment of hnRNPA1 comprises two tandem RNA recognition motifs (RRM1 and RRM2) that together form a modular RNA-binding unit mediating sequence-specific interactions with single-stranded RNA (Figure ).

Structural organization of the hnRNPA1 UP1 domain. The UP1 fragment comprises two tandem RNA recognition motifs (RRM1 and RRM2) that form a modular RNA-binding unit. The domains are shown as surface representations highlighting the interdomain interface.

Altered expression or mutation of hnRNPA1 has been implicated in several neurodegenerative and neuromuscular disorders, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, frontotemporal dementia, and multisystem proteinopathy. , These observations highlight the importance of hnRNPA1 not only as an RNA-binding factor but also as a dynamically regulated protein whose conformational plasticity contributes to disease-relevant molecular pathways.

Natural compounds derived from plants provide structurally diverse and evolutionarily optimized chemical scaffolds, many of which exhibit favorable pharmacokinetic properties and reduced toxicity compared to fully synthetic molecules. , Computational screening offers an efficient first step for identifying biologically relevant candidates, enabling rapid prioritization while minimizing experimental cost. , Accordingly, we adopt a reverse-pharmacology framework that integrates computational screening with mechanistic structural analysis, supporting the initial translational exploration of affordable plant-based small molecules. ,

The interdomain interface between RRM1 and RRM2 of hnRNPA1 represents a dynamically flexible region implicated in regulatory communication between RNA recognition motifs. Modulating this interdomain plasticity without directly occluding canonical RNA-binding surfaces may provide a structurally permissive route for functional regulation.

We hypothesize that small-molecule binding at the RRM1–RRM2 interdomain interface may modulate conformational dynamics of hnRNPA1 through interdomain coupling without directly disrupting RNA-binding contacts.

To test this hypothesis, the computational workflow was designed around three orthogonal criteria.(1)Structural localization of ligands to the interdomain cleft,(2)Quantitative stabilization of interdomain dynamics relative to the apo ensemble,(3)Preservation of protein–RNA interaction energetics.

Materials and Methods

Phytochemical Library Preparation and High-Throughput Virtual Screening

A curated library of 2847 KEGG-linked phytochemicals was retrieved from the PubChem database. Ligands were processed using Open Babel and PyRx preprocessing tools. Protonation states were assigned assuming physiological pH (7.4). Explicit tautomer enumeration was not performed; the dominant tautomeric form available in PubChem was retained. Since the majority of screened phytochemicals consist of relatively rigid polyphenolic and steroidal scaffolds with limited tautomeric variability under physiological conditions, omission of exhaustive tautomer

Enumeration is unlikely to substantially affect ligand prioritization at the high-throughput screening stage. Stereochemistry was preserved as defined in the source database. Energy minimization was conducted using the MMFF94 force field prior to docking.

The UP1 domain of hnRNPA1 was selected as the screening target. Experimentally resolved structures corresponding to the UP1 fragment (PDB IDs: 6DCL and 2LYV) were obtained from the Protein Data Bank and validated by sequence–structure mapping. These structures served as templates for docking and subsequent molecular dynamics simulations. Initial HTVS was performed using static crystal structures for computational tractability.

To avoid a priori bias toward predefined cavities and to enable identification of potential binding sites, docking was conducted using a blind docking strategy encompassing the entire UP1 surface. The docking grid was defined to span the conformational envelope of UP1 (59.01 × 63.36 × 61.05 Å), allowing unbiased sampling of surface- accessible cavities, including dynamically accessible interdomain regions rather than restricting the search to canonical RNA-recognition surfaces. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina implemented in PyRx 0.8, with the receptor prepared in PDBQT format and all 2847 ligands docked using an exhaustiveness of 25 and nine output binding modes per ligand.

Ligand prioritization was performed using a heuristic composite ranking index implemented in a custom Python-based workflow. This index was designed exclusively for screening triage and not as a calibrated predictor of binding affinity. All components were standardized using Z-score normalization prior to integration. Empirical weights of 0.5, 0.2, and 0.3 were assigned to the docking energy, pose-consistency, and tractability terms, respectively, to emphasize predicted binding while retaining sensitivity to docking reproducibility and basic physicochemical suitability. The lower-bound RMSD reported by AutoDock Vina was used solely as a pose-consistency indicator within the docking solution set and not as a measure of thermodynamic stability. Similarly, the descriptor-based term represents a coarse proxy for chemical tractability rather than a formal ADMET prediction. To minimize pose-level redundancy, ligand-level ranking was implemented by retaining only the highest-scoring docking pose for each PubChem CID. Sensitivity analysis was performed by perturbing weighting coefficients by ± 10%, confirming that the composition of the top-ranked subset remained stable. The prioritization workflow and associated data sets are available at: https://github.com/romenmeitei/SMA

Pose Level Spatial Analysis

Docking outputs were analyzed at the pose level, yielding a total of 23,184 poses across ligands. For each pose, the ligand center of mass (COM) was calculated. Ligand poses were classified as interdomain-cleft proximal when the COM was located within 8 Å of the centroid of residues 88-101, defining the interdomain interface between RRM1 and RRM2. To characterize spatial organization, COM coordinates of pocket-proximal poses were subjected to density-based clustering using the DBSCAN algorithm (ε = 3.5 Å, minimum samples = 2). This approach enabled identification of dominant binding clusters while excluding spatial noise. In addition, ligand–pocket interaction characteristics were quantified using a contact-based metric defined as the fraction of ligand atoms within 4.5 Å of any pocket residue, along with variability across poses to assess interaction consistency.

Grid Based Null Model for Spatial Localization

To evaluate whether interdomain-cleft localization exceeded random spatial expectation, a Monte Carlo null model was constructed using the same docking search space. Random points were uniformly sampled within the docking grid, and the expected fraction falling within 8 Å of the interdomain-cleft centroid was calculated. This distribution was compared with the observed fraction of docking poses classified as pocket-proximal, enabling assessment of spatial enrichment relative to random sampling using established Monte Carlo-based approaches. −

Focused Docking Validation

To evaluate robustness of docking results with respect to grid definition, selected ligands (Withanolide D, Ginkgetin, Amentoflavone, and Rutin) were redocked to the 6DCL structure using both blind and site-focused docking grids. For blind docking, the search space encompassed the entire UP1 domain. For site-focused docking, a reduced grid was centered on the interdomain cleft (residues 88-101) with dimensions of 26 × 18 × 26 Å. Docking pose localization was evaluated using COM-based distance analysis. Clustering of pocket-proximal poses was performed using DBSCAN to identify recurrent binding configurations. Interaction characteristics were further assessed using the contact-based metric described above.

Docking Protocol Validation and Methodological Considerations

To assess docking protocol behavior, the cocrystallized RNA fragment from the 6DCL structure was removed and subjected to redocking using identical parameters. Due to the intrinsic limitations of AutoDock Vina in handling large, flexible nucleic acid ligands, RNA redocking yielded substantial deviation from the crystallographic pose (RMSD >30 Å), reflecting methodological constraints rather than grid misplacement. Because no experimentally validated small-molecule ligands for the UP1 domain are available, conventional benchmarking approaches (e.g., ROC-AUC analysis) could not be performed. Therefore, docking reliability was assessed using internal consistency criteria, including (i) full-surface blind docking, (ii) reproducibility across independent crystal structures (6DCL and 2LYV), and (iii) convergence of binding regions across docking and molecular dynamics analyses.

Pocket Detection and Analysis

Pocket detection was performed independently of docking results using fpocket (v4.0). The apo UP1 structure was prepared by removing heteroatoms and nonessential water molecules. , The fpocket algorithm applies Voronoi tessellation and alpha-sphere clustering to identify surface cavities. For each pocket, descriptors including volume, hydrophobicity, and druggability score were computed. Pocket centroids were extracted and distances between pocket centers and ligand COM positions were calculated to assess spatial correspondence. Structural visualization was performed using UCSF ChimeraX.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the UP1 structure derived from PDB 6DCL. The apo system was prepared by removing RNA and nonessential molecules. Ligand-bound systems were generated using Amentoflavone, Ginkgetin, and Withanolide D based on top-ranked docking poses. System preparation and parametrization were carried out using CHARMM-GUI, applying the CHARMM36m force field for proteins and CGenFF for ligands. − All simulations were executed using GPU-accelerated GROMACS. Trajectory analyses included root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), hydrogen bonding, and interdomain center-of-mass distance measurements. A 4.0 Å heavy-atom cutoff was selected to capture stable short-range van der Waals and hydrophobic contacts commonly used in MD-based contact analyses while minimizing inclusion of transient long-range encounters. Trajectory processing and analysis were performed using MDAnalysis and MDTraj. , Systems were solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm. Equilibration was conducted in two stages. Production simulations were performed with a 2 fs time step, using Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) for long-range electrostatics and a 12 Å cutoff for short-range interactions. Ensemble Docking from MD-Derived Conformations

Twenty conformational snapshots of the UP1 domain were extracted at 10 ns intervals from 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations of both the apo and RNA-bound (holo) states. This yielded a total of 40 receptor conformations (20 apo and 20 holo), selected to balance conformational diversity with computational tractability, consistent with prior ensemble docking studies.

All structures were energy-minimized, uniformly protonated, and converted to PDBQT format using AutoDockTools. Receptor coordinates were aligned, and a single state-independent docking grid was defined by computing the global minima and maxima of atomic positions across all conformations using Python (NumPy), thereby encompassing the full conformational envelope of UP1.

Ensemble docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.1.2 in a Google Colab environment. Withanolide D (PubChem CID: 161671), selected from the HTVS prioritization workflow, was independently docked to each receptor conformation. For each conformation, 20 docking poses were generated using an exhaustiveness of 16.

For downstream analysis, the best-scoring pose from each ligand–conformation pair was retained. Center-of-mass coordinates of these poses were clustered using the DBSCAN algorithm (ε = 6 Å, min_samples = 5) to identify recurrent binding regions without predefining the number of clusters, while principal component analysis (PCA) was used for visualization of spatial distributions. Binding energy distributions were analyzed using pandas and SciPy. Structural and cluster-level visualization was performed using Matplotlib and UCSF ChimeraX.

All analyses were conducted in Google Colab using Python 3.10 with NumPy, pandas, scikit-learn, SciPy, and Matplotlib. A schematic overview of the computational workflow is provided in Figure .

Results

High-Throughput Virtual Screening

Spatial analysis of receptor 2LYV blind-docking data set yielded 23,184 poses from 2576 ligands, of which 373 poses (1.61%) were classified as interdomain-cleft proximal based on a center-of-mass (COM) distance threshold of ≤8 Å. Comparison with a grid-based Monte Carlo null model derived from the actual docking search space demonstrated that this fraction does not exceed random spatial expectation (Figure ). These results indicate that the interdomain cleft is not globally overrepresented across the blind-docking ensemble.

Despite this low global occupancy, clustering analysis of cleft-proximal poses revealed a dominant and geometrically coherent binding mode, with the majority of proximal poses converging into a single DBSCAN cluster. Notably, this cluster comprises poses originating from a large number of chemically distinct ligands, indicating that the observed binding configuration is structurally reproducible rather than ligand specific.

Residue-wise contact frequency analysis further demonstrated consistent interaction patterns across ligands, with prominent hotspots observed within residues 93–101 of the interdomain region. In particular, residues around 95–100 exhibited the highest contact frequencies, suggesting that this segment represents the core interaction interface within the cleft.

The global distribution of docking poses showed a broad peak at larger COM distances (∼15–20 Å), consistent with surface-wide sampling under blind-docking conditions, alongside a smaller localized population near the interdomain region. Importantly, no strong correlation was observed between docking affinity and spatial proximity to the cleft, indicating that cleft localization is not driven solely by binding energy but reflects a geometrically constrained binding mode.

Together, these results suggest that although the interdomain cleft is infrequently sampled in global docking, poses that access this region converge into a consistent structural configuration, supporting its role as a constrained but reproducible binding site.

Validation of Binding Localization Using Focused Docking

Under blind (large-grid) conditions, ligand poses were broadly distributed across the protein surface, with only a subset localizing near the interdomain cleft (Figure ). In contrast, site-focused docking significantly increased the proportion of poses proximal to the defined binding region, resulting in enhanced spatial convergence within the cleft.

Comparative analysis of COM distance distributions showed that Ginkgetin and Withanolide D exhibited a pronounced shift toward lower distances under focused-grid conditions, indicating strong localization within the interdomain interface. Amentoflavone displayed a similar but less pronounced shift, suggesting partial dependence on grid constraints. Rutin, included as a comparator, exhibited a more dispersed distribution and was not considered for mechanistic interpretation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of pose coordinates further supported these observations, revealing tighter clustering for Ginkgetin and Amentoflavone, consistent with stable binding localization, whereas the comparator ligand showed a more diffuse spatial distribution.

Residue-Level Interaction Profiling

To characterize ligand–protein interactions within the interdomain region, residue-wise contact frequency analysis was performed for residues 88–101 (Figure ). All three primary ligands Withanolide D, Ginkgetin, and Amentoflavoneexhibited consistent engagement with key residues, with prominent interaction hotspots observed at residues 93, 94, 96, and 97. Among these, Ginkgetin and Amentoflavone displayed broader interaction networks spanning multiple residues, whereas Withanolide D showed a more localized interaction pattern centered primarily around residues 94 and 97. Structural visualization confirmed that ligand poses cluster within the interdomain cleft, consistent with the observed contact profiles. These findings indicate that while multiple ligands can access the cleft region, they exhibit distinct residue level interaction signatures, reflecting differences in scaffold geometry and binding orientation.

Although Withanolide D was not the top-ranked compound based on docking affinity, it was prioritized for further analysis due to its balanced physicochemical properties, including moderate molecular weight, favorable lipophilicity, and a structurally tractable steroidal scaffold. These features support its suitability as a structurally tractable probe for evaluating ligand-associated dynamic effects. Importantly, while docking identified interaction hotspots within residues 93–97, subsequent MD simulations revealed a shifted but adjacent interaction core centered on SER85 and ARG86, indicating dynamic refinement of ligand positioning within the interdomain interface. Ginkgetin, a biflavonoid, belongs to a class of polyphenolic compounds characterized by relatively rigid, planar structures with limited conformational flexibility and a propensity for π-driven surface interactions. Such interaction profiles have been reported to favor broad contact interfaces rather than localized binding pockets. In contrast, steroidal lactones such as Withanolide D possess a semirigid core with flexible substituents, enabling more localized and adaptable binding modes. This distinction provides a structural basis for selecting Withanolide D as a probe for evaluating localized dynamic perturbations within the interdomain region.

Consistent with the hypothesis that the interdomain region represents a dynamically accessible regulatory surface, the majority of top-ranked docking poses localized to cavities spanning the RRM1–RRM2 interface rather than to canonical RNA-binding residues (Figure ). Among these, pocket 4, located adjacent to residues Asp88–Leu96, was the largest accessible cavity (volume 1257 Å3); however, it exhibited low predicted druggability (0.193) and moderate hydrophobicity (15.19) (Table ). Comparative Molecular Dynamics Screening of Scaffold-Diverse Ligands

The 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations performed for the UP1 complexes with Amentoflavone, Ginkgetin, and Withanolide D were evaluated using MM-PBSA analysis based on multiple frames extracted from equilibrated regions of the trajectories.

Trajectory-averaged MM-PBSA energies indicated that Ginkgetin exhibited the most favorable interaction energy (ΔG ≈ −12 kcal/mol), whereas Amentoflavone and Withanolide D showed comparable but less favorable values (ΔG ≈ −8.6 kcal/mol) (Figure ). These values represent relative enthalpic estimates of ligand–protein interaction strength, as entropy contributions (−TΔS) were not included in the MM-PBSA calculations.

MM-PBSA energetic analysis of Amentoflavone, Ginkgetin, and Withanolide D bound to the hnRNPA1 UP1 domain. (A) Energy decomposition showing the mean van der Waals (ΔVDWAALS), electrostatic (ΔEEL), polar solvation (ΔEGB), and nonpolar solvation (ΔESURF) contributions calculated over equilibrated trajectory frames. (B) Trajectory-averaged total interaction energy (ΔTOTAL), with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM).

Energy decomposition analysis showed that ligand binding was primarily driven by van der Waals interactions, followed by electrostatic contributions, while polar solvation opposed binding. This energetic profile is consistent across all three ligands and reflects a balance between favorable nonpolar interactions and desolvation penalties.

Although Ginkgetin exhibited more favorable interaction energy, Withanolide D was selected for extended simulations because its steroidal scaffold provides a structurally tractable framework for probing ligand-associated dynamic effects on RNA binding. In addition, Withanolide D displayed stable interaction patterns within the interdomain region during the trajectory, supporting its suitability for detailed conformational analysis.

Overall, these results indicate that all three compounds form stable complexes with the UP1 domain and support the feasibility of ligand association within the interdomain region. However, the observed differences in MM-PBSA energies should be interpreted comparatively rather than as absolute binding free energies.

Based on the structural stability observed in these simulations and the suitability of Withanolide D as a representative scaffold, extended simulations were subsequently performed to investigate ligand-associated dynamic effects on the UP1–RNA complex.

Ligand Binding Stability Analysis during MD Simulation of Withanolide D

The structural snapshots and ligand density mapping indicate that Withanolide D consistently bound within the interdomain cavities formed between RRM1 and RRM2 throughout the holo MD trajectory. Further, the ligand maintains a stable orientation near the central interface, contacting residues that also contribute to RNA recognition.

Residue-level interaction analysis based on heavy-atom contact frequencies reveals that ligand stabilization is predominantly mediated by a compact hydrophobic pocket rather than a single dominant polar hotspot (Table ). The highest contact frequencies were observed for PRO92 (98.3%), GLY93 (91.7%), LEU96 (88.4%), and PHE102 (82.9%), indicating persistent ligand enclosure within a nonpolar environment. Among these, GLY93 exhibits a moderate hydrogen bond occupancy (13.8%), representing the only notable directional polar interaction. In contrast, ARG91 shows comparatively lower contact frequency (43.6%) and minimal hydrogen bond contribution (1.1%), suggesting a weak polar role.

Molecular Dynamics Analysis of UP1 in Apo, RNA-Bound, and Ligand-Bound States

To evaluate the impact of RNA and ligand association on UP1 dynamics, 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations were analyzed over the equilibrated 100–200 ns window. Backbone stability assessment revealed mean RMSD values of 3.35 ± 0.54 Å for the apo system, 3.57 ± 0.33 Å for the RNA-bound complex, and 4.55 ± 0.66 Å for the holo system (Figure ). RNA binding produced a modest increase in average backbone deviation relative to apo UP1 but reduced fluctuation amplitude, indicating consolidation of the conformational ensemble around the RNA-bound state. Residue-level flexibility analysis further clarified these effects. Maximum residue-specific shifts ranged between −0.54 Å and +0.78 Å, supporting a model of localized dynamic modulation rather than widespread rigidification.

Essential dynamics analysis using common PCA demonstrated that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 26.85% and 24.11% of the total conformational variance, respectively. RNA binding markedly reduced collective motion along PC2, with an approximately 52% reduction in standard deviation relative to apo UP1, consistent with restriction of a dominant interdomain mode. However, the holo system exhibited the largest dispersion along PC1 (SD = 17.53), indicating redistribution of conformational variance rather than compression of global dynamics. Thus, ligand binding reshapes the conformational landscape instead of locking the structure into a single rigid state.

Analysis of dynamic cross-correlation matrices revealed mean negative correlation values of

–0.314 for apo, −0.248 for RNA-bound, and −0.252 for the holo system. RNA binding attenuated anticorrelated interdomain motions by approximately 21%, reflecting enhanced coupling between RRM1 and RRM2. The holo complex displayed correlation patterns closely resembling the RNA- bound state, indicating that Withanolide D does not further suppress long-range interdomain dynamics. Collectively, the integrated RMSD, RMSF, PCA, and DCCM analyses indicate that RNA binding is the principal stabilizing factor governing UP1 conformational dynamics. Rather than functioning as a strong global stabilizer, Withanolide D induces moderate conformational expansion and redistributes dynamic variance within the RNA-stabilized ensemble while preserving overall structural integrity.

Consistent with the dynamic correlation analysis, the center-of-mass distance between RRM1 and RRM2 displayed substantial fluctuations in the apo system, including transient domain compact events. RNA binding reduced these fluctuations, indicating enhanced interdomain coupling. In contrast, the ligand-bound system exhibited interdomain distance profiles comparable to the RNA- bound state, without further attenuation of domain mobility. These results suggest that

Withanolide D preserves the RNA-stabilized interdomain arrangement rather than imposing additional structural restraint.

To evaluate whether ligand binding at the interdomain cleft is dynamically coupled to the RNA-binding region, we quantified the relationship between ligand proximity and protein–RNA interaction metrics over the 200 ns trajectory. This analysis revealed weak but statistically significant relationships between ligand proximity and both protein–RNA contact counts (Spearman ρ = −0.099, p < 10–5) and RNA-binding-site geometry (ρ = 0.125, p < 10–7), indicating subtle coupling between the ligand-binding interface and RNA-contact behavior as shown in Figure S4.

State-based analysis further showed that frames in which the ligand was positioned closer to the interdomain hotspot exhibited increased protein–RNA contacts and reduced RNA-site distances compared to ligand-distant states (p < 0.001), suggesting that ligand association is accompanied by subtle shifts in RNA-binding region dynamics.

Although these effects are modest in magnitude, they provide evidence consistent with weak dynamic coupling between the ligand-binding interface and RNA-contact behavior. These observations are consistent with indirect coupling within the conformational ensemble.

Ensemble Docking and Ligand-Induced Modulation of the UP1 Conformational Landscape

Ensemble docking analysis was performed using Withanolide D to evaluate how conformational states of the UP1 domain influence ligand-binding behavior. Docking was carried out across molecular dynamics-derived conformations representing both apo and RNA-bound (holo) states of the protein. The average binding energies obtained for apo and RNA-bound conformations were comparable (−8.54 ± 0.65 kcal/mol and −8.75 ± 0.67 kcal/mol, respectively), with no statistically significant difference (t = 0.711, p = 0.486). These results indicate that RNA binding does not substantially alter the overall binding affinity landscape for Withanolide D.

To evaluate whether the selected ensemble docking conformations adequately represented the accessible structural landscape, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the MD trajectories using Cα atomic fluctuations. Projection of the trajectories onto the first two principal components revealed broad conformational sampling for both apo and RNA-bound states, with the apo trajectory exhibiting comparatively wider dispersion (Figure B). Importantly, the ensemble docking snapshots were distributed across multiple conformational regions within the PCA landscape, indicating that the selected receptor ensemble captured substantial conformational diversity rather than a single dominant structural state.

Clustering analysis of the ensemble docking conformations in PCA space further demonstrated that both apo and RNA-bound structures occupied multiple conformational basins (Figure A). Several regions showed preferential enrichment of RNA-bound conformations, suggesting that RNA association modulates the accessibility of specific structural substates that may influence ligand-binding orientations and local interaction environments.

These observations are consistent with a conformational selection model, in which ligand-binding sites become differentially accessible depending on the underlying protein conformational state rather than being induced solely by ligand interaction.

Importantly, while these findings indicate that ligand binding is influenced by RNA-dependent conformational states, they do not demonstrate a direct mechanistic or causal pathway linking ligand binding to functional modulation of RNA recognition. Instead, the results support a model in which ligand association is accompanied by modest redistribution of conformational states and dynamic variance within the protein ensemble.

Consistent with this interpretation, molecular dynamics analyses revealed that ligand binding does not induce global rigidification or strong suppression of interdomain motion but rather produces localized interaction hotspots and subtle shifts in flexibility, particularly in distal regions. Correlation analyses further indicated only weak but statistically significant coupling between ligand proximity and RNA-binding region metrics, suggesting that any functional linkage is likely indirect and limited in magnitude.

Taken together, these results indicate that Withanolide D can associate with the interdomain surface of the UP1 domain and influence its conformational landscape in a quantitatively modest and dynamically distributed manner, rather than acting as a strong global modulator. Further experimental validation or advanced computational approaches (e.g., perturbation-response scanning or network analysis) would be required to establish a definitive mechanistic pathway.

Protein–RNA Interaction Energetics and Contact Dynamics

The RNA-bound system exhibited favorable protein–RNA interactions characterized by substantial van der Waals and electrostatic contributions (Figure ). Specifically, the RNA-bound complex displayed a van der Waals interaction energy of ΔLJ = −25.49 kcal/mol and an electrostatic contribution of ΔCoul = −21.83 kcal/mol, yielding a total interaction energy of −47.32 kcal/mol. These values indicate stable recognition of the RNA motif by the UP1 domain, consistent with the known role of aromatic and positively charged residues within RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) in stabilizing RNA binding through stacking and electrostatic interactions.

(B) Electrostatic interaction energies between UP1 and RNA. (C) Residue-wise decomposition analysis showing energetic contributions of key RNA-binding residues within the UP1 domain. The holo system exhibits reduced interaction energies for several aromatic and positively charged residues involved in RNA recognition, suggesting ligand-induced weakening of the protein–RNA interface.

Upon binding of Withanolide D, a modest reduction in the protein–RNA interaction strength was observed. In the holo system, the van der Waals and electrostatic contributions decreased to ΔLJ = −22.91 kcal/mol and ΔCoul = −19.62 kcal/mol, respectively, resulting in a total interaction energy of −42.53 kcal/mol. This corresponds to an overall weakening of approximately 4.8 kcal/mol relative to the RNA-bound system, suggesting that ligand binding induces subtle conformational changes that partially destabilize the UP1–RNA interface.

Residue-wise energy decomposition further supports this observation (Figure C), revealing that several key RNA-interacting residuesincluding Phe17, Phe23, Arg47, Arg55, Phe59, Lys87, Arg88, and Arg92exhibit reduced energetic contributions in the holo system. Many of these residues belong to the canonical RRM aromatic and basic residue clusters responsible for RNA stacking and phosphate backbone stabilization. The reduced energetic contributions of these residues in the ligand-bound state suggest that Withanolide D binding subtly perturbs the RNA- binding surface, weakening key contacts that stabilize the protein–RNA complex.

Together, these results indicate that Withanolide D does not completely disrupt RNA binding but induces a measurable attenuation of interaction strength, consistent with ligand-associated modulation of protein–RNA interactions. This energetic weakening, combined with the residue-level changes observed in the decomposition analysis, is consistent with the hypothesis that ligand binding may influence the conformational dynamics of the UP1 RNA-binding interface, thereby partially destabilizing the protein–RNA complex.

Discussion

The primary objective of the present study was to evaluate whether small-molecule binding at the interdomain interface can influence the conformational landscape of the protein without directly occluding canonical RNA-binding surfaces. Blind docking across the entire protein surface revealed that only a small fraction of poses (∼1.6%) localized to the interdomain cleft, a value that did not exceed random spatial expectation based on a Monte Carlo null model.

In hnRNPA1, this interdomain region has been implicated in modulating communication between the RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) and has been described as a regulatory hotspot in related RRM-containing proteins. The overall results indicate that the cleft is not a globally preferred binding region under unbiased sampling conditions. However, clustering analysis demonstrated that poses accessing this region converge into a geometrically coherent and reproducible binding mode across chemically diverse ligands. Similar behavior has been reported for flexible protein surfaces in which conditionally accessible binding regions emerge through conformational fluctuations rather than constituting deeply preformed pockets. Ligand-binding sites in flexible proteins may not be strongly enriched in global docking distributions but can still represent structurally meaningful interaction regions when accessed.

In this context, the interdomain cleft of UP1 appears to function as a conditionally accessible binding surface rather than a dominant or energetically favored pocket. Within this framework, Withanolide D emerged as a suitable candidate for further analysis. Although not the top-ranked compound based solely on docking affinity, it fell within the top 5% of screened ligands and exhibited physicochemical properties consistent with drug-like behavior, including moderate molecular weight (∼470 Da), balanced lipophilicity, and controlled hydrogen-bonding capacity. This combination of ranking and physicochemical suitability supports its selection as a representative scaffold for probing ligand-associated dynamic effects on UP1 conformational behavior.

Withanolide D belongs to a broader class of steroidal lactones derived from Withania somnifera, compounds that have been widely reported to modulate cellular signaling pathways, reduce neuroinflammatory responses, and influence protein–ligand interactions in diverse biological contexts. − Although these studies are not directly related to hnRNPA1, they highlight the capacity of withanolide scaffolds to engage protein targets and modulate dynamic molecular processes. In the present context, the semirigid core and flexible substituents of Withanolide D may facilitate localized and adaptable binding within the interdomain region, supporting its use as a tractable probe for evaluating ligand-associated dynamic effects in RNA-binding proteins.

Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that all three representative ligands, Amentoflavone, Ginkgetin, and Withanolide D form stable complexes within the interdomain region. In particular, Withanolide D exhibited persistent localization at the RRM1–RRM2 interface, with a dominant interaction hotspot centered on SER85 and ARG86. Despite this localized stability, global structural analyses indicated that ligand binding does not induce rigidification of the protein. Instead, RMSD, RMSF, and radius of gyration analyses revealed modest increases in conformational variability, particularly in distal regions. These findings suggest that ligand binding is associated with localized stabilization coupled with redistribution of conformational flexibility, rather than global structural stabilization. This behavior is consistent with prior studies on RNA-binding proteins, where ligand association often modulates dynamic ensembles without inducing large-scale structural locking.

It is important to note that the energetic estimates derived from MM-PBSA analysis represent relative enthalpic contributions to ligand–protein interactions, as entropy terms (−TΔS) were not included in the calculations. Consequently, the reported values should be interpreted comparatively rather than as absolute binding free energies. Within this context, the observed differences between ligands provide a qualitative basis for ranking interaction strengths but do not imply precise thermodynamic quantification.

Comparative simulations of apo, RNA-bound, and ligand-bound states demonstrated that RNA binding is the primary determinant of UP1 conformational stability. The RNA-bound system exhibited reduced fluctuation amplitude and attenuated interdomain anticorrelated motion relative to the apo state, indicating enhanced interdomain coupling. In contrast, addition of Withanolide D did not further suppress interdomain dynamics. Instead, the ligand-bound system retained dynamic characteristics similar to the RNA-bound state, with only modest redistribution of conformational variance. These results indicate that ligand association does not override RNA-mediated stabilization but rather operates within an already constrained conformational ensemble. This observation aligns with established models of RNA recognition motifs, in which RNA binding stabilizes domain orientation while preserving residual flexibility necessary for functional adaptability.

Quantitative analysis of the relationship between ligand positioning and RNA-contact metrics revealed weak but statistically significant correlations. Although frames with closer ligand proximity exhibited subtle but consistent differences in RNA-contact behavior, the magnitude of these effects was small. Importantly, such weak correlations are insufficient to establish a direct mechanistic or causal linkage between ligand binding and functional modulation of RNA recognition. Instead, these results suggest the presence of indirect and distributed coupling within the conformational ensemble, rather than a well-defined allosteric pathway.

From a statistical perspective, correlations of this magnitude (|ρ| ≈ 0.1) are indicative of subtle associations rather than strong mechanistic relationships. Therefore, while ligand association appears to influence the dynamic environment of the RNA-binding interface, the effect is best interpreted as modulatory rather than deterministic.

Ensemble docking analysis further demonstrated that RNA binding alters the accessibility of ligand-binding regions without significantly changing average binding energies. The absence of significant energetic differences between apo and RNA-bound states suggests that ligand affinity is not strongly dependent on conformational state. However, clustering of docking poses revealed that certain binding regions are preferentially sampled in RNA-bound conformations. This behavior is consistent with a conformational selection model, in which ligands preferentially bind to pre-existing conformations rather than inducing new ones. Such a mechanism is widely observed in flexible proteins and supports the interpretation that ligand binding to UP1 is governed by state-dependent accessibility rather than strong induced structural transitions.

Because RNA-binding proteins often lack deeply buried druggable pockets, modulation of dynamically accessible interdomain regions may represent an alternative strategy for influencing RNA-associated regulatory behavior without requiring direct competitive inhibition at canonical RNA-binding interfaces.

The results of this study suggest that the interdomain surface of hnRNPA1 represents a structurally permissive but dynamically regulated region that can accommodate small-molecule binding. However, the modest magnitude of ligand-induced effects indicates that such interactions are unlikely to produce strong functional modulation in isolation. Instead, ligand association may serve to subtly reshape the conformational landscape of the protein, potentially influencing RNA recognition indirectly. This type of modulation may be relevant for targeting RNA-binding proteins, which often rely on dynamic ensembles rather than rigid structural states for function. Although not enriched in global docking distributions, the interdomain cleft exhibits reproducible structural convergence when accessed, supporting its role as a conditionally accessible interaction region.

Limitations and Future Direction

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, docking-based localization does not provide direct evidence of binding affinity or specificity. Second, molecular dynamics simulations, while informative for dynamic behavior, are inherently limited by force-field approximations and finite sampling time scales. Third, the absence of experimental validation prevents direct assessment of functional consequences.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study provides a computational framework for understanding how small molecules may interact with the interdomain surface of the hnRNPA1 UP1 domain and influence its conformational landscape. By integrating molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, ensemble docking, and conformational landscape analysis, the study demonstrates that Withanolide D can stably associate with dynamically accessible interdomain regions across multiple protein conformational states.

Importantly, the results suggest that ligand binding is associated with subtle but reproducible redistribution of conformational populations and localized modulation of protein dynamics, particularly in regions functionally linked to RNA recognition. Although the observed effects are quantitatively modest and do not establish a strong long-range allosteric mechanism, they support the possibility that interdomain ligand association may influence the dynamic conformational equilibrium governing RNA-binding behavior.

The ensemble docking and PCA analyses further indicate that RNA association alters the accessibility of specific conformational substates, highlighting the importance of considering protein conformational heterogeneity in studies of ligand recognition within RNA-binding proteins. These findings provide new insight into the dynamic coupling between ligand association, conformational flexibility, and RNA-bound structural states in hnRNPA1.

Given the central role of hnRNPA1 in RNA metabolism and disease-associated dysregulation, the present work establishes a basis for future experimental validation and advanced computational investigations aimed at exploring interdomain ligand targeting as a potential strategy for modulating RNA-binding protein function.
