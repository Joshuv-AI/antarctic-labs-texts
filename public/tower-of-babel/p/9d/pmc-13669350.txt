From association to causality: mechanistic pathways linking the gut microbiome to psychiatric disorders
Authors: Sultan Almarzooqi, Lidya K. Yassin, Fatima Alnuaimi, Salma Alketbi, Jurga Skrabulyte-Barbulescu, Noud AlAhbabi, Dana Alremeithi, Rawdha Almarashda, Saif Almazrouei, Safa Shehab, Yauhen Statsenko, Mohammad I. K. Hamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The microbiota–gut–brain axis (MGBA) has emerged as a key framework for understanding how peripheral biological systems influence brain function and behaviour. However, despite extensive associative evidence linking gut microbiome to psychiatric disorders, robust causal and mechanistic insights remain limited. This review critically evaluates current evidence to determine whether microbiome alterations contribute to psychiatric pathophysiology and inform therapeutic strategies. We outline methodological frameworks for causal inference, highlighting the limitations of cross-sectional designs and the need for convergent evidence from longitudinal studies, experimental models, and human genetic approaches. We then synthesize mechanistic pathways linking the microbiota to brain function, including immune signaling, neuroendocrine regulation via the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, neural communication through vagal and enteric pathways, and intestinal and blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity. Across these systems, microbial metabolites and immune mediators emerge as key mediators, although direct causal mechanisms in humans remain incompletely established. Disorder-specific evaluation across major depressive disorder (MDD), anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder (BD), schizophrenia (SCZ), and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) reveals heterogeneous but converging evidence for microbiome involvement. Although preclinical and interventional studies support biological plausibility, human evidence remains constrained by confounding, variability, and limited mechanistic validation. Translational strategies, including psychobiotics, dietary interventions, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), and microbiome-based biomarkers, show promise but remain methodologically limited. Overall, the gut microbiome represents a biologically plausible and modifiable contributor to psychiatric disorders. Advancing toward clinical application will require integrative, longitudinal, and mechanism-driven research to enable precision psychiatry grounded in causal evidence.

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders remain difficult to explain through brain-centric models alone. Although genetics, neurodevelopment, neurotransmitter signaling, and psychosocial stress are central to psychiatric pathophysiology, they do not fully account for the heterogeneity, systemic features, or variable treatment responses observed across conditions such as major depressive disorder (MDD), anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder (BD), schizophrenia (SCZ), and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Within this context, the microbiota–gut–brain axis (MGBA) has emerged as a framework for understanding how intestinal microbial communities may influence brain function, behaviour, affective regulation, and vulnerability to psychiatric illness [1–3].

The human gut microbiome is shaped by host genetics, diet, medication exposure, stress, and immune activity. Through metabolic, immune, endocrine, and neural routes, microbial signals may influence host physiology beyond the intestine. Key candidate mediators include short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan and kynurenine metabolites, bile acid derivatives, microbial cell wall components, and immune mediators [4, 5]. These molecules may affect psychiatric-relevant pathways, including systemic inflammation, blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, microglial activation, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA)-axis regulation, vagal and enteric signaling, neurotransmitter metabolism, and synaptic plasticity.

Despite growing evidence linking microbiome alterations to psychiatric disorders, a central question remains unresolved: do these alterations contribute causally to psychiatric pathophysiology, or do they mainly reflect downstream effects of illness, medication, diet, lifestyle, and chronic stress? This distinction is critical because associative findings may support biomarker discovery, whereas causal and mechanistic evidence is needed to justify microbiome-targeted prevention or treatment strategies. Many current studies remain vulnerable to confounding, reverse causation, and limited reproducibility, particularly given the strong influence of psychotropic medications and other clinical exposures on microbial composition [6–8].

Accordingly, this review frames microbiome psychiatry through a continuum from association to causality. Rather than presenting the microbiome as a universal cause of psychiatric disease, we evaluate whether microbiome-related metabolites and pathways represent biologically plausible, modifiable contributors to psychiatric phenotypes. We examine causal inference standards, summarize key mechanistic routes linking microbial activity to brain function, compare evidence across psychiatric disorders, and assess the translational readiness of microbiome-based interventions. By emphasizing mechanistic clarity, reproducibility, and graded causal evidence, this review aims to clarify when microbiome variation is incidental, when it is consequential, and when it may be therapeutically targeted within precision psychiatry.

Defining and assessing causality in microbiome brain research

In this review, we use causality to refer to evidence that microbiome variation changes psychiatric outcomes; causal inference to refer to the methods used to estimate or test such causal effects in human and experimental research; and mechanistic contribution to refer to biologically plausible pathways through which the microbiome may influence host neurobiology, even when definitive causal effects in humans remain unproven. The rapid expansion of research on the microbiome–gut–brain axis (MGBA) has generated compelling associations between gut microbial composition and a wide range of psychiatric disorders. However, the field now faces a critical inflection point: while correlations are abundant, robust causal evidence remains limited. As several scholars have cautioned, equating microbial associations with mechanistic influence risks overstating the evidentiary strength of current findings [7, 8]. For translational psychiatry, this distinction is not merely conceptual; it determines whether microbiome-targeted interventions can be rationally designed, tested, and implemented. Causality in microbiome brain research is inherently complex. Gut microbial communities operate within a dynamic host environment shaped by diet, stress, medication exposure, immune signaling, and neuroendocrine activity. Psychiatric conditions themselves can alter many of these factors, introducing substantial risks of reverse causation and confounding [7, 8]. Traditional infectious disease frameworks, such as Koch’s postulates, are insufficient to capture the ecological and multispecies nature of microbial systems [7]. Accordingly, establishing causality requires a structured, multimethod approach integrating conceptual frameworks, experimental manipulation, human genetic inference, and translational validation [7, 8] as shown in (Fig. 1). The following sections outline the principal methodological standards used to define and assess causal evidence in microbiome brain research, with emphasis on their strengths, limitations, and relevance for psychiatric translation. In this chapter, causal inference is conceptualized as a graded hierarchy of evidence: (i) statistical association with appropriate confounder control, (ii) temporal precedence in longitudinal designs, (iii) experimental manipulation demonstrating necessity or sufficiency, (iv) human genetic or interventional validation, and (v) translational reproducibility across independent cohorts. From association to causal criteria

Establishing causality in microbiome brain research requires adapting classical epidemiological reasoning to complex microbial ecosystems. The Bradford Hill criteria remain relevant, particularly regarding temporality, consistency, plausibility, and experimental evidence. Contemporary epidemiological scholarship emphasizes that Hill’s viewpoints function as guiding heuristics rather than rigid rules and must be interpreted within a pluralistic evidentiary context [9]. However, microbiome studies often show modest effect sizes and inconsistent replication across cohorts due to environmental variability and methodological heterogeneity [7, 10]. Researchers studying microbiome associations should anticipate an expected balance between two research factors, which include effect size and cross-cohort consistency. The multiple factors that shape microbial composition through their interactive effects with dietary patterns, medication usage, geographical location, host genetic makeup, and technical measurement differences lead to biologically relevant associations that display minor strength and fail to produce exact taxonomic replication across diverse study groups. Microbiome evidence achieves greater reliability when it receives backing from consistent directional findings or functional patterns that converge rather than from significant organism-specific changes that occur in particular disorders [11–13]. Temporality is especially critical. Most psychiatric microbiome studies are cross-sectional, limiting the ability to determine whether microbial alterations precede disease onset or arise secondary to behavioural, dietary, or pharmacological changes [14]. Moreover, traditional pathogen-focused logic, such as adaptations of Koch’s postulates, must be reconsidered in community-level systems where functional consortia rather than single organisms may drive host effects [15]. Thus, causality in microbiome–brain research is best conceptualized as a probabilistic and graded construct rather than a binary determination.

Causal modeling and confounding

Distinguishing causation from confounding is one of the central methodological challenges in microbiome brain research. Host genetics, diet, medication exposure, inflammation, stress, and socioeconomic factors can simultaneously influence microbial composition and psychiatric outcomes, creating complex causal networks. The psychiatric microbiome research field requires scientists to recognize psychotropic medications as active factors that disrupt causal relationships in studies, instead of treating these medications as standard background variables. Antidepressants and antipsychotics, together with mood stabilizers and related agents, can change gut bacterial composition through their direct effects, their ability to kill certain bacterial strains, and their capacity to affect bacterial drug metabolism through both direct and indirect relationships. Psychotropic exposure needs proper modeling because it directly connects with illness severity and chronicity, metabolic status, and treatment history. Because improper modeling leads to false microbiome-psychiatric connections and prevents the identification of disorder-relevant signals, researchers must model psychotropic exposure correctly. The field requires causal modeling to include psychotropic use as a factor that changes with time through its effects on health outcomes [16]. Directed acyclic graphs provide a structured framework for representing these dependencies and clarifying potential backdoor pathways that may generate spurious associations [14]. These approaches are grounded in the counterfactual framework of causal inference, which formalizes assumptions about potential outcomes and target trial emulation. Structural equation modeling further illustrates how shared upstream variables can produce microbiome psychiatric correlations even in the absence of direct biological effects [17]. Structural causal models provide a formal mathematical basis for representing causal assumptions and identifying identifiable intervention effects under specified conditions [18]. Given the high dimensionality of microbiome datasets, traditional adjustment strategies may be insufficient. Stratified modeling and statistical standardization techniques have been proposed to mitigate confounding and estimate population-level microbial effects under complex dependency structures [19]. These frameworks underscore that causal interpretation requires explicit modeling of assumptions and counterfactual structures, rather than reliance on statistical association alone, reinforcing the need for methodological transparency in translational research [20].

Experimental evidence for causality

Experimental models have been central to advancing causal inference in microbiome brain research, particularly through germ-free (GF) and gnotobiotic animal systems. These models allow controlled manipulation of microbial communities, enabling direct testing of necessity and sufficiency. Researchers establish that a microbial factor or pathway is essential for neurobiological or behavioral effects to take place because they demonstrate that removing or disrupting this element leads to decreased expression of the corresponding phenotype. Scientists demonstrate that introducing or transferring a specific microbial community, taxon, or metabolite can produce the effect in a host that initially showed no symptoms of the condition. Studies using GF animals have demonstrated that the absence of microbiota alters stress reactivity, neurodevelopment, immune signaling, and neurotransmitter pathways, supporting a functional role of microbes in brain physiology [21]. Colonization with defined microbial consortia can partially restore these alterations, providing evidence that microbial signals influence host neurobiology. Such experiments demonstrate microbial necessity and partial reversibility but do not fully resolve pathway specificity or clinical generalizability. However, the interpretability of GF models remains limited. It should also be noted that GF and related depletion models may overestimate effect sizes relative to the subtler and more heterogeneous forms of dysbiosis observed in humans. Because these models involve near-complete microbial absence or marked perturbation, the resulting phenotypes are likely to be larger and more internally coherent than those arising from naturally occurring microbiome variation in clinical populations. Experimental findings from such systems are therefore highly informative for mechanism discovery, but their magnitude should not be assumed to map directly onto human psychiatric effect sizes [22]. GF animals exhibit atypical immune maturation, altered BBB permeability, and differences in neurodevelopment that persist even after microbial reconstitution [23]. These developmental artifacts complicate the distinction between microbial effects and host adaptations to sterile conditions. Consequently, while GF models demonstrate biological plausibility, their translational relevance to human psychiatric conditions requires cautious interpretation. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments further contribute to causal inference by testing whether microbial communities are sufficient to transfer behavioral or physiological traits. In several preclinical studies, transplantation of microbiota from individuals with psychiatric symptoms into recipient animals has reproduced aspects of anxiety-like or depressive-like behavior, supporting the hypothesis that microbial configurations can modulate brain function. However, replication across laboratories is inconsistent, and observed effects may depend on host–microbiome compatibility, housing conditions, or experimental design variability [7]. Moreover, FMT cannot distinguish whether transferred effects arise from specific taxa, community interactions, or microbial metabolites. Without identifying the active mechanisms, FMT provides evidence of sufficiency but not specificity. As emphasized in recent consensus statements, experimental models should be interpreted as mechanistic probes rather than direct analogues of human disease [10]. Taken together, experimental approaches demonstrate that microbial signals can influence neurobiological processes relevant to psychiatric outcomes. Yet, their strength lies in mechanistic exploration rather than definitive causal confirmation. Robust causal inference requires integrating experimental findings with human data and conceptual modeling.

Human causal inference approaches

While experimental models provide mechanistic insight, establishing causality in microbiome brain research ultimately requires evidence from human populations. Longitudinal cohort studies represent a critical step by enabling the assessment of temporality. By tracking microbial composition before symptom emergence, these designs help evaluate whether microbiome alterations precede, rather than follow, psychiatric change. In psychiatric research, such designs are essential to disentangle whether microbial shifts precede symptom onset or arise secondary to altered diet, sleep, medication use, or stress physiology. However, even prospective studies remain vulnerable to confounding from lifestyle, diet, medications, and stress-related physiology [14]. To strengthen inference within observational human data, design-based causal approaches grounded in counterfactual reasoning, particularly those based on the Rubin causal model, can be used to emulate target trials and make assumptions explicit [24]. Interventional studies provide stronger causal leverage by testing whether targeted manipulation of the microbiome changes psychiatric outcomes. Trials involving probiotics, dietary interventions, and FMT have reported modest improvements in selected symptom domains, but heterogeneity in interventions, small sample sizes, and variability in microbial engraftment limit interpretability. Importantly, observed clinical changes may reflect indirect effects such as placebo response, metabolic shifts, or immune modulation rather than direct microbial signaling, underscoring the need for mechanistic readouts alongside symptoms [10]. Genetic epidemiological approaches, particularly Mendelian randomization (MR), have emerged as an additional strategy to strengthen causal inference. By using host genetic variants associated with microbial traits as instrumental variables, MR can reduce confounding and limit reverse causation, and recent work has applied MR across psychiatric phenotypes to suggest potentially bidirectional relationships between microbial taxa and mental health outcomes [25, 26]. MR leverages the random allocation of alleles at conception as a natural experiment to strengthen causal inference under specific instrumental variable assumptions [27]. Nonetheless, MR rests on instrument validity assumptions and may miss context-dependent or environmentally driven microbiome effects. Moreover, most microbiome-associated genetic instruments explain only a small proportion of microbial variance, potentially limiting statistical power and interpretability. Transparent reporting and careful evaluation of instrument strength, horizontal pleiotropy, and population stratification are essential for valid interpretation [28].

Finally, advances in mediation analysis and causal machine learning expand the toolkit for disentangling complex microbiome host interactions. Causal mediation frameworks allow formal testing of whether microbial features lie on the pathway linking exposures (e.g., stress, diet, medications) to psychiatric outcomes, estimating indirect effects while adjusting for measured confounders [28]. In parallel, causal machine learning and econometric approaches address high dimensionality and non-linearity, supporting more robust effect estimation under complex confounding structures [17, 29]. Collectively, these human-based approaches bridge experimental plausibility and clinical relevance. However, no single method is decisive; robust causal inference in microbiome brain research depends on converging evidence across longitudinal, interventional, genetic, and computational lines of inquiry.

Mechanistic pathways linking microbiota to brain function

Immune-mediated pathways

This section evaluates mechanistic contribution and biological plausibility, not definitive human causality. Over the past decade, the MGBA has become a key framework for understanding how peripheral systems influence the central nervous system (CNS). Early work mainly described associations between GM and psychiatric phenotypes, but immune signaling is now recognised as a central conduit linking microbial dynamics to brain and behaviour [30, 31]. The immune system acts as an active interface through which microbial signals are converted into neurobiological change [32, 33]. Human data support a role for immune dysregulation in psychiatric disorders. Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL-6), tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α and IL-1β, and acute phase proteins such as C-reactive protein (CRP), are repeatedly observed in subsets of individuals with MDD, SCZ, BD and stress-related conditions [31, 32, 34, 35]. Higher inflammatory burden is associated with greater symptom severity, cognitive impairment and reduced response to conventional antidepressants, suggesting that immune activation is not merely epiphenomenal [35, 36]. Neuroimaging and postmortem work further implicate neuroinflammatory changes and microglial activation in specific patient subgroups [31, 32]. In parallel, mucosal immunology and microbiome research show that gut microorganisms strongly regulate host immunity. Commensal microbes shape innate and adaptive immune development, influence T cell differentiation, tune inflammatory tone, and help maintain barrier integrity [30, 37]. Dysbiosis, whether driven by stress, infection, diet, or antibiotics, can disturb this balance, increasing intestinal permeability, allowing translocation of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and activating systemic inflammatory pathways [31, 33, 38].

Together, these findings raise a key mechanistic question: This section evaluates whether immune signaling satisfies criteria for a causal mediator linking microbiota alterations to psychiatric outcomes, rather than representing a secondary correlate of disease. To what extent do microbiome-driven immune changes contribute to psychiatric pathophysiology, although causality in humans remains incompletely established? Immune signaling meets several criteria for a plausible mediator. Microbial products and metabolites engage pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), inflammasome complexes, and cytokine cascades in peripheral tissues [35, 37], while circulating inflammatory mediators can alter BBB permeability, activate endothelial cells, and influence microglial state [31, 39]. Experimental manipulation of immune pathways using GF models, faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), cytokine administration, or anti-inflammatory treatment can induce or ameliorate behavioural phenotypes relevant to depression, anxiety, and psychosis [30, 31, 35, 40]. Importantly, these effects appear stratified rather than universal. Not all patients show elevated inflammatory markers, and microbiome alterations do not always lead to systemic inflammation [32, 34]. Immune signaling is therefore best conceptualised as a context-dependent mediator that may define biologically distinct subtypes of psychiatric disorders [36, 41]. Such stratification has clear translational implications, as immune-targeted or microbiota-modulating interventions may preferentially benefit individuals characterised by inflammatory dysregulation [30, 36].

Microbiota regulation of peripheral immunity

Pattern recognition signaling and innate immune activation

The intestinal immune system maintains continuous interaction with the gut microbiome (GM), supporting immune tolerance under homeostatic conditions. Dysbiosis, however, shifts this balance toward sustained immune activation. Microbial components such as LPS engage pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), activating NF-κB signaling and promoting production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) [35]. Elevated circulating markers of endotoxemia, such as LPS-binding protein (LBP), have been reported in subsets of patients with mood and psychotic disorders. Dysbiosis may also activate inflammasome pathways, particularly NLRP3, which regulates IL-1β and IL-18 maturation. Preclinical studies show that microbiota alterations can prime inflammasome activation and induce depression-like behaviours, while inhibition of these pathways attenuates symptoms. Human studies similarly report increased inflammasome-related markers in psychiatric populations. Although largely associative, these findings support a role for microbiota-driven immune activation in a subset of patients.

Intestinal barrier integrity and low-grade endotoxemia

The intestinal barrier regulates systemic exposure to microbial products. Dysbiosis, stress, and inflammation can disrupt tight junction integrity, increasing gut permeability (“leaky gut”) and allowing translocation of microbial components such as LPS into circulation, thereby promoting systemic inflammation [31, 33]. Clinical studies report markers of barrier dysfunction in psychiatric disorders, including elevated zonulin, endotoxin antibodies, and LBP. For example, increased IgA and IgM responses to gram-negative bacterial LPS have been observed in depressed patients, suggesting bacterial translocation and immune activation [42]. However, causality remains unclear due to the cross-sectional design. Preclinical models provide stronger mechanistic support: stress-induced dysbiosis increases permeability, inflammation, and behavioural changes, while microbiota-targeted interventions can partially restore barrier function and reduce inflammatory responses. These findings support a pathway linking dysbiosis, barrier disruption, and immune activation.

Microbial metabolites as immune regulators

Microbial metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), play a key role in immune regulation. SCFAs promote regulatory T cell differentiation, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and support epithelial barrier integrity [38]. Reduced abundance of SCFA-producing taxa has been consistently reported in depression and related disorders. Large cohort studies show depletion of butyrate-producing genera such as Coprococcus and Dialister in depression, alongside associations between microbial metabolic capacity and quality-of-life measures [43]. Tryptophan metabolism represents another critical pathway. Inflammatory activation shifts tryptophan toward the kynurenine pathway via indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), generating neuroactive metabolites such as quinolinic acid. Altered kynurenine metabolism has been observed in depressive and psychotic disorders, linking microbial metabolism, immune activation, and neurotransmission. The MGBA requires gut hormone research because they function as a downstream effector that develops from microbial metabolites, SCFAs, tryptophan derivatives, and bile acids. The gut microbiota system can control glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion because its microbial fermentation products create bile acid signaling pathways, which activate enteroendocrine L-cells through SCFA-responsive receptors and through bile acid–TGR5 pathways. GLP-1 signaling controls glucose metabolism and digestive system function, hunger control, and immune system metabolic activities, which establish it as a potential connection between microbial metabolism and body metabolism and brain function. The existing evidence mainly comes from studies about metabolic conditions, yet the framework with GLP-1 inclusion brings forth a discussion about gut bacteria signaling, which extends past traditional metabolites and reveals a new hormonal pathway through which gut bacteria affect neurobehavioral outcomes [44].

The microbiota–Th17 cell–brain infiltration pathway represents one adaptive immune mechanism that requires special attention. Specific gut microbes, classically segmented filamentous bacteria in preclinical models, can promote intestinal Th17 differentiation while microbiota-dependent epithelial signals help determine whether these cells maintain homeostatic functions or develop pathogenic inflammatory characteristics. The Th17 cells, which have multiplied, will release their effector cytokines, especially IL-17A and IL-22, which will disrupt tight-junction integrity in brain endothelial cells while inducing chemokine and adhesion molecule production and enabling leukocyte movement through the BBB. The pathway holds biological significance because Th17 cell infiltration and IL-17 signaling activation led to increased microglial activation and neuroinflammation. The study found that Th17 bacteria developed in the gut from microbiota that accumulated in the hippocampus for the psychiatric study. The study also found that IL-17A neutralization reduced microglial activation, which helped reduce behavioral abnormalities. Overall, the GM shapes peripheral immune tone through both structural components and metabolites, influencing inflammatory responses and potentially modulating psychiatric risk [45, 46].

From systemic inflammation to neuroinflammation

Peripheral immune activation alone does not explain psychiatric symptoms unless there are biologically credible routes by which systemic inflammatory signals influence the brain. Accumulating evidence indicates that such routes are both anatomically and molecularly plausible. Circulating cytokines, microbial products, and other immune mediators can affect the CNS via BBB modulation, endothelial activation, neural signaling through afferent nerves, and direct engagement of brain resident immune cells.

Blood-brain barrier dynamics and endothelial signaling

The BBB serves as a selectively permeable interface between the systemic circulation and the CNS. Under physiological conditions, tight junctions between endothelial cells tightly regulate the passage of molecules and immune cells. Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β can alter BBB integrity by modulating tight junction proteins and increasing vascular permeability. As described above, microbiota-associated barrier dysfunction and microbial translocation may amplify systemic inflammatory signaling; at the neurovascular level, these processes are relevant insofar as they compromise BBB integrity and facilitate neuroimmune communication [47]. Human data, although indirect, are increasingly supportive: elevated peripheral inflammatory markers associate with neuroimaging indices of vascular dysfunction and altered BBB integrity, as well as greater central penetration of immune mediators. Beyond overt permeability changes, endothelial cells function as immune sensors; cytokine exposure induces prostaglandins and chemokines that signal to adjacent neural tissue without frank barrier breakdown. Even relatively subtle peripheral immune activation may therefore influence central function via endothelial transduction mechanisms.

Microglial priming and activation

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS, represent a central node in immune-mediated neurobiological change. Under homeostatic conditions, microglia survey the neural environment and support synaptic pruning, plasticity, and repair. Peripheral inflammatory signaling can shift microglia toward a primed or activated phenotype characterised by increased cytokine production and heightened responsiveness to secondary insults. GF models have been particularly informative. Microglia from GF mice show immature morphology and impaired immune responsiveness, which can be partially restored by colonisation with complex microbiota or short-chain fatty acid administration. These findings indicate that microbial signals are required for normal microglial development and that dysbiosis-associated inflammation may promote exaggerated microglial activation. Peripheral cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α can signal across the BBB via transport systems or indirectly via endothelial intermediates, while systemic inflammation increases central chemokine expression and recruitment of peripheral immune cells. Once activated, microglia release IL-1β, reactive oxygen species, and excitatory mediators, contributing to synaptic remodelling and altered neuronal signaling. Imaging studies using ligands that bind to markers of microglial activation, such as the translocator protein (TSPO), have reported increased signal in subsets of individuals with depression and SCZ, particularly in those with elevated peripheral inflammation, supporting the plausibility of an immune microglial contribution to psychiatric symptomatology.

Inflammasomes, cytokine cascades and neurotransmitter systems

Inflammasome signaling provides another mechanism linking immune activation to neural dysfunction. Peripheral activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome increases maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, cytokines capable of shaping synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and monoaminergic neurotransmission [47]. In rodent stress models, pharmacological or genetic inhibition of NLRP3 reduces central cytokine levels, attenuates depressive-like behaviours, and can restore aspects of hippocampal neurogenesis, suggesting that inflammasome activation is a functional mediator rather than a passive marker [47]. Inflammatory signaling also intersects with neurotransmitter systems. Pro-inflammatory cytokines upregulate IDO, diverting tryptophan into the kynurenine pathway and generating metabolites such as quinolinic acid, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor agonist with excitotoxic and pro-inflammatory properties. Altered kynurenine metabolism has been reported in depressed and psychotic cohorts and is increasingly viewed as a link between immune activation and glutamatergic dysregulation. Cytokines additionally influence dopamine synthesis, release, and reuptake, providing a mechanistic framework relevant to psychosis and motivational deficits. Collectively, these pathways illustrate how systemic immune activation, potentially initiated or amplified by GM alterations, can propagate centrally through BBB modulation, endothelial signaling, microglial priming, and cytokine-driven effects on neurotransmitter systems, thereby reshaping synaptic plasticity, circuit function, and behaviour. Chronic stress can promote dysbiosis, impair the intestinal epithelial barrier, and alter the production of microbial metabolites. This process increases the passage of microbial substances, which include LPS, into the body, which leads to both local and systemic inflammatory responses. The stress response system activates the HPA-axis function while it releases glucocorticoids, which together disrupt gut barrier protection and the balance of gut bacteria. This process creates a feedback system that links neuroendocrine stress pathways to immune response activation outside the brain. The body-wide disturbances work together to affect the neurovascular unit because cytokines, microbial substances, and activated endothelial cells change the BBB function, and the immune system can communicate with the brain through chemokine signals. The processes that occur in the CNS lead to the activation of microglial cells and astrocytes, which then cause changes in kynurenine and monoaminergic pathways that result in the breakdown of cortico-limbic pathways responsible for mood, salience, and stress response. The MGBA shows how intestinal barrier dysfunction, immune activation, HPA-axis dysregulation, and BBB changes create a series of interdependent processes that maintain each other. The immune-mediated cascades operate differently in distinct disorders and patients because their effects depend on how the host responds to environmental factors and the stage of the disease [30, 48].

Immune pathways and disorder-specific psychiatric phenotypes

Immune-mediated mechanisms linking the GM to the brain do not operate uniformly across psychiatric conditions. Converging evidence suggests that inflammatory signaling characterises biologically distinct subgroups within diagnostic categories, with important implications for both causal inference and targeted intervention.

MDD: the inflammatory subtype

MDD has the strongest evidence for immune dysregulation. Elevated IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP are repeatedly reported in subsets of patients, with higher inflammatory burden associated with greater symptom severity and poorer treatment response. Consistent with the metabolite-related mechanisms outlined above, MDD has also been associated with reduced abundance of SCFA-producing taxa, supporting a potential role for impaired microbial anti-inflammatory capacity in inflammatory subtypes of depression [49]. As outlined above, kynurenine-pathway activation may provide one mechanism linking inflammation to mood dysregulation in inflammatory subtypes of MDD. Interventional data further support a contributory role: several trials report modest antidepressant effects of anti-inflammatory agents and psychobiotic strategies, particularly in patients with elevated inflammatory markers, although results remain heterogeneous. Together, these findings support the concept of an inflammatory MDD subtype in which microbiota-driven immune activation is especially relevant.

SCZ and psychosis: immune activation across developmental windows

In SCZ spectrum disorders, immune alterations are evident at multiple stages. Pro-inflammatory cytokines are elevated in first episode psychosis and acute relapse, with partial normalisation during remission in some cohorts. Human evidence further links gut bacterial translocation directly to SCZ symptom severity. In a case-control study of SCZ patients (n = 80), serum IgA responses to Hafnia alvei, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Morganella morganii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae were significantly elevated in patients with deficit SCZ compared with non-deficit SCZ, consistent with the previously described barrier-translocation pathway and suggesting that this mechanism may be especially relevant to the deficit phenotype. A combined model incorporating IgA responses to K. pneumoniae, IgM responses to five gram-negative enterobacteria, and lowered natural IgM explained 41.5% of the variance in Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) negative subscale scores, indicating that gut bacterial translocation coupled with impaired compensatory immune regulation may contribute to negative symptom severity and neurocognitive impairment [42]. Genetic data implicating immune-related loci and complement pathways further support an immune contribution to risk. Maternal immune activation models demonstrate that prenatal inflammatory insults can produce long-lasting abnormalities in behaviour, microglial activation, and dopamine signaling, and that microbiota composition modulates the severity of these phenotypes. In established SCZ, altered GM profiles have been linked to peripheral inflammation and cognitive impairment, and integrative studies suggest that inflammatory markers may mediate associations between microbial composition and brain structure or function. These convergent lines of evidence support an immune-mediated pathway contributing to psychosis in at least a subset of patients.

Stress-related and anxiety disorders: immune sensitisation and allostatic load

Chronic stress is a potent modifier of both GM and immune function. Stress-related glucocorticoid changes can alter microbial diversity, increase intestinal permeability, and prime innate immune responses, promoting a state of low-grade systemic inflammation. In rodent models, stress-associated dysbiosis increases peripheral cytokines and microglial activation in limbic regions, while microbiota-targeted interventions can mitigate anxiety and depression-like behaviours [47]. Human studies report elevated inflammatory markers in conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder and generalised anxiety disorder, although mechanistic links to the microbiota are less well defined. Overall, the bidirectional relationship between stress physiology, immune signaling, and the gut microbiome suggests that the gut–immune interface contributes to allostatic load and stress-related psychopathology [49].

Heterogeneity and stratification

A consistent theme across disorders is heterogeneity: not all patients show elevated inflammatory markers, and not all microbiome alterations are accompanied by systemic immune activation. This variability likely reflects differences in genetic background, environmental exposures, diet, metabolic status, and developmental timing. From a mechanistic perspective, immune signaling may function as a mediator in some individuals and a modifier in others, depending on whether dysbiosis precedes immune changes or arises secondarily from primary immune or neuroendocrine disturbances. Recognising immune-mediated subtypes has direct translational implications: biomarker-guided stratification using C-reactive protein (CRP), cytokines, and microbiome-derived signatures may help identify patients most likely to respond to microbiota-targeted or anti-inflammatory interventions, in line with emerging precision psychiatry frameworks.

Evidence for causality: experimental and interventional insights

Demonstrating causality in a bidirectional system such as the microbiota-gut-brain axis is challenging. Nevertheless, converging evidence from GF and antibiotic models, FMT, immune challenge paradigms, and interventional trials supports the idea that immune signaling can mediate behavioural effects downstream of microbial alterations [47].

GF and antibiotic-depletion models

GF animals demonstrate in a very direct way that microbial presence is required for normal immune and behavioural development. Compared with conventionally colonised controls, GF mice show altered stress responsivity, changes in anxiety-like and social behaviours, and broad immune abnormalities, including altered cytokine production and lymphoid tissue development. Microglial maturation is also impaired in GF states and can be partially restored by colonisation with complex microbiota or administration of short-chain fatty acids, indicating that microbial signals are necessary for normative neuroimmune development and microglial function. Antibiotic-induced microbiota depletion models further highlight reversibility. Recent neuroanatomical evidence further suggests that antibiotics-induced dysbiosis can alter dendritic morphology of adult cortical interneurons, linking microbial disruption to structural changes in inhibitory circuitry [50–52]. Disruption of microbial communities alters peripheral cytokine profiles, modifies microglial activity and induces depression like or anxiety-like behaviours that can be mitigated when the microbiota is restored or when immune pathways are targeted pharmacologically [47, 53]. For example, Hao and colleagues showed that dysbiosis promotes complement-C3-dependent microglia-mediated synapse pruning and depression like behaviour, and that inhibiting this immune pathway reverses both synaptic and behavioural changes. These models support a mechanistic contribution of immune signaling to microbiota behaviour relationships, while also reminding us that GF conditions and high-dose antibiotic exposure do not fully recapitulate the more subtle patterns of dysbiosis observed in human psychiatric disorders [23].

FMT and phenotype transfer

FMT offers more direct evidence that microbial communities can transfer behavioural and immune phenotypes across hosts. Transplantation of microbiota from individuals with MDD into microbiota-depleted rodents has been reported to induce anhedonia-like and anxiety-like behaviours, alongside changes in circulating cytokines and shifts in tryptophan kynurenine metabolism [47, 54]. In the study by Cai and colleagues, FMT from stressed or depressed donors not only reproduced depressive behaviours in recipient mice but also increased peripheral inflammatory markers and altered hippocampal neurogenesis, effects that could be partly normalised by transplantation from healthy donors [54]. Similar approaches using microbiota from patients with psychotic disorders have produced changes in behaviour and neurochemistry in animal recipients. Transplantation of microbiota from drug-free individuals with SCZ has been shown to induce SCZ like behaviours and dysregulated kynurenine metabolism in mice, together with changes in immune markers. Key preclinical studies using SCZ-derived microbiota or SCZ-enriched bacterial transplantation are summarized in (Table 1). Outside classic psychiatry, FMT from patients with post-stroke cognitive impairment leads to worse cognition, higher circulating LPS and pro-inflammatory cytokines, and reduced butyrate in recipient animals, again suggesting co-transfer of microbial, immune, and behavioural features [55]. In several of these paradigms, immune alterations co-transfer with behavioural changes, and blockade of specific inflammatory pathways, including complement signaling and cytokine cascades, attenuates these effects [40]. Cytokine administration and immune challenge models

Immune challenge studies show that systemic inflammation alone can generate symptoms that resemble psychiatric states. Interferon-based treatments frequently precipitate depressive syndromes, and experimental endotoxin administration in healthy volunteers raises IL-6 and other cytokines while inducing transient low mood, social withdrawal, and reduced reward responsivity [56]. In animal models, peripheral cytokines or LPS activate microglia, disrupt monoaminergic and glutamatergic signaling, and produce depression like behaviours that are reduced by anti-inflammatory drugs or genetic attenuation of immune pathways [47]. Non-human primate work with interferon alpha further supports a direct link between cytokine exposure, monoamine changes, and depressive behaviour [57]. These convergent findings indicate that inflammatory activation is sufficient, at least in some individuals and contexts, to reshape mood and motivation in clinically meaningful ways.

Anti-inflammatory and microbiota-targeted interventions

Interventions that lower inflammation or alter the microbiota provide evidence for partial reversibility. Anti-inflammatory agents, such as nonsteroidal drugs, cytokine antagonists, and minocycline show modest antidepressant or adjunctive benefits, particularly in patients with higher baseline inflammation [58]. Probiotic and prebiotic trials, including work with Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025 [59], report small to moderate improvements in depressive symptoms alongside changes in microbiome composition and tryptophan metabolites, although effects on cytokines are inconsistent and highly variable across studies [60].

Applying causal criteria to the microbiota immune brain axis

Taken together, current data support several causal criteria for immune mediation. Microbial products modulate PRRs, inflammasomes, and cytokines, which in turn influence blood-brain barrier function, microglia, and neurotransmission, establishing biological plausibility [47]. GF and antibiotic models, FMT, immune challenges, and pharmacological trials all show that altering microbial or immune pathways changes behaviour, with partial reversibility and cross-species convergence from rodents to humans [56]. At the same time, strong bidirectionality and confounding from factors such as diet, obesity, medication and stress mean that observational work cannot by itself prove causality, underscoring the need for mechanistic and genetic approaches. To address these limitations, the upcoming microbiome–psychiatry studies should establish standard procedures for handling confounding variables through their use of directed acyclic graphs, which will result in necessary adjustment requirements that will include both medication and antibiotic exposure, as well as dietary, adiposity, stress, and sleep factors. The longitudinal research should implement mixed-effects models together with compositional microbiome data analysis methods and sensitivity testing, which will either exclude or differentiate between participants who had recent antibiotic treatment, infections, dietary modifications, or medication changes. The use of standardized reporting frameworks such as STORMS will enhance both transparency and reproducibility of research results [61, 62].

Neuroendocrine and stress pathways (HPA axis)

Overview

Stress activates the sympathetic nervous system and the HPA axis, resulting in increased catecholamine and glucocorticoid release [63]. The HPA axis is a central neuroendocrine system in humans and one of the key communication pathways within the MGBA [64]. GM, the HPA axis and the brain communicate via three principal routes: humoral signalling through microbial metabolites and gut hormones, immune-mediated signalling involving pro-inflammatory cytokines, and neural circuits encompassing the enteric nervous system (ENS), vagus nerve and spinal afferents [65–67]. HPA hyperactivity is associated with anxiety, depression and metabolic syndrome [68] whereas functional gastrointestinal (GI) disorders, including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), exhibit altered HPA axis activity [69]. GI symptoms also frequently co-present across psychiatric disorders including depression, anxiety, SCZ and post-traumatic stress disorder, collectively supporting a bidirectional GM–HPA axis interaction. Understanding the correlational and causal basis of this bidirectionality- specifically how microbial signals calibrate HPA reactivity is critical for microbiome-targeted strategies to modulate stress biology in psychiatric disorders. To evaluate causality within this bidirectional loop, three questions must be addressed: (i) does microbial manipulation alter HPA reactivity, (ii) how consistently is stress associated with changes in gut microbial composition and function, and (iii) are these mechanisms demonstrable in humans rather than inferred from animal models? The following sections evaluate evidence using this framework.

GM modulating stress response

Human evidence linking the gut microbiome (GM) to stress-related HPA-axis activity remains limited and is based largely on small cross-sectional or probiotic studies [70, 71]. In patients with IBS, altered cortisol and ACTH levels were associated with reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus [72]. Similarly, prebiotic supplementation reduced cortisol awakening responses in a small RCT, suggesting that GM manipulation may influence endocrine stress responses [73]. However, causal inference in humans remains constrained by limited mechanistic evaluation and small sample sizes. Stronger causal evidence derives from preclinical models, where microbial manipulation alters HPA-axis reactivity. Colonisation with Bifidobacterium infantis normalized exaggerated stress responses in germ-free mice, whereas pathogenic Escherichia coli amplified them [74]. Proposed mechanisms include immune activation, with dysbiosis-associated increases in LPS, IL-6, TNF-α, and corticosterone, as well as microbial metabolite signaling. In humans, SCFA mixtures reduced cortisol responses to psychosocial stress, whereas butyrate alone did not, suggesting that coordinated metabolite signaling may be required to modulate endocrine stress pathways [75–77].

Stress modulating GM

Psychological stress not only activates the HPA axis but also reshapes GM composition and function as shown in (Fig. 3). Preclinical models show that early-life stress (ELS) induces persistent alterations in microbiota, immune activation, and HPA-axis regulation, supporting a developmental programming effect [78]. Early-life adversity may also produce long-lasting circuit-level changes, including altered inhibitory interneuron number and size in stress- and reward-related regions such as the amygdala and nucleus accumbens [79, 80]. In humans, stress is consistently associated with reduced microbial diversity and depletion of SCFA-producing taxa across age groups and stress contexts [81, 82]. However, findings remain heterogeneous, and most human studies are cross-sectional, limiting causal inference. One key mechanism linking stress to microbial alterations involves disruption of intestinal barrier integrity. Chronic stress may further impair barrier function via glucocorticoid- and cytokine-dependent pathways, as shown by increased permeability and inflammatory markers in high-stress populations. Preclinical studies provide stronger mechanistic evidence. Together, these findings support a bidirectional model in which stress-induced neuroendocrine and immune signalling alters gut microbial ecology through barrier disruption and impaired host defence mechanisms. However, human evidence remains largely associative, and mechanistic pathways are predominantly derived from animal studies. Establishing causality will require longitudinal, multi-modal human studies integrating microbiome, endocrine, immune, and barrier function measures. Neural pathways: vagus nerve and ENS–CNS communication

The vagus nerve is the main neural pathway of the gut–brain axis, carrying predominantly afferent signals from the GI tract to the CNS. Around 70–80% of vagal fibres are sensory, relaying information from the stomach, small intestine and proximal colon to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla [84]. These afferents express receptors that respond to mechanical stretch, luminal nutrients, inflammatory mediators and microbial metabolites, allowing continuous monitoring of the gut environment [85]. Recent genetic and optogenetic studies have identified molecularly distinct vagal sensory subtypes that encode nutrient and mechanical signals with circuit specificity [86], highlighting that vagal signaling is anatomically organized rather than functionally uniform. From the NTS, signals are projected to regions involved in stress and emotional regulation, including the hypothalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, insula and medial prefrontal cortex [87]. Through these connections, gut-derived signals can influence autonomic function, stress responses and affective processing. At the same time, vagal efferents regulate GI motility and secretion, and modulate immune activity via the cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex, where acetylcholine inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [84]. Disruption of vagal signaling has been associated with altered stress reactivity and neuropsychiatric vulnerability, and experimental manipulation of vagal pathways influences both GI physiology and central neurochemistry [84]. Assessing neural causality in the MGBA requires more than anatomical plausibility. In this section, we evaluate evidence using three minimal criteria: (i) microbial manipulation (composition or function), (ii) demonstration that vagal signaling is required (or selectively engaged) for downstream effects, and (iii) measurable changes in circuits relevant to psychiatric function or phenotypes, ideally in humans. Most available studies satisfy these criteria only partially and predominantly in preclinical models.

Given this dual sensory and modulatory capacity, the vagus nerve represents a plausible anatomical substrate through which microbial activity in the gut could affect central function. Microbial metabolites, particularly SCFAs and tryptophan-derived compounds, provide one such interface. SCFAs influence epithelial tight junction integrity and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production through inhibition of MAPK (Mitogen-activated protein kinase) and NF-κB signaling pathways [88]. They also act as ligands for free fatty acid receptors such as GPR41 and GPR43, expressed on enteroendocrine and immune cells [4], thereby indirectly shaping vagal afferent excitability. Beyond these peripheral effects, butyrate has been shown in stress-exposed mice to increase hippocampal serotonin concentrations and upregulate tight junction proteins such as occludin and ZO-1, partially restoring BBB integrity and exerting antidepressant-like effects [89]. However, these findings primarily support biological plausibility rather than demonstrated human neural mediation. Human studies directly linking SCFA shifts to vagal afferent firing or circuit-level changes remain limited, and causal relationships have not been established in clinical populations. Tryptophan metabolism provides another potential link between the gut microbiome and brain function. Gut microbes convert tryptophan into bioactive compounds, including indoles and kynurenine derivatives [90]. Although peripheral serotonin produced by enterochromaffin cells does not cross the BBB, it can activate vagal afferents and indirectly influence central signaling [91]. In addition, kynurenic acid and quinolinic acid differentially regulate glutamatergic neurotransmission through neuroprotective and neurotoxic effects, respectively [90]. Together, these pathways support a biologically plausible role for microbial metabolites in modulating stress- and mood-related neural circuits, although most evidence remains preclinical.

Evidence from GF models

If microbial signals play a role in regulating central stress responses, their absence would be expected to produce measurable neurobiological changes. Evidence from GF mouse models supports this idea. Mice raised without microbial exposure show exaggerated HPA axis responses, with increased ACTH and corticosterone release following stress [92]. This pattern is consistent with impaired negative feedback from hippocampal and prefrontal regions, which are involved in regulating HPA activity and receive indirect input from vagal projections via the NTS. Beyond endocrine changes, GF mice demonstrate reduced hippocampal serotonin and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels, altered neurogenesis, structural changes in the amygdala, and differences in prefrontal cortical myelination [93, 94]. These findings are consistent with broader developmental evidence that extracellular molecular signals regulate dendritic architecture and neuronal circuit maturation during brain development [95, 96]. Behaviourally, they show altered anxiety-like responses and impaired sociability. Notably, transplantation of microbiota between strains can transfer aspects of these behavioural phenotypes, supporting a causal role for the microbiota in shaping stress-related behaviours [94]. However, GF models demonstrate microbial necessity rather than pathway specificity and do not isolate vagal mediation. Because these animals lack microbiota from birth, immune maturation, microglial development, and BBB integrity are altered, complicating inference [97, 98]. Consequently, some behavioural and HPA-axis differences may reflect developmental programming rather than ongoing, acute microbiota-dependent signaling through a defined neural route.

Vagotomy studies: establishing neural specificity

Vagotomy studies provide evidence that some microbiota–brain effects are mediated through the vagus nerve. In rodents, administration of Lactobacillus rhamnosus (JB-1) reduced anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviours and altered GABA receptor expression in limbic regions; these effects were abolished following vagotomy, suggesting that intact vagal signaling is required [94, 99]. Additional studies report that vagotomy prevents microbiota-associated reductions in stress-induced corticosterone release and alters immune and neurochemical responses, including hippocampal microglial activation and serotonergic signaling [100]. Together, these findings support a contributory role for the vagus nerve in microbiota–brain communication. However, vagotomy studies remain limited for causal interpretation. Standard subdiaphragmatic vagotomy disrupts both afferent and efferent pathways, making pathway-specific effects difficult to isolate [101]. Surgical vagotomy also produces broad physiological changes, including altered GI motility, immune signaling, and metabolic regulation [102]. Moreover, many reported behavioural outcomes are indirect proxies that do not identify specific neural circuits, and findings have not been consistently replicated across animal models. Thus, while vagotomy studies strengthen the biological plausibility of vagal involvement in microbiota–brain signaling, their translational relevance to human psychiatric disorders remains uncertain [103].

Structure and functional organization of the ENS

The enteric nervous system (ENS) consists primarily of the myenteric plexus, which regulates motility, and the submucosal plexus, which controls secretion, absorption, and local blood flow [104]. Often referred to as a “second brain,” the ENS processes luminal and mucosal signals before transmitting information to the CNS through vagal and spinal pathways [105]. Gut microbes influence ENS activity through neurotransmitter, immune, and endocrine pathways. Serotonin produced by enterochromaffin cells regulates enteric reflexes and communicates with sensory neurons, while microbial activity can also affect GABAergic and dopaminergic signaling [99, 106, 107]. Experimental studies further show that microbial colonization alters enteric neuronal excitability and motility, with cytokines and HPA-axis signaling providing additional regulation [108, 109]. Gut–brain communication also involves spinal afferents and specialized epithelial–neural interfaces, including enteroendocrine and neuropod cells, which rapidly transmit luminal signals to sensory neurons [110]. These bidirectional pathways integrate neural, immune, and endocrine signaling to influence stress, reward, and emotional regulation, reinforcing the concept of the gut–brain axis as an integrated neuroimmune–endocrine network rather than a single neural pathway [108] as shown in (Fig. 4). Barrier integrity and neurovascular interfaces

Integrated organization and function of the intestinal barrier

The intestinal barrier is a multilayered system that regulates selective permeability while preventing translocation of harmful luminal contents. It comprises mucus, epithelial cells with tight junctions, antimicrobial peptides, immune components, and microbial interactions that collectively maintain homeostasis [111]. This coordinated structure allows nutrient absorption while limiting entry of pathogens and antigens. Barrier integrity depends primarily on the mucus layer and epithelial junctions. Goblet cell–derived mucus forms a protective interface that separates microbes from the epithelium, with a dense inner layer largely devoid of bacteria and an outer layer containing commensals [112]. Beneath this, epithelial cells interconnected by tight and adherens junctions regulate paracellular permeability, while Paneth cells secrete antimicrobial peptides such as defensins and lysozyme to support mucosal defense [113]. Tight junction function is dynamically regulated through two main pathways: a pore pathway allowing small solute passage, and a leak pathway permitting larger molecules when junctional integrity is disrupted [114]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines play a central role in this process. IL-13 increases claudin-2 expression and enhances pore conductance, whereas TNF-α activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK), leading to cytoskeletal contraction, occludin internalization, and leak pathway activation [115]. These mechanisms provide a molecular basis for increased intestinal permeability under inflammatory conditions. Disruption of the mucus and epithelial barrier facilitates translocation of microbial products such as LPS, triggering systemic immune activation. Clinical studies report elevated markers of bacterial translocation, including zonulin, LPS-binding protein (LBP), and soluble CD14, in MDD, BD, and SCZ, often correlating with symptom severity [116–118]. Collectively, these findings support a model in which intestinal barrier dysfunction contributes to systemic inflammation, BBB disruption, and neuroinflammatory processes, linking peripheral gut alterations to central neurobiological changes within the MGBA.

Microbial metabolites and barrier integrity

Maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity is essential for host–microbiota homeostasis. Microbiota-derived metabolites, particularly SCFAs, support epithelial proliferation, mucus production, tight junction stability, and immune tolerance, whereas dysbiosis disrupts these processes and promotes inflammation [119]. Microbial metabolites also influence BBB integrity; barrier disruption permits inflammatory mediators such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) to enter circulation and the CNS, contributing to endothelial dysfunction and neuroinflammation [120, 121]. Chronic stress further impairs BBB integrity through downregulation of tight junction proteins such as claudin-5, promoting vascular permeability and depression-like phenotypes [122]. The BBB is maintained by the neurovascular unit, including endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes, and is modulated by microbial metabolites and immune signaling [123]. SCFAs reinforce epithelial and BBB function, promote regulatory T-cell differentiation, and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas dysbiosis compromises barrier integrity and facilitates systemic and neurovascular inflammation [124]. Additional metabolites, including bile acids and lipid-derived mediators, also influence barrier and immune function through pathways such as GPCR and TLR signaling [119]. Together, these findings support a central role for microbial metabolites in regulating barrier integrity and gut–brain axis signaling.

Disorder-specific evaluation of causal evidence

To enable systematic comparison across disorders, causal evidence is evaluated using four domains: (1) human observational evidence, (2) interventional studies that manipulate the microbiome, (3) mechanistic support from experimental and translational models, and (4) translational readiness for clinical application. Convergence across these domains increases confidence that microbiome alterations contribute to psychiatric pathophysiology rather than reflecting secondary consequences of illness. Human microbiome evidence

Cross-sectional associations

Multiple case-control studies report that individuals with MDD exhibit altered gut microbial composition compared with healthy controls [128]. One of the most consistent findings is a reduced abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing genera such as Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus, which play important roles in anti-inflammatory signaling and gut-brain communication [43]. Large population-based metagenomic analyses further demonstrate that microbial pathways involved in neurotransmitter metabolism are associated with depressive symptoms and quality of life measures [43]. Meta-analyses report overall differences in gut microbial composition between individuals with MDD and healthy controls; however, effect sizes remain small, and methodological limitations restrict causal interpretation [126]. Most studies are cross-sectional, making it difficult to determine whether microbiome alterations contribute to depression or arise as a consequence of the disorder [129]. This raises the possibility of reverse causation, whereby depression-related behavioral and clinical factors may themselves influence GM [126, 130]. For example, many study cohorts include medicated patients, and antidepressant treatment has been shown to alter microbial composition, introducing pharmacologic confounding [130]. In addition, lifestyle factors frequently associated with depression, including diet, physical activity, smoking, and sleep disturbance, are strong determinants of gut microbial structure and are often incompletely controlled in observational studies [126].

Replication and consistency across studies

A reduced abundance of short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria is among the more reproducible findings across microbiome studies in MDD [125, 126]. However, replication at the level of specific genera or species remains inconsistent [125, 130]. Substantial heterogeneity also exists across study populations, sequencing methodologies, and analytical approaches [125, 129]. Moreover, microbial patterns reported in MDD overlap with those observed in other psychiatric conditions, including BD and SCZ, raising questions about diagnostic specificity [130]. Consequently, although a general pattern of gut dysbiosis is supported, no universal microbiome signature uniquely characterizing depression has been established [125, 130]. Longitudinal evidence examining microbiome alterations before the onset of MDD remains limited, as most studies assess microbial composition during established depressive episodes or in relation to treatment response [126, 129]. Beyond observational designs, MR approaches have recently been applied to investigate potential directional relationships between specific microbial taxa and depression risk using host genetic variants as instrumental variables [131]. Although this approach strengthens causal inference compared with observational studies, current findings remain preliminary and inconsistent and therefore do not provide definitive evidence for specific microbial drivers of MDD. Thus, human microbiome studies in MDD provide consistent associative evidence but remain limited by a lack of prospective cohort studies and direct causal confirmation in humans.

Intervention trials: does manipulating the microbiome improve depression?

Probiotic RCT

Because observational studies cannot establish causality, interventional trials such as those using probiotics to modify the gut microbiome represent an important next step in evaluating whether microbiota influence depressive symptoms. Several studies have investigated multistrain probiotic formulations containing Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species [132, 133]. In a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, probiotic supplementation for 8 weeks produced significantly greater reductions in Beck Depression Inventory scores compared with placebo in adults with MDD [132]. Another randomized study comparing probiotic, prebiotic, and placebo interventions similarly reported greater symptom improvement in probiotic and synbiotic groups [133]. Some studies additionally report biological changes accompanying symptom improvement [134]. For example, probiotic supplementation alongside selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor therapy has been associated with modulation of tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism and improvements in cognitive functioning [134]. Despite these promising findings, most probiotic trials involve relatively small sample sizes, short intervention durations, and considerable variation in microbial strains and dosages [135]. Meta-analyses therefore report a small-to-moderate pooled antidepressant effect but emphasize substantial heterogeneity and the need for larger, well-controlled trials [135]. Variable mucosal colonisation, whereby some GI tracts permit probiotic engraftment while others resist it, may also contribute to the heterogeneous clinical responses observed across trials [136]. Microbiome-active drug delivery systems may partially address these limitations by repositioning the microbiome as an active component of therapeutic delivery. In preclinical models, stimuli-responsive carriers, biofilm-stabilised formulations and surface-functionalised probiotic strains have improved mucosal retention and site-specific delivery [137]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus surface-functionalised with reactive oxygen species-responsive prodrug nanomicelles enhanced colon targeting and intestinal barrier repair, and reduced gut–brain-axis-linked neuroinflammation in a rodent colitis model [137]. However, psychiatric translation remains untested, and inter-individual microbiome variability, transient colonisation and unresolved regulatory pathways for hybrid biologic–material constructs remain key translational challenges.

Dietary interventions

While probiotic interventions directly target the gut microbiome, dietary modification offers an additional indirect strategy for influencing microbial composition and metabolic activity. By altering nutrient intake through high-fiber or Mediterranean-style diets, dietary interventions may support SCFA-producing bacteria and modulate gut-brain signaling, providing another potential avenue for alleviating depressive symptoms. In the SMILES trial, individuals with moderate to severe MDD who received Mediterranean-style dietary support showed significantly greater improvements in Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) scores compared with a social support control group [138, 139]. Meta-analyses of dietary intervention studies similarly report reductions in depressive symptoms with Mediterranean-style and whole-food diets [138]. However, most of these trials did not include detailed microbiome analyses, leaving it unclear whether microbial changes directly mediate symptom improvements [135]. Nevertheless, nutritional research indicates that high-fiber diets can increase SCFA-producing bacteria and alter microbial metabolic activity, supporting the biologic plausibility of microbiome-mediated effects on mood [138].

FMT

Beyond dietary and probiotic strategies, more direct manipulation of the gut microbiome can be achieved through FMT, which has been explored as a potential intervention for depressive symptoms. Clinical trials evaluating FMT specifically for primary MDD are extremely limited. Most available evidence comes from small observational reports or studies in GI populations in which mood outcomes were assessed secondarily, and preclinical studies have shown that FMT from individuals with MDD can induce depressive-like behaviors in animal models [140]. Consequently, the strongest evidence linking FMT to depressive phenotypes remains preclinical, particularly from phenotype-transfer experiments in animal models.

Symptom outcomes and causal inference

Across probiotic and dietary trials, depressive symptoms are typically assessed using validated scales such as the BDI, HAM-D, and MADRS, with several studies reporting statistically significant improvements relative to control conditions. Yet the clinical meaningfulness of these effects remains uncertain, as a recent meta-analysis reports highly heterogeneous effect sizes that attenuate substantially after exclusion of high-risk trials [141]. Furthermore, only a few intervention studies simultaneously measure microbiome changes and clinical outcomes, and formal mediation analyses are rare. An ideal mediation analysis would test a sequential pathway in which intervention-induced microbiome changes alter downstream metabolites, such as SCFAs or tryptophan metabolites, which in turn contribute to changes in psychiatric symptoms [142]. These limitations weaken causal inference regarding whether microbiome modulation directly drives symptom improvement. Overall, microbiome-targeted interventions appear capable of influencing depressive symptoms, but current evidence remains insufficient to confirm that these clinical effects are directly mediated by microbiome changes. Compared with cross-sectional studies, randomized probiotic and dietary trials provide stronger causal inference; however, the absence of consistent microbiome mediation analyses prevents confirmation that clinical improvement is microbiome-driven rather than secondary to other biologic or psychosocial mechanisms.

Mechanistic support

Inflammatory pathways

Low-grade systemic inflammation is consistently observed in depressed individuals, with meta-analyses reporting elevated peripheral IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α compared with healthy controls [143]. While these circulating markers reflect peripheral immune activation, central neuroinflammation, including increased TSPO binding consistent with microglial activation in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex and insula has also been reported in MDD using in vivo positron emission tomography (PET), indicating that inflammatory processes in depression are not restricted to the periphery [144]. Together, these findings support the characterization of depression as a proinflammatory state. Gut dysbiosis may contribute to systemic inflammation by increasing intestinal permeability, allowing bacterial components such as LPS to enter the circulation and activate innate immune signaling [145]. Clinical evidence links markers of intestinal permeability with depressive symptom severity and systemic inflammation [146]. SCFAs, produced by beneficial microbiota, maintain epithelial barrier integrity and modulate immune responses. Reduced abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria may weaken barrier function and diminish anti-inflammatory signaling, potentially amplifying immune activation associated with depressive symptoms [147]. Taken together, these findings provide biologically plausible mechanisms linking GM alterations, immune dysregulation, and depressive symptoms. Importantly, these inflammatory mechanisms are consistent with human findings of elevated cytokines in MDD, supporting translational convergence between microbiome alterations and established depression biology.

HPA axis dysregulation

Dysregulation of the HPA axis is commonly observed in depressed patients, with evidence of hyperactivity and altered cortisol responses compared with healthy controls [148]. Experimental and translational studies suggest that the GM regulate stress physiology through bidirectional interactions with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [149]. GF animals exhibit exaggerated stress responses that normalize following microbiota colonization, indicating a regulatory role of the microbiome in stress physiology [149].

SCFAs and neurotransmitter pathways

Butyrate, a key SCFA, exhibits anti-inflammatory properties and strengthens intestinal and potentially blood-brain barrier integrity [150]. Human microbiome studies report reduced abundance of butyrate-producing taxa, including Faecalibacterium, Lachnospira, and Subdoligranulum, in depressed individuals, suggesting deficient SCFA production [125]. In preclinical models, SCFA supplementation suppresses microglial activation, reduces neuroinflammation, and improves depressive-like behaviors [150]. However, whether SCFA concentrations achievable in humans are sufficient to directly exert comparable CNS effects remains uncertain. Gut-derived SCFAs undergo extensive colonic and hepatic metabolism, plasma concentrations remain in the μM range, and brain uptake across the BBB appears limited; therefore, SCFA effects on the human CNS may depend largely on indirect MGBA pathways, including peripheral immune modulation, endocrine signalling, and vagal afferent activation [88]. Gut bacteria are capable of synthesizing or modulating neuroactive compounds, including γ-aminobutyric acid, serotonin, and dopamine, thereby influencing host neural signaling [151]. Gut microbes also influence host tryptophan metabolism, potentially diverting tryptophan toward the kynurenine pathway, which increases neurotoxic metabolites and contributes to inflammation-associated depression [152]. Microbial activity additionally modulates peripheral serotonin, GABA, and dopamine pathways, linking microbiota alterations to mood regulation and stress responses. The microbiota-gut-brain axis integrates neural, endocrine, immune, and metabolic signaling pathways linking intestinal microbial activity to CNS function.

Phenotype-transfer animal models

In one influential study, transplantation of microbiota from patients with MDD into GF mice induced depressive-like behaviors and metabolic alterations, providing experimental evidence that microbial communities can influence mood-related phenotypes [153]. FMT from depressed patients into GF or antibiotic-treated mice induces depressive-like behaviors, impairs hippocampal neurogenesis, and alters neurochemical profiles relative to recipients of healthy microbiota. Conversely, transfer of healthy microbiota into depressed animal models ameliorates depressive-like behaviors, normalizes neurotransmitter levels, and reduces proinflammatory cytokines [154]. While these studies provide strong mechanistic evidence, direct causal confirmation in humans remains limited because longitudinal and mechanistically integrated clinical studies are still scarce.

Translational readiness

Strength of causality

Observational studies consistently link GM alterations with depressive symptoms; however, most are cross-sectional and cannot establish causality [128]. Mechanistic and preclinical studies offer stronger evidence that microbiota influence mood-related pathways, including inflammation, neurotransmission, and HPA axis regulation [152]. Human intervention trials using probiotics and dietary modification demonstrate modest improvements in depressive symptoms, although formal mediation analyses confirming microbiome-driven effects are rare [138, 139]. Overall, the evidence indicates moderate causal support, with converging findings across observational, mechanistic, and interventional domains. The absence of prospective premorbid microbiome data and the limited use of formal mediation analyses in human intervention trials remain key barriers to stronger causal classification.

Reproducibility

Some patterns, particularly reduced abundance of SCFA-producing taxa, are consistent across studies [125]. However, replication of specific microbial signatures remains inconsistent because of differences in population characteristics, diet, geographic location, and sequencing methodology.

Clinical feasibility

Dietary modification and probiotic supplementation are the most feasible microbiome-targeted strategies in clinical practice. They can be implemented alongside standard antidepressant treatments at relatively low cost and with minimal infrastructure. In contrast, FMT is logistically complex, requires specialized facilities, and is currently approved primarily for GI conditions rather than psychiatric disorders [155]. Although regulatory pathways for microbiome-based live biotherapeutics are increasingly defined, psychiatric applications remain investigational and continue to face challenges related to donor suitability, manufacturing consistency, and regulatory oversight; the mechanism–metrics–modifiers framework further emphasizes mechanistic understanding, predictive microbial metrics, and demonstrated potential to modify the microbiome as central components of clinical translation [156]. However, these components remain insufficiently validated for routine clinical application in MDD.

Safety considerations

Dietary interventions generally have favorable safety profiles. Probiotic trials report few serious adverse events in medically stable adults, but long-term safety data remain limited [157]. FMT carries greater safety and regulatory concerns, including potential adverse effects, and remains experimental for depression. Although a small pilot trial demonstrated feasibility and safety, clinical efficacy has not been established [158]. More broadly, strengthened regulatory frameworks, including harmonized international standards, validated analytical methods for microbiome characterization, and consensus definitions of healthy versus dysbiotic states will be necessary to support the safe and reproducible clinical translation of microbiome-based therapies [159]. Such considerations are likely to become particularly important as microbiome-targeted interventions are extended to psychiatric disorders, including MDD.

Causal strength verdict

Overall, current evidence supports moderate causal involvement of GM alterations in depressive symptoms. Human studies consistently demonstrate microbiome differences in individuals with MDD, but most are cross-sectional and therefore susceptible to confounding. Mechanistic evidence, including inflammatory signaling, HPA axis modulation, microbial metabolite pathways, and phenotype-transfer animal models, supports biologic plausibility. Interventional trials using probiotics and dietary modification show modest improvements in depressive symptoms, although direct mediation by microbiome changes remains unclear. Thus, the gut microbiome should be considered a biologically plausible and potentially modifiable contributor to depression risk. However, current evidence is insufficient to support microbiome-targeted therapies as primary treatments for MDD.

Anxiety disorders

Anxiety disorders comprise a group of psychiatric conditions characterized by excessive and persistent fear or anxiety responses that impair functioning [160]. Common forms include generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder, panic disorder, and agoraphobia. These disorders are highly prevalent, frequently chronic, and often comorbid with conditions such as depression and IBS [160]. Despite established treatments, including serotonergic medications and cognitive behavioral therapy, many patients experience treatment resistance, highlighting the need for improved mechanistic targets. The gut-brain axis has therefore emerged as a potential framework for understanding anxiety pathophysiology and identifying new therapeutic approaches [160, 161]. Interest in microbiome involvement in anxiety initially arose from preclinical studies showing that gut microbial composition can influence stress responses and anxiety-like behavior. Experiments using GF animals, antibiotic depletion, probiotic administration, and FMT demonstrate that microbial signals can affect multiple biologic pathways relevant to anxiety, including HPA axis activity, immune signaling, vagal nerve communication, and neurotransmitter production [161]. Within this framework, the gut microbiome is best conceptualized as a modulatory system that influences stress reactivity and emotional regulation through interacting neuroendocrine, immune, and neurochemical pathways, rather than as a primary causal driver of anxiety disorders. Human microbiome evidence

Human microbiome studies in anxiety disorders remain limited, and findings are often inconsistent. Some studies report reduced microbial diversity in individuals with mood and anxiety disorders, suggesting potential links between gut microbial composition and stress resilience [162]. Alterations in bacterial groups involved in neuroactive metabolite production have also been described. Reductions in beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, which can produce neurotransmitters including γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and serotonin, have been reported in mood and anxiety disorders [163]. Cross-diagnostic microbiome analyses further suggest that psychiatric conditions may be associated with reduced abundance of anti-inflammatory, short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera such as Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus, alongside increases in potentially proinflammatory taxa including Eggerthella [130]. Functionally, these changes suggest a shift toward reduced production of beneficial microbial metabolites and increased proinflammatory signaling, which may influence stress-related neurobiological pathways. Microbial dysbiosis may also contribute to inflammatory signaling along the gut-brain axis. Increased production of bacterial components such as LPS can stimulate cytokine release and influence neural circuits involved in stress and emotional regulation [162, 164]. Across studies, the most consistent signal is not a specific taxonomic profile but a functional pattern characterized by reduced anti-inflammatory and metabolically beneficial microbial activity alongside increased inflammatory signaling, whereas variability in specific taxa likely reflects differences in study populations and methodologies. While these findings suggest biologically plausible links between microbiome alterations and anxiety, disorder-specific microbial signatures remain difficult to identify. These limitations highlight the need for more standardized, disorder-specific, and longitudinal human studies.

Intervention trials

Clinical studies investigating microbiome-targeted interventions for anxiety have primarily focused on psychobiotics, including probiotics and prebiotics. The concept of “psychobiotics” describes microorganisms that confer mental health benefits through interactions with the gut-brain axis [165]. Several randomized trials report modest improvements in psychological distress or anxiety-related symptoms following probiotic supplementation. For example, a probiotic combination containing Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum reduced psychological distress in healthy volunteers compared with placebo [166]. Prebiotic supplementation with trans-galacto-oligosaccharides has also been associated with improvements in anxiety scores in individuals with IBS [167], and other studies in healthy participants report modest mood improvements following probiotic consumption [168]. However, meta-analyses indicate that the overall evidence remains mixed. Some studies report moderate improvements in anxiety or depressive symptoms, while others find minimal or no significant effects [162]. Differences in probiotic strains, treatment duration, and study populations likely contribute to these inconsistencies. Overall, current interventional evidence suggests modest and context-dependent effects rather than robust clinical efficacy. These findings likely reflect heterogeneity in microbial strains, lack of stratification by baseline stress or inflammatory status, and short intervention durations relative to chronic disease processes. As such, current evidence does not exclude a therapeutic role for the microbiome but instead indicates that more targeted, mechanism-driven, and population-specific interventions are required.

Mechanistic support and causal strength

Several mechanisms may explain how GM influence anxiety-related behavior. These mechanisms can be integrated into a systems-level model linking microbial activity to stress regulation through interconnected neuroendocrine, immune, and neural pathways. Microbial metabolites can modulate neurotransmitter systems such as GABA and serotonin, which are central to emotional regulation [169]. Communication between the gut and brain may also occur through vagal nerve signaling. Animal studies provide further mechanistic support. GF mice show exaggerated stress responses and increased anxiety-like behavior compared with conventionally colonized animals [169]. In addition, probiotic administration reduces anxiety-like behavior in rodents, while vagotomy abolishes these effects, suggesting that gut-brain communication via the vagus nerve is important for microbiome-mediated behavioral changes [99]. Inflammatory signaling may represent another pathway linking microbiota alterations and anxiety. Microbial dysbiosis can promote systemic immune activation and cytokine release, which may influence neural circuits involved in stress regulation [162, 164]. Collectively, these findings support a model in which gut microbiome alterations influence anxiety-related behavior through interactions between immune activation, neurotransmitter modulation, and neural signaling pathways. However, because human microbiome studies remain heterogeneous and disorder-specific microbial signatures are unclear, the overall causal evidence for microbiome involvement in anxiety disorders remains weak to moderate. From a causal inference perspective, existing evidence supports biological plausibility and strong experimental data in animal models but remains limited in consistency, temporality, and disorder-specific human validation.

PTSD

PTSD is a trauma- and stressor-related psychiatric disorder that can develop after exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence. The disorder includes symptoms of intrusive memories, avoidance of trauma-related cues, negative changes in cognitive functions and emotional state, and constant hyperarousal, which often leads to sleep problems, anxiety, depression, and functional disabilities [170]. The MGBA can help identify early PTSD risk after trauma because research shows that gut microbial patterns after trauma predict future post-traumatic symptom development [171]. Diet–microbiome interactions may also be important, with lower Mediterranean diet (MED) adherence and altered microbial features reported in individuals with higher PTSD symptom burden [172]. The field requires reproducible PTSD-specific microbial signatures and confirmed therapeutic targets which currently do not exist.

Human observational evidence

The assessment process for specific disorders necessitates PTSD, as research shows that trauma-related psychological disorders lead to alterations in gut microbiota, which scientific studies demonstrate through their different evaluation methods. The AURORA study established a connection between stool metagenomic profiles collected immediately after trauma and the development of post-traumatic neuropsychiatric disorders, which included PTSD symptoms, thus proving that microbiome characteristics serve as early indicators for post-trauma risk assessment [173]. The study showed that women with higher PTSD symptoms had reduced MED adherence, together with specific gut microbial traits, which included protective species, thus establishing that microbiome composition, dietary habits, and PTSD symptoms interact with each other. The scientific studies show substantial differences in their demographic makeup because they investigate different trauma types while participants follow various dietary and medication regimens and experience different health conditions [172, 174], yet scientists have not yet identified consistent microbial taxa that link to specific disorders.

Interventional and human causal inference evidence

Research on PTSD treatment through interventional methods shows less progress because the field has not yet developed sufficient evidence that exceeds what can be studied through observational and preclinical research [174]. The existing randomized clinical trials that test microbiome-targeted treatments have not yet established their effectiveness in treating fundamental PTSD symptoms [175]. The 2024 Mendelian randomization study found genetic links between various bacterial traits that increase risk for PTSD [171], while showing no evidence that PTSD affects the composition of gut microbiota, which creates an initial link between human genetic research and observational studies. This review found no human clinical trials or case reports that confirmed FMT usage in PTSD patients, but a recent preclinical study in a PTSD-like rat model showed that autologous FMT impacted fear extinction and brain molecular changes [176]. The current research on PTSD treatment methods, especially FMT, needs more research because it only shows preliminary results, which require human translational studies to determine clinical significance.

Mechanistic and experimental evidence

The evidence for how microbiomes contribute to PTSD exists in better mechanistic form for animal studies than for human studies [177]. The research that tests single prolonged stress together with its related research methods has established that traumatic stress leads to gut dysbiosis and intestinal permeability changes, inflammatory activation, and alterations in stress-related behavior. The experimental research shows that the composition of microbiota can affect how people respond to traumatic stress by demonstrating that gut microbial features show PTSD-like states and determine how people will respond to trauma before and during its occurrence [178]. The research on rodents demonstrated that probiotic treatment could restore intestinal integrity and microbial composition while treating PTSD-like behavioral problems [177]. The majority of psychiatric disorder evidence remains preclinical for all disorders, while scientists have not yet confirmed the mechanisms through which PTSD operates in human patients.

BD

BD is a chronic mood disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of mania, hypomania, and depression. In addition to mood instability, BD is associated with metabolic abnormalities and increased cardiovascular risk. Research suggests that immune dysregulation and systemic inflammation contribute to BD pathophysiology, which has led to increasing interest in the gut-brain axis as a potential contributor to disease mechanisms [179]. Within this framework, the gut microbiome may be conceptualized as a modulatory system that interacts with metabolic, immune, and neuroendocrine pathways, potentially influencing mood stability rather than acting as a primary causal factor.

Human microbiome evidence

Human microbiome studies in BD remain limited but suggest differences in gut microbial composition compared with healthy controls. One early study found that microbial composition was associated with illness severity and identified reduced abundance of the anti-inflammatory, short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genus Faecalibacterium in individuals with BD [180]. Because butyrate-producing bacteria play an important role in regulating intestinal barrier function and immune responses, reductions in these species may contribute to inflammatory pathways involved in BD. More recent studies have reported additional microbial alterations. Researchers observed differences in GM composition in patients hospitalized for bipolar depression, including reduced abundance of potentially beneficial microbial species [181]. Similarly, altered microbial diversity and taxonomic composition were reported in individuals with bipolar depression compared with healthy controls [154]. Functionally, these findings suggest a shift toward reduced anti-inflammatory and barrier-supporting microbial activity alongside potential increases in proinflammatory signaling, consistent with broader evidence of immune activation in BD. This highlights the major methodological challenge of medication confounding in BD, as psychotropic treatments can independently alter microbial composition. More broadly, systematic reviews conclude that microbial alterations in BD are plausible but remain difficult to interpret because of confounding factors such as medication use, diet, and metabolic comorbidities [182]. Across studies, the most consistent signal is not a specific taxonomic signature but a functional pattern characterized by reduced beneficial microbial activity and increased inflammatory potential, whereas variability in specific taxa likely reflects differences in study populations and treatment exposure.

Intervention trials

The observed benefits may reflect indirect effects on immune or metabolic pathways rather than direct modulation of core mood symptoms. SCZ

SCZ is a chronic, clinically and biologically heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by positive symptoms such as psychosis, negative symptoms such as social withdrawal and affective flattening, and persistent cognitive impairment. Its etiology is multifactorial, involving polygenic risk, epigenetic modification, immune dysregulation, environmental exposures, and alterations in gut microbiome composition and function; however, no single unifying mechanism has been established [184]. Global increases in prevalence and disability-adjusted life years between 1990 and 2019 further underscore its sustained public health impact [185]. Neurobiologically, SCZ is characterized by dysregulation of dopaminergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic signaling across key regions, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and striatum [186]. A meta-analysis of 59 studies demonstrated elevated glutamate and related metabolites in the basal ganglia, thalamus, and medial temporal lobe, reinforcing glutamatergic dysfunction as central to pathophysiology [187]. As only a proportion of genetic variance maps to discrete loci, intermediary systems such as the gut–brain axis may help link genetic vulnerability and environmental exposure to downstream neurochemical disturbance. Within this framework, the gut microbiome is best conceptualized not as a primary causal driver but as a dynamic disease modifier that interacts with genetic and environmental risk, primarily through immune, metabolic, and neurotransmitter-mediated pathways.

Human microbiome evidence

A growing number of case-control sequencing studies report that SCZ is associated with alterations in gut microbial composition and metabolic function, although findings are not entirely uniform. Across cohorts, a recurring pattern is a shift away from short-chain fatty acid-producing genera and toward taxa associated with inflammation or metabolic disturbance. One recent multi-omics study compared drug-naive first-episode patients, chronically treated patients, and healthy controls, integrating 16S rRNA sequencing with neuroimaging and cognitive data [188]. Patients demonstrated reduced abundance of Blautia and increased Proteus and Arthrobacter, with changes observed across illness stages. Functional pathway analysis suggested reduced bile acid biosynthesis and increased lipid metabolism, indicating that microbial alterations may extend beyond taxonomy to metabolic signatures. Earlier work in medication-free samples reported enrichment of facultative anaerobes and increased Proteobacteria [189–191]. At the genus level, reductions in Coprococcus, Roseburia, and Blautia have been described repeatedly [189]. These taxa are major SCFA producers, and reduced SCFA availability has been linked to impaired gut barrier integrity and altered immune signaling, including elevated proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Conversely, genera such as Collinsella have been associated with increased intestinal permeability and proinflammatory cytokine induction, including IL-17a, in other inflammatory conditions [192], raising the possibility of similar barrier dysfunction in SCZ. Across studies, the most reproducible signal is a reduction in SCFA-producing taxa alongside enrichment of organisms associated with inflammatory and metabolic dysfunction, whereas variability at the taxonomic level likely reflects differences in cohort characteristics, sequencing approaches, and treatment exposure rather than true biological inconsistency. Some studies also suggest that microbial composition may relate to clinical phenotypes. An abundance of taxa, including Veillonellaceae and Lachnospiraceae has been associated with greater symptom severity [153], although causality cannot be inferred. Interpretation of these findings remains complicated by treatment effects. Antipsychotic exposure alters gut microbial composition. For example, 24 weeks of risperidone in first-episode patients increased Bifidobacterium and Escherichia coli while reducing Clostridium coccoides and Lactobacillus. Because many studies include medicated cohorts, separating disease-associated dysbiosis from pharmacologically induced changes remains a central methodological limitation. Substantial heterogeneity across studies is expected given differences in geographic location, diet, sequencing methodology (e.g., 16S rRNA versus metagenomics), and clinical variables, including illness stage and medication exposure. These factors can significantly influence taxonomic composition and may account for inconsistent identification of specific genera across cohorts. However, convergence at the level of microbial function particularly reduced SCFA production and increased proinflammatory signaling suggests that functional alterations may represent a more robust and biologically meaningful signal than taxonomic profiles alone [37].

Intervention trials

In a 4-week open-label study, administration of Bifidobacterium breve A-1 was associated with reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms [193]. Participants who responded demonstrated fewer negative symptoms and higher baseline Parabacteroides abundance. Changes in inflammatory markers, including reduced TNF-α and increased IL-22, were also observed, suggesting a possible immunomodulatory mechanism. However, the absence of a control group limits causal inference. In contrast, a 14-week RCT using Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG and Bifidobacterium animalis Bb12 did not demonstrate significant improvement in PANSS scores compared with placebo [183]. Overall, current interventional evidence does not demonstrate consistent or robust clinical benefit. Available trials are underpowered and heterogeneous, limiting the conclusions that can be drawn about psychobiotic efficacy in SCZ. Differences in bacterial strain selection, lack of stratification by baseline inflammatory or metabolic status, and relatively short treatment durations in the context of a chronic neurodevelopmental disorder may all contribute to inconsistent outcomes [183, 193]. Whether the lack of consistent clinical benefit reflects limited microbiome involvement in SCZ or insufficient targeting of interventions to underlying pathophysiological subtypes remains unresolved. Future studies incorporating patient stratification, mechanistically informed strain selection, and longer treatment durations will be necessary to determine whether microbiome modulation can produce clinically meaningful effects in SCZ.

Mechanistic support

Several biologic pathways support a potential mechanistic link between gut dysbiosis and SCZ, which can be integrated into a convergent systems-level model. The proposed gut–microbiota–brain pathways implicated in SCZ are summarized in (Fig. 8). Consistent with observational findings, reduced abundance of SCFA-producing genera may impair intestinal barrier integrity, increasing permeability and facilitating translocation of microbial products that promote systemic immune activation, including elevated cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [189]. Genera such as Collinsella, which are associated with altered barrier function in inflammatory conditions, may further exacerbate this process [192]. Sustained immune activation can in turn influence central neurotransmitter systems, including dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling, which are central to SCZ pathophysiology. In parallel, microbiome-associated alterations in tryptophan metabolism may shift the balance toward the kynurenine pathway, reducing serotonin synthesis and generating neuroactive metabolites that modulate NMDA receptor function [90]. Evidence from animal models further supports this integrated pathway: FMT from patients with SCZ into GF mice induces behavioral changes alongside alterations in hippocampal glutamate, glutamine, and GABA concentrations [153]. Collectively, these findings support a model in which gut microbial alterations contribute to SCZ-relevant neurochemical and behavioral changes via interconnected immune, metabolic, and neurotransmitter pathways. Causal strength

Current evidence supports a biologically plausible association between gut microbiome alterations and SCZ but provides limited causal confirmation. While animal studies, particularly FMT experiments, demonstrate that microbiome alterations can induce behavioral and neurochemical changes consistent with SCZ phenotypes, human evidence remains largely cross-sectional and associative [190]. As such, directionality cannot be established, and reverse causation, whereby disease state, diet, or treatment influences microbial composition, remains a significant concern. Confounding factors, including antipsychotic exposure, smoking, dietary patterns, and illness chronicity, further complicate interpretation and limit causal inference. From a causal inference perspective, existing evidence supports biological plausibility and experimental evidence in animal models but lacks consistency, temporality, and specificity in human populations. Accordingly, the gut microbiome is best considered a contributory and potentially modifiable factor rather than a primary causal driver of disease. Longitudinal cohort studies and adequately powered, stratified interventional trials will be necessary to determine whether microbiome modulation can meaningfully influence disease trajectory. Emerging evidence supports a systems-level model in which gut microbiome alterations bridge environmental and genetic risk in SCZ through interconnected immune, metabolic, and neurotransmitter pathways, functioning as a modifiable disease modifier rather than a primary cause.

Translational implications and therapeutic opportunities

FMT: evidence, challenges, and future directions

FMT is a medical procedure that transfers fecal microbiota from healthy donors to patients with recurrent or persistent C. difficile infection. The same principle of microbial “resetting” has prompted interest in FMT as a means of modulating gut–brain signaling in psychiatry. Experimental studies using probiotics, antibiotics, GF animal models, and FMT demonstrate that GM influence mood and anxiety-related behavior. Emerging human data suggest that restoring a healthier microbial configuration via FMT may alleviate symptoms in MDD, anxiety disorders, and other psychiatric conditions [195]. However, research in this field remains limited, as only a small number of human FMT studies have been conducted in psychiatric populations. Although research on FMT in psychiatry has advanced, significant methodological and clinical challenges remain. The scientific community has not yet determined whether single-donor or pooled-donor approaches produce better outcomes or which dosing schedule should be used. These variables influence microbial engraftment, persistence, and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, FMT achieves pooled cure rates of approximately 91% in recurrent C. difficile infection [140], where treatment targets a single pathogen and clinical outcomes are clearly defined. In psychiatric conditions, by contrast, evidence is limited to small uncontrolled case series and underpowered pilot trials [195], and observed effects may be inflated by the high placebo responses associated with invasive procedures. Moreover, in most psychiatric FMT studies, psychiatric symptom measures remain secondary or exploratory endpoints, whereas primary outcomes typically focus on feasibility, safety, or microbiome-related changes, further limiting interpretation of clinical efficacy [158]. The field needs robust evidence, which necessitates conducting large, randomized studies that use blinded protocols and standardized procedures to achieve sufficient sample size for comparing different dosing methods across multiple research studies. Safety remains a critical concern. Standardized FMT protocols have enabled its use in large clinical populations, demonstrating generally favorable safety profiles. The determination of safety in healthy people holds particular importance for psychiatry because physical health problems can make it difficult to identify which adverse reactions result from antidepressant treatments [196, 197].

Progress in psychiatric FMT research requires a shift from empirical observations to mechanistic understanding, which establishes specific treatment methods for different patient groups Systematic reviews highlight substantial heterogeneity in study design, donor selection, dosing, and delivery methods, limiting comparability and interpretation [195]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that microbiota from depressed patients induce depression-like behavior in animal models, whereas microbiota from healthy donors can reverse these effects [198]. The upcoming FMT studies for mood disorders and other mental health conditions will utilize metagenomics and metabolomics, and immune and endocrine markers to gather multi-omic data, which will allow researchers to track clinical development across multiple time points while identifying patient groups that will respond to treatment based on their specific microbial and host characteristics and donor selection based on their ability to address specific biological weaknesses [199, 200]. The researchers want to designate patient groups that require treatment based on three criteria their initial gut microbiome composition, their biochemical markers of inflammation, and their specific psychological diagnosis [201, 202]. Green and colleagues propose a framework calling for standardized methods and shared metrics to link human and animal studies, so that FMT can help translate microbiome-based interventions into personalized psychiatric treatment [197]. Overall, current evidence for FMT in psychiatric disorders remains preliminary, with strong preclinical support but limited and heterogeneous clinical validation.

Psychobiotics

Probiotics are live microorganisms that exert beneficial effects on host health when consumed in sufficient amounts [203]. Psychobiotics were subsequently conceptualised as probiotics with potential mental health effects mediated through interactions with the MGBA, including the production of neuroactive compounds such as GABA and serotonin [203]. Psychobiotics are live microorganisms that may confer mental health benefits through interactions with the MGBA [204]. Their proposed effects involve several interconnected pathways, including modulation of neurotransmitter signaling, regulation of stress responses through the HPA axis, and attenuation of inflammation via microbial metabolites such as SCFAs [205]. However, causal mechanisms in humans remain incompletely established, and there is still no consensus regarding the most effective strains, combinations, doses, or treatment durations. Meta-analytic evidence suggests modest therapeutic potential. A meta-analysis of 19 RCTs reported a small but significant reduction in depression scores with probiotic treatment compared with placebo, with greater effects in participants younger than 60 years [206]. Another meta-analysis found that multi-strain formulations performed better than single-strain interventions, indicating that psychobiotic effects are unlikely to represent uniform class effects and instead appear to depend on specific strains, formulations, and clinical contexts [207]. Nonetheless, heterogeneity across strains, study populations, and outcome measures limits causal interpretation. In MDD, several double-blind RCTs suggest that psychobiotics can engage gut–brain pathways relevant to symptom improvement. Lactobacillus helveticus R0052 and Bifidobacterium longum R0175 reduced depressive symptoms and increased serum BDNF, although the mechanistic relevance of BDNF remained uncertain [208]. Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025 improved clinician-rated depression scores and altered tryptophan-related metabolites, suggesting modulation of gut tryptophan metabolism. A 14-strain adjunctive formulation also reduced depression severity [209]. However, not all trials have been positive: Lactobacillus plantarum 299v altered kynurenine metabolism without improving affective symptoms, indicating that biochemical changes do not necessarily translate into antidepressant effects. Across studies, inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and cortisol often remained unchanged, suggesting that commonly measured systemic markers may not fully capture relevant mechanisms.

Evidence in SCZ, BD, stress-related populations, and perinatal cohorts is more limited and inconsistent. Some trials report improvements in psychiatric symptoms or stress-related endocrine markers, whereas others show no psychiatric benefit despite changes in zonulin, cortisol, or GI symptoms [210, 211]. The candidate psychobiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus (JB-1), which produced anxiolytic-like effects and reduced stress-induced corticosterone via vagus nerve–dependent mechanisms in stress-sensitive mice models, failed to modify stress, mood, HPA-axis responses, inflammation, or cognitive performance in healthy male volunteers, underscoring the challenges of translating preclinical findings to humans [212]. Overall, these findings suggest that psychobiotics may influence gut–brain pathways, but clinical effects remain variable across strains and populations. Interpretation of the literature is constrained by major methodological limitations. Trials are typically short, use heterogeneous formulations and psychometric scales, rarely include microbiome or metabolomic profiling, and seldom stratify by sex, baseline inflammation, or host microbial composition [213, 214]. Background medications, especially antipsychotics, may further confound outcomes by independently altering gut microbial ecology [16]. As a result, current evidence supports psychobiotics as a promising but still mechanistically unresolved adjunctive strategy rather than an established psychiatric treatment. Future progress will depend on shifting from empiric probiotic supplementation toward precision psychobiotics guided by baseline microbiome features, metabolic signatures, and host biological context [205]. Integrating microbiome profiling, multi-omics, and biomarker-based stratification into clinical trials will be essential for identifying responsive subgroups, selecting rational strains, and determining whether psychobiotic effects are truly causal and clinically meaningful [215, 216].

Diet and metabolite targeting

Diet is increasingly recognised as a modifiable factor in mental health. Western dietary patterns are associated with increased depressive risk, whereas Mediterranean-style diets (MED) rich in fibre and plant-based nutrients are linked to lower rates of depression and anxiety [138, 217]. Proposed mechanisms include modulation of the gut microbiome and related metabolic pathways, particularly SCFAs, tryptophan metabolism, and inflammatory signaling [161, 218]. Clinical trials, including the SMILES, HELFIMED, and AMMEND studies, report improvements in depressive symptoms following MED-based interventions [139, 219, 220]. However, most studies are limited by modest sample sizes, short follow-up, and difficulty isolating diet-specific effects. Whether these benefits are mediated through the gut microbiome also remains unclear. Systematic reviews show inconsistent effects of MED interventions on microbiota composition and SCFA production [221], while evidence for metabolite-mediated psychiatric effects in humans remains limited. Although SCFAs and butyrate show stress-reducing and antidepressant-like effects in experimental models, clinical evidence remains sparse and inconsistent [76, 222]. Overall, dietary interventions show therapeutic promise, but the microbiome-mediated mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely established.

Microbiome-based biomarkers in psychiatry

The MGBA generates biological signals across neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic systems [2, 223, 224]. These interactions can be assessed through microbial metabolites such as SCFAs, butyrate, and tryptophan-derived compounds [225, 226]. However, most microbiome studies in psychiatry remain cross-sectional and taxonomic, limiting mechanistic interpretation and causal inference. While animal models provide insight into gut–brain pathways, translation to human clinical outcomes remains incomplete. Microbiome-based biomarkers are increasingly explored in psychiatric disorders, including BD, where alterations in taxa such as Faecalibacterium and Actinobacteria have been reported [180, 227]. In depression, a standardized multi-cohort 16S meta-analysis identified candidate microbiome-associated biomarkers enriched for Bacteroidetes, Parabacteroides, and Bacteroides vulgatus and depleted in Firmicutes and Dialister, suggesting potential diagnostic utility, although predictive performance in external validation cohorts was modest [228]. Emerging evidence suggests that functional features, including SCFA production and tryptophan metabolism, may offer more biologically relevant biomarkers than taxonomic composition alone [229]. Similar approaches are being applied to SCZ and anxiety disorders, although disorder-specific signatures remain underdeveloped. To improve robustness, biomarker research is shifting from single taxa to ecosystem-level and multi-omic approaches. Network-based analyses and microbial community structures capture functional interactions and yield more reproducible biosignatures than individual markers [230]. Integration of microbiome data with metabolomics, immune markers, and host genomics further enables identification of biologically defined subgroups relevant to precision psychiatry [231]. Advanced computational frameworks, such as integrative multi-omics models, support linking microbial features to clinical outcomes. Despite this progress, microbiome-based biomarkers remain influenced by confounding factors, including diet, medication, and lifestyle, which are often insufficiently controlled. As a result, most identified biomarkers remain associative rather than causal. Advancing the field will require validation at multiple levels, including mechanistic characterization beyond cross-sectional associations, standardized analytical pipelines, longitudinal multi-omic profiling of well-phenotyped cohorts, and independent replication of candidate signatures before microbiome-based biomarkers can be translated into clinical psychiatry [232].

Limitations, pitfalls, and methodological challenges

Reproducibility and methodological heterogeneity

Despite rapid expansion of microbiome–gut–brain research, translation from association to causation has proven substantially more difficult than early enthusiasm suggested. Human microbiome studies operate at the intersection of epidemiology, sequencing technology, microbial ecology, and high-dimensional statistics, yet methodological standardization has lagged behind discovery. As a result, heterogeneity in sampling, sequencing, analytic pipelines, and reporting practices continues to limit reproducibility and cross-study comparability. These structural limitations constrain mechanistic inference and hinder responsible clinical translation. Beyond laboratory variability, the intrinsic structure of microbiome data complicates interpretation. Sequencing generates relative rather than absolute abundance estimates, rendering datasets inherently compositional. Because increases in one taxon necessarily alter the measured abundance of others, analytical decisions critically shape inferred community shifts [233, 234]. Quantitative microbiome profiling further demonstrates that relative abundance measures can obscure absolute microbial load differences, indicating that compositional data may misrepresent true biological shifts [235]. Consequently, reported microbial “signatures” may reflect analytic framing as much as underlying biology. Even when harmonized pipelines are applied, large population studies reveal substantial cohort-dependent variability. Microbial patterns associated with aging, health, or resilience differ across demographic strata, comorbidity profiles, and environmental contexts, emphasizing that reported “signatures” are frequently context-specific rather than universal [236].

Statistical constraints, specificity, and causal inference

Statistical challenges further constrain interpretation. Microbiome datasets are sparse, characterized by zero counts exceeding what is expected from standard statistical distributions (“zero-inflated”) [237], and high-dimensional, creating substantial multiple testing burdens and increasing susceptibility to false discovery. Cross-disease meta-analyses demonstrate that many microbial associations are modest in magnitude and frequently shared across unrelated conditions, suggesting that some observed shifts may reflect nonspecific responses to illness or inflammation rather than disease-specific mechanisms [238]. Critical perspectives emphasize that large metagenomic datasets, while powerful, can generate compelling but potentially misleading narratives from correlational structures that remain highly sensitive to modeling decisions and covariate inclusion [239]. Efforts to strengthen inference through host genetic approaches, including microbiome genome-wide association studies and MR, represent important methodological advances. However, microbiome heritability is generally modest, genetic instruments often explain only a small fraction of microbial variance, and associations show limited replication across cohorts [240]. Moreover, microbial composition is dynamic and environmentally responsive, challenging assumptions of stability and temporality required for causal inference. Longitudinal profiling demonstrates that although core taxa may persist, substantial temporal fluctuation occurs within individuals, complicating assumptions of stability required for causal modeling [241]. Theoretical integration of microbiomes into quantitative genetic models further suggests that microbial contributions may alter both the mean and variance of host phenotypes in context-dependent ways, complicating classical models of trait heritability and adaptation [242]. Collectively, these constraints indicate that most microbiome–psychiatric associations remain probabilistic and hypothesis-generating, rather than established mechanistic pathways.

Confounding and context dependence in psychiatric cohorts

Confounding is a major challenge in psychiatric microbiome research because factors that influence microbial composition are also closely linked to psychiatric diagnosis, symptom severity, and treatment exposure. Medications, including psychotropics, antibiotics, and metabolic agents, can substantially alter microbial composition and function, making it difficult to distinguish disease-related changes from treatment effects [239, 243]. Additional factors such as diet, smoking, alcohol use, sleep disturbance, obesity, chronic stress, and socioeconomic status further shape the microbiome and vary across psychiatric populations [236]. Most studies also remain cross-sectional, limiting causal inference. Behavioral and physiological changes associated with psychiatric disorders, including altered diet, stress exposure, and circadian disruption, may themselves modify the microbiome, creating potential reverse causation pathways. As a result, disentangling bidirectional interactions within the gut–brain axis requires longitudinal and multimodal study designs.

Translational gaps: from animal models to human mechanism

Mechanistic understanding of the MGBA has been driven predominantly by animal models. These models provide critical mechanistic insight but differ substantially from human conditions in the ecological and developmental context. GF and antibiotic-treated rodents have demonstrated that microbial manipulation can influence neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, stress responsivity, immune signaling, and neurotransmitter systems. However, GF models represent profound ecological deprivation rather than subtle dysbiosis and observed behavioral phenotypes may reflect developmental adaptation to lifelong microbial absence. Differences in strain, housing conditions, diet, and developmental timing substantially influence outcomes, limiting direct extrapolation to heterogeneous human psychiatric disorders. Subtle microbial shifts observed in clinical populations cannot be equated with the profound microbial absence or depletion modeled in laboratory systems. Human studies linking microbial composition to mood and cognitive phenotypes often rely on taxonomic profiling without parallel functional validation, constraining inference regarding specific metabolites or immune pathways mediating behavioral effects [43]. Interventional strategies including psychobiotics and FMT are being explored, yet safety concerns remain salient. Reports of serious adverse events, including transmission of multidrug-resistant organisms, underscore the need for rigorous donor screening and regulatory oversight [244]. Even beyond psychiatry, broader microbiome science continues to confront reproducibility challenges across laboratories and experimental systems [245]. These translational limitations have important implications for the commercialisation of microbiome-based therapeutics, where live biotherapeutic products face unresolved challenges related to strain standardisation, manufacturing reproducibility, long-term stability, regulatory classification, and demonstration of consistent efficacy across heterogeneous host microbiomes [246]. Accordingly, therapeutic translation should proceed only based on longitudinal, mechanistically informed human studies incorporating functional readouts alongside symptom measures.

Conclusion

The MGBA provides a compelling framework linking peripheral biology to brain function and behaviour. While associations between GM and psychiatric disorders are well established, the key challenge remains demonstrating causality. Distinguishing whether microbial alterations are drivers, modifiers, or consequences of psychiatric illness is essential for meaningful clinical translation. Current evidence supports a model in which the microbiome influences psychiatric outcomes through interconnected pathways, including immune signaling, neuroendocrine regulation, neural communication, and barrier integrity. Among these, immune-mediated mechanisms show the strongest support, linking dysbiosis to systemic inflammation, BBB disruption, and neuroinflammatory processes. The HPA axis represents a bidirectional interface between stress and microbial ecology, while vagal and enteric pathways provide anatomical routes for gut–brain communication, although direct human evidence remains limited. Barrier dysfunction further integrates these systems by enabling microbial products to drive systemic and central inflammation.

Causal evidence varies across disorders. MDD shows the most consistent convergence across observational, mechanistic, and interventional studies, supporting moderate causal involvement, particularly in inflammatory subtypes. Anxiety and BDs show weaker and more variable evidence, suggesting a modulatory role. In SCZ, the microbiome appears to function as a disease modifier within broader immune and metabolic processes. Because PTSD is closely linked to chronic stress, HPA-axis dysregulation, inflammation, and altered barrier function, it provides a biologically plausible model for examining how stress-associated microbiome changes may contribute to psychiatric vulnerability. However, evidence from animal models, immune–endocrine pathways, and metabolite-focused studies remains incomplete, and large-scale validation is needed to establish causal evidence and inform early clinical interventions.

Most human studies are cross-sectional and vulnerable to confounding from diet, medication, and lifestyle. Methodological heterogeneity and reliance on animal models further limit causal inference and reproducibility. As a result, much of the current evidence remains associative rather than definitively causal. Translational approaches, including psychobiotics, diet, FMT, and biomarkers, show promise but remain mechanistically unresolved and clinically inconsistent. Moving forward, the field must shift toward mechanism-driven, stratified approaches rather than empirical interventions. Advancing microbiome psychiatry will require explicit prioritisation of longitudinal multi-omic randomised controlled trials with pre-specified mediation analyses to test whether microbial alterations causally contribute to psychiatric outcomes, alongside standardised analytical pipelines, independent replication across well-phenotyped cohorts, and stratification by biologically defined subgroups to support precision psychiatry. In conclusion, the gut microbiome is best understood as a biologically plausible and modifiable contributor within a complex, bidirectional system rather than a primary cause of psychiatric disorders. Progress will depend on transitioning from association to causation through rigorous, mechanism-based research capable of supporting clinically actionable interventions.
