Laser ultrasonic detection for defects of LAM components based on variable time window intensity mapping with adaptive 2σ thresholds
Authors: Zhuangzhuang Wan, Xue Bai, Jian Ma, Zhaowen Xu, Yaolu Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetallurgical defects in metal laser additive manufacturing (LAM) are inevitable due to complex non-equilibrium thermodynamics. A laser ultrasonic system was designed for detecting surface/near-surface defects in the layer-by-layer LAM process. An approach was proposed for ultrasonic imaging of defects based on variable time window intensity mapping with adaptive 2σ threshold denoising. The Gaussian mixture model hypothesis and expectation-maximization algorithm can automatically differentiate between components dominated by defects and background noises, thereby providing an adaptive threshold that accommodates detection environments and surface roughness levels. Results show that the ultrasonic wave reflection at defect boundaries diminishes far-field ultrasonic intensity upon pulsed laser irradiation on surface defects, enabling defect size and location characterization. This method is applicable to LAM samples with a significant surface roughness of up to 37.5 μm. It can detect superficial and near-surface defects down to 0.5 mm in diameter and depth, making it significant for online defect detection in additive manufacturing.Keywords: Laser additive manufacturing, Laser-ultrasonic online testing, Metallurgical defects, Ultrasonic imaging, Adaptive threshold denoising

Introduction

Laser additive manufacturing (LAM) technology, as an advanced manufacturing method, primarily utilizes a high-energy laser beam to heat raw materials. It simplifies complex three-dimensional (3D) forming into a two-dimensional (2D) lamination process for producing intricate metal components. It offers advantages such as high processing precision, efficient manufacturing, and optimal material utilization. LAM has found extensive applications in the aerospace, medical, automotive, and electronic industries [1], [2], [3]. However, the LAM forming process involves non-equilibrium thermodynamics complexities that can lead to metallurgical defects, including cracks (hot cracks and solidification cracks) and holes (metallurgical pores, keyholes, shrinkage holes, and non-fusion). If these defects are not detected in situ during printing, they may expand into macroscopic defects due to accumulated stress during subsequent deposition processes. This will affect the stability and consistency of additive manufacturing quality, thus limiting the widespread application of the technology [4], [5]. The inherent characteristic of lamination processing enables online nondestructive testing (NDT), which is a crucial approach for enhancing reliability in LAM-formed parts.

Online NDT methods for metal LAM primarily include X-ray computed tomography (XCT), infrared thermography, optical photography, structured light imaging, and ultrasonic detection [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. XCT non-destructively reveals the internal details of objects based on the interaction of X-rays with atoms. However, its installation and implementation require careful consideration of potential radiation risks [11]. Infrared thermography enables the identification of defects by analyzing the surface and internal heat transfer of specimens, providing advantages such as high efficiency and large-area detection. However, the detectability of defects can be influenced by surface conditions and background radiation. Additionally, accurately determining the size and shape of defects presents challenges [12]. The optical photography technique offers exceptional resolution and real-time performance, making it a valuable tool for detecting powder bed defects. However, its effectiveness in identifying metallurgical defects could be improved due to the challenging characteristics of LAM metal surfaces, such as high roughness and low reflectivity [12]. The technique of structured light imaging relies on computer and photoelectric technologies to perform real-time detection of geometric morphology and deformation of LAM parts. However, the interference caused by specimen reflectivity or arc light from processing laser sources can impact the projection of structural light and image capture, thereby increasing the challenges associated with metallurgical defect detection [13].

Ultrasonic detection technology has garnered significant attention due to its high sensitivity, penetration capabilities, directivity, and wide range of material applications. Ultrasonic waves can undergo reflection or scattering at defects due to the substantial difference in ultrasonic impedance between microstructures and defects. This allows for metallurgical defect detection through analysis of ultrasonic wave properties. Researchers have explored the feasibility of employing traditional piezoelectric ultrasonic techniques for online detection. Rieder et al. [14], [15] installed a piezoelectric longitudinal wave transducer with a frequency of 10 MHz beneath the substrate to monitor the metal 3D printing process. They observed changes in porosity caused by variations in laser power; however, they failed to detect the embedded hole defects due to the fixed position of the ultrasonic transducer during the printing process. Furthermore, when the substrate temperature exceeds that required for coupler gasification, there is an increased risk of detachment of the piezoelectric transducer during the detection process.

Compared to the traditional piezoelectric ultrasound technique, laser ultrasound is a novel ultrasonic testing technology that offers advantages for rapid, long-distance, and non-contact detection of materials in harsh industrial environments, such as high temperatures, and high pressures. Moreover, a single emission of the pulse laser enables the simultaneous generation of ultrasonic Rayleigh, longitudinal, and shear wave modes [16], [17]. This capability allows for the detection of distinct metallurgical characteristics, including surface defects and residual stress, as well as internal microstructures and defects. Consequently, laser ultrasonic detection exhibits significant advantages and holds great potential for online detection of metallurgical defects in LAM [18], [19]. Smith et al. [20] conducted online detection of the elastic moduli of materials in the selective laser melting (SLM) process by utilizing ultrasonic Rayleigh wave velocity based on spatially resolved acoustic spectrum (SRAS) technology. Park et al. [21] employed femtosecond laser ultrasound and laser polishing technology to enhance the spatial resolution to the micron level, enabling layer-by-layer online evaluation of mechanical properties such as Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio for laser cladding materials. Apart from assessing mechanical properties, there has been increasing attention to defect detection. Cerniglia et al. [22] performed B-scan imaging on samples produced by laser melting deposition (LMD), which contained near-surface through-holes or artificial surface cracks, demonstrating the feasibility of employing laser ultrasound technology for detecting defects during deposition processes. Davis et al. [23] detected hole defects with a diameter of 2 mm in C-Scan images but could not identify porosity.

The ultrasonic detection experiments revealed that the presence of surface roughness and vibration environment can lead to a reduction in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of defects [24]. Post-processing techniques utilizing Delay and Sum (DAS) imaging have demonstrated their proficiency in high-resolution detection of defects with low SNR; these encompass synthetic aperture focusing technique (SAFT) and total focusing method (TFM), both based on full matrix capture (FMC). The surface artificial hole defects with a diameter of 0.3 mm in SLM-manufactured AlSi10Mg and 316 L were examined by Lv et al. [25] using the SAFT method. Pieris et al. [26] introduced FMC and TFM in data post-processing, enabling unambiguous identification of built-in defects with a diameter of 0.5 mm and a depth of 26 mm within the SLM aluminum alloy specimens in laser ultrasonic C-scan images. Although the DAS method improves the resolution of C-scan images and the accuracy of defect detection, attaining real-time and rapid ultrasonic imaging poses a formidable challenge. Additionally, storing full matrix data requires high-performance data acquisition equipment. Traditional noise reduction techniques, such as wavelet denoising, enhance ultrasonic characteristic signals related to defects [27]. However, their adaptabilities to the complex environment and significant individual differences in surface roughness need improvement. The application of unsupervised machine learning intelligent denoising [28], [29] enables adaptive noise reduction. However, it takes a relatively long imaging time when dealing with large-scale data. Therefore, the urgency lies in exploring an ultrasonic imaging technique capable of fulfilling the requirements for adaptive and rapid online detection of metallurgical defects. The present study proposes an ultrasonic imaging method that utilizes variable time window intensity mapping (VTWIM) with adaptive 2σ thresholds. This approach effectively addresses the challenge of fluctuating background noise levels caused by complex environmental conditions and significant variations in surface roughness. Moreover, it can effectively fulfill the real-time requirements of online detection through time-domain analysis of ultrasonic signals captured from each scanning point. Its effectiveness in detecting submillimeter surface/near-surface hole defects in LAM samples has been further validated.

The manuscript is structured as follows: Section 2 briefly introduces the material preparations and laser ultrasonic experimental methods. In the subsequent section, we delve into the finite element (FE) simulations and present experimental findings pertaining to the propagation of laser-induced ultrasound waves in the presence of defects. Section 4 proposes an adaptive imaging method for defect detection using laser-ultrasonics. Section 5 validates the proficiency and efficacy of the proposed method in detecting various defects, including blind hole defects on samples with different surface roughnesses, optically invisible superficial defects, as well as near-surface cylindrical silt defects. Finally, we provide our main conclusions.

Materials and experiments

To rigorously validate the proposed method's effectiveness, three AlSi10Mg aluminum alloy specimens having dimensions of 50 × 70 × 20 mm³ were manufactured by SLM. Subsequently, we entrusted Shandong Ruixiang Mould Co., Ltd., which is qualified with International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 9001:2015 quality management system certificate, to precisely fabricate diverse artificial defects. Specifically, on the upper surface of the first specimen, blind hole defects varying in diameter (D=2.85, 1.9, 0.95, 0.5, 0.4, and 0.3 mm) while maintaining a constant depth, H, of 3 mm, were introduced (Fig. 1(a)). Furthermore, a superficial blind hole defect with a diameter and a depth of 0.5 mm was created on the second specimen's upper surface (Fig. 1(b)). For the third specimen, we created a cylindrical silt defect with a diameter of 0.5 mm located 0.5 mm below the surface (Fig. 1(c)). The surface roughness was assessed using the portable MITUTOYO SJ210 surface roughness tester according to ISO1997 standard specifications. Notably, even within specimens manufactured under identical process parameters, variations in their average surface roughness can be observed. Our findings indicate that the average surface roughness values for the three SLM specimens depicted in Fig. 1(a-c) were approximately 8.4 μm, 6.7 μm, and 7.7 μm, respectively.

SLM-manufactured specimen containing (a) surface blind hole defects varying in diameter (D=2.85, 1.9, 0.95, 0.5, 0.4, and 0.3 mm) while maintaining a constant depth, H, of 3 mm, (b) a superficial blind hole defect: D=0.5 mm and H=0.5 mm, and (c) a near-surface cylindrical silt defect with a diameter of 0.5 mm located 0.5 mm beneath the surface; (d) LMD-manufactured specimen containing surface blind hole defects with varying diameters of 2.85, 1.95, 0.95, and 0.5 mm and a constant depth of 3 mm.

Additionally, to evaluate the proposed method's defect detection performance on specimens with varying surface roughness, another AlSi10Mg aluminum alloy specimen having dimensions of 200×100×20 mm³ was fabricated using LMD technology. Blind hole defects on its upper surface, maintaining a depth of 3 mm and varying diameters of 2.85, 1.95, 0.95, and 0.5 mm, were further introduced (Fig. 1(d)). Due to the significant surface height fluctuations caused by roughness, the height variations exceeded the measurement range of the MITUTOYO SJ210 tester. Consequently, the roughness of the LMD samples was measured using the 2206B roughness tester from Harbin Measuring & Cutting Tool Group Co., Ltd, revealing an average surface roughness of 37.5 μm.

The laser ultrasonic experimental system is illustrated in Fig. 2. The Ultra 50 Nd: YAG nanosecond laser from Quantel by Lumibird emits a pulse laser beam with a wavelength of 1064 nm and a pulse width, τ, of 8 ns. The laser beam is transmitted through an energy transfer fiber. It passes through a collimating lens and becomes a focused laser beam at the focal point with a spot diameter, r0, of 0.95 mm, achieved using the field lens. This focused beam incidents the newly formed layer of the printed workpiece. As a result, the local area of this layer experiences a rise in transient temperature, leading to thermal expansion, known as thermoelastic effects. Ablative effects such as melting, gasification, and plasma may occur at higher incident power densities. Under these effects, the pulse laser emission stimulates ultrasonic Rayleigh waves, which are detected using two-wave mixing (TWM) interference technology by an AIR-1550-TWM receiver provided by Intelligent Optical Systems, Inc. It is equipped with a continuous laser source operating at a wavelength of 1550 nm, with a detection bandwidth ranging from 25 kHz to 125 MHz. The ADLINK’s PCIe-9852 data acquisition card collects and stores the ultrasonic signals in real-time with up to 200 MS/s sampling rate.

The laser ultrasonic testing system and the scanning detection scheme.

The galvanometer, positioned between the collimating lens and the field lens, is utilized for precise control of pulse laser triggering and automatic scanning detection of arbitrary plane movement locus. With a maximum scanning format of 140 mm × 140 mm and a repeated positioning accuracy of 3 μm, it operates as follows: Based on coordinate information from preset scanning points, the galvanometer adjusts its deflection angle and sends a pulse trigger signal to activate the flashlamp in the pulse laser. After a delay of 500 μs, the flashlamp transmits this pulse trigger signal to initiate the Q-switch operation. Subsequently, the Q-switch initiates laser output with the Flashlamp-to-Q-switch (FLQS) delay time. This delay time can be adjusted within the range of 145 μs to 500 μs to regulate the output energy of the pulse laser. In our experiments, an FLQS delay time of 280 μs was employed, which corresponded to a pulsed laser energy of 8 mJ, and a power density, P0, of 141 MW/cm2. The generated ultrasonic waveform was primarily dominated by the slight ablation regime. It is an intermediate between thermoelastic and ablative regimes, characterized by a weak melting of the solid surface accompanied by a relatively noticeable thermoelastic effect. The primary reason for choosing this mechanism as the preferred option lies in its ability to maintain a relatively high SNR under the stringent conditions of high-resolution real-time online signal acquisition, outperforming the thermoelastic mechanism. Note that the mechanism in question inflicts minimal damage to the material's surface, resulting in a spot mark that can be erased using alcohol. More importantly, these subtle damages are often covered by subsequent printing layers, resulting in no harm to the final performance of the workpiece. It is precisely these substantial advantages that have facilitated the widespread adoption of this mechanism in preliminary research [30].

The scanning scheme is illustrated in the partially zoomed-in image in the right corner of Fig. 2. The current forming layer is referred to as the xOy plane. The galvanometer controls the pulse laser to perform a raster scan on this plane at equidistant intervals in both x and y directions, denoted as ∆d. The 2D coordinate set of all scanning points is represented as Mnxn,yn. Here, n denotes the number of scanning points, and n=1,2,...,N. The detection point is fixed at an arbitrary point M0x0,y0 on the specimen’s surface to minimize the influence of material surface roughness on ultrasonic signal amplitude. The distance from the boundary to this point must exceed half of the wavelength of Rayleigh waves to ensure non-superposition between the reflected and transmitted waves. Subsequently, all laser ultrasonic signals from these scanning points are stored in a 3D array, Ux,y,t, which denotes the measured ultrasonic signals at a specific time and at a given scanning point (Bold fonts represent vectors or tensors). By applying the reciprocity theorem, this experimental setup can be considered equivalent to traversing detection at all points of Mnxn,yn with a fixed excitation point at M0x0,y0. Consequently, it becomes possible to extract ultrasonic Rayleigh wave amplitudes at different times for all scanning points and observe their propagation characteristics.

The ultrasonic Rayleigh wave generated by the point-like laser source is observed to propagate smoothly from right to left, exhibiting a slight convex shape in the reference sample. At t0=10.3 μs, prior to the arrival of the ultrasonic wave at the defect, the observed wavefront amplitude between these two samples exhibited a certain degree of fluctuation. This amplitude fluctuation is primarily attributed to the non-uniform roughness of the sample surface. The minor additional contributions stem from the instability of the laser ultrasonic system, which has two primary causes: Firstly, there is an inconsistency in the output energy of both the pulsed and continuous lasers. Secondly, slight variations in the incident pulsed laser energy at various scanning points occur due to the deflection characteristics of the galvanometer. While, at t0=10.5 μs, corresponding to the pulse laser spot falling into the defect, significantly reduced ultrasonic amplitudes are detected at all scanning points within the defect. The ultrasonic amplitude at the defect's center is found to be 82.5 % lower than that of the corresponding point in the reference sample. At t0=10.7 μs, corresponding to partial overlapping of the pulse laser spot with the defect, a 73.8 % smaller ultrasonic amplitude is still observed at the center of the wavefront compared to that in the reference sample. Yet, this amplitude is still four times higher than when the excitation point is situated at the defect center at t0=10.5 μs. This distinct behavior can be leveraged to develop defect imaging techniques that rely on the significant amplitude decrease at the defect location, thereby avoiding the formation of shadow areas. Such a finding holds immense significance for locating and characterizing defects.

FE simulations of ultrasonic propagation characteristics at defects

To reveal the physical mechanisms of the experimental phenomena mentioned above, FE simulations were conducted on the ultrasonic propagation for the case of the irradiation of pulsed laser spots on defects. These simulations were executed utilizing the commercial FE software ABAQUS, exploring the propagation characteristics of ultrasonic waves at the defect boundaries.

Considering the axisymmetric nature of the geometric model when a pulsed laser spot irradiates a hole defect, we simplified the 3D geometric model to a 2D axisymmetric model, featuring a radius of 10 mm and a height of 10 mm. A blind hole defect with a diameter of 2.85 mm was set at the center of the model. Its depth was set to be 3 mm and 0.5 mm, respectively, corresponding to the artificial surface hole defects with two different depths. The material was assumed to be isotropic and homogeneous, with Young's modulus of 70.2 GPa, Poisson's ratio of 0.33, and a density, ρ, of 2700 kg/m3. The upper boundary was set as the free boundary, while the other boundaries were configured as absorptive boundaries, indicated by the triangular symbols in Fig. 5, to reduce the influence of boundary reflection on the ultrasonic wave propagation.

FE mode for ultrasonic wave propagation in sample with surface blind hole defects. The triangular symbols denote absorptive boundaries.

In the FE simulation of ultrasonic wave propagation, the settings of mesh size and time interval are crucial for ensuring the convergence and stability of the results. These parameters determine the sampling accuracy of the model for ultrasonic waves in the space and time domains, respectively. Generally, the mesh size (Le) of the FE model is chosen to be between 1/20 and 1/10 of the minimum wavelength (λmin), while the time interval needs to satisfy the condition: ∆tFE ≤ 1/(20fmax), with fmax denoting the maximum frequency [32]. Based on the −6 dB bandwidth of the A-scan signal spectrum, its maximum effective frequency, fmax, is determined to be 5 MHz, corresponding to a minimum wavelength of approximately 0.6 mm. This allows us to derive that the mesh size (Le) should be no more than 0.03 mm, with a corresponding time interval of 10 ns.

Theoretical model of laser-ultrasonic imaging method for visualizing defects

The SNR of the laser ultrasonic online testing signal could be better, primarily due to three primary noise sources. First and foremost, ultrasonic scattering noises are associated with a large surface roughness. Secondly, low-frequency white noises are prone to be generated in complex environments characterized by high temperature, high pressure, and vibrations. Lastly, laser excitation for ultrasound efficiently produces high-frequency electrical noises. Considering these factors, effective signal denoising is crucial in improving defect detection accuracy. Although utilizing multi-pulse laser excitation at each point can reduce the effect of signal instability and enhance the SNR, it also significantly increases scanning time as Nave increases. Therefore, considering the detection efficiency, we set Nave to 5 and further incorporate low-pass filtering denoising techniques, achieving a significant enhancement in SNR to 16 dB, marking a more than twofold improvement. The resulting signal matrix is denoted as U¯denx,y,t.Compared to the amplitude analysis, ultrasonic wave intensity analysis demonstrates robustness against random noises and transient fluctuations. Based on the variation in ultrasonic amplitude within defects and in the undamaged region, we propose employing the VTWIM method for achieving ultrasonic defect imaging. Subsequently, an adaptive denoising method is proposed for high-accuracy defect detection by analyzing the ultrasonic characteristics of background noises.

Adaptive 2σ threshold denoising based on the Gaussian mixture models

It is important to note that a large surface roughness can significantly reduce the ultrasonic intensity, leading to false detection of defects. There are three main reasons: 1) The presence of variations in the microscopic morphology and surface flatness leads to a reduction in optical reflectivity, thereby impacting the ratio of alternating and direct currents in the laser ultrasonic receiver. Consequently, this affects the SNR of ultrasonic signals; 2) Ultrasonic scattering occurs at microstructural boundaries on rough surfaces during the propagation of ultrasonic Rayleigh waves, resulting in the decrease of ultrasonic intensity [24], [34]. 3) Notable inhomogeneities exist in the distribution of surface roughness, leading to variations in incident ultrasonic intensities.

Laser ultrasonic scanning detection was performed at randomly selected 7000 points with a spacing interval of no less than 0.4 mm in both the SLM- and LMD-manufactured reference samples without defects. In fact, the inspection time is profoundly impacted by the repetition frequency of the pulsed laser. Due to budgetary constraints, the pulsed laser employed in our experiments operates at a repetition frequency of 20 Hz. Consequently, for 7000 scanning points, where each point is illuminated repeatedly with five pulses, the entire scanning process takes approximately 30 minutes. However, the scanning efficiency would undergo a remarkable enhancement if a pulsed laser with a high repetition frequency is employed. As an example, when the repetition frequency is increased to 1000 Hz [35], a frequency commonly used in industrial applications, the scanning time would be drastically reduced to 35 seconds.

The incident ultrasonic intensities I at each scanning point were plotted for SLM and LMD samples in Fig. 9(a) and (b), respectively. The SLM sample exhibits significant fluctuations with a mean of −3.26 dB and a standard deviation of 1.54 dB (denoted as μ0 and σ0, respectively). The reduction in ultrasonic intensity exceeds −13 dB, comparable to or even more significant than the defect-dominated ultrasonic intensity level, thus acting as significant background noise. Compared to the SLM sample, the attenuation of ultrasonic Rayleigh wave intensity for LMD samples with larger roughness fluctuates more noticeably; with a reduction of 37 % in minimum value, 43 % decrease in average value, and an increase in the standard deviation by 29 %. As depicted in Fig. 9(c-d), the probability distribution statistics of the data are represented by blue histograms. Subsequently, these histograms are converted into a set of points (I, P(I)), where I represents the center of each bin, and P(I) denotes the experimental probability. This probability is computed by normalizing the absolute frequency with respect to the total number of events, thereby reflecting the bin's height. Following this, a curve is fitted to these points using a Gaussian function, and the fitting results are plotted as red curves in Fig. 9(c-d). The results indicate that the incident ultrasonic intensities fit a normal probability distribution for both SLM and LMD specimens with a goodness-of-fit above 0.94. Moreover, the probability distribution within the interval of μ0−2σ0,μ0+2σ0 exceeds 95 %.

After considering the influence of material surface roughness, it is feasible to select a fixed threshold associated with the standard deviation of the ultrasonic intensity in the reference sample for normalizing the incident ultrasonic intensity of all scanning points. However, the manufacturing process of LAM specimens may result in individual differences in surface roughness between samples, even when identical process parameters are employed. If the threshold obtained from the reference sample is too large compared to that of the test sample, it may result in missed detections of small defects; conversely, if it is too small, there may be an increase in background noises, leading to false detections. Therefore, selecting an adaptable threshold that can adapt to complex environments and accommodate diverse levels of surface roughness is crucial.

We have observed that the ultrasonic intensity within the defects also follows a Gaussian distribution but with a mean value of greater magnitude than that in the flawless region. Consequently, the incident ultrasonic intensities in the full-field scanning format of samples with defects conform to a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), indicating a linear superposition of Gaussians. The fixed threshold based on statistical data from the reference sample is found to be unsuitable for detecting defects in LAM specimens with high levels and substantial degrees of inhomogeneity in surface roughness. Results and discussions

Detection for surface blind hole defects

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method in detecting surface blind hole defects, we mainly targeted the optically invisible superficial defects on SLM samples. Subsequently, we conducted thorough inspections on SLM and LMD samples with varying surface roughness levels, containing deeper blind holes with different diameters. These cases aim to illustrate the robustness of the proposed method in overcoming limitations posed by different defect depths and varying surface roughness when measured from the same side. Throughout the experimental process, we ensured the consistent utilization of a laser spot diameter of 0.95 mm, thereby guaranteeing that the laser-induced ultrasonic signals possess uniform central frequencies and bandwidths.

The fast and high-resolution automatic scanning detection by laser-ultrasonics can be achieved through synchronous coordinating control of the galvanometer and pulsed laser. However, there is a trade-off between the efficiency and resolution of defect detection. Given fixed defect diameter and laser spot size, reducing the scanning interval can enhance defect detection accuracy but decrease overall efficiency. Our research has revealed that insufficient scanning points within defects result in the formation of serrated or incomplete edges. Conversely, as the number of scanning points increases within the defect, ultrasonic signals become more proficient at capturing the intricate details of the defect edge. This correlation leads to a power-law growth in detection accuracy as the ratio of defect diameter to scanning interval increases. Based on our findings, it is advisable to ensure that the ratio D/∆d exceeds ten. This recommendation is crucial in determining an optimal scanning interval that comprehensively balances both detection accuracy and efficiency.

Detection for optically invisible superficial defects

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method in detecting superficial hole defects, we performed a comprehensive testing on SLM specimens that harbored a 0.5 mm-depth blind hole defect of 0.5 mm in diameter. The inspection of this defect poses a significant challenge as its depth is comparable to the height fluctuations caused by surface roughness.

The line-structured light technology, tailored for online detection of additive manufacturing surface morphology, is employed to identify this superficial defect for further comparison with laser-ultrasonic inspection. Equipped with a laser source of 405 nm wavelength, the structured light projector achieves a remarkable measurement accuracy of 40 μm for the surface height of standard gauge blocks under static conditions. To mitigate the effects of minute vibrations during the robotic arm's movement, we conducted static data collection of surface height contour curves for two distinct scan lines: one traversing the defect and the other bypassing it. Utilizing the least-squares median line (indicated as the red lines) as the reference horizontal line, we plotted the respective results in Fig. 13, with the dashed line representing the defect-free scan line and the solid line indicating the defective scan line. The center of the defect is located at the origin.

Surface height contour curves along two distinct scan lines: (a) showcasing the scan line traversing the 0.5 mm-depth superficial defect with a diameter of 0.5 mm, and (b) presenting the defect-free scan line. The least-squares median line (indicated in red) serves as the reference horizontal line, and the origin marks the center of the defect.

Upon a comprehensive data analysis, we observed that the defect-free scan line exhibited a maximum profile peak height, Rz, of 0.12 mm, whereas the defective scan line displayed a slightly higher Rz of 0.15 mm. Notably, the height detection result for the 0.5 mm-deep defect was only −0.1 mm. It is speculated that the significant deviation in height measurement could be attributed to factors such as roughness, color, and reflectivity, which can influence the clarity and contrast of the laser stripe image when the line laser is projected onto the specimen surface. This outcome highlights the challenges encountered by line-structured light technology in identifying a 0.5 mm-deep blind hole defect on the rough surface of SLM specimens. Furthermore, within the scanning length, the average surface waviness, λwaviness, reaches 3.3 mm, which directly reflects the difficulty in identifying blind hole defects with a diameter of 0.5 mm. It is worth mentioning that online inspection often relies on the scanning movement of the robotic arm, and the inevitable minute vibrations during this movement adversely impact the measurement accuracy of surface point cloud coordinates, thereby affecting the detection of surface defects.

The laser ultrasonic inspection was further conducted and the resulting image is presented in Fig. 14. The imaging result clearly demonstrates the existence of the defects. The diameter of the fitted circle, denoted as D', serves as the measured defect diameter. Within this manuscript, the ultrasonic imaging outcomes distinctively represent the actual defect contour in solid blue and the circular fitting contour in dashed black lines. The ultrasonic imaging result from Fig. 14 demonstrates that the defect edges are distinct, the positioning is precise, and the diameter detection accuracy was approximately 90 %. This outcome highlights a significant advantage of the proposed method in detecting invisible defects.

Upon analyzing the results, it becomes apparent that, for SLM samples with an average surface roughness of 8.4 μm, our method achieves high-precision imaging of defects with diameters of 0.5 mm and above, capturing complete and precise contours along with an accuracy exceeding 84 % in size detection. Notably, the highest false detection rate of 3.14 % was observed for the defect with a diameter of 0.5 mm, marking a slight decline in performance compared to the misdetection-free outcomes observed in Fig. 14. This slight reduction in imaging quality can be largely attributed to the increase in surface roughness from 6.7 μm to 8.4 μm.

Despite having a roughness four times greater than SLM samples, the proposed ultrasonic imaging method can identify all defects on LMD samples. However, due to higher roughness levels, edges of defects appear incomplete and exhibit significantly serrated contours in the ultrasonic imaging. There are significant background noises for the defects with diameters of 0.95 and 0.5 mm, resulting in a false detection rate exceeding 2.2 %. As listed in Table 2, it is found that defect imaging accuracy for LMD samples is lower compared to SLM samples, fluctuating around 80 %. As the defect diameter grows, the differences in detection accuracies relative to SLM sample results become increasingly apparent.

Further laser ultrasonic testing is conducted on SLM samples containing smaller defects with diameters of 0.3 mm and 0.4 mm. As depicted in Fig. 16 (a), the ultrasonic imaging results reveal that the size detection accuracy remains nearly 90 % at D=0.4 mm; however, background noises are present with a false detection rate up to 4.42 %, indicating that the reduction of ultrasonic intensity caused by roughness at specific local points may be greater than that caused by defects. As depicted in Fig. 16 (b), at the smallest diameter ratio of D=0.3 mm, the defect contour exhibits noticeable distortion, and significant noises appear in the defect-free areas, leading to a false detection rate of 3.84. In this scenario, the accuracy of defect size detection is rapidly reduced to 53 %.

To gain a deeper understanding of this phenomenon, we conducted FE simulations to analyze the ultrasonic wave propagation when a laser spot of a fixed diameter (0.95 mm) irradiates defects of six varying diameters. We recorded the ultrasonic signal amplitude at the far-field detection point of No. 14 and plotted its ratio to the incident signal amplitude as a function of the defect diameter, as illustrated by square symbols in Fig. 16 (c). Furthermore, the outcomes derived from FE simulations are compared with the experimental measurements, which are depicted by circular symbols in Fig. 16 (c). The findings indicate that when the defect diameter is bigger than the laser spot size, as depicted by the black dashed circle, the entirety of the incident energy is confined within the defect, resulting in a substantial decline in the far-field signal amplitude. This reduction is estimated to be no less than 90 % according to FE simulations and 80 % based on experimental measurements. Conversely, when the defect diameter diminishes below the spot size, the signal amplitude drops to approximately 25 %-35 % of the incident signal, signifying a reduction of approximately 65 %-75 %. In this case, a portion of the laser spots extends beyond the defect region when their centers align (as shown by the red dotted circle). This allows a fraction of the ultrasonic energy to arrive at the receiving point without encountering any defects, resulting in a less significant reduction in ultrasonic energy that may hinder the resolution of detecting such defects. Nonetheless, experimental ultrasonic imaging depicted in Fig. 15 demonstrates that the defect remains discernible in the scenario even when the defect diameter is half the spot size. It is suggested that achieving higher precision in defect size detection necessitates a defect-to-spot diameter ratio of no less than 0.5.

Detection for near-surface cylindrical silt defects

To validate the proficiency of our proposed method in identifying near-surface defects, we fabricated a specimen containing a cylindrical slit defect, measuring 0.5 mm in diameter and positioned 0.5 mm below the surface. Using the defect axis as a symmetrical reference, pulsed laser excitation points were uniformly positioned on both sides of this line, evenly spaced at 0.05 mm intervals. The pulsed laser scanning was conducted in a direction perpendicular to the cylindrical silt defect's axis. For each scanning line, the detection location remained stationary along the scanning direction, maintaining a minimum distance of 7 mm from the excitation point to mitigate any interference stemming from undesired modes, including skimming longitudinal and shear waves. Regarding the characterization accuracy of defect size, accurately characterizing the size of near-surface defects poses greater challenges than that of surface defects based on the proposed method. Specifically, the wavelength of the Rayleigh wave serves as a crucial factor determining the distance of interference enhancement between the reflected and transmitted Rayleigh waves. As shown in Fig. 18 (b), this distance of interference enhancement is approximately 0.5 mm, which is in good agreement with the half-wavelength calculated based on its period and phase velocity of the Rayleigh wave. Consequently, the width of the disturbance area caused by the defect observed is roughly 1 mm, revealing an inherent limitation of the utilized ultrasonic frequency in precisely measuring the width dimension. By reducing the diameter of the laser spot or employing a mask aperture modulation [20], we can effectively increase the frequency of ultrasonic Rayleigh waves, thereby reducing the interference distance and enhancing the accuracy of defect size characterization. The corresponding work is currently underway.

Conclusions

This study proposes a novel laser ultrasonic imaging method for defect detection based on the VTWIM method combined with adaptive 2σ threshold denoising. This method is specifically designed for defect detection in layer-by-layer LAM processes, enabling accurate localization and quantitative size characterization of hole defects. The adaptive 2σ threshold considers the Gaussian probability distribution characteristics of undamaged areas associated with complex environments and material surface roughness. It can be calculated using the EM algorithm, assuming that the ultrasonic intensity at the full-field scanning detection points follows a GMM. The GMM hypothesis and EM algorithm can automatically differentiate between Gaussian components dominated by defects and undamaged areas, thereby providing an adaptive 2σ denoising threshold that accommodates the detection conditions and surface roughness levels.

The findings from FE analysis and experimental testing unequivocally revealed that, upon irradiation of surface defects with a pulsed laser, the reflection effect occurring at the defect boundary significantly diminished the far-field ultrasonic intensity. This reduction in intensity was pivotal in enabling the proposed method to accurately determine the size and precise location of defects. Laser ultrasonic scanning detection was performed on samples containing surface hole defects with varying diameters, manufactured by SLM and LMD techniques. These specimens displayed notable disparities in their average surface roughness, measuring approximately 8.4 μm and 37.5 μm, respectively. The ultrasonic imaging results validate the effectiveness of our proposed method by enhancing defect edge features while reducing background noise. The proposed method boasts a remarkable ability to detect surface blind hole defects with a diameter of no less than 0.5 mm, delivering comprehensive imaging edges that exceed 80 % size detection accuracy. Furthermore, it demonstrates its proficiency in identifying visually imperceptible superficial hole flaws, as well as near-surface cylindrical silt defects, each characterized by a depth and diameter of 0.5 mm. Results demonstrate that laser ultrasonic imaging holds promising potential for in-situ detection during manufacturing processes. The micro-level laser spot diameter is anticipated to attain high-resolution and high-precision micro-defect detection.
