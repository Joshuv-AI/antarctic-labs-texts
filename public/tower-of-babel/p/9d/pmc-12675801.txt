Differences in the Outcomes of Early Active Mobilization Following Flexor Tendon Repair Between Zone 1 and Distal Zone 2 Injuries
Authors: Koji Moriya, Yutaka Maki, Hisao Koda, Masahiro Odagiri, Shota Matsuzawa, Naoto Tsubokawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeComplete release of the A4 pulley is recommended for flexor tendon repair to allow smooth tendon excursion, provided the proximal sheath is mostly intact. However, it is unclear whether the active range of motion of finger joints differs when the flexor digitorum profundus tendon is repaired at different levels with A4 pulley release. We evaluated differences in the outcomes of flexor tendon repair of zone 1 and zone 2A injuries using a six-strand suture (the Yoshizu #1 technique) and complete release of the A4 pulley, followed by an early active mobilization protocol.MethodsThis retrospective case series analyzed 27 fingers from 22 patients: 12 index, 5 middle, 4 ring, and 6 little fingers. There were 13 zone 1 injuries in 12 patients and 14 zone 2A injuries in 10 patients. All tendons were repaired using the same technique, followed by a controlled active mobilization regimen initiated within the first three postoperative weeks. The follow-up period averaged 7 months (range: 3–13 months).ResultsNo tendon bowstringing was evident in any finger. Rupture of one flexor tendon repair occurred 8 weeks after primary surgery in one patient with a zone 1 injury. Excluding this case, no significant differences were observed in the active range of motion of the two finger joints between patients with zone 1 and 2A injuries. However, active motion of the distal interphalangeal joint of zone 1 was significantly reduced compared with that of zone 2A, given the increase in the total extension deficit.ConclusionsIn this series, although complete release of the A4 pulley and postoperative treatment were performed in the same way, zone 1 injuries demonstrated greater total extension deficits and failed to achieve distal interphalangeal joint motion outcomes comparable with those observed in zone 2A injuries.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.Key words: A4 pulley, Flexor tendon repair, Venting, Zone 1, Zone 2A

Flexor tendon injury localization between the A4 and A2 pulleys is a important negative predictor for active range of motion (AROM) after primary repair.1 In zone 1 or distal zone 2 injuries, the resistance to gliding of the repaired tendon can be increased when the A4 pulley is repaired or insufficiently released. Therefore, many surgeons release the A4 pulley entirely to ensure free excursion of the tendon repair. This does not trigger clinically significant bowstringing.2, 3, 4, 5 In terms of flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon excursion and bowstringing after complete release of the A4 pulley, Savage reported that the maximal changes associated with incisions into even all four pulleys (the C1, A3, C2, and A4 pulleys) were small, based on the results of a biomechanical study using fresh cadavers.6 Thus, biomechanical changes caused by complete release of the A4 pulley may not clinically compromise the outcomes of flexor tendon repair. However, it remains unclear whether differences exist in the finger joint AROM after primary flexor tendon repairs during which the A4 pulley is released at different levels of FDP tendon division.

The present study was performed to evaluate differences in the outcomes of flexor tendon repair using a six-strand suture with the Yoshizu #1 technique7 and complete release of the A4 pulley, followed by early active mobilization between zone 1 and distal zone 2 injuries. We hypothesized that, provided complete release of the A4 pulley and postoperative management were performed in a similar manner, treatment outcomes would be similar regardless of minor variations in the extent of release of other pulleys and the injury site.

Methods

This was a retrospective case series study. Potential subjects were identified by searching our institutional billing records between 2012 and 2020 for use of the International Classification of Diseases 10 code S561. The inclusion criteria were complete FDP tendon laceration, with or without concomitant flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendon laceration in zones 1 and 2A (from the distal margin of insertion of the FDS tendon to the proximal margin of the insertion on the middle phalanx)8; tendon repair using a six-strand suture (Yoshizu #1 technique which is a combination of the Tsuge suture using a looped thread and the modified Kessler suture using double threads with two needles attached to monofilament nylon)7; complete release of the A4 pulley; rehabilitation via early active mobilization; and a follow-up period of >2 months. The exclusion criteria were replantation or finger revascularization, extensor tendon injury, fractures, joint injuries, skin defects, and missing data during the treatment period. Patient demographics and injury details were retrieved from hospital records. The level of division of the FDP tendon and the location of the excised pulley were determined by the first author based on surgical records, which were recorded by the surgeons within a few days after surgery. All surgical records of our patients have always included simple sketches of the surgical procedures, including the site of injury of these tendons, which enabled us to retrospectively designate the zones of injury and the exact treatment of each pulley of a finger according to the surgical sketches in the surgical notes. If the exact injury zone was not clear from the sketches, the zone was decided upon after careful discussion with co-authors. This study was approved by the local ethics committee, and all patients provided written informed consent for their data to be used in this study.

Surgical methods

All operations were performed under either axillary block or general anesthesia using magnifying loupes. A Bruner’s incision was used to extend the original laceration after thorough debridement of the wound. The level of FDP tendon division was confirmed by identifying the distal stump of the tendon when the finger was fully extended. The injured and adjacent pulleys were opened to expose the cut tendon for a length of approximately ≤18 mm, depending on the size of the finger. Based on the physiological gliding amplitude of the FDP tendon at the repair site (approximately 20 mm in zone 2 and 10 mm in zone 1),9 we always completely incised the A4 pulley during primary repair for zone 1 and 2A injuries (excluding FDP tendon laceration near by the distal interphalangeal [DIP] joint) because the ends of the lacerated FDP tendon were found to be difficult to pass under the A4 pulley, the A4 pulley had to be opened to achieve surgical tendon repair (when the FDP tendon laceration site was close to or under the A4 pulley), or the repaired site was caught on the distal edge of the A4 pulley when the finger was passively in the flexed position. In addition, the C2 and A3 pulleys were released if necessary to allow the repaired tendons to move freely without impingement when the C1 and A2 pulleys were intact. When the FDS tendon was lacerated in zone 2A injury, it was usually repaired using a figure-of-eight suture with 4–0 or 5–0 monofilament nylon. When one slip was cut completely but the other was uninjured, or when the FDS tendon was too small to repair (eg, in the little finger), the lacerated FDS tendon was not always repaired. When the FDS tendon was not repaired, the proximal stump was left in situ. The FDP tendon was repaired using the six-strand suture described in the Yoshizu #1 technique, accompanied by a simple running peripheral epitendinous suture of 6–0 monofilament nylon. Digital nerves (if damaged) were repaired under an operating microscope after the tendon repair had been completed with either 9–0 or 8–0 nylon.

Postoperative treatment

Digit rehabilitation commenced using the controlled active mobilization regime; this was initiated on the first postoperative day to minimize adhesion as soon as possible. The hand was immobilized in a dorsal plaster splint with the wrist in a neutral position and the metacarpophalangeal joints at 30° flexion in addition to a strap band keeping all fingers in flexion (Fig. 1). For the first 2 weeks after surgery, the interphalangeal (IP) joints of all fingers were flexed by the strap band to prevent extension contracture of the finger and minimize the power required to actively mobilize the tendon during the day.

Active IP joint extension exercises without the strap band were initiated on the first postoperative day. Active flexion exercises without passive help were not encouraged because the finger and tendons would be swollen and have more friction. Instead, place and active hold exercises were performed as early active flexions. We also performed controlled passive extension to prevent flexion contractures of the IP joints. Isolated FDS gliding exercises were added regardless of the FDS tendon repair. The splint was removed during hand therapy under the supervision of the therapist.3,10 At night, the strap band was released, and the digits were splinted in a comfortable extended position.

Unassisted active flexion exercises within the restraints of the dorsal plaster splint were allowed at 1 week after beginning the mobilization exercises, and synergistic wrist-motion exercises (the wrist was extended when the digits were flexed, and the wrist was flexed when the digits were extended) were performed at 4 weeks after surgery. At 6 weeks, the dorsal plaster splint was removed, except at night. The splint was removed completely at 8 weeks after surgery, and the patients were permitted to extend the wrist and digits simultaneously. Power gripping was allowed at 12 weeks.

Outcome measurements

A surgeon or therapist who had been part of the treatment team measured active digit motion with a goniometer at the two finger joints (ie, the proximal interphalangeal [PIP] and DIP joints) at the time of the final evaluation. The clinical results were evaluated with reference to the AROM of the PIP and DIP joints using the criteria of Strickland and Glogovac11 and Tang.12 The Tang criteria have a more stringent “excellent” category than the Strickland and Glogovac criteria and the “failure” category to identify cases in which the repair ruptured or severe adhesions and/or joint contracture preventing tendon motion developed, requiring a secondary operation. The system of Moiemen and Elliot,13 which assesses only the range of motion of the DIP joint, was used instead of the assessment methods for zone 2 injuries, which evaluate both the PIP and DIP joints, because zone 1 injuries predominantly affect the function of the DIP joint. Fingers undergoing tenolysis were included in the calculation of the final AROM and functional outcomes.

Complications, such as infection, rerupture, stiffness requiring reoperation, and tendon bowstringing, were monitored during the study. Tendon bowstringing was diagnosed clinically without the use of ultrasound or other imaging modalities, based on the presence of tendon lift-off not only during active flexion but also during resistive finger flexion.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as the mean (SD), and skewed data as the median (interquartile range). The means of normally distributed variables were compared using Student t test or Welch’s t test based on the results of an F-test comparing the variance between the two groups. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. In all analyses, a P value < .05 indicated statistical significance, with a 95% confidence interval. A post-hoc power analysis was not conducted due to the limited sample size.

Results

A total of 29 digits of 24 consecutive patients treated between 2012 and 2020 met the selection criteria; two fingers of two patients were excluded because the medical records did not include adequate surgical notes and lacked objective data at the final evaluation. Therefore, 27 digits of 22 patients were included in the analysis. The patients had an average age of 43 years (range: 18–69 years) at the time of injury, and six were women. The injuries involved 12 index, 5 middle, 4 ring, and 6 little fingers. The injuries were relatively clean in all fingers (cuts made by a sharp instrument, such as a knife or glass, with or without unilateral digital nerve injury to the finger). Discussion

Of the patients who underwent complete release of the A4 pulley for primary flexor tendon repair in zone 1 and 2A injuries, no case evidenced tendon bowstringing. When considered alone, the AROM of the DIP joint was significantly lower in zone 1 injuries compared with that in zone 2A injuries, whereas there were no statistically significant differences between zones 1 and 2A in terms of the AROM of the PIP joint or the combination of the PIP and DIP joints. In addition, the extension deficit of the DIP joint in zone 1 was significantly greater than that in zone 2A.

The average AROM of the PIP and DIP joints in zone 1 injuries managed by early motion protocol after primary flexor tendon repair varies from 130° to 166°, and the tendon rupture rate is 0%–7.1%.15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Chang et al20 reported that the mean AROM for zone 2A was 145° without tendon rupture. Similarly, Moriya et al4 noted that no tendon ruptures occurred in any of the patients with a mean AROM of two joints of 149° after flexor tendon repair in zone 2A, followed by early active mobilization. Our results are consistent with previous series with rupture rates of 8% in zone 1 and AROM at the final visit of 148° for zone 1 and 152° for zone 2A. Moiemen and Elliot reported that the rates of good and excellent results for repair of zone 1 injuries were substantially lower than those of zone 2 injuries in terms of the total range of motion of the finger.13 The present study showed no significant differences in the AROM of the PIP and DIP joints according to the zone of injury. However, early active mobilization following primary repair for zone 1 injuries could not restore good DIP joint function compared with zone 2A injuries. We think that the outcome in zone 1 injury would not be similar to that in zone 2A from the DIP joint motion due to the increase in total extension deficit obtained, even with the same surgical procedures (eg, repair technique and release of the pulleys) and postoperative treatments. However, the retrospective nature of our study did not permit the determination of causal relations.

Loss of PIP joint extension is a well-known factor affecting the outcome of primary flexor tendon repair. However, DIP flexion contracture has not been recognized as a problem.16,21 In previous studies, the mean extension deficits at the DIP joint after early active mobilization followed by zone 1 flexor tendon repair were 8°, which is consistent with our results.16,18 In zone 1, especially over the DIP joint, the palmar plate may be injured primarily, combined with FDP tendon laceration, or undergo scarring later during healing, resulting in joint contracture.13 In addition, Amadio et al22 noted that hemorrhage within the sheath or around the flexor tendons consequent to vincular rupture could be responsible for increased peritendinous fibrosis and decreased final AROM in zone 2 injuries. The laceration site in zone 1 is nearer the vinculum brevia of the FDP tendon than zone 2A (Fig. 3). The increase in extension deficit of the DIP joint may be related to the greater vulnerability of the short vinculum of the FDP tendon in zone 1 injuries compared with zone 2A injuries. In addition, our postoperative flexed finger position may worsen the DIP joint extension after zone 1 primary flexor tendon repair.

Schematic drawing of the relationship between tendon laceration and vincular injury. A The vinculum brevia of the FDP tendon is vulnerable in zone 1 injury. B Injury of the vinculum brevia of the FDP can be avoided in zone 2A injury because the vinculum brevia is remote from the FDP injury site.

Minor tendon bowstringing following partial or complete release of the pulleys and flexor tendon repair in zone 2 can be detected by ultrasound imaging, even in the absence of clinically apparent bowstringing.23 This phenomenon may be associated with the slight limitation of DIP joint flexion observed in the present study. However, Reissner et al23 reported that minor tendon bowstringing detected by ultrasound falls within normal physiological limits and does not result in clinically considerable functional impairment.

This study had some limitations, including the sample size, variations in follow-up duration, and potential bias associated with the retrospective design. A small sample size in this study may render the statistical analysis inadequate for comparison. Risks of bias were evident when we used the ROBINS-I tool to evaluate our study.24 This tool categorizes potential biases in nonrandomized studies into the following seven domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. Potential confounders, such as patient age, the large number of surgeons relative to the small sample size, heterogeneity in pulley management except the A4 pulley, and follow-up time, could not be adjusted for in zone 1 and 2A injuries. Variations in the extent of pulley release may potentially influence treatment outcomes; however, a comparative analysis could not be conducted because of the limited sample size in this study. However, the risks of bias associated with participant selection, missing data, or selection of the reported results would be low to moderate because the factors associated with these domains are relatively homogenous. In addition, we lacked sufficient statistical power to reach conclusions, and it is therefore conceivable that the outcomes might have changed if more fingers had been included in our study.
