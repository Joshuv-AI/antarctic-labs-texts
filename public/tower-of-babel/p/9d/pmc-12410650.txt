Association between prone position ventilation and tracheal injury score in intensive care unit patients with invasive ventilation: a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study in China
Authors: Pei Zhang, Huaihai Lu, Wenbin Tian, Ning Yu, Ye Tian, Shuxian Ma, Ya Liu, Chunhua Yin, Zhigang Cai, Lining Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTObjectivesAt present, the available evidence regarding the relationship between prone position ventilation (PPV) and tracheal injury score remains inadequate. This study seeks to gain deeper insights into the relationship between PPV and tracheal injury score in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with invasive ventilation.DesignA secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study.SettingOur study was conducted in a Class III hospital in Hebei Province, China.ParticipantsPatients aged over 18 years admitted to the ICU between 31 May 2020 and 10 November 2023 with a tracheal tube and expected to be on the tube for more than 24 hours.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was tracheal injury score, assessed via fibreoptic bronchoscopy for hyperaemia, ischaemia, ulcers and perforation. Lesions were classified as moderate, severe or confluent based on extent.ResultsAmong the 149 selected participants, the average age was 58.4±16.2 years, with 65.8% being male. The results of adjusted linear regression showed that PPV was associated with tracheal injury scores (β: 5.33; 95% CI 3.84 to 6.82). Subgroup analysis indicated that PPV is significantly associated with an increased tracheal injury score, and this association remains consistent across different ages, sex, septic shock and non-septic shock groups, and duration of intubation <8 days and ≥8 days groups (p<0.001).ConclusionsOur findings suggest PPV may be associated with tracheal injury score, highlighting a potential link between PPV and tracheal injury score.Trial registration numberChiCTR2000037842, registered 3 September 2020 retrospectively registered, https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=57011.Keywords: Intensive Care Units, Adult intensive & critical care, INTENSIVE & CRITICAL CARE

Introduction

As the application of high-flow oxygen therapy and non-invasive mechanical ventilation has expanded, the proportion of patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation has gradually decreased.1,3 A recent large-scale, multicentre epidemiological study involving 927 intensive care units (ICUs) has shown that the percentage of patients undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation via endotracheal intubation dropped from 95% in 1998 to 86% in 2010.4 However, there are still many critically ill patients who require invasive mechanical ventilation.

Tracheal lesions are a frequently encountered complication of endotracheal intubation in critically ill patients.5 To prevent potential complications such as tracheal mucosal ischaemia or tracheal perforation, it is essential to maintain tracheal cuff pressure (Pcuff) within the range of 25–30 cmH2O.6

Prone position ventilation (PPV) is a critical intervention for improving oxygenation in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and reducing mortality rates.7 With the global spread of COVID-19, the use of PPV has seen broader application. In a study evaluating the treatment of critically ill COVID-19 patients with ARDS, it was found that as many as 79% were managed using PPV.8 However, although PPV is not an invasive procedure, it is complex and has many potential complications. Several studies have shown that PPV can lead to complications such as stress injury, unplanned extubation, falls, aspiration and arrhythmias, with an incidence as high as 56.9%.9,11 At present, the available evidence regarding the relationship between PPV and tracheal injury score is inadequate.

Based on the above studies, we hypothesised that PPV may be an important indicator of tracheal injury score. The aim of this study was to demonstrate the association between PPV and tracheal injury score.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This secondary analysis used data from a previously published prospective cohort study.12 The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University has waived the ethics approval for this secondary analysis in accordance with the policy on reuse of de-identified data. This decision is based on four factors: the complete anonymisation of the source dataset (originally approved under IRB no. 2020-T010 at the same institution), the absence of additional risks to participants, consistency with the future research provisions in the original study’s consent terms and the lack of need for additional patient involvement.

Prior to this data analysis, we performed a priori sample size calculation using G*Power 3.1. Based on a multiple linear regression model (fixed model, R² increase) with an effect size f²=0.15, significance level (α)=0.10 and desired power (1-β)=0.90, the analysis indicated a minimum required sample size of 141 participants. Ultimately, 149 patients were enrolled. A post hoc power analysis was subsequently conducted with G*Power 3.1 using the actual sample size (n=149), yielding α=0.10, f²=0.15 and β=0.92. Written consent was obtained from the patients or their proxies. Inclusion criteria for this study were patients aged 18 or older who were intubated and expected to require mechanical ventilation for at least 24 hours. Patients already enrolled in another trial, those who had undergone mechanical ventilation for over 24 hours at the time of eligibility screening, those with a prior tracheostomy on ICU admission or those with a history of two or more tracheal intubations were excluded from the study.

PPV protocol

PPV is indicated for patients with ARDS meeting the following criteria13 14: (1) moderate-to-severe ARDS: defined by a ratio of arterial partial pressure of oxygen to fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ≤150 mmHg; (2) persistent hypoxaemia: despite 12–24 hours of optimised mechanical ventilation settings, including positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) titration; (3) absence of absolute contraindications such as unstable spinal injury or uncontrolled intracranial hypertension.10 15

The manoeuvre was performed by a team of five trained staff: one dedicated to airway/line protection and four coordinating patient turning. Positioning involved free suspension of the chest and abdomen (avoiding supports under the pelvis or thorax to prevent compression), head rotation to one side using a specialised pressure-relief cushion and arms placed in a ‘swimmer’s position’ or alongside the body. Securement of endotracheal tubes, vascular catheters and other important cannulas was reinforced, with enteral nutrition held during turning.10 16 17 Arterial blood gases, respiratory mechanics (plateau pressure, driving pressure) and haemodynamics were monitored before and after prone positioning. Ventilator settings adhered to a lung-protective strategy (tidal volume ≤6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure <30 cmH2O).18 FiO2 was titrated downwards based on oxygenation response after proning, while PEEP was individualised using oxygenation and respiratory mechanics data. Daily PPV sessions lasted ≥16 hours and were discontinued if (a) supine PaO2/FiO2 >150 mmHg measured under conditions of FiO2 ≤0.6 and PEEP ≤10 cmH2O or (b) life-threatening complications occurred (eg, tube dislodgement).10 15

Treatment protocol

All patients enrolled in this study who required endotracheal intubation received routine intensive care. The diagnoses of septic shock were established at baseline according to the new Third International Consensus Definitions (Sepsis-3.0) criteria and recorded as categorical variables.19 Pcuff was measured using a manual manometer every 4 hours, and Pcuff management was continued until the cessation of mechanical ventilation or death. The target Pcuff range was maintained between 25 and 30 cmH2O.

Fibreoptic bronchoscopy protocol

All patients included in the study underwent fibreoptic bronchoscopy at the time of extubation to assess for tracheal injuries. Prior to extubation, 3 mL of 2% lidocaine was atomised through the tracheal tube, and oral topical anaesthesia with 2% lidocaine was administered. The fibreoptic bronchoscope was lubricated with tetracaine hydrochloride gel, and intravenous propofol (1–2 mg/kg) was administered to achieve sedation. After aligning the disinfected tip of the fibreoptic bronchoscope with the tip of the tracheal tube, ensuring they were at the same level, the tracheal tube securing device was removed. Following cuff deflation, the fibreoptic bronchoscope and tracheal tube were simultaneously withdrawn by two whole markings, equivalent to a length of 4 cm. Subsequently, the fibreoptic bronchoscope was inserted approximately 0.5 cm to inspect the tracheal mucosa within its field of view. The endotracheal tube and fibreoptic bronchoscope were then removed on completion of the evaluation. During the examination, the propofol dose was adjusted according to the patient’s sedation state to ensure comfort and minimise coughing. Patients requiring percutaneous tracheotomies also underwent fibreoptic bronchoscopy. However, patients who died before extubation did not undergo fibreoptic bronchoscopy, and post-mortem assessment of tracheal ischaemia was not conducted for these patients.

Definition of tracheal injured lesions

The tracheal injury score (range 0–10) was the primary outcome, evaluated using the scoring system in online supplemental table S1.20 Four blinded respiratory therapists independently performed and interpreted fibreoptic bronchoscopy at extubation.

Covariates

Patient characteristics at ICU admission were prospectively recorded and include the following: age, sex, medication history, alcohol and smoking status, body mass index (BMI) and chronic diseases including hypertension, diabetes, immunosuppression, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, dyspnoea and chronic renal failure. The causes for ICU admission were also documented. During the ICU stay, data were collected on the tracheal tube size, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II scores, temperature, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, platelet count, prothrombin time, international normalised ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, oxygenation index (OI), lactic acid, albumin, globulin, prealbumin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, urea nitrogen, creatinine, procalcitonin, microbiological examination, specimen origin for microbiological examination, dosage of norepinephrine, dosage of plasma, red blood cell transfusion, duration of intubation, mechanical ventilation time, total norepinephrine, total epinephrine and length of ICU stay (ICU LOS).

Follow-up procedure

We conducted follow-up through telephone inquiries, with a cut-off date of 30November 2023 for participant follow-up. Telephone follow-ups were conducted 1 month after discharge, during which any difficulty breathing, shortness of breath or chest tightness experienced by participants was recorded as monitoring indicators.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was performed a priori using G*Power 3.1 for the primary analysis (PPV association with tracheal injury score in the full cohort, n=149). Based on multiple linear regression (effect size f²=0.15, α=0.10, power=0.90), 141 participants were required. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for determining whether variables were normally distributed. All normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD, and skewed continuous variables were described as median (IQR. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%). Comparison of continuous variables among groups was performed with the use of the independent samples Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test depending on the normality of the distribution, and categorical data were compared by X2 or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. The effect of PPV on tracheal injury score was evaluated using linear regression models (regression coefficients β and 95% CI with adjustment for major covariables. The selection of confounding factors was based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical relevance, existing literature and statistically significant results (p<0.05) from univariate analyses. To control for confounding effects, a bidirectional change-in-estimate approach was employed: covariates were sequentially added to a base model containing only PPV while simultaneously removed one by one from a full-variable model. Variables causing >10% change in PPV’s β-coefficient were retained, with multicollinearity controlled by restricting variance inflation factors to <2.0 (online supplemental Stable 3). Ultimately, 13 variables prespecified based on biological plausibility and literature support were included: age, sex, medication history, smoking, BMI, APACHE II score, tracheal tube size, septic shock, OI, duration of intubation, PPV, total norepinephrine and total epinephrine. So we constructed three models: Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for age, sex, BMI, APACHE II score, septic shock, OI and duration of intubation. Model 3 additionally adjusted for Medication history, smoking, tracheal tube size, PPV, total norepinephrine and total epinephrine. Moreover, to avoid model overfitting, we constructed additional models (detailed in online supplemental Stable 4), and the results remained robust. Additionally, because age, sex and duration of intubation were important confounding factors, stratified analyses were conducted to compare the relationship between PPV and the tracheal injury score across different age groups, sex and duration of intubation groups. Age was categorised based on clinical cut-off points, while the duration of intubation was categorised based on the median. An interaction test was then performed. The subgroup analyses were conducted by subgroup variables. Interaction across subgroups was tested using the likelihood ratio test. We repeated all analyses with the complete data cohort for comparison. We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the findings of the study and how our conclusions can be affected by applying various association inference models. The calculated effect sizes and p values from all these models were reported and compared. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics analysis platform (Version 1.9, Beijing, China, http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Since the Free Statistics platform automatically excludes observations with missing data during model building and given the minimal missingness (0–4%) in our dataset validated by Little’s Missing Completely At Random test confirming completely random missingness (χ²=166.078, df=218, p=0.996), we refrained from data imputation. The specific details of missing data for each variable are shown in online supplemental Stable 5. Models were developed after excluding observations with missing values in the covariates: OI, total norepinephrine and total epinephrine. The final sample size included in the analytical models was 143. For robustness verification, sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) complete-case modelling via systematic deletion of observations with missing covariates (online supplemental Stable 6) and (2) re-analysis after mean imputation of missing covariates (online supplemental Stable 7).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design of the study or the dissemination of the findings.

Results

Baseline characteristics of selected participants

Out of the 169 patients, 20 (11.8%) were excluded from the study. Among them, 10 patients were excluded because they or their family members refused fibreoptic bronchoscopy; five patients passed away before undergoing bronchoscopy and could not have their tracheal mucosal injury score assessed, while another five patients were excluded because they required re-intubation. The data analysis was performed on 149 participants. A flow chart illustrating the study’s selection process is presented in figure 1. We grouped the enrolled patients according to whether the tracheal injury score was greater than 5 and listed the baseline characteristics of these patients in table 1. The results indicate that the two groups are similar in age and sex distribution, with no significant differences. The distribution of medication history, alcohol consumption, smoking, BMI, presence of chronic diseases, reasons for ICU admission and APACHE II scores is also comparable between the two groups, with no significant differences. Table 2 presents a comparative analysis of ICU patients undergoing mechanical ventilation with and without PPV, highlighting significant disparities in platelet counts, lactic acid levels and intubation duration. Notably, PPV associates with extended ventilation times and higher APACHE II scores, suggesting greater disease severity. Additionally, the distribution of Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli varies significantly between groups.

Linear regression analyses

Linear regression analysis was chosen based on the correlation matrix plots between different covariates and tracheal injury score. The results of univariate regression analysis (online supplemental Stable 2) indicate that age, sex, medication history, smoking, BMI, tracheal tube size, coexisting chronic diseases, APACHE II score, OI, dosage of norepinephrine, total norepinephrine and total epinephrine were not significantly associated with the tracheal injury score. However, concomitant septic shock, duration of intubation and PPV were found to be positively correlated with the tracheal injury score. To further investigate the independent effects of PPV on the tracheal injury score, we constructed three models using multivariate linear regression analysis. Due to two missing values in OI and four missing values in total norepinephrine and total epinephrine, the final sample size included in Model 2 was 147, while Model 3 included 143 samples. The effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals are reported in table 3. The results showed that in the crude analysis (Model 1), there was a statistically significant positive association between PPV and the tracheal injury score (β: 6.51; 95% CI 5.16 to 7.87), suggesting that patients with PPV had higher tracheal injury scores. This association remained significant in the multivariable analysis (Model 3) (β: 5.33; 95% CI 3.84 to 6.82), after adjusting for age, sex, medication history, smoking, BMI, APACHE II score, tracheal tube size, septic shock, OI, duration of intubation, PPV, total norepinephrine and total epinephrine. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant positive association between sepsis shock and the tracheal injury score in Model 3 (β: 2.80; 95% CI 1.35 to 4.25). At last, there was a statistically significant positive association between the duration of intubation and the tracheal injury score in Model 3 (β: 0.23; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.39), suggesting that for each additional day of endotracheal intubation, the tracheal injury score increased by 0.23 points.

Discussion

Our study’s findings align with previous research,20 confirming the prevalence of tracheal lesions among patients who undergo intubation. This reaffirms the critical need for diligent monitoring and preventive strategies to reduce intubation-related complications. Our research offers valuable insights into how PPV and the duration of intubation are associated with tracheal injury in critically ill patients. To our knowledge, this is among the few studies to specifically examine the link between PPV and tracheal injury in this patient population. The observed positive association between PPV and tracheal injury score, persisting after multivariable adjustment, suggests PPV may be linked to greater injury severity. Additionally, we found a positive association between the duration of intubation, the presence of septic shock and the extent of tracheal injury score.

PPV is acknowledged as a critical intervention associated with significantly reduced mortality rates in patients with ARDS undergoing mechanical ventilation,21 as recent studies have shown. Importantly, tracheal cuff overinflation, defined as pressures exceeding 30 cmH2O, is a major risk factor for ischaemic tracheal lesions.22 These lesions have been observed across various cuff pressure levels. The study by Lizy et al meticulously assessed how changes in body position affect endotracheal tube cuff pressure in mechanically ventilated patients,23 revealing that minor positional adjustments can lead to substantial deviations from the targeted cuff pressure range, with 40.6% of measurements surpassing the upper threshold. Moreover, a recent large-scale prospective observational study confirmed these findings, indicating that various body position changes cause significant fluctuations in endotracheal tube cuff pressure.24 However, similar to previous studies, it did not address the specific impact of PPV on cuff pressure changes. Therefore, our findings highlight an association between PPV and tracheal injury score, which could reflect increased tracheal cuff pressure during PPV, potentially contributing to ischaemia. It is emphasised that when performing PPV, it is essential not only to prevent complications associated with PPV, such as pressure ulcers, neuromuscular injuries, haemodynamic instability and accidental extubations or tubing dislodgement,10 25 but also to monitor the impact of changes in endotracheal tube cuff pressure on tracheal injury after the patient is placed in the prone position. However, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this correlation require further investigation.

We also found that septic shock also showed a positive association with tracheal injury scores, with patients who had septic shock and underwent PPV exhibiting higher tracheal injury scores. The possible mechanism could involve inflammation caused by the presence of the endotracheal tube, along with repetitive ischaemia reperfusion.26 Further research is needed to validate our findings and explore the detailed relationship and potential mechanisms.

Notably, the observed association between PPV and tracheal injury score remained consistent across subgroups defined by age, sex, septic shock status and duration of intubation. According to the subgroup analysis results, when the duration of intubation was less than 8 days, the mean difference in tracheal injury score was 7.67 (4.79 to 10.55), and when the duration was 8 days or more, the mean difference in tracheal injury score was 4.50 (2.31 to 6.69). Therefore, further research was needed to clarify the interaction between the duration of intubation and PPV and their relationship with tracheal injury score.

This study provides novel insights by examining the relationship between PPV and tracheal injury score. As a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort with rigorous adjustment for confounders, our approach strengthens the evidence for this association. The baseline data completeness enabled robust sensitivity analyses, enhancing confidence in our primary findings. However, our study has limitations. First and foremost, its single-centre design inherently limits the generalisability of our findings. While this facilitated consistent data collection and protocol application, patient demographics, specific clinical practices and local factors at our centre may not be representative of other institutions or broader populations. However, our consecutively enrolled cohort of 149 critically ill patients demonstrates clinically relevant internal representativeness: baseline characteristics (age 58.4±16.2 years, 65.8% male, BMI 25.0±3.6 kg/m², hypertension 36.2%) align with typical ICU populations in similar academic settings, and the majority of admission diagnoses (septic shock, 31.5%; neurosurgery, 44.3%) reflect common critical care pathologies. Importantly, the PPV and non-PPV groups showed balanced demographics, comorbidities and vital signs, supporting internal validity—though the PPV group had higher disease severity (APACHE II 22.5±6.8 vs 19.0±6.4, p=0.004), prolonged mechanical ventilation (367 vs 210 hours, p<0.001) and enriched multidrug-resistant pathogens (93% microbiological positivity vs 61.3%, p<0.001). Thus, while our sample robustly represents complex tertiary ICU cases, its generalizability requires caution and must be validated in multicenter studies. Multicentre validation remains essential for broader generalisability. Second, as an observational study, the associations we observed cannot establish causality and may be influenced by unmeasured confounders. Although we attempted to adjust for all relevant potential confounders in the multivariate model, residual confounding from unmeasured or unknown factors remains possible and could lead to an overestimation of the observed associations. There may be some unmeasured or unknown residual confounders that could lead to an overestimation of the observed associations. Additionally, the use of specific classifications in subgroup analyses resulted in smaller sample sizes, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions from those subgroups. Despite these limitations, our study provides a thorough investigation of the association between PPV and tracheal injury score outcomes, offering valuable further evidence on this topic. Future multi-centre studies are warranted to validate and extend these results.

Conclusions

This study identifies an association between PPV and higher tracheal injury scores, independent of confounders. These results suggest PPV is associated with increased tracheal injury risk. Clinicians should monitor airway status carefully during PPV, particularly cuff pressure changes in prone positioning. While confounding remains possible, our findings highlight a clinically important relationship warranting further investigation. We hope these insights help identify ICU patients at higher risk for tracheal injury.
