Machine learning guided resolution of mechanical trade-off in polymer composites via stress adaptive interface
Authors: Hao Wang, Ji Cheng, Zhangyu Wu, Xianfeng Chen, Siqi Liu, Deyu Niu, Jie Zhang, Kai Jin, Chaobin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing polymer composites that simultaneously achieve high strength, toughness, and impact resistance remains a fundamental challenge due to inherent trade-offs and brittle interfacial failure. Here, we propose a universal toughening strategy that integrates a bone-inspired trabecular interlock architecture with a thermodynamically driven, stress-adaptive interface to enable efficient energy dissipation under mechanical loading. To address multi-objective optimization in composites design, we further develop a data-driven framework combining Pareto Set Learning and Active Learning, which systematically explores the composition–performance landscape to identify balanced, high-performance formulations. The optimized composites exhibit synergistic mechanical properties: strength up to 250 MPa, fracture toughness exceeding 14 MPa·m1/2, and impact resistance of nearly 4.8 J, surpassing most bioinspired and engineered polymer counterparts. The strategy is scalable, chemically versatile, and broadly applicable, offering a programmable route to next-generation lightweight composites for aerospace, transportation, and protection.

Introduction

Achieving synergistic enhancement of strength, toughness, and impact resistance remains a fundamental challenge in structural polymer composites, primarily due to intrinsic trade-offs and interfacial failure under complex loading conditions1–3. While conventional approaches4–12, such as incorporating stiff fibers, nanofillers, or layered architectures, can improve individual properties, they often induce brittle zones or interfacial mismatches that degrade overall mechanical performance.

Nature offers an alternative paradigm where hierarchical architectures and adaptive interfaces synergistically dissipate energy across multiple scales. Although bioinspired designs mimicking bone, nacre, and other natural systems have emerged13–18, most rely on static interfacial reinforcements, including micro-architectured interlocking19, surface functionalized fillers20, polymer grafting21, supramolecular/covalent/ionic bonding22,23, and nanostructured coatings24. Critically, such chemically constrained interfaces lack dynamic stress responsiveness and broad materials applicability.

Herein, we propose a universal toughening strategy that integrates a bone-inspired trabecular interlock architecture with a thermodynamically driven, stress-adaptive interface. This multiscale design enables dynamic energy dissipation, effectively overcoming the classic strength–toughness trade-off. To address multi-objective optimization challenges, we further develop a machine learning (ML) framework combining Pareto Set Learning (PSL) and Active Learning (AL). This data-driven approach efficiently explores the composition–performance landscape to identify balanced, high-performance formulations. Demonstrated across diverse commercial matrices (Polyketone, Polyamide 6) and reinforcements (1D fibers to 2D nanosheets), our strategy establishes a programmable pathway for advanced composite design. Beyond exceptional properties, this work advances mechanistic understanding of architecture–interface–property relationships and provides a generalizable platform for accelerated materials discovery.

Results

Biomimetic trabecular architecture with thermodynamically adaptive interfaces

Inspired by the hierarchical porosity and mechanical efficiency of natural trabecular bone1,2, we establish a biomimetic composite design paradigm governed by thermodynamic principles (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS)25,26. Central to this strategy is the reduction of interfacial enthalpy (ΔH) during the thermal blending, which lowers the energetic barrier for polymer chain interdiffusion and promotes the formation of stable physical interlocks at soft–hard interfaces. This concept is realized in the trabecular interlocked composites (TICs), characterized by aligned reinforcing phases embedded within a robust three-dimensional porous skeleton (Fig. 1b). The stress-adaptive interfaces in these composites rely on dynamic diffusion and entanglement of polymer chains with rigid constituents under thermal activation, thus transitioning from traditional static bonding toward a thermodynamically governed entanglement strategy.Fig. 1Bioinspired trabecular interlock architecture with stress-adaptive interfacial design.a Hierarchical porous architecture of natural trabecular bone serving as the biological prototype. b Proposed composite strategy: a continuous 3D trabecular scaffold interlocks aligned reinforcements through thermodynamically driven soft-hard interfaces, enabling stress-adaptive entanglement.

a Hierarchical porous architecture of natural trabecular bone serving as the biological prototype. b Proposed composite strategy: a continuous 3D trabecular scaffold interlocks aligned reinforcements through thermodynamically driven soft-hard interfaces, enabling stress-adaptive entanglement.

The effectiveness of this approach is experimentally validated in a ternary system, where thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) functions as the entanglement promoter, poly(phenylene ether ketone) (POK) serves as the rigid matrix, and aligned basalt fibers (BF) provide structural reinforcement. Analogous to natural bone structures, TPU forms collagen-like entangled networks, while the POK phase provides structural rigidity, and the shear-aligned BF creates the trabecular scaffold. The hierarchical porous architecture is fabricated via gas-assisted pore formation under controlled thermo-shear conditions. Upon thermal activation, TPU chains become highly mobile and gradually diffuse into the POK matrix and along the BF interfaces. This interdiffusion lowers the interfacial enthalpy (ΔH), thereby reducing the energetic barrier for physical entanglement formation. The resulting dynamic, entangled interfaces enhance compatibility and mechanical integrity. These phenomena are substantiated by thermal analyses, structural characterizations (Figure S5), and molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. S6 and Supplementary Note 2).

Machine learning (ML) driven multi-objective optimization

While the feasibility of the trabecular interlock architecture and thermodynamically driven interfacial strategy has been established, the compositional design space for these polymer composites remains extensive and intrinsically high-dimensional. Traditionally, optimized polymer composite formulations are developed through empirical trial-and-error methods27,28, which involve adjusting one variable at a time (e.g., x1 in Fig. 2a) while keeping others constant. Performance metrics such as f(x1) improve gradually through repeated cycles of formulation, testing, and adjustment. To address the intrinsic trade-offs among strength, toughness, and impact resistance, we introduce a machine learning (ML)–assisted multi-objective optimization framework (Fig. 2b). This data-driven approach systematically explores the composition–performance landscape, identifies approximate Pareto-optimal solutions, and resolves competing performance objectives. Consequently, the framework significantly accelerates the discovery and refinement of high-performance composite formulations.

Pareto set learning (PSL)

An initial baseline dataset is established using a design-of-experiments (DoE) strategy by selecting 50 representative composite formulations spanning the feasible composition space. Each formulation is experimentally fabricated and mechanically characterized to measure tensile strength, fracture toughness, and impact energy dissipation. These data are used to train three independent Gaussian process regression (GPR) surrogate models.

As the training set size increases from 30 to 50, cross-validated predictive performance improves systematically for all three targets, as evidenced by increasing coefficients of determination (R2), decreasing mean absolute errors (MAE), and decreasing mean squared errors (MSE), together with a concurrent reduction in predictive uncertainty quantified by the standard deviation across validation folds (Supplementary Note 3.1 and Fig. S7). Although toughness-related properties exhibit comparatively higher uncertainty at smaller sample sizes, all surrogate models attain statistically reliable accuracy at 50 samples, thereby establishing a robust foundation for subsequent multi-objective optimization.

Building on the validated GPR surrogates, multi-objective optimization is performed using Pareto set learning (PSL). PSL learns a mapping from preference vectors λ, which encode user-defined trade-offs among competing objectives, to optimized formulations x by scalarizing the multi-objective problem into a tractable single-objective form29. By sampling 1000 preference vectors, the trained PSL model generates a diverse and structured set of Pareto-consistent candidate solutions spanning a broad range of engineering priorities (Supplementary Note 3.2 and Fig. S8).

Active learning (AL)-guided optimization

To further improve optimization efficiency and solution quality, we integrate active learning (AL) with multi-objective Bayesian optimization to iteratively refine the learned Pareto set and guide exploration of the trabecular interlocked composites (TICs) design space30,31. Specifically, we adopt an acquisition strategy that combines a lower confidence bound (LCB) criterion with hypervolume improvement (HVI) to select small batches of informative and non-dominated candidates from the PSL-generated solution pool for experimental validation (Supplementary Note 3.3). This uncertainty-aware strategy balances exploitation of high-performing regions with exploration of poorly sampled areas, enabling targeted expansion of the experimentally validated performance envelope under a fixed experimental budget.

Starting from the baseline dataset (Fig. 3a), the experimentally sampled formulations in the strength–fracture toughness space follow an approximately monotonic trend, with impact energy dissipation co-varying with the high-performance region. However, the initial data are concentrated around intermediate strength (≈220–235 MPa) and fracture toughness (≈12–13.5 MPa·m1/2), leaving the extreme high-performance boundary sparsely sampled. With successive AL iterations (Figs. 3b and S9), newly selected formulations progressively populate the upper-right region of the design space, corresponding to tensile strengths exceeding ~245 MPa and fracture toughness values above ~14.5 MPa m1/2, while maintaining high impact energy dissipation (>4.6 J). The efficiency of this frontier expansion is quantified by hypervolume (HV) analysis. More than 90% of the total normalized HV gain is achieved within the first ten additional experiments, driven by a dominant early HV increase (ΔHV ≈ 0.012), followed by rapid saturation with marginal gains below 0.001 per iteration. Across five AL iterations, learning-curve analysis further reveals systematic improvements in cross-validated predictive accuracy and progressive stabilization of the surrogate models, particularly for fracture toughness and impact energy dissipation (Fig. S10). Independent hold-out testing confirms robust out-of-sample generalization for all three properties, with no evidence of systematic prediction bias (Fig. S11).

Finally, benchmarking against a classical response surface method (RSM) under identical experimental budgets (50 initial formulations plus 25 additional trials) demonstrates that the combined PSL + AL framework consistently drives the Pareto front toward higher-performance regimes and achieves over 90% of its terminal normalized hypervolume improvement within the first ten additional experiments. In contrast, RSM yields only marginal performance gains even after exhausting the full experimental budget (Fig. S12). These results establish that uncertainty-aware, iterative learning enables substantially higher sample efficiency and reliability than conventional quadratic design-of-experiments baselines.

Preference-guided solution extraction

After completion of five active learning iterations (25 additional experiments), the PSL model is retrained using the expanded dataset, yielding the optimized Pareto set shown in Fig. 3d–f. The resulting Pareto-consistent solutions exhibit a pronounced and organized structure across both composition and performance spaces. In the TPU–BF–POK composition space (Fig. 3d), the solutions are confined to a relatively narrow compositional window rather than being dispersed across the full admissible domain, revealing a well-defined high-performance basin.

A consistent structural organization is observed in the three-dimensional performance space (Fig. 3e), where the Pareto-consistent solutions form a smooth, manifold-like trajectory instead of a diffuse cloud. This behavior indicates that the achievable multi-objective trade-offs are constrained by a common structural regime, within which improvements in tensile strength are accompanied by predictable and coupled variations in fracture toughness and impact energy dissipation, rather than arbitrary performance fluctuations.

This structured Pareto manifold enables preference-guided engineering decision making (Fig. 3f and Table S2). By specifying target preference vectors, representative solutions corresponding to balanced, strength-prioritized, fracture-toughness–prioritized, and impact-energy–prioritized design modes can be directly extracted along the learned manifold. The close agreement between the prescribed preference vectors and their matched counterparts confirms that PSL preserves preference information during solution generation, allowing explicit design priorities to be translated into concrete compositions and predicted performance outcomes.

These results demonstrate that the combined AL–PSL framework not only accelerates the discovery of high-performance Pareto fronts but also constructs a mechanistically admissible and queryable design manifold, enabling practical and transparent composite formulation design under competing performance objectives.

Quantitatively, the absolute properties cluster tightly. Tensile strength spans approximately 248–250 MPa (maximum deviation ~1.2 MPa relative to the balanced formulation, <0.5%, Fig. 4d, g), and fracture toughness remains near 14.8–14.9 MPa m1/2 (differences ~0.02 MPa m1/2, Fig. 4e, h), both within experimental scatter. Impact energy dissipation is similarly concentrated (~ 4.65–4.71 J, Fig. 4f, i), with the impact-first design exhibiting a small but reproducible increase (~ 0.06 J, ~1%) relative to the balanced reference. Expressed as relative differences with respect to the balanced formulation, the shifts remain modest yet directionally consistent with the prescribed preferences, supporting that preference guidance primarily refines trade-off ordering within an already optimized performance envelope.

To contextualize these validated formulations within a broader materials landscape, we benchmark their mass-normalized performance against representative natural and engineered materials using Ashby-type maps (Fig. 4j–l; Tables S3 and S4). All four designs occupy a narrow yet high-performance region across the examined property spaces, consistent with convergence toward a Pareto-optimal plateau governed by a shared deformation and energy-dissipation mechanism. In the specific strength–specific fracture toughness map (Fig. 4j), the optimized TICs fall within the advanced composite and bone-like regime, positioned well above conventional polymers and non-technical ceramics. When density is explicitly considered (Fig. 4k), all formulations remain in a lightweight regime (<1000 kg/m3) while maintaining fracture toughness on the order of 10–20 MPa m1/2, highlighting a rare combination of low mass and damage tolerance. A similar clustering is observed in the specific impact force–specific energy dissipation map (Fig. 4l), where the TICs exhibit concurrent enhancement of impact resistance and energy absorption, comparable to or exceeding reported Bouligand and gradient composite architectures.

The close grouping of the four designs across these Ashby maps therefore reflects convergence toward a Pareto-optimal performance plateau governed by a shared deformation and energy-dissipation mechanism, rather than experimental scatter. This convergence underscores the robustness of the preference-guided optimization and provides a reliable basis for Ashby-map–guided materials comparison and selection.

Mechanism analysis

Dynamic stress-adaptive interfacial response

The tight clustering of validated properties and the plateau-like distribution on Ashby maps suggest that the optimized designs share a common, stable dissipation mechanism. We therefore interrogate the dynamic interfacial response of a representative TIC formulation (balanced preference) using in situ tensile SEM and strain-resolved Raman spectroscopy, complemented by impact/ballistic observations. This formulation sustains tensile strains up to ~600% without catastrophic failure (Fig. S13). In drop-weight impact tests, high-speed imaging captures rapid deformation followed by rebound (Fig. S14a and Movie S1), and post-impact inspection shows only shallow indentation without macroscopic cracking. By contrast, control specimens lacking the trabecular-interlocked architecture and adaptive interface design (POK–BF NAFCs) exhibit immediate penetration under identical conditions (Fig. S14b). Under ballistic loading (80 m/s), TICs decelerate the projectile and rebound without perforation (Figure S15a), whereas NAFCs show complete penetration (Fig. S15b and Movie S2). These macroscopic observations motivate the following in situ analyses aimed at resolving how stress is redistributed and dissipated at the crack and interfacial scales.

In situ tensile SEM directly visualizes the crack evolution and load-transfer mechanisms operating in TICs. At an applied strain of ~300%, crack opening is effectively shielded by pronounced fiber-mediated bridging, where multiple polymer ligaments anchored on basalt fibers span the crack faces and remain mechanically engaged (Fig. 5a, yellow arrows). Instead of propagating along a straight path, the crack follows a tortuous and deflected trajectory, indicating crack deflection induced by interfacial heterogeneity and mechanical interlocking. Upon further loading to ~500% strain (Fig. 5b and Movie S3), the bridging mechanism remains operative. The load-bearing ligaments undergo progressive stretching and thinning with delayed rupture, rather than abrupt interfacial debonding or clean fiber pull-out. Crack advance remains gradual and continues along a deflected path, accompanied by a pronounced expansion of the surrounding plastic zone (Fig. 5b, blue arrows). These observations demonstrate that the TICs interface does not fail through brittle separation. We next quantify molecular-scale stress accommodation using strain-resolved tensile Raman spectroscopy. The stacked spectra (Fig. 5c) reveal a continuous evolution of the full Raman window with increasing strain. Consistently, the strain–wavenumber heatmap (Fig. 5d) highlights two distinct response regimes: the carbonyl window (1700–1785 cm−1) exhibits strain-dependent but spatially diffuse intensity redistribution, whereas the CH-stretch window (2800–3100 cm−1) progressively develops increased spectral dispersion at higher strain, indicative of enhanced conformational heterogeneity. To quantify the response of polar groups, we track the centroid position of the carbonyl band (Fig. 5e). In TICs, the centroid shift remains relatively small and non-monotonic across the applied strain range, indicating that tensile stress does not continuously accumulate on a narrow subset of carbonyl bonds. Instead, stress is dynamically repartitioned as the physically entangled interface undergoes progressive reconfiguration under load. In contrast, the POK–TPU control (Fig. S17) exhibits a more systematic and monotonic centroid evolution, consistent with a more homogeneous polymer response in which bond-level stress localization can be directly tracked spectroscopically. Chain-level dissipation is further captured by the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the CH-stretch envelope (Fig. 5f). TICs display a pronounced broadening of the CH envelope at high strain, consistent with the activation of segmental mobility, chain rearrangement, and sliding. By comparison, the control sample shows a substantially weaker broadening response, indicating more limited conformational freedom under tensile loading.

These molecular-scale signatures are fully consistent with the in situ tensile SEM observations of sustained fiber-bridging ligaments and an expanded plastic zone at the crack. Together, the spectroscopic and microstructural evidence demonstrates that the trabecular interlocking architecture enables a dynamically activated, stress-adaptive interfacial response, which progressively converts tensile loading into frictional and entanglement-mediated dissipation pathways. This multiscale stress redistribution mechanism underpins the enhanced crack resistance and impact tolerance observed in TICs. At the molecular level, the stress-adaptive interface originates from thermodynamically driven interdiffusion of flexible elastomer chains into the amorphous regions of the rigid polymer matrix and onto the polar fiber surface during thermal processing. This process generates a physically entangled interphase rather than permanent chemical bonding. Under mechanical loading, the entangled interphase undergoes strain-dependent reconfiguration through chain sliding and rearrangement, enabling continuous stress redistribution and adaptive energy dissipation.

Strain-induced structural evolution across length scales

To contextualize the dynamic interfacial responses revealed above within a broader structural framework, we further examine the strain-induced structural evolution of the balanced POK–TPU–BF TICs across multiple length scales. Cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 6a) reveal a hierarchical interfacial architecture underlying the exceptional damage tolerance of the balanced POK-TPU-BF TICs. From low to high magnification (100 μm to 5 μm), multiscale features, including fiber bridging, crack deflection, interfacial tearing, and polymer fibrillation, are observed. At higher resolution, elongated polymer ligaments around the BF and deformed matrix zones indicate the formation of a physically entangled interface. Unlike classical fiber pull-out, the interface maintains continuous contact between BF and the surrounding TPU-POK matrix, without evidence of brittle decohesion. The aligned orientation of BF (yellow arrows) further supports efficient stress transfer before chain disentanglement and relaxation. At the initial stage (0% strain), 2D SAXS and WAXS patterns display nearly circular, isotropic scattering rings (Fig. 6b, c), reflecting a uniform distribution of oriented nanodomains and chain segments without macroscopic alignment. XRD analysis reveals sharp peaks at 2θ ≈ 20.6° and 29.8°, corresponding to crystalline POK reflections, indicating intact semicrystalline lamellae (Fig. S18). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations confirm a dense, interpenetrated interfacial network, where TPU chains form physically entangled bridges across the fiber-matrix boundary (Fig. 6f, left). Thermal analysis shows a sharp melting peak at 229.1 °C and high enthalpy, consistent with well-preserved crystalline domains (Fig. 6g).

Upon moderate deformation (50% strain), TICs exhibit a marked stiffening response, as indicated by the steep increase in the tensile stress-strain curve up to approximately 100% strain (Fig. 4a). SAXS patterns transition from isotropic rings to pronounced anisotropic streaks (Fig. 6b), while WAXS profiles develop well-defined equatorial arcs (Fig. 6c), evidencing progressive alignment of amorphous and crystalline phases along the loading axis. Concurrently, XRD peaks broaden and intensify (Fig. S18), reflecting enhanced short-range order due to chain alignment. MD simulations (Fig. 6f, middle) further support the formation of this partially ordered state, showing coordinated chain disentanglement without interfacial failure. The decrease in melting enthalpy observed in thermal analysis (Fig. 6g) indicates partial disruption of crystalline lamellae, likely resulting from interfacial stress and deformation-induced reorganization.

At higher deformation levels (300% strain), the stress-strain curve enters a plateau with a slight upward trend (Fig. 6a), marking a transition from structural reinforcement to energy dissipation. The reduction in SAXS anisotropy (Fig. 6b) and the emergence of diffuse WAXS rings (Fig. 6c) indicate the breakdown of pre-oriented domains and a loss of microstructural coherence. Increased SAXS intensity at low q-values (Fig. 6d) reflects nanoscale reorganization and interfacial expansion, consistent with the development of a more compliant, dissipative interface. WAXS peaks broaden and diminish in intensity (Fig. 6e), while XRD profiles display flattened peaks and elevated baseline scattering (Fig. S18), evidencing the collapse of long-range crystalline order. DSC analysis reveals a partial increase in melting enthalpy, suggesting local chain rearrangement and recrystallization upon stress release. MD simulations reveal strain-dependent interfacial transitions (Fig. 6f). Initially, TPU chains interdiffuse and form stable entanglements with POK at the BF interface. At 100% strain, chain disentanglement initiates, weakening interfacial coupling. By 300%, chains become disordered yet mobile, allowing dynamic rearrangement and stress dissipation. This strain-dependent evolution suggests an adaptive interfacial response in which the dominant role of the interface shifts from efficient load transfer toward enhanced energy dissipation through chain rearrangement and sliding. In contrast to chemically bonded interfaces that often fail through abrupt decohesion, the physically entangled interface accommodates large deformation through sustained chain mobility and interfacial continuity, thereby delaying catastrophic failure and contributing to the observed impact tolerance.

Hierarchical energy dissipation under impact loading

The balanced POK-TPU-BF TICs feature a hierarchical architecture that imparts outstanding resistance to both low- and high-velocity impacts. Three-dimensional reconstruction (Fig. 6h) reveals a continuous, interconnected porous matrix interwoven with aligned BF, forming a trabecular framework reminiscent of biological load-bearing structures. Cross-sectional SEM images show that BF traverse the porous matrix and are embedded within soft-phase ligaments, promoting both mechanical interlocking and localized deformation accommodation.

Under low-velocity impact (drop-weight testing), reversible pore collapse and matrix shear, together with energy dissipation at entangled interfaces via chain sliding and segmental rearrangement, enable distributed damping. This combination minimizes indentation and provides rebound resilience, consistent with viscoelastic recovery.

During high-speed impact (projectile penetration), alternative energy dissipation mechanisms prevail. Finite element (FE) simulations (Fig. 6i) show that strain localizes beneath the impact site, confined within the trabecular interface region. Local densification of the trabecular skeleton, fiber pull-out and bridging, and interfacial chain disentanglement work in concert to dissipate kinetic energy. These mechanisms effectively suppress crack propagation and prevent catastrophic failure. In contrast, non-architectured fiber composites (NAFCs, Fig. S19) develop extended strain bands and experience complete perforation under identical conditions, highlighting the improved damage localization and structural integrity of TICs.

These findings demonstrate that the TICs design synergistically integrates porous load-diffusing scaffolds, crack-arresting fiber networks, and stress-responsive entangled interfaces to achieve dual-mode impact tolerance. This multiscale energy dissipation strategy preserves mechanical integrity even at low densities, offering a generalizable approach for high-performance protective materials.

Versatility of the design strategy across diverse polymer composite systems

Building on the thermodynamic and structural foundation of the TICs concept, we demonstrate its universality across chemically and morphologically diverse polymer composites. The strategy extends to industrially relevant thermoplastics such as POK and polyamide 6 (PA6), reinforced with one-dimensional fibers (carbon fiber, CF; basalt fiber, BF) and two-dimensional graphene nanosheets (G), reflecting applications in aerospace, automotive, and protective materials. All combinations are compatible with standard processing methods.

For comparability, all TICs are fabricated using standardized formulations and processing conditions based on the balanced-performance baseline. Despite variations in matrix chemistry and reinforcement geometry, each system achieves concurrent improvements in strength, fracture toughness, and ductility. The composites use industrially compatible thermoplastic elastomers (TPU, SEBS) without chemical coupling agents or compatibilizers. As shown in Figs. 7a–c and S21, both POK-TPU-CF TICs and POK-SEBS-BF TICs display ductile deformation and enhanced mechanical properties, with strengths up to 250 MPa and fracture toughness approaching 15 MPa m1/2. The approach also applies to PA6-based systems, where introducing SEBS as a soft phase yields PA-SEBS-BF TICs with markedly improved toughness and ductility compared to conventional PA6 composites lacking interfacial design (Supplementary Note 6). The design strategy is further validated in 2D nanosheet-reinforced systems, the results are shown in Fig. 7d–f. The broad applicability of the TICs approach derives from its reliance on stress-adaptive interfaces and trabecular interlock architectures, governed by thermodynamic chain dynamics and topological constraints rather than specific chemical interactions. Overall, these findings establish TICs as a scalable, material-agnostic platform for developing lightweight composites with high mechanical resilience, enabling broad implementation across structural applications in aerospace, transportation, and defense.

Discussion

We present a universal toughening strategy for polymer composites by integrating a trabecular interlock architecture with thermally driven, stress-adaptive interfaces. This multiscale design activates a hierarchy of deformation mechanisms, including chain alignment, crystallinity modulation, interfacial sliding, and molecular reorganization. These coordinated processes enable efficient energy dissipation under complex loading conditions and overcome the traditional trade-off between strength and toughness. The approach is scalable, independent of specific fillers, and broadly applicable to diverse polymer systems.

To accelerate formulation development, we implement a machine learning-based multi-objective optimization framework that combines Pareto Set Learning with Active Learning. This data-driven strategy establishes an interpretable and efficient design space that links user-defined performance targets to optimal composite formulations. It systematically balances strength, fracture toughness, and impact resistance while reducing experimental workload.

Looking ahead, this integrated design and optimization strategy provides a foundation for intelligent materials discovery. It is compatible with inverse design workflows, additive manufacturing, and sustainable material inputs. The demonstrated applicability across thermoplastics, thermosets, and hybrid systems underscores its potential for advanced structural applications in aerospace, transportation, and protective technologies. Overall, this work delivers both a scalable physical design paradigm and a computational platform to accelerate innovation in high-performance polymer composites.

Methods

Materials

Poly(phenylene ether ketone) (POK, Mw ≈ 3.2 × 105) was used as the rigid thermoplastic matrix. Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU, Shore A ≈ 85) and styrene–ethylene–butylene–styrene (SEBS) were employed as soft phases to promote interfacial chain mobility. Basalt fibers (BF, average diameter ≈ 13 μm, length 1–2 cm) and carbon fibers (CF, diameter ≈ 7 μm) were used as one-dimensional reinforcements, while graphene nanosheets (average thickness ≈ 12 nm, lateral size ≈ 5 μm) served as two-dimensional fillers. Polyamide 6 (PA6) was used as an alternative matrix for universality validation. All materials were used as received. Detailed material specifications are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Composites fabrication

Trabecular interlock composites (TICs) were fabricated via a two-step melt-processing strategy designed to activate thermodynamically driven interfacial entanglement and induce a trabecular architecture. Prior to processing, all polymer components were dried at 80 °C for 12 h under vacuum to remove residual moisture. In the first step, the soft-phase polymer (TPU or SEBS) was melt-blended with the reinforcing phase (BF, CF, or graphene) in a twin-screw extruder at 230 °C, enabling thermal activation of flexible chains and their diffusion onto reinforcement surfaces. In the second step, the thermoplastic matrix (POK or PA6) was introduced and further melt-blended under controlled shear to induce fiber alignment and porous skeleton formation. The extrudates were drawn at a constant speed to promote shear-induced orientation and subsequently cooled to room temperature. For panel fabrication, extruded strips were stacked in a steel mold and hot-pressed at 230 °C under pressure to obtain composite sheets suitable for mechanical testing and structural characterization. Control composites without interfacial and architectural design (denoted as NAFCs) were prepared under identical thermal conditions but without the sequential soft-phase-assisted interfacial activation. Detailed processing parameters are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Structural and thermal characterization

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed using a TA Instruments Q2000 under nitrogen atmosphere. Samples were heated from 50 °C to 300 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1, and the second heating cycle was used for analysis. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 5°–40°. Small- and wide-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS/WAXS) experiments were carried out using a XEUSS SAXS/WAXS system to probe nanoscale structural evolution. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. Fracture morphologies and microstructural features were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and three-dimensional porous architectures were visualized by X-ray computed tomography. Detailed characterization procedures are described in Supplementary Note 1.

Mechanical testing

Uniaxial tensile tests were performed on an Instron universal testing machine at a constant crosshead speed of 3 mm min−1. All specimens were tested under identical gauge length and ambient conditions, with at least three specimens per formulation to ensure reproducibility. Fracture toughness was evaluated using single-edge-notched tension tests in accordance with ASTM D5045. Critical stress intensity factors were calculated following standard procedures. Low-velocity impact resistance was assessed using a drop-weight impact tester equipped with a hemispherical striker, while high-speed impact performance was evaluated using a gas-driven projectile system. Detailed test geometries and data reduction methods are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

In situ characterization

In situ tensile SEM experiments were conducted to visualize crack evolution and interfacial deformation during loading. Strain-resolved tensile Raman spectroscopy was employed to quantify molecular-scale stress redistribution, with spectra acquired continuously during deformation. Raman peak positions, centroid shifts, and FWHM values were extracted to analyze strain-dependent interfacial dynamics. Experimental details are provided in Supplementary Notes 4 and 5.

Machine learning workflow

A machine-learning-guided optimization framework combining Pareto set learning (PSL) and active learning (AL) was used to explore the multi-objective formulation space. The initial dataset consisted of experimentally measured formulations spanning controlled ranges of composition and processing parameters. Gaussian process regression models were trained to predict mechanical properties and associated uncertainties, which were subsequently used to guide iterative candidate selection. Equal experimental budgets were enforced for benchmarking against response surface methods. Full algorithmic details and code implementation are available in Supplementary Information and the associated Code Ocean capsule.

Simulation methods

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed to investigate interfacial chain entanglement and deformation mechanisms, while finite element simulations were used to analyze stress localization and impact-induced damage evolution in trabecular architectures. Simulation methodologies and parameters are detailed in Supplementary Notes 2 and 3.
