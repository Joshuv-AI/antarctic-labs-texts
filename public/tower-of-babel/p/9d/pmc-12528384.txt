The impact of spatial distribution of noise pollution from music recreation facilities and residents’ perceptions in Chongqing municipality
Authors: Jie Gao, Sheng Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid development of the night economy, the effects of noise pollution and the spatial differentiation characteristics of music leisure and entertainment facilities have become important issues in urban governance. Taking Chongqing city as an example, this study utilized AutoNavi POI data, noise complaint records, and field monitoring data to reveal the spatial distribution patterns and residents’ perceptions of noise pollution from music facilities. The study found that: (1) Music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing exhibit a “multi-core” decentralized pattern, differing from the “core solo diffusion” pattern observed in lowland cities. Due to topographic segmentation, Chongqing presents a unique “valley agglomeration-platform jumping” differentiation. The core areas (Guanyin Bridge, Jiefangbei, etc.) overlap with active night economy zones, while secondary nodes (such as university towns) show local agglomerations due to terrain constraints. (2) The distribution of music leisure and entertainment facilities is significantly correlated with noise complaint locations. The Pearson correlation coefficient peaks at a 250 m grid scale (r = 0.75), and the number of complaints increases nonlinearly when facility density exceeds 140 n/km², revealing the critical threshold effect of noise-carrying capacity in mountainous cities. (3) The innovative integration of citizen complaint data to construct a noise perception map verifies the feasibility of using public participation data for identifying noise hotspots. However, this approach is limited by the subjectivity of residents’ complaint behaviour and the spatial coverage bias. The results of this study provide guidance for urban acoustic environment zoning control strategies and offer insights for mountainous cities to balance commercial vitality with acoustic environment governance.Keywords: Music leisure facilities, Noise pollution, Spatio-temporal characteristics, Resident perceptionSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Environmental impact

Introduction

With the rapid development of the night economy and the urban cultural tourism industry, music leisure and entertainment facilities (such as bars, livehouses, and karaoke televisions (KTVs)) serve as key drivers of urban vitality. While these venues enrich the cultural life of residents, they also contribute to increasingly prominent noise pollution problems1,2. In densely populated urban core areas, high-intensity and low-frequency noise exhibits strong penetration and a wide transmission range, disrupting residents’ daily lives and potentially causing long-term negative effects on physical and mental health3–5. This issue is particularly pronounced in high-density mountainous cities such as Chongqing, where complex topographic conditions and a compact spatial layout further exacerbate noise transmission and associated social contradictions6. According to data from 2023, entertainment facilities accounted for 32.6% of noise complaints in the central urban area of Chongqing, with most complaints concentrated in commercial core areas and emerging residential areas. This highlights noise pollution as a key issue affecting the liveability and sustainable development of cities. Effectively characterizing the spatial effects of noise pollution from these facilities and understanding its interaction with residents’ perceptions is not only a technical problem in urban acoustic environmental governance but also a broader social issue requiring a balance between economic vitality and public well-being.

Research on the spatial distribution and societal impacts of commercial facilities offers valuable methodological insights for analysing noise pollution generated by music and entertainment venues. In examining spatial patterns, scholars globally have predominantly employed Point of Interest (POI) data mining—such as extracting KTV locations from platforms like Gaode Maps—alongside kernel density analysis to identify spatial clusters and activity hotspots7–9. These methods are often integrated with GIS-based spatial analysis to correlate facility distributions with empirical noise monitoring data10,11. Regarding social impacts, studies have quantified noise-related effects on residential satisfaction through soundscape mapping and perceptual surveys of affected communities12–14. Additionally, spatial syntax analysis has been applied to assess the relationship between facility accessibility and noise exposure levels15,16. While existing literature provides robust analytical frameworks, current research primarily focuses on the economic functions and transportation-related impacts of commercial facilities, with limited attention to the spatial congruence between noise pollution and residents’ perception. Nevertheless, the methodologies established in these studies remain relevant to the present investigation. Furthermore, prior research on urban noise pollution has predominantly focused on conventional sources, such as transportation and industrial activities17–21. The spatiotemporal heterogeneity of noise from music entertainment facilities and its associated social response mechanisms remain insufficiently explored. The existing literature exhibits three main limitations. First, most studies rely on static sound pressure level monitoring data, overlooking the effects of mountainous terrain on noise propagation, including spatial differentiation and obstruction22–24. Second, analyses of residents’ perceptions are predominantly based on objective physical indicators, with limited consideration of the interaction between subjective complaint data and social and economic factors25–27. Third, noise diffusion models developed for lowland cities struggle to account for the multi-dimensional spatial constraints of mountainous cities, leading to a lack of regional specificity in noise prediction and control strategies28–30. In addition, the correlation between the spatial layout of entertainment facilities and noise pollution remains unclear, particularly due to the absence of multi-scale spatial analyses capable of identifying the threshold effect of “facility density-complaint intensity"31–34.

This study takes the mountainous city of Chongqing as a typical case, focuses on nighttime noise pollution, and constructs a multi-source data fusion analysis framework based on Gaode POI data, nighttime noise complaint records, and nighttime field monitoring data (08:00 PM – 12:00 AM). The main goals of this work are to: (1) reveal the evolutionary characteristics of the “multi-core” spatial pattern of music entertainment facilities in mountainous cities and its topographic driving mechanism; (2) analyse the spatial differentiation patterns of noise pollution complaints and their scale-sensitive relationship with facility distribution; and (3) innovatively integrate citizen complaint data with GIS technology to develop a low-cost, high-timeliness noise pollution perception map. The key research innovations include: identifying a scale threshold effect through multi-scale grid analysis (50–300 m), where the correlation between facility density and noise complaints peaks at a 250 m scale (r = 0.75); and pioneering the application of public complaint data to construct a noise perception map, offering a public participation-based approach to acoustic environment governance.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study selected the main urban area of Chongqing as the research area, considering that the city is a representative example of China’s recent development (Fig. 1). Chongqing reflects many characteristics of China’s economic and social development, such as the urban-rural dual structure and regional development differences. Located in the upper reaches of the Yangtze River, Chongqing’s main urban area covers 5,473 km2 and experiences a subtropical monsoon humid climate, with an average annual temperature of 16–18 ℃. The city receives relatively abundant rainfall, with an average annual precipitation of 11 mm. Precipitation is distributed throughout the year, particularly during the flood season (May to September), which accounts for 70–85% of the annual total precipitation35. Chongqing is a typical mountain city in southwest China, characterised by its undulating terrain along mountains, rivers and steep road slopes. The main urban area is located within the Jinyun, Zhongliang, Tongluo, and Mingyue Mountain Ranges. The Yangtze and Jialing Rivers converge in this area, resulting in a high surface runoff coefficient. In areas with less variation in elevation, such as plains, hills, and low mountains, the population density is higher36,37. The main urban areas of Chongqing include the Yuzhong, Jiangbei, Yubei, Beibei, Dadukou, Nanan, Banan, Shapingba, and Jiulongpo Districts. This area serves as the political, economic, and cultural centre of Chongqing and has the highest concentration of leisure and entertainment facilities38.

Methodology

Kernel density Estimation

Kernel density estimation is an important statistical analysis method for extracting the distribution characteristics of geospatial facilities. It is particularly useful for assessing the distribution density and corresponding feature changes within a given spatial application of a certain element. Kernel density analysis39–41 uses the location of a feature point as the centre of a circle, with a bandwidth h as the radius, to search for and count the number of point-like geographical features within the search area, subsequently calculating the density value. Unlike standard analysis methods such as point density and Tyson polygon, kernel density estimation considers the weight of point features within the statistical range, resulting in more objective and accurate calculations and a smoother, more stable mapping effect42. Standard deviational ellipses

Standard deviational ellipses are mainly used from a spatial perspective to quantitatively explain the central points, distribution directions, and shapes of analysed feature points in space43. The major axis of the ellipse (XstdDist) represents the maximum diffusion direction of the overall spatial distribution, while the minor axis (YStdDist) represents the minimum diffusion direction. Spatial handling method for noise complaints

Using Python crawler technology, 10,269 complaint letters from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023, were obtained from the Chongqing Municipal Government Complaint Mailbox website (https://dc.cq.gov.cn/hlwdc-pc/index?record=istrue). The collected data were formatted accordingly. Keywords such as “concert hall”, “KTV”, “bar”, “dance hall”, “noise”, and “disturbance” were used to filter and extract 698 complaint letters related to music leisure and entertainment facilities. Then, the place name and address information within the 698 complaint letters were processed using Baidu API technology to automatically match locations and obtain the corresponding longitude and latitude coordinates. To ensure accuracy, each coordinate was manually verified and corrected where necessary. After confirming the correctness of the spatial data, the locations of 698 complaint letters were spatially processed using ArcGIS 10.8 software to generate a spatial distribution map of noise pollution complaints related to music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing. Finally, the kernel density algorithm was applied to analyse the spatial distribution of complaint points related to music leisure facilities, resulting in a noise perception map that reflects residents’ experiences of noise pollution.

Correlation analysis method between music leisure facilities and noise complaints

The analysis was conducted using ArcGIS 10.8 by generating a square grid over the study area with the “Create fishnet” tool. In order to explore the impact of different grid scales on the results, five grid scales were established, with side lengths of 50 m, 100 m, 150 m, 200 m, 250 m, and 300 m. The following steps were then carried out for each grid scale. The “Feature to Polygon” tool was used to convert the line layer into a surface layer. At the same time, each grid cell was assigned a unique identifier. The “Spatial Join” tool in ArcGIS 10.8 was applied to assign the same code to both music leisure and entertainment facility points and noise complaint locations within the same grid cell. Using the “Dissolve” tool in ArcGIS 10.8 software, the data were aggregated based on grid identifiers, summing the number of music leisure and entertainment facilities and the number of noise complaint occurrences within each grid cell. Finally, the “Table to Excel” tool in ArcGIS 10.8 software was used to export the results into an Excel file, recording the number of each grid, the number of points of music leisure and entertainment facilities corresponding to the grid, and the number of places where noise complaints occur.

Data sources

The research data included Chongqing’s point of interest (POI) data and citizen complaint data. POI data were obtained from Scott Map (https://console.amap.com/dev/index). Using POI field types and names, POI point data related to music leisure and entertainment facilities were screened. In this study, music leisure and entertainment facilities included concert halls, KTVs, bars, nightclubs, dance halls, etc. The citizen complaint data were collected from the Chongqing resident complaint network platform (https://cqwz.cqnews.net/index). According to the collected citizen complaint information, noise-related keywords (KTV, bar, dance hall, loud noise, disturbing the people, etc.) were used to screen the noise-related complaint information related to music facilities. Then, a word segmentation algorithm was used to extract the place name and address-related content from each complaint. Finally, the spatial location coordinates of each noise complaint were obtained using the Baidu API to match the place name and address spatially.

Results

Spatial distribution characteristics of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing

In this study, 1,738 POI data points for music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing in 2023—including bars, KTVs, and livehouses—were screened using AutoNavi POI data. The spatial distribution of these facilities exhibits a “multi-core” decentralized pattern (Fig. 2a), closely aligning with the distribution of urban commercial clusters and population density. The highest kernel density in core agglomeration areas (e.g., Guanyinqiao, Jiefangbei, and Daping) reaches 12.5/km² (Fig. 2b), accounting for 58.3% of the total number of music facilities in the city. The spatial overlap rate between its distribution and nighttime economic hubs (such as business districts and pedestrian streets) is as high as 82.7%. Secondary dispersed nodes, such as those in university towns and Longzhou Bay, exhibit localized agglomeration patterns with facility densities ranging from 4.2 to 6/km², mainly serving college students and emerging residential populations (Fig. 3). This distribution pattern is strongly influenced by Chongqing’s mountainous topography, which differentiates it from the “core solo diffusion” model observed in lowland cities such as Beijing and Shanghai, where facility density decreases from the centre to the periphery45,46.

Temporal evolution and Spatial pattern changes of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing

From 2013 to 2023, the total number of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing decreased by 241. There was a particularly notable decrease from 2019 to 2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, which significantly restricted travel and contributed to a shift in consumption habits, such as the shift from offline to online consumption47. Mean-square ellipse analysis showed a north-westward shift in the central distribution of these facilities (Fig. 4), with an offset amplitude of 0.83 km (Table 1). This is in line with the regional development plans of Yubei, Jiangbei and University Town. The area of the ellipse increased by 12.7% and the circumference increased by 8.3%, reflecting the outward expansion of music leisure and entertainment facilities in response to urban growth.

Chongqing music leisure and entertainment facilities and noise pollution complaints

The concentration of noise pollution complaints related to music-based leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing follows a “multi-core” distribution pattern. This pattern is influenced by topography, as Chongqing’s numerous mountain ranges divide the urban development space into several parts. Noise pollution complaints related to music-based leisure and entertainment facilities are mainly concentrated in commercial areas such as Guanyinqiao, Three Gorges Square, Daping, and Yangjiaping. In addition, the number of noise pollution complaints related to music-based leisure and entertainment facilities is higher in areas such as University Town, Danzishi, Lianglu, Beibei, and Caijia (Fig. 5).

The strongest correlation was observed at the 250-meter grid scale, indicating the most significant relationship between the number of music leisure facilities and the number of noise pollution complaints at this scale. Discussion

Spatial differentiation mechanism of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing under terrain constraints

This study reveals the formation mechanism of the “multi-core” spatial pattern of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing, primarily driven by the strong constraining effect of the mountainous terrain on urban spatial differentiation48. Unlike the typical “core solo diffusion and decreasing density” model seen in lowland cities such as Beijing and Shanghai49–52, Chongqing’s river valleys, terraces and other topographical features divide the continuous urban development space. These divisions cause facilities to form high-density cores in well-connected valley business districts, such as Guanyin Bridge and Jiefangbei, while secondary nodes develop in densely populated terraces such as University Town and Longzhou Bay48. This finding supports the theoretical hypothesis that topography shapes the spatial differentiation of leisure facilities by restricting land development intensity and traffic accessibility53, offering empirical evidence for the theory of mountainous urban spatial structure. Compared to previous studies, this research finds that the “core intensification-edge diffusion” pattern of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing is significantly influenced by terrain. In lowland urban areas, leisure facilities typically spread evenly along radial or ring road networks51. However, in Chongqing, the facilities follow a “valley agglomeration-platform jumping"54 cluster distribution due to topographic barriers, which highlights the profound impact of the geographical environment on the spatial reconstruction of urban functions55. The research further confirms that the polycentric structure of mountain cities is not merely the result of functional zoning, but rather the outcome of the combined effects of topographic constraints and market demand. This conclusion enhances the theoretical understanding of the “topography-function” coupling mechanism53. However, the study has the following limitations. First, the POI data lack attribute information, such as facility operation scale and traffic counts, which may reduce the explanatory power of the demand side in spatial differentiation. Second, although the elliptic analysis of mean square deviation captures the overall trend of facility distribution, it does not adequately address the blocking effects of terrain features like mountains and rivers on a micro scale. Third, the spatial correlation between terrain undulation and facility density has not been quantitatively assessed. Future research could integrate digital elevation models (DEM) and geographical regression analysis methods to build a quantitative “terrain resistance-facility layout” model. Additionally, incorporating multi-source information such as night light data and mobile phone signalling data could improve the accuracy of mechanism analysis. Cross-city comparative studies could also be conducted to reveal the varying impacts of different terrain types (e.g., mountains, plains, and hills) on the spatial differentiation of leisure facilities.

Innovation and validation of urban noise residents’ perception map construction method

This study introduces an innovative approach by utilizing citizens’ noise complaint data to construct an urban noise residents’ perception map, providing a low-cost and timely spatial analysis method for studying noise related to music leisure and entertainment facilities. In comparison to traditional methods like noise monitoring point layout56,57 and model simulation techniques58,59, this method enables the rapid spatialization of noise pollution perception data by integrating public Participatory Geographic Information Systems (PPGIS) and GIS spatial analysis. The spatial distribution characteristics of “multi-core” music noise complaints in Chongqing (with a core density peak of 4.3/km²) are strongly correlated with the spatial pattern of music leisure and entertainment facilities (r = 0.75), confirming the feasibility of this method for identifying noise hotspots. This study overcomes the limitations of traditional noise mapping methods. Unlike Murphy’s static noise assessment, which relies on fixed monitoring points60, complaint data dynamically reflect the perceived noise hotspots. Furthermore, compared to the land use regression model proposed by Xie et al.22, this method avoids the assumption of spatial homogenization for simulation parameters such as road network and building density, making it more suitable for the heterogeneous topography of Chongqing. However, the complaint data can introduce sampling bias due to differences in residents’ sensitivity, a phenomenon consistent with the subjectivity in noise perception found by Liu et al. in Beijing61.

In order to further verify the accuracy of the urban noise simulation map, the simulated urban noise map was compared and analysed using the field noise monitoring data. Five locations with high simulation values were selected from the urban noise residents’ perception map (Fig. 8). The noise monitoring and reference standard values of these locations were obtained from the official website of the Chongqing Municipal Ecological Environment Bureau (https://sthjj.cq.gov.cn/hjzl_249/). The monitoring period was from 8:00 PM to 12:00 AM in 2022, as shown in Table 5. The field monitoring results indicated that the nighttime noise values at the five locations with high noise simulation values exceeded the reference standard values, indicating excessive noise levels. The reference standard value is based on the “Acoustic Environment Management Standard” issued by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China, which stipulates the environmental noise limits for different acoustic environment functional areas. The verification confirms that the field noise monitoring values support the accuracy of the urban noise simulation map to a certain extent. At the same time, both the simulated noise values and the measured values were standardized (normalized). Then, using the SPSSAU tool, the root mean square error (RMSE) was calculated to be 0.37 and the mean absolute error (MAE) was 0.26. This indicates that the model has relatively good simulation accuracy and can be applied to trend analysis and general quantitative evaluation.

The research results indicate that citizens’ complaints serve as a valuable supplementary data source for urban noise perception map simulation, particularly in areas with sparse acoustic monitoring networks. However, this study also reveals certain limitations. First, the coverage of complaint data is influenced by citizens’ environmental awareness and the accessibility of complaint channels, which may lead to an underestimation of noise pollution in remote areas. Second, keyword-based filtering may result in the incomplete identification of noise pollution complaint letters related to music leisure and entertainment facilities. To address these challenges, future research could explore the application of natural language processing (NLP) techniques to optimize the semantic recognition accuracy of complaint text and integrate complaint data with multi-source spatiotemporal data, such as social media-based soundscape perception, to develop a multi-dimensional noise assessment model.

Spatial coupling mechanism of music leisure facilities and noise residents’ perception

This study employed a multi-scale grid analysis (50–300 m) to examine the scale sensitivity characteristics of the correlation between music leisure and entertainment facilities and noise complaints. The results indicate that the Pearson correlation coefficient increases and then decreases with the expansion of the grid scale, peaking at 250 m (r = 0.75, P < 0.05), suggesting that this scale may be a reasonable analytical unit for capturing the spatial coupling relationship between facilities and noise (Figs. 7 and 8). These findings provide new evidence for the problem of malleable area units (MAUP) in geography, highlighting that spatial analysis results are significantly influenced by grid scale selection62,63. The present research advances the methodological understanding of spatial correlation research on noise pollution, confirming that a medium-scale (250 m) grid effectively balances spatial heterogeneity and data aggregation effects64. This contrasts with the 500 m scale recommended by Pu et al. in studies on lowland urban areas65, likely reflecting the compression effect of spatial fragmentation in mountainous cities on analysis scale66. Additionally, a nonlinear threshold effect67 was identified, showing a surge in complaints when facility density exceeds 140 n/km², which addresses the limitations of noise simulation models based on linear assumptions, such as Kamkar’s model68, and aligns with the concept of “critical threshold” proposed in the acoustic environment carrying capacity theory69. Another limitation of this study is the unquantified impact of terrain undulation on scale effects. Future research could explore the implementation of noise zoning control policies based on the proposed 250 m scale threshold for urban noise management.

Conclusion

The impact of noise pollution from music leisure and entertainment facilities is a key concern in the acoustic environment governance of mountainous cities. Taking Chongqing as an example, this study employed AutoNavi POI data, noise complaint records, and field monitoring data to reveal the spatial coupling characteristics between the distribution of music leisure and entertainment facilities and residents’ noise perception. The study found that: (1) Chongqing’s music leisure and entertainment facilities exhibit a “multi-core” decentralized pattern, differing from the “core solo diffusion” model typical of lowland cities. Due to topographic segmentation, Chongqing’s pattern is characterized by “valley agglomeration-platform jumping”. The centre of facility distribution has shifted 0.83 km northwest over time, while the geographical concentration index indicates an increasing local agglomeration effect from 2013 to 2023. The core areas (Guanyin Bridge, Jiefang Monument, etc.) overlap with active night economy zones, while the secondary nodes, such as university towns, show local agglomeration due to terrain constraints. (2) The distribution of music leisure and entertainment facilities is significantly coupled with residents’ noise perception. The Pearson correlation coefficient peaks at the 250 m grid scale (r = 0.75), and complaints increase nonlinearly when facility density exceeds 140 n/km². This finding highlights a critical threshold effect in the noise carrying capacity of mountainous cities. (3) The study identifies scale sensitivity at a 250 m peak and a nonlinear threshold effect in the relationship between music leisure and entertainment facilities and residents’ noise perception, offering an innovative approach to mapping residents’ noise perception. It also validates the feasibility of using public participation data for noise hotspot identification, offering a comprehensive evaluation of social perception and physical pollution monitoring.

Based on the above research findings, the following suggestions are put forward for the noise pollution control of music leisure and entertainment facilities in Chongqing. (1) Implement differential control thresholds. According to the “density threshold of 140/km²” and “scale sensitivity of 250 meters” revealed by the research, more stringent access standards should be implemented in core areas such as Guanyinqiao and Jiefangbei, while in secondary nodes such as the University Town, local agglomeration is allowed but buffer zones should be set up (e.g., maintaining a distance of ≥ 250 m from residential areas). (2) Terrain - oriented layout adjustment. Utilize the distribution law of “valley agglomeration - platform jump”, and in the planning, prioritize the layout of new facilities in valleys with strong acoustic environment carrying capacity (such as the underground floors of commercial complexes), and try to avoid adding new facilities in sound - sensitive platform areas (such as dense residential areas). (3) Establish a “digital twin” platform for noise pollution, integrating POI, noise complaint data, and real - time monitoring data to achieve accurate traceability. At the same time, further improve the urban residents’ noise complaint platform, enhance the public’s awareness of noise pollution prevention and control, and strengthen natural language processing technology to improve the accuracy of complaint data.

This study has deficiencies in aspects such as micro - scale terrain analysis, business characteristics of music and entertainment facilities, and analysis of residents’ economic and social attributes. Future research can integrate mobile phone signaling data and the DEM model to quantify the terrain resistance effect and enable cross - terrain urban comparisons. At the same time, more in - depth analysis and research can be carried out on aspects such as the scale, type, business hours of music and entertainment facilities, as well as the income levels and age structures of residents.
