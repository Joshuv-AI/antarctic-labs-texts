Framework of Core Competencies for Interdisciplinary School Mental Health Collaboration

School mental health professionals (SMHPs) are increasingly responsible for addressing the complex and growing mental health needs of youth across the United States [1]. This workforce, comprised primarily of school counselors, school social workers, school psychologists, and school therapists, brings overlapping yet distinct training pathways, role expectations, and scopes of practice to service delivery. Although ethical codes and professional standards across these disciplines emphasize collaboration as essential to effective practice [2, 3, 4, 5], many SMHPs report limited formal graduate‐level preparation for interdisciplinary collaboration in school settings [6]. As a result, collaborative practice is often shaped more by local norms and contextual demands than by shared, empirically grounded frameworks, creating a gap between the principles of collaboration and their implementation in coordinated, student‐centered care.

Interdisciplinary collaboration in school mental health is associated with both clear benefits and persistent challenges. Barriers include professional territoriality, role ambiguity, confidentiality concerns, logistical constraints, and funding limitations [6, 7]. At the same time, effective collaboration is supported by strong communication, mutual trust, shared student‐centered goals, and clarity regarding roles and responsibilities [6]. Organizational structures, including leadership support, protected time for collaboration, and formalized processes for team decision‐making, also play a critical role. Despite these insights, there is limited consensus regarding the specific competencies required to enact effective interdisciplinary collaboration in K–12 settings. This lack of clarity constrains the ability of training programs, supervisors, and school systems to intentionally prepare SMHPs for collaborative practice, particularly in complex or resource‐limited environments.

SMHPs provide critical access to care as youth mental health needs continue to rise nationwide [8]. Within K–12 schools, services are often delivered through multitiered systems of support (MTSS), which provide universal, targeted, and individualized interventions [9]. Effective implementation depends on both the availability of trained professionals and coordinated care, awareness of resources, and clearly defined professional roles [10]. SMHPs contribute in complementary ways. For instance, often, school counselors address academic, social–emotional, and career development through instruction, counseling, and consultation [3]; school psychologists provide assessment, intervention, and consultation services [4]; school social workers support emotional and behavioral functioning while connecting students, families, and systems [5]; and school therapists deliver clinical services including diagnosis and treatment [11]. Although these roles are distinct, they frequently intersect in practice, reinforcing the importance of effective interdisciplinary collaboration.

Interdisciplinary collaboration in school mental health involves the intentional integration of expertise across roles to support coordinated service delivery [12, 13]. Often conceptualized as teaming, this approach promotes efficiency, reduces service fragmentation, and improves access to supports [10, 14]. Benefits extend beyond coordination, including reduced teacher stress and improved student outcomes, outcomes that are particularly salient given increasing student needs and post‐pandemic stressors [15, 16, 17]. However, collaboration is shaped by interpersonal and contextual factors. Communication quality, role clarity, and professional boundaries consistently influence team functioning, while factors such as staffing, time, school culture, and case complexity further shape collaborative processes [6, 10, 15, 18, 19, 20]. Team structure and leadership practices, including the use of formal protocols or informal exchanges, also affect how collaboration unfolds [6, 21].

Within interdisciplinary teams, SMHPs may initiate collaboration, assess needs, deliver interventions, communicate with partners, and monitor progress [19]. However, systemic constraints (e.g., competing demands, role diffusion) can limit meaningful engagement in collaborative work [21]. Although the literature identifies foundational collaborative skills such as communication, mutual respect, and shared commitment to student well‐being, these competencies are often described inconsistently and without integration into a unified framework [6, 10, 22, 23]. Existing scholarship provides important conceptual foundations but may not fully reflect the realities of contemporary school mental health practice. Bronstein's model emphasized interdependence, flexibility, and shared ownership of goals as core elements of interdisciplinary collaboration [24, 25]. Subsequent work by Mellin and colleagues examined collaboration processes and contextual influences within school‐based teams [15, 26, 27]. Ball and colleagues further contributed by mapping professional standards to identify competencies supporting interdisciplinary practice [28]. While these models offer valuable insights, much of this work predates current conditions, including increased student mental health needs, expanded MTSS frameworks, and more diverse school populations. As a result, existing frameworks may not fully capture the context‐specific competencies required for effective collaboration in today's K–12 environments.

The present study extends prior work by centering the perspectives of practicing SMHPs through a Delphi methodology. This approach enables the identification of competencies grounded in real‐world practice, capturing the knowledge, skills, and dispositions necessary for effective interdisciplinary collaboration. Unlike prior frameworks, this study offers a consensus‐based, practitioner‐informed model that reflects contemporary school contexts. The resulting framework highlights competencies such as distributed leadership, systems‐oriented thinking, and culturally responsive, student‐centered collaboration. This study provides an updated and actionable set of competencies to inform training, professional development, and policy, thereby strengthening interdisciplinary collaboration in school mental health settings.

Statement of the Problem and Purpose

SMHPs are increasingly relied upon to address growing student mental health needs, yet interdisciplinary collaboration remains constrained by role ambiguity, limited preparation, and the absence of contemporary, empirically grounded frameworks. Although professional standards emphasize collaboration, guidance is largely discipline‐specific and offers limited direction for integrated team functioning. Prior research identifies key interpersonal and contextual influences but lacks consensus on the competencies required across roles. This gap limits the ability of training programs, supervisors, and school systems to effectively prepare SMHPs for team‐based practice. The present study addresses this gap by examining the following research question: What core competencies do interdisciplinary SMH professionals identify as essential for effective collaboration in K–12 schools? Using a three‐round Delphi methodology, the study identified competencies endorsed by an interdisciplinary panel of practicing SMHPs and synthesized them into higher‐order domains, resulting in an integrative framework to guide collaborative practice.

Methods

The Delphi technique is a well‐established method for generating expert consensus on complex or underdefined topics through iterative rounds of data collection with an anonymous panel [29, 30, 31]. It has been widely used in school counseling and related fields to develop professional competencies and practice guidelines [32, 33]. In the present study, a three‐round Delphi design was used to identify core competencies for effective interdisciplinary collaboration among SMHPs. This approach allowed for the integration of diverse professional perspectives while minimizing the influence of hierarchy, making it well‐suited for interdisciplinary contexts.

Panelists were recruited via multiple pathways, including word of mouth, participant recontact, professional listservs, and social media (e.g., school counselor Facebook groups). Panelists were selected based on active engagement in school‐based mental health practice. Eligibility criteria included employment as a school counselor, school social worker, school psychologist, or school therapist in a K–12 setting, at least 3 years of professional experience, and regular participation in interdisciplinary collaboration. These criteria ensured that participants contributed practice‐based expertise and a nuanced understanding of team dynamics. Including actively practicing professionals enhanced the relevance and applicability of the resulting competency framework for training, supervision, and policy.

In Round 1, participants completed a Qualtrics survey consisting of five open‐ended questions designed to elicit perspectives on the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and competencies necessary for effective interdisciplinary collaboration. Questions were developed through iterative discussions among the research team and informed by prior literature on interprofessional collaboration and teamwork [15, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Prompts encouraged participants to draw on their professional experiences and provide detailed responses. The questions addressed: (1) characteristics of effective collaboration, (2) necessary knowledge, (3) critical skills, (4) supportive attitudes or dispositions, and (5) additional competencies needed to address common challenges.

Responses from Round 1 were analyzed to generate initial themes and competency statements. In Rounds 2 and 3, participants evaluated and refined these competencies through rating and feedback, allowing the research team to assess consensus and improve clarity. The final set of competencies was synthesized into four overarching themes with corresponding subcomponents, representing agreement across the interdisciplinary panel. Data Collection

Round 1

In Round 1, participants were invited via email to complete a Qualtrics survey consisting of five open‐ended questions exploring the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and competencies needed for effective interdisciplinary collaboration among SMHPs. Participants had 1 week to respond, with a reminder sent 3 days before the deadline, and were encouraged to provide multiple responses based on their professional experience.

Responses were analyzed using systematic content analysis [34]. Each researcher independently reviewed all responses and developed an initial codebook informed by the data and existing competency frameworks. To ensure consistency, 10 pilot responses were coded collaboratively to establish agreement on coding procedures. Responses were then categorized by question, and the research team met to reconcile discrepancies, remove duplicates, and clarify overlap. This process reduced 544 responses to 143 preliminary competency items. All 47 panelists completed Round 1 (100% response rate).

Round 2

In Round 2, participants received a Qualtrics survey containing the 143 competency items and rated each on a 7‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree) based on its importance for interdisciplinary collaboration. Optional open‐ended fields allowed participants to suggest revisions or provide feedback. Items meeting consensus criteria (median ≥ 6 and interquartile range ≤ 1) were retained, while remaining items were carried forward. A total of 124 items reached consensus. Of the 47 invited panelists, 38 completed Round 2 (81% response rate).

Round 3

Round 3 included the 19 items that did not reach consensus. Participants were provided with anonymized peer feedback and summary statistics (median and interquartile range) for each item before re‐rating them on the same scale. Eight additional items achieved consensus, resulting in a final set of 151 competencies. Of the 38 invited participants, 37 completed Round 3 (97% response rate). This iterative process produced a comprehensive set of competencies reflecting interdisciplinary collaboration in school mental health.

Data Analysis

Following the final round, a multi‐step qualitative analysis was conducted on the 151 competencies. Each researcher independently generated initial codes capturing key concepts, with coding initially organized by Round 1 prompts. In a second cycle, competencies were grouped into broader conceptual categories. The research team met to compare interpretations, resolve discrepancies, and refine category boundaries using constant comparative methods to merge overlapping concepts and ensure coherence. This process resulted in a set of higher‐order domains representing the core areas of competency. These domains were refined for clarity and alignment with the data, forming the practitioner‐informed framework presented in the following section.

Results

Across the three‐round Delphi process, the expert panel reached strong consensus on a comprehensive set of competencies that characterize effective interdisciplinary collaboration among SMHPs. Analysis of the agreed‐upon statements revealed four overarching themes transcending participants' responses to all five Delphi prompts. These themes reflected the knowledge, skills, professional dispositions, and collaborative capacities panelists viewed as important for supporting student well‐being through coordinated team‐based practice.

The first theme to emerge concerned the central role of effective communication and relationship‐building in interdisciplinary work. Panelists emphasized that collaboration depends on clear, respectful, and transparent communication across multiple modes, including in‐person meetings, emails, phone calls, texts, and virtual platforms, ensuring all team members, families, and community partners are informed. Critical skills include active listening, providing constructive feedback, summarizing others' perspectives, articulating goals, and managing difficult conversations with diplomacy and professionalism. Participants highlighted relational dispositions such as empathy, psychological safety, humility, and openness to feedback as important for sustaining trust and cohesion in teams with diverse professional backgrounds. Knowledge of confidentiality laws (FERPA/HIPAA), cultural norms, and school/community context was also described as fundamental for conveying information responsibly while promoting student‐centered decision‐making.

The second theme focused on collaborative leadership and team functioning, emphasizing shared responsibility, accountability, and coordinated teamwork toward student‐centered goals. Panelists described leadership as distributed and flexible, with team members stepping into or deferring leadership based on expertise and task demands. Key skills include organizing structured meetings with agendas, follow‐up procedures, role clarity, and equitable participation, as well as advocating for resources and aligning team actions with broader school objectives. Dispositions such as mutual respect, trust, commitment to shared goals, and recognition of each member's strengths were repeatedly cited as critical for maintaining team coherence. Participants also noted the importance of systems thinking, understanding how individual roles intersect within family, school, and community systems, and integrating knowledge of MTSS and school mental health frameworks to guide coordinated interventions.

The third theme emphasized professionalism, reflective practice, and critical thinking, operationalized as ethical, competent, and self‐aware engagement that integrates evidence, data, and reflection into collaborative decision‐making. Panelists highlighted skills such as accountability, time management, organization, evidence‐based problem‐solving, and using student data to guide interventions across academic, behavioral, and social–emotional domains. Reflective competencies included awareness of personal biases, evaluating assumptions, and adjusting approaches in response to team or student needs. Dispositions such as humility, openness to learning, and commitment to continuous professional growth were described as integral for navigating complex team dynamics. Participants also underscored the importance of knowledge related to interventions, mental health diagnoses, tiered systems, and local resources to ensure decisions are both contextually appropriate and evidence‐informed.

Finally, panelists reached consensus on the importance of cultural competence, equity, and a student‐centered mindset as a guiding framework for collaboration. Effective interdisciplinary practice requires understanding diverse cultural, social, and community contexts, integrating culturally responsive strategies, and addressing systemic inequities that affect student well‐being. Participants highlighted relational dispositions such as empathy, compassion, and commitment to equity and antiracism, alongside skills in advocacy, collaborative problem‐solving, and integrating student, family, and community perspectives. Teams were described as most effective when members explicitly align their roles with student needs, apply trauma‐informed approaches, and collectively focus on holistic social–emotional, academic, and behavioral outcomes. Operationally, this includes structured reflection on equity, coordination across disciplines, and intentional strategies to ensure all students' voices and experiences are valued in decision‐making. Discussion

Using a Delphi approach, this study developed a practitioner‐informed framework of 151 competencies for interdisciplinary school mental health collaboration, organized into four domains: (1) communication and relationship‐building, (2) collaborative leadership and team functioning, (3) professionalism, reflective practice, and critical thinking, and (4) cultural competence, equity, and a student‐centered mindset. These domains provide a structured yet contextually grounded framework to guide practice, training, and policy in K–12 settings. This study offers a granular and actionable model that reflects real‐world collaboration. However, because the panel was primarily composed of school counselors, findings may be most applicable to similar contexts rather than broadly generalizable.

The framework builds on established models of interdisciplinary collaboration. Bronstein's work emphasized interdependence and shared goals as central to team functioning [24, 25], while Mellin and colleagues [15, 26, 27] examined these processes within school‐based teams, and Ball et al. [28] identified competencies across disciplines. The present study extends this scholarship by translating conceptual elements into specific, practice‐oriented competencies. For example, collaborative leadership operationalizes shared responsibility through facilitation skills, structured team processes, and equitable participation. The inclusion of cultural competence and student‐centered practice further expands prior frameworks by explicitly integrating equity and systemic awareness.

Findings align with existing literature emphasizing the importance of role clarity, trust, and coordinated service delivery [26, 28]. Communication competencies highlighted open, multidirectional dialogue and psychological safety, reinforcing prior findings [27] while adding detail on both formal and informal communication practices. Participants emphasized the importance of clear role definitions and minimizing overlap, supporting earlier research on reducing fragmentation in services [26, 28]. These results underscore that effective collaboration depends on both relational and structural supports. Competencies related to leadership and team functioning reflect shared leadership models [24, 25, 27, 28] while offering greater specificity. Participants emphasized facilitation skills that promote accountability and inclusive participation, along with structured meeting practices such as clear agendas and follow‐up procedures. Simultaneously, informal interactions were identified as important for maintaining team cohesion. Professionalism, reflective practice, and critical thinking were also emphasized, particularly ongoing reflection and awareness of bias as mechanisms for improving team functioning in complex school environments.

This study contributes to the literature by operationalizing collaboration in concrete, practice‐based terms. Participants highlighted conditions that support psychological safety and a shared student‐centered vision, as well as mechanisms such as accessible communication, flexible leadership, and data‐informed decision‐making. The framework also emphasizes the collaborative use of evidence‐based practices, addressing how interdisciplinary teamwork is enacted in practice rather than only identifying its components. The domain of cultural competence and equity represents a key advancement. In contrast to earlier models focused primarily on team processes, this framework positions equity and culturally responsive practice as central competencies. Participants emphasized centering student perspectives, addressing bias, and incorporating trauma‐informed approaches, underscoring the role of equity in shaping both collaboration and student outcomes. Finally, the study introduces competencies related to sustainability and implementation. Participants identified the importance of systems thinking, assessing readiness, and supporting long‐term integration of collaborative practices. These findings extend prior work by situating collaboration within broader organizational contexts, highlighting the need for ongoing structural support to sustain effective interdisciplinary teamwork.

Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and Equity

The results of both the Delphi study and the culminating framework of core competencies for interdisciplinary school mental health collaboration address a gap in the field, offering meaningful implications for school health policy, practice, and equity. Regarding school health policy, findings suggest the need to establish formal guidelines that explicitly mandate interdisciplinary collaboration competencies as part of school mental health team standards. The Delphi panel reached strong consensus on competencies related to teamwork, communication, shared leadership, ethical practice, and culturally responsive collaboration, indicating that these capacities are foundational to effective interdisciplinary work. Accordingly, these competencies can be embedded as required expectations within job descriptions, professional standards, or role delineation documents for SMHPs. In addition, the competency framework can inform performance evaluations, professional development requirements, and licensure or credentialing expectations, thereby reinforcing collaboration as a core professional responsibility rather than an optional activity. Critically, grounding policy in competency‐based expectations may also strengthen advocacy for protected time and institutional support for collaboration, addressing a longstanding structural barrier identified across school mental health roles.

In alignment with these policy implications, the competency framework underscores the critical importance of creating organizational conditions that enable collaborative competencies to be enacted in practice. Specifically, schools can establish policies that ensure protected time and structured mechanisms for team reflection, communication, and coordinated decision‐making. Competencies related to effective communication, reflective practice, shared accountability, and coordinated teamwork require intentional organizational structures rather than ad hoc interactions. To support this, schools can schedule regular, protected blocks during the school week dedicated to interdisciplinary meetings, collaborative planning, and case consultation. Additionally, schools can implement structured routines such as guided debrief sessions, reflective discussion protocols, or peer feedback processes, which align closely with the framework's emphasis on continuous reflection, critical thinking, and team functioning. Institutionalizing these processes enables collaboration to function as a routine, supported, and sustainable component of school mental health practice rather than relying on individual initiative.

With respect to school health practice, the competency framework offers detailed and actionable guidance for operationalizing collaboration in day‐to‐day work. Schools can implement structured team routines and shared leadership models that reflect the consensus competencies related to distributed leadership, role clarity, and coordinated decision‐making. For example, interdisciplinary teams can use competency maps to intentionally differentiate collaborative roles within teams (e.g., facilitator, equity advocate, data integrator), allowing leadership to rotate based on situational expertise rather than professional discipline. In practice, a school psychologist might lead data integration and progress monitoring for a student support plan, while a school social worker assumes the equity advocate role to examine contextual barriers, and a school counselor facilitates communication and coordination across partners. These evidence‐based practices directly reflect the panel's emphasis on flexibility, accountability, and expertise‐driven collaboration, offering a practical blueprint for enhancing team functioning.

Finally, implications for school health equity are particularly salient given the Delphi panel's strong consensus around cultural competence, equity, and a student‐centered mindset as core collaborative competencies. These findings signal the imperative to intentionally center student experiences and culturally responsive practices in all interdisciplinary planning and intervention efforts. Teams can enact these competencies by inviting students and families, when appropriate, to co‐construct goals and success indicators, ensuring that interventions reflect students' lived experiences rather than solely professional assumptions. Additionally, teams can utilize culturally responsive case conceptualization templates aligned with the competency framework to deliberately consider language, identity, family values, disability status, and prior school experiences when planning supports. In parallel, schools should establish processes that allow teams to evaluate and address systemic barriers affecting access, support, and outcomes for marginalized students. Competencies related to equity and critical reflection can be operationalized through equity audits of referrals, discipline actions, and service outcomes, as well as through structured equity‐centered dialogue during collaborative decision‐making. These practices reinforce equity as a shared, procedural responsibility embedded within interdisciplinary collaboration rather than an individual or ad‐hoc concern.

Limitations

The study has several limitations worth noting. Firstly, the study's sample notably lacks the voices and lived experiences of SMHPs from diverse racial and ethical backgrounds. The lack of diverse perspectives can limit generalizability and the degree to which the findings reflect attitudes and beliefs across the four professions. Next, the sample was overwhelmingly comprised of practicing school counselors. While this provides significant ecological validity as school counselors commonly outnumber other school mental health professions, it is possible that this professional imbalance skewed the Delphi study's results compared to a Delphi study comprised of an equal number of participants from each profession. Lastly, self‐selection bias is a noteworthy limitation. Specifically, participants volunteered to engage in a multi‐round study, which may reflect stronger interest or investment in interdisciplinary collaboration than is typical among all SMHPs.

Conclusion

Interdisciplinary collaboration is widely recognized as essential to effective school mental health practice, yet schools often lack clear guidance on what high‐quality collaboration actually requires. This study addresses that gap by articulating a set of core competencies grounded in the lived experiences of SMHPs who regularly engage in interdisciplinary work. The resulting framework offers a shared language for understanding, strengthening, and sustaining collaborative practice. As schools continue to respond to increasingly complex student needs, this framework can support more intentional preparation, policy development, and day‐to‐day collaboration among SMHPs, ultimately strengthening the capacity of teams to work together in meaningful and coordinated ways.
