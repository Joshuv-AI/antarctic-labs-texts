“They make it seem harmless”: a qualitative investigation into women’s perceptions about novel gambling promotions and public relations strategies
Authors: Simone McCarthy, Hannah Pitt, Grace Arnot, Samantha L. Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gambling industry uses a range of marketing strategies to increase the appeal and normalisation of their products. While there has been significant focus on how the gambling industry targets young men, there is evidence that the industry is increasingly using innovative marketing strategies to appeal to women. This study aimed to explore young women’s views about gambling promotions and public relations strategies, and their potential impact on women’s gambling attitudes and behaviours.MethodsAn online qualitative survey was conducted with n = 525 women aged 18–40 years living in Victoria, Australia. The survey collected information about the women’s socio-demographic characteristics. Participants were then asked a range of open-text questions about their perceptions of the impact of novel gambling promotions that appeared in social media posts from gambling companies. This included their views about the use of celebrities and influencers, the appeal of novelty betting markets, and the alignment of gambling with women’s sport and social causes. Data were analysed using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis.ResultsThree key themes were constructed from the data. First, participants perceived that novel gambling promotions contributed to the increasing normalisation and social acceptability of gambling among women. Second, these strategies were seen to encourage gambling participation, particularly among women who may not otherwise gamble, by aligning gambling with women’s interests, values, and social contexts. Third, participants raised concerns about the potential for these strategies to increase women’s vulnerability to gambling harm, particularly through the framing of gambling as harmless, empowering, and socially beneficial for women’s events.ConclusionsThis study highlights how novel gambling promotions may be influencing and normalising women’s engagement with gambling and have the potential to increase their risk of gambling related harm. These strategies were often presenting gambling as a fun and socially acceptable activity, while masking its potential consequences. The findings highlight the need for comprehensive regulatory and public health responses, including stronger restrictions on gendered marketing practices, education to counter misleading promotional messages, and targeted harm prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26724-7.

Background

Women are increasingly engaging with a broad range of gambling products [1–3]. For example, recent prevalence data from the Australian state of Victoria shows that gambling participation rates among women are now on par with men, with approximately one in two women gambling each year and around one-third gambling monthly [4]. Despite a recent national prevalence survey which has shown that relatively few Australian women participate in online forms of gambling, including horse and sports betting [5], there is a growing concern about women’s engagement with online betting, particularly among younger Australian women aged 18 to 34, who are at the greatest risk of experiencing gambling harm [6]. This concern has emerged alongside a global shift from land based to online gambling, with digital platforms enabling greater accessibility to gambling products [4]. Researchers have highlighted that current approaches to women’s gambling still mostly focus on individual factors, and overlook the range of social, cultural, commercial and political factors that may contribute to gambling harm for women [7]. A broader focus on the range of determinants that may shape women’s gambling attitudes and behaviours is needed, particularly as women’s gambling engagement has diversified alongside the expansion of novel gambling industry marketing and public relations strategies [8].

There is a growing focus in the gambling literature on how the industry uses marketing to drive consumption and brand recognition, and to position gambling companies as socially responsible actors [8]. This marketing can cultivate legitimacy among policymakers and the public, which the gambling industry and those who financially gain from gambling such as sporting codes and broadcasters may use to delay or avoid regulatory action [9]. As Thomas and colleagues describe, contemporary gambling marketing is greater than just traditional forms of advertising and includes:“The full range of direct and indirect advertising, promotion, sponsorship, incentives, public relations, lobbying, and donations that are strategically used by the gambling industry to promote its products, gain publicity, attract new customers, shape social and cultural attitudes, and build corporate and product image and support” ([8], p. 3).

“The full range of direct and indirect advertising, promotion, sponsorship, incentives, public relations, lobbying, and donations that are strategically used by the gambling industry to promote its products, gain publicity, attract new customers, shape social and cultural attitudes, and build corporate and product image and support” ([8], p. 3).

To date, most of the focus on gambling marketing practices has investigated the impact and influence of gambling advertising on children [10–12], and young men [13–15] – particularly in relation to sports betting. However, more recently, researchers have documented a diverse set of gambling promotional strategies that are used to appeal to women [16–20]. Researchers have demonstrated that gambling companies may use a range of strategies to position different types of gambling as female-friendly, including the “images of hearts, cocktails, fashion, and glitter balls, offers for beauty products, and references to ‘mums’” ([18], p. 7). Researchers have also found that gambling companies increasingly feature women in promotional material (rather than in traditional concerned or affected other roles), and gambling companies use female celebrities to promote their products across a range of social media platforms [21, 22].

Social media sites are increasingly used by the gambling industry to promote their products, including on sites that are popular with young women, such as Instagram and TikTok. TikTok promotions from Australian gambling companies have featured young women (including ex-athletes and influencers) and focused on a range of betting events that may have additional appeal to women [21]. These include markets that align with women’s interests such as the Spring Racing Carnival, reality television programs, global events, and award shows [23, 24]. These novelty markets may be one key strategy through which online gambling companies engage women, particularly those who may not otherwise be interested in traditional sports betting [24, 25]. Researchers argue that such promotions for novelty bets may also reduce perceptions of risk, as women describe feeling more confident placing bets on entertainment content they are familiar with, as compared to events such as sport [21, 22]. Gambling advertising on TikTok has also been criticised for using similar features to user generated content, which may further normalise these promotions, and make them difficult to distinguish as marketing [26, 27]. These promotions often include company branding, and prompt individuals to explore betting markets, with embedded links to download gambling apps. This may reflect attempts to reach new audiences, including young women [23].

Finally, there is evidence that gambling companies are increasingly incorporating gender and equity-focused Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) campaigns into their promotional strategies [28], including sponsorship of women’s sporting teams [29–31]. Gambling companies have shown increased interest in women’s sports, often using ‘equality’ and empowerment framing to position themselves as key actors in creating ‘authentic’ and supportive partners in developing betting markets on women’s sport through the use of sponsorship and ambassadors:“We are proud to have notable talent ambassadors who are supporting women’s leagues and authentically engaging with betting content around women’s sports, and to sustain momentum, we will continue to partner with ambassadors who support and engage in betting women’s sports” ([29], para. 12).

“We are proud to have notable talent ambassadors who are supporting women’s leagues and authentically engaging with betting content around women’s sports, and to sustain momentum, we will continue to partner with ambassadors who support and engage in betting women’s sports” ([29], para. 12).

CSR strategies are well recognised in other areas of health as a way of increasing brand support, and downplaying perceptions of the negative impacts of harmful products on women’s health [32], as well as a way of shaping public perceptions and influencing policy debates [33, 34]. CSR activities include corporate philanthropy, environmental management, supply chain practices, and cause-related marketing [35]. Companies, especially in the alcohol and tobacco sectors, have been known to use CSR to shift attention away from the harmful effects of their products, placing responsibility on consumers instead [36–38]. Sponsorships are a key form of CSR, allowing companies to promote their brands indirectly and associate themselves with trusted institutions and causes. By sponsoring sports events, educational programs, and community initiatives, corporations enhance their public image and build trust among key audiences [39]. Researchers in other areas of health such as alcohol have started to examine how these strategies may be specifically centred on women’s health and equity. This includes alcohol industry sponsorship of International Women’s Day [40] and engaging in ‘pinkwashing’ by supporting breast cancer awareness [41]. One study investigating women’s perceptions of gendered CSR initiatives from health-harming industries found women were concerned about the potential for these initiatives to undermine women’s health [42]. These women called for stronger regulatory controls to limit the influence of health-harming industries. However, there is still a significant gap in understanding how gambling industry strategies intersect with gender, particularly in public health contexts, and how they may be viewed by women.

This study addresses this gap by examining how novel gambling promotions, including CSR initiatives, may influence women’s gambling attitudes and behaviours. The study was guided by three research questions:RQ1. What are women’s views on the influence of novel gambling promotions and public relations strategies?RQ2. How do women think novel gambling promotions and public relations strategies may influence other women’s gambling attitudes and behaviours?RQ3. How do novel gambling promotions and public relations strategies shape women’s perceptions of risk and contribute to gambling harm?

Methods

Approach

This research was part of a broader study investigating the impact of gambling marketing on younger women’s gambling attitudes and behaviours. It involved an online qualitative survey with n = 525 younger women from the Australian state of Victoria. Qualitatively focused online surveys are an innovative method used in qualitative studies that provide an accessible format for individuals to engage in sharing qualitative insights and opinions [43, 44]. Benefits include reaching individuals who may be reluctant or unable to participate in in-depth interviews [45], and addressing power imbalances between researchers and the participant through the anonymous nature of data collection [43, 46]. The use of an anonymous, self-administered online survey helps to reduce social desirability pressures and supports more open and honest reporting [44]. These types of surveys also enable participants to share their opinions at a time and place convenient to them and provide data that can be used for policy impact by generating detailed lived experience insights with the robust sample sizes needed to lend credibility to qualitative research for policy makers [44]. We acknowledge our position as public health researchers that recognise that the gambling industry is a profit driven industry and that a range of individual, social, environmental, commercial, and political determinants influence gambling behaviours and risk of harm. The authors identify as female, and three are young women living in Victoria.

Low risk ethical approval was received from Deakin University prior to data collection commencing (HEAG-H 84_2024).

Sample and recruitment

Participants were recruited through Qualtrics, an online survey company. The study recruited n = 525 women (18 to 40 years) living in Victoria, with soft quotas for diversity in age. This age range was chosen to reflect the study’s focus on younger women and to align with similar studies investigating young women’s engagement with gambling [20, 22]. The age range was designed to capture younger women at different life stages and with varying levels of gambling knowledge and experience. The upper age limit of 40 years selected to maintain the focus on younger adulthood while capturing the diversity of experiences among women approaching mid-life. Victoria was selected as the study setting due to the geographic scope specified by the funding body and because Victoria shares many features of online sports and horse betting with other Australian states, including access to the same major online gambling operators and exposure to nationally regulated gambling advertising on digital and social media platforms [47]. As a result, findings are likely to be relevant to other Australian contexts with similar online gambling advertising environments. Victoria also has a distinctive gambling environment, with electronic gambling machines embedded in community venues, widespread availability of gambling products, and extensive exposure to gambling marketing through broadcast media, which may contribute to differences in exposure between other Australian states and territories such as Western Australia. Potential participants who met the inclusion criteria and who were signed up to Qualtrics’ partner panel providers were sent out the study details. Women were provided a link to the survey, along with the Plain Language Statement. Consent was assumed by participants’ decision to begin and submit the survey. Recruitment through Qualtrics involves the use of multiple panel providers, which provides access to a large and diverse pool of participants. Panel members are registered individuals who receive points for completing surveys. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the sample size was based on other qualitative online panel studies that have been used to gain insight into gambling attitudes, experiences and behaviours [17, 48]. Given the smaller ‘chunks’ of textual data, this sample size provide enough information power to answer the study research questions [49].

Data collection

Data was collected between the 14th of June 2024 and the 1 st of July 2024. Participants took an average of 23 minutes to complete the online survey. The questions participants were asked and which have been analysed for this paper are included as a supplementary file. They were asked a range of questions about their socio-demographics characteristics (age, education, geographic region, employment, country of birth, and language spoken at home) and whether they had gambled in the last 12 months. The questions for this paper focused on understanding the impact of different types of novel promotions that had appeared in social media posts from gambling companies. This included specific questions relating to the use of celebrities and influencers, novelty betting markets relating to female friendly events, and gambling company alignment with women’s sport and women’s causes. Participants were provided with a brief description about these types of promotions and were asked if they had seen them, their attitudes towards these, and whether they thought these strategies would appeal to and influence other young women. The survey questions were developed based on a scan of gambling related social media content conducted between February 2023 and February 2024. A typology from Thomas and colleagues [19] on gambling marketing strategies used to engage women guided the identification of strategies likely to appeal to women, which then informed questions about participants’ exposure to and perceptions of these types of promotional content.

Data analysis

Quantitative data was downloaded to SPSS to calculate basic descriptive statistics and frequencies of the participant socio-demographic information and gambling characteristics.

Braun and Clarke’s [50–52] reflexive approach to thematic analysis was used to interpret the qualitative data. This involved six phases of analysis. First, the research team familiarised themselves with the data. This was comprised of reading and re-reading the data set as a whole and writing down initial reflections and ideas about the data. To ensure critical engagement with the data, researchers also questioned the data they were seeing including challenging and critiquing any initial assumptions to develop a deeper understanding of young women’s responses. SM led the analysis, with the research team meeting regularly to discuss the data, paying close attention to the specific words participants used. Data were coded according to the research questions with researchers labelling responses with both latent and semantic meanings. In developing themes, the analysis considered patterns in participants’ responses both within individual questions and across different types of promotional strategies. While some survey questions explicitly prompted reflection on social acceptability, themes were developed inductively by examining how participants described the perceived influence of novel gambling promotions on women’s attitudes and behaviours across the dataset. Initial codes were then organised into initial themes. These themes underwent a process of refinement, where they were continually reviewed and adjusted by grouping similar codes together or creating new categories. Additionally, responses were scrutinised to identify any that could fit into multiple categories. Themes were then defined and named to ensure they were distinct from one another. Finally in the last phase, the researchers documented their findings and the constructed themes, ensuring alignment with existing literature and the aims of the study. Quotes from participants were used to illustrate examples of the key themes and concepts.

Three themes were constructed from the qualitative data.

Novel gambling promotions contribute to the normalisation of gambling for women

Participants perceived that novel promotional strategies were shaping women’s attitudes by making gambling seem normal and acceptable. Several participants reported that gambling was now seen as “more normal than before”, with many repeatedly using the word “normal” or “normalise” to describe the impact of these promotions on women’s attitudes towards gambling. This perception was shaped both by the depictions of women engaging in gambling in promotions, as well as the sheer volume of gambling messages women were exposed to that were aligned with their interests. Some women felt that gambling promotions that aligned with women’s interests made gambling “feel normal” and contributed to the sense that it was “something everyone does”. Women felt that these novel marketing strategies were a way to make gambling “be seen as more fun and appealing” and suggested that by making it seem fun it would make gambling “much more socially acceptable” for women. One woman suggested that normalising gambling among women would create a “fear of missing out”:

“Would influence by making it feel normal, fear of missing out.” – 38-year-old.

“Would influence by making it feel normal, fear of missing out.” – 38-year-old.

Promotions that used female celebrities and influencers or were associated with female friendly markets and events were described as contributing to the perception of gambling as a more feminised and socially acceptable activity. Some participants felt these strategies made gambling appear more “attractive” and “appealing” to women. Social media influencers were described as “relatable” and “desirable”, and their involvement in gambling promotions was seen to make gambling seem glamorous and aspirational.

“Depending on what influencers are followed - some women might aspire to have a lifestyle similar to them - and therefore feel more inclined to do what the influencer endorses.” – 29-year-old.

“Depending on what influencers are followed - some women might aspire to have a lifestyle similar to them - and therefore feel more inclined to do what the influencer endorses.” – 29-year-old.

Others were concerned about the impact of such promotions and viewed these as exploitative. For example, a few women suggested they took advantage of women’s social and financial vulnerabilities.

“I think many people are also facing cost of living challenges at the moment and therefore may be more vulnerable to products promoted by people they already follow or admire (I.e women with glamorous lifestyles).” – 38-year-old.

“I think many people are also facing cost of living challenges at the moment and therefore may be more vulnerable to products promoted by people they already follow or admire (I.e women with glamorous lifestyles).” – 38-year-old.

Some participants reflected on gambling’s traditionally male-dominated image and described how novel promotional strategies may be created to shift this perception. For example, a few participants perceived that novel promotions were designed to “normalise it as a female activity” and to create the perceptions of more female-friendly environments to gamble. They suggested that this would reduce the sense of intimidation some women felt about engaging in gambling. This included the introduction of novel betting markets, which made gambling seem more relevant to women and extended the appeal of gambling beyond traditional products like sports and horse racing.“I think this makes gambling appear more 'female friendly' as they are evidently attempting to attract females and make gambling have a broader appeal. It presents gambling as more than just about sports.” – 38-year-old.

“I think this makes gambling appear more 'female friendly' as they are evidently attempting to attract females and make gambling have a broader appeal. It presents gambling as more than just about sports.” – 38-year-old.

Others felt this shift was being reinforced through gambling companies’ strategic alignment with women’s sporting events. A few participants expressed concern that this alignment could change the way women engage with sport. Rather than watching to support athletes or enjoy the game, gambling promotions and partnerships were seen to encourage betting as part of the viewing experience.“I think lots of female sport fans want to watch to support their team, other women, etc., but adding gambling company promotions/collaborations seems to encourage and normalise gambling as a part of all sport viewing.” – 26-year-old.

“I think lots of female sport fans want to watch to support their team, other women, etc., but adding gambling company promotions/collaborations seems to encourage and normalise gambling as a part of all sport viewing.” – 26-year-old.

Lastly, CSR strategies which were associated with women’s causes, such as International Women’s Day or breast cancer charity fundraising, were seen as contributing to a more positive and acceptable image of gambling companies. Participants felt these CSR activities used social causes to appeal to women and to position gambling brands as socially responsible. This alignment was perceived as a deliberate public relations strategy aimed at building trust and increasing brand visibility among female audiences. One participant described it as:"A clear marketing tactic to show that they care about diversity and increasing their social footprint and standing out from competitors”. – 39-year-old.

"A clear marketing tactic to show that they care about diversity and increasing their social footprint and standing out from competitors”. – 39-year-old.

Another woman made a similar point, suggesting that this enabled gambling companies to stand out and have people take notice of their brands:“It’s definitely a marketing tactic, these companies are aware that people will take notice if they are involved with women’s charities.” – 26-year-old.

“It’s definitely a marketing tactic, these companies are aware that people will take notice if they are involved with women’s charities.” – 26-year-old.

The role of novel gambling promotions in encouraging women’s engagement with gambling

Many participants believed that novel gambling promotions could directly influence women’s gambling behaviours. Participants commonly used terms such as “encourage”, “attract”, “persuade” and “tempt” to describe how promotions might lead women to gamble more frequently or in ways they otherwise would not.

Celebrity endorsements and influencer promotions were seen as particularly powerful, with several participants suggesting they could motivate women to engage with gambling products. They viewed these types of promotions as an endorsement and perceived that this would influence many young women to try gambling because “their favourites [celebrities or influencers] are”. These women recognised how influential some celebrities and influencers were to young women and that if they promote gambling across social media it would influence women to “at the very least give it a try if they haven’t participated before”. This was because women looked up to celebrities and influencers.

“If women look up to celebrities/influencers then I think it could influence women who have never gambled before to do so” – 33-year-old.

“If women look up to celebrities/influencers then I think it could influence women who have never gambled before to do so” – 33-year-old.

Participants also commented that novelty promotions could encourage women to gamble on events that they otherwise would not gamble on – such as reality television or pop culture events. Participants stated that these markets might feel more accessible and relatable to women:“I think it could increase gambling because it encourages women to gamble on something they feel they have more knowledge about, or feel that they could provide an educated guess to, which may lead to them feeling as though they have better chances at winning.” – 31-year-old.

“I think it could increase gambling because it encourages women to gamble on something they feel they have more knowledge about, or feel that they could provide an educated guess to, which may lead to them feeling as though they have better chances at winning.” – 31-year-old.

Some participants also expressed concern that these types of promotions could particularly influence younger generations. These strategies were seen as deliberately designed to engage new female audiences by aligning with their interests, making gambling appear fun, social, and low risk. Participants suggested that by reframing gambling in this way, such promotions could lead young women and girls to start gambling, including those who may not have previously been interested. The integration of gambling promotions into women’s sport was also described as a tactic encouraging gambling through association with admired athletes and teams.“Women's sports have become more popular than ever, meaning young girls now have positive role models to look up to. If gambling companies are associated with these athletes, it is likely that young children will see this as acceptable behaviour.” – 30-year-old.

“Women's sports have become more popular than ever, meaning young girls now have positive role models to look up to. If gambling companies are associated with these athletes, it is likely that young children will see this as acceptable behaviour.” – 30-year-old.

Some participants indicated that, regardless of the type of novel promotion, they were not interested in gambling and felt these strategies would have little influence on their behaviour. While these participants did not feel personally targeted or persuaded, a few acknowledged that such strategies might still appeal to others.“As a young(ish) woman, I still think gambling has a long way to go before it’s cool or even acceptable among young women. I really don’t see this tactic taking off and getting lots of young women consistently into gambling - but it may encourage people who otherwise would never have thought twice about placing bets.” – 26-year-old.

“As a young(ish) woman, I still think gambling has a long way to go before it’s cool or even acceptable among young women. I really don’t see this tactic taking off and getting lots of young women consistently into gambling - but it may encourage people who otherwise would never have thought twice about placing bets.” – 26-year-old.

The role of novel promotions in shaping perceptions of risk and increasing harm

Participants raised significant concerns about the potential for novel gambling promotions to lower women’s perceptions of the risks associated with gambling, particularly when gambling was presented in “light hearted”, entertaining, or socially appealing ways. Several participants felt that such promotions could draw in women who might not otherwise gamble, gradually leading them into more frequent or riskier gambling behaviours. Novelty betting markets were viewed as especially concerning due to their framing as fun and harmless, which participants believed obscured the potential for serious consequences associated with gambling. As one participant explained, the normalisation of these forms of gambling could have long-term effects on women’s vulnerability to harm:“It may be harmless to bet on these small activities to increase social interaction, a conversation to be sparked, and more clicks towards the company. However, it will slowly become a norm where they may not be an age limit, and it would indirectly influence people who don't gamble and condone it thinking it's safe. Gambling is addictive, like a drug, end of story.” – 29-year-old.

“It may be harmless to bet on these small activities to increase social interaction, a conversation to be sparked, and more clicks towards the company. However, it will slowly become a norm where they may not be an age limit, and it would indirectly influence people who don't gamble and condone it thinking it's safe. Gambling is addictive, like a drug, end of story.” – 29-year-old.

Some women reported a range of risks from novel gambling promotions increasing gambling, including financial harm and addiction. A few women cited their own experiences with gambling and gambling harm when thinking about the potential impact of gambling companies promoting gambling directly to women. One woman suggested that these promotions might lure women into gambling through seemingly harmless pathways:“It’s a slippery slope. They start with something small, fun, and aligned with your interests, but before you know it, you’re betting on things you never thought you’d care about. It’s dangerous.” – 33-year-old.

“It’s a slippery slope. They start with something small, fun, and aligned with your interests, but before you know it, you’re betting on things you never thought you’d care about. It’s dangerous.” – 33-year-old.

Participants reflected on the potential negative impacts of gambling becoming more socially acceptable for women. Some warned that novel promotions could lead women to gamble without fully recognising the risks involved."It will make it far more common place and acceptable but will also fool a lot of women into gambling again without realising that is what they are doing.” – 32-year-old.

"It will make it far more common place and acceptable but will also fool a lot of women into gambling again without realising that is what they are doing.” – 32-year-old.

There was particular concern about the influence of these promotions on younger women, especially those who were fans of celebrities or popular culture that these novelty markets were centred around. These forms of gambling were seen by women as potential entry points into more serious and harmful gambling behaviours. Participants emphasised that the unique framing of novelty markets, combined with their ease of access, could downplay the risks involved, potentially leading to financial losses and addiction. A few participants explicitly described these promotions as portraying gambling as “harmless”, despite the serious consequences they could carry.

“I think there is a lot of harm in promoting gambling in this way for anyone. It makes an addictive activity appear harmless.” – 37-year-old.

“I think there is a lot of harm in promoting gambling in this way for anyone. It makes an addictive activity appear harmless.” – 37-year-old.

“I think the frivolity of these things makes it appear harmless. It is marketing it as a low-stakes affair.” – 39-year-old.

“I think the frivolity of these things makes it appear harmless. It is marketing it as a low-stakes affair.” – 39-year-old.

“They make it seem harmless and can become a light joke.” – 30-year-old.

Women were asked their views about gambling companies who donated to charities that were aligned with women’s health and promoted this on their social media accounts. When considering if this could outweigh the perceived negatives associated with gambling, many participants were uncertain that it would be enough. While some acknowledged the potential visibility these public relations strategies brought to social issues, others felt that they exploited women’s trust by presenting marketing and public relations as genuine concern. Many participants expressed scepticism that such initiatives could mitigate the negative impacts of gambling.“It will make these companies appear more ‘human’ and ‘caring’ when they’re promoting causes, but this will then make vulnerable women more privy to falling victim to gambling.” – 38-year-old.

Discussion

This study aimed to understand how novel gambling promotions and public relation strategies may influence women’s gambling attitudes and behaviours. There were three key areas of discussion in relation to the research questions.

First, the findings revealed that women perceived that some gambling promotions play a key role in increasing the social acceptance and appeal of gambling for younger women. Participants viewed advertisements featuring female celebrities or influencers as particularly effective in appealing to women. These promotions were described as making gambling appear more relatable, desirable, and socially acceptable. This aligns with broader trends in influencer marketing, which has been shown to shape consumer behaviour among younger demographics [53], and supports recent research indicating that younger people perceive influencer marketing as an effective strategy to increase trust of gambling brands [27]. Younger women believed that the strategic use of well-known women could encourage more women into gambling, reinforcing findings from McCarthy and colleagues [24], which demonstrated that the increasing use of women in gambling advertisements was perceived as a way to entice women to gamble. Novelty betting markets were also perceived to reduce barriers to gambling for some groups of women. This aligns with previous research indicating that novelty betting markets appealed to some young women because they felt more confident gambling on these markets than on traditional sports betting [24]. Signing up to gambling accounts for the purpose of engaging in novelty betting enables young women to receive direct marketing in the form of emails and text messages that provide deals and other prompts to gamble [54], which may further increase young women’s engagement in gambling. This finding indicates that novelty betting markets may be one avenue through which gambling companies are attempting to grow their female consumer base, particularly among younger women who have not traditionally engaged with sports betting. Of concern is that these markets, often associated with popular culture and perceived as fun and familiar, may downplay the risks of gambling. For some women, this may increase participation and contribute to gambling harm over time. Policymakers must take proactive steps to regulate the gambling industry’s gendered marketing strategies, particularly those designed to attract younger audiences. Novelty betting markets, especially when tied to pop culture and celebrities, should be subject to greater scrutiny and regulatory oversight. These strategies form part of a broader pattern of normalising and promoting gambling by reinforcing its social and cultural accommodation [8]. Restricting the promotion of these products, including bans on advertising and influencer partnerships, would reduce the industry’s ability to engage vulnerable populations and help protect future generations from harm.

Second, the study revealed that young women were concerned that novel gambling strategies could encourage gambling engagement among women and girls. Participants described how strategies such as sponsoring women’s sport, partnering with female influencers, or aligning with gender equity causes were not just about building brand image, but about attracting women to gambling products. These promotions were seen as deliberately designed to reach women who may not otherwise gamble, particularly by framing gambling as relevant to their interests, identities, and social lives. Some participants suggested that these tactics could shift gambling from something women may have previously ignored into an activity they would consider trying, particularly when it was presented as trendy, appealing, and accessible. This reflects a broader trend in which changing perceptions of gambling as less male-dominated and more socially acceptable for women may be contributing to increased engagement among some groups [22, 24, 55]. Participants also raised concerns that marketing strategies which frame gambling as glamorous, empowering, or harmless may reduce women's perceptions of risk and increase vulnerability to harm. Promotions that position gambling as a casual or everyday activity were viewed as especially influential for younger women and girls, who may be less likely to recognise these behaviours as risky. These strategies were often perceived as forms of image management rather than genuine support for gender equity, reflecting broader critiques of CSR within the gambling industry [8, 56]. These findings point to the need for stronger regulation to counter the positive messages used by the gambling industry to encourage women to gamble. Disrupting the positive portrayals of gambling is essential to denormalising gambling and preventing gambling harm for women. Regulatory efforts must extend beyond restricting direct advertising to include the more subtle, gendered tactics, such as CSR campaigns and alignments with gender equity initiatives that embed gambling within everyday life and associate it with women’s values and interests. These novel gambling promotions increasingly appear on global social media platforms that operate across regulatory boundaries. Leaving responsibility for regulating gambling content to social media platforms is likely to be ineffective. Platform led approaches that prioritise commercial interests over public health limit efforts to denormalise gambling. Australia has the opportunity to take a leadership role in regulating digital gambling advertising in ways that prioritise public health, with the potential to inform the development of global standards for digital gambling promotion. In addition, there is a need for public education to support women, especially young women, to critically interpret these positive messages from the gambling industry and recognise how the gambling industry uses strategic marketing and public relations to expand its consumer base.

Third, the study revealed that women perceived novel gambling promotions as having the potential to increase women’s risk of gambling harm. Participants expressed concern that promotional strategies that portrayed gambling as fun, social, and light-hearted could lead women to underestimate the risks involved. Women warned that these forms of marketing may serve as a pathway into more frequent or harmful gambling behaviours, particularly among women who may not otherwise have considered gambling. These concerns reflect broader critiques of harmful industries and the ways in which they seek to downplay the risks of their product [42, 57, 58]. The framing of gambling as a low-risk, social activity may be an intentional strategy to appeal to women, who are generally more risk-averse than men [2]. Women were critical of CSR campaigns, especially those that aligned gambling brands with women’s causes. While such initiatives may appear socially responsible, participants argued they could further mask the risks of gambling by improving the industry’s reputation and reducing public scrutiny. The scepticism expressed by participants toward CSR campaigns aligned with women’s causes highlights the need to critique these initiatives as part of the broader marketing landscape. The study findings demonstrate the need for robust regulatory frameworks to monitor gambling marketing practices, particularly as companies increasingly circumvent traditional advertising regulations through digital platforms and influencer partnerships. Regulatory frameworks must also recognise CSR initiatives as a form of indirect promotion and consider restrictions on partnerships between gambling companies and women’s health or equity campaigns to prevent the exploitation of gendered causes for commercial gain.

Limitations

There were a number of limitations for consideration. First, the study focused on women aged 18–40 living in Victoria, Australia. There may be other socio demographics factors that might be influencing women’s attitudes and behaviours, particularly in rural areas or other cultural contexts​ where gambling advertising and consumption patterns may differ. Second, only participants registered to the online survey panel companies had access to the survey. Online panel studies tend to recruit more people who engage in gambling activities as compared to community-based prevalence studies which may have impacted the results. While the sample was not intended to be statistically representative, participants were more highly educated than reflected in Victorian census data. This may mean that the perspectives of women with lower levels of formal education were underrepresented. Finally, while the study focused on novel marketing strategies, it may not have captured broader contextual factors influencing gambling attitudes and behaviours.

Conclusion

This study highlights the role of novel gambling promotions in shaping women’s perceptions of gambling, encouraging engagement, and increasing vulnerability to harm. These tactics may lead women to underestimate the risks of gambling, engage with products they may not have otherwise considered, and be exposed to long-term negative impacts. The findings point to an urgent need for regulatory frameworks that address not only direct advertising but also subtler forms of gendered marketing and CSR initiatives. Importantly, the study suggests that gambling companies may be cultivating new pathways into gambling for women as part of broader efforts to maintain and grow profits. These strategies reflect patterns previously documented in tobacco control, where marketing and public relations were used to normalise consumption and expand new consumer groups. By making these potential gendered pathways visible, the findings provide a foundation for public health responses that prioritise disrupting the normalisation of gambling in women’s lives through regulation, prevention, and education. Public education strategies should support women to critically engage with gambling promotions and recognise the commercial and reputational tactics used by the industry. Protecting women from gambling harm requires a comprehensive public health response that challenges the growing social and cultural accommodation of gambling in women’s lives.
