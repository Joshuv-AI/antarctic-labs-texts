A remote sensing framework for predicting and managing waste generation in tropical highland agroecosystems: case study of land use transitions in cameron highlands, Malaysia
Authors: Siti Norliyana Harun, Ricky Anak Kemarau, Mazidah Mazri, Nadrah Ahmad Damanhuri, Marlia Mohd Hanafiah, Noorashikin Md Noor, Azwani Alias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urbanisation, agriculture, and tourism are rapidly transforming land use and land cover (LULC) in tropical highland agroecosystems. In the Cameron Highlands, Malaysia, these transitions are closely associated with rising volumes of solid waste, yet the spatial dynamics remain poorly understood. This study presents a remote sensing-based framework to predict and analyse solid waste generation using multi-temporal satellite-derived LULC data and georeferenced waste datasets from 2020 to 2024. Annual LULC maps were generated via supervised classification of Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 imagery using Google Earth Engine (GEE), while overlay analysis in ArcGIS Pro 3.2 linked waste data to specific land use zones. Correlation and regression analyses revealed strong positive relationships between cropland expansion and waste generation (r = 0.89, p < 0.05), and a significant negative correlation with forest cover (r = − 0.95, p < 0.05). High-waste areas were consistently located within 500 m of built-up and agricultural zones, particularly in Brinchang, Tanah Rata, and Kea Farm. A spatio-temporal model achieved 92% accuracy in predicting waste-intensive zones. Waste peaked in 2023, aligning with major land conversion trends, highlighting the influence of land dynamics on waste proliferation. This integrated approach demonstrates the utility of remote sensing and GIS for highland waste prediction and provides evidence for spatial zoning, ecological buffer preservation, and tailored circular economy strategies. The framework offers a replicable tool for sustainable waste governance in other ecologically sensitive or rapidly urbanising highland regions.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of anthropogenic land use in tropical highland regions represents a critical environmental challenge, particularly in ecologically sensitive agroecosystems where topographical constraints intensify ecological stress. Recent studies have shown that unregulated agricultural expansion and built-up growth in tropical uplands significantly alter hydrological balance, biodiversity integrity, and waste generation patterns1,2. In regions such as the Cameron Highlands of Malaysia, anthropogenic transformation through intensive agriculture, urban expansion, and tourism infrastructure has accelerated over recent decades3,4. These land-use transitions are accompanied by increasing volumes of solid waste, yet their spatial distribution and intensity remain insufficiently quantified. The interaction between land-use changes and waste proliferation is especially pronounced in highland environments due to fragile ecological thresholds, constrained disposal capacity, and complex terrain that limits centralized waste management systems5,6.

In this study, solid waste is defined as any discarded material resulting from industrial, commercial, mining, agricultural, and community activities. It includes garbage, refuse, sludge from wastewater and water treatment plants, and air pollution control facilities. Solid waste is not restricted to physically solid materials but may also include liquid, semi-solid, or contained gaseous forms7,8. The components of solid waste are diverse and include household waste, commercial solid waste, non-hazardous sludge, conditionally exempt small quantity hazardous waste, and industrial solid waste9.

Municipal solid waste (MSW), a subset of solid waste, refers to waste generated from residential, commercial, institutional, and municipal sources. This includes household refuse, commercial waste, non-hazardous industrial waste, and treated biomedical waste10. MSW commonly consists of food and garden waste, paper, textiles, plastics, metals, and glass, as well as waste from offices, schools, hotels, and other service-based establishments10. It also includes construction and demolition debris, street sweepings, park maintenance waste, and sanitation residues, while generally excluding industrial hazardous waste11.

MSW is inherently heterogeneous, exhibiting wide variability in physical, biological, and chemical properties12,13. Effective MSW management therefore requires integrated processes encompassing waste generation, collection, transfer, processing, recovery, and disposal to reduce environmental impacts14. In Malaysia, MSW monitoring has traditionally relied on statistical extrapolation and point-based audits, approaches that often fail to capture spatial and temporal variability in waste generation15. These limitations are amplified in highland regions, where dispersed agricultural activities, seasonal tourism, and informal land development generate waste beyond the coverage of conventional municipal monitoring systems. Consequently, there is a critical need for spatially explicit and temporally resolved waste monitoring approaches that integrate land-use information with waste-generation data to support landscape-scale policy and planning16–18.

Remote sensing and geographic information systems (GIS) have become essential tools for landscape-scale environmental monitoring. Advances in cloud-based geospatial platforms, particularly Google Earth Engine (GEE), enable systematic land use/land cover (LULC) classification and temporal detection of land transformation at high spatial resolution19–22. Despite these advances, applications linking remote sensing–derived LULC data with solid waste generation remain limited in tropical highland contexts. Existing studies have largely focused on urban or lowland environments23–25, leaving a substantial knowledge gap in upland and peri-urban systems where agricultural intensification, tourism, and informal settlements are primary drivers of both land transformation and waste accumulation26,27.

The Cameron Highlands provide a suitable case for examining these interactions due to their complex topography, pronounced land-use transitions between 2020 and 2024, and availability of annual waste data across multiple activity sectors, including agriculture, markets, hospitality, and packaging facilities. The region has experienced extensive conversion from forest to cropland and built-up areas over the past decade, accompanied by increases in sector-specific waste generation, particularly during post-pandemic recovery periods28–30. However, few studies have integrated remotely sensed LULC classifications with spatially disaggregated waste data to systematically examine waste distribution, intensity, and hotspot development in tropical highland settings2,31. This study addresses this gap by developing a remote sensing–based geospatial framework to predict and map solid waste generation in the Cameron Highlands, integrating multi-year LULC classifications with field-verified waste data. Methodology

Study area and data sources

The study was conducted in Cameron Highlands, a highland district located in western Pahang, Peninsular Malaysia (centroid coordinates: 4°35′55.40″ N, 101°29′07.05″ E) (Fig. 1). The district spans approximately 712 km², characterized by heterogeneous terrain comprising ~ 50% steep mountains, ~ 30% rolling hills, ~ 15% valley zones, and ~ 5% lowland plains31. Elevation varies between 300 m and 2,060 m a.s.l., resulting in strong climatic and ecological gradients. The mean annual temperature ranges from 15 to 22 °C, with annual precipitation exceeding 2,700 mm, typical of tropical montane climates. Pedological and geological heterogeneity was captured using soil and lithology datasets from Department of Agriculture Malaysia32, with sandy clay loam and limestone dominating lower elevations, and granite and slate parent materials prevalent at higher altitudes31. This heterogeneity influences runoff, erosion, and waste dispersion processes, reinforcing the area’s suitability for modeling land use–waste interactions.

Data preprocessing and analysis

Data sources and temporal window

The remote-sensing component utilized Sentinel-2 MSI (10 m) as the primary sensor and Landsat-8 OLI (30 m) as a secondary backup (Table 2). Sentinel-2 offered superior spatial resolution and spectral coverage (13 bands), while Landsat-8 provided temporal continuity and radiometric stability since 201333. The dry season (February–May) of each year was selected for analysis due to minimal cloud interference (< 20% mean monthly cloud fraction; rainfall < 150 mm month⁻¹34. This window ensures spectral homogeneity and minimizes phenological noise.

Atmospheric correction and cloud masking

Sentinel-2 Level-2 A surface reflectance products (Sen2Cor-processed) were accessed via GEE (Version 2025). Cloud and shadow masking combined the QA60 and SCL layers with the Fmask v4 algorithm, complemented by an azimuth-based dark-object projection for residual shadow removal. Landsat-8 QA_PIXEL bands were used to mask clouds and cirrus.

Supervised classification and model parameterization

Training and validation data labeling

Training data were generated via manual digitization using VHR imagery (PlanetScope 3 m, Google Earth Pro) and validated against field photos and municipal LULC records (Table 3). Five LULC classes were delineated: Forest, Agriculture, Built-up, Shrubland, and Water. To prevent spatial overfitting, spatially disjoint training (70%) and validation (30%) sets were produced using a minimum-distance buffer of 250 m between them. A total of ~ 500 validation points per year were stratified by class area, ensuring balanced representation35. Label consistency between interpreters was assessed (Cohen’s κ = 0.91).

Classification algorithm

Supervised classification was performed using a Random Forest (RF) algorithm, which has been shown to be robust to non-linear decision boundaries and multi-collinearity in remote-sensing applications36. Training data were digitized from very-high-resolution imagery (Google Earth, PlanetScope) and validated with field photos and municipal land-use records. Five LULC classes were distinguished: Trees/Forest, Built-up, Planted/Crops, Shrubland, and Bare Ground. To mitigate spatial overfitting, polygons were split into spatially disjoint training (70%) and validation (30%) sets using a minimum 250 m separation between them. RF hyper-parameters (number of trees, mtry) were tuned via grid search within GEE, optimizing out-of-bag classification accuracy.

Per-year accuracy was assessed using independent validation points, reporting overall accuracy, class-specific user’s and producer’s accuracy, and the chance-corrected Cohen’s κ statistic following best-practice guidelines for LULC accuracy assessment35. Only pixels with predicted class probability ≥ 0.85 were retained for subsequent change analysis to reduce misclassification noise. For the present statistical analysis, LULC rasters were summarized to district-wide annual areas (ha) in each class. The processed table contained, for each year, the total area of Trees, Built-up, Crops, Shrubland, and Bare Ground. These were converted to shares of total mapped area (e.g. built_share = Built-up area / total area) and differenced year-on-year to obtain simple transition metrics (ΔTrees, ΔBuilt-up, etc.). This produced a compact LULC time series (Fig. “LULC composition in Cameron Highlands, 2020–2024”) that captures the dominance of forest cover and modest yet non-negligible expansion of built-up and cropland.

Linking LULC and waste at the site scale

Because the LULC maps are essentially district-wide fields and the waste data are discrete point measurements, we constructed a site–year analytical table by merging the waste panel with the annual LULC summaries. For each year, the share of total area in Trees, Built-up, Crops, Shrubland, and Bare Ground was attached to all sites sampled in that year. In this implementation, LULC shares thus represent inter-annual changes in the broader land-use context rather than micro-scale composition in a fixed radius around each site, but they still encode the relative expansion or contraction of anthropogenic land uses over time. The resulting table, referred to as waste_lulc, contains, for every site–year pair, the geographic coordinates, functional zone, annual waste mass, district-level LULC proportions, and derived variables such as log_waste. These records form the basis for both mixed-effects and spatial GAM modelling.

Temporal patterns and descriptive spatial analysis

To quantify temporal dynamics of waste generation, annual totals were computed by summing waste_kg across sites within each zone and year. The resulting year–zone matrix highlights shifting dominance from “Other” waste in 2020 to very large flows from packaging facilities in 2023–2024, with hotels contributing a comparatively modest but persistent fraction of total waste. These temporal trajectories were visualised as grouped bar charts with axes scaled in physical units (kg) for interpretability.

The spatial distribution of waste collection points was first examined using a graduated symbol map, where symbol size was proportional to waste_kg and colour encoded functional zone. This visualisation is consistent with established cartographic practice for quantitative point data and supports intuitive assessment of spatial clustering and zone-specific waste intensity37. MDPI Longitude and latitude were displayed on a regular grid to emphasise the compact spatial extent of the district and to maintain comparability with subsequent model-based hotspot maps.

GAMs generalize linear models by representing the expectation of the response as a sum of smooth functions of predictors, estimated via penalized regression splines (Hastie & Tibshirani 1990; Wood 2017). Variance decomposition across simulations was then used to estimate each predictor’s contribution to model output variability.

Model-based hotspot detection

To identify spatial hotspots of waste generation consistent with the fitted spatial GAM, we computed model-based predicted waste for each site–year and used an upper quantile threshold to classify hotspots. Specifically, the 90th percentile of predicted waste_kg across all observations was taken as a high-intensity threshold, and each site–year observation with predicted waste at or above this threshold was flagged as a hotspot. This choice yields a conservative set of high-risk locations while maintaining sufficient sample size for summarisation. Hotspot prevalence was summarised by year and functional zone as the proportion of site–years classified as hotspots. These summaries provide a distribution-free indicator of how concentrated extreme waste loads are within particular economic activities and years, complementing the continuous regression estimates.

A graduated symbol map of predicted waste, with hotspot observations highlighted in red, was then constructed to visualise the spatial arrangement of model-based hotspots. This map can be interpreted as a smoothed alternative to formal Gi* hot-spot statistics40, with the advantage that it is fully coherent with the fitted GAM: it shows where the model expects elevated waste conditional on LULC and zone, rather than where raw waste happens to be large in a single year. In applications where polygonal neighbourhoods and distance-band weights are well-defined, Gi* or Local Moran’s I could be computed on model residuals to further assess remaining spatial clustering41, but here the coordinate-smooth term already removes the dominant spatial trend.

Results

The accuracy of the supervised land use/land cover (LULC) classifications for the five study years (2020–2024) was evaluated using independent validation datasets comprising 500 spatially stratified reference points per year. Validation samples were spatially separated from training data using a minimum-distance buffer to minimise spatial dependence and reduce overfitting, in line with established remote-sensing accuracy assessment practices35,36. For each year, confusion matrices were generated to calculate Producer’s Accuracy (PA), User’s Accuracy (UA), Overall Accuracy (OA), Cohen’s κ, and class-specific F1-scores, following standard protocols42.

To quantify classification uncertainty and assess temporal stability, nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) was applied to each annual validation dataset. Bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals were derived for OA, Cohen’s κ, and class-specific F1-scores. This procedure reduces the influence of class imbalance, which is common in heterogeneous montane landscapes, and provides more robust accuracy estimates than single-run validation approaches43.

The classification accuracy metrics are summarised in Fig. 2. Figure 2a presents PA and UA for all LULC classes (Agriculture, Built-up, Forest, Shrubland, and Water), showing consistently high-class discrimination across all years, particularly for Forest and Water, which achieved PA and UA values exceeding 0.90. Figure 2b shows class-specific F1-scores, indicating stable classifier performance, with Agriculture and Shrubland maintaining F1-scores above 0.80 despite greater spectral heterogeneity. Figure 2c illustrates the temporal variation in OA with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals, which remain narrow and centred between 0.86 and 0.89, demonstrating temporal consistency in classification performance.

Across the five-year period, OA ranged from 0.86 to 0.91, and Cohen’s κ values ranged from 0.84 to 0.89. According to the classification agreement scale of Landis & Koch44, these values indicate “almost perfect” agreement beyond chance. The macro-averaged F1-score exceeded 0.88, confirming balanced classification performance across land-cover classes despite differences in spatial extent. Spatially explicit LULC maps (Fig. 3a) and interannual class trajectories (Fig. 3b) reveal a consistent pattern of landscape change across the Cameron Highlands from 2020 to 2024. Forest cover remained the dominant land class throughout the study period (> 88% of total area); however, a measurable reduction was observed in 2023. This reduction coincided with the largest interannual expansion of cropland (1,899.88 ha), indicating a documented transition from tree cover to agricultural land (Fig. 3b). The aggregated transition matrix (Fig. 3c) confirms this pattern, with Trees → Planted/Crops (1,027 ha) representing the largest off-diagonal conversion.

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated an integrated geospatial–statistical framework to predict and map solid waste generation in the Cameron Highlands by coupling high-resolution multi-temporal LULC classifications with field-verified waste measurements. The findings collectively demonstrate that solid waste production in this upland socio-ecological system is strongly mediated by dynamic land-use pressures—particularly agricultural expansion and urban growth—embedded within a rapidly changing montane landscape. Similar conclusions have been documented in other tropical highland regions experiencing rapid land-use intensification36,51.

The first objective, classifying and quantifying land-use transitions between 2020 and 2024, was achieved using a remote-sensing workflow aligned with best-practice image classification protocols35,42. Forest cover remained the dominant land type (> 87%), yet year-to-year fluctuations revealed a pattern of progressive encroachment along road-accessible forest margins. The sharpest decline occurred in 2023 and corresponded with a substantial rise in cultivated area (+ 1,899.88 ha), indicating agricultural intensification along forest edges—consistent with multidecadal evidence for the Cameron Highlands. Built-up land increased steadily (7.61% in 2020 to 8.28% in 2024), confirming established associations between highland urbanization, tourism expansion, and peri-urban development43. The spatial configuration of these changes (Fig. 3) further indicates that land-use transitions align with topographic gradients and settlement proximity, rather than being spatially random.

The second objective, mapping and analysing the spatial distribution of waste, revealed a distinctly heterogeneous waste landscape (Fig. 5c). Packaging zones produced the highest waste volumes, in some cases exceeding 120,000 kg annually. These findings corroborate established evidence that agricultural supply chains and processing facilities act as intensified waste-production nodes in highland and peri-urban systems52. In contrast, waste from hotel, farmer, and market zones formed smaller but spatially dispersed clusters, reflecting variable human activity and economic specialization. Similar sector-specific waste gradients have been reported in regional waste-generation assessments across Asia and Africa53.

The third objective, statistically assessing the relationship between LULC and waste generation, demonstrated strong and quantifiable linkages. The spatial GAM yielded high predictive agreement between observed and modelled waste (Fig. 5a), while anthropogenic land-cover proportions were the strongest predictors of waste output. Built-up land shares of 7–8% were associated with waste loads between 550,000 and 1,200,000 kg, and cultivated land shares of 1–3% corresponded to 600,000–1,190,000 kg of waste (Fig. 5b). These relationships support the interpretation that small proportional expansions of intensive land uses coincide with large increases in waste burdens, a pattern reported in montane agricultural frontiers43. The GAM’s performance is consistent with established applications of non-linear spatial models for ecological and socio-environmental prediction54.

The fourth objective—identifying temporal trends and spatial hotspots—was met using time-series analysis and spatial hotspot mapping. Waste generation varied markedly across years, peaking at 1,191,920.8 kg in 2023 (Fig. 5a), which coincided with intensified agricultural expansion and increased packaging activity. Spatial hotspots (Fig. 5b) consistently emerged in the western agricultural belt, peri-urban tourism corridors, and central highland settlements. Waste dynamics remained strongly clustered in areas where land-use intensification, transport access, and population activity overlap. These patterns are analogous to global waste-generation hotspots documented in regions undergoing rapid economic and land-use transitions53.

Taken together, Fig. 5a and d indicate a coherent linkage between land-use change and waste production: (1) land-use transitions restructure the spatial distribution of waste; (2) anthropogenic land uses—especially agriculture and built-up areas—are the strongest predictors of waste generation; (3) waste is highly clustered rather than uniformly distributed; and (4) temporal peaks in waste co-occur with years of intensified land-use change. These results show that land-use intensification in montane ecosystems contributes to waste accumulation, particularly where agricultural production, transport networks, and settlement growth intersect. The integrated geospatial–statistical framework developed here provides a replicable basis for zoning enforcement, agricultural waste management, and mitigation of peri-urban expansion pressures. These patterns were further contextualised by integrating annual waste trajectories with LULC transitions (Fig. 6). The annual waste trends, overlaid with LULC classifications, show a strong geographical association between waste accumulation and nearby anthropogenic land-use categories, particularly built-up areas and cultivated/agricultural zones. In 2021, total waste generation declined to 106,302 kg (Table 4; Fig. 6). Spatially, waste clusters were sparse and less concentrated near built-up regions. This pattern is consistent with documented reductions in tourism and market activity during the COVID-19 period, which influenced solid waste volumes55. The proximity analysis (within 1 km) showed limited waste generation near urban nodes during this period.

By 2022, total waste increased to 719,700 kg, accompanied by expansion in cultivated land from 0.46% to 1.63%. Larger waste-generation points appeared near crop-dominant land parcels, particularly in Tanah Rata and Tringkap. This co-location aligns with intensified agricultural activity and associated organic and packaging waste streams26. Built-up zones, although marginally increased, were also associated with medium waste brackets (1975–4189 kg). Year 2023 recorded the highest total waste (1.19 million kg). Built-up areas increased by 276 ha, reaching 7.20% of land cover. The reduction in cultivated land by 0.48% occurred alongside persistent hotspot locations, indicating continued spatial concentration of waste in anthropogenic land-use corridors.

Across 2020–2024, built-up areas expanded from 5136.32 ha to 5587.96 ha, while tree cover declined from 59,637.11 ha to 59,426.12 ha. These land-cover shifts align with pronounced fluctuations in waste production, including the 2023 peak (> 1.19 million kg). This temporal coupling is consistent with waste increases linked to tourism activity, commercial development, and agricultural intensification15,26. The increase in planted/cultivated land during peak waste years reinforces the linkage between land-use intensification and sectoral waste output, including organic residues and plastic packaging. These results align with prior observations that land development alters waste generation rates, while providing finer temporal resolution for highland settings56. GIS-based zonal mapping indicates pronounced clustering of waste generation near urban cores and intensive cultivation zones, particularly within 1 km of built-up areas. High-resolution remote sensing combined with ground-verified waste points identifies Brinchang, Kea Farm, and Tanah Rata as persistent hotspots. The spatial heterogeneity of waste intensity reflects infrastructural density and activity type, with market zones and agro-industrial belts contributing higher volumes per hectare than forested buffers. Over 70% of high-waste points (> 7610 kg) occurred within 500 m of commercial and agricultural land, indicating strong spatial dependency. This pattern is consistent with urban metabolism frameworks57 while adding a topographically specific perspective for upland settlements.

A spatio-temporal hotspot model integrating LULC metrics, distance buffers, and historical waste patterns was validated against 2024 observations, yielding a 92% spatial overlap between predicted and observed high-waste zones and an AUC-ROC of 0.89. These results demonstrate strong discriminative capability when integrating built-up density, road proximity, and cropping intensity as primary predictors, consistent with approaches used in spatial waste modelling58. Built-up areas, although occupying a smaller proportion of the landscape, are spatially associated with high waste volumes, particularly within 250–500 m of dense activity centres (e.g., Brinchang, Tanah Rata, and Kea Farm), where the highest waste intensities (> 7610 kg) were observed. Cultivated land also coincides with elevated waste volumes across multiple years, consistent with seasonal farming cycles and associated agro-packaging streams6. In contrast, tree-covered areas dominate the landscape and show minimal direct waste generation, while remaining spatially adjacent to expanding cultivated zones. These results support distance-based waste management approaches that prioritize monitoring and enforcement near high-risk anthropogenic interfaces58,59. The identification of waste-generation sources associated with specific land uses enables circular-economy interventions tailored to local activity patterns. Cultivated zones represent concentrated sources of biodegradable and plastic waste, supporting targeted composting and material recovery strategies. Built-up commercial areas, where food and packaging waste are concentrated, support on-site segregation and localized recovery systems, consistent with circular economy approaches59,62. Given the statistical associations between built-up/cultivated lands and elevated waste loads, zoning and enforcement can prioritize high-risk interface areas. Embedding waste prediction outputs into land-use permitting and infrastructure planning provides a mechanism to align waste control with sustainable development goals (SDG 11, 12, and 15) in ecologically fragile highland systems63,64.

Conclusion

This study presents an integrated remote sensing and GIS-based framework for predicting and analysing solid waste generation across a rapidly transforming highland agroecosystem, using the Cameron Highlands in Malaysia as a case study. By combining multi-temporal LULC classifications with field-verified waste data from 2020 to 2024, the analysis reveals strong spatial–temporal linkages between land-use transitions and waste proliferation, particularly the conversion of forest and shrubland into croplands, which significantly intensifies sectoral waste generation (r = 0.89, p < 0.05), whereas the expansion of built-up areas showed a more localized spatial association rather than an area-wide linear correlation. Spatial patterns show that more than 70% of high-intensity waste hotspots occur within 500 m of urban or agricultural zones, while forested areas function as ecological stabilisers with minimal direct association to waste.

Importantly, the results demonstrate that waste accumulation in the highlands reflects terrain-conditioned spatial organization of anthropogenic land uses rather than slope functioning as an independent causal driver. Although slope contributed 14% to variance explained in the spatial GAM, it was incorporated as an auxiliary predictor during LULC classification to improve class separability; its significance therefore represents residual terrain-mediated structuring of cropland and settlement distribution rather than a separate waste-generating mechanism. References to accessibility correspond to observed spatial clustering patterns, as no explicit road-distance or transport-network variable was included in the modelling framework. These non-linear interactions produce distinct hotspot patterns; the lack of statistical significance for built-up extent suggests that land-use function—specifically intensive agricultural packaging—is a more critical determinant of waste generation than the mere physical extent of human settlements. The predictive spatial model developed in this study captures these mechanisms with high discriminatory performance, achieving 92% accuracy in forecasting waste hotspots and offering a practical tool for zoning enforcement, infrastructure planning, and circular economy interventions in montane environments.

Beyond identifying spatial waste drivers, the findings highlight the need for zoning regulations that incorporate spatial waste risk, protection of continuous forest buffers, and decentralised waste solutions such as on-farm composting and targeted packaging recovery. Overall, integrating LULC analysis with spatial waste modelling provides a robust pathway for managing environmental consequences of land transitions in tropical highlands, and future research should extend this approach by incorporating socio-demographic factors, climatic variability, and advanced machine-learning techniques to enhance predictive capability across other ecologically sensitive regions.
