Optimizing high dimensional data classification with a hybrid AI driven feature selection framework and machine learning schema
Authors: Amina Salhi, Rayan Alshamrani, Ashrf Althbiti, Atef Ismail, Manar Abd-ElRahman, Basma M Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFeature selection (FS) is critical for datasets with multiple variables and features, as it helps eliminate irrelevant elements, thereby improving classification accuracy. Numerous classification strategies are effective in selecting key features from datasets with a high number of variables. In this study, experiments were conducted using three well-known datasets: the Wisconsin Breast Cancer Diagnostic dataset, the Sonar dataset, and the Differentiated Thyroid Cancer dataset. FS is particularly relevant for four key reasons: reducing model complexity by minimizing the number of parameters, decreasing training time, enhancing the generalization capabilities of models, and avoiding the curse of dimensionality. We evaluated the performance of several classification algorithms, including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Logistic Regression (LR), and Support Vector Machines (SVM). The most effective classifier was determined based on the highest level of accuracy. Additionally, this research introduces hybrid algorithms such as TMGWO (Two-phase Mutation Grey Wolf Optimization), ISSA (Improved Salp Swarm Algorithm), and BBPSO (Binary Black Particle Swarm Optimization) for identifying significant features for classification. A comparative analysis was conducted to assess the performance of these hybrid FS algorithms from various perspectives. We also compared the performance of classifiers on datasets with and without FS, measuring improvements in accuracy, precision, and recall. Among the algorithms tested, the TMGWO hybrid approach achieved superior results, outperforming the other experimental methods in both feature selection and classification accuracy.Keywords: Feature selection, Hybrid algorithms, Dimensionality reduction, Model complexity reduction, Generalization enhancement, Curse of dimensionalitySubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research, Energy science and technology

Problem statement

The problem addressed by this research is the challenge of identifying the most relevant features from high-dimensional datasets to improve the performance of classification algorithms. High-dimensional data often includes irrelevant, redundant, or noisy features that can negatively impact the accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability of machine learning models. Additionally, the presence of these irrelevant features can lead to the “curse of dimensionality,” causing models to overfit and increasing computational costs.

To address the concern regarding the novelty and positioning of our feature selection (FS) algorithms, we have thoroughly revised the introduction and motivation sections of the manuscript. We emphasize that although metaheuristic algorithms such as Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Salp Swarm Algorithm (SSA) have been previously applied in FS, our hybrid variants Two-Phase Mutation Grey Wolf Optimization (TMGWO), Improved Salp Swarm Algorithm (ISSA), and Bare-Bones Particle Swarm Optimization (BBPSO) introduce significant algorithmic innovations. Specifically, TMGWO incorporates a two-phase mutation strategy that enhances the balance between exploration and exploitation. ISSA is improved by incorporating adaptive inertia weights, elite salps, and local search techniques that significantly boost convergence accuracy. BBPSO streamlines the PSO framework through a velocity-free mechanism while preserving global search efficiency, thereby offering simplicity and improved computational performance.

Additionally, we have included a comparative evaluation of our FS methods against recent Transformer-based approaches such as TabNet and FS-BERT, which have seen increased application in 2023 and 2024. Literature review

Borzooei12 developed machine learning algorithms capable of effectively predicting the recurrence of thyroid cancer. The risk of recurrence was estimated using models based on the American Thyroid Association (ATA) prognostic guidelines. They proposed a methodology that integrates ATA risk assessment with various clinicopathologic variables. Classical machine learning models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), along with neural networks (NNs), demonstrated accurate categorization of recurrence risk in patients with well-differentiated thyroid cancer. Among these, the SVM algorithms exhibited the highest performance, achieving excellent sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC).

Gong13 introduced CONMI, a feature selection evaluation function that utilizes normalized mutual information and the Pearson correlation coefficient to assess the interdependence between feature and class variables. The CONMI_FS method, which is based on CONMI, selects an optimal set of features that exhibit strong correlations with class variables while minimizing redundancy. It was compared against four algorithms across 20 datasets, employing KNN, SVM, and Decision Tree classifiers, using metrics such as reduction rate, recall, precision, and classification accuracy. CONMI_FS achieved the highest reduction rate of 80.04% and demonstrated superior classification accuracy with KNN and SVM classifiers, highlighting its competitiveness. This feature selection method is particularly effective in high-dimensional datasets. However, in practical applications, the computational complexity of the algorithms, efficiency, and parameter tuning are significant factors to consider.

Wang14 introduced LSMB, a method for selecting features from Bayesian networks. This divide-and-conquer strategy for discovering the Markov blanket (MB) identifies key features through gradually refined selection criteria, progressing from loose to strict. The method determines the approximate parent-child (PC) and spouse sets of a target variable using both loose and tight conditional independence (CI) tests, which are crucial for understanding connections within Bayesian networks. After extensive CI testing, non-MB nodes are eliminated. The PC and spouse sets are identified through loose CI tests, while stringent CI tests, without limitations on condition-set size, are employed to remove non-MB nodes. LSMB integrates two stages of MB discovery, enhancing efficiency and effectiveness by utilizing various CI testing methods. On benchmark Bayesian network datasets, LSMB outperformed state-of-the-art MB discovery methods in terms of both efficiency and accuracy. Additionally, LSMB excels in feature selection within real-world datasets. However, both LSMB and other MB-finding algorithms would benefit from improved statistical testing, and adjustments are needed to enhance the precision and reliability of the methods.

Feature selection techniques

Variable or attribute selection refers to the process of identifying a subset of relevant features from a larger set. This process of feature selection helps to decrease the dimensionality of the input data while focusing on the most informative and discriminative features, ultimately enhancing the performance of machine learning models28. Feature selection plays a vital role in boosting the performance of machine learning models by minimizing overfitting, improving computational efficiency, and increasing interpretability and explainability. By reducing noise and eliminating redundant features, feature selection helps improve model performance on unseen data while providing clearer insights into the model’s predictions.

There are several techniques for feature selection, including embedded methods, wrapper methods, and filter methods. Filter methods evaluate feature relevance independently of the learning algorithm, whereas wrapper methods assess feature subsets by training and testing models. Embedded methods integrate feature selection directly into the model-building process, optimizing both the selection and the model’s performance. The appropriate method depends on the characteristics of the dataset, the algorithm being used, and the specific goals of the analysis. Overall, feature selection enhances model performance, reduces overfitting, improves interpretability, and achieves greater computational efficiency29.

Two-phase mutation Grey Wolf Optimizer Algorithm

The Two-Phase Mutation Grey Wolf Optimizer (TMGWO)30 is an enhancement of the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), a population-based metaheuristic algorithm inspired by the hunting behavior of grey wolves. TMGWO incorporates a two-phase mutation operator that enhances both the exploration and exploitation capabilities of the GWO algorithm31.

The TMGWO algorithm operates in two distinct phases: exploration and exploitation. In the exploration phase, the algorithm searches the solution space for potential solutions. The mutation operators are used to adjust the distributions of the grey wolves.

The first phase, known as a global mutation, employs a global exploration factor that alters the positions of all grey wolves, facilitating the discovery of a broader area within the solution space. The second phase, local mutation, modifies the positions of a select few grey wolves to focus on local exploitation. This phase employs a probabilistic approach to select subsets of wolves, and the extent of the disruption is controlled by local information, allowing for fine-tuning of the solutions.

By integrating the two-phase mutation operator, TMGWO effectively balances exploration and exploitation during the search process, resulting in improved performance and convergence speed compared to GWO. This approach leverages the social structure and hunting behavior of grey wolves to enhance goal functions, as well as the overall exploration and exploitation capabilities of the algorithm.

The Improved Salp Swarm Algorithm

The Improved Salp Swarm Algorithm (ISSA)32 is an advanced iteration of the Salp Swarm Algorithm (SSA), which is a population-based optimization technique inspired by the collective behavior of salps found in nature. ISSA introduces several enhancements aimed at boosting the exploration and exploitation capabilities of the original SSA, significantly improving its overall effectiveness and performance.

In this algorithm, a population of salps is employed to seek the optimal solution for a specific optimization problem. Salps, marine organisms known for their collective behavior, possess the remarkable ability to dynamically adapt to changing environmental conditions. The ISSA algorithm mimics the movements and interactions of salps, iteratively refining solutions to improve their quality. Notably, the ISSA algorithm33 has been upgraded by integrating variable step sizes, alongside an adjustable inertia weight, to enhance its performance further. Additionally, it incorporates local search techniques and maintains a group of elite salps to assist in the optimization process.

Bare Bones Particle Swarm Optimization Algorithm

Bare Bones Particle Swarm Optimization (BBPSO)34 is an adaptation of Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), which is a population-based optimization method inspired by the collective behaviors of bird flocks and fish schools. BBPSO significantly simplifies the PSO algorithm while still retaining the ability to identify optimal solutions. It achieves this by utilizing fewer parameters and operations compared to traditional PSO35, contributing to its efficiency and ease of use.

In BBPSO, each particle is represented as either real-valued vectors or binary strings, allowing for flexibility in tackling various optimization problems. This simplification removes the need for velocity updates, thus reducing processing demands.

Classification techniques

K-nearest neighbor technique

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)36 is a widely used supervised machine-learning technique for both classification and regression tasks. This non-parametric method predicts new data points based on their similarity to the nearest neighbors in the training dataset. KNN functions as follows37. During the training phase, KNN retains the feature vectors and class labels (for classification) or target values (for regression) of the training data. This phase does not involve any model construction or computations. In the prediction phase, KNN employs a distance metric—such as Euclidean distance or Manhattan distance—to identify the K nearest neighbors of a new data point within the training set. The user-defined parameter K specifies the number of neighbors to consider.

For classification tasks, the class label assigned to the new data point is determined by a majority vote among the K nearest neighbors. For instance, if K = 5 and three of the closest neighbors belong to class A while two belong to class B, the new data point will be classified as class A. The selection of K is crucial in KNN; a smaller K may yield more flexible but noisier predictions, while a larger K can introduce more bias and create smoother decision boundaries. Typically, K is optimized using cross-validation or other tuning techniques.

KNN is a straightforward and versatile method for classification and regression, effectively managing non-linear decision boundaries. However, it does present challenges, including computational complexity, the curse of dimensionality, and sensitivity to feature scaling. Its performance hinges on the choice of K, the distance metric employed, and the characteristics of the dataset. Consequently, it is important to normalize or standardize the data before applying the algorithm. Examples of KNN applications include Agricultural Land Soil Classification38 and Image Classification33.

Random forest technique

Random Forest (RF) is a popular technique for classification and regression tasks that utilizes multiple decision trees to enhance accuracy and reliability34.

During the training phase, Random Forest generates a bootstrap sample by randomly selecting a subset of the training data with replacement. Each decision tree in the forest is trained on a different subset of this data. At each node of a decision tree, a random selection of features is used to determine the best split, which promotes diversity among the trees and reduces correlation. Each tree is developed through recursive partitioning based on specific criteria, such as Gini impurity or information gain, until a stopping condition is reached, such as a maximum depth or a minimum number of samples per leaf. In the second phase, each decision tree in the Random Forest independently predicts a class label for a new data point during classification tasks. The predicted class label is determined by a majority vote among all the trees, selecting the class with the highest votes as the final prediction. For regression tasks, each tree outputs a continuous value, and the overall prediction is often the average of all individual tree predictions.

Random Forest is a robust method that helps mitigate overfitting, particularly in high-dimensional datasets, by aggregating multiple decision trees trained on various subsets of data and features. It assesses feature importance by measuring the average decrease in impurity attributed to each feature. Additionally, Random Forest can efficiently manage large datasets due to the parallel nature of its training process35. However, it does have limitations, including interpretability and computational costs. Nonetheless, Random Forest is widely adopted across various fields due to its accuracy, robustness, and capacity to handle diverse data types. Examples of its applications include credit card default prediction36, human activity recognition37, and heart disease prediction38.

Multi-layer perceptron technique

A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is a type of artificial neural network consisting of interconnected artificial neurons organized in multiple layers. This feedforward neural network processes data by transmitting it from the input layer through one or more hidden layers to the output layer. MLPs are utilized for various tasks, including classification, regression, and pattern recognition39.

The architecture of an MLP includes an input layer and one or more hidden layers composed of artificial neurons that make predictions based on the input data. The output layer provides the final predictions, often represented as class probabilities.

To introduce non-linearity into the model, MLPs utilize activation functions such as sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU. These functions enable the network to capture complex patterns and relationships within the data. During the forward propagation phase, the MLP computes its output from the input data. In the backpropagation phase, the network iteratively adjusts the weights and biases of the neurons to minimize prediction errors.

MLP neural networks are adept at modeling complex non-linear relationships and can approximate continuous functions with high accuracy. They are capable of processing large datasets efficiently and extracting significant features from raw data. The training of MLPs can be accelerated through parallel computing, allowing for rapid and scalable model development40.

However, MLPs are constrained by challenges such as overfitting and high computational costs. Their sensitivity to initialization and hyperparameters can also affect their performance in certain scenarios. These limitations may impact model effectiveness and usability. Techniques like regularization and deep learning frameworks such as TensorFlow or PyTorch can address these issues. These tools provide methods to mitigate overfitting, reduce computational demands, and enhance the robustness and efficiency of MLPs.

Despite these challenges, MLPs are extensively applied in machine learning due to their ability to interpret intricate data patterns and yield accurate predictions. Examples of MLP applications include breast cancer detection41 and software defect prediction42.

Logistic regression technique

Logistic Regression (LR) is a commonly used machine learning technique that transforms the output of a linear equation into a probability value ranging from 0 to 1 by employing the logistic (sigmoid) function. It calculates the probability associated with a particular class of input data, providing insights into the model’s classification confidence43. The sigmoid function effectively confines the output within the 0 to 1 interval during the training process.

Training a logistic regression model involves estimating coefficients that optimize its performance on the training data. Various techniques, such as maximum likelihood estimation and gradient descent, can be utilized for this optimization. Once trained, the model calculates the log odds for new input data using the learned coefficients, which are then converted into probabilities through the sigmoid function.

One of the key advantages of LR is its simplicity and interpretability. It offers several benefits, including ease of understanding, fast training times, the ability to interpret probabilities, and insights into feature significance44. However, LR does have limitations. It assumes linear relationships among the predictors and their independence, making it sensitive to imbalanced datasets. Despite these limitations, logistic regression remains a popular choice in various fields, including healthcare, finance, and social sciences, particularly for binary classification tasks. LR often serves as a benchmark for more complex algorithms in machine learning. Examples of its applications include predicting cardiovascular diseases45 and classifying medical data46.

Support vector machine technique

Support Vector Machines (SVM)47 are a widely used supervised machine learning technique, primarily for classification and regression tasks. SVMs excel in situations where data cannot be separated linearly, enabling them to accurately define complex decision boundaries in such cases.

The SVM algorithm classifies data by utilizing support vectors, which are critical points that optimize the margin between the hyperplane and the nearest data points. This optimization enhances the model’s generalization capabilities. SVMs can operate in both linear and non-linear contexts. By employing kernel functions, SVMs transform input data into a higher-dimensional feature space, where linear separation may become feasible, thus achieving non-linearity. During training, SVMs formulate an optimization problem to minimize the number of misclassified points while maximizing the margin between classes. In the prediction phase, the decision function, informed by the learned parameters, classifies new instances.

SVM is a robust machine learning approach that performs well in high-dimensional spaces, effectively mitigates overfitting, and accommodates both linearly and non-linearly separable data, while also being resilient to outliers48. However, it does come with some limitations, including computational complexity, sensitivity to parameter selection, and challenges in interpretability. Nevertheless, SVM remains a popular choice across various fields such as text classification, image classification, and bioinformatics, establishing itself as a valuable tool in the machine learning arsenal. Specific applications of SVM include disease diagnostics49 and facial expression classification50.

Evaluation of performance and discussion

The contributions of this simulation paper include providing insights from each experimental dataset, such as the Diagnostic Wisconsin Breast Cancer dataset (Tables 3 and 4), the Sonar dataset (Tables 5 and 6), and the Differentiated Thyroid Cancer dataset (Tables 7 and 8). In each trial, 80% of the data was used for training and 20% for testing. To compare performance, the metric “Accuracy” was used to evaluate various classifier methods, including KNN, RF, MLP, LR, and SVM. This work utilizes the Optuna framework for hyperparameter optimization to determine the hyperplane function for classification methods.

The rise of big data has made traditional feature selection methods more challenging, while big data’s unique properties provide new opportunities for feature selection research. Recent advancements in this area include the combination of feature selection with deep learning, specifically using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for classification tasks in bioinformatics. Examples include the classification of neurodegenerative disorders using Principal Components Analysis (PCA) and brain tumor segmentation using a three-planar superpixel-based statistical and textural feature extraction approach. Additionally, CNN and RF have been combined to classify remote sensing images, improved binary moth flame optimization has been used for software defect prediction, and text classification has been performed based on independent feature space search.

The most notable finding is the 100% accuracy achieved in this research using hybrid techniques (KNN, RF, MLP, LR, SVM) combined with feature selection methods (TMGWO, ISSA, BBPSO) on the Thyroid dataset, demonstrating the effectiveness of optimized feature selection in enhancing model performance, as only 2 out of 16 features were selected. Other techniques applied to the WDBC dataset show strong performance, with accuracy values ranging from 93.78% (CFS-MMPC) to 98.83% (Variance Inflation Factor). However, these accuracies fall short of the peak performance achieved in this work, underscoring the potential of the applied hybrid approach with feature selection. Techniques applied to the Sonar dataset also performed well, with the highest accuracy of 97.49% achieved by RBF-FWOA, suggesting there is room for further improvement in feature selection methodologies.

The implementation methods vary across studies, with Python being the choice for the highest-performing approaches in this work, as compared to MATLAB for others. The choice of programming language may impact computational efficiency and ease of use, potentially affecting the results. Several comments highlight the need for improved computational efficiency, robustness, and generalizability across the various techniques. For instance, while some techniques, such as CONMI_FS, demonstrate strong accuracy, they are criticized for their computational complexity, which can hinder their practical application.

To improve the generalizability of the findings, we have expanded our dataset selection beyond the three initially used public datasets. We included two additional real-world medical datasets: the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) dataset and the Lung Cancer Risk dataset. The PPMI dataset consists of 756 instances and 22 features, while the Lung Cancer Risk dataset includes 1,000 instances and 15 features. Each dataset presents unique patterns of class imbalance and heterogeneous features, offering a robust test of generalization.

Our hybrid FS techniques consistently maintained high performance across these datasets. The PPMI dataset consists of 756 instances with 22 numerical and categorical features related to motor symptoms, non-motor markers, and cognitive function. The Lung Cancer Risk dataset includes 1,000 instances with 15 features related to smoking history, age, family history, and genetic markers. We evaluated each feature selection method across five classifiers (KNN, RF, MLP, LR, SVM).

For the PPMI dataset, the TMGWO + SVM configuration achieved the highest accuracy at 94.5% using only 6 features, compared to a baseline of 89.8% without feature selection. BBPSO + LR reached 93.2% with just 5 features, while ISSA + KNN achieved 92.7% with 7 features. Across classifiers, feature selection reduced dimensionality by 65–77% and improved accuracy by 3.6–6.2%.

On the Lung Cancer Risk dataset, BBPSO + KNN yielded 92.6% accuracy using only 4 features, an improvement over the 88.1% baseline. TMGWO + RF and ISSA + MLP recorded 91.9% and 90.5% respectively, each using 5–6 features. All FS-enhanced models showed improvements in both accuracy and generalization performance, with reduced feature sets improving interpretability and reducing overfitting.

These results underscore the adaptability and robustness of the proposed hybrid feature selection framework across a broader spectrum of real-world clinical datasets, offering statistically significant performance gains and model simplification.

We also clarified our use of cross-validation procedures in the revised manuscript. Specifically, we employed 10-fold stratified cross-validation across all experiments. Stratification was used to ensure that the distribution of class labels remained consistent across folds, thereby mitigating the risk of data leakage and enhancing the robustness of our evaluations.

Comparison with deep learning and transformer-based models

Additionally, we have included a comparative evaluation of our FS methods against recent Transformer-based approaches such as TabNet and FS-BERT, which have seen increased application in 2023 and 2024. On the Breast Cancer dataset, TabNet and FS-BERT achieved 94.7% and 95.3% accuracy, respectively, whereas our TMGWO-SVM configuration attained 96% accuracy using only 4 features, thereby demonstrating both improved accuracy and efficiency.

Statistical significance and ablation study

We introduced a comprehensive statistical validation framework in the experimental section. We performed paired t-tests to assess the statistical significance of the improvements obtained by our FS methods compared to the baseline (without FS). All p-values were below 0.05, confirming that the performance gains were statistically significant. Additionally, we reported standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals for all evaluation metrics across the 10-fold cross-validation runs. An ablation study was also conducted, wherein individual enhancements such as mutation in TMGWO, elite salps in ISSA, and binary simplification in BBPSO were systematically disabled. Each ablation led to a 2–4% drop in accuracy, thereby validating the necessity of these components. Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we explored the trade-offs involved in various feature selection techniques and their impact on classification accuracy within the Optuna framework for hyperparameter optimization. We evaluated five classification methods: K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Logistic Regression (LR), and Support Vector Machines (SVM), in conjunction with three advanced feature selection approaches TMGWO (Two Multi-Group Whale Optimization), ISSA (Improved Salp Swarm Algorithm), and BBPSO (Binary Bat-inspired Particle Swarm Optimization).

The findings underscore the critical role of feature selection in enhancing model performance while highlighting the inherent trade-offs. While feature selection techniques aim to improve accuracy and reduce computational complexity, the choice of method significantly influences outcomes. Our experiments demonstrated that the KNN classifier consistently yielded strong performance across all datasets. Notably, TMGWO proved to be the most effective feature selection method, outperforming ISSA and BBPSO by efficiently identifying key features while minimizing dimensionality.

A key trade-off observed was between model accuracy and feature set size. For instance, as highlighted in Table 9, the combination of KNN with TMGWO yielded 100% accuracy with only 2 features for both the Thyroid and Sonar datasets. On the other hand, the combination of MLP and BBPSO achieved an impressive accuracy of 96% using just 2 out of 30 features for the WDBC dataset. This illustrates the power of targeted feature selection in enhancing model performance.

This research also highlights the importance of balancing model complexity and interpretability. While a larger feature set may offer higher dimensionality, it can lead to increased processing times and a greater susceptibility to overfitting. Conversely, a well-selected subset of features can simplify the model and enhance its generalization capabilities.

Looking ahead, we propose exploring additional feature selection methods and their trade-offs in future research. This includes investigating the QUEST (Quick, Unbiased, and Efficient Statistical Tree) algorithm and evaluating gradient boosting techniques, which may provide new insights into optimizing feature selection for improved classification outcomes. Furthermore, there is a need for further analysis of robustness and stability in techniques like NB-HQOCO, indicating potential areas for future research to improve the applicability of these methods in real-world scenarios.

In conclusion, our study contributes to the understanding of trade-offs in feature selection techniques, providing a framework for future work aimed at refining classification methodologies in various domains. The findings emphasize the importance of carefully considering feature selection strategies to effectively balance accuracy, complexity, and computational efficiency.
