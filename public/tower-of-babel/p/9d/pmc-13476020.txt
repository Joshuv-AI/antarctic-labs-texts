Stent-Related Infective Endocarditis Revealing Aortic Cusp Perforation on Intraoperative TEE: A Case Report Altering Surgical Strategy
Authors: Rajesh Madavathazathil Gopalakrishnan, Dheeraj Arora, Akhil Diwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCoronary stent infective endocarditis (IE) is a rare but serious complication of percutaneous coronary intervention. Extension to adjacent valvular structures, particularly causing cusp perforation of the aortic valve, is exceedingly uncommon. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) may reveal complications not detected preoperatively, thereby changing surgical plans. We report a patient who underwent coronary angioplasty followed by persistent fever and suspicion of stent infection. Angiography detected stent related left anterior descending artery aneurysm and preoperative plan for stent explantation and revascularization was made. However, on the operating table, intraoperative TEE demonstrated perforation of the right coronary cusp of the aortic valve with severe aortic regurgitation. Surgical strategy was modified to include stent explantation, coronary artery bypass graft (CABG), and aortic valve replacement. The stent specimen showed evidence of infection. The patient’s postoperative course was favorable under targeted antibiotic therapy. This case illustrates how intraoperative TEE can uncover unanticipated valvular destruction in stent-related IE and meaningfully alter surgical decision-making. Early recognition, imaging vigilance, and flexibility in surgical planning are essential in management of such rare but high-risk complications.

INTRODUCTION

Coronary angioplasty with stent implantation is a common therapeutic modality for coronary artery disease. Infective endocarditis (IE) of a coronary stent is an uncommon complication, and even more rare is its extension beyond the stented coronary artery to involve the aortic valve cusps, especially with perforation. Preoperative diagnosis of valvular involvement in this setting is challenging due to nonspecific symptoms, limitations of transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), and sometimes equivocal imaging prior to surgery.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), particularly intraoperative TEE, has been shown in multiple studies to change surgical plans in infective endocarditis by revealing hidden valvular damage, perivalvular extension, or complications not appreciated preoperatively.[12] The guidelines for use of TEE highlight that intraoperative echocardiography can refine surgical strategy through better anatomical and functional detail.[3]

Literature reports of aortic cusp perforation arising in the context of IE are uncommon; when described, they typically involve native valve infection rather than secondary spread from a coronary stent.[45] A case of iatrogenic aortic valve cusp perforation post-percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has been reported, but without infective involvement of a stent with extension requiring radical surgical change on the operating table.[6]

Herein, we present a case of stent-related infective endocarditis with aortic valve cusp perforation discovered intraoperatively by TEE, necessitating a shift from planned stent explantation and revascularization to also include aortic valve replacement.

CASE SUMMARY

A 63-year-old male with diabetes, hypertension, and prior smoking history presented with persistent fever of three weeks after PCI with stent placement in the left anterior descending artery. He had recurrent chills, malaise, and weight loss. He was referred to our center as a semi-emergency with a provisional diagnosis of infective endocarditis following PCI. The referral documentation included a TTE report suggesting preserved left ventricular function without significant valvular abnormality, and coronary angiography findings showing a stent-related left anterior descending artery aneurysm. Based on these findings, the surgical plan was for stent explantation and CABG.

The patient was already receiving first-line empirical antibiotics including ceftriaxone and gentamicin from the referring hospital. On admission, given the high suspicion of stent-related infective endocarditis, blood cultures were obtained, and empirical coverage was broadened to intravenous meropenem and vancomycin to cover both Gram-negative and resistant Gram-positive organisms. After cultures grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa, antibiotics were de-escalated to targeted intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam for six weeks postoperatively under infectious disease specialist guidance.

Because the patient was hemodynamically unstable and had ongoing sepsis, surgery was planned on a semi-emergency basis based on the available outside TTE and coronary angiogram reports. A repeat TTE was not performed at our center due to the patient’s clinical status. TEE performed after induction of anesthesia revealed a right coronary cusp perforation with severe aortic regurgitation [Videos 1a and b], findings not mentioned in the referral report, thereby necessitating a modification of the surgical plan.

After initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, the infected stent was explanted [Figure 1], aneurysmal repair performed, reverse saphenous vein graft placed to the distal LAD, and the aortic valve replaced with a 28 mm bioprosthesis. Infected material was sent for culture and confirmed bacterial growth.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was extubated within 12 hours, had a stable ICU stay of 3 days, and was discharged on day 10 under targeted intravenous antibiotics. At follow-up, he remained afebrile with normally functioning prosthetic valve on echocardiography.

DISCUSSION

Our case underscores several clinically important points. First, stent-related infective endocarditis is rare. Most published cases of coronary stent infection involve localized stent infections or mycotic aneurysm of the coronary artery, extension into adjacent cardiac structures, particularly valvular cusps is unusual.[78]

Second, diagnostic challenges are significant. In referred semi-emergency cases, clinicians often rely on outside imaging reports when rapid surgical intervention is indicated. The referring echocardiography report in this patient did not describe valvular pathology, likely due to technical limitations or incomplete acoustic windows. The unavailability of the original TTE loops prevented retrospective verification. TTE often fails to detect vegetations or valvular destruction, especially in patients with prior chest interventions or when vegetations are small or mobile. In this context, preoperative imaging may miss valve involvement or underestimate its severity. Intraoperative TEE, however, provided superior anatomic detail and identified right coronary cusp perforation with severe regurgitation, supporting the literature evidence on its indispensable role in verifying and complementing outside imaging before definitive surgical correction.[45] In one large study of 59 patients undergoing surgery for active IE, intraoperative TEE changed surgical plan in around 11.5% of cases due to findings like new vegetations or extension to perivalvular tissue.[1]

Third, the intraoperative TEE’s pivotal role is demonstrated in this case. Because valve damage was only detected after inspection via TEE on the table, surgical strategy had to be modified from an initially planned stent removal and CABG to an open heart surgery to perform AVR. This emphasizes the importance of having intraoperative echocardiography available, especially in complex infective scenarios. Guidelines and reviews support routine use of intraoperative TEE in infective endocarditis to refine surgical planning.[3]

There are reports of native aortic valve cusp perforations in native IE,[4] and cases of iatrogenic cusp perforation after PCI without infection,[6] but to the best of our knowledge, a scenario of infective stent, intraoperative detection of cusp perforation, and an immediate change in surgical strategy to include AVR is extremely rare.

CONCLUSION

Coronary stent infective endocarditis is rare, and extension to involve the aortic valve cusp with perforation is exceedingly uncommon. Our case highlights the crucial role of intraoperative TEE in detecting unrecognized valvular destruction and altering surgical strategy. Early suspicion, comprehensive imaging, and flexibility in intraoperative decision-making are critical to successful outcomes in such complex cases.
