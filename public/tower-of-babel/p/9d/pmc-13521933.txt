Influence of Southeast China’s seawater chemistry on the performance development of cement-sodium silicate grout
Authors: Hang Dai, Lichuan Wang, Qianqian Wang, Liping Li, Zongqing Zhou, Chunyu Zhang, Tao Bai, Hongliang Liu, Haidong Gao, Haiyan Wang, Bo Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCement-sodium silicate (C-S) double slurry grouts are widely employed for surrounding rock reinforcement and water plugging in subsea tunnel construction. However, their performance is highly sensitive to mix proportions and environmental exposure. The long-term durability of these materials and the tunnels they protect is particularly challenged by persistent seawater immersion. This study systematically investigates the effect of real seawater chemistry from four major sea regions in Southeast China on the durability of C-S grouts prepared with a practical engineering mix ratio. The evolution of mechanical properties, hydration products, and microstructure was analyzed to establish correlations between seawater ionic composition and material degradation. Results indicate that a mix proportion of 0.6 water-to-cement ratio (w/c) with 34 Baumé degree (°Bé) sodium silicate delivered optimal performance across all tested marine environments. Early-age strength and compactness were improved by seawater ion infiltration of 1–10 μm, whereas mid- to long-term degradation varied due to sulfate-induced ettringite formation, linked to differing SO42- concentrations. Optimizing the mix proportion generated a dense matrix that converted expansive ion-reaction products into pore-filling materials, thereby enhancing durability in seawater environments. These findings provide practical guidance for optimizing grout mix design to enhance the long-term durability of subsea tunnels in diverse marine environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-51907-y.Keywords: Submarine tunnel, Grouting, Seawater chemistry, Durability, Microstructure, Mix designSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Subsea tunnels are becoming increasingly significant as key transportation infrastructure that connects coastal economic belts. With China’s efforts to strengthen marine economic development and the Belt and Road Initiative, four major subsea tunnel projects are either under construction or currently under planning, include the Dalian-Yantai Subsea Tunnel connecting the Bohai Sea and Yellow Sea, the Ningbo-Zhoushan Subsea Tunnel in the East China Sea, the Taiwan Strait Crossing connecting the East China Sea and the South China Sea, and the Qiongzhou Strait Cross-Sea Tunnel in the South China Sea. These tunnels are expected to effectively break geographical barriers and significantly enhance regional transportation connectivity1. Among them, the Taiwan Strait submarine tunnel is of particular importance, not only for fostering economic cooperation between Fujian and Taiwan but also for playing a key role in promoting exchanges and collaboration between residents on both sides of the Strait.

However, the geological conditions of submarine tunnels are highly complex and variable, and construction often faces the risk of continuously replenished water inrush, which may cause severe casualties and economic losses2,3. Considering construction cost and environmental protection, cement-based materials are generally selected for grouting and reinforcement due to their cost-effectiveness and eco-friendliness. Yet, under subsea flowing water conditions, conventional cement-based materials often fail to meet rapid setting requirements4. In contrast, the cement-sodium silicate (C-S) double slurry grout system, with sodium silicate (water glass) as an activator, exhibits rapid setting and high consolidation efficiency, making it an ideal material for grouting, leakage control, and reinforcement in flowing and water-rich environments.

Previous studies5–7 showed that scientists and engineers can easily adjust the setting time of C-S grouts to fulfill different engineering application demands by varying the Baumé degree of water glass and the water-to-cement ratio. However, the validation of modulus of water glass is always be ignorant. More than that, there is a lack of systematically investigation on their compressive strength development in different curing conditions at long period. In fact, the C-S double slurry grouts are highly sensitive to environmental conditions, particularly with respect to temperature, curing humidity and the ionic conditions in contacting solutions. For example, it was observed that the hardened grouts instantly suffered spalling and breaking when exposed to air with a relative humidity of 60% at 20 °C, leaving no residual strength5. Previous research further studied the compressive and flexural strength development under different simulated erosion solutions. It was found that the C-S consolidation bodies with high water-to-cement (w/c) ratios (1.1–2.5) suffered serious deterioration due to sulfate attack after 56 days of curing in 3 wt% sodium sulfate solutions5. Subsequent studies showed that deterioration of C-S consolidation bodies was the most severe under single SO42- attack (around 0.1–2.0 wt% sodium sulfate in solutions), followed by combined SO42- and Cl- attack, whereas the effect of single Cl- attack (around 0.1–2.1 wt% NaCl in solutions) was relatively minor8. Thus, these findings collectively indicate that the C-S double slurry grouts are highly sensitive to curing conditions, as well as to the species and concentrations of ions in solutions.

However, current understanding of the degradation mechanisms of C-S grout under real seawater erosion still requires further deepening. First, most previous studies have employed simplified single- or binary-salt simulated solutions to evaluate the durability of C-S grout5,8. Furthermore, research on the durability of concrete under real seawater immersion has been conducted on a large scale, indicating that the combined erosion of multiple ions in seawater generates various erosion products such as Friedel’s salt, Mg (OH)2, and ettringite, which affect the microstructure and mechanical properties. Moreover, as the erosion time prolongs, the C-S-H gel partially decomposes into M-S-H gel9–11. These studies have laid a certain foundation for understanding its basic performance evolution. However, real seawater contains multiple aggressive ions such as SO42-, Cl-, Mg2+, and Ca2+, and the synergistic or antagonistic effects among these ions cannot be adequately reflected by simplified solutions. More importantly, compared to ordinary cement-based materials, C-S grout sets extremely rapidly (< 1 min) and hardens rapidly as well (approximately 3 min), and contains a higher amount of silicate gels. Both characteristics contribute to its unique microstructure. Therefore, research conclusions drawn from concrete studies cannot be directly extrapolated to C-S grout, and further targeted investigations are needed to elucidate its behavior. Second, the durability of C-S grout is highly sensitive to its mix proportion12,13. Previous studies found that the durability of C-S consolidation body varies significantly with the mix ratio in solutions of different chloride ion concentrations14. Thirdly, considering the subsea tunnel construction in different marine regions in China, reinforcement projects with C-S double slurries in different geological conditions are very complex. China’s coastal zone spans three primary climate zones, with significant climatic variations and local economic development, resulting into pronounced seawater composition difference. Therefore, systematic investigations on the long-term performance evolution and underlying micro-mechanisms of C-S grout under real seawater conditions in distinct marine regions should be conducted to provide guidance for the selection of grouting materials for surrounding rock reinforcement or subsequent maintenance in submarine tunnels at different regions.

Therefore, to address the aforementioned research gaps, this study investigates C-S double slurry grouts with w/c ratios of 0.6–0.8 and a volume ratio of cement slurry to water glass solution of 1:1, cured in real seawater samples obtained from China’s Four Southeast Seas. The Baumé degree of water glass was adjusted within the range of 26 to 34 to modify the setting time, and the modulus of water glass was set to 3.3 in all experiments. Subsequently, the mechanical properties, hydration product development and microstructural evolution of C-S consolidation bodies under varying seawater chemical compositions were investigated systematically, providing a theoretical basis for the mix design and application of C-S double slurry grout in the construction of the subsea tunnels across four different marine regions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Seawater

To improve the similarity between experimental conditions and actual engineering environments, seawater used for curing in this study was collected from the four major marine regions in China where submarine tunnels have been planned or are under construction. The samples were obtained from the Bohai Strait (Weihai-Dalian marine region), the Qiongzhou Strait (Haikou-Leizhou marine region), the East China Sea (Ningbo-Zhoushan marine region) and the Taiwan Strait (Pingtan-Fuzhou marine region), and were designated as BS, QS, ES and TS, respectively. Seawater samples were collected from the surface layer at the four sampling sites. It is worth noting that the salinity of seawater is changing with both depth and latitudes. Within the upper few tens of meters of the ocean, there exists a mixed layer characterized by a low salinity gradient, where the salinity distribution is relatively uniform, with an average salinity of approximately 34.7 Practical Salinity Units. Below the mixed layer (at depths greater than about 100 m) lies the halocline, where salinity decreases with increasing depth15. In this work, we focus primarily on the influence of surface seawater obtained from different latitudes (between 20- and 38- degrees north latitude) in Southeast China’s sea on the mechanical development of C-S grouts. Experimental methods

Specimen preparation and mix design

This study used practical engineering mix ratios for the mix design, including w/c ratios of 0.6–0.8 and Baumé degrees of 26–34, while the volume ratio (VR) of the double-slurry grout was fixed at 1:1. The selection of these mix ratio designs was based on the practical applications of double slurry grout in engineering projects for leakage control and reinforcement13, as well as in the Yinchuojiliao tunnel grouting project14,18, where satisfactory water-blocking performance was achieved. In our previous work14,18, using a C-S slurry mix ratio with a water-to-cement ratio of 0.6–0.8, a sodium silicate solution of 26–34 °Bé, and a volumetric ratio of double slurry grout was set at 1:1, the grout setting time could be controlled between 30 s and 47 s, while the stone formation rate ranged from 99.3% to 100%, which fulfilled the engineering demands for grouting and sealing in water-rich strata. However, the construction environment of subsea tunnels presents more severe challenges, including ionic corrosion and insufficient early strength. Furthermore, although the durability of C-S consolidation bodies in chloride-rich solutions was confirmed to vary significantly with mix proportion14, their long-term performance in real seawater environments remains poorly understood.

The experimental design and methods are shown in Fig. 2. The development of compressive strength and microstructure in consolidation bodies with diverse mix ratios was investigated under four seawater curing conditions, aiming to evaluate the impact of varying seawater chemistry on their durability.

Experimental design and methods diagram.

Firstly, the cement slurry was prepared by mixing water and cement, followed by stirring the suspension at 1,000 r/min for 6 min to obtain a uniform cement paste. Meanwhile, the 38 °Bé sodium silicate solution was measured using a Baumé hydrometer, and a certain amount of deionized water was added to prepare sodium silicate solutions of different concentrations. Secondly, the cement paste and sodium silicate solution were mixed and subjected to rapid stirring at 240 r/min for 12–15 s. Rapid stirring was necessary because the chemical gelation time of the C-S grout is very short according to our previous studies14. For standard mold casting, insufficient time could lead to incomplete molding or stratified gelation. Therefore, cylindrical steel molds with a diameter and height of 2.54 cm × 2.54 cm were used, and the molds were tapped 2–3 times to remove air bubbles. Subsequently, the specimens together with the molds were placed in a standard curing chamber (temperature T = 20 °C ± 1 °C, relative humidity ≥ 90%) and cured for 24 h. The standard curing conditions for specimens before demolding were based on GB/T 17671—2021. Finally, the molded specimens were transferred into different seawater samples for curing until the designated ages at room temperature. According to GB/T 50082—2024, during seawater curing, the seawater was replaced once a month.

In the experimental setup, freshwater curing was employed as a baseline reference group, and four marine region seawater curing conditions were used as experimental groups to compare and reveal the effects of different seawater ionic characteristics on the mechanical performance and microstructural evolution of C-S consolidation bodies. The use of freshwater curing as a baseline reference helps to analyze the specific chemical effects of seawater ions, while the main variables in this study are the water-to-cement ratio and the sodium silicate concentration. The specific mix proportions are listed in Table 3. Tap water was used both for mixing and freshwater curing and complied with the “Standards for Drinking Water Quality” (GB 5749—2022).

Results and discussion

Compressive strength

Figure 3 shows the development of compressive strength of C-S consolidation bodies with different mix proportions cured in four typical marine regions and under freshwater conditions. Due to the rapid setting characteristics of C-S double slurry grout, compressive strength often exhibits noticeable fluctuations. However, the data indicated that, at the same curing age, the seawater environment and mix proportion were the primary factors affecting the compressive strength evolution of C-S consolidation bodies.

Compressive strength of C-S consolidation body under different seawater curing conditions at different ages: (a) 28 d, (b) 56 d, (c) 90 d. Subfigures a1-a5, b1-b5, c1-c5 represent different curing conditions: X1, water curing; X2, BS seawater curing; X3, QS seawater curing; X4, ES seawater curing; X5, TS seawater curing.

Under freshwater curing (X1), C-S consolidation bodies with a w/c of 0.6 showed a continuous strength growth trend up to 90 days, while other mix proportions maintained increasing strength up to 56 days but exhibited a strength decline at 90 days. In particular, samples with low Baumé (26°Bé) and high w/c (0.8) showed pronounced strength reduction at 90 days. This decline was likely due to the initially higher porosity in high w/c grouts, which diluted the reactant concentration and slowed down later-stage hydration19,20. In addition, long-term freshwater curing can lead to surface decalcification, which suppresses continuous calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) gel formation, hinders further pore filling, and causes strength reduction14.

Under seawater curing, at 28 days, all specimens showed higher compressive strength than the corresponding freshwater controls, especially for samples cured in BS and ES seawater. The reason could be related to the micropore filling effect and will be discussed in detail in Sect. 3.3. As the curing age increased to 56 days and beyond, differences in seawater ionic composition and concentration among marine regions caused varying impacts on mechanical performance of consolidation bodies, showing more complex trends than the continuous growth observed in freshwater specimens. Specifically, while all specimens cured in BS seawater for 28 days showed clear strength improvement, those with low Baumé (especially 26 °Bé) experienced significant mechanical degradation at longer ages (56 and 90 days). As shown in Fig. 3(b2) and (c2) of the BS seawater curing diagram, their strength dropped to 0 MPa due to peripheral cracking under prolonged BS seawater exposure, resulting in a complete loss of structural integrity.

Compared with freshwater curing, all QS seawater-cured specimens (X3) at 28 days showed some improvement in compressive strength. In particular, specimens with w/c of 0.6 and 0.7 exhibited increasing compressive strength with higher Baumé, especially the 34°Bé samples, whose strength exceeded that of freshwater controls at the same age. Specimens with w/c of 0.8 exhibited a significant strength reduction at 56 days, but recovered by 90 days, eventually exceeding the corresponding freshwater controls.

Specimens cured in ES seawater (X4) showed significantly higher compressive strength than freshwater specimens at 28 days. There was almost no strength loss after curing for 56 or 90 days. In particular, some samples with high Baumé mix proportions maintained higher strength than both the freshwater controls and the other samples. However, the 26°Bé & 0.6 w/c specimens lost strength after 90 days.

Under TS seawater curing (X5), the compressive strength of specimens still increased up to 56 days. Notably, the 26°Bé & 0.6 w/c specimens lost strength at 90 days, which was consistent with the results in ES and BS seawaters. These results indicated that, in submarine tunnel construction, the sodium silicate concentration in C-S double slurry grout should not be set below 26°Bé. These trends were closely related to seawater salinity and ionic composition in each marine region.

Therefore, based on the above results, the optimal mix ratios in BS and ES seawaters are 30°Bé & 0.7 w/c and 34°Bé & 0.6 w/c. In QS and TS seawater, the 34°Bé & 0.6 w/c specimens always maintained relatively high strength, which could be the best mix ratio for C-S grouts. Interestingly, the optimal mix ratios in seawater conditions are the same as those in semi-saturated or saturated chloride salt curing conditions, which are 30°Bé & 0.7 w/c and 34°Bé & 0.6 w/c14.

In order to understand the effects of mix ratio on durability development, a statistical analysis using box plots was performed, as shown in Fig. 4. The concentrations of Cl- and SO42- were assumed to be zero for freshwater. According to Fig. 4(a1) - (c1), the average compressive strength of C-S consolidation bodies showed a systematic increasing trend with increasing Baumé degree across different curing ages. From Fig. 4(a2) - (c2), the influence of w/c ratio on compressive strength was less pronounced than that of Baumé degree. Generally, samples with low Baumé degree exhibited the poorest durability, while low water‑cement ratio group showed better anti-corrosion performance under all seawater curing conditions. Based on the summarized results above, the mix ratio of 34°Bé & 0.6 w/c can be well-recognized as the best selection for the submarine tunnels construction in the four marine regions.

Further systematic analysis on the influence of seawater chemical composition on the durability of C-S consolidated samples was conducted, with results presented via box plots in Fig. 5. Although chloride ion concentrations in seawater were approximately ten times higher than sulfate concentrations, no significant correlation was observed between chloride levels and compressive strength reduction across all tested seawater samples at any curing age, as shown in Fig. 5(a1) - (c1). In contrast, the samples exposed to seawater with the highest sulfate concentration (BS) exhibited the most pronounced deterioration in mechanical performance during extended curing periods. This indicates that the concentration of SO42- in seawater has a greater influence on the decline in durability than that of Cl⁻8.

Microstructure analysis

Hydration products development

From a macroscopic mechanical perspective, the C-S consolidation bodies with mix ratios of 30°Bé & 0.7 w/c and 34°Bé & 0.6 w/c exhibited relatively favorable compressive strength development under seawater curing conditions. Therefore, subsequent microstructural analyses mainly focused on specimens with these two mix ratios under different seawater curing conditions and at different ages.

Figure 6 shows the XRD patterns of C-S consolidation bodies with different mix ratios under various curing environments and ages. Analysis indicates that under freshwater curing conditions, the main hydration products of the samples are C-S-H gel and calcium carbonate generated by carbonation. In contrast, under seawater curing, sulfate, chloride, and magnesium ions in seawater further react with the hydration products of the consolidation bodies, resulting in a greater variety of crystalline products. In general, more sulfates productions were formed in BS and QS samples with higher sulfate concentrations in seawater. There was no significant difference in XRD patterns of the other two samples compared with the W3 (34°Bé & 0.6 w/c) and W5 (30°Bé & 0.7 w/c) samples.

XRD patterns of C-S consolidation body under seawater curing in different marine areas. (a)28 d; (b)56 d; (c)90 d.

At early ages (Fig. 6a), the formation of moderate expansive products such as ettringite (Eq. 1) or the infiltration of salts from seawater can fill pores and densify the structure21, thereby enhancing mechanical properties. This explains why all samples cured in seawater for 28 days had higher compressive strength than the water cured samples shown in Fig. 5. At mid- or later ages (Fig. 6b,c), hydration products species develop with increasing curing time, especially for the high sulfate seawater samples, i.e., BS and QS samples. In the highest chloride seawater cured samples, i.e., TS, there was no significant difference in XRD patterns compared with water-cured samples.

By 90 days of curing, the synergistic influence of Mg2+ and SO42- in seawater became increasingly evident, resulting in the formation of leonite (Eq. 2), kieserite, chloromagnesite, and M-S-H in BS and QS samples. The formation of leonite inhibits ettringite formation at later ages, which also fills the pores of the consolidation body and suppresses the deterioration of body matrix in BS5 sample. This explains why the BS5 sample (30°Bé & 0.7 w/c) improved its compressive strength at 90 days compared with that at 56 days. However, in all 0.6 w/c samples with higher compactness or 26°Bé samples with less C-S-H formation and lower initial compressive strength of grout matrix, continuous and excessive expansion formation of expansion products may lead to peripheral cracking and structural deterioration when these samples are cured in high sulfate seawater (e.g., BS1-2, BS4 and BS7)22,23.

Among the four marine regions, although TS seawater has the highest chloride concentration and ES seawater contains the highest suspended solids, the TS3 and ES3 samples showed no significant phase changes compared with QS5 and BS5 after long-term curing. This further indicates that under seawater curing conditions, sulfate ions have the most significant impact on the mechanical properties of the consolidation bodies, followed by magnesium ions, while chloride ions exert a relatively minor influence.

As shown in Fig. 7a, after 28 days of seawater curing, the most probable pore sizes of C-S consolidation bodies in all regions were mainly distributed within the gel pore range of 5–50 nm. However, under freshwater curing conditions, the C-S consolidation bodies exhibited significant micropore structures (1–10 μm), which may be attributed to the rapid setting of the slurry that prevented sufficient densification of internal pores24. The cumulative pore volume curves in Fig. 7b further indicated that under freshwater curing, the proportion of harmful pores larger than 1 μm increased significantly, whereas C-S consolidation bodies cured in seawater contained very few harmful pores above 1 μm. Table 4 suggests that during the 28-day curing, water cured samples had an average pore size of approximately 20 nm, while the filtration of seawater ions resulted in an average pore size of approximately 12 nm. Furthermore, no micropores in the range of 1–10 μm were formed in the seawater-cured grout because pores were filled by filtered seawater ions or hydration products, unlike in the water-cured samples. At the macroscopic level, this was reflected by the higher early compressive strength of seawater-cured C-S consolidation bodies compared with freshwater-cured specimens.

Comparing specimens from different marine regions, the most probable pore size distribution curves of TS3 and ES3 were similar to each other and lower than those of QS5 and BS5, while their total porosity was significantly lower. This was because low water-to-cement ratio and high Bé degree mix ratios of C-S consolidation bodies promoted the formation of a large amount of C-S-H gel, in contrast to the poorer performance of high water-to-cement ratio and low Bé degree specimens. This dense gel structure not only effectively filled the internal micro-pores of the material but also served as the main contributor to the early strength development of C-S consolidation bodies. This phenomenon confirmed that the mix design parameters had a far greater effect on the early pore structure optimization of C-S consolidation bodies than the differences in seawater ion composition.

As shown in Fig. 8a, when the curing age was extended to 90 days, the proportion of harmful pores in freshwater-cured C-S consolidation bodies decreased, and the most probable pore sizes were distributed within the gel pore range. This corresponded well with the continuously increasing compressive strength observed at the macroscopic level. For seawater-cured specimens, although QS5 and BS5 both exhibited reductions in porosity and a shift of the most probable pore sizes from transition pores toward gel pores with increasing curing age, their mechanical performance evolved differently, with BS5 showing strength gain, while QS5 showed strength loss. This difference arose because the SO42− and Ca2+ contents in BS seawater are higher than those in QS seawater, promoting early ettringite formation in BS5. The crystallization of ettringite within pores transforms moderate expansion into a pore-filling effect, thereby enhancing compressive strength.

However, by 90 days, the formation of Leonite induced by Mg2+ erosion suppressed further ettringite generation in BS5, thereby mitigating adverse effects from continued expansion. In contrast, QS5 formed M-S-H, Leonite and Dawsonite (as shown in Fig. 10), which can partially fill pores but resulted from the transformation of C-S-H gel into these weakly cohesive phases. Consequently, QS5 could not maintain later-age strength, leading to a decline in mechanical performance. The pore size distributions of TS3 and ES3 changed little, indicating that the variations in their long-term mechanical properties were attributed not only to alterations in pore structure but also closely related to the evolution of hydration products.

SEM

Figures 9 and 10 illustrate the microstructural evolution of W3 and W5 specimens under different curing environments at 28 days and 90 days.

SEM images of C-S consolidation body under seawater curing in different marine areas for 28 d.

In Fig. 9, after 28 days of curing, the TS3 and ES3 consolidation bodies exhibited a dense microstructure with small pores, consistent with the MIP results, and their mechanical performance was much higher than that of W3. In TS seawater, which had the highest chloride ions concentration, numerous small blocky particles were dispersed in hardened paste and filled the micropore, which increased its compressive strength.

Although W5 had a higher w/c ratio, the hardened paste was denser and exhibited higher compressive strength than that of W3 sample. Due to highest sulfate ions concentration in BS, there were a lot of fine particles dispersed in hardened paste, filling into the micropore, which increased its compressive strength. Therefore, these observations corresponded with the MIP results in Fig. 7, which showed that seawater ions infiltrated the micro-pores (above 1 μm) of the C-S consolidation bodies.

After curing for 90 days in different solutions as shown in Fig. 10, the W3, TS3 and ES3 samples exhibited a denser microstructure than the other samples, indicating that lower w/c ratio and high Baumé degree resulted in a more compact structure at long ages. The differences in structural density are closely related to the characteristics of the C-S-H gel within the material. According to the EDS analysis results, the C-S-H gel formed in consolidation bodies with a higher Baumé degree generally exhibits a higher Ca/Si molar ratio and tends to exhibit a particulate morphology, whereas gels with a lower Ca/Si ratio predominantly display a fibrous morphology. These differences in composition and morphology directly influence the mechanical behavior and durability of the material.

Under varying corrosive ion environments, the morphology of hydration products further undergoes characteristic evolution. The high concentration of chloride ions promotes the fibrous and flocculent morphology of C-S-H gels, enhancing the cementitious properties of the specimens. As a result, TS3 and ES3 exhibited higher compressive strength than W3. In contrast, BS5 and QS5 specimens, cured in seawater with high sulfate ion concentrations, developed hydration products that displayed more aggregated particle formation compared with the loosely structured W5. These morphological features contributed to pore filling and improved structural compactness, leading to significantly higher compressive strength in BS5 and QS5 relative to W5. The observed differences in the morphology of hydration products across varying marine environments further indicate that the type and concentration of ions in seawater play a dominant role in governing the microstructural evolution of hydration products.

Appearance change

Figure 11 presents the surface morphology of erosion and damage of BS4 sample after 56 days of seawater curing in the BS marine region. More sample erosion photos are provided in Figs. S2-S4. From Fig. 11, the erosion damage of C-S consolidation bodies was most pronounced in the BS seawater samples. After 56 days of curing, sulfate ion-induced erosion dominated over chloride ion-induced erosion, forming ettringite (AFt), gypsum, and leonite as sulfate corrosion products. Simultaneously, the leaching of Ca2+ from the interior of the consolidation bodies promoted the formation of a large amount of corrosion products25, which on one hand consumed CH and C-S-H gel hydration products, and on the other hand reduced the surface strength and cohesion of the C-S consolidation bodies. Consequently, parts of the samples exhibited non-continuous spalling, and the cracking propagated from the bottom of the consolidation bodies along weak interfaces in the hydration products toward the surrounding areas, as shown in Fig. 11.

Seawater chemistry and mix ratio synergistic effect on the durability

This study systematically elucidates the mix design principles of C-S grouted stone bodies and their performance evolution in different seawater environments from a multi-scale perspective. The mechanism diagram is shown in Fig. 12. The results indicate that material performance is synergistically controlled by internal and external factors: mix proportion serves as the decisive internal factor, in which the Baumé degree of sodium silicate and the water-to-cement ratio jointly regulate the formation quality and distribution morphology of C-S-H gel, as well as the pore structure of the stone body, constituting the microstructural foundation for the material’s erosion resistance. In contrast, the seawater environment acts as an external challenge, in which differences in the concentrations of erosion ions such as sulfate, magnesium, and chloride ions significantly affect long-term durability through chemical and physical interactions with the microstructure of consolidation body26. Therefore, durability assessments based on the target seawater chemistry must be studied prior to engineering applications to achieve precise alignment between material design and environmental conditions.

Multi-dimensional analysis chart of micro-macro properties for different mix proportions in various marine regions.

From the perspective of the mechanism shown in Fig. 12, the microstructure directly determines macroscopic performance. In systems with low Baumé degrees (26°Bé) and high porosity (Fig. 12b), the C-S-H gel has a higher Ca/Si27 and the microstructure is less compact than that in Fig. 12a. As erosive ions rapidly penetrate, they trigger the generation of expansive products and continuous decalcification and decomposition of C-S-H gel, forming a “deterioration pathway” that ultimately leads to cracking and failure.

Conversely, in dense structures formed with high Baumé degrees and low water-to-cement ratios (34°Bé & 0.6w/c) as shown in Fig. 12a, the C-S-H gel has a low Ca/Si28 and the microstructure is dense, so that ion migration is hindered. And limited erosive products can fill microscopic pores, forming an “enhancement pathway” that achieves structural densification and strength improvement. Although the formation of limited erosive products within microscopic pores generates crystallization pressure, the grout matrix possesses sufficient compressive strength to effectively withstand this pressure.

Therefore, this opposing mechanism clearly demonstrates that the fate of materials in erosive environments is essentially predetermined by the quality of their initial microstructure, which is determined by the mix ratio in this study. This research provides theoretical foundations and practical guidance for the mix design and durability regulation of cement-sodium silicate systems under different working conditions.

Discussion

The results in Table 1 show that the physicochemical characteristics of seawater vary significantly among different marine regions. This variation is mainly influenced by the spatiotemporal distribution of seawater and regional economic development. Even within the same marine region, seawater composition differs across locations and seasons. For cement-based materials, these differences have relatively limited effects on performance. Previous studies have shown that traditional Portland cement-based materials form a hydration product system dominated by C-S-H gel and calcium hydroxide (CH) at the early stage of hydration, with a relatively dense microstructure. The preferential reaction of aggressive ions with CH delays their direct interaction with C-S-H gel, thereby contributing to the material’s favorable resistance to erosion17. However, C-S double slurry grout is highly sensitive to environmental conditions. This discrepancy arises from the rapid setting characteristics of C-S grout, which lead to the formation of an initial skeleton with uneven pore distribution within a short time. The rapid establishment of the C-S-H gel network and its subsequent direct interaction with external ions render it highly susceptible to minor changes in solution chemistry8,16. Therefore, studying the spatiotemporal distribution of seawater ions is crucial for revealing the service performance of the consolidation body.

Submarine tunnel grouting environments differ greatly in practice. This study shows that under unconstrained conditions, specimens with low Baumé degree (lower than 26 °Bé) are prone to early cracking in seawater. The deterioration is primarily attributed to the continuous expansive effect of ettringite and the formation of magnesium sulfate, which lead to the leaching of calcium ions from C-S-H gel, thereby reducing its cementitious properties29. However, previous studies indicate that under constrained conditions, the mechanical properties and microstructural pore characteristics of slightly expansive cement-based materials are superior to those under unconstrained conditions30,31. This suggests that durability assessments should not be based solely on free expansion tests but must consider the in-situ boundary conditions representative of the actual service environment.

Notably, the recommended mix proportion identified in this study (34 °Bé, 0.6 w/c) enhances durability in aggressive environments by converting expansive reaction products into pore-filling phases. This mix proportion has been successfully applied in terrestrial projects, such as the Yinchuojiliao Project18 and the Qu’ao Tunnel Section Grouting Project, demonstrating excellent water plugging performance, thereby providing robust engineering support for the proposed design concept. Building on this, the same design principle holds significant application potential and practical engineering value for future water inrush sealing works in subsea tunnels.

Existing research indicated that the transition period of compressive strength for C-S consolidation bodies mostly occurred between 28 and 56 days32; therefore, the durability test period in this study was extended to 90 days to more comprehensively reveal the long-term performance evolution. However, due to experimental limitations, the 90-day curing period used in this study cannot fully represent the actual long-term service conditions of subsea tunnel materials, which typically span several decades or even longer. Nevertheless, the test results indicate that although some mixtures exhibited cracking at 56 days of curing, but overall, the experimental results showed that the impact of actual seawater on C-S consolidation bodies with different mix proportions exhibited a significant cumulative effect, and the deterioration process displayed both long-term and staged characteristics. Future studies should further focus on the volume stability improvement of C-S consolidation bodies of their service performance.

Conclusion

Based on the distinct physicochemical properties of seawater from four main marine regions in Southeast China and prevailing engineering mix designs for cement-sodium silicate (C-S) double slurry grouts, this study systematically investigated the evolution of mechanical properties, transformation of hydration products, and microstructural changes of C-S grouts under seawater exposure. The main conclusions are as follows:

Significant regional variations in seawater ion composition were observed. Seawater from the Taiwan Strait exhibited the highest pH (8.0) and chloride content (18434 mg/L), whereas the Bohai Strait had the highest salinity (29.4‰) and sulfate content (2313.19 mg/L). The East China Sea contained the highest level of suspended solids (2.90‰), while the Qiongzhou Strait displayed the lowest calcium ion content (557 mg/L).

At early ages, the seawater ions filtration and ettringite formation decreased the average pore size by around 40% and mainly filled the micro‑pore size from 1 to 10 μm of the grout, enhancing the compactness and the compressive strength of all specimens. At later ages, the C-S consolidation body formed under high sodium silicate concentration (34 °Bé) and low water-to-cement ratio (0.6 w/c) exhibited regions with a dense and low Ca/Si microstructure, which can convert the limited expansion stress generated by ion reaction products into pore-filling phases, thereby maintaining structural integrity. In contrast, the porous structure formed under low sodium silicate concentration (26 °Bé) or high water-to-cement ratio (0.8 w/c) tends to promote ion ingress and continuous expansion, leading to cracking and strength loss at later ages, particularly under high sulfate environments.

This study was conducted under isothermal, atmospheric pressure, and static seawater immersion conditions, focusing on the effects of seawater chemical characteristics on the performance of C-S grout. However, physical factors, e.g., water temperature and pressure, in actual subsea tunnel environments should be considered in future work. Seawater temperature decreases non-uniformly with depth, starting from the surface (approximately 0 to several tens of meters), then decreasing at a gradient of about 0.05 °C/m in the seasonal thermocline (at about 100 m), and continuing to decrease at a gradient of about 0.02 °C/m in the main thermocline (from 100 to 1000 m), eventually becoming nearly isothermal in the deep water16. Hydrostatic pressure increases uniformly with depth and is also influenced by salinity. Additionally, surrounding rock constraints after plugging in field conditions also exist. These physical factors may all affect the long-term performance of C-S grout. Therefore, based on the current understanding of chemical effects, further physical factors including temperature, pressure, and boundary constraints should be considered together to reveal the volume stability of C-S grout under chemical-physical-biological multi-field coupling conditions. A durability prediction model should be established that integrates mix proportion parameters, regional seawater chemical characteristics and boundary conditions.
