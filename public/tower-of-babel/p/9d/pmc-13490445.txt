3D lithography of diamond thermal emitters for microscale emissivity control
Authors: Zhuo Wang, Fanrong Zeng, Rongze Ma, Jiaxin Tang, Meng Shi, Jianrong Qiu, Bo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOn-demand control of material emissivity presents a compelling avenue for innovating both fundamental research and engineering applications. However, a solution for high-precision, wide range, and multi-degree-of-freedom emissivity modulation remains an unmet challenge. Here, we demonstrate programmable absorptive microcavity arrays within bulk diamond using a minimalist ultrafast laser-induced composite micro-nanostructuring approach. These microcavities incorporate amorphous layers and numerous random nanostructures, exhibiting exceptional broadband emissivity (reaching ~0.97 across 0.25-25 μm). By tuning the structural characteristics of the microcavities, we demonstrate pixel-level microscale emissivity manipulation within the diamond matrix. The engineered thermal emitters enable multiple advanced performances: high resolution, fast response, angular independence, and remarkable stability. These findings unlock diverse brand-new applications, including 3D thermal displays, thermal encryption, and multi-dimensional information recording. Our methodology represents a new paradigm for establishing a versatile thermal radiation management platform in a highly transparent medium, bridging the gap from 2D on-surface towards 3D free-space emissivity engineering for next-generation thermal-photonics technologies.Subject terms: Optical materials and structures, Materials for devices

Introduction

The emissivity of a material, a fundamental thermodynamic property that quantifies its ability to emit and absorb radiation1–4, is traditionally an intrinsic characteristic determined by its atomic composition and electronic structure5–7. This paradigm is most typically embodied by the blackbody, an idealized thermal emitter whose high emissivity arises from the complete dissipation of incident electromagnetic energy into thermal energy, usually implemented within an enclosed, opaque cavity in reality8–11. For centuries, the pursuit of a high-performance thermal emitter has thus been confined to a material science corollary: opacity is a prerequisite for perfect absorption. This “axiom” has rendered the vast class of transparent wide-bandgap dielectric materials fundamentally incompatible with the high emissivity since their intrinsic nature, characterized by propagating light with minimal loss, is the antithesis of the dissipative, localized energy landscape required for a thermal emitter. Therefore, to date, thermal emitter designs have largely relied on either incorporating highly optically absorptive materials (e.g., carbon nanotubes and nanocomposites12–16) or structuring and reorganizing optically lossy materials (e.g., micro-structured surfaces17–21). However, none of the existing technologies offer precise, wide-range, and multi-degree-of-freedom emissivity tailoring in the 3D free space within transparent media, which represents a major research gap in the practice of material emissivity engineering.

Diamond, renowned for its highest thermal conductivity (up to 2200 W/m·K) and hardness (up to 104 HV) in nature, and wide-bandgap transparency from the ultraviolet to the far-infrared, serves as the benchmark for transparent dielectrics and is considered a star material for next-generation chip-based thermal, photonic, and electronic applications22–25. These exceptional properties, however, render diamond an extremely challenging substrate for achieving wide-range emissivity control. Its high transparency is a direct manifestation of its extremely low free-carrier concentration and weak phonon-photon coupling, making intrinsic energy-dissipation mechanisms exceptionally inefficient. Meanwhile, its extreme physicochemical stability prevents the introduction of absorptive impurities via conventional means. Fortunately, compared to common oxide transparent dielectrics (e.g., quartz, sapphire), diamond’s rich phase transition characteristics, in principle, offer a potential platform for the 3D integration of high-performance emissivity-control functionalities. However, the profound challenge is to exercise multi-degree-of-freedom design over energy-dissipation pathways based on localized phase transitions within this transparent matrix, thereby effectively endowing it with excellent wide-range emissivity controllability.

Here, we report on an innovative transparency-to-near-blackbody-level 3D microscale emissivity control (MEC) strategy in bulk diamond by ultrafast laser lithography of periodic absorptive microcavities (PAMs). Unlike conventional approaches that rely on complex structural design or micro-nano manufacturing processes, PAMs are patterned in one step inside a diamond through ultrafast laser-induced self-organized structuring (Fig. 1a), which is a highly efficient and facile tool for fabricating micro-nanophotonic elements in transparent materials26,27. The high optical absorption of PAMs is realized via the synergy of the loss enhancement from the sp3 to disordered sp3 amorphous phase transition and the significantly increased optical path length caused by the interior random nanostructures (Fig. 1b). In our approach, the density (controlled by the period), Z-axis length, and spatial distribution of PAMs can be finely engineered via the laser parameters and writing path (Fig. 1c), thereby achieving selective emissivity tailoring in 3D free space (Fig. 1d).

This approach maximizes the superior physicochemical properties of diamond and directional energy processing capabilities of ultrafast lasers, enabling on-demand, cross-scale 3D emissivity modulation with high controllability, spatial resolution, sensitivity, and stability. All these factors contribute to the distinctive advantages of this method in innovating multi-dimensional thermal irradiation applications unattainable with existing technologies. As a proof of concept, we demonstrate the broad prospects of this MEC platform by implementing 3D thermal displays, multi-dimensional information recording, and thermal encryption schemes, highlighting its transformative potential for developing multi-degree-of-freedom, wide-range, and precise emissivity control in highly transparent material systems.

Results

Mechanism of emissivity modulation in diamond

In diamond, PAMs are realized by leveraging ultrafast laser-induced non-stoichiometric material modification, without introducing any additional lossy materials. The observed “S”-shaped geometry of the periodic microcavities (Fig. 2a) originates from the self-organized interference field formed within the focal volume during ultrafast laser irradiation. As clarified in our previous studies, ultrafast laser pulses-excited multiphoton ionization at the focal center generates high-density plasmas that act as a scattering center28,29. The scattered waves from the center interfere with the obliquely incident light waves around the focal point, establishing a focal spatial interference field in the focal region (Supplementary Fig. 1). The structural asymmetry of the PAMs arises primarily from a seed effect of the already modified region, where the pre-modified zone strongly enhances the local optical absorption, preferentially promoting further modification on the side closer to the previously modified region30,31. Consequently, the observed periodic microcavities exhibit a scanning-direction-dependent tilt rather than a polarization-dependent one (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The resulting products of this laser-induced modification process in diamond are PAMs, whose periodicity arises from the ultrafast laser-induced interference dynamics rather than artificially planned scanning paths. Optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that PAMs exhibit a highly regular macroscopic morphology but contain plenty of random nanoscale sub-structures inside, forming a rough inner surface (Fig. 2b). Our high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) characterization reveals that PAMs are essentially a composite phase-transition structure (Fig. 2c), where an amorphous diamond layer exists between the cavity and the diamond matrix32 (Fig. 2d). More interestingly, the random nanostructures in PAMs are also encapsulated by an amorphous diamond layer (Fig. 2e). No obvious amorphous graphite-like material is observed in HRTEM, which is consistent with the results of Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The emissivity modulation capability of PAMs arises from the synergy of two factors. First, in contrast to the surrounding single-crystal diamond matrix, a wide-bandgap dielectric renowned for its remarkable transparency, amorphous diamond enables significant enhancement of optical absorption, which arises from the introduction of numerous localized states (e.g., defect states, tail states) into the bandgap by structural distortions and dangling bonds33,34. Second, the interiors of the PAMs contain numerous random nanostructures (e.g., cracks, holes, and refractive-index changes). These structures act to scatter, refract, and diffract electromagnetic waves, thereby exponentially increasing their optical path length within PAMs, which can be intuitively presented in the Monte Carlo simulation results (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Note 1). In summary, while phase transition provides the foundational absorption, the microcavity array, arising from self-organized structuring, creates discrete, light-trapping units whose geometry, amorphous layer, and interior structures synergistically yield emissivity modulation performance.

Based on this structural configuration, our theoretical simulations indicate that the emissivity of PAMs should be higher than 0.95 (Fig. 2g), radically modifying the original optical characteristics of the diamond matrix and approaching a near-blackbody level. Experimentally, we characterized the optical properties of the PAMs in the wavelength range from the visible to the infrared regime (0.25–25 μm). Experimental results demonstrate a considerably low specular reflectivity and transmittance (Supplementary Fig. 4), and the accordingly derived emissivity generally reaches ~0.97 in the test band (Fig. 2h), agreeing well with our theoretical simulation. Notably, the emissivity of PAMs is comparable and even surpasses that of commercially available blackbodies (e.g., metal velvet black foil and graphene black paint) in the infrared regime, highlighting the outstanding light-trapping ability of PAMs.

Parametric emissivity control in diamond

Typically, the functional structures employed for material emissivity modulation are artificially designed, featuring parametric details and excellent controllability35,36. Nevertheless, this methodology concurrently introduces escalated structural complexity and manufacturing challenges, particularly problematic for flexible, precise emissivity tailoring in ultrahard materials like diamond. Traditional unstructured diamond phase transition engineering offers inherent simplicity for absorption enhancement, but suffers from limited controllability, impeding precise emissivity control. The PAMs proposed here are essentially a semi-random structure that merges the high-efficiency creation of random nanostructures with the precise controllability of engineered designs. Our approach eliminates the need for specifically designing individual absorptive microcavities, as they form spontaneously through the ultrafast laser-induced self-organization of diamond. The emissivity modulation is achieved fundamentally through control of the structural features of the microcavity array as a whole, namely, its period and Z-axis length. Notably, while optical aberrations provide an initial elongation of the focal volume, they are not applicable to the parametric control of PAMs. In practice, these structural features are mainly tuned by adjusting key laser parameters. Experimentally, we demonstrate that the period and Z-axis length of PAMs are positively correlated with the laser scanning speed and laser pulse energy, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). By adjusting the pulse energy and scanning speed, the period of PAMs can be flexibly tuned from 1.5 to 6 μm (Fig. 3a, left), corresponding to a duty cycle from 0.16 to 0.85, and the Z-axis length of PAMs can be tuned over a remarkable range of 10 to 140 μm (Fig. 3a, right). In principle, this amplitude of manipulation over structural characteristics provides significant flexibility for manipulating the emissivity of the laser-modified area (Fig. 3b).

This emissivity manipulation can be characterized by detecting the radiative temperature of the laser-modified regions filled with differently structured PAMs. Experimentally, the radiative temperature of the laser-modified regions exhibits significant dependence on the Z-axis length of PAMs, where it first increases and then tends to be consistent with the heating plate temperature (Fig. 3c), exhibiting wide-range tunability. Unlike conventional emissivity modulation structures made from lossy materials, which inherently possess a high emissivity, limiting their tunable range, diamond exhibits broadband near-zero intrinsic emissivity (highly transparent medium). Such a low baseline enables a significantly larger emissivity tunable range. Experimentally, we demonstrate that the emissivity of the laser-modified region can be adjusted to a remarkable range from ~0.05 to ~0.97 by tuning the structural characteristics of the PAMs (Fig. 3d). On this basis, we can produce a large number of laser-modified regions with gradient thermal radiation properties in the diamond matrix (Fig. 3e), where the thermal imaging results agree well with both the theoretical and spectroscopic results (Supplementary Fig. 6). Based on these obtained data, a quantitative laser parameter-emissivity relationship link can be established (Supplementary Fig. 7), laying the foundation of PAMs-based in-substrate thermal applications.

Advantageous performances of our approach

The MEC approach proposed in this work combines the favorable performances of both diamond and ultrafast laser micro-nanostructuring. This unique emissivity modulation mechanism offers a series of notable advantages. Firstly, the optical loss of PAMs comes from their interior random nanostructures, which result in highly rough and irregular boundaries. Although the PAMs exhibit a clear periodicity much smaller than most infrared wavelengths, coherent interference and resonance effects do not occur due to the absence of smooth, clear interfaces between the microcavity and diamond matrix. Consequently, the emissivity modulation based on PAMs should be angularly independent. As shown in Fig. 4a, our theoretical calculation indicates that the transmittance of PAMs remains low and consistent at different angles and wavelengths. Experimentally, we demonstrate that the apparent radiative temperature of a 3D MEC architecture remains approximately the same at 12 typical tilted angles within the view-factor constraints of the imaging system, achieving omnidirectional emissivity modulation consistency (Fig. 4b). These results highlight the wide-angle feasibility of our MEC approach. Secondly, ultrafast laser modification in transparent dielectrics mainly relies on nonlinear optical absorption, which allows for achieving considerably higher processing resolution due to the threshold effect of strong field-matter interaction. We demonstrate precise local emissivity modulation within an ultracompact footprint as small as 5 × 5 µm2, enabling pixel-level MEC (Fig. 4c). This dimension is limited by the thermal imager used for characterization. While the ultrafast laser process itself is capable of creating structures smaller than this, current infrared imaging accuracy imposes a limitation on the resolution of the microscale emissivity-modulation that can be experimentally measured.

Compared to existing emissivity modulation schemes, our approach leverages diamond’s exceptional intrinsic properties to deliver superior performance. For example, diamond’s high electromagnetic transparency (leading to extremely low intrinsic emissivity) results in a high emissivity modulation contrast. Thermal infrared imaging indicates that patterned PAMs exhibit significantly high emissivity modulation contrast even at room temperature (Fig. 4d). Additionally, the PAMs are inherently protected by the diamond matrix’s ultrahigh stability, rendering them resilient to diverse extreme environments and external disturbances. We demonstrate that the MEC elements exhibit exceptional operational stability in high-temperature extreme environments. Notably, the emissivity remains nearly unchanged after 12 h of operation at 700 °C in air, and the thermal infrared images of the MEC patterns show no degradation following 12 h of annealing at 1000 °C in vacuum (Fig. 4e). In stark contrast, conventional commercial blackbodies fail under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8). Further experiments show that, although oxidation occurs when the diamond is heated in air (e.g., 800 °C, 1 h), the microcavity structures still maintain a strong emissivity contrast and enable gradient emissivity modulation comparable to the pre-annealing performance (Supplementary Fig. 9). This robustness arises because the PAMs are fully embedded within the diamond matrix, which provides effective protection against harsh conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10), ensuring their ultra-high reliability. Moreover, the minimal modified volume fraction combined with the 3D burial of PAMs within the pristine diamond matrix results in a negligible impact on the macroscopic thermal conduction of diamond (Supplementary Fig. 11), which enables them to fully harness diamond’s ultra-high thermal conductivity, thereby achieving a considerably high response speed to external thermal stimuli. As shown in Fig. 4f, the PAMs exhibit a rapid response time of approximately 200 ms, which is limited by the temporal resolution of our thermal imager. This measured value represents an upper bound under the current experimental conditions, and may underestimate the actual material response speed.

3D thermal radiation management-based applications

By leveraging the exceptional controllability of PAMs, we further demonstrate the potential of our MEC method for diamond-based 3D thermal radiation management in applications such as thermal encryption/anti-counterfeiting and information recording. Generally, there are three fundamental modes of encryption/anti-counterfeiting. The most straightforward one utilizes the ultra‑broadband emissivity of PAMs. In this approach, information can be encrypted within a diamond by introducing a wavelength‑selective shielding layer that blocks light transmission within specific bands (e.g., the visible range) while permitting unimpeded transmission in the long‑wave infrared region (Supplementary Fig. 12). Second, by utilizing ultrafast laser with different parameters, PAMs possessing distinct structural features can be fabricated, with each corresponding to a unique emissivity modulation module. Concurrently, under varying thermal stimuli, a specific emissivity modulation module exhibits different thermal radiation contrasts. These characteristics enable a temperature‑responsive thermal encryption strategy.

As proof-of-concept, we demonstrate that confidential information can be concealed by spatially arranging PAMs with different structural designs. Under visible‑light illumination or thermal imaging at an incorrect temperature (e.g., 40 °C), the thermal radiation contrast corresponding to the designed two PAMs is negligible, and the differences are indistinguishable whether viewed through an optical microscope or a thermal imager, leading to false information “8888” (Fig. 5a, upper and middle). Crucially, the hidden information (“2026”) becomes legible only when the sample is heated to a specific temperature (e.g., 50 °C), as illustrated in Fig. 5a, lower. This behavior allows PAMs to be engineered with much more complex structural combinations, paving the way for multi‑level thermal multiplexing-based information encryption and anti‑counterfeiting applications. As a proof of concept, we rationally programmed structural characteristics of PAMs to encode local emissivity as encrypted infrared information, thereby printing a grayscale infrared moon pattern with high spatial resolution. The pattern is divided into three segments: the maria, the terrae, and the eclipse area, each corresponding to PAMs with different lengths and periods. By optimizing the laser processing parameters, these segments are hard to recognize in the visible image or under incorrect parameters during thermal imaging (Fig. 5b, upper). By applying the correct decryption keys (e.g., imaging temperature of 50–60 °C), the encrypted information (a sequence of moon patterns representing a complete lunar eclipse) can be successfully extracted (Fig. 5b, middle and lower).

The exceptional capability of our approach for precise 3D emissivity modulation enables emissivity programming in free space, thereby facilitating multi‑dimensional thermal information recording. Specifically, by finely tuning laser processing parameters, multiple MEC pixels with distinct emissivity can be created to display radiative temperatures with a linear gradient spanning 16 distinct levels (Fig. 5c). This performance permits pixel‑level 4D information multiplexing, incorporating three spatial coordinates along with the radiative temperature dimension. Utilizing hexadecimal ASCII encoding, wherein each pixel represents a hexadecimal digit ranging from 0 to f, with two pixels collectively encoding a single character, the text message “Multi‑dimensional!” is successfully recorded using only 36 pixels (Fig. 5d). Unlike conventional 2D surface‑based thermal radiation management approaches, our MEC strategy fully utilizes the vertical spatial dimension to achieve genuine multi‑layer thermal infrared information (encoded using a quaternary system) writing and readout in 3D free space (Fig. 5e). This advance substantially enhances spatial efficiency and information density. To the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first demonstration of quantitative-emissivity‑manipulation‑based multidimensional information encoding operations inside a highly transparent medium. These results establish an innovative digital‑printing methodology for fabricating thermally encoded functional structures and offer promising avenues for next‑generation in‑chip infrared device integration.

Discussion

Traditionally, achieving high emissivity relies on constructing 2D micro-nanostructures on the surface of lossy substrates or coating with absorptive materials. Transparent dielectrics, characterized by extremely low optical absorption, are considered to be unfavorable for achieving wide-range emissivity modulation. In this study, we propose and demonstrate 3D lithography of micrometer-scale thermal emitters in bulk diamond. Compared with existing technologies, our approach employs ultrafast laser-induced self-organized micro-nanostructuring to create thousands of regularly arranged absorptive microcavities consisting of amorphous diamond layers and interior high-density random nanostructures in a single step, dispensing with complicated and time-consuming one-by-one structure design or production. The synergy of the loss enhancement from the amorphous diamond phase and the significantly increased optical path length caused by the random nanostructures enables considerably high emissivity (reaching ~0.97) across an ultra-broadband wavelength range of 0.25–25 μm. Driven by various ultrafast laser parameters, the structural characteristics of these absorptive microcavities can be flexibly tuned to tailor the radiative temperature of the laser-modified regions, which enables the construction of functional domains with on-demand thermal radiation properties in diamond. This approach can maximize the favorable performances of both diamond and ultrafast laser micro-nanostructuring to possess a series of superiorities in wide-angle feasibility, high controllability, sensitivity, stability, and response speed, exhibiting great potential in thermal encryption, anti-counterfeiting, and multi-dimensional information recording.

The structures demonstrated here enable precise emissivity control at arbitrary locations within the transparent host, offering unique advantages in multi-degree-of-freedom thermal radiation engineering (Supplementary Table 1). However, as with any dielectric thermal emitter, the externally detectable emission remains limited by Fresnel reflection losses at the material-environment interface, which reduce the effective outcoupled emissivity below the intrinsic absorptivity of the embedded features. Achieving maximal external emission performance in practical devices will therefore require complementary surface engineering to minimize these reflection losses (e.g., via antireflection coatings). The internal volumetric approach presented in this work is intended to expand emitter design flexibility in 3D and can be synergistically combined with surface optimizations in future implementations.

In summary, these findings represent a significant breakthrough in decoupling the emissivity from the host material’s intrinsic properties, where extreme transparency and absorption can be co-located in the same material system and precisely tuned and patterned at the microscale. The revealed mechanism and established methodology are anticipated to facilitate the 3D integration of thermal radiation management systems, with profound implications across diverse applications. In the future, it would be exciting to fuse this 3D emissivity manipulation strategy with advanced energy conversion, lab-on-a-chip, micro-electro-mechanical systems, infrared sensing, and fourth-generation semiconductors to advance innovative devices and functionalities, facilitating the development of next-generation diamond-based thermal technologies.

Methods

Ultrafast laser processing

In this study, commercially available single-crystal diamonds produced by chemical vapor deposition were used in ultrafast laser-induced self-organized structuring. The PAMs were imprinted in diamond by a mode-locked regeneratively amplified Yb: KGW-based ultrafast laser (PHAROS, Light Conversion Ltd) operating at a wavelength of 1030 nm (Supplementary Fig. 13). The laser polarization direction is parallel to the scanning direction. Generally, the laser was focused 60 µm below the surface of the sample via a 50× objective lens (NA = 0.8), the laser repetition rate is set to 150 kHz, the pulse duration is set to 1 ps, and the pulse energy and scanning speed are adjustable to realize 3D MEC. Specifically, in Fig. 4b, the pulse energy is 0.1–0.4 µJ, the scanning speed is 640 µm s−1, and the writing depth is gradually varying from 100 µm to 285 µm to generate the 3D face mask shape within a 1 mm-thick bulk diamond. A single-layer irradiation is used to fabricate this pattern. In Fig. 4c, the pulse energy is 0.1 µJ, and the scanning speed is 100 µm s−1. In Fig. 4d, e, the pulse energy is 0.8 µJ, and the scanning speed is 300 µm s−1. In Fig. 5a, the pulse energy of the area shaped in 2026 is 0.3 µJ, the scanning speed of this area is 500 µm s−1, and the pulse energy and scanning speed of the other area are 0.1 µJ and 200 µm s−1, respectively. In Fig. 5b, the pattern is divided into three segments: the maria, the terrae, and the eclipse area. The pulse energy of maria area is 0.8 µJ, the scanning speed of maria area is 1000 µm s−1; the pulse energy of terrae area is 0.4 µJ, the scanning speed of terrae area is 500 µm s−1; the pulse energy of eclipse area is 0.2 µJ, the scanning speed of eclipse area is 80 µm s−1. In Fig. 5d, the scanning speed is 800 µm s−1, and the pulse energy varies from 0.04 to 2.2 µJ, as illustrated in Supplementary Table 2. In Fig. 5e, the pulse energy varies from 0.1 to 0.3 µJ, and the scanning speed is 640 µm s−1. The line spacing within patterns is 10 µm, and each line is scanned only once. A bottom-up configuration is typically adopted when fabricating 3D structures.

Structural characterization of periodic absorptive microcavities

The spatial morphology of the prepared absorptive microcavities was preliminarily characterized using a color optical microscope (Olympus BX53). For the SEM characterization, the processed sample was first polished to expose part of the microcavity-formed region on the surface (YZ-plane). Subsequently, a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Gemini300) was employed to examine the structural features of the microcavities. For the HRTEM characterization of the amorphous phase transition within the scope of the absorptive microcavity, the processed sample was first polished to expose part of the microcavity-formed region on the surface (XZ-plane). Then, the exposed microcavity-formed region was sliced along the Y-axis using a focused ion beam (FIB, Fei Helios Nanolab G3 UC) to obtain the slice containing the cross-section of the microcavity-formed region on the XY plane. Specifically, the sample was thinned with a gallium ion beam at 30 kV. Then, relatively lower accelerating voltages of 5 kV and 1 kV with a beam current from 15 to 5 pA were used for further thinning to reduce the ion beam damage. After that, a 0.5 kV ion beam was used for the final cleaning of specimens. HRTEM images were captured by using an FEI Tecnai F20 microscope operated at 200 kV.

Spectral and infrared imaging characterization

According to Kirchhoff’s law, the spectral emissivity of a sample is calculated from its spectral reflectance and transmittance. The reflectivity/transmission ranging from 0.25 to 1.1 μm was characterized using an ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared spectrometer (Cary 5000) coupled with an integrating sphere. The reflectivity/transmission ranging from 1.1 to 25 μm was characterized via a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Bruker Vertex 70). The FTIR spectrometer was equipped with an InGaAs detector testing spectra from 1.1 to 2.5 μm and a DTGS detector testing spectra from 2.5 to 25 μm. The specular reflectivity was measured, the measurement angle was set to 10°, and the spot size was typically 6 mm. Additionally, the FTIR spectrometer was equipped with an infrared microscope and a MCT detector, providing a high signal-to-noise ratio and high sensitivity detection in micrometer-scale regions. To ensure the accuracy of measurement results, the instrument is calibrated using high-precision emissivity standard materials. A gold-coated mirror (with a reflectance value of 1) is utilized as the calibration reference standard for measuring reflectance spectra, while air (with a transmittance value of 1) serves as the calibration standard for measuring transmittance spectra. Both the calibration process and the actual testing are conducted under identical instrument settings and the same environment to prevent external environmental factors from influencing the measurement results. To reduce the uncertainty and errors during testing, each spectrum is collected with 32 repeated scans, and we performed three measurements on a sample and averaged the results. Prior to measurement, the sample surfaces were cleaned using high-purity anhydrous ethanol to remove oils and other contaminants, followed by drying with compressed air and placement in a clean environment for standby. The theoretically specular reflectivity, transmittance, and emissivity of diamond with PAMs were calculated based on the finite-difference time-domain method. Infrared imaging of the PAMs in diamond was performed using an infrared thermal camera (FOTRIC 240M) with a focal plane array detector and a primary response band of 7.5–14 µm. The field of view is 29°×22°.
