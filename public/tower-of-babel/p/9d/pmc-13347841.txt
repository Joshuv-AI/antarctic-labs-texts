Finerenone attenuates chronic fibrotic remodeling during the AKI-CKD transition in an ischemia-reperfusion-induced acute kidney injury mouse model
Authors: Ruoshan Lian, Siyi Rao, Zongli Zheng, Sha Tang, Jianxin Wan, Yi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: The transition from acute kidney injury (AKI) to chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major cause of poor prognosis in renal injury, and preventing the progression from AKI to CKD has important clinical significance. Finerenone exerts renoprotective effects, however, its role in the AKI-CKD transition remains unclear.Methods: An ischemia-reperfusion (IR)-induced AKI mouse model was established in male C57BL/6 mice by unilateral renal artery clamping combined with contralateral nephrectomy. Finerenone was administered at doses of 10 mg/kg or 15 mg/kg, and renal function, aldosterone levels, podocyte injury, inflammatory responses, and renal fibrosis were evaluated at days 8 and 28 to investigate pathological mechanisms underlying the AKI-CKD transition.Result: Finerenone significantly attenuated IR-induced renal injury and improved renal function during the acute phase and throughout the subsequent AKI–CKD transition. Compared with 10 mg/kg, finerenone at 15 mg/kg more effectively reduced podocyte injury, dampened the inflammatory response, and attenuated renal fibrosis. Mechanistically, finerenone suppressed the p38 phosphorylation and reduced nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation during the AKI-CKD transition.Conclusion: Finerenone exerts dose-dependent renoprotective effects during AKI progression and the subsequent AKI–CKD transition, potentially by suppressing podocyte mineralocorticoid receptor overactivation and concomitant reductions in renal inflammation and fibrosis, accompanied by attenuation of p38 phosphorylation and NF-κB activation during the AKI–CKD transition.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was historically regarded as a largely reversible, self-limited syndrome. However, accumulating evidence indicates that many AKI survivors experience incomplete recovery and subsequently progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD), and in some cases to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). AKI is now recognized as a major determinant of subsequent CKD and a key driver of the AKI-to-CKD transition. Nevertheless, clinical management of AKI has largely relied on supportive care, symptomatic treatment, and there is currently no effective therapeutic method to reduce renal tissue injury, promote renal repair, or prevent renal chronicity. Therefore, preventing the progression of AKI to CKD transition has emerged as a focus of attention in the international nephrology community [1].

The relatively clear pathophysiological mechanisms underlying chronicity after AKI include microvascular endothelial cell injury, inflammatory response, and abnormal activation of renal tubular epithelial cells [2], but they cannot explain the progressive and persistent proteinuria that occurs during the chronicization of the kidney after AKI. A prospective study by Horne et al. found that persistent proteinuria is often detected in patients after AKI, which is closely associated with subsequent CKD [3]. The occurrence of proteinuria suggests possible podocyte injury and is related to the progression of kidney diseases [4,5]. Our previous studies have confirmed that podocyte injury is one of the causes of AKI and renal chronic fibrosis after AKI [6,7]. Unveiling the molecular mechanisms regulating podocyte injury is of great significance for in-depth research on the development of proteinuria and the effective prevention and treatment of glomerulosclerosis after AKI.

NR3C2 (Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 3, Group C, Member 2) encodes the MR (mineralocorticoid receptor), which is one of the key players in organ inflammation and fibrosis. Through hormone signal transduction and activation of aldosterone target gene expression, MR regulates various physiological and pathological processes [8]. When overactivated, MR induces the transcription of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic genes in various cells, thereby leading to inflammation and fibrosis [9]. Studies have reported MR overactivation in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated with CKD [10,11]. MR overactivation increases NADPH oxidase activity to induce a series of oxidative stress responses and mediates inflammatory and fibrotic processes, resulting in renal lesions such as glomerular hypertrophy and decreased renal blood flow, and ultimately leading to the occurrence and development of kidney diseases [12]. Blocking MR overactivation can inhibit inflammation, fibrosis, and renal injury mediated by this pathway [13]. Finerenone is a non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) developed based on a dihydropyridine structure, with high selectivity and strong affinity for MR [14]. Finerenone can effectively block inflammation, fibrosis, and adverse cardiovascular and renal events mediated by MR overactivation [15]. Basic research has shown that finerenone can block renal injury caused by MR overactivation, delay the progression of kidney disease, and confer renal benefits. A study by Lattenist et al. [16] suggested that preventive MR blockade with finerenone during renal ischemia prevents renal dysfunction and renal tubular injury, reduces the expression of KIM-1 and NGAL, decreases the expression of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and collagen I, reduces proteinuria and renal vascular resistance, and prevents the transition from AKI to CKD. MR is expressed on podocytes, and we hypothesized that during the process of renal chronic fibrosis after IR-AKI, MR on podocytes is overactivated, the expression levels of various inflammatory factors, such as nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and TGF-β, are increased, and the transcription of pro-fibrotic mediator genes is enhanced, thereby causing renal inflammation and fibrosis [17].

In this study, a mouse model of chronic renal fibrosis following IR-induced AKI was established in C57BL/6 mice to investigate the protective effects and mechanisms of finerenone on podocyte injury during renal chronic fibrosis after AKI. We hypothesize that finerenone may improve podocyte injury by blocking MR through the inhibition of p38 phosphorylation and NF-κB activation, thereby alleviating chronic renal fibrosis following IR-induced AKI.

Materials and methods

Materials

Forty 16-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (wild-type mice) weighing 25–28 g were provided by Shanghai Slack Company, with the production license number: SCXK (Hu) 2022-0004. The mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade Experimental Animal Center of Fujian Medical University. Housing conditions were: constant temperature (22 ± 2 °C), constant humidity, and artificial lighting with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness per day. Food and water were available ad libitum. Rabbit anti-β-actin (Immunoway,YM8343), rabbit anti-nephrin (Immunoway, YT3036), rabbit anti-C3 (Abcam, ab200999), rabbit anti-IL-1 (Immunoway, YM8498), rabbit anti-IL-6 (Abmart, TD6087), rabbit anti-TGF-β1 (Abways, CY5309), rabbit anti-MCP-1 (Abcam, TD7577), rabbit anti-p-p38 (Immunoway, YP0338), rabbit anti-NF-κB (Immunoway, YM8209), and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (Zhongshan Jinqiao, China) were used in this study.

Methods

Experimental grouping and establishment of an AKI-to-CKD transition model

The experimental unit was one mouse. Forty male C57BL/6 mice aged 16 weeks (25–28 g) were randomly divided into the control (CON), ischemia-reperfusion (IRI), finerenone 10 mg/kg intervention (IRI+Fin10), and finerenone 15 mg/kg intervention (IRI+Fin15) groups by the random number table method, with 10 mice in each group. Surgical procedures were performed in a randomized order to minimize time-related confounding effects. Investigators responsible for histological evaluation, immunoblot quantification, and data analysis were blinded to group allocation until completion of statistical analysis. To establish a chronicization model after acute kidney injury, mice were fasted for 8 h before surgery with free access to water, and anesthetized with 3% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) intraperitoneally. A 1.5–2.0 cm midline abdominal incision was performed to expose the renal artery. The right renal artery was occluded with an atraumatic micro-artery clamp, and the kidney was observed to turn from bright red to purplish black. After 30 min of occlusion, the artery clamp was removed to restore blood perfusion, and the kidney turned from purplish black back to bright red. The peritoneum, muscles, and skin were sutured layer by layer. The duration of ischemia was selected based on prior studies [6]. The Control group received no treatment, and postoperative awakening time, drinking, appetite, activity, and urine output were assessed. Starting from the first day after surgery, mice in the IRI+Fin10 group were administered finerenone intragastrically at 10 mg/kg/d for 4 consecutive weeks, while those in the IRI+Fin15 group were administered finerenone intragastrically at 15 mg/kg/d for the same duration. On day 8 after surgery, mice were anesthetized with 3% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) intraperitoneally, the left renal pedicle was ligated, and the left kidney was removed. At the designated experimental endpoints, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 60 mg/kg. Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal reflex and response to noxious stimuli. Then, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel. Death was confirmed by cessation of respiration and heartbeat before tissue collection. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and the American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. On days 8 and 28 post-surgery, the blood pressure levels and body weights were recorded; 24-h urine was collected to detect UPE, and blood was obtained from the retro-orbital venous plexus to detect Scr and serum aldosterone. On day 28, mice were sacrificed to obtain kidney samples. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were predefined prior to the study. Humane endpoints were predefined before the study. Animals were monitored at least once daily after surgery, with more frequent observation during the early postoperative period. Monitored signs included body weight loss, reduced mobility, abnormal posture, ruffled fur, decreased food or water intake, labored breathing, wound infection or dehiscence, severe distress, or moribund condition. Animals reaching humane endpoints would be euthanized immediately under deep anesthesia. Animals were excluded if ischemia was not successfully induced (absence of visible color change during arterial clamping), if severe perioperative complications occurred, or if death occurred before the predefined endpoint. No mortality was observed following the IRI surgery or the subsequent contralateral nephrectomy in any experimental group. Day 8 was selected to assess acute renal injury, whereas day 28 was chosen to evaluate chronic fibrotic progression. Serum creatinine level was the primary outcome, reflecting renal function during the AKI–CKD transition. HE staining and Masson staining were used to observe renal fibrosis, and pathological damage grading and scoring were performed; electron microscopy was performed to observe the pathological changes of podocytes in renal tissue. Western blot was used to detect the protein expression of nephrin, IL-1, IL-6, MCP-1, TGF-β, C3, NR3C3, p-p38, and NF-κB. Immunohistochemistry was used to assess the protein expression of nephrin, IL-6, TGF-β, C3, p-p38, NR3C3, and NF-κB. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC FJMU2023-Y-1032).

Measurement of urinary protein excretion, renal function indices, and plasma aldosterone

On days 8 and 28 post-surgery, mice were placed in metabolic cages to collect 24-h urine, which was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was stored at −80 °C. After all samples were collected, they were rewarmed and 24-h urinary protein was detected using an automatic analyzer. On days 8 and 28 post-surgery, 0.5 mL of blood was collected from each of the 40 mice with a capillary glass tube by the retroorbital venous plexus blood collection method. SBP was measured in conscious mice using a noninvasive tail-cuff system. The measurement was performed three consecutive times for each mouse, and the average value was recorded as the final reading. After centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min, the serum was aspirated and stored at −80 °C. After rewarming, Scr and serum aldosterone were determined on an automatic biochemical analyzer. The aldosterone content in the serum was detected with an aldosterone assay ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological analysis of renal tissue

The left kidney was excised under anesthesia with 3% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally, fixed in 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, and cut into 3 μm serial sections. Routine dewaxing and rehydration were performed, and the sections were stained according to the instructions of the HE staining and Masson staining kits. Rapid dehydration with gradient concentrations of ethanol, xylene clearing, and mounting with neutral balsam were conducted. After air-drying, the histopathological structure of the mouse kidney tissue was observed under a light microscope.

Semiquantitative scoring of tubular injury in renal sections

Mouse renal tissue samples were stained with HE and Masson routinely, and pathological scoring of the kidney was performed under a light microscope. For HE staining, 10 non-overlapping fields were randomly selected from each section under a 200× field of view, scored and recorded; the mean value was used for statistical analysis. (1) Degree of glomerular mesangial hyperplasia: 1 point for no mesangial hyperplasia, 2 points for mild mesangial hyperplasia, 3 points for moderate mesangial hyperplasia, and 4 points for severe mesangial hyperplasia. (2) Degree of glomerulosclerosis: 1 point for no glomerulosclerosis, 3 points for glomerulosclerosis rate <25%, 3 points for glomerulosclerosis rate between 25% and 50%, and 4 points for glomerulosclerosis rate >50%. (3) Renal tubulointerstitial score was based on 4 indicators: renal tubular degeneration and necrosis, renal tubular atrophy, interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, and the presence of interstitial fibrosis. According to the scope and severity of lesions, scores were assigned as 1, 2, 3, and 4 points for none, <25%, 25%-50%, and >50%, respectively. For Masson staining, 10 non-overlapping fields were randomly selected from each section under a 200× field of view. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using the ImageProPlus software according to the percentage of collagen-positive staining area in the total field area, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Detection of the protein expression levels of related indicators in renal tissue by Western blot

Renal tissue proteins were extracted with a lysis buffer containing RIPA, and protein concentration was determined using a Bradford protein quantification kit. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), transferred onto membranes, and blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature (25 °C) for 30 min. Primary antibodies were added, including: rabbit anti-β-actin (1:5000 dilution; Immunoway, YM8343), rabbit anti-nephrin (1:1000 dilution; Immunoway, YT3036), rabbit anti-C3 (1:200 dilution; Abcam, ab200999), rabbit anti-IL-1 (1:2000 dilution;Immunoway, YM8498), rabbit anti-IL-6 (1:1000 dilution; Abmart, TD6087), rabbit anti-TGF-β1 (1:1000 dilution; Abways, CY5309), rabbit anti-MCP-1 (1:2000 dilution; Abmart, TD7577), rabbit anti-p-p38 (1:1000 dilution; Immunoway, YP0338), and rabbit anti-NF-κB (1:2000 dilution; Immunoway, YM8209). After incubation at 37 °C for 1.5 h, the membranes were rinsed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 10 min each. HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The membranes were rinsed 3 times with TBST for 10 min each, then exposed and developed. Image analysis was performed with the White/Ultraviolet Transilluminator system software. With β-actin as an internal reference, the ratio of the absorbance value of the target protein to that of β-actin was used to represent the level of the target protein.

Immunohistochemical detection of related indicators in the renal tissue

Immunohistochemistry was used to detect the expression of IL-6, MCP-1, TGF-β, C3, p-p38, and NF-κB in the renal tissue. High-pressure acid retrieval was performed for IL-6, MCP-1, TGF-β, C3, p-p38, and NF-κB. Primary antibody concentrations were: rabbit anti-IL-6 (1:500 dilution; Abmart, TD6087), rabbit anti-MCP-1 (1:500 dilution; Abmart, TD7577), rabbit anti-TGF-β (1:200 dilution; Abways, CY5309), rabbit anti-C3 (1:500 dilution; Abcam, ab200999), rabbit anti-p-p38 (1:500 dilution; Immunoway, YP0338), and rabbit anti-NF-κB (1:1000 dilution; Immunoway, YM8209). PBS was used instead of the primary antibody as a negative control. Positive reaction sites were brown, and cell nuclei were light blue. Under a microscope at 400×, 10 fields were randomly selected for each section. Image analysis was performed using the Image-Pro Plus software. Staining intensity was quantified as average optical density (AOD), defined as integrated optical density divided by the positively stained area (IOD/Area), and the mean AOD per sample was used for statistical comparison.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was performed using the SPSS 27.0 statistical analysis software. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Measurement data with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for comparisons among multiple groups, and the LSD test was used for pairwise comparisons. If the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance were not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Renal function and aldosterone levels during ischemia-reperfusion-induced AKI

Finerenone, a novel non-steroidal MRA, was administered intragastrically to C57BL/6 mice to downregulate MR overactivation. At day 8 after IRI, mice were assigned to four groups: the control (CON), ischemia-reperfusion (d8-IRI), finerenone 10 mg/kg intervention (d8-IRI+Fin10), and finerenone 15 mg/kg intervention (d8-IRI+Fin15) groups (Figure 1). To investigate renal function changes in mice during IRI-AKI, body weights, serum creatinine (Scr), serum aldosterone, and 24-h urinary protein excretion (UPE) were assessed on day 8 after IRI intervention. Compared with the CON, IRI mice exhibited significantly higher UPE, Scr, and aldosterone levels at day 8 (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d8-IRI group, the d8-IRI+Fin10 and d8-IRI+Fin15 groups had significantly decreased body weights (both p < 0.05), reduced UPE (both p < 0.05), and dose-dependently decreased Scr levels (all p < 0.05). In addition, compared with the d8-IRI group, there were no significant changes in serum aldosterone levels in the d8-IRI+Fin10 and d8-IRI+Fin15 groups (both p > 0.05) (Figure 2(A–D); Table 1).

Effects of finerenone on renal function and aldosterone during renal chronic fibrosis after AKI

To examine the improvement effect of finerenone on renal function during the chronic phase following AKI, mice at day 28 after IRI intervention (n = 10 per group) were divided into the CON, d28-IRI, d28-IRI+Fin10, and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups. Body weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), Scr, UPE, and serum aldosterone were measured on the 28th day after IRI intervention. Compared with the CON group, IRI mice at day 28 exhibited significantly higher SBP, UPE, Scr, and aldosterone levels (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the d28-IRI+Fin10 group showed decreased Scr levels (p < 0.05), and the d28-IRI+Fin15 group after finerenone intervention had significantly reduced SBP, UPE and Scr levels (all p < 0.05). Scr was further reduced in Fin15 group compared with the Fin 10 group (p < 0.05), indicating a dose-dependent effect. Notably compared with the IRI group, serum aldosterone levels in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups were increased (both p < 0.05) (Figure 2(E–I); Table 2).

Effects of finerenone on renal pathology during renal chronic fibrosis after AKI

To assess the improvement effect of finerenone on renal chronic fibrosis after AKI, renal manifestations were examined by HE staining and Masson staining under a light microscope, and the fine structure of the kidney was observed by electron microscopy. HE staining showed partial glomerular capillary loop atrophy, mesangial cell proliferation, renal tubular endothelial cell shedding and vacuolation, interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, and protein casts in some renal tubular lumens in the d28-IRI group. In the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups after finerenone intervention, only a small amount of mesangial cell proliferation was detected; the structures of renal tubules and the interstitium were relatively clear and intact, only a small amount of renal tubular endothelial cell shedding was found in renal tubules, and a small number of inflammatory cells infiltrated the interstitium (Figure 3(A)). Pathological damage scoring of mouse kidney showed that compared with the control group, scores for glomerular mesangial hyperplasia, glomerulosclerosis, and renal tubulointerstitial injury in the d28-IRI, d28-IRI+Fin10, and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups were increased (all p < 0.05). Masson staining showed small patchy blue-stained tissues around the glomeruli, basement membrane, and renal interstitium in the d28-IRI group. In the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups, only a small amount of filamentous blue-stained tissues was found around the glomeruli, basement membrane, and renal interstitium (Figure 3(B)). Compared with the d28-IRI group, scores for glomerular mesangial hyperplasia, glomerulosclerosis, and renal tubulointerstitial injury, as well as the renal tubular collagen fraction, in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups were decreased in a dose-dependent manner (all p < 0.05) (Figure 3(C–D)).

Transmission electron microscopy revealed extensive podocyte foot process fusion, segmental foot process detachment, and exposed GBM in the d28-IRI group; partial podocyte foot process fusion, segmental foot process detachment, and exposed GBM were observed in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups (Figure 3(E)).

Western blot results showed that compared with the control group, the protein expression levels of functional protein Nephrin in the renal tissue of the d28-IRI group were significantly increased (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the protein expression levels of Nephrin in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups after finerenone intervention were decreased (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI+Fin10 group, Nephrin in the d28-IRI+Fin15 group were further decreased (all p < 0.05) (Figure 3(F)).

IHC results showed that compared with the control group, the protein expression levels of functional protein nephrin in the renal tissue were significantly increased in the d28-IRI group (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the protein expression levels of nephrin in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups after finerenone intervention were decreased (p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI+Fin10 group, nephrin was further decreased in the d28-IRI+Fin15 group (all p < 0.05) (Figure 3(G)), indicating dose-dependent effects.

Finerenone suppresses renal inflammatory and pro-fibrotic responses during AKI-to-CKD transition

Since inflammation and fibrosis are common pathological features of chronicization after AKI, the effects of finerenone on renal inflammatory factors and fibrotic markers in IRI-AKI were further studied. Western blot results showed that compared with the control group, the protein expression levels of inflammatory factors (IL-1, IL-6, MCP-1), the fibrotic marker TGF-β1, and complement C3 in the renal tissue of the d28-IRI group were significantly increased (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the protein expression levels of the above indicators in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups after finerenone intervention were decreased (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI+Fin10 group, the above indicators in the d28-IRI+Fin15 group were further decreased (all p < 0.05) (Figure 4(A and B)). IHC results showed that compared with the control group, the protein expression levels of inflammatory factors IL-6, the fibrotic marker TGF-β1, and complement C3 in the renal tissue were significantly increased in the d28-IRI group (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the protein expression levels of the above indicators in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups after finerenone intervention were decreased (p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI+Fin10 group, the above indicators were further decreased in the d28-IRI+Fin15 group (all p < 0.05) (Figure 4(C and D)), indicating dose-dependent effects.

Finerenone downregulates NR3C2/MR overactivation and renal chronic fibrosis after AKI via the p-p38 and NF-κB signaling pathways

Next, the mechanism underlying the improvement of renal chronic fibrosis after AKI by finerenone was explored. Western blot showed that compared with the control group, renal protein expression levels of NR3C2, p-p38, and NF-κB were significantly increased in the d28-IRI group (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the protein expression levels of the three above markers were decreased in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups (p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI+Fin10 group, the protein expression levels of the above indicators were further decreased in the d28-IRI+Fin15 group (p < 0.05) (Figure 5(A and B)), indicating dose-dependent effects. IHC results showed that compared with the control group, renal protein expression levels of NR3C2, p-p38, and NF-κB were significantly increased in the d28-IRI group (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI group, the protein expression levels of the above three markers were decreased in the d28-IRI+Fin10 and d28-IRI+Fin15 groups (all p < 0.05). Compared with the d28-IRI+Fin10 group, the protein expression levels of the above indicators were further decreased in the d28-IRI+Fin15 group (all p < 0.05), suggesting dose-dependent effects (Figure 5(C and D)).

Discussion

CKD is a progressive disease characterized by structural and functional renal damage driven by multiple heterogeneous etiologies and pathological pathways, with the condition often progressing to ESRD. Epidemiological and experimental studies indicate that AKI often results in incomplete renal recovery and represents an independent risk factor for the development and progression of CKD. The AKI–CKD transition has emerged as a major public health concern requiring urgent resolution worldwide. The relatively clear pathophysiological mechanisms underlying renal chronic fibrosis after AKI include microvascular endothelial cell injury, inflammatory response, and abnormal activation of renal tubular epithelial cells. Previous studies have indicated that complement C3 is involved in renal inflammation and chronic fibrosis after ischemia-reperfusion AKI [7]. Despite advances in the understanding of AKI and post-AKI chronic fibrosis, current strategies remain largely supportive, lacking effective targeted interventions. Therefore, thoroughly clarifying the key molecular mechanisms of chronic renal injury after AKI and exploring intervenable therapeutic targets are of great clinical significance.

This study systematically evaluated the protective effects of Finerenone in a non-diabetic IRI-AKI mouse model. The main findings indicate that Finerenone significantly improves post-AKI renal dysfunction, proteinuria, and chronic fibrosis in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, these effects are mediated by antagonizing podocyte NR3C2/MR overactivation, suppressing p38 MAPK and NF-κB signaling, and thereby mitigating inflammation, fibrosis, and AKI-CKD transition.

Tubulointerstitial fibrosis and maladaptive repair of renal tubular epithelial cells are central pathological features of AKI-to-CKD progression. Podocyte has been underappreciated in AKI chronicization [18,19]. In the present study, we innovatively focused on podocytes to explore this under-investigated glomerular component, providing a complementary perspective on post-AKI chronic remodeling. Persistent proteinuria after AKI indicates glomerular filtration barrier impairment, with podocyte injury serving as a major cellular basis [20,21]. In addition, glomerular–tubular crosstalk provides a mechanistic link between podocyte injury and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier may increase tubular exposure to filtered proteins, complement components, cytokines, and other injurious mediators, thereby aggravating tubular inflammation and fibrotic remodeling. As highly differentiated cells with extremely low regenerative capacity, podocytes are very sensitive to ischemia, inflammation, and metabolic disorders. In this study, obvious podocyte structural damage and downregulated expression of the functional protein Nephrin were detected, along with increased expression levels of various inflammatory and fibrotic factors. This reinforces that podocyte injury is a worthwhile factor in the AKI–CKD transition.

Finerenone is a highly selective nonsteroidal MRA with protective renal and cardiovascular benefits, predominantly due to its anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects [22]. Previous large-scale randomized controlled clinical trials (e.g. FIGARO [23], FIDELIO [24]) have confirmed the renal and cardiovascular protective effects in diabetic kidney disease. This study further expands its potential indications, confirming for the first time in a non-diabetic post-IRI-AKI fibrosis model that finerenone can effectively inhibit the AKI–CKD transition. Indicators such as serum creatinine, 24-h urinary protein, and inflammatory/fibrotic factors (IL-1, IL-6, MCP-1, C3, TGF-β) were significantly improved with Finerenone, exhibiting dose dependence. Histological improvements included reduced glomerular atrophy, tubular dilation, and interstitial collagen deposition. Notably, finerenone treatment led to only a modest reduction in systolic blood pressure. This observation is consistent with clinical data indicating that the cardiorenal protective effects of finerenone are largely independent of its hemodynamic actions [25]. Therefore, the attenuation of chronic fibrotic remodeling observed in our study is likely attributable to the direct NR3C2/MR signaling rather than a consequence of pronounced blood pressure lowering. These findings suggest that finerenone not only improves functional abnormalities after the acute phase of AKI but also delays or blocks the transition from AKI to CKD by inhibiting sustained inflammatory and fibrotic responses.

The dose-dependent pattern observed in our preclinical model is consistent with clinical dose-finding evidence (e.g. ARTS-DN [11]), supporting that finerenone significantly reduced UACR in patients with T2D-associated CKD in a dose-dependent manner. Although direct dose conversion between mice and humans is limited by species-specific pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences, the concordance between our preclinical findings and clinical dose-finding data suggests that mineralocorticoid receptor blockade may exert dose-dependent renoprotective effects across experimental and clinical settings.

MR, a ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor encoded by the NR3C2 gene, plays an important regulatory role in inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and tissue fibrosis [26]. Through hormone signal transduction and activation of aldosterone’s target genes, MR controls various physiological and pathological processes and exerts important regulatory effects in organ inflammation and fibrosis. Previous studies have confirmed that MR overactivation is involved in the occurrence and progression of various kidney diseases. The role of MR activation in regulating renal blood perfusion is mainly reflected in normalizing renal blood flow and limiting renal injury through pharmacological NR3C2/MR antagonism [27], but its mechanism of action in post-AKI renal chronic fibrosis remains unclear. The current study found that finerenone’s effects involve suppression of NR3C2/MR overactivation, reducing downstream p38 MAPK and NF-κB signaling, and consequently lowering expression of IL-6, TGF-β, and C3. This confirms that MR overactivation is a core driver of post-AKI renal injury. Notably, the elevated serum aldosterone levels observed in the model suggest that finerenone’s renoprotective effect may depend primarily on blockade of aldosterone–MR pathway activation and downstream signaling rather than suppression of aldosterone production. A similar mechanism has been observed in previous clinical studies, with heart failure patients deriving benefits via inhibited myocardial fibrosis even if aldosterone levels do not decrease after treatment with MR antagonists [28,29]. Therefore, the current results indicate that MR overactivation in IRI is a core factor in renal injury, and the protective effect of MR inhibitors is independent of aldosterone level reduction.

This study is the first to elucidate in a non-diabetic post-IRI-AKI fibrosis model that finerenone effectively downregulates local inflammatory factors, improves renal chronic fibrosis, and thereby ameliorates proteinuria, renal function, and long-term renal prognosis in IRI-AKI mice. This provides new experimental evidence for the clinical pharmacotherapy of AKI. Multiple preclinical and clinical studies (e.g. CONFIDENCE [30], FIDELIO-DKD [15], FIGARO-DKD [23]) have confirmed that MR antagonists exert renal protective effects in CKD, such as diabetic kidney disease, and combination therapy with SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA [31] may provide beneficial effects in reducing cardiovascular and renal events and improving overall survival. However, the effect of finerenone in non-diabetic CKD remains to be confirmed by further studies [32]. This study suggests that the application of finerenone in clinical AKI may help patients improve proteinuria and renal function deterioration, reduce end-stage renal outcomes, and open up new horizons for the prevention and treatment of post-AKI renal chronicization.

However, this study also had certain limitations. These results provide preliminary mechanistic evidence implicating the NR3C2/MR–p38 MAPK/NF-κB axis in post-AKI chronic renal remodeling [33,34]; the upstream signaling pathways and transcriptional regulatory networks responsible for finerenone-mediated protection remain to be fully defined. Several candidate pathways warrant further investigation, including the NR3C2/MR–SGK1 axis, Rac1-mediated MR activation, oxidative stress-related signaling, and downstream inflammatory and extracellular matrix remodeling pathways [35]. It mainly focused on the p-p38 and NF-κB signaling pathways, while fibrosis involves a complex network of multiple signaling pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin, and whether finerenone affects other pathways requires further exploration.

In summary, this study confirms that finerenone can significantly ameliorate renal function injury, inflammation, and chronic fibrosis in mice after renal IRI. The core mechanism may involve antagonizing the overactivation of renal MR and possibly inhibiting the p-p38 and NF-κB signaling pathways, ultimately alleviating inflammatory responses and chronic fibrosis. These findings provide a theoretical basis and necessary preliminary data for expanding the application of finerenone in the prevention and treatment of post-AKI chronic kidney disease, deepen the understanding of the mechanism underlying the renoprotective effect of finerenone, and provide insights and experimental basis for further exploring the mechanism by which finerenone improves the AKI–CKD transition, which may help design new therapeutic approaches after AKI.

Conclusion

Collectively, this study indicates that finerenone attenuates renal inflammation and fibrotic remodeling after ischemia-reperfusion injury, improves renal function, and confers sustained renoprotection in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, these data suggest that finerenone suppresses MR activation and, downstream signaling, thereby inhibiting p38 phosphorylation and NF-κB activation, which may contribute to reduced renal inflammation and fibrosis during the AKI-to-CKD transition. These findings not only provide strong evidence for the potential therapeutic value of finerenone in the prevention and treatment of chronic kidney disease after AKI but also deepen the understanding of the mechanism of AKI-CKD transition. Moreover, our results provide new experimental clues for further studying the mechanism of renal chronic fibrosis after AKI and exploring new intervenable therapeutic targets.
