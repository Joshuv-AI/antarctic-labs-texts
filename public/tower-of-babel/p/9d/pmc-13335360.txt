Comparative performance of insulin resistance-related indices in predicting adverse cardiovascular events among individuals with NAFLD and MASLD: a multi-center cohort study
Authors: Yanqiu Huang, Yadan Xu, Chenghao Zhang, Wen Gu, Yue Li, Haojia Zhang, Tian Wang, Tangyi Su, Yangchen Lhamu, Yichen Chen, Liang Zhou, Lipeng Hao, Yang Yang, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) are closely linked to insulin resistance and elevated cardiovascular risk, triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-related indices, the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and the cardiometabolic index (CMI) have emerged as practical surrogate markers for cardiometabolic risk assessment in these populations. However, it remains unclear whether the associations of these indices with adverse cardiovascular events remain consistent when transitioning from the NAFLD to the newly defined MASLD framework, and a comprehensive comparison of these indices across both diagnostic criteria is lacking.MethodsThis study included 4693/3266, 74,173/67,864, and 7823/10,576 individuals with MASLD/NAFLD from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), UK Biobank (UKB), and China Pudong cohort, respectively. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic spline analyses, and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were employed to assess associations. Linear regression models evaluated relationships between TyG-related indices and cortical/subcortical structural volumes. Mediation analyses examined the role of oxidative stress, phenotypic aging, and inflammatory markers.ResultsMost IR-related indices demonstrated nonlinear, predominantly J-shaped associations with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and related mortality, especially within the UKB. TyG-WC optimally predicted 3-year CVD mortality in MASLD/NAFLD across UKB and NHANES, as well as ischemic stroke (IS) in UKB-MASLD. TyG-WHTR was the strongest predictor for CVD mortality in the Pudong cohort and myocardial infarction (MI) in UKB-MASLD. Notably, elevated TyG-WHTR was consistently associated with heightened risks across all endpoints: CVD mortality (NAFLD: UKB: HR 1.60, 95%CI 1.38–1.84, NHANES: 1.90, 1.34–2.70, Pudong: 1.24, 1.10–1.40; MASLD: UKB: 1.59, 1.38–1.82, NHANES: 1.86, 1.36–2.54, Pudong: 1.70, 1.43–2.03), MI (NAFLD: 1.20, 1.09–1.33; MASLD: 1.22, 1.11–1.34), and IS (NAFLD: 1.41, 1.24–1.61; MASLD: 1.39, 1.23–1.57). Structural neuroimaging analyses revealed significant negative correlations between TyG-WC/TyG-WHTR and subcortical volumes (P < 1 × 10−4). Mediation analyses indicated that oxidative stress, phenotypic aging, and inflammatory markers collectively accounted for 1.4–27% of the observed associations.ConclusionsInsulin Resistance-Related Indices demonstrate robust clinical utility in predicting CVD and mortality in NAFLD/MASLD across three distinct cohorts, with oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and accelerated aging serving as potential mechanistic pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03208-x.Keywords: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Triglyceride-glucose index, Atherogenic index of plasma, Cardiometabolic index, Adverse cardiovascular events, Cohort study

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as one of the most prevalent chronic liver conditions worldwide and is increasingly recognized as a multisystem metabolic disorder rather than an isolated hepatic disease [1, 2]. The relationship between MASLD and cardiovascular disease (CVD) is complex and bidirectional, rooted in a shared pathophysiological basis encompassing endothelial dysfunction, systemic inflammation, visceral adiposity, and insulin resistance (IR) [3–5]. The same pathophysiological pathways that disrupt hepatocyte function and promote visceral fat deposition are intimately linked to the development of serious cardiovascular conditions, including coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and overall cardiovascular mortality [6–8]. Beyond progressive liver injury, individuals with MASLD/NAFLD therefore experience a substantially elevated risk of CVD, which represents the leading cause of mortality in this population. The condition demonstrates particularly high prevalence among metabolic risk groups, affecting approximately 75% of individuals with obesity and 68.71% of those with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2, 9]. Notably, the prevalence of NAFLD/MASLD is also markedly elevated among individuals with prediabetes, reflecting the central role of insulin resistance across the full spectrum of glucose dysregulation, from early metabolic dysfunction to overt T2DM [9]. The excess cardiovascular mortality documented in patients with fatty liver disease [8], combined with the strong bidirectional interplay between MASLD and T2DM mediated through shared insulin resistance pathways [9], further underscores the clinical urgency of effective cardiovascular risk stratification in this population.

IR is a central pathophysiological feature linking hepatic steatosis to cardiometabolic complications [9, 10]. Although the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is the reference standard for IR assessment, its invasiveness and complexity limit routine clinical application. In this context, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has gained attention as a practical surrogate marker of IR, demonstrating robust associations with cardiometabolic outcomes in general populations [11–13]. More recently, obesity-adjusted TyG derivatives, such as TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), and TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHTR), have been proposed to enhance risk discrimination by integrating metabolic and anthropometric information [14, 15]. In parallel, other composite metabolic indices, including the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), and cardiometabolic index (CMI), have shown prognostic relevance for cardiovascular events and mortality in population-based studies [16, 17]. However, existing evidence is fragmented and largely limited to single indices, single cohorts, or Western populations. Importantly, it remains unclear whether these indices exhibit consistent predictive performance across different diagnostic frameworks (NAFLD vs. MASLD), ethnic backgrounds, and clinical settings, and which marker provides the most robust risk stratification for cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD/NAFLD.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive, multi-center cohort study using data from the UK Biobank (UKB), National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), and China Pudong cohort. Our objectives were to (1) systematically compare the prognostic performance of TyG-related indices, AIP, METS-IR, and CMI for cardiovascular events and mortality under both NAFLD and MASLD frameworks; (2) evaluate the consistency of these associations across ethnically and geographically diverse populations; and (3) explore potential mechanistic pathways involving oxidative stress, inflammatory markers, phenotypic aging, and brain structural alterations. By integrating large-scale epidemiological data with mechanistic analyses, this study aims to refine cardiovascular risk stratification and inform precision prevention strategies for patients with MASLD/NAFLD. Moreover, we conducted an exploratory analysis of the associations between the best-performing IR-related indices and brain structural volumes using UKB neuroimaging data, informed by prior evidence linking metabolic dysregulation and adiposity to structural brain alterations [18].

Materials and methods

Study population

This study conducted analyses based on the UKB, NHANES databases, and China Pudong Cohort. The UKB is a large-scale biomedical database that has been collecting genetic, lifestyle, biological sample, and health information from 500,000 participants since 2006. After excluding individuals with missing data on blood indicators, insulin resistance indices, outcomes, or covariates, final analytical samples comprised 67,864 NAFLD and 74,173 MASLD (Figure S1). US NHANES is a research program aimed at assessing the health and nutritional status of individuals in the United States, with approximately 5,000 nationally representative samples recruited every two years. China Pudong Cohort is a community-based prospective cohort study. Volunteers were recruited since 2019, and baseline data were collected. Following exclusion for incomplete data, 3246 NAFLD and 4693 MASLD cases remained in US NHANES 1999–2018 (Figure S2), while the Pudong cohort included 10,576 participants with NAFLD and 7823 participants with MASLD for final analyses (Figure S3).

Definition of TyG-related index, atherogenic index of plasma, and cardiometabolic index

This study utilized seven indices including TyG, TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHTR), AIP, METS-IR, and CMI. We calculated the indices using the following formulas:

TyG = Ln [Triglyceride (TG) (mg/dL) × Glucose (mg/dL) / 2];

TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI;

TyG-WC = TyG × Waist Circumference;

TyG-WHTR = TyG × WHTR = TyG × (Waist Circumference / Height);

AIP = log10 (TG (mmol/L)/High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (mmol/L));

METS-IR = Ln[2 × Fasting blood glucose (mg/dL) + Fasting triglycerides (mg/dL)] × BMI (kg/m2) / Ln(HDL_C (mg/dL));

CMI = (TG (mmol/L) / HDL-C (mmol/L)) × (Waist Circumference / Height) [15, 19–21].

Definition of NAFLD and MASLD

The definition of fatty liver disease was based on the Fatty Liver Index (FLI). The FLI was calculated using the equation: FLI = (e0.953×ln(TG)+0.139×BMI+0.718×ln(GGT)+0.053×waist circumference−15.745)/(1 + e0.953×ln(TG)+0.139×BMI+0.718×ln(GGT)+0.053×waist circumference−15.745) × 100. We applied the criterion with a FLI score ≥ 60 to identify individuals with fatty liver disease in the UKB and NHANES [22]. The Pudong cohort determined the presence of fatty liver based on the abdominal ultrasound. NAFLD was referred to as those with fatty liver who had no excessive alcohol consumption (defined as < 2 drinks/day for men or < 1 drink/day for women, a drink contains 14 g of alcohol) and tested negative for hepatitis B and C virus infections. MASLD was defined as individuals with fatty liver disease who did not have excessive alcohol consumption (defined as ≥ 30 g/day for men or ≥ 20 g/day for women) and had no other causes of steatosis, along with the presence of one or more cardiometabolic risk factors: (1) WC > 94 cm for men or > 80 cm for women, or BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2; (2) Fasting blood glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L (≥ 100 mg/dL) or Gycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) or current use of glucose-lowering medications, or 2-h post-load glucose levels ≥ 7.8 mmol/L (≥ 140 mg/dL); (3) Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or on antihypertensive medications; (4) TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or taking lipid-lowering medications; (5) HDL-C ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) for men or ≤ 1.3 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) for women, or ongoing related treatments [23].

Definition of adverse cardiovascular events

In the UKB, follow-up data of the outcomes was obtained through electronic linkages with hospital admission and cancer registry records in England, Wales, and Scotland. Information related to deaths in the Pudong cohort was obtained from the Pudong CDC Death Surveillance System. For US NHANES database, relevant mortality files linked to the National Death Index (NDI) were utilized. All-cause mortality was characterized as mortality resulting from any cause. Additionally, disease-specific mortality was determined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Specifically, we defined cardiovascular mortality according to ICD-10 codes (I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69) [24].

Definition of oxidative stress, phenotypic aging and inflammatory markers

In both US NHANES and UKB databases, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in blood samples were measured using immunoturbidimetric assays. Neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts were determined through automated hematology analyzers. Oxidative stress biomarkers included uric acid (UA) and UA/HDL-C ratio (UHR) [23]. UA and HDL-C levels were measured using enzymatic kinetic methods. The residuals obtained from the regression of predicted age on chronological age were used to assess phenotypic age acceleration. Participants with residuals greater than zero were considered to exhibit accelerated aging. The phenoAge was computed by the established algorithm: phenotypic age = 141.50225 + , Mortality risk = − 19.907 − 0.0336 × albumin + 0.0095 × creatinine + 0.1953 × glucose + 0.0954 × ln(CRP) − 0.0120 × lymphocyte% + 0.0268 × MCV + 0.3306 × RDW + 0.00188 × ALP + 0.0554 × WBC, with all parameters calculated as described in the original article [25].

Brain imaging

Brain MR imaging data from the UKB were collected during the second follow-up visit using a Siemens Skyra 3 T scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Structural brain metrics were derived from quality-controlled T1-weighted images processed through FreeSurfer. Cortical morphometry (surface area, volume, and mean thickness) was extracted using FreeSurfer’s surface-based atlas phenotypes, while subcortical volumes were obtained via the aseg segmentation tool. Our analysis incorporated 68 cortical regions from FreeSurfer’s aparc atlas and 40 subcortical regions from the aseg atlas [18].

Covariates

Potential confounders adjusted for in the analyses included age, sex, race/ethnicity, income level (assessed by Townsend deprivation index in the UKB and by poverty-to-income ratio (PIR) in the NHANES), educational attainment, smoking status, drinking status, total cholesterol (TC) levels, and hypertension. Age and TC were treated as continuous variables. All the remaining variables were specified as categorical: sex (male, female), household income level (low, medium, high), educational attainment (less than high school, high school and above), smoking status (yes or no), drinking status (yes or no) and hypertension (yes or no). In the UKB, race was categorized into five groups: White, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, Chinese, and Mixed and other race. In contrast, US NHANES dataset classified race into Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and the other race. The Townsend index in the UKB data was divided into three categories based on tertiles, whereas in US NHANES, low income was defined as PIR < 1.3, and high income as PIR > 3.5. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg or self-reported diagnosis or receiving relevant treatment.

Statistical analysis

For baseline characteristics, continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percent). Group differences in continuous variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-tests, whereas categorical variables were compared by chi-square (χ2) tests.

This study initially employed the Cox proportional hazards model to explore the association between tertiles of seven indices and outcomes, with the first tertile of each index serving as the reference. Two statistical models were adjusted. Model 1 in the Pudong cohort included age, sex, educational attainment, smoking status, and drinking status, and Model 2 further adjusted for TC and hypertension. The UKB and NHANES cohorts additionally adjusted for race/ethnicity and household income level (PIR for NHANES and Townsend index for UKB). The results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All NHANES analyses were conducted in accordance with the official NHANES analytic guidelines, incorporating 20-year combined sampling weights spanning 1999–2018, along with stratification variables and primary sampling units (PSUs), to account for the complex survey design and ensure nationally representative estimates with valid variance estimation. Prior to reporting the results, the proportional hazards assumption was formally verified for all primary Cox models using the Schoenfeld residuals test, and the assumption was satisfied for most primary exposure-outcome associations across all three cohorts (global test P > 0.05).

To delve deeper into the relationships between IR-related indices and different outcomes, we fitted restricted cubic splines (RCS) using three and four knots, and identified clinically meaningful threshold values based on the inflection points of the spline curves, beyond which the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality increased substantially. A non-linear P-value less than 0.05 was considered indicative of a non-linear trend between exposure and outcome. This analysis was adjusted for the covariates in Model 2. Next, we utilized time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) to examine the predictive effect of the exposure variable. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% CI was employed to evaluate the 3-year predictive ability of the seven indices for different outcomes. A higher AUC value indicated better predictive performance [26].

Subsequently, mediation analysis was conducted to examine the mediating effects of inflammatory markers and accelerated aging. Based on the brain structure data from the UKB, multiple linear regression model was performed between the indices that exhibited the best predictive performance and different brain regions. Corresponding t-values and P values were extracted to ascertain whether these indices could potentially result in adverse cardiovascular outcomes was by influencing brain structure. Two-sided linear regression model was adjusted for age, sex and imaging scanning sites [18]. To mitigate the likelihood of Type I errors, P values were adjusted through the application of the Bonferroni corrected [27]. Brain structures with P-values less than 10−4 were further selected for visualization in brain structure maps. Additionally, we stratified the overall population into subgroups based on sex to assess the interaction of sex on exposure and outcomes. Furthermore, to validate the stability of our findings, we categorized the indices into quartiles and conducted Cox proportional hazards model analyses in the UKB.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings. Firstly, in the UKB, additional adjustment for history of diabetes and lipid-lowering medication use was incorporated into the multivariable models to evaluate whether these metabolic confounders materially influenced the observed associations. Secondly, participants who experienced cardiovascular events within the first two years of follow-up were excluded to minimize the potential for reverse causation. Thirdly, given the substantial population overlap between the NAFLD and MASLD definitions, a dedicated subgroup analysis was performed in individuals classified as MASLD but not NAFLD, representing the population uniquely captured by the updated diagnostic criteria, to further characterize the similarities and differences in predictive performance across the two diagnostic frameworks. Fourthly, to validate the robustness of findings based on FLI-defined hepatic steatosis, hepatic fat content in the UKB was additionally ascertained using proton density fat fraction (PDFF) derived from magnetic resonance imaging, and the primary analyses were repeated in this imaging-confirmed sub-sample. To address potential selection bias arising from complete-case restriction in the China Pudong cohort, multiple imputation was performed using the MICE package in R, and the primary analyses were repeated in the multiply imputed datasets. To examine whether the observed associations were driven by obesity-related metabolic dysregulation, additional analyses were restricted to individuals with normal BMI meeting NAFLD or MASLD criteria, thereby evaluating the predictive performance of IR-related indices in a non-obese steatotic liver disease population. Furthermore, given the older age profile of the China Pudong cohort (mean age 71–72 years), Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were performed for CVD mortality analyses, treating non-CVD death as a competing event, to account for the potential violation of the independent censoring assumption inherent to standard Cox models in this population. Subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) and 95% confidence intervals are reported in the supplementary materials.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and SAS (version 9.4.8). A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

The baseline characteristics of the included patients with MASLD/NAFLD from three national cohorts (74,173/67,864 in the UKB, 4693/3266 in US NHANES, 7823/10,576 in China Pudong) are summarized in Table 1. Individuals with MASLD/NAFLD in Pudong cohort had a significantly higher mean age than those in the UKB (71.0/72.6 vs. 57.0/57.0) and US NHANES (71.0/72.6 vs. 54.3/55.0), while no significant difference existed in mean age between MASLD and NAFLD in the UKB (P value = 0.753) and US NHANES (P value = 0.080). Among the three cohorts, the proportion of non-smoking and non-drinking individuals in NAFLD was higher than in MASLD. Compared with the UKB and Pudong cohort, US NHANES exhibited higher BMI (33.9 ± 5.9) and WC (112.1 ± 12.5) values, but lower HDL-C (46.0 ± 12.3) levels. All seven indices in the Pudong cohort were significantly different in NAFLD and MASLD, but not in US NHANES.

Associations of TyG-related index, AIP and CMI with adverse cardiovascular events in NAFLD and MASLD

Figure 1 and 2 demonstrate that these seven indices are significantly associated with CVD and mortality based on model 2 in NAFLD and MASLD across three national cohorts. The UKB recorded 6276/6840 deaths, 1576/1720 incident IS cases and 2514/2687 incident MI cases over a mean follow-up of 13.2–13.5 years in patients with NAFLD/MASLD. All indices except TyG and AIP index exhibited significantly elevated risks for all mortality, CVD mortality, MI and IS in the highest tertile (NAFLD/MASLD: HR range of T3 versus T1: all-cause mortality: 1.07–1.42/1.07–1.41; CVD mortality: 1.19–1.60/1.17–1.60; MI: 1.20–1.41/1.19–1.43; IS: 1.14–1.41/1.14–1.39). We further evaluated associations between quartile-based classifications of all seven indices and risks of CVD and mortality (Table S1). Higher index levels demonstrated significant positive associations with increased CVD incidence and mortality risk. Additionally, results from Model 1 (Tables S2 and S3) were broadly consistent with those of Model 2.

Sensitivity analyses

The robustness of the primary findings was supported across multiple sensitivity analyses. In the subgroup of individuals classified as MASLD but not NAFLD, elevated IR-related indices remained largely and significantly associated with higher risks of cardiovascular events and mortality, confirming that the observed associations were not an artefact of population overlap between the two diagnostic frameworks (Table S20). In the UKB sub-sample with MRI-derived PDFF-confirmed hepatic steatosis, associations between IR-related indices and cardiovascular outcomes were directionally consistent with those obtained in the FLI-defined primary analyses, validating the robustness of findings to the method of hepatic steatosis ascertainment (Table S21). In the China Pudong cohort, multiple imputation analyses yielded results broadly consistent with the complete-case analyses, suggesting that complete-case restriction did not materially introduce selection bias (Table S22). Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for history of diabetes and lipid-lowering medication use produced effect estimates consistent with the primary models, confirming that these metabolic confounders did not materially alter the observed associations (Table S23–S24). After excluding participants who experienced cardiovascular events within the first two years of follow-up, the associations remained largely unchanged, indicating that reverse causation was unlikely to account for the primary findings (Table S25–S26). In the subgroup of individuals with normal BMI meeting MASLD or NAFLD criteria, elevated IR-related indices were similarly associated with higher cardiovascular risk, demonstrating that the associations were not solely driven by obesity-related metabolic dysregulation (Table S27–S28).

In the Fine-Gray competing risk analyses of the China Pudong cohort, treating non-CVD death as a competing event, TyG-WHTR, TyG-WC, TyG-BMI, and METS-IR remained significantly associated with CVD mortality under both the MASLD and NAFLD frameworks, with TyG-WHTR demonstrating the largest subdistribution hazard ratios in both populations (sHR: 1.227, 95% CI 1.075–1.400 for MASLD; sHR 1.184, 95% CI 1.090–1.285 for NAFLD). By contrast, TyG, AIP, and CMI did not reach statistical significance in the MASLD framework after competing risk adjustment, likely reflecting the attenuated metabolic variance inherent to the more stringent MASLD diagnostic criteria, though their associations remained significant under the NAFLD framework (Table S29). Collectively, these competing risk analyses provide a more conservative yet robust confirmation of the primary findings in this older cohort, where non-CVD mortality represents a substantial competing event.

Discussion

This study represents the first multinational prospective cohort investigation examining the associations between TyG-related indices, AIP, CMI and cardiovascular disease incidence/mortality in MASLD/NAFLD populations. Our findings revealed a J-shaped relationship between elevated insulin resistance-related biomarkers (including AIP and CMI) and increased cardiovascular risk. Notably, TyG-WC and TyG-WHTR demonstrated superior predictive capacity for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Mechanistic analyses identified oxidative stress, phenotypic aging, and inflammatory markers as partial mediators of these associations. The robustness of these findings was confirmed through sensitivity analyses employing multiple adjustment models and subgroup stratifications, demonstrating consistent results across diverse demographic and clinical populations.

The TyG index is widely recognized as a practical and reliable marker of insulin resistance, with established value in assessing cardiovascular disease and mortality risk in MASLD/NAFLD populations [28]. Recent research suggests that obesity-adjusted TyG indices (particularly TyG-BMI and TyG-WC) offer enhanced prognostic capability compared to the standard TyG index alone [29]. Chen et al. recently demonstrated significant associations between TyG-BMI/TyG-WC and all-cause, cardiovascular, and diabetes-related mortality in NAFLD/MASLD, identifying nonlinear positive correlations with all-cause mortality [14]. Aligning with these findings, our study confirmed TyG-WC as the strongest predictor of cardiovascular mortality in both the UKB (primarily White, mean age 57) and NHANES (multiethnic, mean age 54–55) cohorts. However, in the Pudong cohort (exclusively Asian, mean age 71–72), TyG-WHTR showed superior performance for predicting both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. These divergent findings likely reflect ethnic variations, age differences, and methodological distinctions in waist-to-height ratio assessments. Importantly, TyG-WHTR retained significant predictive value for all-cause mortality and myocardial infarction in the UKB and NHANES MASLD populations, supporting its use as a cardiovascular risk biomarker. Future dedicated clinical risk prediction studies incorporating Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) analyses against a prespecified baseline model are warranted to formally establish the incremental predictive utility of TyG-WC and TyG-WHTR in clinical practice. Although few studies have evaluated TyG-derived indices for NAFLD/MASLD prognosis, the potential of AIP and CMI remains poorly characterized in this population. Our analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between AIP and cardiovascular mortality in both UKB and NHANES cohorts. Similarly, elevated CMI levels showed a positive linear relationship with increased cardiovascular mortality risk, though without significant nonlinearity. Both markers demonstrated moderate predictive performance for cardiovascular outcomes, exceeding the standard TyG index but remaining inferior to obesity-combined TyG indices. The MASLD framework, by explicitly requiring the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors, more precisely identifies individuals in whom metabolic dysregulation is the dominant driver of cardiovascular risk, representing a conceptual advantage over the exclusion-based NAFLD criteria. Minor differences in predictive performance between the two frameworks are likely attributable to the distributional shift in IR-related indices resulting from the more stringent metabolic selection criteria of MASLD, rather than reflecting fundamentally distinct disease mechanisms.

Existing literature has predominantly utilized the FLI, a well-established steatosis marker validated in European and American populations, for hepatic steatosis diagnosis. Consistent with this approach, we implemented FLI for both the US NHANES and UK Biobank cohorts [22, 30]. However, given potential ethnic variations in FLI thresholds and biochemical marker differences that might affect diagnostic precision, we employed abdominal ultrasonography for hepatic steatosis assessment in the Pudong cohort. Our results, corroborated by previous investigations, indicate that obesity-modified TyG indices (TyG-WC and TyG-WHTR) represent robust predictors of mortality risk in adult MASLD/NAFLD populations across different geographic regions, providing clinicians with a valuable risk assessment tool.

Recent clinical and epidemiological studies increasingly recognize the prognostic value of TyG-related indices, though their biological mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. The mediation analyses in the present study provide preliminary mechanistic insights into these pathways. Among those examined, oxidative stress emerged as the predominant mediating mechanism (UA and UHR: 15.0%-24.8%), consistent with evidence that chronic hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia drive reactive oxygen species production, promoting endothelial injury and atherogenesis. Accelerated phenotypic aging contributed similarly substantial mediation proportions (18.7%-27.0%), supporting the concept that IR-driven metabolic burden amplifies cardiovascular vulnerability through biological aging acceleration. Inflammatory markers, including CRP and immune cell counts, accounted for comparatively smaller but statistically significant indirect effects (1.4%-10.4%), reflecting the role of chronic low-grade inflammation in linking metabolic dysfunction to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Taken together, TyG-WC and TyG-WHTR may promote cardiovascular injury through a coordinated cascade of oxidative damage, aging acceleration, and immune activation. As these analyses were exploratory and all mediators were measured at baseline alongside the exposure variables, the observed mediation proportions should be interpreted as estimates of pathway contribution rather than evidence of strict causal mediation.

As a further exploratory analysis, we examined the associations between the best-performing IR-related indices and brain structural volumes, given that subcortical brain alterations are recognized markers of biological aging consistent with the mechanistic framework of this study, and that ischemic stroke represents one of the primary cardiovascular endpoints examined herein. Neuroimaging analysis revealed that IR induces structural alterations in cortical and subcortical regions, with individuals exhibiting higher TyG-WC showing volume increases in key brain areas including the caudate nucleus, putamen, nucleus accumbens, lateral ventricles, and thalamus, potentially explaining IR’s association with cerebrovascular disease and mortality. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive, and their clinical implications require validation in future longitudinal studies with dedicated neurological endpoints. Substantial evidence connects IR with oxidative stress, systemic metabolic inflammation, and accelerated aging [29, 31–33]. IR disrupts hepatic gluconeogenesis suppression while preserving lipogenic capacity, leading to hepatic lipid accumulation [34, 35]. This ectopic fat deposition promotes metabolic stress and elevates mitochondrial ROS production, overwhelming antioxidant defenses and generating oxidized LDL, malondialdehyde, and homocysteine that collectively impair endothelial function [33, 36, 37]. Simultaneously, hepatic fat triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine release (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) while suppressing adiponectin, exacerbating systemic inflammation and perpetuating IR [38, 39]. Emerging evidence suggests that IR, inflammation, and oxidative stress may act synergistically to accelerate aging processes [31]. Dyslipidemia-induced intracellular lipid accumulation promotes cellular senescence through multiple mechanisms including oxidative stress-mediated telomere shortening, aberrant mTOR/PPARγ signaling, inflammatory cytokine production, and lipotoxicity-induced organelle dysfunction and ER stress [31, 40–43]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that fatty acid synthase inhibitors (e.g., C75) reduce senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors (e.g., IL-1α/β, IL-6) [44, 45]. These pathological changes collectively contribute to endothelial damage, chronic low-grade inflammation, hypercoagulability, and hypofibrinolysis [46, 47], disrupting hemostatic balance and increasing thrombosis risk while promoting plaque instability, thereby elevating myocardial infarction and stroke risks. This cascade of oxidative stress, inflammation, and accelerated aging appears to underlie the elevated premature mortality observed in NAFLD/MASLD patients with cardiovascular complications.

This study possesses several important strengths that enhance its scientific validity and clinical relevance. Our comprehensive evaluation of TyG-related indices, AIP and CMI in relation to cardiovascular outcomes within NAFLD/MASLD populations revealed TyG-WC and TyG-WHTR as particularly robust prognostic markers. This finding supports the clinical applicability of these indices across current diagnostic frameworks for fatty liver disease. The inclusion of three multinational prospective cohorts provides robust cross-validation of the associations between TyG-derived indices, CMI, AIP and mortality risk in NAFLD/MASLD patients. Additionally, our investigation of oxidative stress, epigenetic aging, and inflammatory biomarkers as mediators offers novel mechanistic insights that may inform future translational research.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting these findings. Given the large number of indices, outcomes, cohorts, and exploratory analyses, multiple-testing burden should be acknowledged. The consistency of findings across three independent cohorts mitigates concerns regarding false-positive results; nevertheless, exploratory findings should be interpreted with caution pending independent replication. All IR-related indices were assessed at a single baseline time point, which does not capture the dynamic nature of metabolic health. Longitudinal changes in these indices over time may provide additional prognostic value beyond baseline measurements alone, and future studies incorporating repeated assessments are warranted to evaluate whether delta values of IR-related indices more accurately reflect evolving cardiovascular risk. While we implemented rigorous statistical adjustments, the observational nature of this study cannot fully eliminate potential residual confounding from unmeasured variables. The near-homogeneous ethnic composition within individual cohorts precludes formal comparisons of predictive performance across ethnic groups. The neuroimaging analyses were exploratory in nature and should be interpreted with caution; future studies with longitudinal brain imaging data are needed to establish the temporal and causal relationships between IR-related metabolic burden and structural brain alterations. Furthermore, the mediation analyses were restricted to biomarkers available within the respective databases, and since all mediators were measured at baseline alongside the exposure variables, the observed mediation proportions should be interpreted as exploratory estimates rather than evidence of strict causal mediation. Other biologically plausible mechanistic pathways warrant investigation in future studies. Finally, the markedly higher AUC values observed in the China Pudong cohort should be interpreted with caution, as the older age distribution and shorter follow-up period likely contribute to higher event rates and more pronounced metabolic risk gradients, which may inflate discriminative performance metrics relative to the other cohorts.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings establish significant associations between TyG-derived indices, AIP, CMI, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD/NAFLD populations across three independent multi-ethnic cohorts, with TyG-WC and TyG-WHTR demonstrating statistically superior predictive capacity for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Mechanistic analyses identified oxidative stress, inflammatory biomarkers, and accelerated phenotypic aging as significant mediators among the pathways examined, though these findings should be interpreted as exploratory estimates rather than evidence of strict causal mediation. These results underscore the necessity for systematic prognostic monitoring in MASLD/NAFLD patients and call for deeper molecular investigations into the oxidative stress, inflammatory, and phenotypic remodeling processes that contribute to cardiovascular deterioration in the context of IR.
