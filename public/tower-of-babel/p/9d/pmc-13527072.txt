Evaluation of composites interphase mechanical properties considering CZM and FGM behaviors using single-fiber composite tensile tests
Authors: Hossein Hosseini-Toudeshky, Yasin Rezaee, Azizollah Navaei, Masoud Saber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mechanical behavior of the matrix–fiber interphase plays a critical role in the performance of composite materials, as damage often initiates and propagates from this region. This study combines experimental and numerical approaches to investigate the interphase mechanical properties between glass fibers and epoxy matrix using specially designed single-fiber composite specimens. Tensile tests are performed at a low loading rate, and the obtained elastic responses are matched to those calculated from finite element analyses (FEA) of three-phase models by varying the interphase mechanical properties. Two modeling strategies were applied: (1) a functionally graded material (FGM) approach, in which Young’s modulus varies across the interphase thickness, and (2) a cohesive zone model (CZM) representing the fiber–matrix interphase. The CZM parameters were calibrated to match the experimental elastic modulus. Results show that the cohesive zone model better reproduces the experimental elastic response than the FGM approach. This improvement is attributed to the CZM’s capability to capture interphase softening near the composite’s failure surface, effectively representing interphase damage initiated prior to the final failure of the fiber and matrix.Keywords: Single-Fiber Composite, Interphase, Finite Element Method (FEM), Cohesive Zone Model (CZM)Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Polymer matrix composites (PMCs) are advanced materials composed of reinforcing fibers embedded within a polymer matrix. The interfacial region between the fiber and matrix, known as the interphase (Fig. 1.a), plays a decisive role in determining the overall performance of the composite1,2. Rather than being a simple boundary, the interphase represents a transitional zone where the mechanical and chemical properties gradually vary between the fiber and matrix due to physical constraints, chemical interactions, and processing conditions2. Its characteristics govern essential properties such as mechanical strength, stiffness, fatigue resistance, thermal stability, and chemical durability. A strong, well-bonded interphase ensures efficient load transfer and enhances overall mechanical performance3, whereas weak adhesion leads to stress concentrations, debonding, and premature failure. Consequently, understanding and controlling interphase adhesion are paramount for the design of high-performance PMCs4.

Polymers used in composite fabrication are broadly classified as thermoplastics and thermosets. Thermoplastics can be remelted and reformed, offering recyclability, while thermosets form irreversible cross-linked networks that provide superior thermal stability, strength, and durability under demanding conditions5. Reinforcements may appear as continuous or discontinuous fibers, whiskers, particles, or flakes within a continuous matrix. Common reinforcement materials include glass, carbon, and aramid fibers—where aramid and carbon fibers exhibit greater elongation and impact resistance, favoring thermoplastic matrices, while glass fibers offer higher tensile strength and thermal conductivity, making them suitable for thermosetting matrices6.

Interfacial adhesion in composites arises from a combination of physical, chemical, and mechanical interactions. Physical forces such as van der Waals interactions provide limited bonding, while chemical adhesion—resulting from molecular chain entanglement and reactions between active surface groups—forms much stronger bonds, often enhanced through surface treatments or primers7. Mechanical adhesion further improves bonding as the matrix penetrates surface irregularities of the fibers, creating microscopic interlocks8. Under mechanical loading, stress gradients arising from the mismatch in fiber and matrix properties often cause damage initiation and progression within this region, making the interphase a critical zone influencing the composite’s durability, fracture resistance, and long-term performance9.

Understanding the failure mechanisms at the fiber–matrix interface is essential for improving interphase adhesion and overall composite performance. Damage mechanisms in unidirectional composite lamina include fiber pull-out, fiber fracture, interfacial debonding, and crack propagation (Fig. 1.b)10. Cracks generally initiate within the matrix, often originating from weak zones or surface defects, and propagate under applied stress. Debonding commonly starts at localized stress concentrations within the interphase, where stress gradients caused by the mismatch in mechanical properties between fiber and matrix lead to a gradual loss of adhesion12.

The interphase region often serves as the initiation site for phenomena such as atomic slip and delamination, emphasizing the need for accurate modeling of its mechanical behavior. The Cohesive Zone Model (CZM) provides a robust framework for simulating damage initiation and propagation at the fiber–matrix interface under various loading conditions13.

The subsequent propagation of interfacial cracks depends on the interfacial toughness and the mode of loading (mode I, mode II, or mixed-mode). Mixed-mode fracture—combining tensile and shear stresses—is frequently observed in PMCs, making the study of mode mixities essential for predicting crack growth behavior. Numerical techniques such as finite element modeling (FEM) and cohesive zone models (CZM) are widely employed to evaluate stress distribution, identify potential debonding sites, and simulate interfacial crack propagation under various loading conditions4,14.

Reported studies in the literature indicate that early research primarily analyzed the fiber/matrix interface as a two-dimensional surface or layer. However, subsequent investigations have shown that such models are insufficient to fully characterize the contact region between the fibers and the matrix. In reality, the fiber–matrix junction contains a three-dimensional region, referred to as the “interphase,” where the structural properties of the fibers and matrix interact to form a distinct bonding zone.

Numerous analytical models have been developed to predict adhesion behavior within the interphase of PMCs. Simple rule-of-mixture (ROM) approaches estimate composite properties from fiber and matrix fractions but often oversimplify interphase interactions, neglecting interfacial bonding and stress concentrations. More advanced micromechanical models, such as the Halpin-Tsai equations, consider fiber geometry and orientation, improving predictions for high fiber-volume composites, though they still assume simplified interphase behavior. The Mori-Tanaka model offers a more rigorous treatment of micromechanical interactions, particularly for composites with heterogeneous microstructures.

Another approach involves the Functionally Graded Material (FGM) model, where the mechanical properties of the interphase vary continuously from the fiber to the matrix. By representing the interphase as a gradual transition zone, this method captures the progressive change in material behavior across the fiber-matrix interface. Different functional distributions of Young’s modulus along the interphase thickness have been investigated within representative unit cells, providing improved insight into interfacial stress distribution and composite performance15.

By incorporating a finite-thickness cohesive zone where progressive debonding occurs, CZMs capture the complex fracture behavior of the interphase, enabling reliable predictions of failure mechanisms and guiding the design of more durable and resilient composite materials.

Mechanical tests, including single-fiber composite tests16, fragmentation tests17–19, and pull-out tests, are commonly used to directly evaluate interfacial shear strength and fracture energy16. In single-fiber tests, a fiber embedded in a polymer matrix is subjected to tensile or shear loading, while fragmentation tests assess fiber–matrix debonding in composites with multiple fibers. Pull-out tests determine the force required to extract a fiber from the matrix20. Although these methods provide valuable quantitative data for validating analytical models, their interpretation can be challenging due to variations in specimen preparation, failure modes, and the influence of other constituents16.

Microscopic techniques, such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM)21,22 and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), provide detailed insights into the morphology and microstructure of the fiber–matrix interphase. SEM reveals fracture surface features, including the location and nature of debonding, while TEM enables nanoscale observation of interfacial structures such as interdiffusion layers23. These methods complement mechanical testing by providing qualitative information on bond quality, with SEM observations indicating an average interphase thickness of approximately 2 μm24.

Spectroscopic and advanced characterization techniques further enhance understanding of the interphase. Laser Raman spectroscopy (LRS)25 and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)16,26 reveal interfacial chemistry and bonding, while X-ray computed tomography (XCT)3 provides 3D imaging of fiber distribution and voids. Nanoindentation2 measures local mechanical properties, including gradients in elastic modulus and hardness across the interphase. Together, these methods offer a comprehensive view of the structural, chemical, and mechanical behavior of the interphase region.

The adhesion properties of the interphase are governed by the physicochemical characteristics of fibers and polymer matrices, which control compatibility, wetting, and interdiffusion. Surface treatments, such as sizing20 and chemical modifications16,21,26, enhance bonding by altering fiber surface chemistry, with fiber type also playing a key role: carbon fibers generally exhibit strong adhesion2, glass fibers often require treatment2, and natural fibers need specialized treatments like alkali or silane coupling agents15,25. Matrix properties—including viscosity, polarity, and molecular weight—further influence wetting and chain interdiffusion27, while additives such as coupling agents26,28, nanoparticles29,30, and other modifiers15,31 can improve chemical bonding, mechanical interlocking, and overall compatibility. Together, these factors are critical for tailoring interfacial properties and optimizing composite performance.

Fiber pull-out and fiber breakage are major contributors to composite failure, alongside matrix cracking and void formation. Fiber pull-out occurs when debonded fibers slide out of the matrix, governed primarily by interfacial shear strength and fiber length, whereas fiber breakage results when tensile stresses exceed the fiber’s strength. Matrix cracking can propagate toward the fiber–matrix interface, triggering debonding, while voids—often introduced during processing due to incomplete wetting or trapped air—reduce adhesion strength and promote premature failure. The interplay between these mechanisms determines the overall fracture behavior and durability of polymer matrix composites32,33.

Manufacturing conditions, including curing temperature, pressure, and time, strongly influence interfacial quality in composites. Proper temperature enhances wetting and bonding, while excessive heat can cause residual stresses or degradation. Pressure minimizes voids and improves fiber–matrix contact, and curing time controls crosslinking and stress transfer. Optimizing these parameters is therefore essential for strong adhesion and enhanced composite performance30.

This paper presents experimental and numerical analyses of single-fiber composite specimens to investigate the mechanical properties of the glass fiber–epoxy interphase. Tensile test results are compared with FEM simulations of identical specimens modeled with three distinct phases. Two modeling approaches are considered: one with a radially varying Young’s modulus across the interphase, and another using a cohesive zone model (CZM) at the fiber–matrix interphase.

Specimens fabrication

Several relevant standards are available for determining the tensile properties of plastic materials. Among them, ASTM D638-14 outlines procedures for testing both unreinforced and reinforced plastics using dumbbell-shaped specimens. This standard offers different specimen types based on the material’s geometry and mechanical characteristics. Type IV specimens are particularly recommended for materials exhibiting higher ductility and a thickness less than 4 mm.

In this study, specimens with ASTM D638-14 (Type IV) dimensions were fabricated to evaluate the accuracy of the tensile test setup equipped with strain gauges, by comparing the overall mechanical response of these specimens to local strain measurements obtained from Digital Image Correlation (DIC) is an optical technique that enables precise measurement of displacement34 on standard specimens. Importantly, to investigate the properties of the interphase region, it is necessary for this region to represent a considerable fraction of the overall specimen. To achieve this, the thickness of the composite specimens was minimized, thereby amplifying the contribution of the interphase properties to the overall behavior of the composite, particularly in terms of its elastic response.

For preparation of the single-fiber composite specimens, the EPL-1012 epoxy resin was utilized in conjunction with E-glass fibers; Epoxies are thermosetting prepolymers composed of molecules containing multiple epoxide groups. This unique characteristic enables epoxies to undergo a curing process, transforming into durable final products with exceptional adhesive properties and high chemical resistance35.

The hardener employed alongside EPL-1012 plays a pivotal role in initiating the curing process, thereby converting the resin into a rigid thermosetting polymer matrix. In this study, an amine-based hardener, predominantly composed of aromatic amines, was selected due to its high reactivity and capacity to form robust three-dimensional cross-linked networks, which are critical for enhancing mechanical performance. Depending on the formulation, hardeners may include amines, polyamides, or anhydrides. Precise control over the mixing ratio and curing conditions is imperative to achieve optimal mechanical and thermal properties. Based on the specifications provided in the technical datasheet, the recommended mixing ratio of the hardener to resin is 15:100.

The primary advantages of glass fibers include their high strength-to-weight ratio and cost-effectiveness, while a significant limitation is their relatively poor adhesion to polymer matrices, which can affect the overall mechanical performance of the composite material. E-glass fiber is one of the most widely used glass fibers in various industries, distinguished by its relatively larger diameter compared to other types. Previous studies using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) have reported an average diameter of about 20 μm (Fig. 2.a)36. This larger diameter promotes the formation of an expanded interphase region around the fibers, which significantly affects the composite’s final mechanical and physical properties.

(a) SEM images of E-glass fiber36. (b) Dimensions of the single fiber composite specimen.

Thin molds with the specified dimensions (Fig. 2.b), were fabricated by laser cutting silicone sheets with a thickness of 0.5 mm (Fig. 3). A single fiber was precisely positioned at the center of each mold, and EPL-1012 epoxy resin, mixed with the hardener at the specified ratio, was injected into the molds.

(a) Laser cutting. (b) Silicon molds.

The selection of an appropriate curing cycle depends on the type of matrix material. For the resin system employed in this study, the manufacturer recommends two curing schedules: either 6 h at 60 °C or 7 days at room temperature. Maintaining proper mixing ratios and curing conditions is essential for achieving the desired mechanical and thermal properties.

In this study, to maximize the mechanical performance of the composite, the prepared samples were initially preheated at 60 °C for 5 min to eliminate air bubbles entrapped during the mixing process. The specimens were then cured at room temperature for 7 days to ensure complete polymerization. It is worth to note that by curing the samples at room temperature, the curing residual stresses are not occurred. After curing, in order to increase the fiber volume fraction within the composite structure and consequently enhance the influence of interphase properties on the composite’s overall behavior, the specimens were carefully sanded to reduce their thickness to 0.2 mm (Fig. 4).

Experimental results

The mechanical properties of E-glass fibers were characterized in a recent study by the authors37 through a series of single-fiber tensile tests performed at very low strain rates (Fig. 5a). As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the fibers exhibited a fully elastic response throughout the tested strain range, with a measured Young’s modulus of 70,000 MPa.

(a) Single glass fiber tensile test36. (b) Stress-Strain behavior of glass fiber36.

The mechanical properties of both single-fiber composite specimens and fiber-free small-sized specimens were subsequently evaluated through tensile tests conducted to failure (Fig. 6.a). The overall strain of each specimen during testing was determined from the precise displacement measured between the grips of the tensile testing machine throughout the experiment. To ensure accurate characterization of the mechanical response, all tests were performed at a very low strain rate.

(a) Tensile test setup for composite specimens, (b) Comparison of stress-strain results obtained from DIC and the Tensile Testing Setup for fiber-free specimen.

To validate these measurements, multiple specimens were tested under identical conditions, and the average values of their mechanical properties were calculated. In addition, the validity of the stress–strain results obtained from the tensile testing setup was verified using the Digital Image Correlation (DIC) technique on a fiber-free specimen with standard dimensions. For this purpose, local strains in the specimen’s central region, where the stress distribution is relatively uniform, were obtained from two DIC-based virtual extensometers and compared with the displacement-based measurements from the tensile testing machine. As shown in Fig. 6(b), the good consistency between the two methods, both in terms of the overall response and the maximum applicable stress, confirms the reliability of the experimental procedure. The slightly higher strain values observed in the central region are attributed to localized deformation and the onset of necking.

The results indicate that the single-fiber composite specimens exhibited a slightly larger Young’s modulus compared to the unreinforced specimens of identical dimensions, a difference attributable to the properties of the interphase region. Moreover, the observations show that the composite specimens failed near the ultimate strain of the fibers indicating the small effects of fragmentation or other progressive damage mechanisms for single-fiber composite specimens. This behavior is likely due to the thin geometry of the specimens, which restricts the development of such failure modes.

Finite element modeling

The single-fiber composite specimen is modeled as a three-phase material, comprising the matrix, fiber, and interphase, using FEM. The properties of the interphase are analyzed through two distinct approaches: the Cohesive Zone Model (CZM) to simulate element separation and damage initiation within the interphase, and the Functionally Graded Material (FGM) model to account for the gradual variation of material properties across the interphase region.

For finite element analysis, a single-fiber composite specimen with specified dimensions was modeled using Abaqus software. The elastic properties of the constituent materials, including the glass fiber and epoxy resin matrix, were independently defined. The Young’s modulus of the glass fiber was set to 70,000 MPa, while the Young’s modulus of the epoxy resin was set to 3,000 MPa. To reduce computational effort, geometric and loading symmetries were utilized, and only a quarter of the specimen was modeled by applying symmetry boundary conditions on the and planes.

The boundary conditions were defined by applying a symmetry constraint along the z-axis at one end of the specimen, while a tensile load was imposed on the opposite end. The load was incrementally increased in a controlled manner, allowing the mechanical response of the composite specimen to be characterized throughout the loading process. Additionally, owing to the uniformity of the stress distribution along the specimen, only a 5-mm section was modeled for the assessment of its elastic behavior.

Nonlinear finite element analysis (FEA) was performed using three-dimensional solid elements to achieve accurate modeling of the interphase under various analytical conditions. The Young’s modulus, as the key parameter governing the elastic response of the composite specimen, was computed by averaging the stress and strain extracted from multiple representative elements along the principal stress direction. To ensure the robustness and reliability of the numerical results, a mesh-dependency study was carried out to assess the sensitivity of the analysis to the discretization scheme. The FEA was conducted using three mesh densities consisting of 12,000, 80,500, and 235,000 elements, respectively. The displacement of a selected node on one side of the model was monitored, and the results demonstrated convergence with an error of less than 0.002%.

In models employing CZM approach (Fig. 9), the cohesive elements embedded within the interphase region are responsible for transferring stresses between the fiber and the matrix. As illustrated in the figure, the elements located at the loading surface reach their maximum traction at a load threshold of 50 MPa, beyond which damage initiation occurs. Up to this level, the specimen exhibits fully elastic behavior. Once the applied load exceeds this threshold, the interphase cohesive elements progressively attain their traction limit, undergo damage evolution, and ultimately fail, leading to separation and the formation of a damaged or voided region that interrupts stress transfer. Accordingly, the elastic regime in CZM-based modeling is defined up to this critical load. To capture damage initiation and the subsequent separation of interphase elements, the scalar stiffness degradation parameter (SDEG) is monitored. As shown in Fig. 10, SDEG represents the reduction in stiffness of cohesive elements relative to their initial cohesive stiffness, ranging from zero in the undamaged (elastic) region of the mixed-mode CZM law to one at complete failure of the CZM element. To accurately represent the mechanical response of the interphase and maintain simulation fidelity, a single layer of three-dimensional cohesive elements must be incorporated within the interphase region.

CZM model

Cohesive zone modeling (CZM) is a sophisticated numerical approach employed to simulate the behavior of bonding and adhesive regions or interfacial zones within materials where failure, delamination, or surface separation may occur. CZM facilitates the investigation of the interphase response under applied loads, particularly in the presence of cracks, fractures, or other surface discontinuities. This methodology is extensively utilized for modeling the adhesive behavior of materials in layered composites, with several standardized tests available for characterizing the cohesive properties of such systems. Notably, the double cantilever beam (DCB) and end notched flexure (ENF) tests are widely recognized method for mode-I and mode-II damage conditions. Specific experimental techniques for identifying the cohesive properties of the interphase have been outlined in the introduction, emphasizing the pivotal role of designing tailored experiments to accurately characterize these properties within the scope of this research. To describe the mechanical behavior and failure mechanisms within the cohesive zone, various models are employed, including bilinear, trapezoidal and exponential models (Fig. 11.a).

(a) Different models of cohesive zone behavior37, (b) Traction-Separation diagram38.

The bilinear model is one of the most widely used methods for simulating progressive material failure due to its effective balance of simplicity and accuracy. While the exponential model provides a more detailed description of the cohesive zone, the bilinear model remains a practical and reliable alternative, making it suitable for the present study. The Traction–Separation law (Fig. 11.b) is a key concept in CZM, defining the relationship between traction (force per unit area) and separation (relative displacement) within an interphase. Traction drives the detachment of surfaces, while separation measures the extent of interfacial damage. This law is essential for capturing the mechanical behavior of the cohesive zone and predicting failure mechanisms in composite materials.

When loading is applied under specific conditions and in different modes, additional parameters are required to evaluate the influence of these loading modes on the mechanical behavior of the bonded region. In the simulations performed in this study, separation of the interphase elements occurs predominantly under mode-II loading. The CZM formulation was implemented in Abaqus using a mode-independent traction–separation law defined in the Properties module, where the linear cohesive behavior and corresponding parameters were specified. The adhesive response was assumed to be identical for all separation modes, and therefore, the contributions of the other modes were considered negligible in the overall results.

In selecting the parameters for the traction–separation curve, several combinations of interphase properties—such as cohesive strength and fracture energy (toughness)—were evaluated. The combination that produced an effective elastic modulus most closely matching the experimental measurements was chosen (Table ***). Functionally Graded Material (FGM)

Near the fiber surface, a gradient in the distribution of molecular constituents is typically observed due to the molecular interactions between the glass fiber and the epoxy matrix. This phenomenon reveals the presence of a transitional region—commonly referred to as a Functionally Graded Material (FGM)—in which the mechanical and chemical properties vary gradually across the interphase. In this study, the interphase region is modeled as an FGM characterized by a quadratic variation in Young’s modulus. According to previous investigations15, several functional forms have been proposed for describing the distribution of Young’s modulus within this region. Among these candidates, the selected quadratic function provides an elastic response that most closely matches the experimental behavior (Eqs. 3, 4). Thus, after evaluating multiple distribution functions, the one demonstrating the highest agreement with test results was adopted as the optimal representation of the Young’s modulus variation across the interphase. To satisfy equilibrium and compatibility conditions between the fiber and the surrounding matrix, the material properties within the interphase region must vary continuously as a function of the radial distance from the fiber surface (Fig. 13). This region, referred to as the functionally graded interphase, is defined analogously to conventional FGMs and is conceptualized as an infinite series of concentric layers spanning the interfacial zone. Each layer is assumed to maintain perfect bonding with its adjacent layers, ensuring full displacement compatibility and preventing interfacial separation or defect formation. Despite the perfect bonding assumption, the stress state within the interphase evolves gradually from one layer to the next, giving rise to a smooth and continuous stress distribution across the region (Fig. 14).

Unit cell configuration15.

To accurately implement this spatially varying material behavior in the finite element model, the USDFLD (User-Defined Field) subroutine in ABAQUS is employed. This subroutine enables the assignment of field variables as explicit functions of radial position, from which the local material properties—such as Young’s modulus—are computed at each integration point. Through USDFLD, the graded nature of the interphase is captured with high fidelity, ensuring that the numerical model reflects the intended functional variation consistent with the physical interphase behavior.

The boundary conditions were defined similarly to the CZM approach. A symmetry boundary condition was applied along the z-axis at one end of the specimen, while a gradually increasing load was imposed at the opposite end. The entire model was discretized using quadratic elements to ensure higher accuracy in capturing stress and displacement gradients (Figs. 15 and 16).

Results

The traction–separation law for CZM was defined with an interfacial fracture energy of 0.03 mJ/mm² and an interfacial strength of 10 MPa, values that govern both the onset of interfacial debonding and the energy required for full separation. These parameters allow the model to realistically simulate the stress transfer and energy dissipation processes observed during testing. The bilinear traction–separation relationship effectively captures both the initial elastic loading and the progressive softening associated with interphase damage evolution.

The initiation and progression of damage in the interphase region were investigated. The SDEG parameter, obtained as an output variable from ABAQUS, was used to characterize the damage evolution. The gradual increase of this damage parameter with increasing applied load is illustrated in Figs. 17, 18, 19 and 20.

A direct comparison between the CZM and functionally graded material (FGM) approaches highlights their fundamental differences. While the FGM model represents the elastic response uniformly across all regions of the composite, including the interphase, it does not capture the localized damage and debonding effects. this limitation becomes evident in the finite element method (FEM) contour plots shown in Figs. 21, 22, 23 and 24, where the CZM model clearly demonstrates stress concentration zones and interfacial damage progression, whereas the FGM model depicts a smooth but less realistic distribution.

The cohesive zone modeling (CZM) approach demonstrates superior capability in simulating the elastic behavior of the composite sample, as it more accurately captures the initiation and progression of damage within the interphase region. Unlike purely elastic models, CZM allows localized damage initiation to be identified before any significant global stiffness reduction occurs. Even when elements within the interphase enter the damage zone, the composite sample continues to exhibit elastic behavior at the macroscopic scale, which aligns well with experimental observations of composites.

Conclusion

The results confirm that cohesive zone modeling (CZM) provides a more realistic representation of interfacial adhesion behavior in composite materials compared to the functionally graded material (FGM) approach. By incorporating fracture energy and interfacial strength into the traction–separation law, CZM successfully captures both elastic response and the progressive initiation of interphase damage, while the FGM model oversimplifies the interfacial behavior.

The superior agreement of the CZM predictions with experimental tensile test results validates its suitability for studying interfacial mechanics in composites. Overall, CZM is identified as the more reliable and physically representative approach for simulating interfacial adhesion behavior in composite systems.
