Lupus vorax: A rare case of facial tuberculosis in a patient with Noonan syndrome
Authors: Francesco La Monaca, Stefano Buffon, Giovanni Genovese, Giorgio Alberto Croci, Carlo Alberto Maronese, Federica Derlino, Angelo Valerio Marzano, Luisa Sarno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLupus vorax is a rare, aggressive ulcerative variant of lupus vulgaris, the most common form of cutaneous tuberculosis, characterized by rapid tissue destruction, extensive scarring, and potential disfigurement. We report the case of a 30-year-old man with Noonan syndrome who presented with chronic facial ulcerations initially suspected to represent pyoderma gangrenosum (PG). The lesion, involving the upper lip, nasal alae, and left eyelid region, had been present for two years with progressive worsening. Initial histology suggested PG or granulomatosis with polyangiitis; however, PCR and culture from a subsequent biopsy confirmed a multisensitive Mycobacterium tuberculosis strain. Quadruple anti-tubercular therapy led to complete lesion resolution within nine months, leaving residual scarring and functional sequelae. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of differentiating PG from infectious mimickers, the need for multiple diagnostic modalities in atypical presentations, and the importance of considering rare etiologies, even in patients with genetic disorders potentially associated with immune dysregulation.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Lupus vorax, Pyoderma gangrenosum

Introduction

Cutaneous tuberculosis is mostly caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, but it may also result from Mycobacterium bovis or inoculation with the bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccine, an attenuated strain of M. bovis [1], [2]. While extrapulmonary tuberculosis accounts for approximately 10 % of all tuberculosis cases, cutaneous tuberculosis comprises only a small fraction of these [3].

Lupus vulgaris is a frequent presentation of cutaneous tuberculosis, typically occurring in individuals with moderate to high immunity against the tubercle bacilli and a high degree of tuberculin sensitivity [4]. Among its clinical variants, the ulcerative-vegetating form - also known as lupus vorax -is rare and particularly aggressive [5]. Lupus Vorax is characterized by skin ulceration associated with crusting, extensive scarring, and potential involvement of deeper tissues, which may result in disfigurement. Mucosal involvement has also been described.

Due to its protean clinical manifestations, lupus vulgaris may mimic a wide range of dermatologic conditions, including pyoderma gangrenosum (PG), leishmaniasis and granulomatosis with polyangiitis [5].

PG is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by painful ulcers with undermined, violaceous borders. It remains a diagnostic challenge, due to its clinical variability, lack of specific histopathological features, and broad differential diagnosis. Diagnostic delays and misdiagnoses are common. Histological examination is crucial to rule out granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and additional staining may be required to exclude infectious causes such as deep fungal infections, syphilis, leishmaniasis, and mycobacterial diseases, particularly in refractory or atypical cases [6], [7].

Herein, we present the case of a 30-year-old patient referred to our dermatology unit with a two-year history of facial ulcerations initially suspected to represent PG, which was ultimately diagnosed as lupus vorax.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male, affected by Noonan syndrome, presented to our clinic with a large, vegetating, partially bleeding ulcer involving the upper lip, the nasal alae and vestibule, and the medial canthus of the left eye (Fig. 1a). The lesion had been present for approximately two years and had progressively worsened. The patient denied any systemic symptoms. Prior to presentation, a maxillofacial CT scan had been performed at another hospital under the suspicion of a neoplastic process, but it showed no evidence of bone involvement.

A skin biopsy was performed under the initial clinical suspicion of PG. Histopathologic examination revealed pseudocarcinomatous epidermal hyperplasia, dermal inflammation with a prominent neutrophilic component, granulomas with multinucleated giant cells, endothelial swelling, occasional thrombi, and nuclear debris. The differential diagnosis suggested by the pathologist included PG and granulomatosis with polyangiitis.

Following the biopsy, the patient was lost to follow-up for a total of six months. He returned with significant worsening of the lesions. Clinical examination revealed an extensive ulcerative and vegetating lesion affecting the upper lip, with hypoplasia of the nasal tip and alae, cicatricial changes, and involvement of the upper and lower left eyelid with ulcerative and crusted vegetations (Fig. 1b). The lesion had grown to such an extent that it compromised oral hydration and feeding.

The patient was then hospitalized for further evaluation. A new skin biopsy was performed under the clinical suspicion of cutaneous mycobacterial infection versus granulomatosis with polyangiitis or PG (Fig. 2). Specimens were collected for culture and PCR analysis targeting the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra testing had already been performed on the first biopsy, while for the second biopsy PCR was directly carried out on the skin specimen. The culture was incubated on Lowenstein–Jensen medium. Histopathological examination of the second biopsy revealed a predominantly granulomatous dermatitis and mucositis with superimposed abscess formation. Ziehl–Neelsen staining did not reveal acid-fast bacilli. However, the patient’s QuantiFERON-TB test was positive, and both PCR and culture confirmed the presence of a multisensitive M. tuberculosis complex strain. The combination of histopathologic, molecular, and culture findings established the diagnosis of lupus vulgaris, vorax variant.

H/E, 40x), acanthosis with pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia is observed, alongside a dermal, inflammatory infiltrate comprising lymphocytes, accumulation of (mostly) neutrophilic granulocytes and histiocytes, with Langhans-type, multinucleated giant cells (image b. Based on these findings, oral anti-tubercular therapy with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol was initiated. Following clinical stabilization and improvement in oral intake, the patient was discharged with instructions to continue treatment at home.

The patient was regularly followed-up for the duration of anti-tubercular therapy, which was completed after nine months. At the final follow-up visit, the cutaneous lesions had fully resolved, leaving behind residual scarring with tissue retraction of the nasal tip, nostrils, and upper lip (Fig. 1c). A repeat CT scan of the maxillofacial region confirmed the absence of bone involvement, but showed nasal septal deviation and left-sided lagophthalmos. The patient was subsequently referred to otolaryngology and ophthalmology for further management.

Discussion

Lupus vorax represents a rare, ulcerative variant of lupus vulgaris, characterized by rapid tissue destruction, extensive scarring, and potential disfigurement [8]. While lupus vulgaris is the most common form of cutaneous tuberculosis, the vorax variant is infrequently reported in the literature and poses a significant diagnostic challenge, particularly in non-endemic regions. Clinically, it may closely mimic inflammatory, granulomatous, or infectious dermatoses, including pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) [9], leishmaniasis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and deep fungal infections. In our case, the chronic ulcerative lesions with undermined borders and absence of systemic symptoms initially suggested PG, leading to a delayed diagnosis.

The differentiation between PG and infectious mimickers is often difficult due to overlapping clinical and histopathological features. In PG, histology typically shows a dense neutrophilic infiltrate, whereas lupus vulgaris is associated with granulomatous inflammation; however, this distinction is not always clear-cut, especially in early or atypical stages. Ziehl–Neelsen staining can confirm mycobacterial etiology when positive, but its sensitivity is limited, particularly in paucibacillary cutaneous tuberculosis. In our patient, Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative, and the diagnosis was ultimately established through the combination of histopathologic findings, PCR positivity for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, and culture confirmation of a multisensitive strain. This underlines the importance of employing multiple diagnostic modalities in suspected cutaneous TB [10], [11].

Interestingly, our patient also had Noonan syndrome, a RASopathy caused by germline mutations in components of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway. Noonan syndrome has been increasingly recognized to confer an elevated risk of immune dysregulation, with reported associations to systemic lupus erythematosus, autoimmune thyroiditis, and celiac disease [12], [13], [14]. In a cohort of 42 molecularly confirmed RASopathy patients, 14 % fulfilled criteria for autoimmune diseases, and more than half had detectable circulating autoantibodies [14]. These findings suggest that aberrant RAS/MAPK signaling may disrupt immune tolerance or lymphocyte development, potentially contributing to susceptibility toward immune-mediated or infectious conditions. Lupus vulgaris — the ulcerative cutaneous manifestation of Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection — typically affects the face and may evolve into destructive skin ulcers if untreated. While Noonan syndrome has not been directly linked to increased susceptibility to cutaneous tuberculosis, the documented immunological impairments (autoimmunity, possible dysregulated T-cell responses) could hypothetically predispose individuals to atypical or opportunistic infections, including lupus vulgaris. However, as of now, there is no specific clinical or experimental evidence confirming such a link.

We report this rare case of lupus vorax initially misdiagnosed as PG to emphasize the importance of thorough clinical evaluation and the need to consider uncommon etiologies in dermatological practice. Early recognition of such entities is critical to avoid unnecessary treatments and to initiate appropriate therapy promptly. In our patient, anti-tubercular treatment with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol led to complete resolution of the cutaneous lesions within nine months, with marked improvement in quality of life, although residual scarring and functional sequelae persisted. This case also underscores the adage “old but gold” in clinical medicine, reminding clinicians of the enduring relevance of revisiting classic diseases in the era of evolving medical knowledge and technology.
