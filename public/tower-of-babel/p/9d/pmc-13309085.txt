Mechanisms and Safety of Therapeutic Strategies in Meibomian Gland Dysfunction: A Narrative Review
Authors: Conrad Helau, Shuowen Zhou, Liangyu Zhou, Ying Chen, Kendrick Co Shih, Hua Wang, Yau Kei Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is a chronic and widespread abnormality of the meibomian glands that often results in symptoms such as eye irritation and blurred vision, significantly affecting patients’ daily activities. While conventional treatments like warm compresses and meibomian gland expression remain widely used, their limitations have driven the development of novel therapeutic technologies.SummaryThis review synthesizes evidence from 71 clinical and experimental studies in Medline databases on both established and emerging therapeutic strategies for MGD, including physical interventions (warm compresses, eyelid cleansing, probing and expression, thermal pulsation systems such as LipiFlow®, and intense pulsed light) and pharmacological approaches. Across these modalities, most interventions improve symptoms, tear film stability, and meibum quality primarily by enhancing glandular outflow and modulating ocular surface inflammation. Device-based physical therapies typically offer greater and more long-lasting improvements than conventional methods but are limited by cost, access, and a lack of long-term safety data.Key MessagesThough the current range of MGD treatments cannot regenerate affected glands, their secreting functions can be greatly improved. Clinicians should therefore individualize treatment options according to disease stage, meibography findings, comorbid ocular surface conditions, and patient-specific factors such as tolerance, adherence, and cost.

Introduction

Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is a chronic, diffuse abnormality of the meibomian glands (MGs), commonly characterized by obstruction of the ducts with qualitative and quantitative reductions in glandular secretion. MGD often results in alterations of the tear film, symptoms of eye irritation, inflammation, and ocular surface disease [1]. The prevalence of MGD among different populations varies widely. The International Workshop on Meibomian Gland Dysfunction reported prevalence rates of 3.5–69.3% among populations globally over the age of 40 [2]. Asian population cohorts have higher prevalence rates, which may result from racial differences or increased genetic predisposition [2–5]. Numerous factors are identified to influence the pathogenesis of MGD, including intrinsic and extrinsic factors like age, sex, hormone levels, blepharitis, rosacea, as well as excess screen time, contact lens wear, smoking and alcohol consumption, Demodex mite infestations, and medications [2, 6, 7]. Lifestyle modifications, like limiting screen time, opting for alternatives to contact lenses, and reducing smoking and alcohol consumption, thus serve as efficient and convenient interventions for managing MGD [8].

MGD is widely considered to develop as a result of MG blockage, caused by hypertrophy of the ductal epithelium and hyperkeratinisation of the circular orifice epithelium [9]. Histologically, MG degeneration manifests as acinar atrophy and periacinar fibrosis that result in loss of functional glandular tissue [10, 11]. When this pathological change occurs, meibum expression is reduced, causing downstream issues like excess tear evaporation, elevated tear osmolarity, release of inflammatory cytokines, decreased tear film stability, and dry eye disease (DED) symptoms [12]. Hence, a series of chain reactions are connected together in a “vicious circle,” namely abnormal MG secretion, eyelid inflammation, conjunctival inflammation, corneal inflammation and damage, microbiological changes, and DED [6, 13].

Owing to the multifactorial nature of MGD pathogenesis, a universally accepted diagnostic criterion has yet to be established. However, in 2011, The International Workshop on Meibomian Gland Dysfunction proposed a staging framework based on meibum quality, gland expressibility, and corneal staining scores. This classification system ranges from stage 1, characterized by asymptomatic symptoms, up to stage 4, which includes several severe clinical manifestations like inflammation and lid margin dropout (Table 1). Furthermore, the framework accounts for the presence of co-existing ocular surface and/or eyelid disorders [13]. The staging of MGD severity enables eye care providers to decide on treatment strategies that are more effective for particular clinical scenarios.

The primary goal of treatment for MGD was to ameliorate the flow of MG secretions, and thus to help re-establish tear film stability [14]. Although lifestyle modifications should be recommended as initial steps in managing MGD, first-line treatments also include warm compresses, eyelid cleansing, and probing for the removal of obstructed meibum. Antibiotics and anti-inflammatory agents may be further administered to stabilize the local microenvironment [6, 15]. Artificial lubricants and fatty acid supplements can aid in improving tear film stability and meibum quality, respectively [16–18]. In addition to these conventional treatments, various innovative medical treatment options have emerged over the years. For example, electronic heating devices like LipiFlow® aim at decreasing meibum viscosity and increasing lipid levels present on the lid margin [19, 20], while intense pulsed light (IPL) therapy alleviates signs and symptoms of MGD by reducing telangiectasia and by targeting several other inflammatory mechanisms [21, 22]. Ultimately, these treatment options aim to optimize residual gland function but do not reverse the degeneration of MGs [23]. To the best of our knowledge, this review presents a comprehensive and up-to-date analysis of the mechanisms, effectiveness, and safety of MGD treatments. Previous reviews often focused on some specific categories of treatments such as warm compresses, omega-3 supplements or antibiotics rather than encompassing all available treatment modalities for MGD [24–26]. While efficacy and safety are typically the primary concerns when evaluating treatments, we have extended our discussion in conjunction with mechanisms in this review, providing a holistic profile of each therapy strategy.

Method

Search Strategy

A literature search was conducted on the Medline database via PubMed. The search spanned from the database’s inception to March 27, 2024. The following search terms were used to identify suitable articles in the English language: “(meibomian gland dysfunction) AND (treatment) NOT (review [Publication Type]) Filters: Clinical Trial, Randomized Controlled Trial.”

Inclusion Criteria

1.Potential RCTs or clinical trials that elucidated mechanism or evaluated safety of treatments for MGD.2.Patients with a definite diagnosis of MGD but without the following conditions that could potentially affect the assessment or interfere with the treatment effectiveness, e.g., active ocular infection or inflammation; ocular surgery; contact lens wearers; and systemic disease or medications associated with DED.

There were no age, gender, or race restrictions.

Study Selection

The screening and reviewing phases included duplication removal, title and abstract screening, and full-text screening. After the duplicate studies being removed in Covidence, the title and abstract screening and full-text screen were performed by two reviewers (C. Helau, S. Zhou) independently based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Differences regarding the inclusion/exclusion of a study were resolved through discussion. During title and abstract screening, irrelevant studies were excluded from the analysis. During full-text screening, studies unrelated to treatment safety or mechanisms were excluded from the analysis.

Results

The electronic searches last conducted on March 27, 2024, retrieved a total of 163 records (see Fig. 1 for the PRISMA flow chart). After including 53 additional records identified through citation searching and removing 17 duplicates, the remaining 199 titles and abstracts were screened while 97 references irrelevant to the scope of the review were excluded. Apart from 3 unretrieved references, 98 full-text copies of papers were assessed for eligibility. After excluding 27 ineligible articles, a total of 71 studies were included in the review.

Conventional Physical Treatments

Warm Compresses and Lid Hygiene. The basis of applying warm compresses is to remove the obstructed meibum in MG rapidly and at low-cost. The melting point of meibum is elevated in MGD conditions (35°C) compared to non‐MGD conditions (32°C) due to altered lipid composition [6, 64]. By applying direct heat to the eyelids, warm compresses reduce the lipid viscosity so the meibum can flow smoothly through the MG ducts to form a complete lipid layer on the tear film after blinking [15]. Warm compresses are shown to alleviate MGD symptoms by significantly reducing glandular secretion of lysophospholipids, which are detergent-like molecules that typically destabilize the tear film lipid layer and cause excessive tear evaporation [27].

Besides basic materials like washcloth or towel, commercially available warm compresses such as EyeGiene® masks and MGDRx eye bags® have been developed for convenient treatment for MGD. Significant improvement of ocular outcomes based on slit eye examinations has been shown after the treatment applying these warm compresses [28–30]. It is noteworthy that a 10-min application of MGDRx eye bags® can increase the outer eyelid temperature to 36.8°C [28], which is above the typical melting point of meibum in MGD patients [65, 66], thereby significantly decreasing meibum viscosity [29]. In a study applying heat pads as warm compresses, 2 adverse reactions out of a group of 140 patients (1.4%) were recorded, indicating a low incidence of complications [20]. No ocular disorders were found in slit-lamp microscopic examinations, while visual acuity (VA) and corneal topography remained unaffected after treatments in most of the patients [29, 67].

Demodex mites have been increasingly recognized as a significant contributing factor in MGD. Studies indicate that more than 50% of patients with MGD tend to exhibit co-existing Demodex infestation [68–70]. While these ectoparasites are typically considered commensal, they can cause pathological infestations in high densities [71]. Residing in eyelash follicles and MGs, Demodex mites shed chitin exoskeletons and generate biological debris, which contributes to physical ductal obstruction [72, 73]. Studies have also shown that Demodex mites are involved in inflammatory pathways that exacerbate ocular surface dysfunction [71, 73–76]. Eyelid hygiene is a key first-line management strategy for reducing MG obstruction and mitigating Demodex-associated inflammation. Regular cleansing with specialized scrubs, cleansers, and shampoos improves the local microenvironment by removing dead skin cells and debris associated with Demodex infestation [32]. This strategy, recommended once or twice a day, may reduce the risk of inflammation and secondary bacterial infections, thereby preventing the perpetuation of the “vicious cycle” of DED [1, 32, 77]. Tea tree oil scrubs, in particular, are recommended for MGD patients with secondary Demodex-related anterior blepharitis [13]. Eyelid cleansers are often commercially sold as pre-medicated wipes (e.g., OCuSOFT®, Johnson’s® baby shampoo), and foams (e.g., TheraTears®) [33]. These cleansers significantly improved the tear film lipid layer and reduced ocular inflammation for patients with blepharitis, who are at higher risk of developing MGD [78].

Although eyelid cleansing is a common treatment for MGD, safety concerns may arise due to its potential to cause ocular irritation or trigger allergies. In a prospective, randomized, single-blind trial, Johnson’s® baby shampoo top-to-toe (No More Tears formula) and OCuSOFT® Lid Scrub Original Foaming Eyelid Cleanser (OSO) were compared, revealing the two most common complications to be eye irritation (48.1% for Johnson’s® and 44.4% for OCuSOFT®) and burning sensations (51.9% for Johnson’s and 37.0% for OCuSOFT®) [33]. It is worth noting that some of the patients’ burning sensations can persist up to 12 weeks post treatment (37% for Johnson’s® and 40.7% for OCuSOFT®) [33]. Another study comparing tea tree oil (TTO) shampoo with regular eyelid shampoo on the treatment of MGD concluded that although TTO shampoo was more efficient than regular eyelid shampoo in soothing DED symptoms and in improving meibum expression, the former also resulted in more frequent ocular surface irritation over a 3-month period [34]. Aside from baby shampoo, the deep cleaning device, an electric brush that cleans the lid margins with 0.9% saline, has also been developed for eyelid hygiene [35]. No complications or adverse events occurred during the treatment process for the participants using the deep cleaning device, although the authors noted that there is a potential risk of corneal damage, which can be mitigated by wearing a safety contact lens.

Overall, warm compress and lid hygiene are among the least costly and most readily available options to alleviate MGD, as the relevant procedures can be performed by the patients themselves at home [32, 79, 80]. They are a mainstay treatment advised for all stages of MGD (Table 2). These strategies are generally safe as only minor problems like eye irritation, tearing, but not severe adverse events like VA decrease or allergic reaction, have been reported. However, the efficacy of these strategies highly relies on patient compliance, as long-term maintenance is necessary for managing MGD effectively [29].

Probing and Manual Expression of the MGs. Probing and manual expressing MG are other common techniques to relieve glandular obstruction. The former involves inserting sterile probes (often 2 and 4 mm in length) into the orifice of the MG ducts to alleviate any blockages and promote meibum secretion [12]. Meanwhile, manual MG expression (MGX) involves applying firm digital pressure to the lids with a paddle or rolling forceps before massaging them, allowing any stagnant meibum to be physically cleared from the ducts, leading to normal meibomian secretion and flow [12].

The effects of probing and MGX are usually rapid. Several studies found that many patients report immediate symptomatic relief post treatment, with restored meibum levels and significantly improved viscosity [81–83]. Probing also shows promising results as an adjuvant therapy to other MGD treatments. For instance, results of a study supported that the combination of probing with IPL could maximize the therapeutic benefits for patients with severe MGD [42]. The amelioration of tear break-up time (TBUT) and ocular symptoms such as Standard Patient Evaluation of Eye Dryness score (SPEED) was more apparent in the probing-IPL combination group than the probing-only and IPL-only groups [42]. However, though the effects of probing and manual expression are instant, these procedures need to be performed multiple times to keep the MG ducts unblocked in the long term [84]. For example, a study found that while TBUT values of MGD patients differed significantly before a single session of probing compared to 3 months after probing, Schirmer’s test I results showed no statistically significant differences [43]. Furthermore, a single procedure session was inadequate for 73.3% of patients (n = 30), as they exhibited signs of recurring MGD symptoms within 3 months post-probing. In contrast, patients who underwent probing procedures 3–4 times presented improved ocular outcomes up to 3 months post-treatment. These findings suggest that, similar to warm compresses, MG probing may require long-term administration to achieve sustained therapeutic benefits. However, MG probing may face challenges related to patient compliance, as noted with other long-term management strategies for MGD [44].

While studies show that patients encounter few adverse effects following probing [41], manual MGX is associated with a higher incidence of complications. In a study investigating the effects of a MG squeezer device, the operation was not a completely painless process even though topical anaesthesia has been applied [37]. Furthermore, participants who received the MG squeezer all had conjunctival congestion and mild puffiness of the eyelids for a few minutes after treatment. During the procedure of MG squeezing, subconjunctival or eyelid haemorrhages occurred in 22.2% of the patients, though all recovered the next day. No other serious adverse events were observed in a visit 1 month later. Studies combining MGX with warm compresses, however, observed no complications [38].

In general, the rapid effects of probing and manual expression of MG render them as suitable strategies for immediate relief of MGD symptoms. The International Workshop on Meibomian Gland Dysfunction recommends patients with stage 2 and above disease severity (Table 2) to institute “moderate to firm massage and expression of MG secretions” following lid hygiene and warming [13]. However, many patients undergoing manual MGX may face various degrees of haemorrhage and discomfort posttreatment, although these complications resolve eventually [39]. Combination therapy of probing or manual expression with warm compresses may help reduce inflammation and hasten recovery. However, while probing is associated with few adverse events, the procedure has to be performed in clinical settings. Additionally, the results of the procedure are highly contingent on the physician’s expertise and training [12]. Overall, both treatments are safe and effective in the short run but require careful administration to minimize potential complications.

LipiFlow®

The LipFlow® system (Johnson & Johnson Vision, Milpitas, CA, USA) is a commercially available vectored thermal pulsation therapy device for MGD. This 12-min clinical procedure is designed to heat the palpebral surfaces of both the upper and lower eyelids, while applying graded pulsatile pressure to the outer eyelid surfaces [20, 85]. The system consists of single-use lid warmers and eye cups [39]. The former directs dry heat from an electrically controlled heater to the anterior surface of both eyelids, and the latter straddles both sides of the eyelid and applies graded pulsatile pressure to the MG. These two mechanisms are performed simultaneously in the system to promote meibum secretion and clear MG obstruction [39, 46].

Despite being a recent development in MGD treatment, the vectored thermal pulse therapy device is clinically successful in relieving the signs and symptoms of MGD [20, 46, 85]. It reduces the viscosity of abnormally thick meibum through required heat, while simultaneously evacuates stagnant meibum that obstructs the MG ducts by applying graded pulsatile pressure to the eyelid [44]. Compared to conventional warm compresses which need reheating and replacement every 2 min for optimal heat transfer, this system features an embedded precision heater that maintains the temperature of the palpebral conjunctiva between 41 and 43°C [20].

The efficacy of LipiFlow® has been demonstrated in clinical trials conducted across multiple countries. In a study from the USA, a single session of LipiFlow® led to an increase in TBUT by 1.5 s and a reduction in SPEED scores by 6.2 points within 2 weeks, outperforming warm compresses (+0.1 s TBUT change, −3.5 points) [20]. This improvement in gland function was echoed in a Chinese trial, where MG secretion scores (MGS) in the LipiFlow® group saw a sustained increase from 8.84 to 12.84 over 3 months, a gain that was both larger and more durable than in the warm compress group [47]. An Austrian study reported a significant increase in TBUT from 5.6 to 8.8 s and a strong trend in meibum secretion score (MSS) improvement from 9.5 to 14.4 at the 3-month follow-up [25]. In addition, a research from Singapore confirmed that a single LipiFlow® session provides rapid and lasting symptomatic relief, reducing irritation scores by 30.5% at 4 weeks, that is equivalent to a level of improvement comparable to 4 weeks of warm compress twice daily [46]. Collectively, this body of evidence showcases LipiFlow®’s role as an effective and efficient treatment modality for MGD.

LipiFlow® has demonstrated a relatively favourable safety profile as a treatment with the reported occurrence of adverse effects ranging from 2.9% to 6.7% across clinical studies [20, 25, 46, 48, 86]. Although a few non-serious adverse events (eyelid pain, conjunctival vascular injection, blepharitis, foreign body sensation, eye dryness, allergic conjunctivitis, stye, and mild puffiness of the eyelids) were reported in a series of studies [20, 25, 46, 47, 49], some of them were not directly related to the procedure, and no serious device-related adverse events were reported. Best-corrected VA and intraocular pressure (IOP) were found to remain stable after the treatments [20, 46, 48].

IPL Therapy

IPL therapy initially emerged as a dermatological treatment for acne rosacea that works by emitting a spectrum of wavelengths ranging from the visible (580 nm) to near-infrared (1,200 nm) [86]. Concurrent ocular surface health improvements observed in patients undergoing IPL for dermatologic manifestations suggest its potential in MGD treatment [55, 87]. Thus, IPL therapy has been modified and refined for this purpose [88]. Although the exact mechanism of action has not been elucidated, multiple related hypotheses were proposed to explain its effects in alleviating MGD. When directed at the eyelids, the elevated temperature reduces viscosity of meibum, decreasing MG obstruction and replenishing the lipid layer of the tear film [52]. The absorption of heat from the IPL also stresses the endothelial walls of the blood vessels, inducing coagulation, thrombosis, and ultimately destruction of superficial blood vessels. As a result, this treatment reduces the scale of any inflammation contributing to MGD [53]. Furthermore, the targeted wavelengths of IPL may also prevent microbial proliferation at the eyelid by disrupting bacterial cell walls, mitigating the enzymatic effect of bacteria on meibum secretion [54].

A study focused on clinical outcomes of IPL treatment reported a significant improvement in tear film stability, with the mean TBUT rising from a pretreatment value of 4.5–14.88 s following three monthly treatments. Concurrently, patients experienced considerable symptomatic relief, reflected by a decrease in the OSDI score from 22.25 to 6.5 over the same time period [62]. A few other studies demonstrated similar TBUT improvements, ranging from +2.5 to +9.6 s across a span between 6 and 12 weeks [55, 60, 61].

Moreover, in a study comparing IPL and a conventional treatment combining eyelid gland massage and hot compresses, the former outperformed the latter in terms of total effective rates and the therapeutic effects [38]. The combination of IPL and MGX therapy is also likely to enhance the treatment efficacy [89, 90]. On a molecular level, IPL was proven to be useful in significantly reducing inflammatory cytokines such as CXCL1, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), TNF-α, IFN-γ, CCL11, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-13, IL-17A, and TIMP-1 [60, 62, 91–95]. As the level of several inflammatory cytokines have been shown to strongly correlate with MGD-related DED severity [89, 90], the reduction in inflammatory markers could be an important mechanism by which IPL helps ameliorate MGD. It is noteworthy that various cytokines showed a downward trend during treatment, suggesting that the vicious cycle of DED was suppressed [91].

Several studies have examined the adverse effects of IPL treatment. In a randomized controlled study, the incidence of adverse events was 8.9% in the IPL treatment group [96], with allergic conjunctivitis, bacterial conjunctivitis, skin pain, and blepharitis being reported [96]. However, cases of seasonal allergic conjunctivitis and blepharitis were determined by the treating physician to be unrelated to the procedure nor the device. Mild pain and burning, along with swelling or redness of face, were also reported in some studies [55–58]. In most cases, however, these symptoms self-resolved within a limited period. Furthermore, no device- or procedure-related adverse events were reported in many studies [38, 59–61, 93–95, 97–105].

IPL treatment still faces certain limitations despite its clinical efficacy. For instance, IPL cannot be used on subjects with a Fitzpatrick score higher than IV, as the particular wavelength of light puts patients with darker skin at a higher risk of skin damage [52]. The pigmented iris is also one of the most vulnerable regions as it absorbs light with the same wavelength range of IPL. Possible side effects are minor and transient, including pain, erythema, blistering, and superficial crusts [59]. IPL treatment is also operator-dependent and requires the patient to be in clinical settings for treatment. Additionally, the MG in the upper eyelids should not be directly radiated by IPL due to the risk of light penetration through the eyelid and affecting intraocular structures, which can cause permanent eye damage [52]. Therefore, protective eye shields must be placed over the eyes during treatment. Similar to LipiFlow®, the cost of IPL can be exorbitant as it costs USD 300 to USD 600 per session and multiple treatments may be needed to sustain long-term effects [6].

Given the eyelid-warming mechanism of LipiFlow® and IPL therapy, these treatments should be considered for patients with stage ≥2 MGD (Table 2). By applying controlled heat and, in the case of LipiFlow®, simultaneous mechanical pressure, these modalities help liquefy and express stagnant meibum, thereby alleviating gland obstruction and improving tear film stability. As a result, we propose that these treatment modalities are particularly beneficial for patients in the early to moderate stages of MGD, where gland function can still be preserved with appropriate intervention.

Innovative Physical Treatment Modalities

A series of innovative medical devices have been designed to deliver controlled thermal therapy to the eyelids. These thermal treatments were demonstrated to relieve MGD by sufficiently liquefying hardened meibum and clearing gland obstructions. Separate studies evaluating the performance of a novel eyelid-warming device (Blephasteam®) in MGD patients [106, 107] demonstrated a significant improvement in self-reported symptoms after 3 weeks and a reduction of OSDI from 39.1 to 26.6 marks after 4 weeks, though MGS and TBUT remain largely unchanged [106, 107]. Another thermo-mechanical device, Tixel (Novoxel®, Netanya, Israel), was designed to transfer heat to the skin by means of a titanium tip heated to a uniform temperature in between 397 and 400°C, briefly contacting the skin [108]. Improvements in parameters after 2 weeks including SPEED II scores (16.5–11.8), TBUT (2.7–6.5 s), corneal staining score (2.0–0.5), MGD score (2.7–1.2), and rate of lubricant use (3.4–1.9 times a day) revealed strong therapeutic potential of this device. No major side effects were observed during the follow-up periods. An original warm moist air device developed in Japan for MGD patients increased TBUT significantly from 4.7 to 8.6 s after 4 weeks of twice-daily application [109].

Besides thermal therapies, novel treatments in development for MGD have tried to target one or more of the primary mechanisms of MGD. Quantum molecular resonance is a technique in which low-intensity; high-frequency electric currents are administered to a biological tissue through contact electrodes. Evaluation of this treatment modality for MGD determined promising anti-inflammatory effects [110, 111] along with a well-tolerated safety profile [112]. Another advanced technology, low-level light therapy, employs near-infrared light to induce mitochondrial light absorption and cell photoactivation, leading to alterations in inflammatory protein expression [113]. A study comparing the safety and efficacy of low-level light therapy and IPL for the treatment of MGD observed satisfactory results in both treatments [97]. Although the lack of RCTs for these new emerging treatments results in insufficient evidence for clinical application, they still show great promise in enhancing the efficacy and safety of MGD treatment.

Antibiotics

The ocular surface is home to bacteria such as Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus aureus, and Propionibacterium acnes that produce lipolytic enzymes [12]. If these bacteria proliferate abnormally, these enzymes can significantly hydrolyse the lipids in meibum, leading to release of free fatty acids and inflammatory mediators that destabilize the tear film [144]. By controlling bacterial growth, antibiotics can inhibit bacterial lipase activity that otherwise degrades the structure of normal meibum [39].

Azithromycin and Doxycycline. The two antibiotics commonly prescribed for MGD patients are azithromycin, a broad‐spectrum macrolide antibiotic, and tetracycline derivatives such as doxycycline [145]. These two groups of drugs prevent protein synthesis and inhibit bacterial growth by selectively blocking prokaryotic ribosomal subunits required for mRNA translation [145–147]. Besides, both drugs have well-described broad anti-inflammatory effects beyond antibacterial activity, such as the suppressed production of inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8, and IL-17 [148–151]. Azithromycin, which has potent activity against many Gram-negative species, is also known to promote anti-inflammatory phenotypes among macrophages [152, 153]. Doxycycline inhibits MMP-9 activity [154, 155] and induces apoptosis of inflammatory cells [156]. Additionally, the application of topical azithromycin to the MGs lowers the elevated temperature at which abnormal meibum liquifies by affecting the phase transition parameters of the lipids secreted, restoring normal meibum flow [157].

The therapeutic efficacy of antibiotics in managing MGD is well-documented [114, 115, 157, 158]. Utilizing 1% azithromycin eye drops in conjunction with warming eyelid compress demonstrated significant improvements in lid inflammation, lipid layer quality and quantity, and tear film stability over a 2-week treatment period [158]. Aside from improvements in clinical signs, MGD patients who received 4 weeks of topical azithromycin therapy reported global amelioration in symptoms like itching, drying, welling, and burning sensations [157]. Oral doxycycline was also shown to reduce symptoms and signs of MGD [159]. In a study, patients with chronic refractory MGD were prescribed either 20 mg (low dose group) or 200 mg (high dose group) of doxycycline capsules twice a day. Compared to the control group, both the low and high dose groups showed statistically significant differences in TBUT, Schirmer test, and improvement of subjective symptoms [116].

An RCT showed that for patients with stage ≥2 MGD patients who did not respond to prior conservative management, a 5-day course of oral azithromycin provided significantly better overall clinical response than a 1-month course of oral doxycycline. The azithromycin treatment also led to fewer side effects and better ocular staining results [115]. While both drugs offer significant short-term relief of MGD signs and symptoms, patients on doxycycline are more likely to experience no symptom improvements, possibly due to antibiotic resistance [114, 115, 160]. The shorter duration and reduced dosage of azithromycin therapy may help improve treatment adherence and reduce side effects [115]. Yet, despite the merits of azithromycin, improvements in clinical signs (corneal fluorescein staining [CFS], TBUT, Schirmer’s test I, meibum grade) only persisted up to 3 months when daily topical 1.5% azithromycin was prescribed for 30 days, as observed in a retrospective study [117]. These findings suggest that antibiotics may only exert transient positive effects for MGD patients, with these improvements usually reverted upon drug cessation.

Moreover, adverse effects are notably prevalent in MGD treatments involving antibiotics. Systemic adverse effects are common, with a study documenting 2 cases of discontinuation in the azithromycin group and one in the doxycycline group caused by burning ocular sensations and severe headaches, respectively [118]. In addition, while ophthalmic adverse effects occurred at higher frequency in the azithromycin group, doxycycline exhibited a greater incidence of systemic adverse effects, reflecting distinct side effect profiles for these treatments [118]. Further research highlights gastrointestinal disturbances as prevalent systemic side effects. During a follow-up visit, gastrointestinal issues were significantly more frequent among MGD patients receiving oral doxycycline (26%) compared to oral azithromycin (4%) [114]. Afflicted patients also suffered from drug-induced nausea, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, and decreased appetite. Similarly, another study revealed that 54.9% of participants in the azithromycin eyedrop group experienced side effects such as eye irritation and blurred vision, whereas 19.8% of the oral doxycycline group reported gastrointestinal disturbances [119]. When taken systemically, doxycycline exhibits additional effects aside from gastrointestinal problems, such as itchy skin, urticaria and erythematous papules, and stomatitis. The adverse effects of doxycycline are also dose dependent; studies that prescribed 1-month medication reported side effects in 39.1% of the high dose patient group and 17.4% in the low dose group, as compared to 6.4% in the control group [116].

Various factors may also exacerbate the severity and prevalence of systemic adverse reactions. Several studies include patient exclusion criteria, noting that doxycycline and azithromycin are contraindicated for individuals with a history of liver disease, pregnancy, breastfeeding, contact lens wearing, allergies to azithromycin or tetracyclines, and conditions like vernal or atopic keratoconjunctivitis, among others [114, 116, 118, 119]. Additionally, these antibiotics may paradoxically cause further inflammation due to disruption of normal ocular microbiota [161]. These concerns may partly explain why, for instance, oral tetracycline derivatives should be reserved for stage ≥3 cases presenting with moderate to marked clinical signs, such as significant gland obstruction and pronounced inflammation, rather than for milder, more easily managed forms of MGD (Table 3) [13]. Healthcare providers should carefully consider the benefits and potential adverse effects when selecting between azithromycin and doxycycline for MGD treatment. Notably, azithromycin is often preferred due to its lower risk of systemic adverse effects compared to tetracyclines.

Other Antibiotics. Studies have also tested the use of minocycline and sulfacetamide for alleviating MGD, including their use in combination therapies [41, 123, 162]. Their side effects have been recorded with varying severities and types. For instance, while a combination of oral minocycline and artificial tears improved moderate and severe MGD symptoms by reducing inflammatory cytokine levels, notably IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17α, and tumour necrosis factor-α [162]. Conversely, no serious adverse effects occurred in trials involving a combination of topical sulfacetamide and prednisolone after MG probing [41]. Likewise, the use of sulfacetamide and betamethasone demonstrated no allergic reactions among patients with IOP measurements remaining within normal limits [123]. These findings indicate that while adverse effects are present in other antibiotic therapies for MGD, their safety profile varies largely depending on specific treatment regimen and combination. Fortunately, these studies reported recovery from side effects following discontinuation of treatment [162]. If a MGD patient also has a history of or exhibit anterior blepharitis, topical antibiotic or antibiotic-steroid combinations are recommended adjunct treatments [13]. However, these antibiotics have not been as extensively investigated as azithromycin and doxycycline, and further research needs to be done to better elucidate their mechanisms of action.

Anti-Inflammatory Agents

Inflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of MGD, commonly manifesting as blepharitis and meibomitis [121]. Certain anti-inflammatory agents have the potential to alleviate chronic MGD conditions by targeting the underlying inflammatory mechanisms that exacerbate glandular abnormalities.

Corticosteroids. Studies testing low dosages of corticosteroids, such as prednisolone [41], betamethasone [123], and loteprednol [124], when paired with other conventional treatments such as antibiotics, probing, or eyelid scrubs, lead to a substantial degree of MGD relief with few significant adverse effects. Parameters such as Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) and Symptom Assessment iN Dry Eye (SANDE) significantly decreased following combined treatment of MG probing and post-procedural topical sulfacetamide/prednisolone ointment [41], while Schirmer’s scores significantly improved [123]. Steroid therapies are recommended for patients with MGD who have inflammatory comorbidities, including exacerbated inflammatory ocular surface diseases, phlyctenular keratitis, and chalazions [13]. The International Workshop on Meibomian Gland Dysfunction in 2011 also concluded that topical corticosteroid therapy should be only used in the treatment of acute and transient exacerbations in MGD and patients must be regularly monitored for any elevation in IOP [13].

Beyond modulating expression of inflammatory genes, another class of steroid hormones, namely androgens, are shown to play a large role in maintaining MG physiology. In the glands, these hormones act to suppress genes associated with keratinization, such as Sprr2a, Sprr2b, Sprr3, keratin17, while stimulating keratinization-inhibiting genes like Sprr1a and genes like fatty acid synthase involved in lipid metabolism [163–166]. The role of androgens in MG function is further evidenced by studies that show patients with androgen deficiency, such as post-menopausal women, have an increased risk of developing MGD than healthy people [167–171].

However, investigations involving serum androgen levels with MGD and DED prevalence in post-menopausal women seem inconclusive. For instance, while a study found that among post-menopausal women, those with severe evaporative DED had significantly lower serum oestrogen and testosterone levels [172], another study reported no significant differences in androgen levels between MG expressibility and meibum quality gradings in the same demographic [173]. Furthermore, post-menopausal women who were given different variations of hormone replacement therapy had significantly different rates of DED [174]. Hence, the evidence around the efficacy of hormone replacement therapy and/or the potential of androgen supplementation as a treatment for MGD remains unclear.

Lifitegrast. Another immunosuppressing drug tested in MGD patients is lifitegrast. This non-steroidal medication antagonizes the binding of T-cell surface proteins, thereby inhibiting ocular surface T-cell-mediated inflammation [122]. When administered twice daily for 42 days, 5% lifitegrast ophthalmic solution was shown to significantly improve symptoms of eye dryness compared with placebo [127, 128] and was found to be safe and well tolerated in patients with inflammatory MGD [125].

Maxicalcitriol. Hyperkeratinisation of the MG orifice can lead to obstructive forms of MGD, resulting in glandular degeneration and atrophy [175]. Maxicalcitol, a non-calcaemic analogue of active vitamin D3, acts akin to androgens. It is posited to treat obstructive MGD because it suppresses the proliferation of keratinocytes and promotes their differentiation of keratinocytes by binding to vitamin D receptors [176]. Typically used to treat hyperkeratotic skin conditions such as psoriasis, ichthyosis, and acne vulgaris [177], topical maxicalcitol ointment application was demonstrated to reduce plugging of MG orifices and increase MG area [178].

MGD patients frequently exhibit signs of oxidative stress in their ocular tissues [179]. Antioxidant agents, such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), have shown potential in mitigating these symptoms [123, 180]. NAC serves as a precursor to reduced glutathione, a critical antioxidant that directly neutralizes reactive oxygen species in ocular tissues [123, 180, 181]. The therapeutic effects of NAC extend beyond its direct antioxidant properties; it also modulates inflammatory pathways by influencing the cellular redox status. Specifically, NAC decreases the activity of nuclear factor kappa B, a key transcription factor that regulates the expression of pro-inflammatory genes. By reducing nuclear factor kappa B activation, NAC attenuates inflammation, which is a major contributor to MGD pathophysiology [180]. Studies have shown significantly improved clinical symptoms of MGD following topical NAC therapy, demonstrating similar efficacy to topical steroid-antibiotic combination [123, 180].

Manuka Honey. In addition to antioxidant therapy, antimicrobial approaches for MGD have also been investigated [182]. Manuka honey eye gel has been explored as an adjunctive treatment alongside conventional MGD therapies such as warm compresses, lid massage, and eye drops [183]. Recognized for its antimicrobial and wound-healing properties, Manuka honey targets dysbiotic bacterial flora in the ocular region, a known contributor to oxidative stress and MGD pathophysiology [182, 184–186]. Its antimicrobial efficacy is attributed to multiple mechanisms, including its high osmolarity, acidic pH, and low water content, which collectively inhibit bacterial colonization [187]. Furthermore, upon dilution on the ocular surface, bee-derived glucose oxidase catalyzes the production of hydrogen peroxide, exerting additional antimicrobial effects [188].

Recent studies have demonstrated significant therapeutic benefits of Manuka honey-based treatments in MGD patients [183, 189, 190]. Patients treated with Optimel 98% gel showed significant improvements in meibum quality and gland expressibility. Furthermore, the same study determined that optimel 16% drops led to significant reductions in total eyelid marginal bacterial colony counts, notably S. epidermidis [182].

Beyond its antimicrobial properties, Manuka honey exhibits significant anti-inflammatory effects. It has been shown to downregulate MMP-9 expression, a proteolytic enzyme and proinflammatory cytokine implicated in MGD pathogenesis [191]. Notably, optimel treatments have been associated with a reduced reliance on lubricants, underscoring their multifaceted role in managing MGD through both antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [182, 189]. Given the central role of inflammation and DED in MGD pathogenesis, anti-inflammatory therapies have emerged as viable and effective treatment options, particularly for patients with stage ≥3 and above (Table 3).

Lipid-Based Eye Drops

An alternative approach to alleviate hyper-evaporation in MGD involves replenishing the tear film lipid layer with external ophthalmic solutions. NOV03 is one of the most advanced emerging therapies for DED associated with MGD, having just been approved by the FDA in 2023 [130]. It is a single-entity, water-free, preservative-free ophthalmic drop consisting of an anhydrous semifluorinated alkane, namely perfluorohexyloctane [130]. Aside from its ability to dissolve thickened meibum and reverse meibum flow blockages, the low surface tension and amphiphilic nature of the semifluorinated alkane forms a stable lipid film with optimal spreading abilities [131, 132]. An in vitro study found that compared to saline alone, saline with NOV03 saw a reduction in the evaporation rate by 80% [134]. Being non-aqueous in nature eliminates the need for preservatives that oftentimes may induce inflammation and exacerbate DED caused by MGD. Despite its amphiphilic qualities, NOV03 has a refractive index similar to that of water, thereby leading to minimal light scattering and visual disturbances [192, 193].

In two phase 3, multicenter, randomized, double-masked clinical trials [130, 133] conducted in the USA evaluating NOV03, signs and symptoms of DED were assessed through total CFS (National Eye Institute scale) and visual analogue scale (VAS) eye dryness score, respectively. After 8 weeks of treatment, significant improvements in CFS and VAS dryness score were reported, suggesting that NOV03 may be a highly effective treatment for DED associated with MGD. Meanwhile, no serious adverse events in either trial were reported. The most common ocular adverse events like blepharitis, conjunctival hyperaemia, conjunctival papillae, ocular hyperaemia, blurred vision, hordeolum, and VA reduction, did not raise clinically meaningful safety concerns. In these studies, NOV03 demonstrated good tolerability.

As a relatively new potential treatment, NOV03 is still in the process of becoming commercially available. However, its strong efficacy, ease of administration, and favourable safety profile, suggest that NOV03 can be a viable treatment option for MGD patients, especially if they have associated dry eye symptoms (Table 3) [130, 133]. More clinical research data in the future will contribute to a better understanding of this treatment modality.

Secernents

Diquafosol, a P2Y2 receptor agonist, has been shown to stimulate mucin secretion from conjunctival goblet cell, thereby reducing symptoms of DED [135]. Because P2Y2 receptor expression is present in the sebaceous and ductal cells of MGs, diquafosol also exerts an effect on meibum secretion, although the mechanism is still unclear [136, 137]. In a study evaluating the effect of topical diquafosol sodium on MGD, the tear lipid layer thickness (LLT) significantly increased from 12.2 to 17.0 nm after 3 months of daily treatment [138]. Moreover, the number of telangiectasias and plugged gland orifices significantly decreased after 1 month of treatment, potentially relieving patients with inflammatory forms of MGD. A prospective study investigating patients with obstructive MGD revealed a marked reduction in ocular symptoms, lid margin abnormalities, superficial punctate keratopathy (SPK) score and meibum grade after 4 months of topical diquafosol administration four times a day. Quantitative image analysis found that the ratio of the MG area relative to the total analysis area significantly increased. The morphological improvement of the glands corresponds with the potential efficacy for treating obstructive forms of MGD with this topical secernent [194]. However, diquafosol is currently solely approved for treatment of ocular diseases in Japan and select Asian countries, with no approvals granted in other regions including the USA [3, 139].

Fatty Acid Supplementation

The lipid composition of meibum in MGD patients is often abnormal, characterized by increased saturation of specific lipids and reduced meibum fluidity [195]. These pathological changes impair the normal spreading and stability of the tear film, raising the osmolarity index of the ocular surface and inducing inflammation and stress [195, 196]. The International Dry Eye Workshop and International Workshop on Meibomian Gland Dysfunction recommended advises incorporating dietary supplementation with omega-3 fatty acids to correct the altered lipid composition of meibum typically found in MGD patients [13, 196]. Mechanistically, oral omega-3 fatty acid supplementation is shown to increase levels of unsaturated fatty acids in meibum secretions [140]. Unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points and exist as liquid state at body temperature; consequently, this lipid composition improves meibum flow whilst preventing ductal blockage and stagnation. Furthermore, the metabolites released during the breakdown of omega-3 fatty acids contain anti-inflammatory properties that can impede the inflammatory pathways involved in MGD [141]. The increased quality of MG secretions reduces tear film evaporation and the symptoms of DED; studies have demonstrated significant improvements in tear film osmolarity, TBUT, and OSDI in patients with long-term supplementation compared to the control group [140, 142].

Concentrations of proteolytic enzymes such as MMP-9 may also mediate the effect of omega-3 fatty acids on ocular dryness and meibum disorders [140, 141]. Typically, MMP-9 is produced by epithelial cells on the ocular surface during stressful conditions like MGD or DED. Patients who took omega-3 fatty acid supplements for 12 weeks exhibited a reduction of MMP-9 levels by 67.9% [140]. In comparison, MMP-9 concentrations in the control group with linoleic acid reduced by 35.0%. The greater reduction in MMP-9 levels may alleviate hyperosmotic stress and degree of inflammation on the ocular surface epithelium [191, 197, 198].

Despite the observed improvements associated with omega-3 fatty acid supplementation for MGD, this treatment strategy may also exert minor side effects. A study involving 64 patients identified 2 (3.1%) patients with digestive upset following omega-3 treatment [142]. These symptoms arose within the first month of treatment; however, the patients recovered immediately after discontinuing the supplementation. Though fatty acid supplementation is previously advised as a complementary therapy for stage ≥2 disease patients rather than being assigned as a primary treatment approach (Table 3), recent studies have found significant improvement in MGD markers and test-results post-supplementation.

Discussion

MGD is a multifactorial condition that is commonly encountered in clinical practice [199]. Obstruction of terminal duct and qualitative/quantitative alterations in the secretion of the glands are key features of this disorder, leading to a range of symptoms and signs in patients [9]. This review has provided an overview of studies that investigated the mechanism, efficacy and safety of the treatments for MGD. The studies included in this review evaluated therapies broadly categorized into two groups: physical treatments, which includes conventional physical treatments, LipiFlow®, IPL, and some innovative physical treatment modalities, as well as pharmacological interventions.

Conventional physical treatments are commonly used as first-line strategies to ease MGD symptoms. Warm compresses and eyelid hygiene are often employed in conjunction to lower meibum viscosity whilst maintaining a healthy local microenvironment, thereby reducing the risk of inflammation and infection. MG probing and manual expression rely on direct mechanical action to unplug clogged glandular ducts, facilitating meibum flow onto the ocular surface [29, 32]. These treatment strategies are generally safe with few reported adverse events, such as eye irritation and transient conjunctivitis [20, 33, 37]. Though conventional physical treatments are widely accessible and cost-effective, their efficacy varies from case to case depending on the severity of the condition [29, 43]. To overcome these limitations, novel physical treatment modalities like LipiFlow® and IPL have been developed in recent years [20, 46, 48, 52, 130, 200]. These approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in ocular surface parameters, such as TBUT and OSDI even in moderate to advanced cases. Their efficacy can be further enhanced when combined with MGX [58, 59]. Although adverse events like mild pain and burning have been observed in a few studies, most of them typically resolve without intervention [131, 132].

In addition to physical treatments, several pharmacological medications have been investigated for MGD management, targeting different pathological mechanisms. For instance, antibiotics like azithromycin and doxycycline exhibit both antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, while anti-inflammatory agents, such as corticosteroids and Lifitegrast, modulate ocular surface inflammation. Lipid-based formulations, including NOV03, have shown promising results in stabilizing the tear film and improving clinical signs of MGD [130, 133]. However, systemic medications are often associated with notable side effects, limiting their long-term use [114, 119, 157]. Therefore, patients’ overall health conditions, including pre-existing diseases and medication tolerability, should be carefully assessed before initiating systemic therapy to minimize potential risks.

Given the multifactorial nature of MGD, a combined treatment approach integrating physical, pharmacological, and adjunctive therapies may offer superior outcomes by addressing different pathological mechanisms simultaneously. Tailoring treatment strategies based on disease severity (MGD stages) and individual patient profiles may help optimize therapeutic efficacy while reducing the risk of adverse effects.

In recent years, greater attention has been given to other causative factors of MGD, such as Demodex infestation, altered keratinization, and lipid dysregulation, which has led to the development of targeted therapeutic approaches. For example, lotilaner ophthalmic solution 0.25% (XDEMV®), a newly approved gamma-aminobutyric acid-gated chloride channel GABA-Cl antagonist, directly addresses Demodex-induced meibomianitis by inhibiting parasite-specific gamma-aminobutyric acid-gated chloride channel channels, leading to spastic paralysis and death of the mites [201]; the keratolytic effects of AZR-MD-001 may help reducing gland blocking and induce meibum flow [202]. Although clinical data on these emerging treatments remain limited, their mechanism-driven approach holds significant potential for more personalized and effective MGD management.

This narrative review has some limitations. Firstly, several studies employed retrospective designs rather than RCT designs [55, 94, 203]. Secondly, commercial devices used to assess tear film parameters may differ in their measurement principles and repeatability, so perhaps these metrics are most appropriately interpreted relative to the specific device and protocol used in each study rather than as interchangeable absolute values across trials [204]. Additionally, evidence of long-term efficacy and safety profile is insufficient for some of the novel or less common treatment strategies, such as Lipiflow and IPL [106, 108, 110, 130]. A meta-analysis was not undertaken due to considerable heterogeneity in evaluation parameters, making it unfeasible to perform a meaningful quantitative analysis.
