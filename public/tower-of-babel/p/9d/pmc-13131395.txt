Artificial Intelligence-Assisted CMR Scanning vs Standard-of-Care
Authors: Raymond Y. Kwong, Benedikt Bernhard, Michael Jerosch-Herold, Leslie K. Lee, Jon Hainer, Tuan Luu, Isabela Reis Marques, Marianna Daibes, Bob Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical adoption of cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is hampered by procedural complexity and magnetic resonance imaging access.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to investigate the benefits of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted CMR in clinical practice.MethodsPatients at a tertiary-care center underwent CMR via conventional, operator-directed (ODS) or AI-assisted (AIA) scanning over 3 years. Scan time, scan time variation, image quality using a validated 5-point scale, scan failure, and survival were compared between groups, stratified across CMR indications and technologists’ experience.ResultsFrom 2020 to 2024, 6,080 consecutive patients (age 57 ± 17 years, 55% males) underwent 6,545 studies (1,635, 25% AIA, 4,910, 75% ODS) for 5 primary indications. Average scan time and its coefficient of variation were reduced by 23% and 31%, respectively, with AIA compared to ODS (37.9 ± 6.9 vs 49.2 ± 13.0 min; P < 0.001). Scan time reduction by AIA was consistent across indications and range of technologists’ experience, and remained significant after adjusting for key demographic and clinical differences (P < 0.0001) or propensity score. Scan quality score was higher in AIA for cine images (4.09 ± 0.8 vs 4.03 ± 0.8; P < 0.001), late gadolinium enhancement (4.08 ± 0.8 vs 3.98 ± 0.7; P < 0.001), and perfusion (4.28 ± 0.6 vs 4.14 ± 0.6; P < 0.001). Scan failure leading to 90-day repeat CMR was lower in AIA compared to ODS (0.6% vs 1.4%, P < 0.001). After its introduction in 2021, AIA use progressively increased to 78% by 2023. At a median follow-up of 29 months, patient survival did not differ between groups (log-rank P = 0.500).ConclusionsIn this nonrandomized comparison, AIA improved scan time, scan time consistency, image quality, and 90-day scan failure across top CMR indications, compared to ODS.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) offers multifaceted imaging of cardiac structures, function, and tissue characterization and substantially impacts patient management. CMR has class I recommendations in numerous practice guidelines to provide crucial diagnostic and prognostic information, complementing echocardiography.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Its excellent ability to combine anatomical information with tissue characterization makes CMR a cost-effective tool in patients with various forms of cardiomyopathy.6,7 Expanding indications and growing evidence on its excellent diagnostic and prognostic features have led to an increase in the number of CMR scans worldwide.8, 9, 10 However, access to CMR scans is constrained due to the complexity of the scanning procedure, the availability of an experienced technologist, and the long scan time with the need for repetitive breath holds.11 The advent and increasing implementation of new sequences into clinical practice may lead to even longer and more complicated scan protocols. As a result, CMR practice is primarily restricted to experienced tertiary-care centers. High demand and long waiting times underscore the need for more efficient scanning methods.

Artificial intelligence (AI) in CMR image analysis has improved workflow by encompassing image segmentation, postprocessing, and analysis. However, AI-assisted and optimized CMR scanning have not been performed for routine clinical use. In this consecutive observational cohort, we tested the hypothesis that clinical care delivery using an AI-assisted CMR scanning method results in significant improvement of CMR scan time, consistency of CMR care delivery, and image quality, across common primary CMR indications.

Methods

Study design

Consecutive patients referred for CMR at the Brigham and Women’s Hospital for one of the 5 primary CMR indications (cardiomyopathy, stress imaging, ventricular arrhythmia, valvular heart disease, and pericardial diseases) were included in the present study between January 2020 and April 2024. Patients undergoing CMR due to congenital heart disease, research protocols, and scans aborted after <15 minutes were excluded. Before introducing the AI-assisted scanning (AIA) method, study protocols were standardized in 2019 to ensure a consistent protocol for all studies referred for each of the 5 indications. CMR protocols applied to both operator-directed scanning (ODS) and AIA, across the 5 indications, are presented in Table 1. Based on daily availability of scanning slots, scan date, and CMR technologists’ discretion, patients underwent CMR either by ODS (ie, MR-technologist) or utilizing an AIA as described subsequently. The decision to run an AIA or ODS scan was made before the scan started. CMR scans were conducted at either a 1.5-T (Magnetom Aera) or a 3-T scanner (Magnetom, Prisma Fit, both Siemens, Erlangen) and interpreted by a Core Cardiology Training Symposium level II or III reader, with at least one Core Cardiology Training Symposium level III supervising reader. In both groups gadolinium-based contrast agents were administered in a weight-based fashion (0.1 mmol/kg body weight for all rest and stress CMR studies). Likewise, regadenoson as preferred stress agent was administered as a 0.4 min/5 mL intravenous injection over 10 seconds followed by a 10 mL saline flush in stress scans. The study aligns with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee. AI-assisted CMR

CMR scanning using AIA was achieved using Vista AI MRI software version 3.2 (Vista AI, Inc), a Food and Drug Administration–approved platform that integrates machine learning to enhance CMR acquisition. This software, running on a workstation connected directly to an existing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner, automates and standardizes the image acquisition process. The software automates and enhances the consistency of the prescription of imaging planes, where machine learning determines the optimal 3-dimensional orientation for standard views. It also provides automated cardiac segmentation, generating masks that facilitate accurate assessment of cardiac function and measurements. The system can also detect image artifacts that may degrade diagnostic quality, helping technologists to determine whether reacquisition is necessary. In addition, the software automatically adjusts acquisition parameters to improve image quality, including but not limited to interrogation for flip angles, minimal field-of-view, center frequency (frequency scout), encoding-velocity of phase contrast imaging, and inversion time (using inversion time scout imaging) for late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging and its automated application. A variety of neuronet works are employed for these tasks. Exhaustive detailed network architecture and training descriptions would be publications in their own right. However, to address specific examples: 1) the localization process employs an end-to-end training strategy to regress the imaging planes based on a multihead input and multiple-output model used in an iterative fashion;12 2) masks generated by trained segmentation models are used to isolate specific structures to facilitate measurements;13,14 and 3) a variety of artifact detection models are used for different tasks from classifiers, regression models for in-distribution outliers.15,16 Other methods (U.S. patent US-10121104-B1) are being evaluated for out-of-distribution detectors.

Scan duration and image quality

The scan duration (in minutes) was calculated from the acquisition time of the first to the last image. Any time spent on image reconstruction was included in the scan duration. Scan time coefficient of variation (CV) was expressed as a ratio of the SD of scan time to the mean scan time. Scan duration of the AIA group includes all sequences in the original AIA protocol and any optional sequences deemed necessary to address clinical questions raised. Imaging quality of cine, perfusion, and LGE images were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (eg, 5: excellent quality, 4: good quality with mild artifacts, 3: diagnostic scan with moderate artifacts, 2: severe artifacts resulting in limited diagnostic yield, and 1: nondiagnostic scan). We defined a CMR study as efficient if image quality of cine, perfusion, and LGE were all ≥4 on the Likert scale and a total scan time <50 min.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with R software (version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Descriptive data were presented as frequencies (percentages) or mean ± SD. Non-normally distributed variables identified by a Shapiro-Wilk test were provided as the median and IQR. Chi-square or Fisher exact tests were conducted to compare frequency distributions for categorical variables. In contrast, independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare means or medians for continuous variables. The Levene test for homogeneity of variance was used to ascertain homoscedasticity of CMR scan time across groups, such as daytime intervals. A linear regression multivariable model was built to compare scan times by AIA vs ODS, adjusting to key demographic and clinical factors. Mixed-effect models are additionally presented in the supplemental material. To additionally balance differences across groups, we employed a propensity (PS)-matched analysis at a 1:1 matching with the “nearest neighbor method” and a caliper of 0.1. All variables from Table 2 were included in the matching. Boxplots and smoothed Loess spline curves were employed to depict the variations in scan times across daytime intervals visually. The need for clinically indicated follow-up CMR scans and survival following the index CMR were compared using Cox proportional hazards and a log-rank test for AIA vs ODS scans, with the end point of all-cause death extracted from the U.S. Social Security Death Index at standardized follow-up times. Scan failure leading to 90-day repeat CMR was defined as any repeat scan at our institution due to nondiagnostic or technically limited index scans or due to new or evolving clinical indications. Any random-occurring missing data were imputed to the mean value of the variable. Results

Patient selection and clinical characteristics

From 2020 to 2024, 6,080 consecutive patients (mean age 57.5 ± 16.7 years, 55% male) who underwent 6,545 CMR studies across the top 5 primary indications were included. Another 11 studies (4 AIA and 7 ODS) were excluded due to premature termination of scanning due to patient or technical factors. Of those, 4,910 (75.1%) and 1,635 (25.0%) patients underwent ODS and AIA, respectively (Figure 1). Clinical characteristics of the study cohort stratified by scanning type are shown in Table 2. Eighty-three percent of studies (N = 5,432) were performed on the 3T scanner. Cardiovascular risk factors were common, with 34%, 27%, 10%, and 6% of patients reporting a history of chronic arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and smoking, respectively. An established history of coronary artery disease by either prior myocardial infarction or prior percutaneous coronary intervention was present in 206 (3.1%) and 263 (4.0%), respectively. Twenty-one percent of studies (N = 1,369) were for hospitalized inpatients. Common symptoms triggering referral for CMR were dyspnea (n = 2,082; 31.8%) and chest pain (n = 1,238; 18.9%). The mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 52% and 54% in the ODS and the AIA group, respectively. Participants underwent CMR following protocols for suspected cardiomyopathy (n = 4,124, 63%), stress CMR for chest pain syndromes (n = 904, 13.8%), ventricular arrhythmia (n = 766, 11.7%), pericardial disease (n = 504, 7.7%), or valvular heart disease (n = 247, 3.8%) (Table 3).Figure 1Study CONSORT DiagramFlow diagram demonstrating the enrollment of patients to either artificial intelligence-assisted scanning (AIA) or operator dependent scanning (ODS) during the course of the study. Scan time reduction by AIA compared to ODS

The average scan time was 49.2 ± 13.0 min and 37.9 ± 6.9 min in the ODS and the AIA group, respectively (P < 0.001), representing an overall 23% relative scan time reduction (effect size −11.7 [95% CI: −11.2 to −12.2] min) in the AIA group. In the ODS group, the median scan time was 48.1 (IQR: 15.1; total range 30-119) minutes, whereas in the AIA group, the median scan time was 37.8 (IQR: 9.0; total range 16.1-78.5) minutes. Significant scan time reduction was observed across all primary CMR indications (Figure 2). Compared to the ODS method, scan time using the AIA method was reduced by 23.1% for assessment of cardiomyopathy (48.0 ± 12.2 vs 36.9 ± 6.1 min), by 18.5% for stress CMR (54.2 ± 13.5 vs 44.2 ± 7.8 min), by 26.0% for evaluation of ventricular arrhythmia (48.8 ± 12.5 vs 36.1 ± 6.5 min), by 18.4% for pericardial disease (46.7 ± 11.3 vs 38.1 ± 6.5 min), and by 28.9% for valvular disease (56.8 ± 18.9 vs 40.4 ± 5.6 min). Scan time reduction was consistent at 1.5 T scanners (15.4% reduction; ODS 49.5 ± 12.6 min vs AIA 41.9 ± 9.4 min) and at 3T scanners (23.2% reduction; ODS 49.2 ± 13.2 min vs AIA 37.8 ± 6.8 min). Compared to ODS, the CV of the scan time was reduced by 31% in the AIA group (26.4% vs 18.2%; P < 0.001). Similar pattern of CV reduction were observed across all 5 study indications (Figure 3). In Supplemental Figure 1, the results for the scanning time of the ODS and the AIA group in each period of the study is outlined. CMR experience of the scanning technologists (from 15 years to 3 months) did not modify the scan time reduction by AIA compared to ODS (effect modification P = 0.88) (Figure 4). The ODS group had an intraday variation with a higher average scan time during the early afternoon hours. In comparison, scan time in the AIA group was more consistent throughout the day (Figure 5). The random-effect SD for technologists was small (3.7 min) relative to residual scan-to-scan variability, indicating that differences among technologists do not explain the observed reduction in afternoon scan times with AIA (Supplemental Table 1). Scan time reduction in the AIA group was consistent in a PS-matched cohort, including 1,588 AIA and 1,588 ODS patients (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). Multivariable model of scan time comparing ODS and AIA

Table 2 demonstrated demographic and clinical factors different between ODS and AIA. Need for repeat CMR scanning and study cross-over

Compared to ODS, AIA group had a lower incidence of repeat CMR scanning during the follow-up period (n = 337, 6.9% vs n = 34, 2.1%, P < 0.01) and a longer time-to-repeat CMR scanning (log rank P < 0.001) (Figure 8). Compared to ODS, AIA group demonstrated a trend toward lower cumulative 30-day and 60-day rates of repeating CMR studies (P = 0.06 and P = 0.07, respectively); and significant lower cumulative 90-day and 1-year rates (P = 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 9). Study cross-over, defined as repeat CMR scanning using the alternative scanning method during the follow-up period, was observed in 1.5% (n = 76) of the ODS group, and 0.9% (n = 15) of the AIA group (P = 0.07). Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the performance utility of a new commercially-available AI-assisted CMR scanning technique in the delivery of a high-volume clinical care where the demand for high clinical throughput hangs in balance with diagnostic robustness. We observed that AIA achieved a significant reduction in scan time and an increase in scan time consistency, at comparable or even higher image quality compared to the standard ODS. For delivery of a high-volume CMR clinical service relying primarily on a single cardiovascular dedicated scanner at our institution, an overall 47% reduction of scan time variance (6.9 vs 13 minutes) represents a significant improvement in the consistency of imaging care delivery. The benefits from AIA were consistent across the 5 most common CMR indications at our institution, diverse performance experience of MRI technologists, and operational periods of workdays. Mortality following CMR was low, with no significant differences across groups. Our experience drawn from a large consecutive clinical cohort supports that AIA overcomes the major hurdles of CMR care deliver and may facilitates a wider clinical use of CMR. Our institution observed that the incremental costs of AIA were small, compared to the patient benefits from improving CMR access and the revenue generated from performing more CMR scans using existing MRI scanner.

Our observed scan time and scan time variance should be interpreted within the context of the demands of the clinical practice and preferred pulse sequence protocols. Recently, the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance white paper advocated for a routine 30-min CMR protocol that consisted of cine, stress perfusion, and LGE imaging only.17 Our program at a tertiary-care center was often tasked to address multiple clinical issues in complex or sicker patients that limits high patient throughput. Compared to the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance 30-min protocol, our protocols (Table 1) were more comprehensive: T1 and T2 mapping, and T2-weighted imaging were routinely included for cardiomyopathy; whereas rest perfusion was included for myocardial perfusion reserve. These differences should be considered when comparing reported mean scan times of CMR methods.

Previous studies investigating AI-assisted CMR scans were restricted to planning and shimming and still required significant input from MRI technologist during the scan.18, 19, 20 Additional studies evaluating automated planning have shown fewer planning errors and reduced idle time with AI-guided scanning compared ODS, suggesting improved efficiency in defined settings.21,22 Although these studies focused on individual components or proof-of-concept implementations, our study uniquely demonstrates the feasibility of an end-to-end AI-assisted CMR scan across multiple indications in routine practice in a large cohort, encompassing planning, tuning, and monitoring of various sequences. Conventional CMR protocols require a high-skilled CMR technologist, competent in the application of numerous pulse sequences and protocols.23 Achieving these complex abilities requires a time- and cost-intense education. Suboptimal training can decrease diagnostic efficacy of the scans and negatively impacts patient management by poorly tolerated long scan times and breath holds. Our analyses indicated that AI assistance not only helped to master these skills and achieve high scan efficacy in less experienced technologist, but also reduces scan times in assistance of experienced technicians. More accurate adjustment of scan planes, correct selection of T-inversion and velocity encoding times, and automized breath-hold commands likely accounted for the reduced scan time with AI guidance and avoided the need to reassess sequences and repeat-breath holds. Inaccurate slice prescriptions or coverage of the heart, and off-resonance artifacts on steady-state free precession-based sequences, were the most common reasons for reduced scan quality in cine and perfusion images. Inadequate myocardial nulling due to an incorrect inversion time was a common cause of reduced LGE image quality. AIA improved these factors compared to ODS, reflected by comparable or higher image quality scores.

In our study, also the coefficient of scan time variation was substantially reduced when the scan was AI guided. Interindividual differences and varying performances during the day are subject to human nature that can be accounted for with AI. Apart from scan time reduction, improvements of study-to-study uniformity are key elements to facilitate CMR scheduling and high patient throughput in clinical practices facing limited scanner accessibility.

Further improvement of AI-assisted scans could be achieved by AI-based real-time readings, that might offer the opportunity to further adjust scan protocols while scanning. Howard et al. developed a deep learning-based approach to screen images for abnormal features in the first few minutes. The developed system provided an automated early alert of abnormal features (eg, left or right ventricular dilatation, left ventricular hypertrophy, pleura effusion etc) with high diagnostic accuracy.24 Integrating a tool presented in this study and combining it with automized postprocessing analysis and quantitative analysis of volumetric measurements, myocardial strain, LGE, and perfusion might soon allow overcoming long scan durations as one of the most significant limitations of CMR.

Study limitations

The results of this study should be interpreted in the light of several limitations. First, AIA scanning protocols at present have not been fully developed to perform CMR scanning on patients with complex cardiac anatomy and congenital heart disease. However, our observations demonstrated that AIA could be easily adapted as the default method in the vast majority of CMR indications in an adult CMR program. Second, this was a nonrandomized comparison of AIA vs standard of care ODS methods. Patient characteristics and inpatient status differed, which we addressed by PS-matching and multivariable models. During the initial 12 months of AIA implementation and training, utilization of AIA was subject to technical familiarity in using AIA by the CMR technologist and the study indications (AIA protocols of valvular and pericardial disease were introduced in later months). However, utilization of AIA has progressively increased and became the default first line CMR method across all indications and the benefits in clinical throughput maintained in the AIA group. Third, despite the use of standardized indication-based protocols, the choice and number of pulse sequences vary slightly to address specific clinical questions. With the large sample size and the differences among protocols being minimal, we do not believe this issue impacted the key findings of the current study. Fourth, we were able to significantly increase imaging slot availability given the scan time improvement from AIA, but we do not have data at present to compare the cost-effectiveness of the 2 methods. Additional relevant metrics of comparative benefits from AIA were not easily quantifiable and thus not captured at present. For example, the work-related stress experienced by the technologists and comparative patient experience were not systemically quantified, although our technologists uniformly reported improved scanning-related work stress by AIA despite a higher average workload compared to before AIA was introduced. Fifth, a blinding of clinicians reading the scan and rating the image quality was not feasible. Expectancy bias cannot fully be ruled out.

Finally, data on downstream testing were not available and repeat CMR was the only surrogate for scan failure.

Conclusions

AI-assisted CMR scanning introduced over several months in our center reduced the average scan time and enhanced scan time consistency and clinical throughput by comparable or even higher image quality across all major CMR indications, compared to the current standard of care scanning.
