Incidence of Hodgkin and Non‐Hodgkin Lymphomas: Comparison of the Military and General Population
Authors: Kimberly R. Robins, Julie A. Bytnar, Christin B. DeStefano, Craig D. Shriver, Kangmin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLymphoma risk varies by demographics, environmental/occupational exposures, and medical care between active‐duty service members (ADSM) and the general US population (GUSP). This study compared age‐adjusted incidence rates and trends of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) between these groups.MethodsData were obtained from the Department of War's Central Cancer Registry and the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database, including individuals aged 18–59 diagnosed with lymphoma between 1998 and 2014. Incidence rate ratio (IRR) and 95% CIs were calculated to compare the incidence of HL and NHL between ADSM and GUSP, with analyses stratified by sex, race, age, stage, and histology.ResultsLymphoma incidence was lower in ADSM than GUSP (HL: 0.93 [0.86, 1.00]; NHL: 0.82 [0.77, 0.88]); specifically, for men (HL: 0.89 [0.82, 0.97]; NHL: 0.69 [0.64, 0.74]), White (HL: 0.87 [0.80, 0.95]; NHL: 0.80 [0.73, 0.86]), and Black individuals (HL: 0.82 [0.68, 0.99]; NHL: 0.61 [0.51, 0.73]). Incidence was lower among ADSM aged 18–39 (HL: 0.91 [0.84, 0.99]; NHL: 0.74 [0.68, 0.81]), but similar at ages 40–59. ADSM had lower HL (early‐stage: 0.86 [0.77, 0.96]; nodular lymphocyte predominant: 0.88 [0.80, 0.97]) and NHL (early stage: 0.86 [0.77, 0.96]; advanced stage: 0.87 [0.78, 0.97]; diffuse large B‐cell: 0.65 [0.58, 0.74]; other histologies: 0.83 [0.74, 0.93]) incidence.ConclusionLymphoma incidence was lower in ADSM than GUSP, particularly among men and younger individuals. Based on prior literature, this may be related to the healthier status of ADSM and earlier detection and treatment of medical conditions that can predispose to lymphoma. Diminished differences between the two populations for older individuals may reflect cumulated military‐related exposures within a generally healthier population.

Introduction

In the United States, approximately 8570 new cases of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and 80,620 new cases of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) are diagnosed annually, representing 0.4% and 4% of all new cancer cases, respectively. HL is more common among younger adults (median age of diagnosis = 39 years), men (2.8 per 100,000 persons, age‐adjusted), and non‐Hispanic White individuals (2.9 per 100,000 persons, age‐adjusted). NHL, by contrast, is more prevalent among older adults (median age of diagnosis = 68), but also occurs more frequently among men (22.5 per 100,000 persons, age‐adjusted) and non‐Hispanic White individuals (20.4 per 100,000 persons, age‐adjusted) [1, 2]. Because both HL and NHL are more common in men, groups that are overrepresented in the active‐duty service member (ADSM) population and given increasing concerns about toxic exposures in military settings, it is essential to examine lymphoma incidence in the military.

Lymphoma incidence is influenced by a combination of biological, environmental, diagnosis factors, and cancer reporting (data collection and completeness of cancer registries). Exposures to environmental and occupational risk factors for lymphoma may differ between ADSM and the general US population (GUSP). Certain subgroups of ADSM encounter military‐related exposures with known lymphoma relevance, such as radiation, tactical herbicides, pesticides, and benzene‐related compounds through fuel vapors and solvent use [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. On the other hand, ADSM must meet rigorous health and fitness standards upon enlistment and every 6 months thereafter, making them, on average, healthier than the general population [8, 9, 10]. Lymphomas encompass a spectrum of clinical behavior, with aggressive subtypes manifesting rapidly through enlarging lymphadenopathy, night sweats, and fatigue, while indolent forms have a more gradual onset, may remain stable or regress spontaneously, and are often diagnosed incidentally, either through radiographic imaging or upon progression to symptomatic, advanced‐stage disease [11, 12]. Thus, accessibility to care may affect the diagnosis and treatment of lymphoma and predisposing medical conditions for lymphoma [13, 14, 15]. ADSM receive comprehensive medical care through the Department of War (DoW) Military Healthcare System (MHS) eliminating financial barriers to healthcare, which has contributed to improved survival of ADSMs with lymphoma compared to matched civilians [16]. Healthcare within the MHS may facilitate diagnosis and treatment of predisposing medical conditions and thus affect the incidence of lymphoma. Therefore, we hypothesized that incidence of lymphoma may vary between the military and general population because of combined effects of these factors.

Despite the unique exposures and healthcare environment of the military population, research on lymphoma incidence among ADSM is limited. One study found 50% increased odds of developing NHL among Vietnam War veterans compared to nondeployed counterparts, though this analysis focused solely on a specific era and the veteran population [17]. Another study within the DoW observed a 12‐fold increase in the odds of diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) among ADSMs injured in combat, though the sample size was limited [18]. In terms of burn pit exposure, a recent paper did not reveal excess lymphoid malignancies among burn pit exposed military personnel who served at Joint Base Balad in Iraq compared to matched ADSMs who did not deploy to Iraq [19]. A broader literature review reported lower NHL incidence among Navy and Air Force personnel compared to the GUSP, but most studies had methodological limitations such as small cohorts and short follow‐up periods [20].

Given the unique demographic composition, occupational exposures, and universal healthcare access within the active‐duty military population, a focused analysis of lymphoma incidence in this population is warranted. This study aims to compare age‐adjusted incidence rates (AaIRs) and temporal trends of HL and NHL between ADSM and individuals in the GUSP using standardized cancer registry data, to better understand how these differences may influence disease burden and diagnosis.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Study Populations

Data for ADSM were obtained from the DoW's Central Cancer Registry (CCR), which contained demographic, tumor, treatment, and outcome variables for DoW beneficiaries treated or diagnosed with cancer in the MHS [21]. Patients 18–59 years of age within the MHS pathologically diagnosed with HL or NHL between 1998 and 2014 were included in this analysis. ADSM aged 60 years and older were excluded due to the small number of persons within this age group in the military, while retirees and dependents were not included because the study subjects were ADSM. Active‐duty annual population counts were retrieved from the Defense Manpower Data Center.

Data for the GUSP were obtained from the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER‐9) database which included cancer registries from Atlanta, Connecticut, Detroit, Hawaii, Iowa, New Mexico, San Francisco‐Oakland, Seattle‐Puget Sound, and Utah, representing approximately 10% of the US population [22]. Annual population size for the US general population was obtained through SEER.

Study Variables

HL and NHL were defined based on the International Classification of Diseases—Oncology—Third edition (ICD‐O‐3) morphology and topographic codes, listed in Appendix Table A1. Tumor stage was defined as local, regional, and distant based on the SEER summary staging manual [23]. Age at diagnosis was dichotomized into 18–39 and 40–59 years—using 39 years as the cut‐point because young adults are defined as those 39 years of age or younger and may differ in tumor biology compared to those of an older age [24, 25]. Racial groups included White, Black, and other due to limited number of persons of other races. HL was classified as classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL) and nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (NLPHL) and NHL was classified as DLBCL, follicular lymphoma (FL), chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL), and other NHL histologies (Appendix Table A1) [26].

Statistical Analysis

AaIR per 100,000 person‐years and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the Tiwari method to calculate CIs [27]. The military population from 1998 to 2014 was used as the standard population for age‐adjustment to more heavily weigh the younger ages that are overrepresented in the military. Person‐years at risk were calculated by summing the annual number of ADSM within each calendar year. Incidence rate ratio (IRR) and its 95% CI were calculated to compare ADSM and the general population in incidence of HL and NHL. IRRs and their CIs were also estimated by sex, race, age, tumor stage, and histology.

To analyze temporal trends, the average annual percentage change (APC) in incidence of HL or NHL was calculated and compared at different timepoints using log‐linear Joinpoint Regression. Data were modeled under the assumption of constant variance. Trend analyses were further examined by tumor stage. Due to the small sample of women and men of other races, trend analyses were only conducted for White men. Statistical analyses were completed using SAS software (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, version 9.4) and Joinpoint Regression (version 4.9.0.0, NCI), with significance set at p < 0.05 [28, 29].

Results

There were 1635 cases of lymphoma (HL: n = 772, 47%; NHL: n = 863, 53%) in the military population and 56,560 cases in the general population (HL: n = 11,456, 20%; NHL: n = 45,104, 80%). Overall, incidence rates of lymphoma were marginally lower in ADSM than the GUSP for HL (IRR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.86, 1.00) and NHL (IRR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.77, 0.88; Table 1). The incidence rates of HL and NHL were lower among men (HL: IRR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.82, 0.97; NHL: IRR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.64, 0.74) in ADSM than the general population. ADSM had a lower incidence of HL and NHL than those in the general population for both Whites and Blacks (HL: IRR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.80, 0.95; NHL: IRR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.73, 0.86 for Whites and HL: IRR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.68, 0.99; NHL: IRR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.51, 0.73 for Blacks); but had a higher incidence of HL (IRR = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.01, 1.92) and NHL (IRR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.12, 1.84) for other races. Incidence rates of HL and NHL in ADSM were lower among those aged 18–39 years (HL: IRR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.84, 0.99; NHL: IRR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.68, 0.81) than the general population, while this was not observed among individuals aged 40–59. Incidence rates were lower in ADSM than the general population for NLPHL (IRR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.80, 0.97), DLBCL (IRR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.58, 0.74), and tumors of other NHL histology (IRR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.74, 0.93).

When stratified by sex (Table 2), HL incidence among ADSM men was significantly lower than in the GUSP only for White men (IRR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.75, 0.91); rates for Black men were not significantly different (IRR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.65, 1.01). Among ADSM men aged 18–39, HL incidence remained lower (IRR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.82, 0.98). The analysis stratified by tumor stage shows a lower incidence rate in the military for advanced disease (IRR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.66, 0.90). For NHL, incidence among ADSM men was significantly lower for both White (IRR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.61, 0.72) and Black individuals (IRR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.41, 0.61) compared to the general population. Lower rates were also observed in ADSM men aged 18–39 (IRR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.58, 0.70) and 40–59 (IRR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.71, 0.91). By stage, incidence was reduced in ADSM at all stages: local (IRR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.71, 0.93), regional (IRR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.50, 0.74), distant (IRR = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.61, 0.76), and unknown (IRR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.30, 0.57) compared to the general population. By histology, ADSM men had lower incidence of DLBCL (IRR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.47, 0.61) and other NHL histologies (IRR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.61, 0.78). HL and NHL incidence rates among women (with a relatively small number of patients) were not significantly different between ADSM and the general population.

Among White men in the general population, HL AaIRs were relatively stable from 1998 to 2014; whereas there was a significant decline in HL incidence among ADSM during this time (ADSM: APC = −2.33; general population: APC = 0.13; Figure 1A). The trends in AaIR of NHL were relatively stable in both populations from 1998 to 2010 (APC = 0.55), with a decline in incidence of NHL starting in 2010 in both populations (APC = −7.81; Figure 1B). The trend in APC in AaIR from 1998 to 2014 was statistically equivalent between ADSM and the GUSP among White men diagnosed with local HL and NHL, regional HL, and distant HL and NHL (Figure 2). In ADSM and the general population, there was a significant decline in incidence only from 1998 to 2014 among White men with local HL (APC = −5.97; Figure 2A).

Discussion

Overall, incidence rates of both HL and NHL were modestly lower among ADSM compared to the GUSP. This pattern was particularly evident for HL and for select NHL subtypes with sufficient case counts for analysis. Among men, who comprised most of the military population, incidence rates for both HL and NHL were generally lower, which was observed for both White and Black groups. When stratified by age, the differences between the two populations were significantly reduced for individuals aged 40–59 years. Among women, differences in incidence were not statistically significant, likely due to smaller case numbers and limited power to detect small differences.

The lower incidence of both NHL and HL observed among ADSM, particularly among men, may be partially attributable to the generally healthier status of active‐duty personnel. Due to the military's rigorous medical and fitness standards, ADSM are generally healthier than the broader US population at enlistment and throughout their service, which may reduce their underlying risk of developing lymphoma [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. In addition, universal access to healthcare within the military may facilitate earlier detection and management of health conditions that can precede or predispose lymphoma—such as autoimmune disorders (e.g., Sjögren's syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus), chronic viral infections (e.g., latent Epstein–Barr virus, hepatitis C virus, Helicobacter pylori ), or immunodeficiency states [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Timely and effective treatment of these conditions that can heighten the risk of lymphoma, as a result of the high level of access, could contribute to the observed lower incidence in the military. It is noteworthy that the age range of the study populations was 18–59 years and therefore our findings do not apply to older ages with higher incidence rates, in which active‐duty members rarely fall.

One strength of this study is inclusion of stage at diagnosis [41]. Among men, the incidence of NHL was significantly lower in ADSM compared to the general population across all tumor stages, suggesting that the lower incidence rates observed among ADSM were not specific to tumor stage. If improved access to care in the military led to earlier detection, one might expect a higher proportion of early‐stage cancers in this population, potentially masking a true difference in overall incidence. However, our finding that incidence differences persisted across all stages argues against this explanation. Our findings are consistent with a 2006 study that also reported lower incidence rates of HL and NHL among men in the military compared to the general population [42]. However, that earlier study did not examine incidence by tumor stage or include more recent data. To our knowledge, no prior studies have directly compared stage‐specific lymphoma incidence between ADSM and the GUSP. By stratifying by stage, our analysis provides a more contemporary and detailed assessment of these patterns.

When examining lymphoma incidence by histologic subtype, several notable differences between ADSM and the general population were observed. By histology, DLBCL NHL incidence was significantly lower in ADSM than in the general population. Lower incidence was also observed for NLPHL among men. Both subtypes had larger patient numbers than other histologies, which may partly explain these findings; however, it remains unclear whether the reduced incidence reflects underlying factors such as occupational exposures or immune‐related mechanisms for specific histologic subtypes of the disease.

While initial enlistment screening and ongoing fitness requirements may contribute to an overall healthier baseline population and thus lead to lower incidence rates in the military, it is possible that cumulative military‐related exposures among long‐serving personnel could attenuate this effect over time [41, 43]. Based on the literature, exposures related to military occupation, such as radiation, chemical agents (benzene, herbicides, pesticides, and so on), and airborne carcinogens, may contribute to elevated lymphoma risk [3, 4, 6, 7, 44, 45], though available data remain limited and heterogeneous. Older military personnel may have longer cumulative exposures to these factors due to duration of service and therefore may be prone to a higher risk of lymphoma. However, the possible effects of such cumulative environmental and occupational exposures could have been neutralized by the benefits of overall healthier status and better care of predisposed medical conditions among long‐serving personnel [31, 34, 35]. As a result, no significant interpopulation differences were observed for this older age group except for NHL among men.

Our analysis also identified declining trends in the incidence of both HL and NHL among ADSM, with a particularly notable decline in HL incidence among White men. While similar declines were observed in NHL incidence in the general population, HL rates in SEER remained relatively stable over the same period. Human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) is a known risk factor for NHL. While highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), initiated in 1996, might lead to reductions in certain AIDS‐defining NHL subtypes, non‐AIDS‐related NHL types and HL might not be similarly affected [46, 47]. Therefore, the identified differences in the trends of incidence between the military and general population warrant research on factors associated with the differences.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, underreporting may have influenced incidence estimates in CCR, as some small military treatment facilities with limited manpower may not have reported all cancer cases. Also, CCR may not capture cases who develop lymphoma after leaving the military. However, the higher incidence rates of certain cancers in CCR compared with SEER suggest that the impact of underreporting may be limited. Second, due to deidentification of cancer registry data, some ADSM patients could have been included in both datasets. However, this is unlikely to substantially bias results, given the small proportion of ADSM in the general population and the lack of data‐sharing agreement between CCR and SEER. Third, differences in case consolidation processes between CCR and SEER could contribute to observed population differences. Fourth, small numbers of female and minority ADSM limited the power to conduct robust subgroup and temporal trend analyses. Finally, we did not have information on deployment history, occupation, or exposures, which are important factors to consider and should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, incidence rates of both HL and NHL were significantly lower among ADSM compared to the GUSP, particularly among men and younger individuals. These differences may reflect the generally better health status of ADSM due to military medical and fitness standards, as well as the benefits of universal access to healthcare within the military system detecting and treating lymphoma predisposition conditions. The absence of differences among those aged 40–59 may suggest that cumulative occupational exposures might counteract the protective effects of healthier status. No significant differences were observed among women, possibly due to biological factors or limited sample sizes. These findings underscore the need for future research to examine environmental and behavioral risk factors and to further explore differences in lymphoma incidence between military and civilian populations.
