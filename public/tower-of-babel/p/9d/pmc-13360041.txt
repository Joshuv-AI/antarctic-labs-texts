Comparative evaluation of multiplex bead assay, polymerase chain reaction, and rapid diagnostic test for P. falciparum detection in community surveys in Malawi
Authors: Monica Soko, Victoria Seffren, Tinashe A Tizifa, Dean Sayre, Laura C Steinhardt, Laura Castro, Pius Masache, David Twapokera Mzinza, Fraction Kunseu Dzinjalamala, Kamija S Phiri, Karl B Seydel, Julie R Gutman, Jessica McCaffery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate detection of malaria infections, particularly asymptomatic and low-density infections, is essential for effective surveillance and control efforts. This study compared the performance of three malaria detection platforms for identifying Plasmodium falciparum infections in a community-based survey setting.MethodsCross-sectional household surveys were conducted in three high-transmission districts in Malawi from February–March in 2022 and 2023. From 2960 participants, samples from 604 children aged 2 months to 14 years were analyzed for P. falciparum infection using three detection methods: WHO-prequalified HRP2-based rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), a multiplex bead-based assay (MBA) for histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2) and Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) antigens, and a real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting P. falciparum 18S rRNA, which was considered the gold standard test.ResultsPCR detected a P. falciparum prevalence of 24.0% (145/604; 95% CI 20.7–27.6%) compared to 29.5% by RDT (178/604; 95% CI 25.9–33.3%). RDT performance was suboptimal compared to PCR, with RDT sensitivity of 48.3% (95% CI 39.9–56.7%) and specificity of 76.5% (95% CI 72.3–80.3%). Seventy-five PCR-positive samples were RDT-negative, possibly representing low-density infections below RDT detection limits, while 108 RDT-positive samples were PCR-negative, consistent with persistent HRP2 antigenemia following parasite clearance. Receiver operating curve analyses demonstrated that the MBA for both antigens showed limited discriminatory power against PCR, HRP2 (AUC = 0.63, 95% CI 0.58–0.69) and pLDH (AUC = 0.61, 95% CI 0.56–0.67). However, MBA demonstrated strong agreement with RDTs, (HRP2: AUC = 0.93 (95% CI 0.90–0.96); pLDH: AUC = 0.88, (95% CI 0.85–0.92), indicating comparable antigen detection capabilities. PCR was estimated to be 41.3% less expensive in start-up costs and 32.2% less in processing costs compared to the MBA.ConclusionsIn high-transmission, community-based settings, the malaria antigen detection MBA demonstrated a limited ability to detect P. falciparum infections when compared to infection identified by PCR. Real-time PCR is increasingly feasible for use in surveillance where infrastructure permits. These findings underscore the importance of selecting diagnostic tools based on surveillance objectives, local epidemiology, and available laboratory capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05913-0.Keywords: Malaria, P. falciparum, Multiplex bead assay, Rapid diagnostic tests, Real-time PCR

Background

Malaria, a mosquito-borne parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium species, continues to pose a major global health burden, especially in tropical and subtropical regions. In 2024, there were an estimated 282 million new malaria cases, with 94% of cases occurring in Africa primarily among children [1]. There were 610,000 deaths were attributed to malaria, with 75% of deaths occurring in children under 5 years [1]. Accurate detection of malaria infection is essential for clinical diagnosis, disease management, and treatment, as well as public health surveillance to inform nationwide strategies for prevention and control.

Although microscopy remains the diagnostic gold standard [2], its accuracy depends heavily on operator expertise and detection of low-density infections is more challenging. Microscopy also carries a substantial infrastructure burden, requiring a trained microscopist, access to reliable reagents and clean slides, well-maintained microscopes, and consistent electricity, resources that are not reliably available in all endemic settings [3]. To address this, simple, low-cost rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), which permit delivery of rapid results at scale without requiring extensive training or infrastructure [2], were developed, and are now the most widely used diagnostic tool for P. falciparum in endemic regions.

Most malaria RDTs detect P. falciparum-specific histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2), which is highly expressed during clinical stages of infection [4]. HRP2-based RDTs provide greater clinical sensitivity for P. falciparum infections than other Plasmodium antigens that serve as diagnostic targets such as Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) [5]. RDT performance is evaluated through the WHO Malaria RDT Product Testing Programme, which includes a minimum panel detection score of 75% at 200 parasites per microliter (p/µL) [6]. These RDTs typically have a limit of detection of approximately 100–200 p/μL [2], limiting their identification of infections below these thresholds. An additional disadvantage of HRP2-based RDTs is the long half-life of HRP2 [7, 8], leading to the possibility of a positive test weeks to months after clearance of parasitemia [5, 9].

Repeated exposure to malaria parasites over time leads to the development of partial immunity, or premunition, which does not prevent infection but suppresses symptoms or their severity [10]. As a result, a large proportion of infections in endemic communities with high transmission are asymptomatic with parasite densities below the limits of detection of microscopy and RDTs [11–13]. These submicroscopic or sub-RDT infections can nonetheless contribute to onward transmission [14] and are thus important to address in malaria elimination efforts [15–17].

Molecular techniques, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), have high sensitivity and specificity, enabling the detection and characterization of low-density infections. While not amenable to widespread point-of-care testing, PCR testing has become increasingly accessible due in part to expansion of testing infrastructure during the recent COVID-19 pandemic [18]. Additionally, surveillance studies have incorporated the use of multiplex bead-based assays (MBA) that detect and quantify multiple malaria antigens [19, 20]. MBA has also been used to streamline sample screening prior to further molecular characterization of parasite variants that present biological threats to malaria control efforts (such as those without the genes encoding the HRP2 diagnostic target) [21].

To assess the suitability, strengths, and limitations of each method for identifying P. falciparum infection, this study compared the performance and associated costs and feasibility of three malaria detection platforms: a P. falciparum-specific real-time quantitative PCR [22], a malaria antigen detection MBA [23], and HRP2-based malaria RDTs, among participants of a malaria Community Case Management (mCCM) survey conducted in Malawi between 2022 and 2023.

Methods

Study design and enrollment

This study used samples from 604 children aged 2 months to 14 years collected during cross-sectional household surveys designed to assess the effectiveness of a mCCM intervention within the context of a cluster randomized clinical trial (registered with the Pan African Trial Registry, PACTR202508639314232) in three districts in Malawi with high perennial malaria transmission, (Ntchisi and Salima (21–40% PfPR2_10) and Neno (11–20% PfPR2_10) [24]. The surveys were conducted 12 months apart, coinciding with the peak malaria transmission season in February–March 2022 (baseline survey before intervention) and February–March 2023 (post-intervention endline survey). Survey areas and eligible households were enumerated and selected using two-stage random selection in the catchment areas of health facilities participating in the trial. A standardized questionnaire was administered to each household to collect demographic information and information on illnesses in the preceding two weeks for each household member. A capillary blood sample was obtained from all children aged two months to 14 years for RDT and preparation of dried blood spots (DBS) for subsequent laboratory testing. All children with positive RDTs were treated with artemether-lumefantrine (AL) or, if they had been treated within the two preceding weeks, were referred to the nearest health facility for evaluation, as per Malawi National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) guidance.

The parent study protocol was reviewed and approved by the College of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (COMREC) (Certificate number P.09/21/3411) and by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Human Research Protection Office Institutional Review Board (Protocol 7381). In addition, approval for study conduct was obtained from the NMCP and Community Health Programme at the Malawi Ministry of Health and the relevant District Health Offices, District Executive Committees, and Traditional Authorities. Written informed consent for survey participation was obtained from either the head of household or a household member aged ≥ 18 years; written informed consent was provided for capillary blood collection by each child’s parent or guardian. In addition, assent for blood collection was obtained from children aged seven to 14 years in the presence of their legal guardians. This study was submitted as protocol number P.07/2024/181: “Comparative Analysis of Malaria Detection Methods–Multiplex Bead Assay vs PCR” to the Malawi University of Science and Technology and received ethical approval.

Malaria rapid diagnostic testing

Participants were tested at the time of sample collection using one of the two WHO-prequalified HRP2-based RDTs: SD Bioline Malaria Ag P.f (Catalog# 05FK60; Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, Illinois) or Paracheck Pf (Catalog# 302,030,025; Orchid Biomedical Systems, Goa, India) following the manufacturer’s recommended procedure. A result was recorded as positive if both control (C) and test (T) lines were visible, negative if only the control line appeared, and invalid if no control line appeared. Test results were documented electronically using REDCap software (version 8.5.17; Vanderbilt University). Following RDT collection, three to four spots of blood were collected on a Whatman 903 filter paper card and air dried. These DBS were stored at 2–8 °C in individual sterile resealable plastic bags and transported to the Molecular Parasitology laboratory at Kamuzu University of Health Sciences in Blantyre, Malawi.

DNA extraction and PCR

In December 2024, DNA was extracted from a 6 mm DBS punch using a methanol–water extraction method, as described previously [25]. The extracted DNA was interrogated by Taqman PCR for the multi-copy gene P. falciparum 18S rRNA as described previously by Hermsen et al. [22] which has since been validated in a subsequent study by Wang et al. [26] to have a limit of detection (LOD) of approximately 0.3p/μL.

The PCR thermocycling and analysis were performed using a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR instrument. The reaction included an initial hot-start step of 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/elongation at 60 °C for 1 min. An autogenerated fluorescence threshold was used to obtain Ct values; Ct values > 40 were considered negative for P. falciparum. A standard curve was included on each PCR plate using triplicate wells of serial dilutions of Pf2004 strain P. falciparum parasites cultured in vitro. Curve quality was assessed on each plate by evaluating the coefficient of determination (R2), slope, and absolute Ct values at each standard concentration. Data from a plate were included in the analysis only if all standard curve parameters met predefined quality control criteria. Ct values obtained from each experimental well were compared with the standard curve to calculate an estimated parasite density in units of p/μL.

Malaria MBA data analysis and sample selection for PCR testing

A total of 2960 DBS samples were randomly selected from the 4387 DBS samples received by the laboratory for analysis using a malaria antigen detection MBA in August 2023. The MBA methods used for this study, including bead coupling conditions [27], are described in the Supplemental Methods.

A Luminex MagPix instrument automatically calculated median fluorescence intensity minus background (MFI-bg) values by subtracting the MFI of the buffer blank well from the MFI of each sample well on the same plate. The resulting data were exported to Microsoft Excel (Redmond, WA, USA). R software (R version 4.4.0) was used for assessing data quality and conducting analysis. Samples with bead counts < 20 for any antigen target were identified and re-run on subsequent plates. If a sample produced low bead counts on repeat testing, it was excluded from further analysis. After these exclusions, data from all plates were compiled into a single Excel file for downstream analysis.

A finite mixture model was applied to assign cutoff values for HRP2 and pLDH antigen positivity. MFI-bg values were log-transformed and modelled using a two-component finite mixture model implemented with the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm via the mixtools package in R software [28]. The mean (μ) of the lower component of the model, representing the presumed negative distribution, was identified, and the positivity cutoff was defined as the mean plus three standard deviations. Based on this cutoff, MFI-bg values for each antigen were dichotomized as positive or negative. Each sample was then categorized into one of four groups: HRP2 + /pLDH + , HRP2 − /pLDH + , HRP2 + /pLDH − , or HRP2 − /pLDH − .

Due to cost and reagent constraints, 604 specimens were selected for PCR testing using stratified random sampling by antigen category to reflect the overall distribution of antigen positivity across the dataset as follows: HRP2 − /pLDH − (n = 378), HRP2 − /pLDH + (n = 7), HRP2 + /pLDH − (n = 61), and HRP2 + /pLDH + (n = 158).

Data analysis

Laboratory results for RDT, PCR, and MBA were merged with questionnaire data from the two cross-sectional surveys using unique sample identifiers. Demographics were described and test positivity (positive/total tested) for RDT, MBA (pLDH antigen and HRP2 antigen separately), and PCR was calculated. Estimates of test positivity and associated test parameters with binomial confidence intervals (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV)) were calculated for RDT using PCR as the gold standard reference diagnostic.

Antigen levels reported in MFI-bg were compared by first adjusting all values ≤ 0 to 0.1 to allow for log transformation, as negative values were due to raw MFI values lower than the MFI observed for buffer background, indicating the absence of any detectable antigen. Intensity of the adjusted MFI-bg results were plotted as positive/negative based on the comparator diagnostics, RDT and PCR. Statistical comparison of median MFI-bg values between groups was conducted using Mann–Whitney tests. Data analysis was performed and visualizations created in R software (R version 4.4.0) and in GraphPad Prism 5.03 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA), including receiver operating curve (ROC) analyses, two-tailed Mann–Whitney tests, and calculation of sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratios (LR) based on different MFI-bg cutoff values. Positive likelihood ratio (LR⁺) values were interpreted as previously described, with LR+ values between five and ten indicating moderate diagnostic value, 2–5 indicating a small increase in the likelihood of disease following positive assay result, and 1–2 indicating minimal increase in the likelihood of disease following positive assay result [29].

MBA re-optimization and workflow documentation

After preliminary comparative analyses between the MBA and PCR, several components of the MBA were re-optimized to improve performance (Supplemental Figs. 1–4, Supplemental Table S6). Re-optimization experiments for the malaria antigen MBA included evaluation of assay buffers, bead coupling conditions, and capture and secondary antibody selection and concentrations. Recombinant HRP2 and PfLDH antigens were used to assess signal response, dynamic range, and potential cross-reactivity. Re-optimized bead conditions were evaluated in both singleplex format, in which one recombinant antigen was tested with one bead type, and 2-plex format, in which both recombinant antigens were tested simultaneously within the same well to assess anti-HRP2 and anti-pLDH bead performance. Full experimental details are provided in the Supplemental Methods. The performance of the re-optimized MBA against different P. falciparum genotypes with variable HRP2/3 production capacity was evaluated using eluted DBS prepared from well-characterized laboratory cultured strains (wild-type (pfhrp2 + /pfhrp3 +), a pfhrp2-deleted strain (Dd2), and a pfhrp3-deleted strain (Hb3)) prepared across a range of parasite densities within the biological range of infection (10, 100, 1000, and 10,000 p/µL).

The re-optimization included a documentation of the overall workflow for the re-optimized MBA compared to the workflow for a standard PCR assay to highlight practical implications for assay implementation. A resource comparison and cost analysis for 500 samples was calculated by pricing out equipment, materials, reagents from vendor websites as of June 2025 or, if pricing was not available on the website, by requesting a quote, and documenting personnel time for each step of the assay workflow (Supplemental Table S7a–c).

Results

Participant demographics and malaria test results

This analysis included results from 604 children with results for RDT, PCR, and MBA: 48.2% (291) were male, 33.8% (204) were between the ages of 0–4 years, 36.4% (220) were between 5 and 9 years of age, and 29.8% (280) were aged 10–14 years (Table 1). Twenty percent (123) of participants reported fever within the two weeks before enrollment. Approximately 30% (178) of participants tested positive by RDT and 24.0% (145) were positive by PCR; fever in the past two weeks was reported by 24.2% (43/178) and 22.1% (32/145) of those positive by RDT and PCR, respectively. For HRP2 antigen, the observed median MFI-bg was 101.1 (IQR: 100.0–139.8) and MBA test positivity was 36.3% (219/604). For pLDH antigen, the median MFI-bg was 72.5 (IQR: 71.5–83.5) and MBA test positivity was 27.3% (165/604) (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Comparison of malaria antigen MBA and PCR performance

Despite statistically significant differences in MFI-bg values between PCR-positive and PCR-negative groups for both HRP2 and pLDH (two-tailed Mann–Whitney test P-values < 0.0001; Fig. 2A, B, respectively), the MBA demonstrated poor discriminatory performance for both HRP2 (area under the curve, AUC = 0.63, 95% CI 0.58–0.69) and pLDH (AUC = 0.61, 95% CI 0.56–0.67) compared to PCR (Fig. 2C, D, respectively). The HRP2 MBA demonstrated its highest sensitivity with an MFI-bg threshold of 0.3 at 65.2% (95% CI 57.5–72.8). The estimated specificity at this threshold was 54.0% (95% CI 49.5–58.5), resulting in positive likelihood ratio (LR⁺) of 1.4, which suggests a limited ability to distinguish infected from uninfected individuals (Supplemental Table S2). To obtain a LR⁺ of at least 2, an MFI-bg threshold of 30.3 would increase specificity to 78.8% though diminish sensitivity to 42.7%.

HRP2 MFI-bg values showed strong agreement with RDT results, with a LR⁺ of 10 resulting from sensitivity of 85.9% (95% CI 80.1–90.3) and specificity of 91.6% (95% CI 88.5–93.8) at an MFI-bg threshold of 14.75 (Supplemental Table 4). Performance of the re-optimized MBA with P. falciparum strains exhibiting varying pfhrp2/3 genotypes

HRP2 detection (Supplemental Fig. 5a) yielded parasite density-dependent MFI-bg signals for all wild-type P. falciparum strains across the tested parasite densities. Additionally, the pfhrp3-deleted PfHb3 strain produced HRP2 signals at 10,000 and 1000 p/μL like wild-type strains, as expected due to the presence of HRP2 antigen. Conversely, the pfhrp2-deleted PfDd2 strain showed very low to negligible HRP2 MFI-bg signals at 1000 p/μL, consistent with the absence of HRP2 antigen. For PfLDH detection (Supplemental Fig. S5b), all P. falciparum culture strains, irrespective of their pfhrp2/3 genotype, exhibited clear dose-dependent increases in MFI-bg signals, consistent with the detection of increasing levels of PfLDH independent of the absence of either HRP2 or HRP3 antigens.

Comparison of the MFI-bg signals for HRP2 versus PfLDH (Supplemental Fig. 5C) demonstrated that pfhrp2/3 wild-type strains and the pfhrp3-deleted PfHb3 strain consistently produced clear dose-dependent increases in MFI-bg signals for both HRP2 and PfLDH antigens. However, the pfhrp2-deleted PfDd2 strain showed an important inconsistency. At the highest density of 10,000 p/µL, this strain unexpectedly produced a substantial HRP2 MFI-bg signal (7034), and a high PfLDH signal (3162), with both HRP2 and PfLDH signals being comparable to those observed from wild-type strains at similar parasitemia levels. In contrast, at 1000 p/µL, the PfDd2 sample exhibited a profile of high PfLDH signal (185 MFI-bg) coupled with a very low HRP2 signal (15 MFI-bg), consistent with the absence of the pfhrp2 gene.

Comparative analysis of cost and time for the re-optimized MBA versus qPCR

The time to process 500 samples was estimated at 31 days regardless of testing method. Equipment costs were estimated to be $128,704 for the MBA compared to $75,529 for the PCR. Cost of reagents and consumables to perform each assay, once laboratory infrastructure is accounted for, was estimated to be $13,860 for MBA and $9,395 for PCR (Supplemental Fig. 6, Supplemental Table 7a–c). PCR was substantially less expensive in both start-up and run costs, estimated at 41.3% less than MBA for equipment start-up costs and 32.2% less expensive for processing 500 samples.

Discussion

Identifying the optimal assay to estimate the burden of malaria under various use-cases is necessary to support malaria control, particularly in highly endemic regions with propensity for submicroscopic/sub-RDT infections [12, 17, 30, 31]. While PCR is highly sensitive, it is time-consuming to process large numbers of samples. Given the potential for high throughput and low-cost workflows, an MBA that detects low-density infections with high sensitivity could be used to screen samples from large community surveys to identify those in need of further analysis. However, when compared to PCR, the MBA, as used in this study, demonstrated limited discriminatory power to identify P. falciparum infection, which may be due to the potential for the assay to produce false-positive results due to lingering HRP2 antigen [32] as well as false-negative results due to unidentified deletions of the pfhrp2 and/or pfhrp3 genes in the parasite population [33]. Positive likelihood ratios of MBA for both HRP2 and pLDH were estimated at less than two when MFI-bg thresholds that optimized sensitivity and specificity were applied, highlighting that the MBA, like RDTs, may not detect low density infections.

In this study population the MBA performed comparably to RDT, which is aligned with findings from other surveillance studies that have demonstrated comparable performance between the assays and described the MBA’s ability to detect HRP2 and pLDH at somewhat lower concentrations than conventional RDTs [34, 35]. While the antigen detection MBA may offer enhanced analytical sensitivity relative to RDTs, the MBA is designed as a research tool, not a point-of-care diagnostic test. The sub-optimal sensitivity and specificity of the MBA likely reflect both antigen persistence following parasite clearance and limited dynamic range for detecting low-density infections. In high-transmission areas like Malawi, where many individuals have experienced recent infections, antigen-based assays may overestimate prevalence, while PCR more accurately detects current infections. These results highlight the importance of aligning assay target biology with surveillance objectives.

Furthermore, in our study, DBS samples were collected during community surveys up to two years prior to PCR testing, in an environment where ambient temperature and humidity can be elevated. The delay in running the PCR, coupled with environmental conditions previously shown to reduce PCR sensitivity from filter-paper blood samples [36–38], may have reduced recovery of DNA from samples while parasite antigen remained detectable. Thus, the observed RDT + and pLDH + or HRP2 + discordance with PCR– may reflect a combination of both antigen persistence following recent infection and imperfect recovery of parasite DNA from DBS samples, consistent with reports describing the sensitivity of DBS-based PCR to storage conditions.

Recent WHO guidance recommends that pfhrp2/3 gene deletion surveillance be conducted via HRP2-based RDT screening of symptomatic cases, in combination with a secondary test such as microscopy or a pLDH-based RDT, followed by confirmatory testing via PCR as the standard method for identifying false-negative HRP2-based RDTs results due to pfhrp2/3 gene deletions [39]. Although some studies have used the malaria antigen detection MBA as an alternative screening approach to identify HRP2-negative, PfLDH-positive samples from dried blood spots, the WHO cautions that such strategies should only be used when RDT-based screening is not feasible. Importantly, MBA results must be clearly correlated with the clinical performance of RDTs to generate actionable estimates of RDT failure rates, which are critical for determining whether a programmatic switch in diagnostic policy is warranted [39]. Therefore, while MBA can provide valuable supplementary data on limits of detection when assessing the field performance of RDTs [34], the operational constrains and detection threshold limit the broader application of MBAs, particularly in programmatic contexts such as identifying samples for subsequent molecular testing for pfhrp2/3 deletion surveillance or other routine activities aimed at detecting these deletions. Furthermore, the MBA limit of detection described here is based on the specific antibody and antigen combinations and concentrations used; different assay performance results might be seen with other combinations. Additionally, studies relying on antigen detection from DBS samples must ensure optimal storage conditions, as both pLDH and HRP2 antigens may decay rapidly, up to 70% after 8 days of storage at room temperature [40].

While microscopy remains the traditional gold standard for malaria diagnosis and provides valuable information on parasite density and species identification, it was not performed as part of this community survey, preventing comparison of the performance of PCR, RDT, and MBA to microscopy. However, as the PCR was quantitative, performance was assessed at the PCR-estimated parasite density of 200 p/μL.

Despite infrastructure requirements, PCR was found to be more operationally feasible than the MBA due to lower costs and simplified workflow. Thus, although both assays required approximately the same processing time for 500 samples, the PCR workflow was less complex, required fewer reagents, and had substantially lower start-up and per-sample reagent costs. These findings indicate the potential of PCR-based surveillance in resource-limited settings, provided that key infrastructure and staff are in place.

Conclusions

This study highlights the strengths and limitations of three diagnostic approaches, HRP2-based RDTs, MBA, and real-time PCR, for detecting P. falciparum infections in a high-transmission, community-based survey setting. Point-of-care diagnostic tests such as HRP2-based RDTs are designed to guide immediate treatment decisions in symptomatic individuals, whereas PCR and MBA are used primarily in research or programmatic monitoring. While HRP2-based RDTs remain valuable for clinical diagnosis, their limited sensitivity and the persistence of HRP2 antigen post-clearance restrict their utility for estimating true infection prevalence. The MBA offers value by improving sensitivity over RDTs, but it is neither sensitive nor as specific as PCR. Additionally, the MBA’s current reliance on custom reagents, specialized infrastructure, and interpretive complexity make it less suitable for routine programmatic use. When the necessary infrastructure and workforce are available, PCR is a practical and scalable option which provides high sensitivity and specificity across the full range of parasite densities, making it well suited for accurately estimating true infection prevalence in national surveillance and monitoring programs. Ultimately, assay selection must be guided by the surveillance objective, population context, parasite epidemiology, and operational feasibility, as these findings underscore the strengths and limitations of each platform and the importance of aligning tool selection with available laboratory infrastructure.
