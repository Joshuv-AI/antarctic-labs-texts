An Unusual Case of Periapical Cemento‐Osseous Dysplasia
Authors: Emanuel Harouni, Elham Radan, Parish Sedghizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeriapical cemento‐osseous dysplasia (PCOD) is a benign fibro‐osseous condition often discovered incidentally during radiographic examinations. Accurate diagnosis is crucial to prevent unnecessary treatment. Conservative management with regular monitoring is typically sufficient, as illustrated in this case involving a 62‐year‐old female patient with a two‐decade follow‐up.

Introduction

Periapical cemento‐osseous dysplasia (PCOD) is a benign fibro‐osseous lesion affecting the periapical region of the anterior mandible. It is characterized by the replacement of normal bone with fibrous tissue and cementum‐like material. PCOD is most commonly found in middle‐aged African American women, though it can affect individuals of any race or gender [1]. This condition is often asymptomatic and is discovered incidentally during routine radiographic examinations [2]. The purpose of this case report is to discuss the clinical, radiographic, and histopathological features of PCOD, emphasizing the importance of accurate diagnosis and appropriate management [1].

Case History/Examination

A 62‐year‐old female patient, originally from Jamaica, presented to the University of Southern California School of Dentistry (USCSD) for evaluation of a mild bony expansion in the lower anterior mandible, accompanied by a radiopaque area detected on a panoramic radiograph. The patient reported that this lesion was first identified 20 years ago, when she was 42 years old.

The patient was previously evaluated at USCSD in 1990 for a bony lesion located in the anterior mandible. She reported being seen by multiple doctors and undergoing several radiographic examinations. Recently, she has experienced dysesthesia, described as numbness, on the left side of her chin. She recalled a similar sensation occurring 7–8 months ago. Additionally, the patient mentioned that her mother had a similar bony lesion, although she did not experience any associated numbness. Given the patient's complaint of recurrent numbness in the left chin region, potential involvement of the mental nerve was considered. The mental nerve, a terminal branch of the inferior alveolar nerve, exits the mandible via the mental foramen and provides sensory innervation to the chin, lower lip, and labial mucosa of the anterior mandibular teeth [3]. Panoramic imaging and full‐mouth radiographs were carefully examined to assess the relationship of the lesion to the mental foramen. Although the lesion extended from distal of tooth #22 to distal of #27, there was no radiographic evidence of canal impingement or destruction of the mental foramen. However, given the proximity of the lesion to the mental foramen and the patient's subjective dysesthesia, transient neural compression due to buccal and lingual cortical expansion could not be ruled out. This symptom was monitored over time, and the patient did not report worsening of numbness during follow‐up visits, supporting a conservative management approach [4, 5].

Clinical intraoral examination revealed a symmetrical, stable osseous expansion in the anterior region of the mandible, specifically behind the incisors near the floor of the mouth. No other abnormalities were detected in the dental or alveolar tissues upon palpation. The patient was subsequently referred to the Oral Surgery Clinic for further consultation and a bone biopsy.

A biopsy of the hard tissue confirmed the diagnosis of cemento‐osseous dysplasia. Histopathological evaluation revealed characteristic features of PCOD, including fibrous connective tissue with interspersed cementum‐like calcifications (Figure 1).

Biopsy showing fibrous connective tissue interspersed with cementum‐like calcifications.

The oral pathologist observed that the buccal and lingual expansion in the anterior mandible remains unchanged. The area is firm to palpation without erythema, pain, or ulceration. The anterior mandibular teeth are non‐carious, vital, non‐mobile, and non‐sensitive to percussion. Additionally, no plaque, calculus, deep pocketing, recession, bleeding, or purulence were noted.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for PCOD includes other fibro‐osseous lesions such as focal cemento‐osseous dysplasia, florid cemento‐osseous dysplasia, ossifying fibroma, fibrous dysplasia, and endodontic inflammatory lesions in the early radiolucent stage [3, 5]. It is crucial to differentiate PCOD from these conditions to avoid unnecessary treatment, such as endodontic treatment or extraction, especially since the teeth associated with PCOD are typically vital [6]. Additionally, other well‐defined radiopaque lesions in the anterior mandibular region that should be considered include idiopathic osteosclerosis (IO), condensing osteitis (CO), odontoma, cementoblastoma, cemento‐ossifying fibroma (COF), osteoma, osteoblastoma, and osteoid osteoma (OO) [3].

Investigations

A recent panoramic view (Figure 2) and full‐mouth series (FMX) (Figure 3) reveal a large, space‐occupying lesion with mixed radiodensity in the anterior mandible, extending from canine to canine and from the alveolar ridge to approximately 1–2 cm above the inferior border of the mandible. Periapical radiographs show a vague but intact lamina dura, no evidence of root resorption, and mild distal displacement of the incisors. The lesion exhibits a sclerotic, well‐defined border with an internal radiolucent rim containing centrally located, hyperdense opaque masses. Additionally, bilateral hypercementosis is observed in the endodontically treated first mandibular molars.

Treatment

The therapy notes indicate that this lesion has been present and well‐documented for at least two decades. Surgical intervention is not recommended at this time because the patient is asymptomatic, and no additional pathosis is present beyond the typical periapical cemento‐osseous dysplasia. Surgery in such cases is not advisable due to the risk of delayed healing, which can occur because of the unpredictable vascular supply to large lesions. Additionally, surgery can lead to complications such as infection or issues related to grafting if extractions or implants are performed after the lesion is removed. Given that the diagnosis is well established and the patient remains asymptomatic, the risks, costs, and invasive nature of bone surgery outweigh the benefits. Furthermore, a pathologic fracture is not a concern in this case, as discussed with the patient. The patient can proceed with routine dental treatment, as there are no contraindications related to this PCOD.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

This case highlights the benign nature of periapical cemento‐osseous dysplasia and the importance of a conservative approach to management. Regular follow‐up and patient education are key components in ensuring a healthy outcome for patients with PCOD [7]. Accurate diagnosis and avoiding unnecessary treatment are critical, especially when managing long‐standing, asymptomatic lesions.

Discussion

PCOD is a common fibro‐osseous lesion with a distinct clinical and radiographic presentation [2]. Accurate diagnosis is essential to avoid unnecessary invasive treatments [6]. The condition has a good prognosis, and most cases can be managed conservatively with regular monitoring [5].

Clinical Presentation

Patients with PCOD are typically asymptomatic. The condition is usually detected during routine dental radiographs, where it appears as a radiolucent lesion in the early stages, becoming progressively radiopaque over time [8]. In some cases, patients may present with mild discomfort or swelling in the affected area [7].

Radiographic Features

Radiographically, PCOD lesions are initially radiolucent, reflecting the fibroblastic activity and the presence of fibrous tissue [6]. As the lesion matures, it undergoes a mixed radiolucent–radiopaque phase and eventually becomes predominantly radiopaque due to the deposition of cementum‐like material [4]. The lesion typically surrounds the apex of vital teeth, which is a key diagnostic feature distinguishing it from other periapical pathologies [9].

Histopathological Features

Histopathologically, PCOD is characterized by the presence of fibrous connective tissue intermixed with varying amounts of cementum‐like and bone‐like calcifications [1]. The calcified structures are typically acellular and resemble cementum [10]. Inflammatory cells are usually absent, and the overlying epithelium appears normal [7].

Management

Management of PCOD is typically conservative and primarily involves observation and regular follow‐up to assess for any changes in the lesion or the development of symptoms, as the condition is benign and self‐limiting [1]. In asymptomatic cases, no treatment is required [8]. However, if the patient experiences symptoms such as pain or infection, conservative surgical intervention may be necessary [4]. It is important to educate patients about the nature of the condition and the importance of maintaining good oral hygiene to prevent secondary infections [9].

In this case, the patient's lesion has been stable for over two decades, underscoring the importance of an accurate diagnosis to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.
