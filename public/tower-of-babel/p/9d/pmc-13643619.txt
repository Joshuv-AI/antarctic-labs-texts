Feasibility and Safety of Venetoclax for Cytoreduction During Induction Therapy in Newly Diagnosed Acute Promyelocytic Leukemia
Authors: Yu Wang, Rong Xiao, Xiaobing Huang, Yi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and safety of venetoclax as a cytoreductive strategy during induction therapy in newly diagnosed acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), and to provide an exploratory description of its early efficacy.MethodsThis was a single‐center retrospective analysis including 29 patients newly diagnosed with APL between January 2023 and December 2025. All patients received induction therapy with all‐trans retinoic acid (ATRA) plus arsenic trioxide (ATO) and were treated with different cytoreduction strategies based on clinical decision‐making: venetoclax group (n = 16) or conventional cytoreduction group (hydroxyurea ± anthracycline, n = 13).ResultsThe proportion of hematologic complete recovery (HCR) at Day 28 was significantly higher in the venetoclax group than in the conventional group (93.75% vs. 46.15%, p = 0.003). The median time to HCR was shorter in the venetoclax group (26 vs. 28 days, p = 0.002). The median time to molecular complete remission (MCR) was also shorter (92 vs. 110 days, p = 0.001). In addition, the venetoclax group required significantly fewer red blood cell and platelet transfusions and had a shorter duration of hospitalization. There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of differentiation syndrome (DS) between the two groups.ConclusionVenetoclax appears to be a feasible and safe cytoreductive option during induction therapy in APL and may be associated with faster hematologic recovery. These findings require further validation in prospective studies.

Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) is a distinct subtype of acute myeloid leukemia characterized by the presence of the PML–RARA fusion gene. With the introduction of all‐trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide (ATO), APL has become one of the most curable forms of leukemia. However, early mortality during the induction phase remains a significant clinical challenge, primarily attributable to severe hemorrhage and infection. During induction therapy, leukocytosis and heightened inflammatory responses may increase the risk of differentiation syndrome (DS) and bleeding complications, underscoring the critical importance of effective cytoreductive strategies. Currently, hydroxyurea and anthracycline‐based regimens are commonly used for cytoreduction; however, their clinical utility is limited by treatment‐related toxicities, including myelosuppression, infectious complications, and cardiotoxicity. Venetoclax, a highly selective BCL‐2 inhibitor, has been approved for the treatment of various hematologic malignancies and has recently attracted increasing attention in the context of APL. Emerging evidence suggests that venetoclax demonstrates notable activity in relapsed/refractory (R/R) APL. For instance, Jiang et al. reported that venetoclax‐based combination regimens achieved high remission rates in patients with R/R APL [1]. In addition, the leukemia group at Beijing Children's Hospital was among the first to explore the use of venetoclax for cytoreduction in pediatric APL. In their single‐center study, all five children who received venetoclax achieved complete hematologic remission by Day 28 of induction therapy [2]. Based on these encouraging findings, the Chinese Children's Leukemia Group (CCLG) has initiated a nationwide multicenter prospective study (ChiCTR2300069288) to further evaluate the efficacy of venetoclax in pediatric APL. In adult APL, however, the clinical evidence for venetoclax remains limited, primarily consisting of case reports and small cohort studies, and high‐quality evidence regarding its role during induction therapy in newly diagnosed patients is still lacking. Against this background, the present study aims to describe the clinical application of venetoclax as a cytoreductive agent in newly diagnosed adult APL patients, and to evaluate its safety and feasibility, thereby providing foundational data for future prospective investigations.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was a single‐center retrospective analysis. Patients diagnosed with APL in the Department of Hematology at our institution between January 2023 and December 2025 were included for data analysis. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of APL confirmed by bone marrow morphology and positivity for the PML::RARA fusion gene; (2) newly diagnosed APL; and (3) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria: (1) Prior antileukemia treatment before admission or relapsed/refractory disease; (2) presence of severe uncontrolled active infection; and (3) pregnant or lactating women.

Treatment Regimens

Induction Therapy

All patients received standard differentiation induction therapy consisting of ATRA at a dose of 25 mg m−2 day−1 in combination with ATO at 0.16 mg kg−1 day−1 or Realgar‐Indigo naturalis formula at 60 mg kg−1 day−1. Induction therapy was administered for approximately 1 month until complete remission (CR) was achieved.

Cytoreductive Therapy

Cytoreductive therapy was initiated when the peripheral white blood cell (WBC) count exceeded 4 × 109/L. The choice of cytoreductive strategy was determined by the treating physician based on WBC level, bleeding risk, infection status, and overall clinical condition.

Venetoclax group (n = 16): Venetoclax was initiated at 100 mg on Day 1, escalated to 200 mg on Day 2. On Day 3, escalation to 400 mg was considered if the WBC count was still elevated and the patient showed good tolerance to the prior doses; if not, the dose was kept at the level of the previous day. Conventional cytoreduction group (n = 13): Six patients received hydroxyurea monotherapy (initial dose 0.5–3 g/day, adjusted according to WBC count). For patients with WBC > 10 × 109/L, daunorubicin was added to hydroxyurea (n = 7). All patients received standard supportive care, including prophylaxis for tumor lysis syndrome and DS.

Supportive Care

Transfusion support: Red blood cell transfusion was administered when hemoglobin was < 60 g/L or when clinically significant anemia symptoms were present. Platelet transfusion was given when platelet count was < 30 × 109/L or in the presence of active bleeding. Transfusion thresholds were consistent between the two groups.

Infection management: Empirical broad‐spectrum antibiotics were initiated when body temperature exceeded 38.3°C for more than 1 h, or > 38.0°C for more than 1 h in the presence of neutropenia.

Management of DS: Dexamethasone at a dose of 5–10 mg/day was administered upon the occurrence of DS.

Discharge criteria: Absence of active bleeding, afebrile for > 48 h, achievement of hematologic complete recovery (HCR), and adequate oral intake.

Efficacy and Safety Assessment

HCR was defined as neutrophil count > 0.5 × 109/L and platelet count > 50 × 109/L, assessed by daily peripheral blood counts during induction. Time to HCR was recorded as the first day on which both criteria were met. Following achievement of HCR, patients underwent bone marrow morphologic evaluation and PML–RARA transcript monitoring by RT‐qPCR (sensitivity 10−4) in bone marrow samples. Molecular assessments were performed after HCR was documented, with subsequent monitoring every 4 weeks during consolidation. Both groups followed identical post‐HCR monitoring protocols.

DS: Diagnosed according to established criteria, defined by the presence of at least two of the following: fever, weight gain, hypotension, respiratory distress, pericardial or pleural effusion, and acute kidney injury. Adverse events: Graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), Version 5.0.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were described as median (range) or mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Between‐group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 23.0.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 29 newly diagnosed patients who met the diagnostic criteria for APL were included in this study. Among them, 16 patients were assigned to the venetoclax group, with a median age of 38 years (range, 28–71 years). Two patients (12.5%) presented with a WBC count ≥ 10 × 109/L at diagnosis and were classified as high‐risk. In the conventional cytoreduction group, 13 patients were included, with a median age of 49 years (range, 18–66 years). Two patients (12.5%) had a WBC count ≥ 10 × 109/L at diagnosis, and one patient had extramedullary infiltration; these patients were classified as high‐risk. Overall, baseline characteristics, including age at diagnosis, risk stratification, and mutational profile, were generally comparable between the two groups (Table 1).

Efficacy Outcomes

All patients received standard induction therapy consisting of ATRA at 25 mg m−2 day−1 in combination with ATO at 0.16 mg kg−1 day−1 or Realgar‐Indigo naturalis formula at 60 mg kg−1 day−1, administered until CR was achieved, with a total duration of approximately 1 month. In the venetoclax group, five patients reached the maximum dose of 400 mg/day. The median duration of venetoclax exposure was 5 days (range, 3–7 days). Among the 16 patients receiving venetoclax, 15 patients (93.75%) achieved HCR by Day 28 of induction therapy. The remaining patient who did not achieve HCR by Day 28 reached HCR on Day 31. In the conventional cytoreduction group, the Day‐28 HCR rate was 46.2% (6/13), which was significantly lower than that in the venetoclax group (p = 0.003). Among the seven patients who did not achieve HCR by Day 28, all subsequently achieved HCR between Days 31 and 34 (Table 2).

Safety Outcomes

Myelosuppression: In the venetoclax group, 5 patients (31.25%) developed agranulocytosis, and 11 patients experienced platelet counts < 30 × 109/L during treatment. In the conventional cytoreduction group, 9 patients (69.23%) developed agranulocytosis, and 11 patients had platelet counts < 30 × 109/L during treatment.

Differentiation syndrome: DS occurred in four patients (25%) in the venetoclax group and six patients (46.2%) in the conventional cytoreduction group. All cases were mild and improved after treatment with dexamethasone and supportive care.

Infectious events: During induction therapy, both groups developed varying degrees of neutropenia‐associated infections, predominantly respiratory tract infections, followed by intra‐abdominal infections. Some patients also developed bloodstream infections and oral mucositis. In the venetoclax group, 12 patients (75%) experienced pulmonary infections of varying severity, mainly bacterial in origin; one patient had concomitant influenza A virus infection, and one patient had pulmonary fungal infection. In addition, two patients developed intra‐abdominal infections, one developed oral mucositis, and one patient developed skin and soft tissue infection. In the conventional cytoreduction group, 11 patients (84.6%) had pulmonary infections; one case rapidly progressed to Type I respiratory failure requiring noninvasive ventilatory support. Six patients developed pulmonary fungal infections, including one case of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. Two patients had bloodstream infections, two patients had intra‐abdominal infections, one patient had oral mucositis, and one patient had skin and soft tissue infection. Although no significant difference in overall infection rate was observed between the two groups, the conventional group showed a higher proportion of severe infections (Table 3).

Discussion

APL is a distinct subtype of acute myeloid leukemia, most commonly characterized by the t(15;17)(q22;q12) translocation resulting in the PML::RARA fusion gene [3]. Since the introduction of ATRA into clinical practice in the 1980s, the CR rate of APL has increased to over 90%, with markedly improved prognosis and long‐term survival. However, in real‐world clinical practice, 5%–10% of patients still die in the early phase after diagnosis due to severe complications such as hemorrhage, infection, or DS. This risk is particularly pronounced in high‐risk patients with an initial WBC count > 10 × 109/L. The high early mortality is mainly attributed to two factors. First, rapid leukocytosis during induction therapy may trigger differentiation syndrome, further exacerbating coagulopathy and bleeding risk. Second, conventional cytoreductive strategies are often associated with profound myelosuppression, leading to prolonged neutropenia and a significantly increased risk of infections. Therefore, how to optimize cytoreductive strategies while maintaining therapeutic efficacy and reducing treatment‐related toxicity remains a key unresolved issue in APL induction therapy.

Venetoclax is a highly selective BCL‐2 inhibitor that induces apoptosis by releasing the inhibition of proapoptotic proteins (e.g., BAX/BAK) and activating the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway. Beyond its canonical proapoptotic effects, accumulating evidence suggests that venetoclax also inhibits mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), thereby disrupting the energy metabolism essential for leukemia stem cell (LSC) survival and selectively eliminating LSCs [4, 5]. In APL, studies have demonstrated that leukemic promyelocytes commonly overexpress BCL‐2 and exhibit marked dependence on OXPHOS, providing a strong biological rationale for venetoclax‐based therapy. Mechanistically, recent evidence further indicates that venetoclax may induce accumulation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, and promote degradation of the PML–RARA fusion protein, thereby enhancing leukemic cell killing [6]. In ATO‐resistant models (e.g., NB4‐AsR1 and UF1 cell lines), venetoclax in combination with ATO has shown significant synergistic effects, suggesting potential utility in overcoming arsenic resistance [6]. In addition, ATRA has been shown to induce differentiation, suppress proliferation, and enhance apoptotic signaling, thereby further potentiating the antileukemic effects of venetoclax, indicating mechanistic complementarity between these agents [7].

From a translational perspective, although venetoclax has been established as a cornerstone therapy for older or unfit patients with acute myeloid leukemia [8], its application in APL remains investigational. Preliminary clinical evidence has shown encouraging signals. In relapsed/refractory APL, small cohort studies have reported that venetoclax‐based regimens can achieve high CR rates in patients resistant to ATO, with some patients reaching CMR, suggesting potential efficacy in the refractory setting [9]. Furthermore, in variant forms of APL with reduced sensitivity to ATRA or ATO (e.g., RARA fusion variants), case reports have also indicated possible activity of venetoclax, thereby expanding its potential therapeutic scope [10, 11, 12].

In addition, in the induction treatment of newly diagnosed APL, venetoclax has been explored as a cytoreductive agent in clinical practice, demonstrating unique therapeutic potential. A pioneering study from the leukemia team at Beijing Children's Hospital reported its use in pediatric APL, showing that the venetoclax group achieved higher Day‐28 HCR compared with conventional therapy, along with lower incidence of DS and reduced transfusion requirements [2]. In summary, these findings suggest that venetoclax may offer potential advantages in optimizing early treatment response and reducing treatment‐related toxicity in APL.

In the present study, the venetoclax group achieved a Day‐28 HCR of 93.8%, which is broadly consistent with the results reported by Beijing Children's Hospital in pediatric APL (100%). In terms of remission kinetics, both HCR and MCR were achieved earlier in the venetoclax group than in the conventional cytoreduction group. Several methodologic considerations are relevant to the interpretation of these time‐to‐event endpoints. First, HCR was determined solely by daily peripheral blood count monitoring and was independent of scheduled bone marrow examinations, as bone marrow assessment was performed only after HCR had been documented. Therefore, the observed 2‐day difference in time to HCR (26 vs. 28 days) cannot be attributed to differences in bone marrow sampling intervals or detection bias. Second, MCR assessments were conducted after HCR in both groups following identical protocols, with monitoring every 4 weeks during consolidation, minimizing systematic assessment bias between the two arms. Third, the kinetic findings were internally consistent across multiple independent endpoints: higher Day‐28 HCR rate, shorter time to MCR, reduced transfusion requirements, and shorter hospitalization. This pattern is consistent with the known mechanism of venetoclax in AML, where inhibition of BCL‐2 induces leukemic cell apoptosis and accelerates tumor burden clearance. Nevertheless, the absolute median difference in time to HCR was small (2 days), and given the small sample size and retrospective design, this difference should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis‐generating.

It is important to emphasize that, in the context of ATRA plus ATO‐based therapy, the overall CR rate of APL already exceeds 90%, and all patients in both groups ultimately achieved CR in this study. Therefore, differences in Day‐28 HCR are more likely to reflect differences in hematologic recovery kinetics rather than differences in final therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, MCR in APL is typically achieved progressively during the consolidation phase. At present, there is insufficient evidence to suggest that earlier attainment of MCR translates into long‐term survival benefits, such as improved overall survival (OS) or progression‐free survival (PFS). Accordingly, the clinical significance of this finding, particularly the modest absolute difference in HCR timing (2 days), remains uncertain and further validation in large‐scale, long‐term follow‐up studies is warranted.

Regarding safety, this study further suggests that venetoclax‐based regimens may have potential advantages in reducing early treatment‐related complications. DS remains one of the most clinically challenging adverse events during APL induction therapy. In this study, DS occurred in 10 patients overall, with no significant difference between the two groups. This may be partly attributable to the limited sample size and potential selection bias inherent to the retrospective design. Alternatively, it may indicate that although venetoclax theoretically reduces leukemic burden and may attenuate ATRA/ATO‐driven differentiation stress, the occurrence of DS is multifactorial, influenced by baseline tumor burden, leukocyte kinetics, and individual inflammatory responses. Therefore, the observed trend requires further confirmation in larger prospective studies.

In terms of myelosuppression, both groups experienced varying degrees of neutropenia, with a comparable incidence of Grade ≥ 3 neutropenia. However, further analysis indicated that the conventional cytoreduction group was more frequently associated with severe infectious complications, including respiratory failure and sepsis. This suggests that, beyond the duration of cytopenia, conventional regimens may also be associated with delayed functional immune reconstitution, thereby increasing the risk of invasive infections and infection‐related organ failure.

In addition, patients in the venetoclax group showed a significantly reduced dependence on blood product transfusions. Although transfusion support can rapidly correct anemia and bleeding tendency, its associated risks (such as transfusion reactions and infectious transmission) as well as resource limitations cannot be ignored in clinical practice. Venetoclax may indirectly reduce transfusion requirements by achieving faster leukemic clearance and improving coagulopathy, thereby not only reducing healthcare burden but also improving treatment safety. Given the high risk of early hemorrhage in APL, reduced transfusion requirement may also reflect more rapid correction of coagulation abnormalities and disease control.

Of note, in terms of long‐term toxicity, anthracycline‐associated cardiotoxicity remains a major limitation in conventional cytoreductive regimens. Anthracyclines exert their effects by intercalating into DNA and generating ROS, leading to irreversible myocardial injury [13]. ATO has also been associated with QT interval prolongation and arrhythmia risk [14]. Therefore, reducing anthracycline exposure in APL treatment has clear clinical significance. Although this study did not systematically assess cardiac toxicity, mechanistically, venetoclax primarily targets the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway rather than inducing oxidative stress or DNA damage. As such, it is unlikely to exacerbate myocardial oxidative injury or synergize with anthracycline‐related cardiotoxicity, potentially offering a more favorable cardiovascular safety profile, particularly in patients with preexisting cardiac disease or high cardiovascular risk.

In addition to the safety and long‐term toxicity considerations outlined above, differences in healthcare resource utilization were also observed between the two groups. From the perspective of healthcare resource use, the venetoclax group demonstrated significantly shorter mean hospitalization duration (28.2 vs. 35.3 days, p = 0.006) and reduced requirements for blood product transfusions. These differences suggest that the venetoclax‐based strategy may contribute to healthcare resource savings during APL induction therapy, which typically demands intensive supportive care. Furthermore, seven patients (53.8%) in the conventional cytoreduction group received daunorubicin, whereas no anthracyclines were administered in the venetoclax group. Given that anthracyclines are associated with cumulative dose‐dependent cardiotoxicity [13] and that patients with APL often survive for decades after cure, avoiding anthracycline exposure during induction may offer underappreciated long‐term benefits, even if short‐term clinical outcomes appear comparable. Although the incidence of differentiation syndrome did not differ significantly between the two groups in this small cohort, the conventional group experienced a higher proportion of severe infectious complications, including respiratory failure requiring noninvasive ventilation and P. jirovecii pneumonia, which themselves add to healthcare costs. However, this study was not designed as a formal cost‐effectiveness analysis, and drug pricing data were not systematically collected. Whether the observed differences in resource utilization translate into actual cost savings or long‐term clinical benefits remains uncertain and warrants prospective studies incorporating formal health economic evaluations.

In summary, the present findings suggest that incorporating venetoclax as a cytoreductive strategy in APL induction therapy may not only facilitate rapid and deep hematologic remission, but also potentially reduce differentiation syndrome, shorten the duration of neutropenia, decrease infection risk, and lower transfusion requirements. Furthermore, this strategy may help reduce anthracycline‐related toxicity, warranting further validation in prospective clinical studies.

Conclusion

This retrospective study suggests that, in induction therapy for newly diagnosed APL, venetoclax‐based cytoreductive regimens may confer multiple clinical benefits, including a higher Day‐28 HCR, earlier achievement of CR and molecular remission, reduced blood product transfusion requirements, and shorter hospitalization duration. These findings indicate that a treatment strategy combining venetoclax with ATRA and ATO may have potential advantages in rapidly controlling leukemic burden and improving early clinical status, thereby optimizing the overall induction treatment course in APL. However, these results require further validation in higher‐quality prospective studies. Ongoing prospective clinical trials (e.g., ChiCTR2300069288) are expected to provide higher‐level evidence regarding the role of venetoclax in APL.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single‐center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, which limits statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Second, treatment regimens were selected based on individualized clinical judgment, introducing unavoidable selection bias and potential confounding factors. In addition, incomplete documentation in some historical cases may have affected the accuracy of certain variable analyses. The control group also consisted of heterogeneous cytoreductive regimens, which may have reduced internal consistency in group comparisons. Furthermore, the absence of long‐term follow‐up data precludes evaluation of the impact of different treatment strategies on PFS and OS. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive conclusions. Future well‐designed multicenter prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these observations.
