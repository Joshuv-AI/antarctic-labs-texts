Understanding medical students’ knowledge, attitudes, and preparatory needs for future prescribing of long-acting injectable pre-exposure prophylaxis
Authors: Kenneth Cole, Jacob Bleasdale, Amy Hequembourg, Gene Morse, Sarahmona Przybyla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInjectable pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) offers a novel approach to Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) prevention and may increase accessibility for people vulnerable to HIV acquisition. However, effective implementation will require that healthcare providers are aware and knowledgeable of injectable PrEP. One avenue to increasing awareness and knowledge among healthcare providers is to educate future providers during their medical education. From June to August 2022, we conducted in-depth, semi-structured interviews with medical students (N = 32) to better understand their perceptions and attitudes toward injectable PrEP, as well as their preparatory needs for prescribing injectable PrEP in the future. Thematic analyses highlighted two primary domains: (1) Perceptions of injectable PrEP and (2) Preparatory educational needs for prescribing injectable PrEP in the future. Expansive themes centered around low injectable PrEP awareness and knowledge, concerns about the accessibility of injectable PrEP, patient-centered benefits of injectable PrEP, interest in developing patient-provider communication skills, desire for comprehensive education about injectable PrEP and its prescribing practices, and preferences for enhanced learning about injectable PrEP. Results from this study suggest medical students desire specific, patient-centred educational opportunities to prescribe injectable PrEP in the future. Understanding these preparatory needs will assist with developing innovative training opportunities centred on HIV prevention and treatment.Keywords: HIV, Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), Injectable PrEP, Medical students

Introduction

Despite remarkable efforts to end the HIV epidemic in the United States (U.S.), HIV remains a significant public health concern, with 39,201 new HIV infections and an estimated 1.1 million people living with HIV in 2023 [1]. Although antiretroviral therapy (ART) has evolved to effectively manage HIV infection [2], current HIV incidence underscores the need for continued innovation in HIV prevention. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has emerged as an effective biomedical strategy for preventing HIV, and when taken as prescribed, has been shown to reduce acquisition by approximately 99% [3, 4].

Since 2012, PrEP had only been available as a daily oral medication; however, in 2021, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved cabotegravir as the first ever, long-acting injectable for HIV PrEP [5]. Results from clinical trials found cabotegravir to be highly efficacious at preventing HIV acquisition when administered as an intramuscular gluteal injection every two months [6–8]. Current research among patients suggests great interest in injectable PrEP [9, 10]. For instance, among a sample of 688 gay and bisexual men who have sex with men, injectable PrEP was the most preferred mode of administration [9]. Furthermore, injectable PrEP offers a promising and novel administration route for HIV prevention, particularly for people who have difficulties adhering to oral medications [11–13].

For injectable PrEP to be effectively implemented in clinical settings, healthcare providers must be aware and knowledgeable of the medication and be willing to prescribe it. Limited healthcare provider awareness of oral PrEP and barriers at the individual- (e.g., lack of knowledge and awareness, feelings of discomfort), interpersonal- (e.g., patient-provider communication), clinic- (e.g., competing clinical priorities, times constrains), and community- (e.g., HIV- and PrEP-related stigma) levels have resulted in lower-than-expected implementation [14–17] and missed opportunities for PrEP intervention [18, 19]. Recent studies have demonstrated high awareness of injectable PrEP among healthcare providers [20–22] and have highlighted several benefits to its wider implementation, including greater privacy, increased adherence, and reduced pill burden [20–23]. However, the potential for new PrEP modalities, such as injectable PrEP, to make meaningful strides at preventing HIV acquisition is critically dependent on healthcare providers’ knowledge, awareness, and willingness to engage in dialogue with eligible individuals and, ultimately, prescribe it.

One avenue to maximize the public health impact of injectable PrEP is to train future healthcare providers. Currently, there is a large body of literature exploring oral PrEP awareness and knowledge among medical students [24–28]. Previous research has shown that medical students have high oral PrEP awareness [24, 26]; yet, report inadequate training [27, 28] and low familiarity with its prescribing guidelines [26]. While there is an abundance of research examining attitudes toward oral PrEP, there is even less literature examining medical students’ awareness, knowledge and attitudes toward injectable PrEP [29].

Given the emergence of injectable PrEP and the lack of research exploring future healthcare providers’ perspectives, a more nuanced understanding of medical students’ knowledge, awareness, and perceptions of injectable PrEP is needed. Further, qualitative insights may help inform the development and refinement of PrEP-related training opportunities within health professional curricula. The purpose of this study was to qualitatively explore medical students’ perceptions of injectable PrEP and preparatory needs to prescribe it in the future.

Methods

Study participants and procedures

Students enrolled in the Doctor of Medicine (MD) program at one public university in New York State were recruited for study participation via student listservs and study flyers. Participants were eligible if they were (1) a current MD student; (2) at least 18 years old; (3) able to read, write, and speak English; and (4) able to access and complete a brief survey and qualitative interview. Eligible participants were sent a study information sheet, completed a brief online survey, and were scheduled for a qualitative interview.

Interviews were conducted from June to August 2022 via videoconferencing technology or phone. Based on our previous work on oral PrEP [16, 26], the semi-structured interview guide directed discussion regarding injectable PrEP knowledge, awareness, and preparatory educational needs. Interviews were approximately 41 min in length and continued until data saturation was achieved and no new information emerged. Participants received a $50 Amazon gift card for their participation. All study procedures were approved by the University at Buffalo Institutional Review Board with a waiver for written informed consent, in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Analytic approach

With participant consent, interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed. Recordings were transcribed verbatim, and all files were transferred confidentially. We performed a thematic content analysis to identify semantic-level themes using a five phase approach [30, 31]. First, three qualitative analysts independently reviewed the data to develop an initial codebook. Second, two analysts coded an initial subset of transcripts (n = 6) using the initial codebook. Phase three consisted of arbitration, codebook modifications, and the recoding of these six transcripts. The analysts regularly compared codes, resolved discrepancies, and revised the codebook to establish a final codebook. In the fourth phase, the two analysts independently coded the remaining transcripts (n = 26). Finally, the team identified the prevailing domains, themes, and selected representative quotes. Rigor and trustworthiness were assessed during the data collection and analytic phases using criteria set forth by Guba [32].

Perceptions about injectable PrEP

Participants’ perceptions of injectable PrEP spanned three themes. These included: (a) low injectable PrEP awareness and knowledge, (b) concerns about the accessibility of injectable PrEP, and (c) patient-centered benefits of injectable PrEP.

Low injectable PrEP awareness and knowledge

Participants described varying levels of PrEP awareness, which was most commonly acquired from didactic lectures describing ART for HIV treatment during courses in the first two years of medical school. Students described how their education primarily focused on the pharmacological properties of ART and PrEP. Because of this focus, participants described that they were more likely to be aware of PrEP, including injectable PrEP, based on its pharmacology, rather than other clinical aspects, such as side effect profiles, HIV/STI testing frequency, or eligible patient populations.

Despite most participants being aware of oral PrEP (84.4%), a vast majority were unaware of injectable PrEP (75.0%). Given its novelty, most students noted that they had not learned about injectable PrEP, which was perceived to contribute to low overall knowledge of the medication. The few participants who were aware of injectable PrEP noted that their knowledge and awareness was based on other personal or professional experiences, such as volunteer experiences with local sexual health clinics, and was unrelated to their medical school education.

Concerns about the accessibility of injectable PrEP

When presented with information about injectable PrEP (e.g., dosing schedule, potential side effects), participants identified multiple concerns for prescribing it in the future. The most discussed concern was accessibility, specifically regarding patient transportation, time, and cost. For instance, participants were concerned that injectable PrEP may not be accessible to patients with limited or unreliable transportation. Participants noted that this may hinder patients’ ability to attend injection appointments every two months, resulting in poor adherence. Participants also described how time constraints may exacerbate transportation barriers. Patients who rely on public transportation might require more time off from work to travel to and from their injectable PrEP appointments, and more frequent office visits might increase the time associated with injectable PrEP. Participants noted that this may make it more difficult for patients to remain adherent. Participants also noted that insurance-related and out-of-pocket costs may impact accessibility. Due to its recent FDA approval, participants described concerns regarding the capability and willingness of insurance companies to cover costs associated with an injectable medication when an equally effective oral medication is available. Relatedly, participants expressed concerns regarding prohibitive out-of-pocket costs and how such costs may deter interest among patients.

Patient-centered benefits of injectable PrEP

Despite marked accessibility concerns, participants identified many patient-centered benefits to injectable PrEP, including reduced pill burden, better adherence, and increased discretion. Many participants noted that receiving an injection every two months removes the daily pill burden that is commonly associated with oral PrEP. Participants noted that this could improve adherence and compliance with one’s treatment regimen. Discretion and medical autonomy were also identified as primary benefits of injectable PrEP. Participants described how the absence of pill bottles might mitigate unwanted conversations about a patient’s PrEP use and HIV status, subsequently reducing PrEP-related stigma. Further, participants described how injectable PrEP may promote medical autonomy, as an injectable form of medicine allows for more privacy.

Preparatory educational needs for prescribing injectable PrEP in the future

Participants highlighted critical preparatory needs for prescribing injectable PrEP in the future, including (a) interest in developing patient-provider communication skills, (b) desire for comprehensive education about injectable PrEP and its prescribing practices, and (c) preferences for enhanced learning about injectable PrEP.

Interest in developing patient-provider communication skills

To prescribe injectable PrEP in the future, participants expressed the need for additional training in patient-centred communication strategies. Specifically, participants noted that their sexual health training would benefit from spending additional time on effective approaches to build rapport with patients and skill-based experiences navigating conversations on uncomfortable topics, such as sexual behaviours. Participants noted that lacking these patient-provider communication skills would make it difficult for them to discuss injectable PrEP with future patients.

To build effective sexual health communication skills, participants described how having experiential learning opportunities, such as patient simulations and role-playing activities, would be essential. Participants highlighted how standardized patients (i.e., actors role-playing as patients) are a useful strategy for developing more effective communication skills. Opportunities to have in-depth discussions about sexual health with standardized patients would allow participants to safely navigate conversations about sexual health, including injectable PrEP, and become more comfortable engaging with patients on these topics. With effective educational opportunities for patient-provider communication, participants noted that they would be more comfortable having conversations about injectable PrEP.

Desire for comprehensive education about injectable PrEP and its prescribing practices

Participants noted that additional educational opportunities to enhance their knowledge of injectable PrEP and its prescribing practices would be valuable and informative. Specifically, participants expressed a need to learn about regimen specifics, pharmacological mechanisms, and eligible patient populations. For instance, understanding the pharmacokinetics of injectable PrEP would allow participants to feel more confident when discussing the medication with patients. Participants also expressed the need for education on injectable PrEP candidacy that extends beyond identifying eligible patient populations but also includes discussions on how various social and structural factors, such as financial concerns and transportation barriers, can influence whether injectable PrEP is feasible for some patients. Overall, participants noted that this knowledge would help ensure that both themselves and their patients have the necessary information to make informed decisions.

Preferences for enhanced learning about injectable PrEP

When asked about injectable PrEP learning experiences, most participants expressed a desire for incorporating injectable PrEP lectures taught by current PrEP providers into existing curricula. Participants noted that didactic learning opportunities with healthcare providers who have experience discussing the medication with patients and have prescribed it in their clinics would best prepare them to prescribe injectable PrEP in the future. The ideal timing of these learning experiences varied; some participants thought it would be helpful to receive this information in their pre-clinical years, while others believed it would be most beneficial during clinical rotations, residency, or through Continuing Medical Education (CME) sessions. Other participants believed that having both pre-clinical and clinical experiences would be the best option as it would ensure that all students have educational opportunities to learn about injectable PrEP. Often, participants’ preferences for learning about injectable PrEP were shaped by their experiences with contraception education and counselling. Participants noted that there are multiple contraception-related learning experiences throughout their training and highlighted that education about injectable PrEP could be similarly implemented.

Discussion

This study explored current medical students’ perceptions of injectable PrEP, as well as their preparatory educational needs to prescribe it in the future. Currently, there is limited research understanding attitudes toward injectable PrEP among medical students [29], and no current research that seeks to qualitatively explore the perspectives of injectable PrEP specifically among medical students post-FDA approval. Our data revealed that while participants were largely unaware of injectable PrEP, medical students expressed high interest in prescribing it in the future, given they received sufficient training.

Participants discussed how different facets of injectable PrEP, such as its dosing frequency and potential side effects could promote or hinder its uptake. Further, participants commented critically on the role that social determinants would play in injectable PrEP implementation and identified critical components for accessing care. Participants identified several accessibility concerns, including transportation and cost as potential barriers to uptake, which is consistent with current research regarding injectable PrEP implementation from both the patient and provider perspectives [20–22, 33]. Yet, students also identified potential facilitators of uptake that were consistent with existing literature. For instance, decreased pill burden and increased privacy associated with injectable PrEP have been seen as promising patient-centered benefits of this new form of HIV prevention [33].

Consistent with previous work [29], participants in our study described a general lack of awareness and knowledge about injectable PrEP. This is most likely due to the fact that injectable PrEP received FDA approval in December 2021, and study enrollment and data collection occurred shortly thereafter; nevertheless, participants highlighted multiple training needs. Similar to previous findings regarding provider training needs [16], medical students in our study sought more comprehensive education on injectable PrEP prescribing practices, pharmacological mechanisms, and eligible patient populations. Our findings contribute to existing evidence that PrEP providers must be adequately trained on PrEP-specific information and underscores the need for greater emphasis on injectable PrEP prescribing practices in medical education.

Participants highlighted opportunities for training that would strengthen their ability to prescribe injectable PrEP in the future. Experiential learning opportunities to gain skills in patient-provider communication were identified as a high priority. Previous work has shown when students are exposed to culturally competent and LGBTQ + inclusive standardized patient encounters, their confidence in having sexual health-related conversations increases [34]. Yet, participants in our study noted how the absence of such encounters may limit their readiness to discuss injectable PrEP in the future. Our qualitative findings suggest similar learning opportunities are needed to increase students’ comfort discussing and prescribing injectable PrEP in the future, as normalizing conversations with patients about injectable PrEP has been identified as an important facilitator to broader injectable PrEP implementation [33].

It is noteworthy that participants’ preparatory educational needs for prescribing injectable PrEP mirror providers’ training preferences for prescribing oral PrEP [16] and are congruent with training modules previously developed by health professional students for an oral PrEP interprofessional community education initiative [35]. Our findings, coupled with previous work, suggest that many educational needs may be consistent across PrEP modalities (i.e., injectable, oral), despite each modality having unique implications for clinical implementation (e.g., administering an injection vs. prescribing an oral medication). Further, limited research has examined differences in knowledge, attitudes, and preparatory needs across PrEP modalities (oral PrEP vs. injectable PrEP) among medical students, or how such differences may impact their comfort and willingness to prescribe each PrEP modality in the future, highlighting an area for future research.

Students identified various preferences for learning about injectable PrEP in the future, including host, training style, and time of training in relation to their medical training. Surprisingly, students had diverse opinions on the preferred timing for injectable PrEP education. Participants often discussed that their preclinical years were most ideal to learn about injectable PrEP prescribing practices and patient-provider communication, leaving the clinical years for greater in-depth experiences about injectable PrEP implementation and for opportunities to discuss it with patients. CMEs were identified as potential opportunities to refresh providers’ knowledge and provide key updates, such as revised clinical treatment guidelines that are regularly revised. Our findings highlight the need to provide medical students with multiple opportunities to learn about injectable PrEP throughout their medical education and beyond. Integrating injectable PrEP into both didactic and hands-on learning experiences throughout medical school has the potential to improve student’s comfort discussing injectable PrEP with patients, and ultimately, prescribing it in the future.

Strengths and limitations

This study has some limitations. First, although participants were diverse in terms of race, gender, and sexual identity, this study was restricted to medical students at one public university in New York State; therefore, our findings may not be transferable to medical students at other institutions or students in other academic training programs (e.g., nurse practitioner and pharmacy students). Future qualitative studies should be extended to other geographic areas, medical schools, and students in other academic training programs. Second, our study relied on a convenience sampling strategy to recruit participants, and we did not require individuals to report a priori PrEP awareness, which may have affected the level of specificity shared during the interviews. Lastly, we conducted this study prior to the FDA approval of twice-yearly injectable PrEP. More research is needed to explore how future healthcare providers perceive additional HIV PrEP options, such as twice-yearly injectable PrEP, and event-based oral PrEP, given the rapidly growing prevention landscape. Despite these limitations, our study is among a burgeoning body of research exploring training requisites for future healthcare providers, thereby complementing the existing quantitative work within the field.

Conclusions

It is critical to understand the viewpoints of future clinicians, such as medical students, to help improve injectable PrEP uptake and adherence, as healthcare providers are key stakeholders in ending the HIV epidemic. Results from this study suggest medical students desire specific, patient-centred educational opportunities to prescribe injectable PrEP in the future. Understanding these preparatory educational needs will assist with developing innovative training opportunities centred on HIV prevention and treatment. Optimizing these training opportunities will require future research that extends beyond medical students’ perspectives to include other health professional students, such as physician assistant, nurse practitioner, and pharmacy students. Understanding these training needs will equip providers to prescribe injectable PrEP in the future, potentially increasing its implementation within communities most impacted by HIV.
