Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is a Class Ia indication for coronary artery disease diagnosis in the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology 2021 chest pain guidelines.1 However, this technology requires patient exposure to ionizing radiation and its associated cancer risks,2 which remains a major barrier to widespread adoption, particularly in younger individuals and women.

Take-Home Messages

•Standardized CCTA scan protocol interventions can yield meaningful radiation dose reductions by maximizing CT scanner technical capabilities, using vendor technical support, and maintaining consistent expert image review and technologist feedback.

Project Rationale

Radiation dose reduction strategies in CCTA are well established to reduce unnecessary radiation exposure and minimize the harmful effects of ionizing radiation.3 We aimed to reduce radiation dose from CCTA by implementing standardized, patient-specific CCTA scanning protocols using the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) principle.4 Noting the recent increase in reimbursement for CCTA,5 predictable increase in patient volumes will require a streamlined workflow to optimize patient safety and maintain diagnostic accuracy.

Project Description

This quality improvement (QI) project was initiated at The University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB) Clear Lake campus. A General Electric (GE) Revolution Ascend 128-slice, 4-cm detector, computed tomography (CT) scanner was identified as our target intervention scanner. Before the intervention, all patients, irrespective of age, heart rate, or body mass index (BMI), underwent scanning using a retrospective gated acquisition of 0% to 95% of the cardiac cycle. CCTA scan parameters were adjusted on site by a fellowship-trained, level III, advanced cardiac imaging cardiologist in collaboration with the lead site CT technologist and a GE clinical applications CT specialist.

We implemented the following QI interventions:

1.In patients with resting heart rate <65 beats/min, prospectively gated acquisition was limited to 50% to 70% phase of the cardiac cycle.

In patients with resting heart rate <65 beats/min, prospectively gated acquisition was limited to 50% to 70% phase of the cardiac cycle.

2.In patients with resting heart rate >65 beats/min, dose-modulated, retrospectively gated acquisition was limited to 30% to 80% of the cardiac phase.

In patients with resting heart rate >65 beats/min, dose-modulated, retrospectively gated acquisition was limited to 30% to 80% of the cardiac phase.

3.Site CT technicians were educated on the importance of tailoring every CCTA acquisition to the patient's age, heart rate, BMI, and study indication.

Site CT technicians were educated on the importance of tailoring every CCTA acquisition to the patient's age, heart rate, BMI, and study indication.

4.The scan field of view was limited to extend from below the tracheal bifurcation to the base of the heart to encompass only the heart, except in cases of post–coronary artery bypass, where the cranial field of view was extended to the clavicles to visualize the internal mammary artery origins and subclavian arteries (Figure 1).

Project Outcome

The protocol change occurred on November 26, 2024. Pre-QI data were collected from July 26, 2024 to November 25, 2024, and post-QI data were collected from November 27, 2024 to March 26, 2025. Data from 146 patients were reviewed; the patient characteristics are shown in Table 2. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to compare various measures before and after the QI initiative, based on the normality of the data. Normality was assessed both visually and using the Anderson-Darling test and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U test) was specifically used to compare CCTA radiation doses before and after the QI initiative.

The results showed a significant 63.4% reduction in CCTA radiation doses between the pre- and post-QI groups: 19.4 mSv (95% CI: 18.03-21.08) versus 7.1 mSv (95% CI: 4.52-9.02), P < 0.0001 (Figure 4). A larger decrease in median radiation dose was observed in women (66.3%) compared with men (61.6%) (Figure 5). Incorporating the radiation dose of the calcium score into the scan resulted in a higher total radiation dose and similar patterns of dose reduction between the sexes, 61.5% reduction in women versus 57.4% in men (Figure 6). There was an increase in BMI-dose correlations postintervention (correlation coefficient = 0.29, P = 0.015) compared with preintervention (correlation coefficient = 0.47, P ≤ 0.0001) (Figure 7).

Project Impact

The impact of the intervention was an improvement in patient safety and scan efficiency by lowering cumulative radiation exposure, as well as a 32% increase in patient volume (average, from 25 scans/month to 34 scans/month). Image quality was also significantly improved, resulting in increased diagnostic scan accuracy.

Discussion

Its high diagnostic accuracy, noninvasive nature, and ability to provide both anatomic and functional information make CCTA a preferred first-line modality for low- to intermediate-risk patients. CCTA radiation dose depends on multiple factors, including acquisition protocol, patient body habitus, heart rate, and operator expertise. Advances in CT technology currently include scanner-assisted dose-reduction strategies, however inconsistent protocol application, lack of CT technologist expertise, and imaging physician oversight often result in higher than necessary radiation exposure. Our institution identified a need for standardized, patient-centric CCTA scanning protocols to ensure optimal balance between diagnostic quality and radiation safety. Our experience supports the feasibility and effectiveness of integrating ALARA-driven, patient-centric protocols into daily CCTA practice. The magnitude of dose reduction aligns with, and in some cases exceeds, reductions reported in multicenter registries and randomized studies. In the ERASIR study, which compared filtered back projection (FBP) and adaptive statistical iterative reconstruction (ASIR) among 574 patients undergoing CCTA at 3 centers, ASIR was associated with a 44% reduction in the median radiation dose compared with FBP.7 Another study showed that imaging at 100 kV reduced the effective dose by 40% to 50% regardless of the CCTA protocol or type of CT system being used.8

The PROTECTION VI study, a large international multicenter registry of over 4,000 patients from 61 sites across 32 countries, aimed to estimate radiation dose levels associated with CCTA procedures and investigated dose-reduction techniques in clinical practice.9 Similar to the findings in our study, low (90-100 kVp) tube potential protocols lowered median radiation exposure by 50% compared with conventional settings.9 Our study employed heart rate, BMI, clinical indication, and other patient characteristics to determine the optimal scanner protocol, a concept also proposed in the PROTECTION VI study. Despite evidence demonstrating that almost 80% of CCTA radiation dose reduction can be achieved, there are still significant gaps in the global implementation of dose-reduction strategies.10 For instance, the median site radiation dose in the PROTECTION VI study ranged from 0.8 to 29.3 mSv across all 61 included sites. This significant variation in median dose across the different sites emphasizes the urgent need for site-specific training and the adoption of patient-centric CCTA scan protocols. Several centers in the United States are still using high doses of radiation for CCTA, similar to our preintervention institutional median radiation dose of 19.4 mSv overall and 21.4 mSv in women. Our study addressed this need at our institution. The observed greater dose reduction that we saw in women versus men (66.3% vs 61.6%) is clinically significant, given their higher lifetime risk of radiation-induced malignancy.

The limitations of our project include the small sample size, short follow-up duration, and subjective assessment of image quality and diagnostic accuracy. These limitations are currently being addressed at our institution with ongoing data collection. The success of this initiative relied on technical CT scanner adjustments, CT technologist education, and collaboration with the advanced imaging cardiologist to modify CCTA protocols based on likely clinical indications, for example diagnosing coronary artery disease versus endocarditis on preoperative evaluation. Storing protocols on the scanner console ensured rapid selection and reduced the risk of defaulting to nonoptimized settings under time pressure, thus improving efficiency. Improved postintervention BMI-dose correlation confirmed that the technologists were consistently applying BMI-based adjustments, further personalizing care.

Conclusions

Implementation of a standardized patient-centric protocol for a university CCTA program was successful in significantly reducing patient radiation dose.
