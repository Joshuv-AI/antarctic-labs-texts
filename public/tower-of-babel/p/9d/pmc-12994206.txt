Elevated phthalate exposure and metabolic susceptibility increased breast cancer risk: A 20-y follow-up study in Taiwan
Authors: Hui-Chi Chen, San-Lin You, Ching-Hung Lin, Chien-Wen Sun, Chun-Ju Chiang, Hwai-I Yang, Li-Yu Wang, Chien-An Sun, Jessica Liu, Chin-Lan Jen, Yuh-An Chen, Yen-Shen Lu, Ann-Lii Cheng, Shu-Li Wang, Chao A. Hsiung, Chien-Jen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis prospective epidemiologic study in an East Asian cohort demonstrates that women with both high di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) exposure and a high percentage of mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP%) have a significantly increased risk of breast cancer, establishing clear temporal causality. MEHP% reflects metabolic susceptibility modulating DEHP-associated breast cancer risk. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for individual differences in DEHP metabolism in environmental health research. Integrating MEHP% into risk assessments may support the development of personalized prevention strategies and targeted monitoring efforts across diverse populations. This has important implications for public health surveillance and risk stratification, enabling the identification of individuals more susceptible to DEHP-related breast cancer and informing more effective prevention strategies.

Phthalates are widely used in various plastics, pharmaceuticals, and personal care items such as film formers, stabilizers, dispersants, lubricants, binders, emulsifying agents, viscosity control agents, gelling agents, and suspending agents. Humans are exposed to phthalates mainly through ingestion and inhalation. Some phthalates, such as butyl benzyl phthalate (BBzP), dibutyl phthalate (DBP), and di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), may alter or mimic estradiol in vitro and may increase the risk of breast cancer in Sprague-Dawley rats (1–3).

The evidence from human studies on phthalates and breast cancer is currently limited and inconclusive (4–6). The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified DEHP as a Group 2B human carcinogen (7). Case–control studies have reported inconsistent findings; some found associations between breast cancer risk and monoethyl phthalate (MEP) or specific DEHP metabolites like mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) and mono-(2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl) phthalate (MECCP), while others found no significant link (4, 5, 8).

A major limitation of most previous research is the lack of causal temporality inherent in case–control designs. Changes in lifestyle, healthcare, or medical therapy following a breast cancer diagnosis may lead to altered phthalate exposure, potentially biasing the observed associations (9). Therefore, prospective studies are essential to establish the true temporal relationship between phthalate exposure and breast cancer risk.

Findings from existing prospective studies remain inconsistent (10–12). While some cohort studies reported no association, others have suggested that not only the concentration of DEHP metabolites but also the ratios of primary DEHP metabolite (MEHP) to secondary DEHP metabolites warrant further investigation of individual metabolites, their ratios, and their overall summation (11).

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an especially concerning upward trend observed in East Asia. Since the 1980s, the incidence of breast cancer has increased markedly, particularly among younger women in this region (13). Similarly, the incidence of other estrogen-related malignancies, including type I endometrial cancer and endometrioid ovarian carcinoma, has also risen substantially (14, 15).

These epidemiological patterns suggest that estrogen-related environmental exposures, including endocrine-disrupting chemicals like phthalates, may be contributing factors. This nested case–control study in an East Asian cohort aimed to assess the association between breast cancer risk and phthalate exposure, preserving correct causal temporality. To assess potential effect modification, we examined whether the association between phthalate exposure and breast cancer risk varied across key hormonal and demographic factors. Stratified analyses were conducted by age at enrollment, early menarche, years of estrogen exposure, body mass index (BMI), menopausal status, age at diagnosis, and urinary levels of other phthalate metabolites. A primary aim of this study was to evaluate MEHP%—the percentage of MEHP in ∑DEHP metabolites—as a biomarker of individual metabolic susceptibility. Given the central role of endogenous estrogen exposure, we further investigated whether early menarche modified this association to clarify whether breast cancer risk increases when high phthalate exposure co-occurs with this established hormonal risk factor.

Discussion

This community-based prospective study, with a nested case–control study design, found an increased breast cancer risk associated with high ∑DEHP metabolites combined with high MEHP% in an East Asian cohort of 11,923 women with 20 y of follow-up in Taiwan. We also observed a dose–response relation with breast cancer risk for urinary MEHP level in quintile. MEHP%, an indicator of DEHP metabolic susceptibility, likely plays a role in modulating the DEHP-related breast cancer risk, and could explain the inconclusive findings in previous studies.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) declined to classify DEHP as a human carcinogen in 2012 due to concerns about 1) a lack of clear temporality, 2) inconsistent effects among the four DEHP metabolites measured, and 3) the absence of a dose–response relationship for all DEHP metabolites. However, there was sufficient evidence for DEHP’s carcinogenicity in experimental animals (7). Several mechanisms may explain the carcinogenic effects of DEHP in humans, including mutagenic activity through chromosomal aberrations, sister chromatid exchanges, and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) activation (17–19). AhR activation promotes mammary tumor cell growth, inhibits apoptosis, and enhances epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, which may link DEHP exposure to breast cancer risk (20). Additionally, exposure to DEHP and other phthalates such as DEP has been associated with increased levels of inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, potentially leading to DNA damage (21, 22). Importantly, DEHP also mediates drug resistance by directly targeting AhR in human breast cancer, further complicating treatment outcomes (23). Assessing DNA repair enzyme activity and phthalic acid ester lipase function may help identify populations at higher risk for phthalate-related breast cancer.

Over the past decade, evidence from case–control and prospective studies on phthalate-related breast cancer in humans remains inconclusive. Case–control studies in Mexican and Alaskan women reported a significant association between elevated urinary levels of certain DEHP metabolites, especially MEHP, and increased risk of breast cancer (5, 6). But no association was found for individual DEHP metabolites in the Long Island Breast Cancer Study (8). The first prospective nested case–control study reported no association between urinary phthalate level and breast cancer risk among postmenopausal women in the United States (10). Another prospective nested case–control study reported ethnic differences in associations between DEHP and breast cancer in the multiethnic study in Los Angeles and Hawaii Natives. An increased breast cancer risk was associated with high MEHP% and ratios of MEHP to secondary DEHP metabolites in Whites, elevated urinary levels of MEHP and MECPP were associated with an increased breast cancer risk in Native Hawaiians, while no such association was observed in Japanese, African, or Latino Americans (11). The UK Biobank prospective cohort study reported that each quartile of DEHP yearly average exposure in tap water was positively associated with an increasing risk of malignant neoplasm (HR = 1.05, 95 % CI = 1.03 to 1.07), carcinoma in situ (HR = 1.08, 95 % CI = 1.04 to 1.11), and benign neoplasm (HR = 1.13, 95 % CI = 1.07 to 1.20) (12). In this prospective nested case–control study, which accounted for correct temporality, we found an increased breast cancer risk associated with high ∑DEHP metabolites combined with high MEHP%, as well as a dose–response relationship across quintiles of urinary MEHP levels.

Discrepancies across studies may be attributed to a complex interplay of methodological and population-specific factors. Variations in study design, such as differences in follow-up duration and the distribution of menopausal status in the cohorts, are critical factors that influence comparative results. Furthermore, differences in the levels and intrinsic variability (e.g., SD) of exposure biomarkers also contribute to divergent findings.

Beyond these factors, variations in DEHP exposure levels and metabolic susceptibility are key to understanding the inconsistent associations. MEHP, the primary metabolite of DEHP, plays a critical role in DEHP metabolism and may be more toxic than its secondary or tertiary metabolites. MEHP% represents the proportion of MEHP in total measured DEHP metabolites; a higher MEHP% potentially indicates limited metabolic conversion of MEHP into downstream phase II or III metabolites. This pattern suggests higher metabolic susceptibility, where intrinsic factors (such as genetic variations, hormone levels, or ethnic differences) and individual environmental exposures (such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals, pollutants, dietary habits, or lifestyle factors) may impair further metabolism. Efficient metabolism of MEHP may mitigate breast cancer risk, even in high-DEHP exposure individuals. Differences in metabolic susceptibility may help explain the inconsistencies observed in previous studies

Ethnic background may play a role in MEHP% variability. For example, in the US postmenopausal breast cancer study (10), the geometric mean of total DEHP metabolites (MEHP, MEHHP, MEOHP, and MECPP) was 117.17 μg/g creatinine among controls, compared to 111.90 μg/g creatinine in this Taiwan study (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Notably, the average urinary MEHP level was lower in the United States than in Taiwan (3.40 vs. 5.88 μg/g creatinine), resulting in a lower MEHP% in the United States (2.9%) compared to Taiwan (5.3%). A higher MEHP% corresponds to lower levels of MEHHP%, MEOHP%, or MECPP%. Across different ethnic groups in the US study (11)—including Whites, Japanese Americans, Native Hawaiians, African Americans, and Latinos—as well as Taiwanese (6.4 μg/g creatinine) in this study, Whites had the highest urinary MEHP levels (7.5), followed by Latinos (7.2), Japanese Americans (7.2), Native Hawaiians (6.2), and African Americans (5.1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Menopausal status also plays a role in MEHP% variability. In this study, postmenopausal participants exhibited lower MEHP% than premenopausal participants—5.93% vs. 8.72% among cases and 5.52% vs. 7.73% among controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Notably, approximately 80% of participants in the multiethnic US study were postmenopausal, which correlates with lower MEHP%. Ethnic differences in DEHP metabolism, together with variations in follow-up duration, menopausal status distribution, and the levels and variability of exposure biomarkers, may contribute to the observed differences in the association between DEHP exposure and breast cancer risk. Ethnic differences in DEHP metabolism, combined with menopausal status, may explain variations in the association between DEHP exposure and breast cancer risk. These differences in MEHP% and the proportion of postmenopausal women across studies may help account for inconsistencies in findings regarding DEHP-related breast cancer risk.

In addition to DEHP and MEHP%, other factors may contribute to the inconsistencies observed across prospective studies. These include age at exposure (premenopausal vs. postmenopausal), cumulative exposure to multiple phthalates, the interval between phthalate exposure and breast cancer onset, and genetic susceptibility. In our study, the combination of high ∑DEHP, high MEHP%, and early menarche age was associated with the highest risk of breast cancer. Puberty is a recognized “window of vulnerability” for breast tissue, as the rapidly expanding ductal tree is especially sensitive to environmental insults. Notably, DEHP itself can influence pubertal timing. For example, a recent rat study demonstrated that prenatal DEHP exposure accelerated pubertal onset in female offspring via alterations in hypothalamic ERα-mediated signaling pathways (24). Thus, the convergence of an extended estrogen-replete period and DEHP-induced endocrine disruption may synergistically drive proliferative and epigenetic changes in mammary progenitor cells, ultimately amplifying breast cancer risk.

This study found that higher urinary MEHP levels were significantly associated with increased breast cancer risk in a dose–response manner, an association not observed for other DEHP metabolites. A dose–response relationship may arise when 1) the toxicant follows a linear nonthreshold effect, or 2) exposure levels fall within the range where linear effects occur. As DEHP exposure levels varied across populations (SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3), the dose–response relationship between DEHP and breast cancer risk may only be detectable in populations with a wide range of exposures. A significantly elevated adjusted OR of 2.68 was observed for participants with both high DEHP exposure and high MEHP%, suggesting that DEHP-related breast cancer risk is influenced by metabolic susceptibility, as indicated by MEHP%. Since MEHP is the primary metabolite, a high MEHP% may result in lower urinary levels of secondary or tertiary DEHP metabolites, potentially explaining the lack of dose–response relationships for these metabolites.

The IARC working group has also raised concerns about measuring phthalate metabolites from a single urine sample, as it may not accurately reflect long-term exposure (25). In this study, we analyzed phthalate metabolite levels in paired urine samples collected at study entry and follow-up visits, with an average interval of 1.46 y. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for urinary DEHP metabolite levels were low (ranging from 0.00 to 0.09), whereas the ICCs for the relative proportions of specific DEHP metabolites within total DEHP metabolites were slightly higher (ranging from 0.08 to 0.31). These findings suggest that DEHP metabolic susceptibility, as indicated by MEHP%, appears to be more stable over time than absolute DEHP exposure levels. The temporal variability in both DEHP exposure and metabolism indicates that odds ratio (OR) estimates based on a single urine sample may be biased toward the null. However, it should also be recognized that high intraindividual variability could lead to spurious associations or even associations in the opposite direction. In addition, some phthalate metabolites (MEHP, MiBP, and MBzP) showed coefficients of variation (CVs) exceeding 20% (26.8%, 23.4%, and 22.0%, respectively). Because the laboratory analyses were blinded to disease status, this variability likely resulted in nondifferential misclassification between case and control groups, which would bias results toward the null and increase the probability of false-negative findings (Type II error). Despite this conservative bias, significant associations were still observed, providing further confidence that the associations are not solely due to measurement error.

Despite these challenges, the observation that high total DEHP metabolites and high MEHP% were significantly associated with breast cancer risk in this study provides more confidence that the findings are unlikely to be due solely to exposure misclassification. However, this study has some limitations. The small number of breast cancer cases and controls limited the precision of OR estimates in certain strata. To improve estimate accuracy, we used the median values from the control group to dichotomize exposure levels and the proportion of specific DEHP metabolites. Additionally, concerns regarding multiple testing were addressed through stratified analyses, which consistently showed similar adjusted ORs across different strata of various characteristics (Fig. 1) and urinary levels of other phthalates (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Our findings suggest that DEHP metabolic susceptibility may influence breast cancer risk associated with elevated DEHP exposure. The intraclass correlation coefficient, accounting for within-individual variation across repeated measurements, indicates that MEHP% is a more stable biomarker than absolute urinary MEHP levels. Incorporating MEHP% into breast cancer risk assessments is crucial, particularly in diverse ethnic populations. These individual differences in DEHP metabolism highlight the need to consider metabolic variations in environmental health studies. Including MEHP% in risk assessments can facilitate the development of personalized prevention strategies and targeted monitoring. These results have significant implications for public health surveillance and risk stratification, helping identify individuals with higher susceptibility to DEHP-related breast cancer and enabling more effective prevention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort.

This nested case–control study was derived from a cohort of community-based cancer screening program (CBCSP) which enrolled participants aged 30 to 65 y old from seven townships in Taiwan. A total of 11,923 female participants were recruited in 1991 to 1992. The recruitment, methods of screening, and follow-up examination have been described in detail previously (26). At baseline recruitment, well-trained study nurses administered a structured questionnaire to participants, who gave informed consent for the interview and biospecimen collection. Information collected by questionnaire interview included sociodemographic characteristics, cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption habits, personal and family history of cancer, age at menarche and menopause, parity, oral contraceptive use, and body mass index (BMI). Urine specimens were obtained and frozen at −30 °C until analysis.

Ascertainment of Breast Cancer Cases.

The newly diagnosed breast cancer was ascertained through computerized linkages with the National Cancer Registry database in Taiwan (27, 28). There were 250 pathologically confirmed patients with primary invasive breast carcinoma until 2010. Ten cases with a history of oophorectomy were excluded from this analysis (29, 30). A total of 126 cancer cases with urine samples at study entry were randomly selected. Two female controls without a history of oophorectomy were matched to each case on age at enrollment (±3 y), residential township, date of urine sample collection (±3 mo), and total years of endogenous estrogen exposure. Total years of endogenous estrogen exposure were derived by calculating the time between menarche and menopause or 50 y of age, whichever came first, and then subtracting the number of parity × 0.833 (10/12) years (16). All controls were free of breast cancer when their matched cases were diagnosed. Finally, 119 breast cancer cases and 245 matched controls had adequate spot urine samples at study entry for measuring 11 metabolites of seven phthalates. In addition, follow-up spot urine samples from 152 participants, collected after an average interval of 1.46 y, enabled assessment of within-person reproducibility of urinary DEHP metabolite levels over time. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Taiwan University College of Public Health and by the National Health Research Institutes (NHRI).

Measurement of Urinary Phthalate Metabolite Concentrations.

Urinary concentrations of 11 phthalate metabolites from seven phthalate acid esters (SI Appendix, Table S1) were measured using solid phase extraction coupled with liquid chromatography/electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry at the organic analytical laboratory of the NHRI, according to the published methodology (31–33). They included mono-methyl phthalate (MMP), mono-ethyl phthalate (MEP), mono-isobutyl phthalate (MiBP), mono-n-butyl phthalate (MnBP), mono-benzyl phthalate (MBzP), and mono-isononyl phthalate (MiNP), which were primary metabolites of di-methyl phthalate (DMP), di-ethyl phthalate (DEP), di-isobutyl phthalate (DiBP), di-n-butyl phthalate (DnBP), BBzP, and di-isononyl phthalate (DiNP), respectively. DEHP in humans is first metabolized into MEHP (primary metabolite) and further metabolized into mono-(2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl) phthalate (MEHHP), mono-(2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate (MEOHP), mono-(2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl) phthalate (MECPP), and mono-(2-carboxymethylhexyl) phthalate (MCMHP) (31). Five metabolites from DEHP, including MEHP, MEHHP, MEOHP, MECPP, and MCMHP, were measured. The detection limit was 0.12 ng/mL for MMP, MEP, MnBP, MBzP, and MEHHP; 0.24 ng/mL for MEHP, MEOHP, and MiBP; 0.048 ng/mL for MECPP and MCMHP, and 1.2 ng/mL for MiNP. For measurements below the limit of detection (LOD), values were derived from one-half of LOD (34, 35). Urinary creatinine concentration (mg/dL) was measured using the spectrophotometric method with picric acid as a reagent. For monitoring quality control, aliquot from a pooled urine was used in each batch of assay to estimate coefficients of variance (CV): MMP 19%, MEP 19%, MiBP 23.4%, MBnP 18.0%, MBzP 22.0%, MEHP 26.8%, MEHHP 18.4%, MEOHP 11.1%, MECPP 11.3%, and MCMHP 13.0%.

Urinary phthalate metabolite concentrations were converted from “µg/g creatinine” to “µmol/g creatinine” to express the data on a molar basis. The sum of the five measured DEHP metabolites was presented as ∑DEHP metabolites. The MEHP percentage (MEHP%), defined as the percentage of MEHP in the ∑DEHP metabolites, was derived to reflect the primary DEHP metabolic susceptibility of each participant. The ∑LMW was derived as the sum of MMP, MEP, and MiBP in molecular base. The ∑HMW was derived as the sum of MnBP, MBzP, and ∑DEHP in molecular base. The ∑PHTHA was derived as the sum of ∑LMW and ∑HMW.

Statistical Analysis.

As there was a skewed distribution for urinary levels of phthalate metabolites, geometric means and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed to compare the levels between cases and controls. The dose–response relations between breast cancer risk and of urinary level of phthalate metabolites (in tertiles and quintiles) were first assessed. Tests for linear trend were conducted by including the categorical exposure variable as an ordinal variable (coded 1 to 3 for tertiles or 1 to 5 for quintiles) in the logistic regression models. The odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI associated with breast cancer risk were estimated for dichotomized urinary levels of phthalate metabolites and percentages of specific DEHP metabolites using the median in controls as the cutoff value. All risk predictors significantly (P value < 0.05) associated with breast cancer in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate model using conditional logistic regression analysis. To examine the consistency of the ORs, stratification analyses by age at enrollment, early menarche, years of estrogen exposure, body mass index (BMI), menopausal status, age at diagnosis, and urinary levels of other phthalate metabolites were further carried out using unconditional logistic regression with adjustment for menarche age and matching variables. The intraclass correlation coefficients and their 95% CI were calculated from 152 pair-wise data of phthalate metabolites and percentages of specific DEHP metabolites (36). Statistical significance was determined by two-tailed tests (P < 0.05). All analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System Software (V.9.4; SAS Institute Inc., NC, USA).
