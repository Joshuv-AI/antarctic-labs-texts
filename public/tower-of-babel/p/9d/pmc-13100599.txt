From Trousseau to artificial intelligence: The future of occult cancer detection in unprovoked venous thromboembolism
Authors: Ioannis Tsouknidas, Arjun Jayaraj
Published: 2026

In their publication, Wen et al1 provide a timely and comprehensive review of the literature on the association between unprovoked venous thromboembolism (VTE) and occult malignancy, with a focus on epidemiology, pathophysiological mechanisms, and contemporary screening approaches. The authors highlight several clinical variables, including age, diabetes mellitus, diverticular disease, dementia, aspirin use, recurrent thrombosis, and thrombosis in unusual sites, that may influence the likelihood of identifying an underlying cancer. Given the limited strength of available evidence, they ultimately support current guideline recommendations, which endorse routine cancer screening in patients presenting with unprovoked VTE.1

The conceptual link between thrombosis and cancer dates back to the 19th century, when Armand Trousseau first described this correlation,2 now known as Trousseau's syndrome. This early observation established the premise that a thrombotic event may represent the first clinical manifestation of an otherwise occult neoplasm.3 Since that time, substantial research has sought to elucidate the complex biological interactions between tumor cells, coagulation pathways, and host inflammatory responses.

Despite these advancements, a critical clinical question persists: Which patients with unprovoked VTE are at sufficiently elevated risk to warrant more intensive cancer evaluation? The overall incidence of occult malignancy discovered after an unprovoked VTE remains relatively low, at approximately 5%.4 Accordingly, current guidance from the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis recommends a limited screening strategy combined with routine, age- and sex-appropriate cancer screenings.5 This screening typically includes a thorough medical history, physical examination, complete blood count, calcium level, urinalysis, liver function tests, and chest radiography. Given the low baseline incidence and uncertain cost effectiveness of extensive diagnostic workups, more aggressive screening is not endorsed routinely.5

However, the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis does acknowledge clinical scenarios in which a lower threshold for additional cancer evaluation may be justified. These include patients with recurrent unprovoked VTE, as well as individuals with VTE in unusual sites, in whom focused testing for conditions such as myeloproliferative neoplasms or paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria may be appropriate, depending on anatomical location.5

International guidelines have evolved over time. The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence originally recommended extensive cancer screening in adults aged >40 years with unprovoked VTE (2012).6 This recommendation was revised in 2020,7 favoring only routine screening, after evidence from a 2017 Cochrane review by Robertson et al.8 The updated 2021 Cochrane analysis similarly concluded that, although enhanced testing may identify cancers at earlier stages, current evidence remains insufficient to determine whether such strategies reduce cancer- or VTE-related morbidity or mortality.9 Larger, methodologically robust randomized trials are needed before definitive recommendations can be made.

Efforts to develop predictive tools have intensified, particularly with the emergence of advanced computational methods. Traditional models such as the RIETE and SOME scores have demonstrated limited performance.10, 11, 12 A recent study by Al Raizah et al13 introduced the Riyadh Score, a simple two-variable model using age and hemoglobin, which outperformed both RIETE and SOME in predicting a cancer diagnosis within 12 months.

Several promising avenues for future research are currently in progress. Ongoing investigations include the MVTEP/SOME2 trial (NCT04304651), a French-Canadian study evaluating whether the addition of fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission with computed tomography improves detection of previously missed cancers over a 1-year follow-up period in higher-risk patients (aged ≥50 years) with a first unprovoked VTE. Additional studies include a RIETE-derived risk score investigation (NCT03907583), as well as a prospective cohort evaluating platelet RNA profiling as a novel biomarker strategy for identifying occult malignancy in patients with unprovoked VTE (NCT02739867).

Emerging technologies may further refine cancer prediction. Artificial intelligence models have shown encouraging results in diagnostic and prognostic applications for VTE, although a high risk of bias and limited external validation currently restrict clinical adoption. Achour et al14 recently proposed an artificial intelligence-based model for predicting VTE etiology, and the PLATO-VTE study by Guman et al15 identified 10 differentially abundant plasma proteins in patients with occult cancer, suggesting potential for proteomic biomarkers in future screening paradigms.

The landscape of occult cancer detection in unprovoked VTE is evolving rapidly. Advances in biomarkers, predictive analytics, and targeted imaging hold significant promise for improving risk stratification and guiding more precise screening strategies. As emerging diagnostic tools mature and ongoing trials yield new evidence, a more individualized, patient-specific approach may soon replace the broad, uniform strategies currently in use.
