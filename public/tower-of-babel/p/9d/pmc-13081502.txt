From image to clinical judgment: a randomized near-peer teaching trial on systematic chest radiograph interpretation
Authors: Andres Felipe Yepes-Velasco, Andres Solano, Jorge Hernandez, Andrés Felipe Mora-Salamanca, Jeimy Lorena Moreno-Araque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChest radiograph (CXR) interpretation remains a fundamental yet challenging skill for undergraduate medical students, requiring both perceptual and integrative reasoning. Near-peer instruction and structured checklists have been proposed to enhance learning, but evidence from randomized designs is limited. MethodsWe conducted a randomized educational trial among final-year medical students (n = 50). All participants attended a two-hour lecture on CXR interpretation delivered by intensive care medicine residents. The intervention group received additional training using a systematic checklist for image analysis. Performance was assessed through a 16-item test (interpretation and diagnosis domains). Nonparametric tests and Spearman correlations were applied; internal consistency and item discrimination were analyzed.ResultsCompared with controls, the intervention group achieved higher median scores in interpretation (6 vs. 3), diagnosis (4 vs. 3), and total score (10 vs. 6) (all p < 0.05). Interpretation and diagnosis scores were not correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.12, p = 0.40). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.592, with 56% of items showing excellent discrimination.ConclusionsA brief, resident-led, checklist-guided session significantly improved CXR interpretation and overall performance. Structuring perceptual search through systematic frameworks can rapidly strengthen diagnostic foundations and represents a feasible, low-cost addition to medical curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08971-9.Keywords: Near-peer teaching, Peer-assisted learning, Chest radiograph interpretation, Medical education, Randomized controlled trial, Checklist-based instruction, Diagnostic reasoning, Intensive care, Item analysis, Undergraduate medical students

Introduction

Chest radiography remains a cornerstone bedside investigation in acute and critical care, supporting rapid triage and management decisions. It is one of the most frequently used diagnostic tools in clinical practice—particularly in hospital and critical care settings—owing to its availability, low cost, and utility in guiding medical decisions [1]. However, despite its widespread use, proper interpretation of CXRs continues to face significant limitations. The superimposition of anatomical structures, variability in technical quality of projections, and, most importantly, the high rate of perceptual errors make their interpretation a challenge both for trainees and for non-radiologist specialists [2, 3]. For undergraduate learners, CXR interpretation is challenging: it demands coordinated visual search, pattern recognition, and clinical integration—skills that are rarely taught in an explicit, stepwise manner. Contemporary medical education has increasingly turned to scalable instructional models that enhance learner engagement while reducing reliance on scarce expert faculty time. One such approach is near-peer learning (NPL), in which learners slightly more advanced within the same training pathway teach junior learners [4].

Near-peer instruction (NPL) and peer-assisted learning (PAL) have been widely implemented across undergraduate and clinical training because near-peers can be effective teachers while remaining logistically scalable [5–7]. Proposed mechanisms include cognitive and social congruence—near-peers often share a similar knowledge structure and learning language with junior learners, potentially lowering intimidation and increasing clarification and feedback [8, 9]. Across medical education, syntheses consistently report positive effects on learning outcomes and acceptability for both learners and student-teachers, with evidence spanning clinical skills and knowledge acquisition. In diagnostic imaging education, peer-assisted approaches have also shown comparable knowledge gains to expert-led instruction in chest radiograph interpretation in a randomized trial, supporting feasibility beyond traditional bedside or procedural domains [10].

Checklist-guided reading offers structured scaffolding that may reduce omissions and standardize performance [11]. Randomized evidence supporting structured approaches to chest radiograph (CXR) teaching exists but remains limited and heterogeneous. Across randomized trials, checklist-guided reading has been associated with improved abnormality detection [12], peer-assisted facilitation has produced knowledge gains comparable to expert-assisted approaches in virtual instruction [10], and behavioral fluency training has yielded substantial improvements in interpretation accuracy with retention [13]. Despite these trials, important gaps remain in undergraduate clinical training contexts. Prior randomized studies have not simultaneously evaluated outcomes across distinct domains—perceptual interpretation and diagnostic reasoning—nor reported item-level measurement properties to support replication and refinement. These limitations provide the rationale for the present randomized trial.

This study addresses those gaps by testing a brief, resident-led curriculum that teaches a structured CXR reading tool to final-year medical students, compared with lecture-only instruction. Primary and secondary outcomes were defined a priori before data collection and analysis. We also examined associations among domains and reported internal consistency and item-level discrimination to inform future refinement.

Methods

Study design

This was a randomized controlled educational trial with parallel intervention and control groups conducted between September 2024 and April 2025. Final-year medical students were included. Participants attended a two-hour lecture delivered by two intensive care medicine residents (third- and fourth-year). The lecture focused on the radiographic interpretation of common findings in chest x-rays of critically ill patients, covering anatomical structures, normal reference values, and pathological correlations.

After the lecture, students were randomly allocated (simple randomization) into two groups: intervention and control. In addition to the lecture, the intervention group received training in the use of a systematic assessment tool for chest x-ray interpretation. Finally, both groups completed an online questionnaire.

Educational components (control and intervention)

The base training consisted of a two-hour, lecture-only session on chest radiograph (CXR) interpretation delivered as part of an existing undergraduate teaching curriculum. Content and structure were derived from the training intensive care residents receive during their one-month radiology rotation in postgraduate year 2, and was adapted for final-year medical students. After this base session, participants were randomized to control or intervention.

The control group received no additional instruction beyond the initial two-hour lecture and proceeded to the study assessment. The intervention group received an additional one-hour, lecture-only session introducing a systematic CXR interpretation checklist. No guided practice or supervised hands-on application was included in either arm.

Systematic CXR assessment tool

The checklist was developed using the ABCDE mnemonic for systematic CXR interpretation, as previously described in the literature [14, 15]. The final checklist used in the intervention is provided in full in (Supplementary Material S1).

Assessment development and format

The assessment comprised 16 single-best-answer multiple-choice questions (MCQs) based on eight chest radiographs, with each radiograph paired to one interpretation item and one diagnosis item. Interpretation items assessed perceptual description by asking learners to select the statement most consistent with a radiologist-style report, whereas diagnosis items assessed clinical identification of the underlying problem depicted.

Images and items were developed by the study team to reflect ICU-relevant radiographic findings and were reviewed for clarity, single-best-answer structure, and alignment with the intended domain (interpretation vs. diagnosis). An answer key was defined a priori using the reference standard for each case (radiology report). No formal pre-study psychometric validation was undertaken; however, we report post-administration internal structure and item performance to inform refinement and future reuse, consistent with modern validity frameworks emphasizing evidence from content and internal structure.

Study sample

A formal sample size calculation was not performed. However, based on the work of Kok and colleagues, at least 40 participants were required [12]. Students were recruited during two sessions, the first in September 2024 and the second in April 2025.

Eligible participants were final-year medical students from Universidad de los Andes (Bogotá, Colombia). In Colombia, medical training lasts six to seven years depending on the institution. The final year corresponds to a clinical clerkship, during which students gain supervised experience in various medical specialties. All final-year students had prior curricular exposure to chest radiography interpretation as part of their undergraduate training. Inclusion criteria were attendance at the teaching session and provision of oral informed consent. Students with prior advanced training or experience in radiology were excluded.

Questionnaire analysis

Sixteen questions were developed based on eight chest x-rays and divided into two categories: interpretation and diagnosis. Interpretation questions assessed the ability of students to analyze an image and select the description most consistent with a radiologist’s report. Diagnosis questions evaluated the ability to identify the clinical problem depicted. Each chest x-ray was paired with one interpretation and one diagnostic question. (Supplemental Material 2).

The control group completed the same questionnaire without access to the systematic assessment tool. Responses were collected using the institutional REDCap platform (https://project-redcap.org/). Each response was scored as correct (1 point) or incorrect (0 points). For each student, an interpretation score, a diagnosis score, and a total score were calculated. The interpretation and diagnosis scores ranged from 0 to 8, and the total score ranged from 0 to 16.

Statistical analysis

Score distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and were found to be non-normal; therefore, results are reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Group differences were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine associations between interpretation, diagnosis, and total scores. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3. (https://www.r-project.org/).

Results

A total of 50 students were included in the study. The interpretation, diagnosis and total scores exhibited non-normal distributions, as indicated by the Shapiro–Wilk test (p-value < 0.05). In the control group, the median interpretation and diagnosis scores were 3 points (IQR: 3–4 and 2–4, respectively). In contrast, the intervention group achieved higher median scores, with 6 points for interpretation (IQR: 6–7) and 4 points for diagnosis (IQR: 3–6). Regarding the total score, the control group obtained a median of 6 points (IQR: 5–7.25), while the intervention group reached a median of 10 points (IQR: 8.25–12.75).

Overall, the intervention group outperformed the control group across all assessed domains (Fig. 1), and these differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Spearman correlation analysis showed no association between interpretation and diagnosis scores across all participants (ρ = 0.12, p = 0.40) (Supplemental Material).

The internal consistency of the test was weak (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.592, Interpretation α = 0.61 Diagnosis α = 0.53). The item difficulty index (p), defined as the proportion of students answering correctly, of 11 (68.8%) questions was acceptable (p = 30–70%), three (18.8%) questions were easy (p > 70%), and two (12.5%) questions were difficult (p < 30%). The discrimination index revealed that eight questions had an excellent performance (D ≥ 0.40), one question had an acceptable performance (D = 0.30–0.39), four questions had a marginal performance (D = 0.20–0.29) and three questions had a poor discrimination performance (D < 0.20). Regarding the Point-Biserial correlation, four questions (rpb ≥ 0.40) were categorized as excellent, one as good (rpb = 0.30–0.39), five as acceptable (rpb = 0.20–0.29), one as weak (rpb = 0.10–0.19), three as very weak (rpb = 0.00–0.09) and two as flawed (rpb < 0.00) (Supplemental Material 3, Table S3.1.).

Discussion

In this randomized educational study, we found significant improvement in interpretation and total score following a near-peer training session led by critical care and intensive care medicine residents using a checklist-guided approach. The intervention, focused on teaching a structured scheme for chest radiograph (CXR) reading, produced clear learning gains in final-year medical students. After a common lecture, the group exposed to the checklist-guided session outperformed the control group in interpretation, diagnosis, and total score, with statistically significant overall differences and the greatest advantage in interpretation. These results indicate that combining a near-peer format with a systematic tool offers measurable benefits in real teaching contexts, with high feasibility and low cost, justifying its curricular adoption.

Peer teaching and peer-assisted learning (PAL) have been widely implemented in medical education across a range of skills and knowledge domains [5, 16]. They have been used to teach and review clinical skills (including history-taking and physical examination), procedural skills, and small-group learning for knowledge acquisition and consolidation [6, 17, 18]. Proposed mechanisms include cognitive and social congruence; near-peers often share a similar knowledge structure and learning language with junior learners, which may facilitate clarification, feedback, and psychological safety [8, 19]. It has also been shown that serving as a peer teacher can strengthen confidence and self-efficacy in both clinical and teaching skills, while supporting professional identity development and role-modelling [20]. Overall, the evidence suggests that peer teaching can produce short-term learning outcomes comparable to faculty-led instruction, making it a valuable and cost-effective approach [5]. A practical strength of this intervention is its feasibility: it was delivered by intensive care medicine residents who had completed a dedicated one-month radiology rotation during PGY-2 and who routinely interpret ICU-relevant CXRs in clinical training. This level of near-peer expertise is likely important for content accuracy and instructional credibility.

A second key component of the intervention was the reading checklist, which provides clear scaffolding to organize visual search and reduce omissions in those beginning CXR interpretation. Although it may initially be perceived as more time-demanding, its use promotes systematic detection of findings and standardizes the image review process [21]. This behavior explains why the greatest impact was concentrated in the perceptual/interpretive component: by organizing the exploration and reducing oversights, students recognize more relevant findings. In the same direction, consistent benefits of checklist use in novices have been reported, and improvements in finding identification have been documented when comparing structured versus unstructured reading [12]. Thus, the increase observed in interpretation in our study is consistent with this general pattern.

The interpretation–diagnosis asymmetry we found, with interpretation improving more than diagnosis, is also expected in light of the literature on the development of radiological expertise. Studies using eye-tracking and performance analysis show that “expert” search patterns and other perceptual metrics emerge before fully “expert” diagnostic accuracy [22]. In educational terms, efficient visual exploration routines are consolidated first, followed by the clinical-radiological integration that supports diagnostic judgment. This pattern is consistent with our finding that interpretation and diagnosis scores were not correlated, suggesting that perceptual interpretation and diagnostic reasoning may be partly independent in this context. In other words, improving structured perceptual search may not automatically translate into diagnostic judgment unless learners are explicitly supported to connect radiographic features with clinical inference [23, 24]. Our data therefore reinforce recommendations to combine perceptual scaffolding (checklists, reading sequences) with integration tasks (clinical vignettes, decision algorithms, immediate feedback).

Regarding instructional design, the evidence supports that active and structured approaches—including digital resources and blended learning—outperform passive modalities for interpretation skills and applied knowledge [25, 26]. Furthermore, the use of flipped/asynchronous resources to optimize face-to-face time in case resolution could close the gap toward diagnosis that we observed [27]. Integrating these strategies in a second layer (brief cases anchored to each image, spaced practice) appears to be the logical next step to maximize transfer from perception to clinical decision-making.

Although the overall internal consistency was weak, this value is understandable in instruments that measure related but non-redundant constructs (interpretation and diagnosis) and is consistent with evidence recommending a focus on item analysis (discrimination, distractors) and reporting reliability by subscales rather than seeking a single elevated alpha [28]. Several limitations qualify the inferences. The trial did not include a formal a priori sample size calculation and was based on a pragmatic threshold, which restricts precision for domain-specific effects—particularly diagnosis. In addition, the study was conducted at a single institution within one national training context, which may limit generalizability to programs with different curricula, baseline exposure to imaging, or instructional resources. The assessment was short-term; retention and transfer to real clinical performance remain to be established. In addition, possible baseline covariates (e.g., recent clinical exposure) were not documented, leaving open the possibility of residual confounding. Blinding was not feasible for this educational intervention, and the instructors were part of the study team; despite standardized materials, subtle differences in delivery or instructor enthusiasm may have introduced performance bias. Our primary outcome was measured using an MCQ format, which may introduce cueing and does not fully mirror authentic CXR interpretation, where clinicians must generate findings and impressions without response options. This represents partial misalignment between the desired performance task and the assessment format. We selected MCQs to enable standardized scoring and feasible administration in a randomized classroom-based study; future work should evaluate retention and performance using open-response or structured reporting formats that better approximate real-world interpretation.
