Treatment of the Ectopic Eruption of the Mandibular First Permanent Molar With D‐Loop: A Case Report
Authors: Qiannan Deng, Zhengyuan Chen, Yao Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly diagnosis and timely intervention in ectopic eruption of permanent first molars can prevent premature loss of second primary molars. The novel spring named “D‐loop” offers a simple, lab‐free, and effective method for molar distalization, preserving arch integrity even in cases with significant root resorption.

Introduction

Ectopic eruption of the permanent first molars (PFMs) is defined as a localized disturbance in the eruption pathway, where the tooth is positioned too mesially against the distal surface of the second primary molar and fails to erupt into its normal occlusal position during mixed dentition development [1, 2, 3]. PFMs represent one of the most common pathological conditions encountered during this period and may lead to hypomineralization of PFMs, early root resorption of the second primary molar, reduction in the dental arch perimeter, impaction of the permanent second premolars, and pain due to pulp exposure [4]. Effective clinical management of ectopic PFMs presents a significant challenge for pediatric dentists and is crucial for the proper development of healthy permanent dentition and long‐term occlusal stability.

The prevalence of ectopic PFMs ranges from 2% to 6% in growing children, with a male‐to‐female ratio of 1.4:1. Most cases occur in the maxilla, while only 8.8%–21.7% are observed in the mandible [5]. The prognosis of ectopic eruption is classified as either reversible or irreversible, depending on factors such as eruption angulation, severity of impaction, degree of root resorption in the second primary molar, and bilaterality [6, 7, 8]. Consequently, orthodontic treatment planning should account for the potential of spontaneous correction.

Various treatment options have been reported for managing ectopic PFMs, including elastic separators, interproximal wedging, and distal tipping [3, 9]. Each modality has specific indications and limitations. Among them, the Halterman appliance offers notable advantages due to its simple fabrication, minimal bulk, and versatility for use in both the maxilla and mandible. Additionally, it can be adjusted directly in the child's mouth using Hoe or bird‐beak pliers [10].

Over the years, several modifications to the appliance have been introduced. However, a key drawback remains its dependence on complex laboratory procedures and the frequent replacement of elastics. In this report, we introduce a novel approach using a self‐fabricated uprighting spring, termed the “D‐loop,” which provides an adjustable force to the second primary molar. This design reduces anchorage demand and improves orthodontic efficiency. The appliance is straightforward in construction and can be easily attached to standard components, such as molar tubes or bands, thereby broadening its clinical utility and improving cost‐effectiveness.

This case report illustrates the successful use of an uprighting spring to correct severe bilateral ectopic eruption of the mandibular first molars and underscores its potential as a versatile tool for interceptive clinical management of molar distal uprighting.

Case Examination

A 7‐year‐old girl was referred to our hospital due to abnormal eruption of the bilateral mandibular first permanent molars. She had no systemic diseases and no relevant family history. Figure 1 shows the patient's clinical presentation before treatment.

(J) Reference lines and points used in panoramic radiograph: (a) Occlusal plane; (b) Long axis of the mandibular permanent first molar; (c) Eruption angulation; (d) Magnitude of impaction; (e) Crown width of the second primary molar.

Clinically, the mesial surface of tooth #36 was impacted against the distal cervical region of tooth #75, and tooth #46 had not yet erupted. There was no increased mobility of the bilateral primary second molars. Panoramic radiography revealed severe resorption of both mandibular primary second molars, classified as “very severe,” affecting the mesial roots of both teeth [5].

The patient exhibited a Class I skeletal pattern with a straight facial profile and a normal incisor relationship.

Figure 1 illustrates the reference lines and points used for measurement. The occlusal plane (a) was defined as the average plane formed by the occlusal contacts of the posterior teeth. The long axis of the mandibular PFM (b) was defined as a line extending from the midpoint of the crown to the furcation. The eruption angulation (c) was measured as the mesial angle between the long axis of the PFM and the occlusal plane. The magnitude of impaction (d) was defined as the distance from the mesial maximum convexity of the PFM to a tangent drawn along the occlusal plane. Finally, the crown width of the second primary molar (e) was defined as the distance between its greatest mesiodistal convexities.

As described by Chen et al. [7], the Magnitude of Impaction Index (MOII) was used to reduce distortion effects in panoramic radiographs and incorporate crown width into clinical assessment. The MOII is calculated using the formula: MOII = d/e.

Diagnosis and Treatment

Based on the measurements result, both mandibular PFMs were classified as irreversible cases. Given the patient's straight facial profile and the absence of third molars, the orthodontic treatment objectives were as follows: (1) to upright both mandibular first permanent molars, and (2) to maintain the space for tooth #45 until implant placement could be completed.

The case was managed by distalizing the ectopically erupting molars using a modified Halterman appliance, referred to as the D‐loop (Figure 2).

Details of a D‐loop. (A–D) A D‐loop combined with convertible bands with/without E‐chain. (E) The mechanism of the D‐loop corrected the ectopically erupting permanent first molar. CR, center of resistance. d: Perpendicular distance between the center of resistance and the line of force application. F: Distalizing force.

At the start of the treatment, the impacted and ectopically erupting right mandibular first permanent molar was surgically exposed. A lingual button was bonded to both mandibular first permanent molars to aid occlusion. A 0.190 × 0.025‐in. stainless steel wire was pre‐bent into a D‐loop shape prior to appliance placement (Figure 2). After proper band adaptation, the D‐loop was attached and adjusted to follow the contour of the patient's buccal alveolar ridge, aligning with the occlusal button.

The D‐loop, combined with convertible bands, was bonded to the second primary molars using glass ionomer cement (3 M). An E‐chain was looped over the occlusal button, passed through the D‐loop, and attached to the buccal tube of the second primary molars, delivering approximately 100 g of force per side (Figure 3). Monthly follow‐up visits were scheduled, during which the patient's oral hygiene status was also monitored.

Treatment progress. (A) D‐loop was activated for molar uprighting. (B) 2‐month follow‐up result. (C) 4‐month follow‐up result. (D) 12‐month follow‐up result.

After 2 months of treatment, the mesial ridge of tooth #36 had emerged, and tooth #75 remained stable with no increase in mobility. Therefore, the uprighting appliance on the left side was removed entirely. An elastic separator was placed to promote the spontaneous eruption of the first molars. Simultaneously, the “Ω” loop on the right side was activated, and a new E‐Chain was placed monthly to continue distalizing tooth #46.

After 4 months of treatment, the mesial ridge of tooth #46 became visible, and slight mobility was noted in tooth #85. Only the distal tipping loop was removed at that point. One month later, tooth #46 had reached the occlusal plane, and the mobility of tooth #85 had decreased. All appliances were removed, and the patient was scheduled for follow‐up every 6 months.

Discussion

A smooth transition from primary to permanent dentition is essential for the development of normal occlusion. However, ectopically erupting permanent molars are often asymptomatic and may be overlooked by clinicians. A review of the medical histories of patients with PFMs revealed that only 9.29% were diagnosed promptly. Similarly, Mooney et al. [11] reported a diagnosis rate of 35.7%, with most cases identified by general clinicians or pediatric dentists between the ages of 5 and 7 years.

Recent studies with moderate‐level evidence suggest that 47%–78% of PFMs self‐correct by the age of 7 years [7, 8]. Therefore, early identification of irreversible ectopic eruptions is critical to guide appropriate individualized treatment and reduce the risk of overtreatment. Key indicators of irreversible cases include severe atypical distal root resorption of the second primary molar, a greater degree of impaction, a larger eruption angle, and bilateral occurrence [5, 6, 7, 8].

Atypical distal resorption of the second primary molar is a straightforward and clinically useful marker for prognosis. Resorption is classified into four grades: grades I–II (mild to moderate), where resorption occurs without pulp exposure, are associated with a 69.4% likelihood of spontaneous correction [5]. In contrast, grades III–IV (severe to very severe), which involve pulp exposure, typically require early orthodontic intervention to establish a normal eruptive pathway and preserve the deciduous molar [5]. In our case, both mandibular second primary molars showed atypical distal resorption with pulp exposure, and the left molar was also affected at the mesial root.

The MOI for maxillary first permanent molars was originally described by Barberia‐Leache et al. as the horizontal distance between the point of maximum convexity on the mesial contour of the permanent molar and a tangential plane perpendicular to the occlusal surface at the distal surface of the adjacent primary molar [5]. This measurement demonstrated high inter‐rater reliability and was positively associated with irreversible impaction [5]. However, the method does not account for the distortion inherent in panoramic radiographs.

To address this limitation, Chen et al. [7] introduced the MOII, which normalizes the impaction distance by dividing it by the crown width of the second primary molar. This index reflects the severity of root resorption and minimizes the impact of panoramic image distortion. Their findings indicated that PFMs with higher MOII values (0.282 ± 0.115) were more likely to be classified as irreversible.

Additionally, the mesial angulation of PFMs is significantly greater in irreversible cases compared to those that self‐correct. Mean eruption angulation values were 82.96 ± 10.00 and 95.16 ± 11.61 in irreversible cases [2]. In our patient, the MOII values were 0.25 (left) and 0.41 (right), with corresponding mesial angles of 111° (left) and 123° (right), indicating a strong likelihood of irreversible impaction. Therefore, early intervention was warranted to correct the eruptive pathway.

Treatment modalities for ectopically erupting permanent molars are generally classified into two categories: interproximal wedging, which is suitable for mild impactions, and distal tipping, which is used to reestablish the proper eruptive path in more severe cases (impaction > 2 mm). Various designs have been developed for distal tipping appliances.

One well‐known approach is the Halterman appliance, a traditional distal tipping method used to correct ectopic eruption of the PFM [12]. It typically consists of a band with a distal extension loop fabricated in a laboratory. The traction force originates from activated elastomeric chains, which rely on an adequate distance between the distal extension loop and an occlusal button bonded to the permanent molar. In more severe impactions (> 4 mm), the button may need to be repositioned mesially to increase the effective distance and improve the direction of distal traction.

Another distal tipping device is the K‐loop, composed of three main components: the active, retention, and action sections [13]. It generates force primarily through two closing loops that offer three‐dimensional control via wire bending. This compact design reduces the size of the posterior appliance and improves patient comfort. However, like other appliances, it has limitations. During Reynolds activation, the loop must be detached and re‐bonded, requiring additional chairside procedures.

Our D‐loop design integrates features of both the Halterman and K‐loop appliances. Constructed from 0.019 × 0.025‐in. stainless‐steel wire, it comprises three key components: retention, activation, and traction. The retention section anchors to the adjacent molar, allowing the appliance to be placed and activated in a single visit. The activation component includes an “Ω” loop, which increases total wire length and springiness, facilitating easier reactivation during follow‐up visits, particularly when the impaction exceeds 2 mm.

The primary distal tipping force is generated by an elastic chain stretched from the occlusal button, threaded through the distal traction loop, and secured to the buccal tube of the adjacent molar. This configuration exerts a low, controlled force, reducing the risk of adverse effects on anchoring teeth. Due to anatomical limitations in the posterior region, such as the presence of the Hauler notch, inserting the retention arm from the lingual or posterior side can be challenging. Therefore, in clinical practice, we recommend assembling the D‐loop extraorally and positioning it intraorally as a single unit.

Our patient also presented with a congenital absence of tooth #45. The reported prevalence of congenitally missing mandibular second premolars with retained second deciduous molars is approximately 2.0%–3.22% [14, 15]. Common treatment options for such cases include preservation of the deciduous tooth, orthodontic space closure, implant placement, or other prosthetic interventions [15, 16]. The prognosis of retained deciduous teeth is influenced by factors, including distance from the occlusal plane, a smaller Frankfort mandibular plane angle, larger adjacent interdental spaces, the presence of a deep overbite, and a history of caries treatment [15].

In the present case, the distal root of the mandibular second deciduous molar had already undergone resorption; however, the tooth was retained based on the parents' preference. A definitive treatment plan will be established following the completion of the mixed dentition stage.

Ectopically erupting PFMs during the mixed dentition stage should be diagnosed as early as possible. Increased severity of atypical distal resorption of the second primary molar, greater magnitude of impaction, larger eruption angulation, and bilateral occurrence are indicators of irreversible ectopic eruption.

In conclusion, the present case report introduces a novel uprighting spring, the “D‐loop,” which offers a simple, comfortable, and laboratory‐free solution for managing severe molar impactions. The D‐loop enables safe and effective correction, even in cases where the adjacent primary molar exhibits advanced root resorption.
