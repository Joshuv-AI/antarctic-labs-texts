Background

Papillary fibroelastomas are benign primary cardiac tumors that preferentially arise from the cardiac valves. Cardiac myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumor, and rarely originate from the valvular apparatus. Distinguishing papillary fibroelastoma from myxoma can be difficult based on intraoperative gross appearance and frozen section pathology. Because these two entities differ in terms of recurrence risk and surgical management, diagnostic uncertainty during surgery might influence decisions regarding the extent of resection.

Case presentation

An 81-year-old man was referred to our institution for evaluation of a cardiac mass incidentally detected during a routine medical checkup. He was asymptomatic at presentation. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a mobile mass ~ 10 mm in diameter, arising from the P1 segment of the posterior mitral leaflet (Fig. 1). Degenerative changes were not obvious in the other leaflet segments or the subvalvular apparatus.

(b) Three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography demonstrating the mass arising from the posterior mitral leaflet

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography confirmed a mass attached to the posterior mitral leaflet without evidence of embolic events or extracardiac malignancies. Laboratory tests revealed no signs of infection or systemic inflammation.

The tumor was relatively large and mobile, and surgical intervention was considered appropriate because of the potential risk of future embolic events. A minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS) approach via a right mini-thoracotomy was selected considering the advanced age of the patient and optimal exposure of the mitral valve.

Surgery proceeded via a right mini-thoracotomy through the fourth intercostal space with direct visualization and thoracoscopic assistance (Additional File 1: Video S1). We established cardiopulmonary bypass as follows. We cannulated the right axillary artery for arterial inflow to reduce the risk of cerebral embolism associated with abdominal aortic plaques and inserted a right femoral venous cannula for venous drainage. The aorta was cross-clamped, then cardiac arrest was induced using antegrade cardioplegia.

The mitral valve was exposed through a left atriotomy via the Sondergaard groove. A left atrial vent tube was inserted under direct visualization, taking care to avoid contact with a translucent, gelatinous, yellow mass attached to the P1 segment of the posterior mitral leaflet (Fig. 2a). A myxoma was suspected based on its gross appearance, and it was excised en bloc with a 1–2 mm margin that included surrounding leaflet tissues (Fig. 2b). A small leaflet defect was directly closed using 5 − 0 polypropylene sutures (Fig. 2c).

Intraoperative findings. (a) A translucent, gelatinous yellow mass attached to the P1 segment of the posterior mitral leaflet. Based on the gross appearance, a myxoma was suspected. (b) After tumor excision, intraoperative frozen section analysis suggested myxoma and the resection margin was confirmed to be negative. (c) The leaflet defect after tumor excision was small and closed directly using 5 − 0 polypropylene sutures. Post-repair transesophageal echocardiography after aortic unclamping demonstrated only trivial mitral regurgitation

A focal myxoid stroma in the intraoperative frozen section led to a diagnosis of myxoma (Fig. 3a, b). Negative resection margins were confirmed. However, the final histopathological examination revealed characteristic papillary fronds composed of a central fibroelastic core covered by endothelial cells, which formed a sea-anemone appearance (Fig. 3c, d). The operative time, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and aortic cross-clamp time were 225, 123, and 47 min, respectively. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1 and discharged without complications on postoperative day 10. Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved mitral valve function with trivial mitral regurgitation and no evidence of a residual tumor. Tumor recurrence and valve function were monitored during long-term clinical and echocardiographic followup.

Discussion and conclusions

Papillary fibroelastomas that predominantly arise in cardiac valves generally involve the aortic valve (~ 44%), followed by the mitral valve (~ 35%) [1]. In contrast, cardiac myxomas most frequently arise from the interatrial septum near the fossa ovalis [2].

Recurrence of papillary fibroelastoma after surgical excision is extremely rare [3], whereas cardiac myxomas can recur even after complete resection at a rate of 3%–5% [4].

Papillary fibroelastomas and myxomas appear similar on gross inspection [5]. Furthermore, these tumors might have partially overlapping histopathological features, such asmyxoid stroma [6], and frozen section diagnosis might be affected by freezing artifacts, making intraoperative differentiation challenging. Therefore, careful interpretation is required, and a definitive diagnosis ultimately relies on permanent pathological examinations. When a diagnosis is limited by inconclusive gross findings or the reliability of frozen sections, a change in the final pathological diagnosis should be considered during surgical planning. Under such conditions, adequate resection margins might be required to minimize recurrence. If securing such a margin results in a substantial leaflet defect, more complex repair techniques such as patch reconstruction or valve replacement might require consideration. A margin of ~ 5 mm might provide favorable outcomes for myxomas arising outside the interatrial septum [7]. However, to our knowledge, no recommendations have been established. Hence, the appropriate extent of resection remains controversial.

A right mini-thoracotomy approach provides satisfactory surgical exposure of the mitral valve and left ventricular cavity when tumors arise from the mitral valve or within the left ventricle [8]. Recent outcomes of robot-assisted, total endoscopic resection of papillary fibroelastomas arising from the mitral valve have been favorable [9]. The nonrobotic MICS approach to this case provided sufficient exposure for mitral valve inspection and tumor excision through direct visualization with thoracoscopic assistance. No technical difficulties were encountered. Thoracoscopic assistance offers a magnified operative view that facilitates the precise identification of tumor attachment and helps to secure an adequate resection margin while preserving as much normal leaflet tissue as possible. We achieved safe tumor excision and limited leaflet repair without robotic assistance. This experience suggests the feasibility of a nonrobotic MICS approach via right mini-thoracotomy for selected patients, particularly those with relatively simple posterior leaflet lesions requiring limited repair. Not having to use a robotic platform might also reduce resource requirements. One limitation of our study is that we did not use cardiac MRI mapping sequences, although this together with the behavior of the mass after gadolinium administration might facilitate differentiation between papillary fibroelastoma and myxoma [5]. We prioritized early surgical resection because the lesion was a very mobile 10-mm valvular mass with presumed embolic potential. Furthermore, the excised tumor was not examined in saline to assess the characteristic sea-anemone appearance of papillary fibroelastoma, which might have provided additional macroscopic diagnostic information. This case highlights the difficulty of differentiating papillary fibroelastomas from myxomas intraoperatively. Surgical decision-making should balance adequate tumor resection with preserved valve function when a diagnosis is uncertain.
