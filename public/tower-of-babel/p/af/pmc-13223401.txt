Defining onchocerciasis transmission zones: integrating operational insights with genomic and ecological advances
Authors: Arwa Elaagip, Lassane Koala, Dziedzom K de Souza, Kareen Atekem, Olabanji Surakat, Franklin Ayisi, Adoum Mahamat Oumar, Anne Heggen, Charles D Mackenzie, Daniel A Boakye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnchocerciasis is a parasitic infection caused by the filarial nematode Onchocerca volvulus and is transmitted through the bites of blackflies of the genus Simulium that breed in rivers and streams. The current WHO definition of transmission zones is limited, leaving country programmes to address challenges such as delineating transmission zones correctly, addressing blackfly migration, managing parasite and host movement and handling cross-border transmission issues. A more data-driven approach to the practical definition of transmission zones in African countries would be advantageous. A review of the documents in the WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (WHO IRIS) and using standard search engines (e.g. PubMed, Google Scholar) has identified previously less recognised factors that could enhance the definition and use of transmission areas in onchocerciasis areas. These guiding observations integrate historical operational, entomological, epidemiological and ecological information with genomic aspects.Keywords: blackfly migration, cross-border transmission, delineation extensions, onchocerciasis, operational transmission zones, reinvasion, transmission zones

Introduction

The Senegal and Mali studies provided initial proof that it is possible to eliminate onchocerciasis transmission in Africa using ivermectin.1 Four South/Central American countries have already eliminated parasite transmission in their relatively small endemic areas, and the WHO has verified these achievements. This led the WHO to change the global goal for onchocerciasis from reducing disease (i.e. treating it as a public health problem) to the more exacting goal of eliminating transmission of infection.2 This paradigm shift has meant that all national programmes must now widen their activities; for example, identify the extent of endemicity—previously only hyper-endemic and meso-endemic areas were under mass drug administration (MDA) with ivermectin—and now, under new circumstances, hypo-endemic areas need to be placed under MDA. Nevertheless, there has been a successful reduction in the prevalence of parasite infections in various meso- and hyper-endemic areas of Africa due to vector control and annual/biannual mass MDA,2,3 with Niger recently verified as having achieved national elimination of transmission.4–6 Senegal and specific foci in Mali,7 Uganda,8 Sudan,9,10 Equatorial Guinea11,12 and Nigeria13 have also interrupted transmission, and MDA has been stopped.14

Among the many difficulties in eliminating onchocerciasis transmission in Africa is the challenge of accurately delineating transmission zones (TZs). This is the consequence of a limited understanding of TZs among programme teams, together with limited consideration of influencing factors such as the dynamics of onchocerciasis transmission, vector species diversity and the migration of both vector and human hosts.

It is essential that national programmes clearly understand the rationale for using TZs for onchocerciasis-elimination programmes rather than, as in many large-scale MDA programmes such as those for lymphatic filariasis, using political-administrative zoning for implementation and monitoring. Onchocerciasis transmission is driven by the presence of the vector, which in turn is closely associated with the latter’s unique riverine breeding sites. Thus, geographic and biological factors dominate over administrative indicators in defining the TZs. For this reason, we have carried out the study presented here, and reviewed the information on the subject included in the WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS) files.

Concept of an onchocerciasis TZ

In the current context of onchocerciasis elimination, the WHO encourages endemic countries to delineate onchocerciasis TZs within their borders, where surveillance and intervention activities can be implemented.2,15 However, in reality, a TZ (i.e. the contiguous zone where transmission is taking place) can extend for great distances, crossing country boundaries, usually following the hydrological map rather than the political map. A TZ is an area where parasite transmission can be eliminated without the risk of parasite reintroduction through vector or human migration from areas with ongoing transmission. The concept of an operational transmission zone (OTZ) allows national programmes to define areas within a TZ that can be managed programmatically (i.e. defined, treated and monitored). Onchocerciasis TZs can be either ‘open’ or ‘closed’, depending on whether infected blackflies or people migrate in or out of the identified zone. Because complete closure may be rare, a closed onchocerciasis TZ is defined as one in which the entry or exit of an infected person or fly is relatively rare and has little impact on transmission dynamics.16 The opposite is an open TZ.

The WHO defines a TZ, or a ‘transmission focus’, as a geographical area where Onchocerca volvulus transmission occurs through local breeding vectors, which can be regarded as a natural ecological and epidemiological unit for intervention.16–18 However, this definition is challenging to implement due to the concept of ‘local breeding vectors’ in delineating onchocerciasis TZs. Evidence from the WHO Onchocerciasis Control Programme (OCP) indicates that transmission attributable to vectors arriving from distant areas up to 500 km away, reinvading regions where larviciding had previously eliminated them, is of epidemiological importance.19–21 This highlights the significant limitation of the above WHO definition and an inherent challenge for countries to delineate their onchocerciasis TZs accurately.22 Further indication of the limitation of the WHO definition is seen in a recent analysis of 189 whole mitochondrial genome sequences of adult nematodes across three spatial levels, including data from Ghana, Mali and Côte d’Ivoire in West Africa, where TZs and OTZs are identified using parasite genetic similarities and differences. These data suggest at least two large TZs: one running from North to South between Mali and Côte d’Ivoire, and another running from East to West within Ghana.23

In the context of avoiding constraints imposed by basing zones on national boundaries alone, it is essential to note that one of the main reasons for the success of the vector-targeting OCP is that the programme operated across a wide area of intervention without regard to international borders.24–26 However, the importance of addressing and eliminating onchocerciasis, regardless of country boundaries, was demonstrated well before the implementation of OCP. In 1969, Waddy stated that ‘It is essential that control operations should be conducted without regard to international frontiers. Therefore, inter-country cooperation is indispensable for working out and applying the most economical and effective control schemes.’27 Waddy’s comment was based on the migratory capacity of Simulium damnosum s.l., and he suggested that ‘the whole area from Senegal to Ethiopia should be considered as a single endemic focus’.27

Although onchocerciasis distribution across West Africa extends beyond country borders, in Eastern and Central Africa, the disease may occur in isolated foci. Most often in these isolated foci, the vectors are of the Simulium neavei group or unique species of S. damnosum s.l. It is much easier to eliminate transmission in these isolated foci, as reinfection is rare. Examples are found in Sudan,28 with the first eliminated focus being Abu Hamed,9 with a unique cytospecies of S. damnosum s.l. (Simulium hamedense),29 and a unique strain of O. volvulus,30,31 as well as Uganda, where transmission has been eliminated in 15 closed onchocerciasis TZs where the vectors are S. neavei.32

Rationale

In the current official context, however, it is individual countries that must be verified as achieving the elimination of transmission; they must also be able to delineate OTZs within their respective countries. Without clear guidance, endemic countries face challenges in determining how to do this and have often resorted to using arbitrary demarcations, such as administrative divisions (districts, regions and states), without acknowledging the disease-transmission dynamics in the particular area to characterise OTZs. By contrast, others, such as Chad and Mali, have used a biological approach to delineate their in-country OTZs.33 Knowledge and understanding of the strategies the OCP uses to delineate its intervention zones is likely to be helpful to all countries engaged in the elimination process. Some of the scientific evidence produced by the OCP is not published in peer-reviewed journals but has nevertheless been meticulously archived in reports. Recently, the WHO made these reports available by archiving them on the WHO IRIS. Understanding the OCP’s approach to delimiting its intervention zone (via the western and southern extensions) based on the phenomenon of blackfly reinvasion and miscellaneous information from the published literature could be adopted by countries today to delineate their TZs. The main objective of this review is to provide such guidance for countries.

Methodology

A bibliographic search of archives from several websites was conducted, with the most crucial site being the WHO IRIS (https://apps.who.int/iris). Standard scientific search engines, such as PubMed and Google Scholar, were used to identify relevant documents for the study.

Examples of the keywords assessed were ‘delineation’, ‘open transmission zone’, ‘closed transmission zone’, ‘extensions’, ‘reinvasion’, ‘migration of blackflies’, ‘transmission zones’, ‘operational transmission zones’ and ‘cross-border’.

The IRIS website archives WHO documents, organised by community and collection, as well as by date of publication, author and other relevant categories. Most of the technical and administrative reports of the OCP and African Programme for Onchocerciasis Control programmes are archived on this platform. The focus of this review was to understand the phenomenon of reinvasion, identify its sources and the areas invaded, and analyse the OCP’s strategy to extend its intervention in response to reinvasion to mitigate its impact. Review findings

Vectors of onchocerciasis in Africa

Onchocerca volvulus transmission in Africa is primarily facilitated by the bites of members of the S. damnosum complex and the S. neavei group. In West Africa, including Cameroon, there are >10 cytological forms of the S. damnosum s.l., with 10 cytospecies formerly named.34 Each of these species has different ecological needs and migratory capabilities that inform their distribution.35 Also, they exhibit different vectorial capacities.36,37

Vector migration is essential for determining TZs and OTZs and, therefore, the distribution of onchocerciasis. According to Cheke et al., the long- and short-distance migrations of vector S. damnosum s.l. are influenced by both physiological needs (blood feeding and oviposition) and environmental factors, including the movement of the intertropical convergence zone.37 Various vector species differ in their propensity to disperse and migrate in Africa. For example, S. neavei in Uganda is not known to disperse further than a few kilometres from its breeding sites; consequently, they are isolated.38 Simulium damnosum s.s. can migrate (wind-assisted) up to 500 km in West Africa and carry parasites into controlled areas.19–21

Generally, areas with vector species that have unique ecological niches and short migratory capabilities would create closed onchocerciasis TZs. However, this is not always the case, as migratory species may coexist in sympatry with vectors that exhibit former behaviours. The ability of vectors with strong migratory habits to carry parasites over vast distances creates open onchocerciasis TZs, often crossing international boundaries. Therefore, although some vectors do not fly long distances, they may still be within open onchocerciasis TZs due to their coexistence with migratory species. For example, Simulium leonense does not migrate long distances but occurs sympatrically with Simulium sirbanum, a long-distance migratory species.39 Thus, using only a particular vector species and its migratory habits to delineate the onchocerciasis TZ could be misleading, and there is a need to clearly understand the distribution of all species in the particular area.

Reinvasion of flies in the OCP area

The OCP started in seven West African countries (Ghana, Burkina Faso, Niger, Togo, Benin, Mali and Côte d’Ivoire) and later expanded to cover 11 countries with the addition of Senegal, Guinea-Bissau, Guinea and Sierra Leone. The operations were conducted in phases,19–21 and all areas in the original seven countries were covered in three stages from 1975 to 1977. After satisfactory larvicidal treatments in the Phase I area, the treated areas of the Black Volta basin, the Léraba, and along the Bandama in Côte d’Ivoire were reinvaded by Simulium populations from outside the treated areas.40 The same phenomenon was observed in 1976 and 1977, where reinvasion began at the end of April on the Bandama and Léraba, while the breeding areas, the Sassandra and Marahoué, remained productive.40

With successive phases of treatment in the original programme area, the reinvasion phenomenon spread to areas in southern Mali and northwestern Côte d’Ivoire, as well as to treated regions of Ghana, Togo and Benin. The migrant females still belonged mainly to the two savannah species, S. damnosum s.s./S. sirbanum, although Simulium squamosum, Simulium soubrense and Simulium sanctipauli were also found occasionally.19–21,40

A series of studies, involving the collection of invading flies and rearing larvae for species identification,39 experimental larviciding of potential source breeding sites,41 analysis of wind movements and other environmental factors,36 helped the OCP to extend the original area and thereby resolve the reinvasion phenomenon. These studies provided evidence that TZs are not necessarily limited to ‘locally breeding’ sites.

Distribution of onchocerciasis in Africa

According to the rapid epidemiological mapping of onchocerciasis maps, Africa displays a complex geographic mosaic of parasite TZs categorised as hypo-, meso- and hyper-endemic areas,42,43 shaped by historical spatial density and migration patterns of the blackfly vector and the human host,17,22 and a complex system of river basins and systems in endemic areas. This consistent yet diverse distribution of endemicity suggests that parasite transmission occurs across a continuous mix of overlapping areas with varying transmission rates, which are difficult to delineate separately.

TZs and delineation of OTZs

The current information and the experiences of the OCP indicate that West Africa exhibits an open TZ compared with Central and Eastern Africa;36 in the latter areas, there is a mixture of open and closed TZs.39 Because verification of onchocerciasis elimination is officially at the country level,2 it is challenging for countries within an open TZ to plan for the elimination of transmission without considering the situation in neighbouring countries. Thus, cross-border collaboration is critical to achieve the elimination goals.

Nevertheless, each country must be considered an individual entity regarding the operational activities required to verify the interruption of onchocerciasis transmission. To facilitate operational activities, such as ivermectin distribution and vector control, or to carry out coverage and impact surveys in its Western Extension areas, the OCP demarcated the area into operational zones based on ecological, entomological and epidemiological criteria.44 This strategy could be used by countries located within an open TZ to delineate OTZs and enable elimination activities to be conducted and monitored in each country.

OTZs, therefore, are often subsets of open TZs in individual countries for operational purposes, or possibly entire closed TZs. They can be delineated based on river basins, vector species and epidemiological criteria. Crawford et al. assert that the genomic data suggest that only using river basins for defining individual TZs may not represent the whole story; parasite genetic data may hold another, more detailed view of the complexity of the parasites present in a TZ.3 This type of data may inform the definition of specific OTZs by adding information about the origin of the parasites present and thus potential migration pathways that should be considered in the definition of practical OTZs.

Future tools for delineating TZs

Although data from the OCP and other sources indicate that river systems can delineate TZs, an additional challenge countries face is determining infection sources during the poststop MDA surveillance phase; currently, there are no tools that can reliably identify sources of infection with high confidence. Examining the parasites' genetic structure could fill this gap. Parasites within a TZ interbreed and are genetically more like each other than parasites from a different TZ with whom they do not interbreed. Thus, the likelihood of parasite transmission between two locations (i.e. invasion) can therefore be inferred from the degree of genetic relatedness between them.45–47

Determining the population genetic structure could thus help programmes assess the risk of resurgence associated with alternative scenarios on a longer time scale than with the intrinsically shorter-term decisions about where and whom to treat.3,45 The advantage of DNA-based TZ markers is that they can be designed to be highly specific. The first step identifies variant sites informative for population structure; that is, are they polymorphic and more common or fixed (and thus have different frequency-based likelihoods) in genomes of parasites from one area compared with another.3,45 Crawford et al. found mitochondrial polymorphic sites that could distinguish O. volvulus collected in Ghana from countries in the west of Africa with high statistical likelihood.3 Once such variant sites have been identified for specific geographic areas, parasite genomes from other areas need to be analysed to determine whether the same variants are informative, or whether additional sites are necessary to infer the extent of interbreeding. Once analysis is completed across their distribution, a minimum set of polymorphic sites can be defined that predicts the TZ of origin for any given worm. This process may sound daunting, but the methodology developed in recent years is efficient and is amenable to further optimisation.

Mathematical models, such as EpiOncho and OnchoSim, have been used to estimate when elimination will occur based on preintervention endemicity,48,49 MDA duration and therapeutic coverage levels. Recent modelling incorporating quantitative population genetic measures has demonstrated the utility of this approach for identifying potential sources of reinfection.45 McCulloch et al. extended the original EpiOncho framework for O. volvulus transmission dynamics into a patch model and revealed the importance of vector and human movements in determining the duration and sustainability of elimination of transmission.50

The innovations in parasite genomics and modelling could address the issue of parasite relatedness across different TZs, the connectivity between hypo-endemic, hyper-endemic and meso-endemic regions, as well as the risk of resurgence over a longer time frame.

Conclusions

The strategies the OCP implemented to define its operational areas developed various concepts and approaches to achieve control of onchocerciasis; it considered a wide range of practical measures to address the various entomological challenges (e.g. reinvasion by infected flies) using criteria that included blackfly migration and human presence as the primary criteria. River basins are core elements in delineating a transmission area; this criterion is also central for differentiating an open TZ from a closed zone. Lessons from the OCP’s experience can help countries to identify potential open TZs to delineate OTZs.

In addition to operational and ecological insights, genomic epidemiology presents a promising frontier for defining TZs. Analysing genetic relatedness among parasite populations can support programmes in better understanding transmission dynamics, evaluating the risk of reinvasion and making better informed decisions for onchocerciasis surveillance.
