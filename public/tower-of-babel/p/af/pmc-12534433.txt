Synergy of pyrophosphate and unsaturated nitrogen sites for efficient uranium recovery from concentrated nitric acid
Authors: Yaping Jian, Jiacheng Zhang, Xuewen Cao, Qin Peng, Jun Zhang, Xuefeng Tian, Jianfei Du, Yan Li, Yihui Yuan, Ning Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient recovery of uranium from spent nuclear fuel (SNF) reprocessing in ultra-acidic and radiation-intensive environments is critical for sustainable uranium resources management. However, traditional extractants exhibit instability and reduced uranium adsorption capacity under such harsh conditions. Here, we present a metal-free pyrophosphate-incorporated g-C₃N₄ polymer (PCNx) featuring unsaturated nitrogen sites and pyrophosphate groups for robust uranyl ion coordination. The inherent stability of g-C3N4 endows PCNx with high resistance to concentrated 12 M HNO3 and 500 kGy radiation. The optimal PCN1/3 achieves an uranium adsorption capacity of 75.3 mg g−1 in 12 M HNO3 and a high removal efficiency of 79.1% in a mixed ion solution, with a high distribution coefficient (Kd) of 18,964 mL g−1. This study demonstrates a promising proof-of-concept for designing functional polymers that efficiently recovers uranium from ultra-acidic and radiation-intensive conditions during SNF reprocessing.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Materials science

Introduction

The development of nuclear power technology has greatly advanced human society by providing a substantial and reliable source of energy1. However, alongside its benefits, the generation of highly radioactive SNF presents a significant environmental challenge, posing a considerable threat to both ecological safety and human health2,3. Currently, approximately 10,500 tons of SNF are generated annually from nuclear power plants worldwide4. This SNF typically contains more than 90% unconsumed uranium (U) along with a range of other nuclear fission products, many of which are highly radioactive and hazardous5. Consequently, the recovery and management of these valuable metal resources from high-level SNF is not only crucial for improving the economic efficiency of nuclear energy systems but also for mitigating the environmental impact of nuclear waste disposal6. In addition to resource recovery, reprocessing SNF can significantly reduce the long-term radioactivity of high-level nuclear wastes7. However, the effective reprocessing of SNF remains a complex and technologically challenging task due to the harsh conditions required for the process. SNF must be treated in highly concentrated nitric acid (HNO3), typically at concentrations of 12 M or higher, which is necessary for dissolving the SNF. Furthermore, these processes occur under extremely high radiation levels, often exceeding 100 kGy, which can degrade the materials and technologies used in the reprocessing systems8. Therefore, the development of efficient and robust methods for the recovery of uranium from radioactive SNF under these ultra-acidic and radiation-intensive conditions is a critical global issue that must be addressed to ensure both environmental safety and the sustainability of nuclear power generation.

Various methods have been developed for the recovery of uranium from SNF, each offering distinct advantages and limitations. These methods include chemical precipitation9, evaporation10, ion exchange11, solvent extraction12, and solid-phase extraction (SPE)13. Among these, SPE technology has garnered significant attention due to its promising potential for the separation and recovery of uranium resources, as well as its ability to decontaminate radioactive pollutants. SPE offers several key advantages over traditional methods, including simple operation, the elimination of toxic organic solvents, the absence of a third phase, rapid adsorption kinetics, and the disposal of trace extractants and diluents, making it more environmentally friendly and cost-effective14. Over the past few decades, various solid-phase extractants, such as resins, metal oxides, porous frameworks, and magnetic nanomaterials, have been employed in SPE techniques for the efficient extraction of uranium15. However, only a limited number of these materials are capable of withstanding the extremely harsh environments required for SNF reprocessing, particularly in highly concentrated HNO₃ solutions of up to 12 M. Many traditional extractants suffer from poor acid resistance, which necessitates the use of dilution procedures that lead to time-consuming extraction, high costs, dangerous operations, and the risk of uranyl ions precipitation. As a result, there is an urgent need to develop and explore ultrahigh acid-tolerant solid-phase extractants that can efficiently recover uranium in highly acidic matrix environments.

Phosphate-functionalized polymers have shown great potential in this regard, exhibiting excellent stability and durability in binding metal ions, especially uranyl ions, in highly concentrated HNO3 solutions16–18. However, despite these advancements, to the best of our knowledge, there is still no phosphate-functionalized polymer designed for efficient uranium recovery from ultra-acidic and radiation-intensive environments in SNF reprocessing, which would also require enhanced resistance to interference of co-existing metal ions in the complex SNF matrix. Herein, based on the exceptional stability of carbon nitride under extreme conditions and the strong binding affinity of phosphate groups for uranyl ions, a pyrophosphate-functionalized g-C3N4 material was developed, which incorporates an abundance of pyrophosphate binding sites capable of rapidly forming a stable U-pyrophosphate coordination complex in 12 M HNO₃ media, offering significant promise for uranium recovery in SNF reprocessing (Fig. 1). The pyrophosphate groups, along with the unsaturated nitrogen atoms inherent to the g-C3N4 structure, participate in chemical coordination with uranyl ions. This coordination mechanism not only significantly increases the binding affinity of the material for uranyl ions but also confers high selectivity in the presence of concentrated nitric acid. The material also exhibits outstanding hydrophilicity and radiation resistance, which further enhancing its functionality. As a result, the extractant achieves an impressive uranium adsorption capacity of 75.3 mg g−1 under 12 M HNO₃ conditions, making it the most efficient extractant under such extreme acidic conditions. These properties collectively position the PCNx material as a highly effective and practical candidate for uranium recovery in SNF reprocessing applications.

Results

Material synthesis

The pyrophosphate-functionalized g-C3N4 materials were successfully synthesized through a simple and efficient thermal polymerization process (Fig. 2a). To identify the material with optimal uranium recovery performance, the precursor concentration of Na4P2O7 was carefully adjusted, with the resulting material, PCN1/3, demonstrating the best performance. The synthesized PCN1/3 extractant appeared as a yellow powder (Supplementary Fig. 1). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that both the g-C3N4 and PCN1/3 materials exhibited a bulk sheet-like morphology, with PCN1/3 maintaining a similar structure but distinct modifications due to the incorporation of pyrophosphate groups (Fig. 2b–e). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of PCN1/3 was determined to be 3.69 m² g−1, confirming its feature as a mesoporous material (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Furthermore, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping analysis of PCN1/3 showed uniform distributions of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), phosphorus (P), and sodium (Na) across the material, indicating the successful incorporation of pyrophosphate groups into the g-C3N4 framework (Fig. 2f–k). This uniform element distribution provided strong evidence for the effective integration of the pyrophosphate groups into the structure of g-C3N4, which was crucial for enhancing its uranium recovery capabilities.

Material characterization

Two characteristic peaks at approximately 13.3° and 27.6° were observed in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of g-C3N4, corresponding to the in-plane repetition of tri-s-triazine units and the typical periodic graphitic stacking of the conjugated aromatic skeleton, respectively (Fig. 3a)19,20. As pyrophosphate incorporation increased, the (100) peak gradually disappeared, suggesting that the pyrophosphate incorporation induced in-plane distortion of the heptazine motifs. Additionally, the (002) peak became broader and weaker with the increasing pyrophosphate content, indicating that the stacking of the aromatic structures was inhibited, leading to the formation of thinner sheets. These observations suggested that pyrophosphate incorporation disrupted the regular stacking order of g-C3N4. Furthermore, the Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of all materials displayed the classic features of g-C₃N₄, including the characteristic breathing mode of triazine units at 810 cm−1 and the stretching vibration modes of C − N heterocycles in the 1200-1700 cm−1 range (Fig. 3b)21,22. Broad signals in the 2900-3280 cm−1 and 3280-3670 cm−1 regions were attributed to N − H and O − H stretching vibrations, respectively23. These characteristic peaks were also observed in the pyrophosphate-incorporated PCNx materials, confirming that the fundamental structure of g-C3N4 was maintained. Notably, the incorporation of pyrophosphate introduced additional peaks, including the P-O signals at 992 and 930 cm−1, and a P = O bond signal at 1161 cm−124. Additionally, a C − O breathing mode at 1078 cm−1 was observed for the pyrophosphate-modified samples, indicating the formation of C − O bonds between the pyrophosphate groups and the g-C₃N₄ framework25. As illustrated in the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectra, in addition to the elements C, N, and O found in g-C3N4, the presence of P and Na was clearly evident in PCN1/3, consistent with the EDS elemental mapping and EDS spectra (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 3). In the high-resolution N 1 s XPS spectra, both g-C3N4 and PCN1/3 exhibited three distinct peaks at approximately 401.36, 400.55, and 398.63 eV, which can be assigned to N−Hx, N − (C)3/N ≡ C, and C = N − C groups, respectively (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 4a)26. The high-resolution C 1 s XPS spectra revealed three main peaks for g-C3N4 at around 284.80 eV (C − C), 285.96 eV (C−NHx), and 288.24 eV (N − C = N), while an additional peak at approximately 288.89 eV (C − O) appeared for PCN1/3, indicating the presence of C − O bonds due to the grafting of pyrophosphate (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 4b)27,28. In the high-resolution O 1 s XPS spectra, peaks at 530.77 eV (P = O) and 532.43 eV (C − O/P − O/O − H) were observed for PCN1/3, while only the O − H bond signal was detected in g-C3N4 (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 4c)29. The peak at 535.58 eV was attributed to the Na KLL spectrum, further confirming the doping of Na into the g-C3N4 framework due to the use of Na4P2O7 as a precursor. The Na 1 s binding energy at 1071.5 eV was consistent with that of NaN3 (Supplementary Fig. 4d)25,29. The high-resolution P 2p XPS spectra revealed two peaks at 133.65 and 132.88 eV, corresponding to the P = O and P − O bonds, respectively (Fig. 3g)30,31. Moreover, the inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) results showed that the phosphorus content in the materials increased with the ratio of Na4P2O7 to melamine precursors, reaching a maximum at a ratio of 1:3. However, the phosphorus content decreased when the Na4P2O7 to melamine ratio was increased to 1:2 (Fig. 3h). The hydrophilicity of the extractant plays a critical role in its uranium adsorption performance. The higher phosphorus content, reflecting an increased presence of hydrophilic phosphorus-containing groups, contributed to the enhanced hydrophilicity of the material. This was consistent with the results from water droplet contact angle measurements, which indicated a decrease in the contact angle as the pyrophosphate content increased, thereby confirming the correlation between higher phosphorus content and enhanced hydrophilicity (Fig. 3i)31.

Screening of optimal conditions for uranium adsorption

The uranium adsorption performance was systematically evaluated by controlling a range of experimental variables to gain insights into the influencing factors. The uranium adsorption capacity gradually increased as the precursor ratio of Na4P2O7 to melamine increased from 0 to 1:3, due to the increased phosphorus content of the materials (Figs. 3h, 4a). However, further increasing the Na4P2O7 to melamine ratio from 1:3 to 1:2 resulted in a slight decrease in the uranium adsorption capacity, which was attributed to the decreased phosphorus content. This indicates that a balanced ratio of Na4P2O7 and melamine is crucial for achieving optimal adsorption performance. As the temperature increased from 298.15 K to 318.15 K, a significant enhancement in adsorption capacity was observed, confirming the temperature-dependent nature of the adsorption process (Fig. 4b). Specifically, a uranium adsorption capacity of 95.3 mg g−1 was achieved at 298.15 K in a 4 M HNO3 solution. Given that the reprocessing of SNF typically involves concentrated inorganic acids such as HNO3, it is important to assess the extractant’s performance in different acid concentrations32. The optimal extractant, PCN1/3, exhibited impressive uranium adsorption capacity of 75.3 mg g−1, even in a 12 M HNO3 ultrahigh acidic solution (Fig. 4c). This performance is superior to previously reported extractants under similar conditions (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Tab. 1)16–18,33, further demonstrating the potential of pyrophosphate-functionalized g-C3N4 as an effective material for uranium recovery in highly acidic environments.

Additionally, the presence of high salt concentrations, which are commonly encountered during SNF reprocessing, can significantly hinder uranium adsorption from saline media34. To assess this, the competition between sodium ions (Na+) and uranyl ions (UO22+) was also investigated. The PCN1/3 extractant exhibited only a slight decrease in uranium adsorption capacity when exposed to NaNO3 solutions with concentrations ranging from 0 to 3 M (Fig. 4e). This suggests that PCN1/3 possesses high resistance to interference from competing ions. The long-term stability of the extractant under highly acidic conditions and irradiation is a critical factor for its practical application. The analysis of the long-term stability showed that there was no significant change after being exposed to 12 M HNO₃ and nuclear radiation (Supplementary Figs. 5–8), demonstrating the high stability and durability of PCN1/3 under highly acidic conditions with nuclear radiation8,35. Furthermore, the uranium adsorption capacity of PCN1/3 was assessed after treatment with 12 M HNO3 and radiation exposure, which exhibited nearly unchanged uranium adsorption capacity (Fig. 4f), confirming the high potential of PCN1/3 for use in highly acidic environments and nuclear radiation conditions. This is because PCN1/3 is highly stable in conditions of high acidity and strong irradiation. It retains effective functional adsorption sites that coordinate with uranyl ions, ensuring excellent adsorption performance.

Adsorption performance of PCN1/3 to uranium

The uranium adsorption kinetics showed that the adsorption capacity of PCN1/3 reached 83.8 mg g−1 within 35 min of contact time (Fig. 5a). The kinetic data indicated that the adsorption process of PCN1/3 adhered most closely to the pseudo-second-order model. This suggested that the rate-determining step in the adsorption process was chemisorption, as strong chemical bonds formed between the extractant and the uranyl ions (Supplementary Tab. 2)36. Moreover, the uranium adsorption isotherms showed that the theoretical maximum adsorption capacity of PCN1/3 was approximately 142.6 mg g−1 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Tab. 3). This was confirmed by fitting the experimental data to the Langmuir adsorption model, indicating monolayer adsorption, further emphasizing the high efficiency and specificity of PCN1/3 for uranium uptake37. During nuclear fuel reprocessing, the presence of competing cations makes selectivity a critical factor for uranium recovery. To evaluate the selectivity of PCN1/3, experiments were conducted to simulate the conditions of SNF using synthetic mixtures containing 11 other representative metal ions in a 12 M HNO3 solution (Supplementary Tab. 4). Encouragingly, the uranium sorption efficiency (SE) of PCN1/3 increased with increasing adsorption times, achieving 79.1% efficiency after 6 h (Fig. 5c and Fig. 5d). This performance significantly exceeded that of pristine g-C3N4. The superior SE was attributed to the presence of a large amount of pyrophosphate on the surface of PCN1/3, which facilitated the formation of complexes with uranyl ions, thus enhancing the extractant’s affinity for uranium. The practical application of the extractant depends not only on its adsorption capacity but also on its dosage. Figure 5e shows the effect of different extractant dosages on the uranium SE of PCN1/3. As the extractant dosage increased, the uranium SE improved, eventually reaching a plateau at a dosage of 0.2 g L−1, which would be the optimal dosage for efficient uranium recovery38. Given the high uranyl ions capture properties and high-acidity resistance of PCN1/3, we further investigated the extractant’s performance under more stringent conditions. The uranium SE of PCN1/3 remained at 79.1%, significantly higher than that of other competing ions in the solution (Fig. 5f). Notably, the concentration of Na⁺ increased after adsorption, potentially due to an ion exchange between the Na⁺ in PCN1/3 and the uranyl ions, leading to the release of Na⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 9). The distribution coefficient (Kd) for the uranyl ions was as high as 18,964 mL g−1, which the highest value reported in the literature for extractants used in ultrahigh acid concentrations (12 M HNO₃), indicating that PCN1/3 has a high affinity for uranyl ions (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Tabs. 5, 6)39,40. Notably, only a few extractants can maintain practical feasibility under highly acidic conditions (i.e., HNO3 concentrations exceeding 10 M), as most materials tend to degrade or experience deactivation of their functional sites under such harsh conditions. This highlighted the promising potential of PCN1/3 for being used in real-world applications, specifically in uranium recovery during SNF reprocessing. Furthermore, we analyzed PCN1/3’s selective adsorption of uranyl ions against competing metal ions (Eu3+, Th4+, Cs+, Sr2+, Ni2+, K+, Zn2+, Co2+, Ca2+, Na+, and Mg2+) in 12 M HNO3 solution. The results showed that the uranium adsorption capacity of PCN1/3 was significantly higher than that for other co-existing metal ions, with high selectivity values (SU), including SU/Co of 1580.3, SU/Ni of 208.4, SU/K of 201.7, SU/Zn of 155. 4, SU/Ca of 126.4, SU/Sr of 54.7, SU/Cs of 44.9, SU/Th of 10.2, SU/Eu of 1.3, and SU/Mg of 12.7 (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Tab. 7). These results further confirmed the outstanding selectivity and practical potential of PCN1/3 for uranium recovery in SNF reprocessing. Considering the practical application challenges, we tested the material’s ability to adsorb uranium from a 12 M HNO3 solution using a continuous flow system. The sample was immobilized on the adsorption bed with silica powder, and a uranium solution containing 12 M HNO3 was continuously passed through the chromatographic column (Supplementary Fig. 10). The results indicated that the uranium adsorption capacity of the material after 3 h was 78 mg g−1 (Fig. 5i). These findings showed that the material exhibited excellent uranium adsorption performance in ultrahigh acid environments and has great potential for the development of flow columns for uranium adsorption under such extreme conditions in practical applications.

Adsorption mechanism of PCN1/3 to uranyl ions

To further elucidate the uranium adsorption mechanism of PCN1/3, comprehensive analyses were performed. The EDS elemental mapping analysis revealed that the uranium was uniformly distributed on the surface of PCN1/3 after uranium adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 11)41. In the XPS spectra, in addition to the characteristic peaks for C, N, O, and P that are present in the unmodified PCN1/3, a distinct U 4 f peak was observed in the U-PCN1/3 sample (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 12a)42. Notably, the disappearance of the Na signals in the XPS spectra suggested that an ion exchange had occurred between the Na+ in PCN1/3 and the uranyl ions during the adsorption process, which was also confirmed by an increase in the Na+ concentration in the competing ions solution after adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 12b). Additionally, the FT-IR spectra revealed a new peak at approximately 925 cm−1, which is attributed to the antisymmetric stretching vibration of O = U = O (Supplementary Fig. 13)43,44. These results demonstrated the successful adsorption of uranium.

In the high-resolution N 1 s XPS spectra of U-PCN1/3, three distinct peaks were observed at binding energies of 401.41 eV (N−Hx), 400.19 eV (N − (C)3)/N ≡ C, and 398.80 eV (C = N − C) (Fig. 6b). Comparing these spectra with those from the original PCN1/3 (Fig. 3d), the atomic percentage of the unsaturated N species (C = N − C) decreased, indicating that uranium was likely immobilized by coordination with the unsaturated nitrogen sites in the C = N − C structure within PCN1/3, thus contributing to the uranium adsorption process45. The high-resolution P 2p XPS spectra of U-PCN1/3 showed two main peaks at 133.93 and 133.19 eV, corresponding to P = O and P − O, respectively. After uranium adsorption, these peaks shifted to higher binding energies, which was characteristic of the coordinated interactions between pyrophosphate groups and uranyl ions, confirming that the phosphorus-containing groups in PCN1/3 participated in the uranium adsorption (Fig. 3g and Fig. 6c)46,47. Furthermore, the O 1 s XPS spectra of U-PCN1/3 exhibited a shift in binding energy from 532.43 eV (C − O, P − O, and O − H) and 530.77 eV (P = O) to 532.45 eV (C − O, P − O--U, and O − H) and 531.19 eV (P = O--U, and U = O), further confirming the formation of a uranyl-phosphate complex during the adsorption process (Figs. 3f, 6d)48,49. Moreover, the U 4 f XPS spectra of U-PCN1/3 after uranium adsorption revealed new signals corresponding to U 4f7/2 (380.60 eV) and U 4f5/2 (391.59 eV), which were distinct from the XPS peaks of U 4 f observed in the uranyl nitrate hydrate (UO2(NO3)2·6H2O), indicating that uranium was successfully complexed with the PCN1/3 material (Fig. 6e, f)50.

To gain further insight into the binding environment and local coordination structure of U(VI) adsorbed on the pyrophosphate-functionalized g-C3N4 extractant, extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis and density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed on the U-PCN1/3 complex. The EXAFS results showed two prominent peaks at 1.75 Å and 2.34 Å, which are attributed to the coordination with two axial oxygen atoms (U-Oax) and five equatorial oxygen atoms (U-Oeq), respectively (Fig. 6g, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Tab. 8)51,52. Additionally, a weaker peak was detected, indicating the formation of bond between uranium and unsaturated nitrogen atoms (U − N), which was due to the ion exchange between Na+ ions and uranyl ions during the adsorption process45. Furthermore, DFT calculations confirmed that the Na-incorporated g-C3N4 structure was more favorable for the adsorption of uranyl ions than the pristine g-C3N453. This was due to the more negative formation energy of the Na-incorporated structure (-4.49 eV) compared to the pristine counterpart (-4.09 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 15a–c and Supplementary Tab. 9). Additionally, the adsorption energy of pyrophosphate in g-C3N4 for uranyl ions was found to be negative (-6.65 eV) and the coordination with the uranyl ions occurred on the same plane, indicating that pyrophosphate groups were highly effective in capturing uranyl ions (Supplementary Fig. 15d–f and Supplementary Tab. 10). Based on these findings, the potential optimized structural modes of PCN1/3 and UO22+ ions were proposed. During the adsorption process, the uranyl ion underwent ion exchange with the Na⁺ ion in the PCN1/3 structure, forming U-N coordination bonds with the unsaturated nitrogen atoms within the heptazine framework (Fig. 6h). Furthermore, the uranyl ion also formed coordination bonds with five oxygen atoms on the phosphate groups (P = O and P − O) grafted onto PCN1/3 (Fig. 6i). These results highlight the key interactions and coordination environments that contribute to efficient uranium adsorption by PCN1/3, providing a deeper understanding of the uranium adsorption mechanism and the roles of pyrophosphate groups and unsaturated nitrogen sites in binding of uranyl ions.

Discussion

In conclusion, we presented the proof-of-concept polymeric extractant, PCN1/3, designed for efficient uranium recovery from ultra-acidic and radiation-intensive environments during SNF reprocessing. Synthesized using a straightforward thermal polymerization method, PCN1/3 demonstrated high stability and durability under harsh conditions, including high acidity and significant nuclear radiation. Its excellent performance was attributed to the presence of unsaturated nitrogen sites and abundant pyrophosphate groups, which formed well-preorganized coordination structures for binding of uranyl ions to form stable uranyl complexes. Consequently, PCN1/3 achieved a high uranium adsorption capacity of 75.3 mg g−1 in 12 M HNO3, setting a benchmark for uranium recovery in highly acidic environments. Additionally, the well-constructed coordination structures endowed PCN1/3 with outstanding selectivity for uranyl ions, effectively separating uranyl ions from coexisting ions in SNF, a critical factor for practical applications. Furthermore, even after exposure to 500 kGy of nuclear radiation, PCN1/3 still maintained its initial uranium adsorption capacity, confirming its nuclear radiation resistant. This work established PCN1/3 as a robust, selective, and environmentally resilient material, with significant potential for long-term application in nuclear waste treatment. Moreover, this work also opened avenues for the development of advanced extractants in nuclear waste management and environmental remediation.

Methods

Reagents

The melamine (CP) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Sodium pyrophosphate (AR), Th(NO3)4·6H2O (AR) and CsCl (AR) were obtained from Macklin Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Eu(NO3)3·6H2O (99.9%) was sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. Ethanol (AR), Sr(NO3)2 (AR), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (AR), KNO3 (AR), Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (AR), Co(NO3)2·6H2O (AR), Ca(NO3)2·4H2O (AR), NaNO3 (AR), and Mg(NO3)2·6H2O (AR) were purchased from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd., China. All chemical reagents were used as received, without further purification.

Synthesis of pyrophosphate-functionalized g-C3N4 (PCN)

Firstly, melamine and sodium pyrophosphate (Na4P2O7) were used as precursors, and the material was prepared via a calcination method. Specifically, 1.5 g of melamine and varying amounts of Na4P2O7 (0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 g) were ground together in a mortar for 20 min to ensure thorough mixing. The mixture was then transferred to a 30 mL crucible with a cover and calcined at 550 °C for 4 h, with a heating rate of 3 °C min−1. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the solid product was washed 5 times with ultrapure water and then 5 times with ethanol, respectively. The samples were then dried in an oven at 60 °C and ground for subsequent use. The resulting materials were labeled as PCNx, where “x” represents the ratio of sodium pyrophosphate to melamine. For comparison, unmodified g-C3N4 was prepared under the same conditions, with the exception that sodium pyrophosphate was not added.

Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using the Smart Lab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) at a voltage of 40 kV with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were measured using a Frontier spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) and the samples were prepared by the KBr pellet method. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out by an Escalab 250Xi (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an Al Kα as the excitation source. The mass fraction of P species was obtained by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) using 720 (Agilent, USA). The static water contact angle was measured using the JC 2000D4 analyzer (Shanghai Zhongchen Digital Technology Equipment Co., Ltd., China) by pressing the sample into tablets. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using an S-4800 II FESEM (Hitachi High-Technologies, Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using a Tecnai G2 F30 (FEI, USA) at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. The concentration of metal ions was measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Plasma 3000, NCS Testing Technology Co., Ltd., China) and inductively coupled plasma emission mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (ELspe-2/NexION 5000 G, Princen Scientific/PerkinElmer, China/USA).

Adsorption performance assay

Highly acidic uranium solutions were prepared by diluting concentrated nitric acid with ultrapure water to the desired acidity, followed by dissolving UO2(NO3)2·6H2O to achieve a specific uranium concentration. The adsorption properties of uranium were evaluated by adding 10 mg of material to a 50 mL highly acidic uranium solution, which was gently shaken at 120 rpm on a shaker during the test. Salt tolerance was assessed by introducing different concentrations of NaNO3 into the highly acidic uranium solutions. Gamma irradiation measurements

Gamma irradiation measurements were conducted at the irradiation facility of China Nuclear Hainan Haiyuan Development Co., Ltd. The irradiation device used was the KTJ4 irradiation accumulation radiation device, which is equipped with approximately 60,000 curies of a single 60Co source (divided into east and west source panels). The average energy of the γ-rays produced by the decay of the 60Co radioactive source is 1.25 MeV. During the experiment, the irradiation doses applied to the samples were 200 kGy, 350 kGy, and 500 kGy, respectively.

Computational methods

All the calculations are performed in the framework of the density functional theory with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP 6.3.2)55. The generalized gradient approximation proposed by Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) is chosen for the exchange-correlation potential56. The cut-off energy for plane wave is set to 450 eV. The energy criterion is set to 10−4 eV in the iterative solution of the Kohn-Sham equation. All the structures are relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.02 eV/Å.
