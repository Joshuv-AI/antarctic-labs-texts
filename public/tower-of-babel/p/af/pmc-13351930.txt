Silencing Suppressor Protein p26 of Areca Palm Velarivirus 1 (APV1) Interacts With SGS3 and Promotes Its Degradation Via the Ubiquitination Pathway
Authors: Xianmei Cao, Yutian Wang, Li Zhang, Yongqi Liang, Jiping Zheng, Hongxing Wang, Ruibai Zhao, Xi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRNA silencing is a conserved defence mechanism against viral infections in eukaryotes, whereas many plant viruses evolved viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) to counteract the antiviral silencing in hosts. Areca palm velarivirus 1 (APV1), a member of genus Velarivirus, family Closteroviridae, is a causal agent of yellow leaf disease affecting Areca catechu in Hainan, China. In this work, we identified the APV1‐encoded p26 protein as a multifunctional VSR that suppresses local silencing induced by single‐stranded RNA (ssRNA), systemic silencing induced by ssRNA and double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA), and the systemic spread of silencing signals. p26 was shown to be a viral pathogenicity factor that enhances the accumulation and pathogenicity of recombinant potato virus X (PVX). The suppressor of gene silencing 3 protein of A. catechu (AcSGS3), a key component of the plant antiviral silencing pathway, was proved to be a host factor interacting with p26. Both APV1 infection and p26 overexpression significantly induced NbSGS3 expression in Nicotiana benthamiana. The accumulation level of AcSGS3 protein decreased in the presence of p26, and such degradation was sensitive to the ubiquitination inhibitor MG132. Overexpression of p26 disturbed the interaction between SGS3 and the RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase 6 protein (RDR6), another key component coordinating with SGS3 in antiviral silencing. Our study reveals the molecular mechanism of p26‐mediated suppression of host antiviral defence by targeting SGS3 for accelerated degradation via the ubiquitination pathway.

Introduction

RNA silencing or RNA interference (RNAi), also known as post‐transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS), is a conserved defence mechanism against viral infections in eukaryotic organisms (Baulcombe 2004; Ding 2010). Antiviral RNAi in plants can be divided into three phases: initiation, amplification and systemic spread. In the initiation phase, RNAi is triggered by double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) or secondary structures like hairpin formed by single‐stranded RNA (ssRNA). These viral RNAs are sensed and processed by Dicer‐like nucleases (DCLs) into 21–24 nucleotide (nt) small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that are then recruited into the RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC), mediating sequence‐specific degradation or translational repression of the homologous viral RNAs (Ding 2010; Jin et al. 2021). After initiation with the production of primary virus‐derived siRNAs (vsiRNAs), viral RNA fragments cleaved by RISC serve as the template for the SUPPRESSOR OF GENE SILENCING 3 (SGS3)/RNA‐DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE 6 (RDR6) complex to generate dsRNAs that are subsequently processed into secondary vsiRNAs, leading to the amplification of RNAi (Wang et al. 2011). Amplified RNA silencing is implicated in the spread of RNA silencing signals that move between cells and over long distance throughout the plant, promoting systemic antiviral silencing in distant tissues (Kalantidis et al. 2008; Voinnet and Baulcombe 1997). To counteract antiviral RNAi of host, many plant viruses have evolved to encode one or more viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) that target the RNAi pathway through diverse mechanisms, such as siRNA sequestration, dsRNA binding and inactivation of specific protein components in RNAi through direct binding and/or accelerated degradation (Csorba et al. 2015; Hussain et al. 2021).

Among key components of the RNAi pathway, SGS3 and RDR6 are responsible for the biogenesis of secondary siRNAs and endogenous trans‐acting siRNA (tasiRNA), mediating the amplification of RNAi and promoting the cell‐to‐cell movement and systemic spread of RNAi signals (Fukunaga and Doudna 2009; Kalantidis et al. 2008; Peragine et al. 2004; Yoshikawa et al. 2021, 2013, 2005). In accordance with their interlaced functions in RNAi amplification, SGS3 and RDR6 interact and co‐localize in small cytoplasmic granules designated as SGS3/RDR6 bodies (Kumakura et al. 2009). As the core components for RNAi amplification, SGS3 and RDR6 have been reported to be targets of heterologous VSRs. For example, the P6 protein of rice yellow stunt rhabdovirus (RYSV) interacts with RDR6 and inhibits systemic RNA silencing by affecting RDR6‐mediated secondary siRNA synthesis (Guo et al. 2013). SGS3‐interacting VSRs have been found in multiple virus families, including V2 of tomato yellow leaf curl geminivirus (TYLCV, Geminiviridae) (Glick et al. 2008), p2 of rice stripe virus (RSV, Phenuiviridae) (Du et al. 2011), TGBp1 of Plantago asiatica mosaic virus (PlAMV, Alphaflexiviridae) (Okano et al. 2014), VPg of potato virus A (PVA, Potyviridae) (Rajamäki et al. 2014), RNase3 of sweet potato chlorotic stunt virus (SPCSV, Closteroviridae) (Weinheimer et al. 2016), P4 of rice stripe mosaic virus (RSMV, Rhabdoviridae) (Zhang et al. 2020), NSs of tomato zonate spot virus (TZSV, Tospoviridae) (Chen et al. 2022), PRT of rice tungro bacilliform virus (RTBV, Caulimoviridae) (Naresh et al. 2023), and p20 of citrus tristeza virus (CTV, Closteroviridae) (Zhang et al. 2025). TGBp1 of PlAMV and the phosphoprotein of lettuce necrotic yellows virus (LNYV) interact with both SGS3 and RDR6 (Mann et al. 2016; Okano et al. 2014). Furthermore, a calmodulin‐like protein from Nicotiana benthamiana (NbCaM) is up‐regulated by the βC1 protein of DNA satellite associated with tomato yellow leaf curl China virus (TYLCCNV) and induces NbSGS3 degradation to promote geminivirus infection (Li et al. 2014, 2017). These findings demonstrate the crucial role of the SGS3/RDR6 complex in the antiviral RNAi pathway. As a rare and counterintuitive case, SGS3 is reported to enhance the accumulation of turnip crinkle virus (TCV), revealing a novel negative role of SGS3 in antiviral defence (Liu et al. 2023).

Areca palm velarivirus 1 (APV1), a member of genus Velarivirus, family Closteroviridae, was first identified in an Areca catechu leaf sample affected by yellow leaf disease (YLD) and is a causal agent of YLD in Hainan, China (Wang et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2024). Closteroviruses are mono‐, bi‐ or tripartite positive‐sense RNA plant viruses with flexuous, filamentous virions of 650–2200 nm in length. The genome organization is conserved but complex, encoding about 10 open reading frames (ORF) with a large size of 13–19 kb (Agranovsky 2016; Fuchs et al. 2020). Currently there are seven genera in the Closteroviridae family (https://ictv.global/taxonomy), with Velarivirus being the fourth to be proposed and ratified (Al Rwahnih et al. 2012). However, there is still limited knowledge about the encoded proteins or pathogenesis of Velarivirus and the three genera assigned later (Bluvavirus, Menthavirus and Olivavirus). Likewise, although progress has been made for the detection, genetic diversity, transmission, epidemiology, and cytopathology of APV1 (Cao et al. 2024, 2021; Khan et al. 2022; Peng et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2024), little is known about the functions of proteins encoded by APV1 and the current knowledge are based upon in silico comparative analyses with related closteroviruses.

In this work, we identified APV1‐encoded p21 (ORF4), p26 (ORF8) and p25 (ORF10) as VSRs. Further study showed that p26 interacts with SGS3 and inhibits both local and systemic silencing. p26 was found to up‐regulate SGS3 expression, induce the degradation of SGS3 via the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway, and disturb the interaction between SGS3 and RDR6.

Results

APV1 p26 Suppresses Local RNA Silencing in Nicotiana benthamiana Plants

APV1 was predicted to encode four unique ORFs that showed no significant similarity to known viral proteins, namely ORF4, ORF8, ORF9 and ORF10, which encode the proteins p21, p26, p18 and p25, respectively (Wang et al. 2020). The majority of reported closteroviral VSRs are coded by 3′ end genes and have similar molecular weights of about 20 kDa (Hussain et al. 2021). Therefore, these four species‐specific proteins of APV1 are much more likely to be VSR candidates. To test this hypothesis, four binary plasmids were constructed, individually expressing the APV1 proteins p21, p26, p18 or p25 under the control of a CaMV 35S promoter (p35S‐p21, p35S‐p26, p35S‐p18 and p35S‐p25, respectively). Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains transformed with one of four viral constructs were co‐infiltrated with an Agrobacterium culture carrying a GFP‐expressing construct (p35S‐GFP) into the leaves of wild‐type N. benthamiana plants. Co‐infiltration of p35S‐GFP with the empty vector (EV) or a plasmid expressing the p19 suppressor encoded by tomato bushy stunt virus (p35S‐p19TBSV) served as negative and positive controls, respectively. The inoculated plants were monitored by direct observation under a hand‐held blue light lamp. At 3 days post‐inoculation (dpi), compared to the GFP‐expressing negative control, leaf patches co‐infiltrated with p35S‐GFP and p35S‐p19TBSV showed significantly enhanced GFP fluorescence. For the four viral constructs tested, expression of p18 had no apparent effects on GFP expression, while expression of p21, p26 or p25 enhanced GFP fluorescence, suggesting their bioactivity as VSRs (Figure S1).

Similar to our findings, several members of Closteroviridae have been reported to encode multiple VSRs, including CTV (Lu et al. 2004), pineapple mealybug wilt associated virus‐2 (PMWaV‐2) (Dey et al. 2015), SPCSV (Kreuze et al. 2005) and tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) (Cañizares et al. 2008). Due to research limitations, here we continued to solely focus on the ORF8‐encoded p26 for further assays. The above agroinfiltration assay of p26 was repeated and corroborated with western blot and northern blot analyses that confirmed a significant increase of GFP protein and mRNA levels in the presence of p26, respectively (Figure 1a). Further parallel experiments conducted on N. benthamiana 16c plants showed the suppressive effect of p26 on local silencing caused by transient GFP expression (Figure 1b). These results demonstrate that APV1‐encoded p26 suppresses local RNA silencing triggered by ssRNA in N. benthamiana.

p26 Suppresses Systemic RNA Silencing and Prevents Systemic Spread of the Silencing Signal

We next determined the effects of p26 on systemic RNA silencing. Fully expanded leaves of N. benthamiana 16c plants were co‐infiltrated with p35S‐GFP and p35S‐p26. An empty vector (EV) and p35S‐p19TBSV were used as negative and positive controls in place of p35S‐p26, respectively. Infiltrated plants were monitored for the initiation and development of systemic GFP silencing in the newly emerging leaves. By 10 dpi, systemic silencing was observed in plants infiltrated with p35S‐GFP and EV, while GFP expression persisted in all newly emerged leaves when co‐infiltrated with p26 or p19. By 31 dpi, systemic silencing was almost complete in plants co‐infiltrated with p35S‐GFP and EV, while the majority of plants co‐infiltrated with p26 or p19 retained strong GFP expression in the developed plant (Figure 2a–c). To test whether p26 could inhibit dsRNA‐induced silencing, we conducted similar experiments in which a construct expressing double‐stranded GFP RNA (pTCK‐dsGFP) was used in place of p35S‐GFP which expresses full‐length sense transcripts. Similarly, systemic silencing induced by GFP hairpin was observed in plants co‐infiltrated with EV, while plants co‐infiltrated with p26 or p19 retained large GFP‐expressing areas (Figure 2d–f). These results indicate that p26 is able to suppress systemic RNA silencing induced by both ssRNA and dsRNA.

When local silencing was initially triggered in developmentally older areas (the tip of a leaf or the lower leaves of a plant), the systemic spread of silencing signals could be blocked in the newly developed tissues (the base of a leaf or the upper leaves of a plant), eventually leading to delayed or suppressed systemic silencing (Guo and Ding 2002; Himber et al. 2003). To further investigate whether p26 could prevent systemic spread of silencing signals, p35S‐p26 and p35S‐GFP were separately infiltrated into the tip or the base of the same leaf, or the upper young or the lower mature leaves of the same 16c plant as previously described (Dey et al. 2015; Guo and Ding 2002; Kong et al. 2014). When p26 was expressed in the lower leaves, systemic GFP silencing was obvious in newly emerged leaves at 14 dpi, and continued to progress by 30 dpi. In contrast, when p26 was expressed in the upper leaves, most plants retained strong GFP expression by 30 dpi (Figure 2g–i). Together, these results demonstrate that p26 is a potent VSR that can block the spread of silencing signals and suppress systemic silencing in N. benthamiana.

p26 Enhances the Pathogenicity of PVX and the Local Infection of APV1 in N. benthamiana

Heterologous expression of VSRs by viral vectors has been reported to enhance the symptom severity of the recombinant viruses (Díaz‐Pendón and Ding 2008). To analyse the role of p26 related to viral pathogenicity, p26 was cloned into a potato virus X (PVX) vector (Lu et al. 2003) to generate PVX‐p26. The original PVX empty vector (PVX‐EV) and PVX‐p26 were individually infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. For plants inoculated with PVX‐EV or PVX‐p26, systemic symptoms typical of PVX infections appeared on the upper newly emerged leaves at 6 dpi, and no distinguishable differences were observed among these infected plants (data not shown). However, plants inoculated with PVX‐p26 gradually developed more severe symptoms, which became evident at 10 dpi. Worsening of the symptoms continued with time, including mosaic curling leaves and stunted growth at 13 dpi (Figure 3a). Furthermore, reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) results confirmed that PVX RNA accumulated to higher levels in plants infected with PVX‐p26 at 13 dpi (Figure 3b). These results indicate that p26 enhances the accumulation and pathogenicity of PVX.

To determine whether p26 influences APV1 infection, a GFP‐labelled infectious cDNA clone of APV1 (APV1‐GFP, unpublished data) was agroinfiltrated into transgenic N. benthamiana overexpressing p26 (p26‐OE) or wild‐type plants. GFP fluorescence mediated by local infection of APV1‐GFP was used to monitor the progression of APV1 infection. Results showed that the local infection of APV1‐GFP was enhanced in p26‐OE plants, with increased protein levels of CP and GFP (Figure S2).

p26 Interacts With NbSGS3 and AcSGS3

To investigate the mechanism of p26‐mediated repression of RNA silencing, we next tested whether p26 could interact with key components of the RNAi pathway. SGS3 is the key component in production of secondary siRNAs that served as signals for systemic RNA silencing (Kalantidis et al. 2008; Jin et al. 2021). Given that p26 prevents systemic spread of the silencing signal and suppresses systemic RNA silencing, it is possible that p26 exerts these functions by targeting SGS3. Based on this assumption, the interaction between p26 and NbSGS3 were first tested by using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays in N. benthamiana cells. NbSGS3 was fused to the N‐terminus of YFP to express YN‐NbSGS3, while p26 was fused to the C‐terminus of YFP to produce YC‐p26. Positive interaction between YN‐NbSGS3 and YC‐p26 was verified by the presence of YFP fluorescence, indicating that p26 interacts with NbSGS3 in vivo (Figure 4a). To dissect the original host–virus interaction between areca palm and APV1, the areca homologue of NbSGS3, designated AcSGS3 (GenBank accession PQ496828), was found by BLAST searches against the ArecaceaeMDB database (Yang et al. 2023). Similar BiFC assays confirmed that p26 interacts with AcSGS3 in vivo (Figure 4a). The intracellular distributions of AcSGS3 and p26 were examined by transient co‐expression assays in N. benthamiana epidermal cells. The confocal microscopy results showed that both AcSGS3‐GFP and p26‐GFP were diffused in the cytoplasm and the nucleus, while the BiFC complex of YN‐AcSGS3 and YC‐p26 formed distinct punctate structures in the cytoplasm (Figures 4a and S3). The physical interaction between p26 and AcSGS3 was further supported by yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H), co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP), and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays (Figure 4b–d). Together, these assays confirmed that p26 interacts with both AcSGS3 and NbSGS3. Furthermore, preliminary BiFC assays demonstrated that AcSGS3 also interacts with p21 and p25, the other two VSR encoded by APV1, in planta (Figure S5).

We next mapped the p26‐interacting domains in AcSGS3. Homologous to NbSGS3 (homologue in N. benthamiana) and AtSGS3 (homologue in Arabidopsis thaliana ), AcSGS3 is predicted to contain four conserved domains: the N‐terminal domain (NTD), the zinc finger domain (ZF), the rice gene X and SGS3 domain (XS) and the C‐terminal coiled‐coil domain (CC) according to sequence alignment with relative homologues (Figures 5a and S5) (Cheng and Wang 2017; Liu et al. 2023; Rajamäki et al. 2014). Based on the predicted domain structure, Y2H assays were conducted in which the NTD, ZF, XS and CC domains were individually cloned to fuse with GAL4 activation domain (AD). Y2H results showed that p26 interacted with the XS and CC domains, but not with the NTD or ZF domains in yeast cells (Figure 5b). To further confirm these interactions in planta, four BiFC vectors covering each of the domains were constructed to express the four domains fused to the N‐terminal part of YFP, respectively. Co‐infiltration assays with YC‐p26 showed that all four domains of AcSGS3 could interact with p26 in N. benthamiana cells, but exhibited differential subcellular localization. YN‐XS and YN‐CC seemed to have stronger interaction relationship with YC‐p26, as indicated by brighter fluorescence (Figure 5c). Similar LCI assays gave results consistent with those obtained by BiFC, indicating that p26 interacts with all four domains of AcSGS3 in planta (Figure 5d). These results reveal that AcSGS3 possesses multiple p26‐interacting sites with the XS and CC domains being the major determinants.

p26 Up‐Regulates the Expression of NbSGS3 and NbRDR6 and Represses the Expression of NbARF3 and NbARF4

To examine how SGS3 responds to p26 expression, we generated transgenic N. benthamiana plants (line p26) that constitutively expressed p26 protein under a 35S promoter. RT‐qPCR results showed that NbSGS3 was up‐regulated in p26‐transgenic plants (Figure 6a).

To further determine how SGS3 responds to APV1 infection, we used RT‐qPCR to analyse the changes of SGS3 expression patterns in healthy or APV1‐affected plants. Given that closteroviruses are known to take a long time (usually at least 2 months) to establish systemic infection after inoculation (Ambros et al. 2011; Kurth et al. 2012), healthy areca seedlings were inoculated with APV1 via mealybug transmission (Zhang et al. 2022) and sampled at 5 months post‐inoculation (mpi), while N. benthamiana plants were inoculated with a full‐length infectious cDNA clone of APV1 via agroinoculation (unpublished data) and sampled at 3 mpi. RT‐qPCR results indicated that AcSGS3 in areca seedlings, as well as NbSGS3 in N. benthamiana, were both up‐regulated under systemic infection of APV1 (Figure 6a).

In the conserved RNAi pathway, both SGS3 and RDR6 are required for the biogenesis of tasiRNAs that down‐regulate the corresponding target genes (Peragine et al. 2004; Yoshikawa et al. 2005). We therefore examined the effects of p26 on the expression of SGS3 and RDR6, as well as ARF3 (AUXIN RESPONSE TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 3) and ARF4 (AUXIN RESPONSE TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 4), two marker genes known to be targets of TAS tasiRNAs (Liu et al. 2007; Peragine et al. 2004). RT‐qPCR results showed that the expressions of NbSGS3 and NbRDR6 mRNA were up‐regulated in leaves transiently expressing p26. In accordance with the elevated levels of NbSGS3 and NbRDR6, mRNA levels of NbARF3 and NbARF4 were significantly down‐regulated (Figure 6b).

p26 Induces the Ubiquitination Degradation of AcSGS3 and Interferes With the Interaction Between SGS3 and RDR6

p26 inhibits RNA silencing, while up‐regulating the expression of SGS3. Aside from binding to SGS3, it is possible that p26 accelerates the degradation of SGS3. To test the effects of p26 on AcSGS3 accumulation, we transiently expressed GFP‐AcSGS3 with FLAG‐p26 or with an empty vector in N. benthamiana leaves. Western blotting with the anti‐GFP antibodies showed that the accumulation level of GFP‐AcSGS3 decreased when co‐expressed with FLAG‐p26 (Figure 7a), suggesting that p26 mediates AcSGS3 protein degradation.

The ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway and the autophagy pathway are two main protein degradation pathways in plant cells. To dissect the pathways that are recruited for AcSGS3 degradation, similar infiltration assays were repeated in the presence of MG132 (an inhibitor of the 26S proteasome pathway) or E64d (an inhibitor of the autophagy way). Western blotting results showed that the accumulation level of GFP‐AcSGS3 was enhanced by MG132 treatment, but not by E64d treatment (Figure 7b,c), suggesting that the p26‐induced degradation of AcSGS3 likely occurs through the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway.

SGS3 interacts with RDR6 to form cytoplasmic SGS3/RDR6 bodies that are responsible for the amplification of RNA silencing (Kumakura et al. 2009). We next analysed the effects of p26 on the interaction between SGS3 and RDR6 using serial LCI assays. NbSGS3‐nLUC was co‐expressed with NbRDR6‐cLUC, along with a FLAG‐expressing empty vector or proportionally increased FLAG‐p26. Imaging results showed that the NbSGS3–NbRDR6 interaction was weakened as the protein level of p26 increased (Figure 8a). Further, a competitive glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down assay of p26‐AcSGS3‐AcRDR6 was conducted with increasing amounts of p26. Results showed that p26 disrupted the interaction between AcSGS3 and AcRDR6 (Figure 8b), suggesting that p26 interferes with the assembly of SGS3/RDR6 bodies.

Discussion

Members of the family Closteroviridae possess one of the largest genomes among plant viruses that vary from 13 to 19.3 kb in size with a coding capacity of more than 10 ORFs (Agranovsky 2016; Fuchs et al. 2020). The complex genomes of closteroviruses conservatively encode a replication module (ORF1a and ORF1b) and a unique quintuple gene block (QGB) essential for virion assembly and cell‐to‐cell movement, while the rest of the genes, most of which are located in the 3′ terminus of the genome, are highly diverse among individual viruses. These species‐specific genes usually have no identifiable homologues in other viruses and their functions remain largely enigmatic (Dolja et al. 2006). Exceptionally, a p20 superfamily of RNA silencing suppressors (RSS_P20, pfam11757) has been found in the genus Closterovirus (Reed et al. 2003; Ye and Patel 2005). However, these orthologous VSRs are unique to Closterovirus as no detectable homologues have been found in the other six genera. Currently, more than 20 VSRs (putative or experimentally confirmed) have been reported in the genera Closterovirus, Crinivirus and Ampelovirus of Closteroviridae, while little is known about the VSRs of the other four genera including Velarivirus. In this study, we screened potential VSRs among the species‐specific genes of APV1 using a traditional Agrobacterium infiltration assay, and identified APV1‐encoded p21, p26 and p25 as VSRs that are capable of suppressing ssRNA‐induced local silencing (Figure S1).

To counteract the antiviral RNAi in host, many plant viruses evolved one or more VSRs to ensure successful viral infection. Some VSRs seem to be functionally dedicated, while others are involved in replication, virion assembly, or viral movement (Csorba et al. 2015). In Closteroviridae, CTV, ToCV, PMWaV‐2 and SPCSV have been reported to encode multiple VSRs (Cañizares et al. 2008; Dey et al. 2015; Kreuze et al. 2005; Lu et al. 2004). These VSRs tend to target host RNAi in different aspects and work synergistically to enhance VSR activity. For example, CTV encodes three distinct VSRs: CP, p20 and p23. The intracellular and intercellular silencing are targeted by p23 and CP, respectively, whereas p20 inhibits silencing at both levels (Lu et al. 2004). Moreover, co‐expression of p20 and p23 enhanced the suppression of local silencing (Costa et al. 2016). PMWaV‐2 has been reported to encode four VSRs, among which p20 and CP have local and systemic suppression activity, while p22 and CPd suppress only systemic silencing (Dey et al. 2015). Likewise, our results showed that APV1 employs a multiple‐component RNAi suppression strategy of at least three proteins that suppress local silencing induced by sense RNA (Figure S1). Similar to p20 and CP encoded by PMWaV‐2, APV1 p26 targets both local and systemic silencing and blocks the systemic silencing signal (Figures 1 and 2). Although being a multifunctional VSR, p26 seems to be a relatively weak suppressor of sense‐mediated local silencing compared with p21 or p25 (Figure S1). Interestingly, all three VSRs interact with AcSGS3 in planta (Figures 4 and S5), suggesting a complex role of AcSGS3 in APV1 infection. It is unclear whether p26 works with the other VSRs synergistically.

The coordinated functions of SGS3 with RDR6 are pivotal in sense‐mediated silencing and synthesis of secondary siRNAs and endogenous tasiRNAs (Béclin et al. 2002; Mourrain et al. 2000; Peragine et al. 2004; Yoshikawa et al. 2013, 2005). Mobile siRNAs have been suggested to be the signal for systemic RNA silencing in plants (Himber et al. 2003; Melnyk et al. 2011; Liu and Chen 2018). Given that p26 suppresses sense‐mediated silencing and blocks the systemic silencing signal, it is possible that p26 exerts these functions by interacting and interfering with the SGS3/RDR6 complex. In accordance with this hypothesis, our results confirmed that p26 interacts with AcSGS3, while their co‐localization appears to resemble the small cytoplasmic bodies of SGS3/RDR6 complex (Kumakura et al. 2009) (Figure 4 and S3).

Consistent with its function in the antiviral RNAi pathway, SGS3 generally plays a positive role in defence against plant viruses (Chen et al. 2022; Li et al. 2017; Tong et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2024). In our study, RT‐qPCR tests showed that both APV1 infection and p26‐overexpression induced NbSGS3 expression (Figure 6a), implying that SGS3 responds to play an antiviral role in APV1 infection. Furthermore, p26‐overexpression down‐regulated the expression levels of NbARF3 and NbARF4, two target genes of endogenous tasiRNAs (Figure 6b), suggesting an enhanced SGS3 activity of tasiRNA biogenesis. However, we found that both APV1 infection and APV1 p25 protein (encoded by ORF10) disturb the auxin signalling pathway of the host (unpublished data). Therefore, p26 may interfere with the auxin signalling pathway (including ARF transcription factors like ARF3 and ARF4) in a SGS3‐independent manner. The effects of p26 on tasiRNA production remain to be elucidated in future works.

Accelerated degradation of SGS3 has been reported to be the strategy employed by a few VSRs as well as endogenous regulators of host. Among reported SGS3‐interacting VSRs, the VPg protein of turnip mosaic virus (TuMV), P4 of RSMV, and NSs of TZSV have been proved to induce SGS3 degradation via both the ubiquitination and autophagy pathways (Chen et al. 2022; Cheng and Wang 2017; Zhang et al. 2020). A host factor, NbCaM, functions as an endogenous suppressor of silencing by mediating the autophagic degradation of SGS3 (Li et al. 2017). Similarly, a virus‐induced small peptide (VISP1) interacts with SGS3 and mediates autophagic degradation of SGS3/RDR6 bodies as a selective autophagy receptor (Tong et al. 2021, 2023). In this work, we showed that p26 induced the degradation of AcSGS3, basically through the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway as such degradation was sensitive to the ubiquitination inhibitor MG132 (Figure 7). Furthermore, p26 was shown to disrupt the assembly of SGS3/RDR6 bodies as demonstrated by competitive LCI and GST pull‐down assays (Figure 8).

In summary, our study identified APV1 p26 as a multifunctional VSR that suppresses local silencing induced by ssRNA, systemic silencing induced by ssRNA and dsRNA, and the systemic spread of silencing signals. These VSR bioactivities of p26 could be at least partially explained by the fact that p26 interacts with SGS3 and accelerates the degradation of SGS3. However, considering that some multifunctional VSRs target RNAi with multiple strategies (Csorba et al. 2015; Díaz‐Pendón and Ding 2008; Jin et al. 2021), novel molecular mechanisms of p26‐mediated suppression of host antiviral RNAi are worth exploring in further research.

Experimental Procedures

Virus Source and Plant Materials

APV1 isolate WNY (APV1‐WNY, GenBank accession MK956940) described previously (Wang et al. 2020) was used as the source of sequence for generating APV1‐derived constructs in this work. One‐year‐old A. catechu seedlings were purchased from the Coconut Research Institute of Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences and cultivated in greenhouse under controlled conditions of 28°C ± 2°C, 16 h/8 h light/dark. Healthy areca seedlings were inoculated with APV1 via mealybug transmission as described previously (Zhang et al. 2022). N. benthamiana seedlings (wild‐type and GFP‐transgenic line 16c) were germinated and kept in greenhouse under controlled conditions of 22°C ± 2°C, 16 h/8 h light/dark. N. benthamiana seedlings were inoculated with APV1 via agroinfiltration of a full‐length infectious cDNA clone of APV1‐WNY (unpublished data).

Plasmid Construction

All plasmids used in this study were constructed by using recombination‐based one‐step assembly (Vazyme). The genome of APV1 is AT‐rich and contains numerous potential intron‐splicing sites (unpublished data). For optimized expression in planta, codon optimization of APV1 genes was performed by Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and the optimized coding sequences were cloned into binary expression vectors. The original p26 sequence was used to build the yeast vector BD‐p26 and the recombinant PVX‐p26. Full‐length or individual domains of AcSGS3 (GenBank accession PQ496828) or NbSGS3 (GenBank accession KJ190939) were amplified from cDNA generated from areca and N. benthamiana seedlings, respectively. The CDS of interest with compatible cloning adaptors (15 to 25 bp overlap) were amplified with specific primers (Table S1) and cloned to the linearized empty vectors to generate the desired constructs. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Binary expression vectors p35S‐GFP, p35S‐p21, p35S‐p26, p35S‐p18, p35S‐p25 and p35S‐p19TBSV were derived from the same empty vector that possessed a CaMV 35S promoter‐NOS terminator cassette. p35S‐FLAG‐p26 and p35S‐GFP‐AcSGS3 were constructed with the same vector backbone. pTCK‐dsGFP was constructed by inserting a 398‐bp fragment of the GFP gene in both the sense and antisense orientations into the sites upstream and downstream of a rice intron in the RNAi vector pTCK303 (Wang et al. 2004), respectively.

PVX‐p26 was constructed by inserting the p26 CDS into the SmaI site of the PVX empty vector pGR107 (Lu et al. 2003). The rest of the vectors were described in the corresponding sections below.

Agroinfiltration and Chemical Treatments

Nicotiana benthamiana seedlings at the 4‐ to 5‐true‐leaf stage were used for agroinfiltration. Procedures were as previously described (Chen et al. 2012) with minor modifications. Briefly, a single colony of A. tumefaciens GV3101 transformed with the desired construct was inoculated into 10 mL Luria Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin, 25 μg/mL rifampicin, 20 μM acetosyringone (AS) and 10 mM N‐morpholinoethanesulfonic acid (MES) and grown overnight at 28°C with shaking at 200 rpm until an OD600 of 0.6–1.0 was reached. A. tumefaciens was pelleted by centrifugation at 3000 g for 5 min and resuspended in 10 mL suspension solution (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES). The resuspended bacteria were then centrifuged at 3000 g for 5 min and the pellet was resuspended in infiltration solution (10 mM MgCl2, 200 μM AS, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6) to a final OD600 of 1.0. The suspension was kept in dark for 2–3 h before being infiltrated into fully expanded leaves using needleless 1 mL syringes. For co‐expression of two constructs, A. tumefaciens strains carrying each of the constructs were mixed right before infiltration in equal proportions (1:1) unless stated otherwise.

For chemical treatments, phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 100 μM cycloheximide (CHX) and 2% dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) served as the blank control. The same solution supplemented with 100 μM MG132 (for inhibition of ubiquitination) or 100 μM E64d (for inhibition of autophagy) was infiltrated into the agroinfiltrated leaf patches at 48 h post‐agroinfiltration. Treated plants were kept for an additional 12 h before being sampled for downstream immunoblotting analyses.

RNA Extraction and Northern Blot Assay

Total RNA was extracted from N. benthamiana and areca palm leaves using the Tiangen RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (Tiangen Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA was quantified using the NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then separated in 1.2% agarose gel containing 2% formaldehyde. RNA bands were transferred to the Immobilon‐Ny Transfer Membrane (Millipore) by capillary transfer with 20× SSC buffer and then fixed to the membrane by UV crosslinking. Hybridization and immunological detection of GFP mRNA was performed using the DIG Northern Starter Kit (Roche) following the manufacturer's instructions. Chemiluminescent signals were visualized using the BG‐gdsAUTO710 Mini Imaging System (Baygene).

RT‐qPCR

Reverse transcription of the total RNA was conducted using the Thermo Scientific RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed using the TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech). Actin genes AcActin and NbActin, were selected as the internal controls for areca and N. benthamiana, respectively. At least three biological replicates were used for all experiments and the relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Primer sets used for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S1.

Y2H Assay

Y2H assays were conducted using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two‐Hybrid System (TaKaRa) following the manufacturer's instructions. p26 CDS was cloned into pGBKT7 (BD) to generate BD‐p26, while the full‐length AcSGS3 and each of its four domains were individually cloned to pGADT7 (AD) to generate AD‐AcSGS3, AD‐AcNTD, AD‐AcZF, AD‐AcXS and AD‐AcCC. Recombinant AD and BD vectors, as well as the default controls (AD‐T with BD‐53, or with BD‐Lam) were co‐transformed into Y2HGold competent cells (Weidibio) that were subsequently plated onto SD/−Trp/−Leu double dropout and SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade quadruple dropout plates and incubated at 30°C for 3–4 days.

Protein Extraction Co‐IP and Western Blot Assay

Total protein was extracted from liquid nitrogen‐lyophilized leaf tissue using extraction buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X‐100, 1 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche]). The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C.

For Co‐IP assays, the CDS of GUS‐Myc, AcSGS3‐Myc, and p26‐GFP were inserted between a 35S promoter and an E9 terminator in a binary vector derived from pCAMBIA1300 to generate p35S‐GUS‐Myc, p35S‐AcSGS3‐Myc and p35S‐p26‐GFP, respectively. p35S‐p26‐GFP was transiently co‐expressed with p35S‐GUS‐Myc or p35S‐AcSGS3‐Myc in N. benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. Total protein extracts were mixed with Pierce Anti‐c‐Myc Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at room temperature for 3 h with gentle agitation. The precipitations were washed three times with Tris‐buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) buffer and then analysed by western blotting with anti‐Myc and anti‐GFP antibodies.

For western blotting assays, protein samples were separated by 12% SDS‐PAGE, electroblotted onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane, and detected by immunoblotting. The blots were treated with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using the BG‐gdsAUTO710 Mini Imaging System (Baygene). Statistical analysis of the blots was analysed by the ImageJ software.

BiFC and Confocal Microscopy

For BiFC assays, p26 CDS was cloned to fuse with the C‐terminal YFP in binary vector pFGC‐YC155 to produce YC‐p26, while the full‐length AcSGS3 and each of its four domains were individually cloned to fuse with the N‐terminal YFP in binary vector pFGC‐YN173 to build YN‐AcSGS3, YN‐AcNTD, YN‐AcZF, YN‐AcXS and YN‐AcCC. The recombinant constructs were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. The epidermal cells of infiltrated leaves were examined for YFP fluorescence using the confocal laser scanning microscope LSM 780 (Zeiss) with scan settings for YFP of excitation at 514 nm and emission at 565–585 nm. Images were processed with the LSM software (Zeiss).

LCI Assay

For LCI assays, p26 CDS was cloned to the C‐terminus of luciferase (LUC) to build cLUC‐p26 and AcSGS3 was cloned to the N‐terminus of LUC to build nLUC‐AcSGS3. cLUC‐NbRDR6 and nLUC‐NbSGS3 were constructed with the same vector backbone. The recombinant constructs were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. The leaves were harvested and treated with 1 mM luciferin at 48 hpi, then examined using the NightShade LB 985 In Vivo Plant Imaging System (Berthold Technologies). Images were taken 5 min after exposure to luciferin.

GST Pull‐Down Assay

The AcSGS3 CDS was cloned into the pGEX‐6p‐1 vector, while the AcRDR6 CDS was cloned into the pET‐28a vector. GST‐AcSGS3 and His‐AcRDR6 were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified using a GST‐tag and His‐tag Protein Purification Kit (Beyotime). The pull‐down assay was conducted using the Bio GST‐pulldown kit (Bersin) following the manufacturer's protocol. Proteins bound to the beads were collected, separated by SDS‐PAGE, and analysed by western blot using anti‐GST (TransGen Biotech), anti‐His (TransGen Biotech), and anti‐MBP (ABclonal) antibodies.
