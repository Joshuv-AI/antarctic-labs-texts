Postoperative Changes in Eating Behavior After Sleeve Gastrectomy are Associated With IL-10 Independent of Anthropometric Changes
Authors: Alina Jaroch, Marietta Bracha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObesity is a chronic disease characterized by metainflammation, in which cytokines may influence not only metabolism but also the regulation of eating behaviors. The role of the anti-inflammatory interleukin-10 (IL-10) in modulating behavioral changes following bariatric surgery remains poorly understood. This study aimed to assess the relationship between changes in inflammatory markers and changes in eating behaviors and food preferences following sleeve gastrectomy (LSG).MethodsIn a prospective cohort study, adult patients undergoing LSG (n = 49) were evaluated preoperatively and at 3 and 12 months post-surgery. Measurements included anthropometric parameters, IL-6 and IL-10 concentrations, eating behaviors (TFEQ-13), and food preferences (FPQ).ResultsLSG resulted in significant reductions in body weight, anthropometric measures, and IL-6 (p < 0.0001). Improvements in eating behaviors (reduced emotional and uncontrolled eating) and food preferences (reduced snack intake, p < 0.0001, Kendall’s W = 0.392) were observed. Changes in IL-6 were not associated with eating behaviors. In contrast, changes in IL-10 were significantly associated with changes in starchy food preferences (r = -0.53; p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, ΔIL-10 remained an independent predictor of ΔFPQ starch (B = -0.213; p = 0.003), independent of waist circumference, age, and sex. Anthropometric changes were not significant after correction.ConclusionChanges in eating behavior following LSG were accompanied by changes in inflammatory markers, particularly IL-10. These findings suggest that immune and behavioral adaptations may co-occur during postoperative recovery.Keywords: obesity, cytokines, anthropometry, bariatric surgery, eating behavior

Introduction

Obesity is widely recognized as a chronic disease accompanied by persistent low-grade inflammation, often described as metabolic inflammation or “metainflammation” [1–3]. Unlike acute inflammation, this condition lacks classic clinical symptoms. Still, it involves sustained activation of immune pathways affecting metabolically active tissues such as white adipose tissue (WAT), liver, skeletal muscle, pancreas, intestine, and hypothalamus [4]. Expansion of adipose tissue mass promotes immune cell infiltration and cytokine secretion, leading to increased production of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), as well as alterations in anti-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-10 (IL-10) [5, 6]. IL-10 serves a central immunomodulatory role by limiting excessive inflammatory responses and promoting the resolution of inflammation [7–9]. Notably, IL-10 production has been shown to be reduced in individuals with severe obesity [10]. Beyond its peripheral anti-inflammatory effects, IL-10 may participate in neuroimmune communication pathways that influence central appetite regulation and reward processing [11], suggesting a broader neuro-immuno-metabolic role.

Bariatric surgery, particularly laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), is one of the most effective long-term treatments for severe obesity and its metabolic complications [12, 13]. In addition to inducing substantial and sustained weight loss, LSG is associated with pronounced metabolic and inflammatory changes [14]. Reduction in fat mass contributes to improvements in inflammatory status; however, postoperative cytokine dynamics may not be fully explained by anthropometric reduction. LSG triggers endocrine, neural, and immune adaptations that extend beyond simple fat mass reduction, potentially affecting gut-brain signaling and central appetite pathways [15, 16]. Given the immunoregulatory properties of IL-10 and its decreased baseline levels in obesity, longitudinal changes in IL-10 after LSG may reflect not only restoration of anti-inflammatory balance but also modulation of neurobehavioral mechanisms related to eating behavior.

Eating behavior and food preferences constitute critical determinants of obesity development and postoperative outcomes [17]. Psychological constructs such as emotional and uncontrolled eating - commonly assessed using the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ) - capture vulnerability to affect-driven, dysregulated food intake [18, 19]. Similarly, food preference patterns measured with instruments such as the Food Preference Questionnaire (FPQ) provide insight into hedonic and reward-related aspects of dietary choice [20]. Increasing evidence suggests that inflammatory signaling can influence central neural circuits involved in motivation, reward sensitivity, and energy intake regulation [21]. Therefore, cytokines may act not merely as markers of metabolic improvement but as biological mediators linking immune modulation with behavioral adaptation.

Despite the well-established association between obesity and inflammation, relatively few studies have examined longitudinal relationships between changes in inflammatory markers and postoperative modifications in eating behavior. In particular, the potential role of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, in shaping behavioral and food preference changes after bariatric surgery remains underexplored. Therefore, we hypothesized that (1) LSG would lead to significant reductions in pro-inflammatory markers and changes in eating behavior and food preferences; (2) changes in inflammatory markers would be associated with changes in eating behavior and food preferences, and these associations would remain significant after adjustment for changes in anthropometric measures; (3) changes in anthropometric measures might be associated with changes in eating behavior and food preferences, potentially reflecting a weight-loss driven mechanism.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted between September 2022 and May 2024 in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The local Bioethics Committee approved the study protocol, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The characteristics of the bariatric cohort have been described previously [22]. 49 study participants were enrolled, all adults (age ≥ 18 years) scheduled for a standard laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. Participants were recruited prospectively and consecutively from the bariatric surgical clinic. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Exclusion criteria were severe untreated diseases (e.g., psychiatric disorders, addiction to alcohol or drugs, cancer, acute inflammatory diseases) and use of any pharmacological management of obesity, not comorbidities. Anthropometric, biochemical, eating behavior, and food preference assessments were performed 1 week prior to surgery (baseline) and at 3 and 12 months postoperatively. Of the 49 patients included in the study, blood samples were collected from 45 participants. Complete biochemical profiling was successfully obtained from 39 patients (exclusion due to hemolysis or insufficient sample volume).

Blood Biochemical Analysis

Venous blood samples were obtained from participants after an overnight fast of at least 12 h. Blood was drawn from the antecubital vein into 6 mL EDTA-containing collection tubes. Samples were processed within one hour of collection by centrifugation at 377 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting plasma was divided into 400-µL aliquots, transferred into sterile microcentrifuge tubes, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Plasma concentrations of high-sensitivity interleukin-10 (hsIL-10) and high-sensitivity interleukin-6 (hsIL-6) were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Cloud-Clone Corp., Katy, TX, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocols. The analytical performance of the assays demonstrated intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 10% and 12%, respectively, for both cytokines. The lower limits of detection were < 0.61 pg/mL for hsIL-10 and < 0.31 pg/mL for hsIL-6. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a SPECTROstar Nano microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany).

Anthropometric Measurements

Anthropometric measurements were made in accordance with the principles of nutritional assessment [23]. Height was measured with a portable height measure stadiometer (Seca 213).

Due to changes in equipment availability during the study period, weight and body fat percentage (%BF) were assessed using the InBody 570 at baseline and at 3 months, and the Tanita MC780 MA at the 12-month follow-up. Both devices use segmental bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and are widely used for body composition assessment in clinical practice. While absolute body fat percentage (%BF) values may vary slightly between devices due to methodological differences, the overall direction and magnitude of change observed over the study period remain clinically interpretable. BMI was calculated according to the World Health Organization (WHO) formula. Waist circumference was measured using anthropometric tape with a millimeter scale SECA 201. Waist-to-height ratio was calculated. The 1-year postoperative %EWL was calculated according to other authors: (weight at baseline – weight at 12 months follow-up) / (weight at baseline − ideal body weight) × 100. Ideal body weight (IBW) was calculated from Broca’s formula (height − 100) depending on the participant’s gender (female: subtract 10% of the calculated value, male: subtract 5%) [24].

Eating Behaviors and Food Choices

The Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ) was originally developed by Stunkard and Messick (1985) to assess three dimensions of eating behavior: dietary restraint, disinhibition, and hunger [18]. The questionnaire measures cognitive-behavioral and emotional aspects of eating-related behaviors. TFEQ has been successfully applied in studies involving patients with obesity [25]. In the present study, a short 13-item version of the questionnaire (TFEQ-13) was used, which has been validated for the Polish population [26]. The TFEQ-13 includes three subscales, which in this study were coded as follows:

Cognitive Restraint of Eating (TFEQ-R) – assesses behaviors related to restricting the amount or type of food consumed to control body weight and body image (items Q1, Q9, Q10, Q12, Q13).

Cognitive Restraint of Eating (TFEQ-R) – assesses behaviors related to restricting the amount or type of food consumed to control body weight and body image (items Q1, Q9, Q10, Q12, Q13).

Uncontrolled Eating (TFEQ-J) – reflects the tendency to eat more than usual due to a loss of control over eating or an intense feeling of hunger that may lead to episodes of overeating (items Q3, Q5–Q7, Q11).

Uncontrolled Eating (TFEQ-J) – reflects the tendency to eat more than usual due to a loss of control over eating or an intense feeling of hunger that may lead to episodes of overeating (items Q3, Q5–Q7, Q11).

Emotional Eating (TFEQ-E) – measures episodes of overeating triggered by negative emotions such as low mood, stress, or anxiety (items Q2, Q4, Q8).

Emotional Eating (TFEQ-E) – measures episodes of overeating triggered by negative emotions such as low mood, stress, or anxiety (items Q2, Q4, Q8).

Responses in the TFEQ-13 are provided on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 to 3 (excluding Q13). Scores are calculated separately for each subscale, and no overall total score is computed. Higher scores on a given subscale indicate a greater intensity of the respective eating behavior dimension.

The Food Preference Questionnaire (FPQ) is a research tool used to assess the degree to which respondents like or dislike various food products and food categories. FPQ, developed and validated by Smith et al. [27], comprises the following categories of food products: vegetables (FPQ veggies; 18 items), fruit (FPQ fruit; 7 items), meat/fish (FPQ meat; 12 items), dairy (FPQ dairy; 10 items), snacks (FPQ snacks; 9 items), and starches (FPQ starch; 6 items). Preference for each item is rated using a 5-point Likert scale (0 = not applicable, 1 = dislike a lot, 2 = dislike a little, 3 = neither like nor dislike, 4 = like a little, 5 = like a lot). Preferences for each food category are calculated by summing the scores assigned to the items in that category.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).

Longitudinal changes across baseline, 3 months, and 12 months were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Friedman test for non-normally distributed variables. Significant main effects were followed by appropriate pairwise post hoc tests with the Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (η²p) for ANOVA and Kendall’s W for Friedman tests.

Changes (Δ) were calculated between baseline and 12 months. Associations between changes in inflammatory markers, anthropometric measures, eating behavior (TFEQ), and food preferences (FPQ) were assessed using Spearman correlations with correction for multiple comparisons. Multivariable linear regression was performed to examine whether ΔIL-10 independently predicted ΔFPQ starch, adjusting for Δwaist circumference, age, and sex. Regression assumptions were verified by inspection of residual plots. Influential observations were identified using standardized residuals (> |3|). A sensitivity analysis was performed after exclusion of one influential case to confirm the robustness of the results. Anthropometric variables were not included in further models as their associations did not remain significant after correction.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA 13.3 (StatSoft, Palo Alto, USA).

A significant effect of time was observed for IL-6 concentrations (Friedman test: χ²(2) = 78.00, p < 0.001, Kendall’s W = 1.00), indicating a very strong effect size and highly consistent rank changes across time points. Median IL-6 levels decreased from 1.142 pg/mL (IQR 0.972–1.310) at baseline to 0.16 pg/mL (0.10–0.21) at the 12-month follow-up, suggesting a substantial reduction in systemic inflammation after LSG. IL-10 concentrations also changed significantly (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (W = 0.750), indicating dynamic changes in anti-inflammatory signaling during the postoperative period.

Emotional eating (TFEQ-E) and uncontrolled eating (TFEQ-J) decreased significantly during follow-up (both p < 0.001), with moderate effect sizes (W = 0.501 and W = 0.596, respectively), indicating improved control over eating and reduced emotionally driven food intake. In contrast, cognitive restraint of eating (TFEQ-R) remained stable throughout the study period (p = 0.705; W = 0.008), suggesting no meaningful change in dietary restraint. Snack food preference showed the most pronounced reduction (p < 0.001; W = 0.392). Significant, but small effects, were also observed for meat, dairy, vegetables, and starch products (W = 0.070–0.153), while fruit preference did not change (p = 0.558; W = 0.013).

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons following the Friedman test further clarified the observed time effects. Emotional eating (TFEQ-E) and uncontrolled eating (TFEQ-J) significantly decreased over time (both p < 0.001). Post-hoc analyses revealed marked reductions from baseline at 3 and 12 months (both p < 0.001), with no difference between 3 and 12 months, indicating sustained improvement. In contrast, cognitive restraint (TFEQ-R) remained stable throughout the study period, with no significant pairwise differences. Among food preferences, snack and dairy consumption significantly decreased over time. Snack preference declined markedly from baseline to both 3 and 12 months (both p < 0.001), while dairy preference was reduced at 3 months (p = 0.001) and remained lower at 12 months (p = 0.043). To examine whether changes in inflammatory markers were associated with changes in eating behavior and food preferences, regression and correlation analyses were performed. Correlation analysis showed that changes in IL-6 were not significantly associated with changes in eating behavior or food preferences. In contrast, a moderate positive correlation was observed between ΔIL-10 and ΔTFEQ-J (r = 0.36, p = 0.031), and a strong negative correlation between ΔIL-10 and ΔFPQ starch (r = -0.53, p < 0.001). After Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, only the association between ΔIL-10 and ΔFPQ starch remained statistically significant (p = 0.0007). To examine whether changes in inflammatory markers were independently associated with changes in food preferences, a multivariable linear regression model was constructed with ΔFPQ starch as the dependent variable. ΔIL-10 was included as the primary predictor, with Δwaist circumference (as a proxy for visceral fat accumulation, metabolic inflammation, and cytokine activity), age, and sex as covariates. Inspection of model residuals identified one influential observation (standardized residual > 3). Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding this case (Table 2).

Finally, we examined whether changes in anthropometric measures were associated with changes in eating behaviors and food preferences after LSG. Spearman correlation analyses revealed nominally significant associations between ΔBMI and ΔTFEQ-J (r = 0.35, p = 0.017), as well as between Δweight and ΔFPQ fruit (r = -0.34, p = 0.021). However, these associations did not remain significant after false discovery rate correction for multiple testing. No significant correlations were observed for Δwaist circumference or Δbody fat percentage. Therefore, multivariable regression analyses were not performed for anthropometric predictors.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) is associated with significant improvements in inflammatory markers, eating behavior, and food preferences. However, only changes in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 were associated with postoperative modifications in food preferences, and these associations remained significant after adjustment for anthropometric variables. In contrast, changes in anthropometric measures were not independently associated with behavioral outcomes after correction for multiple testing. Notably, IL-10 emerged as an independent predictor of changes in starch preference, suggesting a specific role of anti-inflammatory pathways in modulating postoperative eating behavior. Collectively, these findings indicate that postoperative adaptations in eating behavior co-occur with immunological changes, particularly involving IL-10, independent of anthropometric changes.

The first finding from this study concerns significant improvements in eating behavior, including reductions in emotional and uncontrolled eating after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. Interestingly, the cognitive restraint domain of the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire remained unchanged. Other researchers have also reported that bariatric surgery is associated with a reduction in maladaptive and uncontrolled eating behaviors, particularly those related to loss of control and emotional eating [28, 29]. Studies using the TFEQ questionnaire further confirmed that emotional and uncontrolled eating are common characteristics in bariatric populations, and are associated with postoperative outcomes, including weight regain, and impact quality of life [30]. The lack of change in cognitive restraint observed in our patients contrasts with reports suggesting that cognitive restraint increases postoperatively [31], and higher levels of restraint may be associated with greater postoperative weight loss [32]. However, in patients undergoing bariatric surgery, cognitive response has shown inconsistent results, with either an increase or no change in other studies [33]. It appears to play a less consistent role in postoperative outcomes, suggesting that improvements in eating behavior are not solely dependent on conscious dietary control [34].

In terms of food preferences, a significant reduction in the preference for highly palatable snack foods, with a moderate effect (p < 0.001, Kendall’s W = 0.392), was observed. The snacks group consisted of nine food products (chips, plain biscuits, chocolate biscuits, cake, ice cream, chocolate, crisps, gummy sweets, sugared cereal), sweet or salty in taste. Significant reductions were also observed in meat, vegetables, dairy, and starch consumption, but the effects were small, suggesting an overall reduction in food intake. Meta-analyses and reviews show that preference for unhealthy foods decreases after bariatric surgery [35, 36]. However, in the long term (≥ 12 months after surgery), no changes are observed [37], a trend also seen for beverages and sweets [38]. Despite considerable differences in results, studies generally show a tendency for patients to choose healthier foods over unhealthy ones (i.e., foods high in simple sugars, fat, and energy density) [39] during the first 6–12 months after bariatric surgery [38, 40]. Interestingly, even postoperative hedonic ratings for high-fat and high-carbohydrate foods decrease, especially following LSG [41]. Both sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass have been shown to reduce hedonic hunger and problematic eating behaviors, likely through a combination of increased satiety, reduced hunger, altered taste perception, gastrointestinal symptoms, and decreased reward responses to food cues [42]. It has been shown that bariatric surgery reduces feelings of hunger and increases postprandial fullness, leading to a spontaneous reduction in calorie intake without the need for deliberate dietary restriction [43]. These effects are accompanied by a marked reduction in portion size and total caloric intake, as measured by dietary energy density (DED, kcal/g). This decrease appeared early after surgery (6 weeks) and contributed to a sustained negative energy balance, even after weight stabilization [44]. Furthermore, reduced activity of the reward system in response to fatty and sweet foods may promote weight loss and the maintenance of new eating habits [38].

Although IL-10 levels are inversely associated with BMI and body fat content, results from studies of patients who have undergone bariatric surgery are inconsistent, with some reporting increases and others decreases depending on follow-up duration and metabolic context [6, 45]. These variations suggest that IL-10 dynamics may be linked to adipose tissue remodeling rather than simply reflecting reductions in fat mass after bariatric surgery. In the present study, multivariable analysis revealed that increased IL-10 was independently associated with a reduced preference for starch-rich foods (bread, bran cereal, porridge, rice, wheat cereal, and rice or corn cereal) after adjustment for age, sex, and waist circumference. In addition to its well-described immunomodulatory properties, IL-10 has been implicated in neuroimmune communication pathways linking peripheral immune status to central mechanisms of appetite regulation and reward processing [11]. Improvements in metabolic status accompanied by reduced obesity-related inflammation have been shown to influence appetite control and food-related behaviors through gut–brain signaling pathways [43, 46]. Moreover, dietary composition may modulate IL-10 expression, particularly through carbohydrate quality and fiber intake, further supporting a bidirectional relationship between immune signaling and food preferences [40]. Taken together, the observed association between IL-10 and starch preference may reflect an interaction between immune recovery, metabolic regulation, and behavioral adaptation, suggesting that IL-10 may serve as a marker of broader postoperative neuroimmune and metabolic adaptation. The observed association between IL-10 and starch preference persisted after adjustment for waist circumference, a proxy for visceral fat distribution. Body fat percentage (%BF) decreased significantly (p < 0.0001) but was not independently associated with eating behavior changes after FDR correction, suggesting that the IL-10 signal is not merely a reflection of adipose tissue mass reduction. The waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) also decreased significantly but did not predict behavioral outcomes. This dissociation between anthropometric and behavioral changes suggests that postoperative immunological and behavioral adaptations may co-occur independently of changes in overall adiposity or fat distribution.

Beyond cytokines, adipokines such as leptin may also contribute to postoperative regulation of appetite and food-related behavior through integrated metabolic and immune pathways [47]. More broadly, adipose tissue-derived signaling molecules constitute a complex regulatory network [48] that extends beyond the inflammatory markers assessed in the present study.

This study provides significant findings, however it has certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First and foremost, the relatively small sample size makes it difficult to generalize the findings to the entire population of patients undergoing bariatric surgery, and the observational nature of the study allows only for the identification of correlations between the variables under study. In addition, the cohort was predominantly female, limiting the feasibility of sex-stratified analyses. It is also worth noting that the dietary behavior data were based on patients’ subjective assessments (self-reported questionnaires). Additionally, the analysis of the cytokine profile was limited to IL-10 and IL-6; combined with the absence of measurements of hormonal axis parameters, this does not allow for a full elucidation of the complex neuroendocrine mechanisms following LSG surgery. It is also worth highlighting the strengths of the presented study. A key advantage is its longitudinal design, which includes measurements at three time points, enabling a reliable assessment of changes in the same patients over the year following surgery. Another strength is the multidimensional approach to patient assessment, combining anthropometric and biochemical parameters with dietary questionnaire data, which provides a broad analytical context. Also noteworthy are the very high participant retention rate and the negligible dropout rate, which significantly enhance the reliability of the collected evidence and minimize the risk of error from missing observations.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that postoperative changes in eating behavior following laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy co-occur with immunological adaptations, particularly changes in IL-10, independent of anthropometric changes. In particular, IL-10 emerged as an independent predictor of changes in food preferences, suggesting a potential role of anti-inflammatory signaling in the regulation of dietary behavior. These findings support the concept that bariatric surgery induces complex neuroimmune and metabolic interactions that extend beyond weight loss and may contribute to behavioral adaptation. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the mechanistic pathways linking immune modulation with appetite regulation and food preferences.
