In vivo and in silico wound healing potential of Cannabis Sativa seed oil through inflammation mediators
Authors: Sinem İnal, Kamil Serdar İnal, Fehmi Metehan Benli, Sedat Gökmen, Berrin Şentürk, Pelin Kasap, Şahane Funda Arslanoğlu, Tolga Güvenç, Dilek Güvenç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates the wound healing potential of Cannabis Sativa seed oil (CSSO), derived from the industrial hemp variety ‘NARLI’. The rich essential fatty acid profile of CSSO presents promising therapeutic opportunities; however, its specific in vivo efficacy and targeted molecular mechanisms in wound management remain underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the in vivo tissue regeneration dynamics and the in silico anti-inflammatory mechanisms of CSSO derived from the ‘NARLI’ hemp variety in an excision wound model.MethodsUsing an excision wound model, 42 rats were divided into two groups: control (untreated) and CSSO-treated. Wound healing was assessed through clinical wound area measurement, histopathological evaluation, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and molecular docking analyses. Wound area measurements were taken on days 7, 14, and 21.ResultsOn day 21, CSSO-treated animals showed a significantly higher wound closure rate (93%) compared to the control group (87.55%) (p = 0.005). Histopathological analysis revealed enhanced neovascularization, increased collagen deposition (p = 0.008), reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (p = 0.020), and increased epithelial proliferation in the CSSO group. Immunohistochemistry findings showed a marked decrease in proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β (p = 0.023) and TGF-β (p = 0.030), and a notable upregulation of angiogenesis and proliferation markers VEGF (p = 0.031) and Ki-67 (p = 0.001). Molecular docking analyses revealed that CSSO-derived fatty acids showed binding affinities (-5.3 to -7.5 kcal/mol) with anti-inflammatory-related proteins (COX-2 and NLRP3) and (-3.1 to -6.1 kcal/mol) binding affinities with wound healing-related proteins (SIRT1 and GSK3β), suggesting a possible mechanistic basis underlying the wound healing potential of CSSO.ConclusionsTopical application of CSSO was associated with improved wound healing outcomes in rats, including enhanced wound closure and favourable histopathological and immunohistochemical changes. Introduction

A wound is defined as a disruption of the anatomical and cellular integrity or continuity of normal skin tissue [1].

Wounds, which may occur accidentally (e.g., trauma) or be created intentionally through surgical incision [2], are commonly categorized according to their duration (acute or chronic) or underlying etiology. Acute wounds arise suddenly and include those caused by surgical incisions, traumatic injuries, or heat exposure, whereas chronic wounds may arise from conditions such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, pressure injuries, and vascular pathologies associated with aging. Causal classifications include pressure injuries, trauma-related wounds, venous leg ulcers, and diabetic foot ulcers [3].

Wound healing is regarded as a tremendously complicated and regulated process, including different cytohumoral events following an injury [4], . The process is typically divided into four phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling ([5, 6]. During wound healing, blood cells, extracellular matrix, and epidermal cells play a major role [7].

Wound healing is a multifaceted biological process orchestrated by the coordinated activity of various cellular components—including neutrophils, macrophages, and fibroblasts—as well as a complex network of signaling molecules such as pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors. Key events during the healing phase, such as angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling, are regulated by factors including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor (TGF), interleukins (IL), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) [8]. Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 are critical mediators of the inflammatory phase of healing. Vascular endothelial growth factor is involved in the formation of new blood vessels. Epidermal keratinocytes are key to extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, skin regeneration, and the secretion of growth factors [9].

In many developing countries, socioeconomic factors and the rising costs of veterinary health services have increased interest in using medicinal plants as alternative treatments for addressing health problems. It has been reported that the use of medicinal plant production contributes significantly to employment, the gross national product, and export potential [10].

Herbal remedies such as Phlomis russeliana [11], Aloe vera [12, 13], Arctium lappa [14], Calendula officinalis [15], Curcuma longa [16], Hypericum perforatum oil, sage, and oregano essential oils [17] have been widely used for promoting wound healing. However, many of these traditional herbal treatments may be limited by variability in their bioactive and lipid content, which can affect their reproducibility and therapeutic consistency. The biological activity of plant oils is closely related to their fatty acid composition, especially the presence of essential fatty acids such as linoleic acid, which is crucial for maintaining skin barrier integrity [18]. In this context, oils with balanced essential fatty acid profiles, such as Cannabis sativa seed oil, may offer potential advantages.

Cannabis sativa var. sativa is a medicinal and aromatic herb from the Cannabaceae family that thrives in temperate and tropical climates worldwide [19]. Various parts of the hemp plant—including seeds, stems, leaves, flowers, trichomes, and oils—contain phytochemicals with diverse biological properties such as cytotoxic, antimicrobial, antioxidant, antihypertensive, and anti-inflammatory effects. These properties make hemp particularly valuable in treating burns and insect bites and developing topical applications [20]. Cannabis sativa seed oil contains abundant flavonoids, terpenes, carotenoids, chlorophylls, and phytosterols—compounds with well-established anti-inflammatory and anti-aging properties. The oil is easily absorbed by the skin and does not block pores, making it an excellent ingredient in a range of skin-calming products such as sunscreens, creams, and lotions. Topical applications like lotions and ointments formulated with hemp oil show great potential in anti-aging skin care [20, 21]. Cannabis sativa seed oil is a plant-derived lipid source characterized by a high content of essential fatty acids, particularly linoleic acid (54–60%), α-linolenic acid (18–23%), and oleic acid (7–12%), which collectively contribute to a physiologically relevant fatty acid profile [20]. The biological effects of plant-derived lipids are largely influenced by their fatty acid composition, with essential fatty acids such as linoleic acid playing a key role in maintaining epidermal barrier integrity and regulating inflammatory responses [21]. Recent studies have substantiated the therapeutic potential of natural antioxidants—including linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid (polyunsaturated fatty acids, PUFAs), as well as oleic acid (a monounsaturated fatty acid, MUFA), flavonoids, and phenolic compounds—in mitigating burn-induced inflammation and accelerating wound healing. PUFAs, in particular, appear to modulate systemic inflammatory responses by influencing a range of immunological and inflammatory mechanisms, including leukocyte chemotaxis, the expression of adhesion molecules, leukocyte-endothelial interactions, platelet aggregation, and vasodilation, among other processes [22]. Hemp seed oil is a plant-derived lipid source with a well-balanced essential fatty acid profile, which may support skin barrier restoration and help regulate inflammation during tissue repair [20, 22].

Cannabis sativa seed oil and its bioactive constituents have been shown to influence key biological processes involved in wound healing, including inflammation, cell migration, and proliferation [21, 23]. In addition, in vitro studies using Cannabis sativa extracts and cannabidiol have demonstrated significant reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8, alongside measurable increases in fibroblast migration and wound closure in scratch assay models, primarily through inhibition of the NF-κB signalling pathway in human keratinocytes and fibroblasts [24, 25]. Furthermore, bioactive peptides derived from Cannabis sativa seeds, particularly WVYY and PSLPA, activate the Nrf2/ARE signalling pathway in human keratinocytes. This enhances antioxidant defenses and increases cell migration, which are critical processes for effective wound healing [26]. Supporting these cellular findings, topical application of Cannabis sativa seed oil has been shown to accelerate wound closure and enhance granulation tissue formation in vivo models. Specifically, murine full-thickness wounds exhibit increased epithelialization and vascularization, whereas burn wounds in rats show enhanced fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition [23, 27]. The incorporation of Cannabis sativa seed oil into collagen-based wound dressings has been reported to improve biocompatibility and provide antioxidant and antimicrobial effects, thereby supporting its potential role in tissue regeneration [28]. These findings suggest that Cannabis sativa seed oil represents a promising candidate for wound healing applications. However, its effects on the coordinated regulation of inflammation, proliferation, and angiogenesis have not yet been fully characterized under standardized in vivo experimental conditions.

The present study aimed to evaluate the roles of these processes in wound healing using an excisional wound model, assessed through histochemical and immunohistochemical analyses. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms underlying the wound healing potential of CSSO-derived fatty acids were investigated via molecular docking analyses targeting Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 Beta (GSK3β) due to their pivotal role in collagen synthesis, tissue repair, and regeneration [29, 30]. In addition, the anti-inflammatory profile of CSSO-derived fatty acids was evaluated against cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) since they are key proteins involved in inflammation, proliferation, and epithelial migration during skin repair, making them significant therapeutic targets in wound healing [31, 32]. The experimental findings demonstrated notable alterations in the expression profiles of key proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), growth factors (TGF-β, VEGF), and proliferation marker (Ki-67).

Material and method

Materials and reagents

Cannabis sativa seeds (‘NARLI’ variety) were obtained from Ondokuz Mayıs University Hemp Research Institute (Samsun, Türkiye). For the surgical procedures, local analgesia and anesthetics (2% Lidocaine, Ketamine hydrochloride, and Xylazine) were purchased from [Adeka and Interhas, Türkiye]. For the immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses, primary antibodies against IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β were supplied by Affinity Biosciences (China) VEGF (Shanghai Coon Koon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and Ki-67 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), an immunohistochemistry (IHC) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States). Routine histological dyes and tissue processing chemicals were procured from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Preparation of CSSO

To obtain cold-pressed CSSO, seeds of the registered industrial hemp variety ‘NARLI’ were used. This variety was developed through breeding studies at Ondokuz Mayis University and is officially listed in the National Variety List. The seeds used in this study were harvested in 2023 and obtained from the Ondokuz Mayis University Hemp Research Institute (Samsun, Türkiye) [33]. The botanical identity and authenticity of the cultivar were verified by Prof. Dr. Şahane Funda Arslanoğlu (Ondokuz Mayıs University Hemp Research Institute, Samsun, Türkiye). A voucher specimen of seeds of Cannabis sativa var. sativa (‘NARLI’ variety) was deposited in the Herbarium of the Hemp Research Institute, Ondokuz Mayıs University, Samsun, Türkiye, under voucher number KAE-2023-05. The cultivar had previously been officially registered by the Variety Registration and Seed Certification Centre (Ankara, Türkiye) on March 11, 2021, following Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability (DUS) testing based on morphological and technological characteristics. According to the official registration data, ‘NARLI’ is characterized by its absent or very low THC content (0.024%), a CBD content of 0.80%, and a 1000-seed weight of approximately 17.6 g [33]. The seeds were stored at + 4°C under 30% relative humidity until use.

After sieving and cleaning, the seeds were cold-pressed using the oil extraction unit at the Hemp Research Institute to obtain crude oil. The crude oil was filtered twice through filter paper to remove any remaining residues. It was subsequently stored in a dark glass bottle at + 4 °C, protected from light, until analysis. Fatty acid composition analyses were carried out on samples taken from this crude oil.

Content analysis of CSSO

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

The content analysis of CSSO was conducted using Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS), in accordance with the methodology outlined by Arena et al. [34].

Sample preparation: A 100 mg sample of CSSO was converted into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) via alkaline transesterification using 200 µL of 2 M KOH in methanol and 2 mL of n-heptane at room temperature. This reaction enables the cleavage of triglycerides and the formation of methyl esters of fatty acids. The FAME-containing organic phase was collected and directly analyzed by GC–MS (Shimadzu, Duisburg, Germany) with a split-splitless injector set (280 °C) and an autosampler. Separation was performed using a Merck Life Science inert cap 1MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) with the oven temperature programmed from 50 °C to 280 °C at 3.0 °C/min, and an injection volume of 0.5 µL. The carrier gas was helium, maintained at an average linear velocity of 30 cm/s and an initial inlet pressure of 26.6 kPa. Mass spectrometry parameters were configured with a mass range of 40–550 amu, an ion source temperature of 220 °C, an interface temperature of 250 °C, and an event time of 0.20 s. Data were analyzed using GC-MS solution software. Identification was carried out using the LIPIDS Mass Spectral Library version 1.0 (Shimadzu Europa, Duisburg, Germany), with LRI values determined on the SLB-IL60 column by injecting pure standards and a C4–C24 standard mixture as the reference homologous series, Peak assignment was based on an MS spectral similarity higher than 85% and a tolerance window of ± 10 LRI. Analyses were repeated and quantitative results expressed as the mean of area percentages.

Animals

Forty-two male Wistar albino rats, aged 3–4 months and weighing 220–250 g, were used in the study. All rats were obtained from Ondokuz Mayıs University Experimental Animal Application and Research Center, and the study was approved by Ondokuz Mayıs University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (decision number: 2022/49). The rats were individually housed in polycarbonate cages under a ventilated laboratory with filtered air at 23 ± 1 °C, 12/12 h light/dark cycle, and relative humidity (50 ± 5%) and fed ad libitum. The rats were randomly divided into two groups with 21 rats in each group: as control (excision without treatment) and CSSO (excision followed by treatment with CSSO) group.

Experimental design and treatment

The excisional wound model was established following the technique described by Dorsett-Martin [35]. After the animals were anaesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (i.p., 50/5 mg/kg), and the dorsal hair was shaved with a hair clipper, asepsis was achieved. A full-thickness experimental wound model was created by making a 1.5 cm diameter circular skin excision from the dorsal region under local analgesia (2% lidocaine, subcutaneous). Wounds were photographed using a digital camera. The control group received no treatment following wound creation. In the CSSO groups, the topical dose was determined based on complete wound surface coverage and applied directly to the wound bed using a micropipette, totally 250 µL. Treatment was initiated immediately after wound creation and was administered once daily at approximately the same time each day. Before each reapplication, the wound area was gently cleansed with sterile physiological saline (0.9% NaCl) to remove residual oil, debris, or exudate, ensuring optimal absorption of the freshly applied oil. This procedure was continued for 7, 14, or 21 consecutive days, corresponding to the predefined evaluation and sacrifice time points. On days 7, 14, and 21 post-wounding, the wound area of seven randomly selected rats from each group was measured.

Animals were monitored daily throughout the study for signs of pain, distress, infection, impaired mobility, and wound-related complications. Humane endpoints were predefined and included body weight loss exceeding 20%, severe wound infection, persistent bleeding, self-mutilation, inability to access food or water, or signs of severe pain or distress unresponsive to supportive care. Animals meeting any of these criteria would have been humanely euthanized before the scheduled study endpoint. No animals met the predefined humane endpoint criteria during the study.

Euthanasia was performed by an intraperitoneal injection of a high-dose combination of ketamine (100 mg/kg, Ketasol 10%, Interhas, Türkiye) and xylazine (10 mg/kg, Xylazinebio 2%, Interhas, Türkiye). This dose was selected to ensure deep anesthesia and loss of consciousness before sacrifice. Animals were monitored for the absence of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes to confirm deep anesthesia and loss of consciousness prior to tissue collection. Death was confirmed by the cessation of respiration, absence of heartbeat, and lack of corneal reflexes.

Wound closure

Wound sites of all animals were photographed and evaluated using a digital camera on days 7, 14 and 21. For clinical evaluation, wound closure was measured and calculated using the Image J software (version: 1.54 g).

Histopathology analysis

At the end of the 21-day experimental period, biopsy samples, including the wound and surrounding skin, were collected and fixed in a 10% buffered formalin solution. The tissues were processed through alcohol and xylene series and paraffin blocks were made with a paraffin dispenser unit (Leica, EG1150). Sections of 5 μm thickness were obtained using a microtome (Leica, RM2125 RTS). The sections were stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (HxE) for histopathologic examination, and wound healing was assessed under a light microscope. The histopathological assessment was carried out using four different criteria (reepithelization, inflammatory cell infiltration, neovascularization and collagen deposition), employing a modified version of the histological scoring system defined by Abramov (Table 1) [36].

New collagen formation was evaluated histochemically using Masson’s trichrome staining method [37]. To objectively evaluate tissue regeneration, a quantitative analysis of collagen proliferation was performed using Image J software (version: 1.54 g). Following the methodology demonstrated by Roy et al. [38], histological images were systematically analysed to determine the collagen density. Briefly, high-resolution images of the stained tissue sections were imported into the software, and appropriate colour thresholding techniques were applied to isolate and quantify the collagen-positive areas specifically. The extent of collagen proliferation was then calculated as the percentage of the positively stained area relative to the total tissue area in each field of view.

Immunohistochemical staining

For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, primary antibodies targeting proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α (AF7014, Affinity, 1:50), IL-1β (DF6893, Affinity, 1:50), IL-6 (DF6087, Affinity, 1:500) and growth factors TGF-β (AF1027, Affinity, 1:250), VEGF (Shanghai Coon Koon Biotech, 1:100) as well as the proliferation marker Ki-67 (AB9260, Merck, 1:100) were used. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the streptavidin–biotin–immunoperoxidase complex technique according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol (Lab Vision™ UltraVision™ LP Detection System, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tissue sections were initially deparaffinized in xylene and subsequently rehydrated through a series of graded alcohol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave oven at 600 watts for 20 min to unmask antigenic sites and then exposed to 0.5% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After protein blocking, the sections were incubated with appropriately diluted primary antibodies either for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C. Chromogen development was performed with AEC (AEC Substrate Kit-ab64252), and counterstaining was done using Mayer’s hematoxylin. Sections were mounted with CC/Mount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma-Aldrich, C9368) and examined under a Nikon Eclipse E600 light microscope.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for TGF-β, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 was semi-quantitatively scored as follows: 0 = no staining, 1 = weak staining, 2 = moderate staining, and 3 = intense staining. VEGF expression was evaluated by determining the mean number of microvessels with positive VEGF immunoreactivity. The Ki-67 labelling index was calculated as the percentage of Ki-67-positive epithelial cells among the total epithelial cells examined under a light microscope.

Retrieval of CSSO-derived fatty acids and target proteins

The 3D structures of each fatty acid found in CSSO, identified through GC-MS analysis, were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (Table S1).

Homology modelling of target proteins

The initial structures of rat COX-2 and NLRP3 proteins were generated using a homology modelling approach, as no experimentally determined 3D structures are currently available for these target proteins in rats. The primary sequences of rat COX-2 (UniProt ID: P35355, Rattus norvegicus) and rat NLRP3 (UniProt ID: D4A523, Rattus norvegicus) were retrieved from the UniProt web server (https://www.uniprot.org). Template structures were selected based on primary sequence identity, experimental method, and resolution.

For rat NLRP3, the cryo-EM structure of mouse NLRP3 (full-length dodecamer; PDB ID: 7VTQ) was selected as the template due to its high resolution (3.55 Å) and high sequence identity (96.34%) [39], . The template selected for rat COX-2 was PDB ID: 3LN1, representing the structure of COX-2 with celecoxib bound at its active site. This template was chosen due to its high sequence identity (96.25%) and favorable resolution, 2.40 Å [40]. The 3D structures of the proteins were generated using the Swiss-Model Homology Modeler [41]. The models were evaluated based on the Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE), and Quaternary Structure Quality Estimation (QSQE) scores, and visualized using UCSF Chimera [42]. For GSK3β and SIRT1 proteins, 3D experimental structures were obtained from the protein data bank. The 3D crystal structures of GSK3β (PDB ID: 1Q5K, 1.94 Å) and the 3D crystal structure of SIRT1 in complex with an activator and substrate, determine determined by X-ray diffraction with 2.74 Å were retrieved from the protein data bank (PDB ID: 4ZZJ) [43, 44].

Molecular docking

The target protein structures obtained through homology modeling were used because they provided clean models free of water molecules and non-standard residues. The docking preparation protocol, including the addition of hydrogen atoms and charge assignment, was performed using the AMBER ff14SB force field with the DockPrep tool integrated in UCSF Chimera [42]. The binding pockets of each target protein were identified based on their respective template structures, as these regions are highly conserved across species. The structures of each CSSO-derived fatty acid were prepared by adding hydrogen atoms and assigning Gasteiger charges to ensure proper protonation states and accurate geometry. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method for 100 steps, followed by the conjugate gradient method for 10 steps. Molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina with 10 binding modes per ligand, an exhaustiveness value of 8, and a maximum energy difference of 3 kcal/mol [45]. Interactions between CSSO-derived fatty acids and target proteins were illustrated using the PLIP web server (https://bio.tools/plip) [46], .

HYDE scoring

To further investigate binding affinity, secondary scoring was conducted using the HYDE method implemented in SeeSAR version 12.0.1 (www.biosolveit.de/SeeSAR). HYDE scoring estimates binding affinities by balancing hydrogen bonding and desolvation effects. While HYDE rewards favorable hydrogen bonding between ligands and residues, dehydration (desolvation) penalizes binding affinity. In addition to estimating binding affinity, SeeSAR visualizes the predicted binding free energy of individual atoms using coronas that range in color from green to red, indicating favorable and unfavorable contributions to the overall binding affinity [47].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0 package program. Ki-67 and VEGF immunohistochemical stainings were compared between control and CSSO groups by independent two sample t test. In addition, the wound area percentage was also evaluated according to the two-sample t test. IL-6 scores were compared using the two-sample independent t test. Furthermore, fibrosis scores evaluated on days 7, 14, and 21 were compared between the control and CSSO groups using the independent two sample t test. TGF-β, IL-1β, and TNF-α scores were compared according to the Mann-Whitney U test. Each of the histopathological evaluation criteria (epithelialization, inflammation, vascularization) was also evaluated according to the Mann-Whitney U test. For parametric tests, all results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for each group, and for non-parametric tests, they were expressed as median (minimum-maximum) values. For statistical evaluations, p < 0.05 was used as significance criterion.

Discussion

‘NARLI’ hemp variety is characterized by intensely green leaves, moderate anthocyanin density in male flowers, and either no or very low tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) content. It is an industrial variety suitable for fiber production, offering high fiber quality and long stem length [33].

Cannabis sativa seed oil is extracted from the seeds of Cannabis sativa and is thought to have a range of biological effects due to its active constituents. Key components of CSSO have been shown to provide therapeutic benefits for conditions such as eczema and burns, as well as contributing to the reduction of hypertension and cholesterol levels [23, 48, 49]. It is particularly rich in essential fatty acids such as linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, omega-3), with an optimum ratio of (3:1) [50]. The predominant major components of NARLI CSSO are linoleic acid C18:2 (54.11%), oleic acid C18:1 (17.78%) and linolenic acid C18:3 (15.19%), which is consistent with previous reports [23]. γ-linolenic acid (GLA) in CSSO is known for its high penetration into the skin, so it is used in cosmetics and has been reported to be effective as sunscreen, skin repair, anti-allergy and anti-aging [51, 52]. The antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties of CSSO constitute its therapeutic effect [53].

Wound healing is a multi-stage process consisting of many dynamics. It consists of an inflammatory phase representing homeostasis and inflammation, followed by a proliferative phase including epithelialization, fibroplasia, angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation, and finally collagen deposition and remodeling [54]. An excision wound model was used to evaluate wound healing activity in control and CSSO-treated rats. The mean percentage of wound area closure was calculated on days 7, 14 and 21. Our findings showed that topical application of CSSO enhanced wound healing in the treated rats: 87.55% of the wound area was closed in the control group on day 21, whereas 93% closure was observed in the CSSO-treated group. In Wistar rat models, full epithelialization typically occurs within 16–18 days [35]. In our study, the 87.55% macroscopic closure rate observed in the control group on day 21 is primarily attributed to our full-thickness model, which involves excising the panniculus carnosus. The removal of this layer limits rapid wound contraction, thereby rendering the healing process more analogous to human secondary intention healing [35, 55]. Furthermore, the apparent delay in macroscopic wound closure was attributed to the retention of the dry scab in our area calculations. However, our subsequent histological evaluations demonstrated complete epidermal regeneration beneath these crusts, indicating that macroscopic evaluation alone may underestimate the true extent of epithelialization.

Furthermore, histopathological and immunohistochemical staining confirmed improved healing quality in treated wounds compared to the control group. These findings indicated increased neovascularization, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and enhanced epithelial proliferation due to CSSO treatment. CSSO contains many bioactive compounds reported to have antioxidant properties, including oleic and linoleic acids. As reported in previous studies [56], these acids are thought to contribute to wound healing by modulating neutrophil activity, which is a major source of free radicals in wound environments. These compounds may be responsible for pharmacological activities. In a wound study, gamma linolenic acid (GLA) significantly inhibited inducible nitric oxide synthase, pro-IL-1β and COX-2 protein expression and intracellular glutathione level in the LPS-induced inflammatory response [57].

The molecular docking analyses revealed that CSSO-derived fatty acids exhibited the most favorable predicted binding profiles toward COX-2 compared to NLRP3, SIRT1, and GSK3β. This may claim that COX-2 may represent a primary molecular target underlying the anti-inflammatory potential of CSSO. These findings are in agreement with previous studies. Anand et al. investigated the binding affinity of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) and linoleic acid (LA) against COX-2 using AutoDock Vina and reported favorable interactions associated with anti-inflammatory potential through COX inhibition [58]. Similarly, alpha-linolenic acid was identified as one of the best docked compounds against COX-2, with a binding energy of -7.0 kcal/mol and hydrogen bond formation with key active-site residues [58]. In addition, the relatively stronger interaction of CSSO-derived fatty acids with COX-2 compared to NLRP3, SIRT1, and GSK3β may be attributed to the structural compatibility of their long aliphatic chains with the hydrophobic channel of the COX-2 active site. Fatty acid substrates generally bind within the COX channel in extended L-shaped conformations, with their carboxylate groups positioned near ARG120 and their hydrophobic tails occupying the hydrophobic groove [59]. This structural characteristic is consistent with the interaction profiles observed in the present study. However, the differences in binding energies among all tested fatty acids were relatively minor. This suggests that multiple fatty acids in CSSO may collectively contribute to its biological effects rather than a single active compound being solely responsible.

However, the present docking analysis was primarily designed to investigate the molecular interaction of CSSO-derived fatty acids with proteins associated with wound healing and inflammatory pathways rather than to perform precise affinity ranking of individual compounds. Therefore, molecular dynamics simulations and MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations were not included in the current study. Future studies incorporating these approaches may provide additional insight into the stability and dynamics of protein–ligand interactions.

The inflammatory response plays an essential role in initiating wound healing. A timely transition from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase is critical for successful tissue repair and regeneration. TNF-α and IL-1β are key proinflammatory cytokines that become upregulated during the inflammatory phase and contribute to initiating of early wound healing responses [60]. The inflammatory phase, effective in the acute phase of wound healing, decreases in the following weeks after the first week, and the proliferative phase begins. By the end of the inflammatory phase, neutrophils are cleared, and the number of macrophages decreases, marking the transition to the proliferative phase [61]. IL-1β and TGF-α, inflammatory cytokines released from these cells, were decreased in the treatment group compared to the control group in our study, supporting the evidence that Cannabis sativa exhibits anti-inflammatory properties. Similarly, it has been reported that both hemp extract and cannabidiol (CBD) can inhibit TNF-α and IL-1β production, thus promoting gingival wound healing [24].

The therapeutic potential of CSSO observed in this study may offer particularly significant implications for the management of impaired wound healing conditions, such as diabetic ulcers. Diabetic wound healing is a complex pathological process characterized by persistent inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and excessive oxidative stress [38, 62]. Given that CSSO effectively modulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) and upregulates angiogenic markers like VEGF, it represents a promising candidate for overcoming the physiological barriers seen in chronic diabetic wounds. Integrating natural bioactive agents like CSSO into advanced wound care protocols could potentially address the underlying dysregulation in diabetic tissue repair, providing a more robust scaffold for regeneration in compromised metabolic environments [38, 62].

NLRP3 inflammasome is a cytosolic multiple protein complex that plays a critical role in the immune system by regulating the activation of caspase-1, which subsequently causes the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, as well as gasdermin D-mediated pyroptotic cell death [32]. In addition to its role in inflammation, NLRP3 inflammasome has been involved in the skin wound healing process via regulating inflammatory mediators, recruiting macrophages, and polarizing to a proinflammatory phenotype [63, 64].

These fundamental roles of NLRP3 inflammasome in tissue repairing, combined with our experimental finding illustrating significant changes in the expression levels of key inflammatory mediators and growth factors such as VEGF, TNF-α, and TGF-β1, suggests that these observations may be explained through molecular interaction between CSSO and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Given the critical effects of NLRP3 in wound healing, the primary aim of molecular docking studies was to observe whether CSSO can bind NLRP3 protein, providing a potential molecular basis between CSSO’s activity and the findings gained in our experimental results.

IL-6, a key cytokine in the acute stage of wound healing, is released by macrophages, keratinocytes, endothelial cells, and stromal cells, promoting the chemotaxis of leukocytes to the wound site [65, 66]. IL-6, which is high in the acute phase, is greatly reduced during the remodeling period. This is thought to be due to apoptosis of leukocytes infiltrating the wound site [67]. Although the difference was not statistically significant in our study, IL-6 expression on day 21 was lower in the CSSO group and higher in the control group. In this period, which is the end of the proliferation period and the beginning of remodeling, inappropriate proinflammatory signaling may negatively affect wound healing. Because inappropriate proinflammatory signaling can delay wound healing and even result in wounds that last much longer and carry a risk of infection [68–70].

A critical process during the proliferative phase of wound healing is angiogenesis, which involves the formation of new capillaries to replace damaged vasculature and reestablish tissue perfusion [61]. The VEGF has an important effect on endothelial cell proliferation, differentiation, migration and cell survival in forming new blood vessels. Consequently, the levels of VEGF within healing tissue critically influence the efficiency and progression of the wound healing process [71–73]. A significant increase in VEGF immunoexpression in the vessels of the CSSO group on day 21 indicates that C. sativa contributes to wound healing by increasing vascularization (p = 0.031). This finding is consistent with previous research reporting that elevated levels of growth factors, such as EGF and bEGF correlate with improved wound healing outcomes [74].

Complete wound closure during the healing process is achieved through the proliferation and differentiation of keratinocytes in the epithelium [75]. Ki-67, a well-known cell proliferation marker, was used to identify proliferating epithelial cells. Higher Ki-67 expression observed in the CSSO-treated group compared to the control group indicates enhanced epithelialisation and more rapid wound closure.

While our study primarily elucidated the wound healing mechanisms of CSSO through histopathological, immunohistochemical, and in silico analyses, the complete spectrum of tissue regeneration also heavily involves the dynamic regulation of local oxidative stress and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling. Recent studies have demonstrated that the biochemical evaluation of antioxidant enzymes (such as SOD, GSH, and CAT) and ECM restructuring markers (such as HPR, HXA, and HUA) provides critical insights into the quality of wound beds [76, 77]. Although the present experimental design focused on molecular docking and immunohistochemical assessment of selected wound-healing markers, further studies including comprehensive biochemical analyses are needed to better elucidate the antioxidant potential and ECM maturation dynamics in CSSO-treated wounds.

One of the most important considerations regarding the topical application of plant-derived oils is their potential occlusive effect, which can facilitate wound healing simply by providing a moist environment and forming a physical barrier. However, the results of this study—specifically the significant downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, alongside the marked upregulation of VEGF and Ki-67—point to a specific biochemical intervention in the inflammatory and proliferative phases of repair. This targeted activity is further supported by our in silico molecular docking findings, which reveal high binding affinities between CSSO fatty acids and key inflammatory mediators. Taken together, these data strongly suggest that the ‘NARLI’ variety of CSSO acts not merely as a passive occlusive dressing, but as an active therapeutic agent that modulates molecular signalling pathways.

Conclusion

In Cannabis sativa studies in anti-inflammatory effects, plant material obtained from cannabis leaves has been used more and there are limited studies on seed oil. Although the wound-healing efficacy of CSSO has been reported to be limited, little is known about the mechanism underlying its action. Here, we investigated its wound-healing potential in rats through inflammation mediators. The results of this study revealed that the local application of CSSO significantly increased the expression of Ki-67, VEGF while reducing the levels of IL-1β and TGF-β1 in wound tissues compared to the control group, demonstrating its anti-inflammatory properties. Although the evaluation of ECM remodelling and antioxidant status represents a limitation of the present study, findings of the current study support the beneficial effects of CSSO on wound healing and suggest its potential use as a therapeutic agent for wound treatment.
