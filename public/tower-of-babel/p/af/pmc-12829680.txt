EUS–guided hepaticoduodenostomy with angled puncture technique to avoid the interposed gallbladder (with video)
Authors: Haruo Miwa, Hiromi Tsuchiya, Kazuki Endo, Ritsuko Oishi, Yuichi Suzuki, Akihiro Funaoka, Shin Maeda
Published: 2025

EUS–guided hepaticoduodenostomy (EUS-HDS) is an effective alternative procedure for obstruction of the right intrahepatic bile duct.[1,2] However, it can be challenging when the other organs, especially the gallbladder, are interposed along the needle tract. A novel echoendoscope (EG-740UT; FUJIFILM Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) has been developed, which facilitates angled puncture.[3,4] Herein, we report a successful case of EUS-HDS with angled puncture technique to avoid the interposed gallbladder [Video 1].

An 83-year-old man with a large hepatocellular carcinoma was admitted with an obstruction of the right hepatic duct [Figure 1]. Transpapillary drainage failed due to the complete obstruction of the bile duct; therefore, EUS-HDS was performed. The dilated intrahepatic bile duct (posterior branch) was visualized from the duodenum; however, the gallbladder was interposed along the puncture tract. We first planned to puncture vertically to avoid the gallbladder. This was followed by down-angulation of the scope to puncture the bile duct [Figure 2]. A 19-gauge needle (EZ shot 3 plus; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced vertically to the liver surface, avoiding the gallbladder. Down-angulation of the echoendoscope shifted the bile duct into the path of the needle. Subsequently, the bile duct was successfully punctured [Figure 3]. Cholangiography showed the posterior branch, and a 0.025-inch guidewire was advanced toward peripheral side. The needle was withdrawn, and a double-lumen catheter was inserted into the bile duct. A 0.035-inch stiff guidewire (Capella 0.035-inch; Japan Lifeline Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was inserted after bile aspiration. A dedicated plastic stent (7-Fr, 10-cm, Through & Pass Type IT; Gadelius Medical, Tokyo, Japan) was placed without additional dilation [Figure 4]. The patient developed mild cholangitis and discharged after improvement of the biliary obstruction.

Computed tomography image shows a large hepatocellular carcinoma at the perihilar lesion, and the right intrahepatic bile duct is markedly dilated.

Schemas of an angled puncture technique. (a) Gallbladder is interposed along the needle tract. (b) Duodenal wall is punctured vertically to avoid the gallbladder. (c) When the needle tip advanced to liver surface, down-angulation is applied to shift the bile duct. (d) Bile duct is punctured.

Ultrasonographic images during an angled puncture technique. Arrowheads point the needle tip. (a) Gallbladder is interposed along the needle tract. (b) A 19-gauge needle is advanced vertically. (c) The needle tip is carefully advanced while avoiding the gallbladder to the liver surface. (d) Down-angulation shifts the bile duct on the needle tract, and the posterior branch is successfully punctured.

Plastic stent placement after the angled puncture. (a) Cholangiography shows the posterior brunch, and a 0.025-inch guidewire is advanced. (b) A double-lumen catheter is inserted, and a 0.035-inch stiff guidewire is added. (c) A dedicated plastic stent (7-Fr, 10-cm, Through & Pass Type IT) is deployed toward the peripheral side. (d) The pig-tail distal end of the plastic stent is successfully placed in the duodenum.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of EUS-HDS with angled puncture technique to avoid the interposed gallbladder. The novel echoendoscope appears to be useful for such difficult cases of interventional EUS.
