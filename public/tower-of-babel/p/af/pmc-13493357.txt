Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of Risankizumab Versus Icotrokinra in Adult Patients with Moderate-to-Severe Plaque Psoriasis
Authors: Jeffrey Crowley, Eingun James Song, Linda Stein-Gold, Paolo Gisondi, Christopher G. Bunick, Vishvas Garg, Emanuele Leoncini, Blair Kaplan, Richard Ta, Bang Truong, Dolly Sharma, Bruce Strober
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRisankizumab (RZB) is an approved injectable IL-23 inhibitor with demonstrated high levels of skin clearance, and icotrokinra (ICO) is an oral IL-23 receptor blocker recently approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. In the absence of head-to-head trials, this study uses matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) to compare RZB with ICO in the treatment of adult patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis.MethodsIndividual patient data from phase 3 RZB trials and summary data from published ICO phase 3 trials were used in this MAIC through week 52. Risk differences between RZB and ICO placebo-adjusted response rates (anchored) were assessed for ≥ 75%, ≥ 90%, and 100% improvement in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI 75, PASI 90, and PASI 100) and Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) of 0 and 0/1 (IGA 0 and IGA 0/1) every 4 weeks up to week 16. Risk differences between RZB and ICO unadjusted response rates (unanchored) at weeks 24 and 52, along with confidence intervals and P-values, are also presented.ResultsAt 16 weeks, the PBO-adjusted response rates (RZB versus ICO) were 80.6% versus 61.7%, with a risk difference (RD) of 18.9% (95% CI 11.5, 26.4; P < 0.001) for PASI 75; 71.4% versus 49.9% (RD: 21.5%; 95% CI 15.6, 27.4; P < 0.001) for PASI 90; 42.3% versus 29.4% (RD: 13.0%; 95% CI 7.9, 18.0; P < 0.001) for PASI 100; 77.8% versus 58.4% (RD: 19.4%; 95% CI 12.4, 26.3; P < 0.001) for IGA 0/1; and 41.6% versus 33.9% (RD: 7.7%; 95% CI 2.3, 13.0; P < 0.05) for IGA 0. Results were consistent in the unanchored analysis at weeks 24 and 52.ConclusionIn this analysis, adult patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis treated with RZB demonstrated greater clinical efficacy as early as week 4 or 8, and through week 52 compared with ICO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01839-2.

Introduction

Multiple treatments are available for the treatment of plaque psoriasis, ranging from topicals as first-line therapy for milder disease to advanced systemic (biologic and nonbiologic) therapies for moderate-to-severe cases, high-impact areas of involvement, or topical failure [1–3]. Biologics, including interleukin (IL)-12/23, IL-17, IL-23, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors, have elevated treatment goals compared with nonbiologics in psoriasis owing to their high clinical efficacy and safety profiles [4]. Risankizumab (RZB) is an approved IL-23 inhibitor that selectively targets the p19 subunit and has demonstrated greater clinical efficacy compared with biologics with different mechanisms of action. RZB demonstrated higher levels of efficacy in head-to-head clinical trials against ustekinumab (an IL-12/23 inhibitor), secukinumab (an IL-17A inhibitor), and adalimumab (a TNF-α inhibitor) [5–8]. Real-world evidence also supports the higher effectiveness of RZB compared with many other biologics [9–11]. In addition, a recently published study provided 6-year long-term safety and efficacy data, supporting continuous use of RZB in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis [12].

In general, nonbiologic systemic therapies have not been as effective as biologics in treating moderate-to-severe psoriasis. RZB has already demonstrated greater clinical efficacy than apremilast, an oral phosphodiesterase 4 inhibitor, in the treatment of moderate psoriasis [13]. RZB also demonstrated greater efficacy than deucravacitinib, a more recently approved oral allosteric tyrosine kinase 2 inhibitor, in an indirect comparison and in a head-to-head trial in systemic-eligible and bio-naïve patients with moderate severity [14, 15]. Icotrokinra (ICO) is a recently approved oral IL-23 receptor blocker for the treatment of moderate-to-severe psoriasis. ICO demonstrated greater clinical efficacy compared with placebo (PBO) and deucravacitinib in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis in two phase 3 trials [16]. ICO has not been directly compared with RZB in the treatment of moderate-to-severe psoriasis.

Clinicians constantly need to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of a large, rapidly evolving number of therapeutic options to make informed decisions in the management of psoriasis. It is not practical to conduct head-to-head trials of every therapy; therefore, indirect comparisons offer valuable insights into the effectiveness of different treatments. Indirect comparison methods such as network meta-analyses are widely used but may be particularly sensitive to differences in population characteristics. The matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) approach is a validated and widely adopted method that helps reduce this bias by using individual patient data (IPD) from trials of one treatment and matching them to the baseline summary statistics of trials of another treatment, enabling comparisons of outcomes [17–19].

The objective of this analysis is to compare the clinical efficacy of RZB with ICO in adult patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis using MAIC.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This MAIC compares clinical efficacy between RZB and ICO in adult patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Only studies meeting prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected in this MAIC. Eligible studies included phase 3, multinational randomized controlled trials that enrolled adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis (defined as body surface area [BSA] ≥ 10%, Psoriasis Area Severity Index [PASI] ≥ 12, and static Physician Global Assessment [sPGA]/Investigator Global Assessment [IGA] ≥ 3) receiving RZB or ICO with common comparators including PBO or ustekinumab. Endpoints at week 16 and beyond were primarily assessed, though earlier timepoints were also evaluated. Studies with published data (full-text or poster) before 31 March 2026, were included in this analysis. For RZB, IPD from phase 3, randomized, double-blinded, PBO-controlled trials, UltIMMa-1, UltIMMa-2, and IMMhance, were pooled together (Supplementary Table S1). Similarly, published aggregate data from ICO trials, ICONIC-LEAD (data from adults only) and ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and -2 were pooled from publications (Supplementary Table S1).

A feasibility analysis was conducted to compare the trial design between UltIMMa-1, UltIMMa-2, IMMhance, ICONIC ADVANCE-1, ICONIC ADVANCE-2, and ICONIC-LEAD. Overall, the inclusion and exclusion criteria and the study designs of the included trials were similar. Since ICONIC-LEAD included both adult and adolescent patients, only adult data were used for this MAIC. For RZB trials, no patients were excluded from the UltiMMa-1 and -2, and IMMhance trials to align inclusion/exclusion criteria with ICO trials (Supplementary Table S1). All RZB and ICO trials were included for all endpoints and time points through week 16. For analyses from week 4 through 52, time points were generally aligned except for week 24. At this time point, UltIMMa-1 and -2 reported data at week 22, while IMMhance reported at week 24. To ensure robust comparison at week 24, only week 22 data from the pivotal UltIMMa trials were used to indirectly compare with ICO’s week 24 data. For the week 52 timepoint, both ICONIC-LEAD and IMMhance studies were omitted from the analysis owing to trial design limitations—in particular, the randomized withdrawal and retreatment occurred at weeks 24 and 28, respectively (Supplementary Table S1).

All six trials included in this study were conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use, Good Clinical Practice Guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki, and applicable local guidelines and regulations. Approvals were obtained from local ethics committees of the relevant countries, and written informed consent was obtained from patients at study initiation. This analysis utilized de-identified data from publications and did not require ethics committee approval.

Outcomes

Efficacy endpoints assessed in this MAIC include the proportion of patients achieving at least 75%, 90%, and 100% improvement in PASI (PASI 75, PASI 90 [almost clear], and PASI 100 [complete clearance]), Investigator’s Global Assessment of 0/1 (IGA 0/1; almost clear), and IGA 0 (complete clearance). Outcomes were defined identically across the included trials. We compared outcomes during the double-blind period at weeks 4, 8, 12, and 16, and beyond the double-blind period at weeks 24 and 52. Week 52 MAIC for PASI 75 was not assessed, since it was not reported in ICO’s publications.

Statistical Analysis

Across all six trials, RZB and ICO were compared with PBO during the double-blind period (weeks 4, 8, 12, and 16); therefore, a PBO-anchored MAIC was conducted at these time points. An unanchored analysis was conducted for weeks 24 and 52 owing to the absence of a PBO arm. The main analysis adjusted for all available baseline characteristics reported across all trials including age, sex (male/female), race (white versus non-white), body mass index (BMI), static Physician’s Global Assessment score (sPGA, equivalent to IGA), percent body surface area (BSA) involvement, baseline PASI, prior phototherapy, and prior biologic therapy for all anchored and unanchored analyses. Additional indirect treatment comparisons using the Bucher method were conducted at week 4, 8, 12, and 16, and naïve indirect comparisons were performed at week 24 and 52 (before matching).

For sensitivity analysis 1, we adjusted for BMI and prior biologic therapy, which are suggested as potential effect modifiers for certain biologic treatments [20]. For unanchored MAICs, since both effect modifiers and prognostic factors need to be adjusted, only the main analysis and naïve comparison were conducted. In sensitivity analysis 2, true mean and standard deviation (SD) of %BSA affected and baseline PASI score from recently published data from the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) 2026 conference were used for matching in place of the estimated median (interquartile range [IQR]) values [21].

Data Preprocessing

IPD from three trials of RZB (UltIMMa-1 and 2-, and IMMhance) were pooled together to match with pooled summary statistics from phase 3 trials of ICO (ICONIC-LEAD and ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and -2). Only RZB arms, ICO arms, and PBO arms from individual trials were included in this study. Since ICONIC-LEAD included both adult and adolescent patients, only data from adults were used. Summary baseline characteristics and outcomes of adult patients from the ICONIC-LEAD trial were calculated by subtracting the summary statistics of the adolescent subgroup from the pooled summary statistics from both adults and adolescents (Supplementary Material) [22]. When median (IQR) data were reported in place of mean (SD), we assumed a normal distribution for such baseline characteristics and converted them into mean (SD) (Supplementary Material). At the time of the main analysis, for continuous variables reported as median (IQR) rather than mean (SD) in ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and -2 (%BSA affected and baseline PASI score), we assumed a normal distribution and converted them to mean (SD) [16, 22]. Upon availability of baseline mean and SD for ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and -2, recently released at AAD 2026, sensitivity analysis 2 was carried out using the mean (SD) of %BSA affected and baseline PASI score from ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and -2 trials.

Weight Calculation and Outcomes Comparisons

Patients in the RZB trials with IPD were weighted so that their weighted covariate statistics (mean and SD for continuous variables, and proportion for categorical variables) matched those reported for the ICO trials. Weights were obtained from a logistic regression model for the propensity to enroll in the RZB trial versus the ICO trial using the method of moments and controlling for baseline characteristics (main analyses: age, sex, race, BMI, sPGA, BSA involvement, baseline PASI, prior phototherapy, and prior biologic therapy, as well as sensitivity analyses: BMI and prior biologic therapy) [17]. Weight distributions were numerically and visually evaluated for skewness and outliers to assess the performance of the matching procedure. In addition, the effective sample size (ESS) was estimated.

For the PBO-anchored analysis, we reported (1) unadjusted response rates up to week 16, (2) PBO-adjusted response rates up to week 16, and (3) risk differences (RDs) between the PBO-adjusted response rates between RZB and ICO for each comparison up to week 16 along with 95% CI and P-values. For the unanchored analysis at week 24 and week 52, we reported (1) unadjusted response rates and (2) RDs between unadjusted response rates between RZB and ICO for each comparison, along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and P-values. Nonresponder imputation (NRI) data was used for all analyses. The robust sandwich method was implemented to estimate standard errors and 95% CIs (maicplus: R 4.3.1 R package version 0.1.2.9000, https://github.com/hta-pharma/maicplus/).

Matching Process

A small reduction in effective sample sizes (ESS) was observed for the anchored MAIC main analysis (RZB versus ICO: ESS = 749, reduction = 25.5%; PBO versus PBO: ESS = 236, reduction = 21.2%), indicating good overlap between study populations (Supplementary Fig. 1). The distribution of weights is described in Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. 1. ESS for unanchored MAICs at week 24 was 466 (reduction = 22.1%) (Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figure S2). ESS for unanchored MAIC at week 52 was 435 (reduction = 27.3%) (Supplementary Figure S3). In the sensitivity analysis 1, ESS was 895 with a 10.9% reduction for RZB versus ICO, and 260 with a 13.2% reduction for PBO versus PBO (Supplementary Table S6). In the sensitivity analysis 2, ESS was 788 with a 21.6% reduction for RZB versus ICO, and 240 with a 20.% reduction for PBO versus PBO at week 16; ESSs were 508 (15.1% reduction) for unanchored MAIC at week 24 and 521 (12.9% reduction) for unanchored MAIC at week 52 (data not shown).

Clinical Outcomes

Placebo Anchored Analysis up to Week 16

Patients treated with RZB achieved higher response rates compared with patients treated with ICO as early as week 4 (PASI 75 and IGA 0/1) or week 8 (PASI 90, PASI 100, and IGA 0) across assessed endpoints. Patients treated with RZB also consistently demonstrated higher response rates at all time points through week 16 across all endpoints. At week 16, 88.1% of patients treated with RZB versus 72.2% of patients treated with ICO achieved PASI 75 (Fig. 1A). The PBO-adjusted response rates of patients who achieved PASI 75 were 80.6% for patients treated with RZB and 61.7% for patients treated with ICO, with an RD of 18.9% (95% CI 11.5, 26.4; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). PASI 90 was achieved by 73.6% of patients treated with RZB and 52.8% of patients treated with ICO at week 16 (Fig. 1B). The PBO-adjusted response rates of patients who achieved PASI 90 were 71.4% (RZB) versus 49.9% (ICO) with an RD of 21.5% (95% CI 15.6, 27.4; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). PASI 100 was achieved by 43.1% of patients treated with RZB versus 30.0% of patients treated with ICO at week 16 (Fig. 1C). The PBO-adjusted response rates of patients who achieved PASI 100 were 42.3% (RZB) versus 29.4% (ICO) with an RD of 13.0% (95% CI 7.9, 18.0; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). IGA 0/1 was achieved by 83.3% of patients treated with RZB versus 66.5% of patients treated with ICO at week 16 (Fig. 1D). The PBO-adjusted response rates of patients who achieved IGA 0/1 were 77.8% (RZB) versus 58.4% (ICO) with an RD of 19.4% (95% CI 12.4, 26.3; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). IGA 0 was achieved in 43.0% of patients treated with RZB versus 35.0% treated with ICO (Fig. 1E). Additional and Sensitivity Analyses

The results were consistent in both the naïve indirect analysis and the two sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S7). The first sensitivity analysis included adjustment for only BMI and prior biologic therapy, while the second used the mean (SDs) reported for %BSA and baseline PASI score. These findings aligned with those from the main analysis, confirming the fidelity of our findings (Supplementary Table S7). Specifically, RZB was associated with statistically significantly higher achievement across all outcomes at all time points up to week 16 for the PBO-anchored analysis and from week 24 to week 52 for the unanchored analysis.

Discussion

Moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated disease that requires lifelong management. Novel therapies continue to emerge, and comparisons help physicians optimize treatment selection for patients. Indirect comparisons, such as MAIC, help assess comparative clinical efficacy when head-to-head randomized clinical trials are unavailable. MAIC is a useful tool for indirect treatment comparison when there is heterogeneity in baseline characteristics between trials, because it adjusts for the differences in baseline characteristics across trial populations [23]. MAIC has also previously been used to demonstrate RZB’s higher efficacy compared with other biologics, including deucravacitinib and guselkumab, in patients with psoriasis [14, 24]. This current MAIC analysis compares the clinical efficacy of RZB with ICO. In the PBO-anchored analysis, response rates in patients treated with RZB were significantly higher than those in patients treated with ICO across all assessed clinical endpoints, starting as early as week 4 or 8 and continuing through week 16. Similarly, response rates in patients treated with RZB were significantly higher than patients treated with ICO across all assessed endpoints in the unanchored analysis at week 24 and week 52.

ICO is a recently approved oral therapy and has only been directly compared with one oral systemic therapy, deucravacitinib [16, 22]. A recent network meta-analysis (NMA) compared ICO with 13 other therapies, including RZB. This NMA assessed complete skin clearance on the basis of PASI 100 or IGA 0 response at week 16 [25]. Results from this NMA showed that ICO was superior to apremilast, deucravacitinib, ustekinumab, and adalimumab but comparable to injected biologics, including guselkumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, and RZB. In contrast, the results of this MAIC show response rates for PASI 100 in patients treated with RZB were consistently significantly higher than the response rates of patients treated with ICO as early as week 8 and up to week 52. Both MAIC and NMA are methods of indirect comparison used when head-to-head clinical trials are unavailable. NMAs are used to compare a wide range of treatments and are employed when only summary-level data are available, while MAIC is advantageous because it uses IPD and allows for adjustment for patient characteristics when comparing two treatments [23]. While the NMA only compared ICO’s efficacy solely at week 16 and for a limited set of outcomes, such as complete skin clearance, this MAIC assessed efficacies for all available outcomes, including the primary endpoint (PASI 90) for both trials at all available time-points, providing a more comprehensive comparison. In addition, since it is unknown which exact trials were included in the NMA for ICO and RZB, and only odds ratios are reported without response rates or other outcomes, interpreting the results may be challenging. Results from a prior MAIC analysis comparing different biologic therapies in psoriasis are consistent with published randomized clinical trials supporting the fidelity of this methodology [23]. The results of this analysis add to the growing evidence supporting the high efficacy of RZB compared with other biologics and nonbiologic small molecule therapies, representing superiority across five mechanisms of action [11].

Achieving higher levels of efficacy, particularly complete clearance such as PASI 100, is clinically meaningful because it is associated with significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes [26, 27]. Importantly, evidence also suggests that reaching higher levels of efficacy earlier may be associated with better long-term outcomes, including more durable disease control and a lower risk of inflammatory comorbidities such as psoriatic arthritis [28–32]. In this MAIC, a higher proportion of patients treated with RZB achieved earlier complete and almost complete skin clearance than patients treated with ICO. Taken together, these findings provide further support for the notion that RZB may achieve durable skin clearance earlier and improve long-term patient outcomes.

This MAIC utilized data from clinical trials, that assess efficacy under ideal, highly controlled conditions. Whereas real-world evidence (RWE) evaluates effectiveness in broader patient population in everyday practice, which reflects practical challenges such as adherence, which may influence outcomes [33]. As a recently approved therapy, while ICO’s efficacy has been demonstrated in clinical trials, how it translates in real world, specifically patients’ adherence to a once daily oral, is unknown while RZB’s efficacy is supported by several years of clinical trials and RWE [11, 34]. Efficacy appears to be the predominant driver of treatment choice for patients with psoriasis. Although route of administration (ROA) is also an important consideration, it generally ranks below efficacy in influencing treatment decisions. Available evidence suggests that preferences for ROA are mixed: while some patients favor less frequent injectable therapies, others prefer oral options [35, 36]. In studies comparing RZB with other oral therapies, patients reported greater convenience with RZB than with apremilast and deucravacitinib, as measured by the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication-9 (Convenience) [13, 15]. At the same time, other analyses suggest that oral therapies may be preferred by some patients and providers, underscoring that preferences are nuanced and population dependent [37–39]. Future long-term drug survival studies in real-world populations may help clarify adherence and persistence with newer oral options relative to injectable therapies.

This study employed an anchored-based approach for comparisons between RZB and ICO up to week 16, but the MAIC for weeks 24 and 52 were unanchored. The anchored comparison preserves randomization in the presence of common placebo arms, whereas the unanchored comparison may exhibit residual bias owing to the lack of a common comparator. In our unanchored analyses, we adjusted for all baseline characteristics reported in our trials and ICO trials. The unanchored MAIC relies on a strong assumption that all potential effect modifiers and prognostic factors are accounted for [40]. Therefore, our analysis may still be subject to residual bias and should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, after week 24, the ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and -2 trials transitioned to an open-label design, meaning both participants and investigators became aware of the treatment assignments [16]. This change introduces several potential biases, including perception and observer bias, which may lead to artificial inflation of the response rates seen in ICO’s trials and potentially overstating its benefits [41]. In contrast, the UltIMMa-1 and -2 trials remained blinded through week 52 [5]. Therefore, the actual differences between RZB and ICO at week 52 may be larger than the estimated differences in our analysis. Secondly, only observed effect modifiers and prognostic factors consistently reported in RZB and ICO trials were adjusted in the MAICs. Residual heterogeneity and case-mix heterogeneity due to unobserved or unmeasurable effect modifiers (i.e., presence of psoriatic arthritis, psoriasis disease duration) may persist and result in bias. However, we expect this bias to be minimal since a literature search did not identify these two variables as effect modifiers. Moreover, the results from the naïve indirect treatment comparison, the main analysis, and the MAIC’s sensitivity analyses are consistent, confirming the fidelity of our findings. RZB directly neutralizes circulating IL-23, whereas icotrokinra, as an oral IL-23 receptor antagonist, blocks downstream IL-23 signaling. Although both approaches inhibit the same pathway, the injectable biologic may provide more complete and sustained pathway suppression, with potential implications for pharmacologic exposure and clinical response. However, the mechanism of action responsible for these differences need to be further investigated. Finally, the generalizability of these results may be limited, as patients enrolled in clinical trials may differ from patients in the real world. Conclusion

This MAIC assessed the comparative clinical efficacy of RZB and ICO in the treatment of moderate-to-severe psoriasis. This indirect comparison showed that patients treated with RZB achieved higher response rates of PASI 75, PASI 90, PASI 100, IGA 0/1, and IGA 0 as early as week 4 and consistently every 4 weeks up to week 16 (anchored analysis), as well as at week 24 and 52 (unanchored analysis). In the absence of head-to-head studies, this indirect comparison may inform treatment guidelines and facilitate shared decision-making, enabling physicians and patients to navigate treatment options to optimize outcomes. Future head-to-head studies are needed to confirm these results.
