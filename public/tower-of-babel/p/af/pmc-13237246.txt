A high-frequency silicon-graphene-germanium barristor
Authors: Xiaoyue Wang, Shaotang Sun, Zishen Qiao, Haiyan Jiang, Changze Li, Haitao Jiang, Jing Bai, Xubo Song, Xufan Li, Xinzhe Wang, Xi Zhu, Shun Feng, Bo Li, Jinhua Liang, Lingfei Wang, Zhongying Xue, Yutaka Ohno, Jianjun Gao, Zengfeng Di, Dongming Sun, Chi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRealising ubiquitous environmental monitoring and smart sensing devices compatible with Internet of Things (IoT) and 6 G networks requires transistors with terahertz (THz) cutoff frequencies (fT) for efficient signal processing. However, the carrier transit time intrinsically limits conventional devices. Vertical two-dimensional (2D) base transistors offer a way to exceed this limit, yet interface losses typically suppress current gain, degrade high-frequency performance, and hinder THz operation. Here, we report a silicon–graphene–germanium barristor that overcomes these obstacles. Wafer-scale single-crystal graphene was epitaxially grown on germanium and integrated with silicon membranes, forming asymmetric Schottky barriers at the graphene–silicon and graphene–germanium interfaces. Using graphene’s quantum capacitance, the asymmetric barriers enable distinct hot-carrier emission at both terminals and greatly increase the current gain, while graphene’s atomic thickness minimises the perpendicular transit time. As a result, the device achieves a current gain up to 1.8 × 107 and an intrinsic fT up to 132 GHz, with modelling and simulation indicating scalability into the THz regime. These findings establish a promising high-frequency transistor paradigm for IoT sensors and systems.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electronic properties and devices

Introduction

From large-scale environmental monitoring to responsive industrial automation and point-of-care diagnostics, the next generation of sensing applications—underpinned by 5 G/6 G sensor networks—will demand unprecedented data transmission rates and processing speeds. Meeting these requirements hinges on transistors with current-gain cutoff frequencies (fT) exceeding 1 terahertz (THz), which are essential for the real-time and energy-efficient operation of advanced sensing systems1. While traditional high-electron-mobility transistors (HEMTs) and heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs) have been successful in microwave and millimetre-wave applications, their performance is constrained by carrier transit time limitations in channels or bases made from bulk materials2,3. These intrinsic restrictions become more pronounced with increasing frequency and have driven the exploration of unconventional device structures that transcend classical semiconductor physics.

Vertical two-dimensional (2D) base transistors have attracted significant attention due to their atomic-scale base thickness and high carrier mobility4. For instance, in a graphene base transistor (GBT), the perpendicular carrier transport across the atomic graphene layer eliminates transit time, and when this is combined with the intrinsic ballistic carrier transport and exceptional thermal conductivity of graphene, GBTs emerge as strong candidates for high-frequency operation5–7. However, practical problems arise from the presence of quantum mechanical tunnelling barriers at the emitter and collector junctions, which hinder efficient charge injection and extraction. These barriers limit current density and frequency response, making it difficult for the device to reach the THz regime8. To overcome these limitations, graphene base heterojunction transistors (GBHTs) have been developed, using semiconductor–graphene–semiconductor perpendicular configurations with Schottky junctions to replace tunnelling junctions9. Although GBHTs give an improved carrier transport efficiency, problems such as interfacial defects and quantum mechanical reflections at the heterojunctions cause carrier backscattering, significantly degrading the current gain10,11. The experimental high-frequency performance also remains limited, with fT around only 1 GHz. The key challenge in developing vertical 2D base transistors is therefore to identify mechanisms that can overcome interface limitations to deliver high current gain, high cutoff frequency, and operational potential in the terahertz regime.

The barristor is a vertical transistor which exploits graphene’s quantum capacitance effect to change its work function, enabling precise control of the Schottky barrier height at semiconductor interfaces and, consequently, the current. Initially designed for digital logic applications, the barristor enables current switching ratios exceeding 105, rivalling traditional silicon-based transistors12. To date, research on barristors has primarily focused on their direct-current (DC) characteristics, with symmetric configurations featuring dual tunnelling junctions13 or back-to-back Schottky junctions14, as well as asymmetric designs combining tunnelling and Schottky junctions12. Additionally, transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD)-based barristors with atomically thin barriers have enabled operation on flexible substrates15. Despite these advances, the high-frequency potential of barristors remains largely unexplored. Notably, graphene’s atomic thickness suggests an inherent compatibility with ultra-high-speed operation, while the barristor’s operating principle circumvents the interfacial barrier limitations inherent to GBTs and GBHTs, implying its potential as a high-frequency device.

Here, we report a silicon–graphene–germanium barristor specifically designed to optimise high-frequency performance. The device was fabricated by direct epitaxial growth of wafer-scale single-crystal graphene on germanium substrates, followed by transfer of silicon membranes on top of the graphene, significantly improving interface quality. The graphene creates an asymmetric band alignment with the silicon and the germanium, resulting in distinct Schottky barrier heights that facilitate differential hot carrier emission at the two terminals, thereby significantly enhancing the current gain. The atomic thickness of graphene also gives a negligible vertical carrier transit time. As a result, the transistor achieves a current gain of 1.8 × 107 and an intrinsic fT of 132 GHz for vertical 2D base transistors. Device modelling and simulations suggest that further engineering could enable operation in the THz range.

Results

Device structure and fabrication

The barristor uses epitaxial single-layer graphene grown on a 4-inch n-type <110> germanium wafer using chemical vapour deposition (CVD) (Fig. 1a). Raman spectroscopy confirmed the formation of a single-crystal monolayer graphene, while atomic force microscopy (AFM) gave the root mean square (RMS) roughness values ranging from 0.292 to 0.497 nm, indicating the flatness across the wafer-scale film (Supplementary Fig. 1). This epitaxial graphene-on-germanium wafer provides a clean interface between the graphene and the germanium, enabling wafer-scale fabrication16.

The barristor comprises an epitaxial graphene source sandwiched between a single-crystal silicon (Si) gate and a germanium (Ge) drain, integrated with Au ground-signal-ground (GSG) pads for high-frequency characterisation (Fig. 1b, c). The circular Si membrane was fabricated from the top Si layer of a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer and transferred onto the graphene using a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp (Fig. 1d, Methods, Supplementary Fig. 2). Ti/Au was used to form an ohmic contact with the Si membrane after ion implantation (Supplementary Fig. 3a). This method enables large-scale transfer while preserving the single-crystal nature of the Si, overcoming the limitation of directly growing single-crystal Si on graphene. A Ti/Au contact was also formed directly on the graphene, providing an ohmic contact (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). After patterning the graphene, a Si/graphene/Ge heterostructure was obtained, as shown in the false-coloured scanning electron microscope (SEM) image (Fig. 1d).

To fabricate electrodes for high-frequency characterisation, first, an Al2O3 dielectric layer was deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD). Wet etching was then applied to open a window in the Al2O3 layer, exposing a defined region of the Ge surface for ion implantation to form an ohmic contact (Fig. 1c, Methods, Supplementary Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3b). The same etching process was subsequently used to expose the Si and graphene contacts (Fig. 1c, Methods, Supplementary Fig. 2h). Finally, Au GSG pads were fabricated on the dielectric layer, connected to the underlying exposed contacts (Fig. 1c, Methods). Fig. 1e shows the fabricated Si/graphene/Ge barristor array.

Device mechanism and direct current characteristics

The epitaxial graphene forms a rectifying junction with the Ge substrate, which has an on-to-off current ratio of 103, an ideality factor of 1.29, and a barrier height of 0.22 eV. For the graphene/Si junction, the corresponding values are 106, 1.55, and 0.64 eV, respectively17, demonstrating the successful formation of Schottky junctions (Supplementary Fig. 4). These asymmetric Schottky barriers enable differential hot carrier emission at the Si and Ge terminals using the graphene’s quantum capacitance effect (Fig. 2a). When the barristor operates, the graphene is grounded, and reverse biases (VGS and VDS) are applied to the Si gate and Ge drain, respectively, which raise the Fermi energy level due to the low density of states near graphene’s Dirac point, a phenomenon known as the quantum capacitance effect of graphene12. This allows the graphene to emit electrons to the Si and Ge, generating currents IG and ID, whose sum equals the source current IS in graphene (Fig. 2a). Since the Schottky barrier at the graphene/Ge junction is significantly lower than that of the Si/graphene junction, the change in ID is expected to be much larger than that in IG when the reverse biases change, leading to a large current gain β. It should be noted that, while we use the conventional barristor terminal designations (source, drain, and gate), our device fundamentally operates as a current-controlled device. Accordingly, we characterise the device performance using the common-emitter current gain β, following the standard convention for bipolar junction transistors (BJTs).

The input (IG-VGS, Fig. 2b), transfer (ID-VGS, Fig. 2c), and output (ID-VDS, Supplementary Fig. 5) characteristics show the expected current behaviour. When VGS varies from 0 to 6 V, the gate current density IG increases by approximately 300 μA/cm2, while the drain current density ID increases by more than 70 A/cm2. This significant difference in current density change implies a large device gain. In fact, a β of 5.6 × 105 at room temperature is achieved at VGS = 1.2 V and VDS = 5 V (Fig. 2d). The influence of the n-Si doping concentration on the on-to-off current ratio was also investigated in Supplementary Fig. 6, showing an increased value with doping concentration.

Multiple barristors were fabricated and measured, and had a current gain β with a sample size of 61 and a peak value of 1.8 × 107 (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 8). These results measured at room temperature represent the state-of-the-art current gain compared to transistors fabricated with group-IV elements (Si, SiC, and SiGe), group-III-V compounds, TMDs, and graphene (Fig. 2f), including both room‑temperature data (solid symbols)6,10,11,18–45 and low‑temperature data (open symbols)14,41,46–49. This is attributed not only to the barristor mechanism with asymmetric Schottky barriers, but also to the material innovations, including a single-crystal Si gate and transfer-free, wafer-scale graphene grown directly on Ge, which effectively decrease the number of lattice defects, impurity-induced leakage currents, and the series resistance, establishing a solid foundation for practical room-temperature RF applications.

In the history of developing vertical 2D base transistors, the graphene base heterojunction transistor (GBHT) was introduced to overcome the limitations of the tunnelling barrier in a graphene base transistor (GBT)9. However, its current gain was severely restricted by the quantum mechanical reflections at the heterojunctions11. In a GBHT, when the semiconductor collector has a low doping concentration, the collector current is too low to achieve a common-emitter current gain, while when the doping concentration is high, the leakage current of the collector junction becomes too large, also preventing a common-emitter current gain. As a result, only a common-base current gain has been observed in GBHTs, severely limiting their practical application. In contrast, our barristor has a large common-emitter current gain and paves the way for the further development of vertical 2D base transistors.

Radio-frequency characteristics

To evaluate the radio-frequency (RF) performance of the barristor, on-chip microwave measurements were conducted across a frequency range of 0.01–40 GHz (see Methods). The measurement setup used GSG electrodes insulated from the substrate by a 1.2 μm Al2O3 layer. This introduced parasitic pad capacitances, connection resistances and inductances, and the semiconducting substrate also caused losses, therefore, open and short de-embedding methods were applied to the measured scattering (S) parameters to obtain the intrinsic performance of the barristor (Methods, Supplementary Fig. 9)50. The intrinsic S parameters were then extracted and converted into hybrid (H) parameters to determine the current gain H21. The H21 as a function of frequency at a constant VGS = 6 V and various VDS values is shown in Fig. 3a, revealing an intrinsic current-gain cutoff frequency fT of 54 GHz at VDS = 7 V. For details on the small-signal equivalent model and simulation results of the barristor, see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 10.

We have also investigated the impact of the core factors on fT, including voltage biases, temperature, substrate doping concentration and device scaling. Fig. 3b shows the effect of DC voltage bias conditions and shows that fT increases with both VGS and VDS. It can be seen that VDS has a more significant impact on fT than VGS does, primarily due to its consistent influence on the transconductance (gm)51. As VDS increases, electrons are more easily emitted from graphene to Ge due to the reduction in the depletion width, leading to an increase in both gm and fT. Fig. 3c shows the impact of temperature from 248 to 323 K, and shows that H21 (inset) and fT increase when temperature decreases, with the highest fT being 106 GHz obtained at 248 K (Supplementary Fig. 11a). On one hand, this is caused by the reduced capacitance of the Schottky junctions at low temperatures, caused by the expansion of the space charge region52. On the other hand, it is also attributed to an increase in gm resulting from a shift in the dominant transport mechanism across the Schottky barrier—from thermionic emission at room temperature to field-induced tunnelling under cryogenic conditions53,54 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Furthermore, we performed high-temperature stability measurements and noise characterisation. The transistor was subjected to a constant temperature of 100 °C for 4 h, and no obvious degradation in either fT or ID were observed (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Fig. 3d shows the impact of Ge doping concentrations (NGe) from 5 × 1015 to 2 × 1017 cm–3, and shows that fT increases with NGe. This is primarily attributed to the reduced space charge region in Ge, which leads to a shorter carrier transit time and, consequently, an increase in device speed.

The aforementioned devices have a device area of 2820 μm2 (a circular cross-section with a 60-μm diameter). Next, we have scaled down the device and fabricated barristors with a reduced active area of 1250 μm2 while employing a Ge doping concentration NGe of 2 × 1017 cm–3 to investigate the scaling effect on high-frequency performance. The results in Fig. 3e clearly demonstrate that devices with a smaller area (1250 μm2) exhibit higher fT compared to larger devices (2820 μm2). The corresponding raw H21 data are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14 and 15. This optimisation results in fT exceeding 100 GHz (Supplementary Fig. 16), reaching a maximum of 132 GHz, with the Gummel’s method used to validate the fT extrapolation55. This represents approximately twice the highest fT reported for vertical 2D base transistors to date (65 GHz)44.

Overall, our RF graphene barristor outperforms the previously reported vertical 2D base transistors, including graphene base transistors (GBTs), graphene base heterojunction transistors (GBHTs) and MoS2 base transistors, in respect to both the cutoff frequency and current gain (Fig. 3f)5,6,10,17,43,44,56.

The model suggests that device biases, doping concentration and Schottky barrier heights can all be optimised to increase the RF performance. First, fT increases with VGS and VDS, and can reach 1 THz at the appropriate bias values. This is attributed to the improved current gain resulting from the increased reverse biases applied to both Schottky junctions (Fig. 4b). A map of fT as a function of doping concentrations NSi and NGe shows that fT is primarily influenced by the doping concentration of Ge, and is expected to exceed 1 THz when NGe approaches 1017 cm−3, due to the reduced depletion layer thickness on the Ge side (Fig. 4c). These trends of the model results are consistent with our experimental results (Fig. 3a, b). Additionally, various semiconductors can, in future, be used for the gate and drain. The relationship between RF performance and Schottky barrier heights has been studied (Fig. 4d), and it was observed that increasing the gate Schottky barrier requires a corresponding decrease in the drain Schottky barrier to enable THz applications.

The compact model above provides the theoretical fT under ideal conditions. To further examine the influence of device scaling and practical non-idealities—such as contact resistivity—we have established a simulation of the Si/graphene/Ge vertical transistor structure using the Silvaco TCAD (Methods, Supplementary Fig. 17). The results indicate that further device scaling and contact resistivity reduction could push fT closer to the theoretical THz regime, which is consistent with our experimental results in Fig. 3e.

Based on the THz modelling results, we further conducted circuit-level performance analysis with quantified gain and bandwidth metrics for sub-THz/THz integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) applications (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). To validate the device’s applicability in next-generation ISAC systems, we designed a single-stage low-noise amplifier (LNA) operating at 120–150 GHz using the ADS software. This demonstration effectively bridges device-level performance metrics to practical system-level design requirements.

To experimentally realise the THz barristor, efforts are needed in two parallel directions. On the one hand, intrinsic performance requires careful material selection and doping concentration optimisation. On the other hand, systematic engineering is essential to mitigate extrinsic effects, which include: reducing contact resistance by suppressing metal-induced gap states (MIGS) at metal/Ge interfaces, minimising parasitic capacitances by device scaling, minimising interface traps through all-epitaxial technology, lowering pad parasitics, mitigating substrate loss using insulating substrates, germanium-on-insulator (GeOI) platforms, or deep trench isolation (DTI)60. Through these advanced device engineering strategies that optimise intrinsic material properties and suppress parasitic effects, the operating frequency of the barristor can be extended into the THz regime.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a silicon–graphene–germanium barristor that achieves a current gain up to 1.8 × 107 and an intrinsic cutoff frequency up to 132 GHz for vertical 2D base transistors. This breakthrough, enabled by a unique structure that exploits graphene’s quantum capacitance and asymmetric Schottky barrier heights, effectively addresses the interface limitations that have hindered the high-frequency performance of previous vertical 2D base transistors. Our results establish this architecture as a promising candidate for terahertz operation, which is pivotal for the data-intensive demands of future IoT and 6 G sensing networks. To fully realise this potential, future work will focus on optimising intrinsic material properties and mitigating extrinsic parasitic effects, thereby translating this laboratory achievement into a technology capable of powering the next-generation ultra-fast sensing and communication systems.

Methods

Preparation of the graphene-on-Ge substrate

An n-type germanium (Ge) substrate (resistivities of 0.01, 0.05, 0.1–1.0, and 35 Ω cm) with a < 110> crystal orientation was cleaned using electronic-grade hydrofluoric acid (HF, 40 wt%) for 2 minutes to remove surface oxides and impurities. The substrate was then placed in a quartz tube, which was evacuated to a base pressure of 0.1 Pa before being backfilled with high-purity argon (Ar, 700 sccm) and hydrogen (H2, 70 sccm). After heating the system to 916 °C, methane (CH4, 2.2 sccm) was introduced to initiate the growth of single-crystal, single-layer graphene.

Fabrication of the Si membranes

An n-type <100> silicon-on-insulator (SOI) substrate with a 1.5-μm-thick, single-crystal top Si layer (resistivity range: 1–20 Ω cm, 0.01–0.025 Ω cm and 0.001 Ω cm) and a 0.5 μm-thick buried SiO2 layer was used to provide the membranes. Phosphorus ion implantation (energy: 30 keV, dosage: 1015 cm–2) was used to heavily dope the surface of the top Si layer for ohmic contact formation. A Ti/Au (5/50 nm) electrode was fabricated by photolithography and electron beam evaporation (EBE). For photolithography, photoresist LOR3A (spin-coated at 3000 r.p.m. for 50 s, baked at 190 °C for 5 min) and S-1813 (spin-coated at 5000 r.p.m. for 50 s, baked at 120 °C for 2 min) were used in sequence. Part of the Si was etched using photolithography and reactive ion etching (RIE) (CF4/O2 = 25: 5 sccm, 13.3 Pa, RF power = 100 W, 10 min 30 s), leaving the circular Si membrane array on the buried oxide layer (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Finally, the buried oxide was removed by placing it in 40 wt% HF for 8 min 30 s, leaving the patterned top Si membranes on the bottom Si substrate, so that the Si membranes could be exfoliated by a commercial polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) film (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Fabrication of the barristor array

The Si membrane array was transferred onto the prepared graphene-on-Ge substrate using a PDMS film, forming the structure of Si/graphene/Ge (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Ti/Au electrodes (5/50 nm) on graphene were then formed by photolithography and EBE, and photolithography and O2 plasma etching (200 W, 180 sccm, 2 min) were used for graphene patterning to ensure the isolation of each device (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). To insulate the GSG pads from the substrate, an Al2O3 insulating layer was deposited on top of the wafer by ALD at 200 °C (precursors: trimethylaluminum and water), and the windows were made to expose Ge for the following phosphorus ion implantation (energy: 30 keV, dosage: 1016 cm–2), aiming to provide an ohmic contact area with the Al/Ti/Au (30/5/50 nm) electrode (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Windows on the Si and graphene were then opened by photolithography and H3PO4 ( > 85 wt%) wet etching for 30 min (Supplementary Fig. 2h). Finally, the GSG-structure electrodes of 800 nm Au were formed by photolithography and EBE (Supplementary Fig. 2i).

The noise factor of the transistor was measured using the noise measurement module of a Rohde & Schwarz ZNA43 vector network analyser. The device under test was biased at the same operating point as used for the S-parameter characterisation (VDS = 7 V and VGS = 6 V). Silvaco TCAD simulation of the Si/graphene/Ge barrister

In the simulation, the Si region features a Gaussian doping profile centred at the gate electrode with a peak concentration of 1.2 × 1018 cm–3 and a characteristic length of 0.45 μm, resulting in a concentration of approximately 1 × 1015 cm–3 near the Si/graphene interface. The Ge doping near the Ge/graphene interface is 2.5 × 1016 cm–3, while the Ge bulk doping is 4 × 1017 cm–3. All three electrodes are configured as ohmic contacts (Supplementary Fig. 17a). As the gate voltage VGS varies, the Schottky barrier heights between graphene and both Si and Ge change accordingly, as illustrated by the simulated band diagrams in Supplementary Fig. 17b. With increasing VGS, the barrier heights on both sides decrease simultaneously, confirming that our device operates based on the barristor mechanism.
