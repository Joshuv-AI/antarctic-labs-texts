Multifaceted effects of proline on intestinal injury, inflammation, and lipid metabolism in piglets induced by porcine epidemic diarrhea virus
Authors: Zhuan Song, Meng Shen, Huanhuan Xu, Yujie Wang, Lintong Zhao, Zhonghua Li, Peng Li, Lei Wang, Di Zhao, Dan Yi, Tao Wu, Yongqing Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of proline on intestinal injury in piglets infected with porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV). Twenty-eight 7-day-old piglets were assigned to four groups: control (CON), proline (Pro), PEDV, and Pro + PEDV. Piglets in the Pro and Pro + PEDV groups received oral proline (500 mg/kg body weight) for 7 days. During the last 3 days, piglets in the PEDV and Pro + PEDV groups were inoculated with PEDV. Under PEDV infection, proline administration increased villus height and the villus height/crypt depth ratio in both the jejunum and ileum, and villus width and villus surface area in the ileum. Proline increased PEDV N protein abundance and interferon β (IFN-β) mRNA level in both intestinal segments, suppressed downstream antiviral genes in infected piglets, but upregulated these genes in the jejunum of uninfected piglets. Proline further increased interleukin 1β (IL-1β) concentrations and the expression of inflammation-related genes in the jejunum of infected piglets. Under PEDV infection, proline increased superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in serum and jejunum, while reducing jejunal H2O2 concentration. Furthermore, proline downregulated genes related to lipid synthesis and ion transport in the jejunum of infected piglets. Transcriptomic and RT-qPCR analyses supported these findings, indicating that proline enhanced immune defense while suppressing lipid metabolism. In conclusion, proline alleviated PEDV-induced intestinal injury by improving villus morphology and enhancing antiviral and antioxidant defenses. However, it promoted viral replication and inflammation, while suppressing lipid metabolism. The multifaceted effects of proline on PEDV infection indicate its practical use warrants careful consideration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00726-026-03547-9.Keywords: Proline, Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus, Piglet, Antiviral response, Inflammation, Lipid metabolism

Introduction

The intestine is not only the primary site of nutrient digestion and absorption but also the largest immune organ in the body. In recent years, intestinal damage induced by diverse stressors has become increasingly prevalent in pigs, impairing growth performance, reducing survival rates, and ultimately constraining the development of the swine industry. Among these stressors, pathogenic infections pose a particularly serious threat. Porcine epidemic diarrhea (PED), caused by porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), has led to significant economic losses worldwide (Zhang et al. 2023). PEDV-induced damage to the small intestine is the primary pathological basis for diarrhea, resulting in high mortality in neonatal piglets and long-term growth impairments in survivors (Wang et al. 2024; Xing et al. 2025). Current strategies for PEDV control rely mainly on vaccination, which provides only partial protection. This highlights the urgent need for alternative or complementary approaches, including nutritional interventions that support intestinal health, as safe and practical measures for PED prevention and control.

Amino acids are indispensable nutrients in diets, serving not only as substrates for protein synthesis but also exerting a wide range of physiological functions (Ma et al. 2022; Meng et al. 2023). Our previous studies revealed that PEDV infection disrupts amino acid metabolism in piglets, markedly impairing the arginine and proline metabolic pathway (Zhang et al. 2019, 2025). Under normal physiological conditions, endogenous production and interconversion of arginine and proline are generally sufficient to meet metabolic demands. However, dietary fluctuations, developmental stages, and pathological stress can render them conditionally essential (Wu et al. 2011, 2018). For instance, neonatal piglets exhibit elevated tissue proline concentrations, indicating increased metabolic requirements (Wu et al. 2011). This is consistent with milk amino acid profiles, where proline accounts for 9.5% of total amino acids in human milk and 11.7% in sow milk, whereas arginine is relatively low (3.6% and 4.4%, respectively)—levels often insufficient to support neonatal growth. The de novo synthesis of arginine from proline in the small intestine of piglets is well-documented (Davis et al. 1994; Kim and Wu 2004). Interestingly, our previous study found that PEDV infection decreased proline concentrations in plasma and decreased both proline and arginine concentrations in jejunal mucosa (Zhang et al. 2019), and that arginine treatment, while enhancing intestinal barrier function, promoted PEDV replication (Shi et al. 2026). Since arginine carries the risk of promoting viral replication, we turned our attention to its precursor, proline, as a potential alternative intervention. Given the intestinal damage and metabolic disruption caused by the virus, proline supplementation may serve to correct the infection-induced metabolic imbalance and provide nutritional support. This possibility aligns with accumulating findings demonstrating the gut-protective functions of proline.

Indeed, in weaned piglets, previous reports have confirmed that dietary proline improved growth performance and gastrointestinal digestibility (Brunton et al. 2012; Kang et al. 2014), enhanced villus height in the jejunum and ileum, and upregulated the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 (Wang et al. 2015), indicating its capacity to strengthen the epithelial barrier. Moreover, proline was shown to activate lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells and promote IL-22 production, thereby maintaining gut homeostasis and alleviating colitis in mouse models (Wu et al. 2023); proline supplementation increased serum C-reactive protein levels and enhanced immune responses in porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2)-infected mice (Ren et al. 2013), suggesting a potential immunomodulatory role during viral challenge; proline also increased plasma superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in piglets challenged with lipopolysaccharide (Kang et al. 2014). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that proline could mitigate PEDV-induced intestinal damage. To test this hypothesis, the present study was designed to evaluate the effects of proline on intestinal morphology, antiviral and inflammatory responses, antioxidant capacity, and the underlying mechanisms in PEDV-infected piglets, with a particular focus on intestinal lipid metabolism, as revealed by transcriptomic analysis.

Materials and methods

Animal experimental design

Twenty-eight male piglets (7 days old, Duroc × Landrace × Large White) were randomly assigned to four groups: Control (CON), Proline (Pro), PEDV, and Proline + PEDV (Pro + PEDV). The experiment lasted 11 days, comprising a 3-day acclimatization period during which all piglets received only milk replacer (nutrient composition shown in Table 1). Body weights on day 4 were comparable among all groups (Table S1), and no diarrhea was observed in any piglet prior to proline administration. From days 4 to 10, piglets in the Pro and Pro + PEDV groups received oral administration of proline at 500 mg/kg body weight (mixed with milk replacer; Sigma-Aldrich), with the daily dose adjusted according to body weight. The dosage was referenced from Kang et al. (2014), who reported that dietary supplementation with 0.5%–1.0% proline improved growth performance in weaned piglets. Following daily weighing, the calculated proline dose was dissolved in 10 mL of milk replacer and administered by oral gavage using a syringe to ensure complete consumption. Piglets in the CON and PEDV groups received equivalent volumes of milk replacer.

On day 8, piglets in the PEDV and Pro + PEDV groups were orally inoculated with 3 mL of PEDV suspension at 1 × 106 TCID50 per piglet. Piglets in the CON and Pro groups received Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium without PEDV. After an overnight fast on day 11, blood samples were collected from the anterior vena cava. Afterwards, all piglets were euthanized by intravenous injection of pentobarbital, intestinal tissue samples were immediately collected. Approximately 1-cm jejunal and ileal segments were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for observation of intestinal morphology. Remaining intestinal tissue was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for further analyses.

Throughout the trial period, piglets were monitored daily for diarrhea occurrence. According to Chen et al. (Chen et al. 2024), fecal scoring was conducted as follows: 0, normal; 1, mushy; 2, somewhat watery; 3, completely liquid. Observations were conducted six times daily (7:30, 11:00, 15:00, 18:00, 19:30, and 21:00) from days 8 to 10 post-PEDV challenge. For each piglet, daily fecal scores were averaged across the six observations. All assessors were trained using standardized fecal photographs and were blinded to treatment allocation.

Analysis of small intestinal morphology

Jejunal and ileal morphology was examined as previously described (Yi et al. 2018a). Briefly, intestinal tissues preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (6 μm). After xylene deparaffinization and ethanol rehydration, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological examination. For each sample, ten optimally preserved villi were selected for imaging using an Olympus BX41 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) coupled with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD).

Antioxidant-related indices in serum and jejunum

Serum and jejunal tissue homogenates were used to assess antioxidant parameters. The enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), as well as the concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were quantified using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Cytokine concentration measurement

The concentrations of interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) in serum and jejunum were measured using commercially ELISA kits (AAT Bioquest, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) following the standard protocols provided by the manufacturer.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from intestinal tissue using the RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was subsequently synthesized using the PrimeScript® RT reagent kit (Takara, Dalian, China) under standard reaction conditions (37 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s). Gene expression quantification was performed on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Alameda, CA) with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, Dalian, China) under the following cycling protocol: 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 34 s. The 2­ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative gene expression, with RPL4 as the reference gene. All primer sequences are listed in Table 2.

Western blotting analysis

Jejunal and ileal tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer using an extraction kit (KeyGEN, Jiangsu, China), centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min, and the supernatants were subjected to protein quantification with a BCA kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated on SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred them to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) using semi-dry transfer blotting. After-blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies followed by secondary antibodies (primary: 4 °C, 16 h; secondary: room temperature, 2 h). Protein band signals were detected using the FluorChem® FC2 imaging system (Alpha Innotech, CA, USA). The antibodies used in this study were as follows: PEDV N (provided by Huazhong Agricultural University, 1:1000), GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, 2118 S, 1:1000).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from jejunal samples and quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to obtain precise RNA Integrity Numbers (RINs). mRNA was selectively captured from total RNA using Oligo(dT)-immobilized magnetic beads, which specifically interact with polyadenylated transcripts. Subsequently, the isolated mRNA was fragmented into approximately 300 bp fragments using a fragmentation buffer, followed by cDNA synthesis through reverse transcription for library construction. The resulting libraries were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina Hiseq platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized using the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with fold change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67, and P-adjust < 0.05. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed via the DAVID platform. Data processing and visualization were conducted using the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.Majorbio.com) as described by Zhang et al. (Zhang et al. 2025). Furthermore, protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING online database (https://string-db.org/), and hub genes were identified using Cytoscape (v.3.7.1) with the CytoHubba plugin.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEMs. Two-way ANOVA was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The main effects of the model included PEDV and proline, and the interactive effect between PEDV and proline. When there was an interactive effect, group differences were determined with Duncan’s multiple range test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Growth performance data obtained before PEDV infection were analyzed using the independent-samples t-tests. For fecal scoring, data are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test.

Results

Proline improved intestinal morphology in PEDV-infected piglets

As shown in Fig. 1A and B, and Table 3, proline administration had no significant effect on the average daily gain (ADG) or fecal score of piglets (P > 0.05). Anatomical observations presented normal intestinal morphology in the CON and Pro groups. In contrast, piglets in the PEDV and Pro + PEDV groups exhibited thinned, translucent intestinal walls and watery luminal contents (Fig. 1C). Consistent with these observations, PEDV infection elevated serum diamine oxidase (DAO) activity (Fig. 1D, P < 0.05). Histological evaluation demonstrated intact, elongated, and well-structured villi in the CON and Pro groups, whereas the villi in the PEDV group were shortened and severely damaged. Proline administration partially alleviated these morphological impairments (Fig. 1E). As shown in Table 4, quantitative analysis indicated significant interactive effects between PEDV infection and proline treatment on villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio in both the jejunum and ileum, as well as villus width and villus surface area in the ileum (P < 0.05). PEDV infection significantly decreased villus height, villus height/crypt depth ratio, and villus surface area in the jejunum and ileum, and decreased jejunal crypt depth and villus width. Under PEDV infection, proline administration significantly improved villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio in both the jejunum and ileum and increased ileal villus width and villus surface area (P < 0.05).

Effects of proline on small intestinal growth and development in PEDV-infected piglets

As illustrated in Fig. 2A-C, PEDV infection did not affect jejunal DNA or RNA concentrations or the RNA/DNA ratio. However, PEDV infection reduced total protein (TP) concentration and the TP/RNA ratio in the jejunum (Fig. 2D and E, P < 0.05). Notably, proline treatment remarkably increased the RNA/DNA ratio (Fig. 2C, P < 0.05). In the ileum, PEDV infection and proline treatment had a significant interactive effect on TP level, whereas proline administration had no effect on TP concentration under infection (Fig. 2D). Proline administration elevated ileal RNA concentration and the RNA/DNA ratio (Fig. 2B and C, P < 0.05) and increased TP concentration in non-infected piglets (Fig. 2D, P < 0.05).

Antiviral effects of proline

The PEDV N gene was used as molecular indicator of viral replication. Compared with the PEDV group, proline administration had no effect on jejunal PEDV N gene expression (Fig. 3A, P > 0.05) but significantly upregulated ileal PEDV N gene expression and protein abundance in both intestinal segments (Fig. 3B-D and Fig. S1-2). Interestingly, significant interactive effects between PEDV infection and proline treatment were observed for several antiviral genes in the jejunum, including interferon β (IFN-β), interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7), myxovirus resistance protein 1 (MX1), 2’-5’oligoadenylate synthetase-like (OASL), and interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15). PEDV infection upregulated the expression of all five genes. Proline administration reversed the PEDV-induced upregulation of MX1, OASL, and ISG15, but further enhanced IFN-β and IRF7 expression. Interestingly, proline administration generally upregulated these antiviral genes in non-infected piglets (Fig. 3E, P < 0.05). Similarly, in the ileum, interactive effects were observed for the same set of antiviral genes. Under PEDV infection, proline suppressed IRF7, MX1, OASL, and ISG15 expression, while further upregulating IFN-β (Fig. 3F, P < 0.05).

Proline strengthened the inflammatory response in PEDV-infected piglets

Serum and jejunal IL-1β concentrations exhibited strong interactive effects between PEDV infection and proline treatment (Fig. 4A and C, P < 0.05). PEDV infection markedly elevated IL-1β levels, and proline further amplified this increase (Fig. 4A and C, P < 0.05). PEDV infection also increased serum TNF-α concentrations (Fig. 4B, P < 0.05). Significant interactive effects were also observed for mRNA expression of inflammatory genes in the jejunum, including IL-1β, IL-8, and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 2 (CXCL2). PEDV infection upregulated the expression of all three genes, and proline further enhanced these responses (Fig. 4D, P < 0.05). Additionally, PEDV infection and proline treatment interacted to regulate genes related to intestinal injury, including S100 calcium binding protein A2 (S100A2), matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13), and mucin2 (MUC2) (Fig. 4E, P < 0.05). PEDV infection generally upregulated S100A2, MMP13, MUC2, and regenerating islet-derived protein 3 gamma (REG3γ). Proline further increased S100A2 and MMP13 expression, while downregulating MUC2 expression under infection (Fig. 4E, P < 0.05).

Proline enhanced the antioxidant function in PEDV-infected piglets

As shown in Fig. 5, significant interactive effects between PEDV infection and proline treatment were detected for serum SOD, CAT and GSH-Px activities. PEDV infection decreased serum SOD and CAT activities, whereas proline restored SOD and GSH-Px activities under infection (Fig. 5A-C, P < 0.05). In non-infected pigs, proline administration decreased serum MDA concentration (Fig. 5D, P < 0.05). No group differences were observed in serum H2O2 concentration (Fig. 5E, P > 0.05).

In the jejunum, interactive effects were observed for SOD activity and H2O2 content. PEDV infection decreased jejunal SOD and CAT activities; proline administration increased jejunal SOD activity and reduced H2O2 concentration under infection (Fig. 6A, B and E, P < 0.05). Additionally, proline administration increased jejunal GSH-Px activity and decreased MDA concentration in non-infected piglets (Fig. 6C and D, P < 0.05).

Effects of proline on the transcriptional profile of the jejunum in PEDV-infected piglets

Compared to the CON group, proline administration upregulated 141 genes and downregulated 586 genes, whereas PEDV infection upregulated 697 genes and downregulated 501 genes. Compared with the PEDV group, proline treatment upregulated 192 genes and downregulated 548 genes (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 7B-a, a total of 13 differential expression genes (DEGs) were identified among the three comparison groups, and 47 DEGs overlapped between the PEDV vs. CON and Pro + PEDV vs. PEDV comparisons. Notably, 12 DEGs upregulated in the PEDV vs. CON comparison were downregulated in the Pro + PEDV vs. PEDV comparison (Fig. 7B-b), while 20 DEGs showed the opposite expression pattern (Fig. 7B-c). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs between PEDV and CON groups were primarily involved in “Amoebiasis”, “TNF signaling pathway”, “Glutathione metabolism”, “Pyrimidine metabolism”, “Ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes”, and “AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications” (Fig. 7C). DEGs between Pro + PEDV and PEDV groups were mainly enriched in “Retinol metabolism”, “Peroxisome”, “Chemical carcinogenesis - DNA adducts”, “PPAR signaling pathway”, “Fat digestion and absorption”, and “Bile secretion” (Fig. 7D). PPI network analysis using the STING database and Cytoscape identified hub genes with distinct functional profiles. In the Pro vs. CON comparison, antiviral-related genes predominated, including IRF7, MX1, OASL, interferon gamma inducible protein 16 (IF16), OAS1, OAS2, and interferon-induced transmembrane protein 1 (IFITM1) (Fig. 7E). As for the Pro + PEDV vs. PEDV comparison, the hub genes were mainly associated with inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism, including IL-1β, CXCL2, CXCL8, toll-like 2 (TLR2), suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3), fatty acid binding protein 1 (FABP1), FABP2, apolipoprotein B (APOB), and APOC3 (Fig. 7F). RT-qPCR results validated the transcriptome findings (Fig. 7E-F).

Discussion

PEDV continues to pose a severe challenge to the global swine industry, primarily because of its ability to destroy the intestinal epithelium of neonatal piglets, causing malabsorption, dehydration, and high mortality. Given the multifactorial pathogenesis involving in broad metabolic dysregulation, the development of effective nutritional interventions is of considerable importance (Bappy et al. 2024). Previous studies have shown that PEDV infection dysregulates the arginine-proline metabolic pathway (Zhang et al. 2019, 2025). Building upon this evidence, the present study examined whether exogenous proline administration could mitigate PEDV-induced intestinal injury. Although proline administration promoted PEDV proliferation, it conferred significant intestinal protection. Specifically, proline improved villus morphology, enhanced antioxidant capacity and modulated antiviral signaling. These protective effects were accompanied by a compensatory shift toward heightened inflammatory activation and reduced lipid metabolic activity, suggesting that proline influences intestinal homeostasis through coordinated immune–metabolic interactions.

PEDV mainly infects the villus epithelium of the small intestine, leading to villus atrophy, epithelial sloughing, and compromised nutrient absorption. Consistent with previous reports (Song et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025), PEDV-challenged piglets in our study exhibited reduced ADG, thinning of intestinal walls, watery luminal contents, and severe villus shortening. Proline supplementation did not improve ADG, likely due to the short intervention period, but it significantly elevated villus height, villus height/crypt depth ratio, and ileal villus surface area in infected piglets (Verdile et al. 2019). These findings indicate that proline attenuates epithelial destruction and promotes structural restoration. Changes in intestinal DNA, RNA, and protein content reflect epithelial proliferation and functional maturation (Ma et al. 2023; Yi et al. 2018b). Proline increased ileal RNA and protein concentrations and elevated the RNA/DNA ratio in both intestinal segments, suggesting enhanced transcriptional and translational activity. Collectively, these results demonstrate that proline supports epithelial renewal and contributes to improved gut integrity under PEDV infection.

PEDV structural gene N is commonly used biomarkers for viral quantification (Lei et al. 2024). In this study, proline increased PEDV N protein abundance both in the jejunum and ileum, suggesting a promotion effect on viral proliferation. Previous evidences have indicated that high arginine availability could promote replication of several viruses, including SARS-CoV-2 (Grimes et al. 2021), bombyx mori nucleopolyhedrovirus (Fei et al. 2025), and oncolytic myxoma virus (Dryja et al. 2023). Notably, the PEDV N protein is rich in arginine residues (Li et al. 2024). A possible explanation of this pro-viral effect is that proline, as a metabolic precursor of arginine, may indirectly support the synthesis of the arginine-rich N protein. The metabolic–viral interactions involving proline–arginine interconversion warrant further mechanistic research.

The type I interferon (IFN) pathway establishes a potent natural defense barrier against viruses by inducing the expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), such as MX1, MX2, and the OAS family via activation of the Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway (Cooper et al. 2024). Critically, the induction of IFNs is tightly regulated by the IRF family, with IRF3 and IRF7 playing pivotal roles in initiation and amplification of this response (Qin et al. 2022). Consistent with previous reports (Song et al. 2024), PEDV infection upregulated the expression of IFN-β, MX1, OASL, and ISG15 in the small intestine. Proline selectively elevated jejunal IRF7 and IFN-β in both the jejunum and ileum (consistent with increased PEDV N expression) but suppressed downstream ISGs (MX1, OASL, and ISG15) in infected piglets, while upregulating all these genes in uninfected piglets. This suggests dual regulatory features: immune-priming in the healthy intestine, and negative feedback regulation (possibly through SOCS2 induction) under viral stress to prevent hyperactivation. Supporting this, proline treatment elevated the expression of suppressor of cytokine signaling 2 (SOCS2), a known negative regulator of JAK/STAT signaling (Huang et al. 2020), in the jejunum of PEDV-infected piglets. Proline-mediated immune regulation has been observed in other viral models, for example, Zakirova et al. (Zakirova et al. 2024) verified that proline suppressed the replication of hepatitis C virus (HCV), which was correlate with polyamine and arginine metabolism, while Ren et al. (Ren et al. 2013) claimed that proline reduced the proliferation of porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) through immune enhancement. Whether the differential patterns in our study are linked to proline-derived polyamine and arginine metabolism requires further investigation.

Upon viral invasion, host cells activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), initiating tightly regulated signaling cascades that promote the production and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and interferons, thereby coordinating antiviral immunity to eliminate the invading pathogen (Carroll et al. 2024). Consistent with the observed upregulation of IFN-β, PEDV infection increased serum and jejunal IL-1β and TNF-α, along with upregulation of IL-1β, IL-8, and CXCL2. Proline further amplified these inflammatory responses in PEDV-infected piglets, in line with previous results indicating proline-induced TNF‑α elevation in PCV2-infected mice (Ren et al. 2013). Within this inflammatory cascade,‌ S100A2 acts as a calcium-sensing protein that promotes cytokine secretion ‌via‌ NF-κB activation (Singh and Ali 2022). MMP13, an inducible matrix metalloproteinase, degrades intestinal collagen, impairing barrier integrity and aggravating inflammation when over-expressed (Koop et al. 2023). PEDV infection upregulated the expression of S100A2 and MMP13, and this effect was further enhanced by proline treatment. Additionally, components of the mucosal barrier also responded to infection. MUC2, the major mucin secreted by goblet cells and localized to the apical intestinal epithelium, forms the structural basis of the mucus layer, thereby limiting microbial contact and reducing epithelial immune activation (Wang et al. 2025). REG3γ, a C-type lectin predominantly produced by Paneth cells, is induced by microbial and inflammatory stimuli and resides within the mucus layer, where it suppresses bacterial translocation and maintains barrier homeostasis (Shin et al. 2022). In this study, concurrent upregulation of jejunal MUC2 and REG3γ following PEDV infection suggests activation of a coordinated defense program aimed at mitigating virus-induced epithelial injury, preventing bacterial translocation, and reducing the risk of secondary infection by reinforcing the mucus barrier and enhancing antimicrobial immunity. Proline treatment downregulated MUC2 expression in infected piglets, reflecting its role in modulating PEDV-induced dysregulation of mucin secretion. Collectively, these findings confirm that PEDV infection induces intestinal damage and triggers robust immune and barrier responses, whereas proline administration further augments host immune activation, potentially enhancing defense capacity against viral pathogens.

In addition to its immunomodulatory properties, proline also exhibited antioxidant activity. Oxidative stress and inflammation are closely interconnected processes commonly observed in pig production (Hong et al. 2024; Lan et al. 2025). Excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative byproducts beyond the neutralizing capacity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes impairs intestinal barrier integrity and compromises immune defense (Radi 2018). Thus, mitigating oxidative stress represents a promising strategy to improve intestinal health (Hong et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024). In the present study, proline primarily enhanced antioxidant defense, as demonstrated by increased serum SOD and GSH-Px levels, along with elevated jejunal SOD and GSH-Px activities in PEDV-infected piglets. This observation is consistent with emerging evidence that proline attenuates cellular oxidative damage, possibly by supporting mitochondrial function (Cheng et al. 2025; Tang and Pang 2016).

To further elucidate the intriguing immunomodulatory effects of proline, we examined the intestinal gene expression profile of PEDV-infected piglets. PEDV infection significantly dysregulated genes enriched in the “TNF signaling pathway”‌, corroborating its role in triggering inflammatory responses. Notably, ‌proline treatment‌ predominantly modulated genes associated with “‌Fat digestion and absorption”‌ and ‌“‌Bile secretion” pathways. The small intestine maintains a dynamic balance between nutrient absorption and host defense, particularly in relation to lipid metabolism under stress or infection (Ko et al. 2020; Turner 2009; Yu et al. 2025). Our findings indicate that proline ‌may shift intestinal epithelial function toward ‌immune defense‌ at the expense of absorptive capacity under PEDV infection, as reflected by upregulation of inflammation-related hub genes and downregulation of lipid metabolism-related hub genes in proline-treated piglets. Apolipoproteins are critical for lipid transport and absorption. Consistent with previous reports (Mao et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025), we found that PEDV infection disrupted lipid metabolic homeostasis, and proline further suppressed expression‌ of apolipoprotein-related genes. Beyond nutrient metabolism, the pathophysiology of diarrhea involves disturbances in ion transport, particularly sodium and potassium fluxes. Although PEDV impaired intestinal integrity, ‌proline exacerbated inhibition of ion transport, which may explain its partial mitigation of villous injury but lack of efficacy in alleviating PEDV-induced diarrhea. To obtain deeper mechanistic insights, ‌single-cell sequencing‌ may help delineate how proline coordinately regulates immune activation and nutrient metabolism suppression under PEDV infection. Additionally, although we previously observed that PEDV infection reduced proline and arginine concentrations in plasma and jejunum (Zhang et al. 2019), we did not directly measure the activities of key enzymes involved in proline-to-arginine conversion, such as ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) and pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase (P5CS), in the intestinal mucosa. Given that PEDV infection causes severe villus atrophy and extensive enterocyte shedding, it is plausible that the enzymatic capacity for proline-to-arginine conversion is compromised post-infection. Future studies employing enzymatic activity assays or targeted metabolomics in isolated enterocytes are warranted to determine whether the conversion pathway remains functional under PEDV challenge.

In summary, this study demonstrates that oral administration of proline alleviates intestinal injury in piglets. Proline improved villus structure, enhanced epithelial growth, and strengthened antioxidant capacity. These protective effects were associated with a coordinated shift toward heightened immune activation and reduced lipid metabolic activity, reflecting an adaptive immune–metabolic reprogramming. Additionally, our results indicate that proline enhances PEDV replication by modulating the interferon responses. This reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism of action for proline during PEDV infection in piglets, which offers a theoretical basis for considering proline as a health-promoting feed additive. Future studies should focus on evaluating long-term outcomes and exploring synergistic benefits with other functional amino acids.
