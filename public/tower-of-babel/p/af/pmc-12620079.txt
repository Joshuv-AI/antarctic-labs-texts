Sex differences in the association between energy‐adjusted dietary inflammatory index and cognitive function in older adults
Authors: Ke Liu, Yan Zhong, Peili Cen, La Dong, Yuhan Chen, Daoyan Hu, Chentao Jin, Rui Zhou, Yuanjie Chen, Yuan Sun, Xiaohui Zhang, Jing Wang, Mei Tian, Hong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONDiet‐induced inflammation may influence cognitive health in older adults, yet sex‐specific associations across distinct cognitive domains remain unclear.METHODSUsing weighted multivariable regression, weighted restricted cubic splines, and weighted risk group analyses (energy‐adjusted Dietary Inflammatory Index [E‐DII] lowest quartiles), we assessed E‐DII associations with four cognitive tests (Animal Fluency Test [AFT], Digit Symbol Substitution Test [DSST], Delayed Recall Test [DRT], and Immediate Recall Test [IRT]) in older adults. All analyses included sex stratification or sex interaction. Weighted mediation analysis by 15 blood biochemical markers was explored.RESULTSIn older males, higher ln(E‐DII) was linked to poorer AFT and DSST performance and increased low cognition risk in AFT, DSST, and DRT; no associations were found in older females. Gamma‐glutamyl transferase mediated the ln(E‐DII)‐DSST association in older males.DISCUSSIONThese sex‐specific links highlight the need for personalized dietary strategies to protect cognitive health in aging populations.Highlights E‐DII exhibits sex‐specific associations with multiple cognitive domains in older adults.Higher ln(E‐DII) is linked to poorer verbal fluency (AFT), processing speed (DSST), and increased low cognition risk (AFT, DSST, and DRT) in older males, but not in older females.Gamma‐glutamyl transferase mediates the association between ln(E‐DII) and DSST performance specifically in older males.Findings support the development of personalized, sex‐specific dietary strategies to preserve cognitive health in aging populations.

BACKGROUND

With the rapid aging of the global population, cognitive decline and dementia have emerged as pressing public health challenges, having profound impacts on the health, autonomy, and quality of life of older adults. 1 , 2 A multidomain intervention (AgeWell.de trial) showed that improving diet reduced dementia risk, highlighting dietary intervention's importance for cognitive function. 3 Among the diverse mechanisms implicated in cognitive deterioration, chronic inflammation has increasingly been recognized as a key driver of neurodegenerative processes. 4 , 5 In this context, growing attention has focused on the modulation of systemic inflammation, particularly through dietary factors and its potential role in influencing cognitive trajectories in aging populations. 6

The energy‐adjusted Dietary Inflammatory Index (E‐DII) is a widely validated tool for quantifying the overall inflammatory potential of an individual's diet. 7 , 8 , 9 It is derived from robust associations between dietary components and inflammatory biomarkers – including pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin [IL]‐1β, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor‐α [TNF‐α]) and acute‐phase reactants (e.g., C‐reactive protein [CRP]), as well as anti‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐4, IL‐10). The E‐DII provides a standardized metric for evaluating diet‐driven systemic inflammation. 8 Elevated E‐DII scores have been associated with an increased risk of various chronic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, 10 cardiovascular disease, and mild cognitive impairment. 11 , 12 Importantly, some of these associations have been found to exhibit sex‐specific differences. 13 While emerging evidence further indicates that higher E‐DII scores may be linked to poorer cognitive performance in older adults, 12 findings remain inconsistent across populations and cognitive domains, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 and sex‐stratified analyses are notably scarce.

Given the well‐documented sex differences in the biological traits (hormonal regulation, 18 , 19 , 20 immune system function, 21 and gut microbiota composition 22 ), as well as behavioral and sociocultural factors (dietary behaviors 23 , 24 , 25 and educational patterns 26 ), the complex interplay of these biological, psychological, and sociocultural elements may play a critical role in modulating the diet–inflammation–cognition pathway and could drive distinct physiological responses to dietary inflammatory stimuli, thereby potentially altering the magnitude or direction of associations between E‐DII and cognitive outcomes. This hypothesis is rarely tested in existing literature, which remains limited and inconsistent. 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 However, most existing human studies have treated sex as merely a covariate or baseline characteristics, 28 , 32 , 33 , 34 rather than examining its potential role as an effect modifier in the diet–cognition relationship. Notably, no studies have specifically investigated whether dietary inflammation (as quantified by E‐DII) exerts differential impacts on distinct cognitive domains in older adults within a sex‐stratified analytical framework.

In this study, we selected four cognitive tests to capture distinct yet interrelated domains of cognitive function, collectively providing a multidimensional profile of cognitive health in aging. The Animal Fluency Test (AFT), Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), Immediate Recall Test (IRT), and Delayed Recall Test (DRT) were used to assess verbal fluency/semantic memory, attention/processing speed, short‐term memory, and long‐term episodic memory, respectively. 35 , 36 , 37 These domains are validated for assessing cognitive decline and distinguishing impairment stages, making them ideal for investigating diet—inflammation–cognition pathways and clarifying how dietary inflammatory potential interacts with sex to influence cognitive aging.

To fill current knowledge gaps, we utilized data from a large, nationally representative sample of older adults to systematically examine the association between E‐DII and performance across the four cognitive domains represented by AFT, DSST, IRT, and DRT. We employed two complementary statistical approaches, multivariable linear regression and restricted cubic splines, to investigate both linear and non‐linear relationships. Interaction terms and sex‐stratified analyses were used to identify potential sex‐specific effects. Furthermore, mediation analyses were conducted to explore biological pathways linking E‐DII and cognitive function, based on 15 blood biochemical markers. These analyses aimed to reveal sex‐specific biological mechanisms underlying the relationship between dietary inflammation and cognitive aging.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a cross‐sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), using a multistage, stratified, probability sampling design to represent the non‐institutionalized U.S. population. 38 All protocols were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For this study, we included adults aged ≥ 60 years from the 2011–2014 NHANES cycles. The 2011–2014 cycles were selected because they are the only NHANES waves with concurrent data for all four cognitive tests central to our analysis (AFT, DSST, IRT, and DRT). Earlier cycles included only DSST, while post‐2014 waves omitted one or more tests (e.g., replacing recall tasks with clock drawing). This restriction ensured consistent, multidimensional cognitive assessment across participants. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1), the final analytic sample comprised 2712 participants.

Selection process for participants’ inclusion in the study.

To ensure national representativeness and valid variance estimation, all analyses incorporated NHANES complex survey design features: sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units. Because this study utilized dietary interview, examination, and questionnaire data, we applied the Day 1 dietary weights (WTDRD1) following NHANES analytic guidelines. Strata (SDMVSTRA) and cluster (SDMVPSU) variables were included to account for stratification and clustering. When combining the two cycles (2011–2012 and 2013–2014), weights were adjusted (divided by 2) as recommended.

E‐DII

Dietary data were obtained from two 24‐h dietary recall interviews using standardized NHANES protocols: The first was conducted in person at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), and the second was administered by telephone 3 to 10 days later. In this study, E‐DII was calculated using data from the first recall only, based on 27 available dietary components and included only food and beverage intake (excluding dietary supplements): alcohol, vitamins A, B6, B12, C, D, and E; β‐carotene, caffeine, carbohydrates, cholesterol, total fat, dietary fiber, folic acid, iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids, protein, niacin, omega‐3 and omega‐6 fatty acids, riboflavin, saturated fat, and thiamine.

For each participant, daily intakes of these components were energy‐adjusted using the nutrient density method (per 1000 kcal), standardized to a global reference database to obtain Z‐scores, converted to centered percentiles, multiplied by literature‐derived inflammatory effect scores, and summed to produce the overall E‐DII score. 8 , 9 Higher scores reflect a more pro‐inflammatory diet, and lower (negative) scores indicate a more anti‐inflammatory profile.

We evaluated the distribution of E‐DII scores across the total population and by sex, focusing on skewness and normality: E‐DII showed right skewness (with skewness values of 4.29 in the total population, 2.77 in males, and 6.63 in females) and non‐normality (Shapiro‐Wilk test, all p < 0.001). Given the right‐skewed distribution and the fact that E‐DII inherently contains both positive and negative values, we performed a segmented natural logarithm (ln) transformation to improve normality: negative values were transformed using −ln(abs(E‐DII) + 1 + 1e‐5), positive values using ln(E‐DII + 1 + 1e‐5), and zero values retained as 0. After transformation, skewness was substantially reduced to 0.146 (total population), −0.11 (males), and 0.401 (females). However, Shapiro‐Wilk tests still indicated non‐normality for all groups (all p < 0.001), confirming the transformation improved symmetry but did not achieve full normality. Q‐Q plots and density distributions of ln(E‐DII) for the total population, males, and females are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Figures S1 and S2), visually showing that the ln transformation reduced skewness and improved distribution symmetry, though normality was not fully achieved.

Measurement of cognitive abilities

Cognitive ability was assessed using the AFT, DSST, and the Consortium to Establish a Registry for AD (CERAD) word list learning test to evaluate participants’ cognitive function. These tests have been demonstrated to be highly reliable in determining an individual's cognitive level. 39 The IRT and DRT are components of the CERAD word list learning test and are utilized to assess the immediate and delayed acquisition of new verbal material. The AFT was used to measure executive function, requiring participants to name as many animals as possible within 60 s, with the total number of correctly identified animals contributing to the final score. DSST was employed to evaluate participants’ reaction speed, sustained attention, and working memory, with the total number of correctly matched numbers and symbols serving as the score. The gold standard for defining poor cognitive performance on the first four cognitive tests has not been established; however, higher scores on all tests indicate better cognitive performance. 40

Covariates

In addition to the aforementioned 27 inflammatory dietary factors and total energy, we also considered several potentially confounding variables. These included body mass index (BMI) calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2), age (as a continuous variable, in years), age categories (60 to 69 years, 70 to 79 years, over 80 years), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity, categorized according to NHANES 2011–2014 standards (variable RIDRETH3), including non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, non‐Hispanic Asian, Hispanic (Mexican American and other Hispanic subgroups), and other/multiracial (non‐Hispanic individuals reporting more than one race or races other than White, Black, or Asian), educational attainment (below grade 9; grades 9 to 11; high school graduate/General Educational Development; some college; college degree, or above), smoking status (never smoked, current smoker, and former smoker), alcohol consumption (1 to 5 times/month; 5 to 10 drinks/month; 10+ drinks/month; non‐drinker), poverty‐to‐income ratio (PIR) (PIR < 1.00 indicating below poverty level; PIR ≥ 1 indicating above poverty level), and the presence of chronic conditions such as emphysema, chronic bronchitis, heart disease, cancer, and stroke.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the survey package for complex survey design adjustments. For categorical variables, descriptive statistics were computed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables without a normal distribution were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (the difference between the 75th and 25th percentiles). Group comparisons for continuous data were assessed via Student's t test (for normally distributed data) or the non‐parametric Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (for non‐normally distributed data), and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐squared test. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p value < 0.05. Missing values were handled based on the variable‐specific missing rate: variables with >10% missing values were excluded to avoid imputation bias. For those with ≤ 10% missing values, group‐matched random imputation was used: Missing values were filled by randomly sampling from 21 complete observations matched by demographic characteristics (age, race/ethnicity, sex, and education; random seed = 123). Imputed values fell within the matched group's distribution range, with minimal impact on data distribution (mean change < 0.01).

Weighted multivariable linear regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models for E‐DII‐cognitive performance associations

To examine the associations between E‐DII and cognitive performance (AFT, DSST, IRT, and DRT), weighted multivariable linear regression and weighted RCS models were used. These models were adjusted for covariates including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, PIR, smoking, alcohol use, and chronic conditions (e.g., stroke and cancer). Sex‐specific effects were evaluated through two complementary approaches: (1) interaction term analysis, which included an E‐DII and sex interaction term in the overall models to formally test for differences in association strength between older males and older females; and (2) sex‐stratified analyses, where separate linear and RCS models were fitted for older male and older female subgroups, using the same covariate adjustment framework as the overall models, to characterize E‐DII‐cognitive performance associations within each sex. All statistical tests across multiple models and cognitive outcomes were corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni adjustment to control the family‐wise error rate.

To assess the robustness of significant associations identified in the primary linear and RCS models, four model specifications were implemented: (1) a base model (full covariate adjustment as described above); (2) a core covariate model (adjusted for BMI, age, race/ethnicity, education, and PIR); (3) a minimal covariate model (adjusted solely for age, race/ethnicity, and education); and (4) an extreme‐value excluded model (excluding E‐DII values outside the 5th to 95th percentiles using simple quantile estimation). This exclusion retained 2440 participants (90.0% of the original analytic sample), ensuring sufficient statistical power.

Weighted risk group analysis: E‐DII quartiles and cognitive performance risk stratification

Risk group analysis examined the association between E‐DII and cognitive test scores (AFT, DSST, IRT, and DRT), categorized into quartiles representing risk groups. Cognitive outcomes were dichotomized into a low cognitive function group and non‐low cognitive function group based on the lowest quartile (with the lowest quartile defined as the low cognitive function group). 41 Survey‐weighted logistic regression models were used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the likelihood of being in the high‐risk group with increasing E‐DII levels, adjusting for covariates including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, PIR, smoking, alcohol use, and chronic disease history (e.g., emphysema, chronic bronchitis, coronary heart disease, cancer, and stroke). Design effects were calculated to evaluate the impact of survey design. Sex‐stratified models assess risk differences between older males and older females. Bonferroni adjustment was applied to control false positives. Sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating the analysis using interview weights (WTINT2YR) instead of examination weights (WTMEC2YR) to verify the robustness of the results.

Weighted mediation analysis: biochemical markers as potential mediators of E‐DII‐cognitive function associations

Mediation analysis examined whether blood biochemical markers mediated the association between E‐DII and cognitive function. We systematically selected potential mediating biomarkers based on a three‐step approach integrating data quality, statistical relevance, and biological plausibility: (1) initial screening for data completeness, excluding indicators with >10% missing values; (2) assessment of Spearman correlations with both the exposure (E‐DII) and cognitive function outcomes (p < 0.05); and (3) selection of 15 candidates (see Table S1 for complete list) with established roles that are either directly or indirectly related to inflammatory processes or neurological disorders, consistent with E‐DII's definition based on blood inflammatory markers and the neurological basis of cognitive function.

We used a three‐model approach to test mediation effects: (1) the exposure–mediator association (a‐path), (2) the mediator–outcome association adjusting for exposure (b‐path), and (3) total exposure‐outcome effect (c‐path). The indirect effect was calculated as a × b, with significance assessed using Sobel test (p < 0.05). All models used survey‐weighted generalized linear models (svyglm) accounting for NHANES complex sampling design. These 15 biochemical markers served as potential mediators, with four cognitive outcomes examined. Covariates included demographics, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. Bootstrap resampling (n = 1000) validated significant results. Multiple‐comparisons correction used Bonferroni adjustment. Sensitivity analyses tested four covariate combinations to evaluate robustness: (1) a no‐covariate model (unadjusted for any covariates); (2) a minimal covariate model (adjusted for age and race/ethnicity); (3) an extended covariate model (adding education level, PIR, and BMI to the minimal model); and (4) a full covariate model (further including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and history of chronic diseases [emphysema, chronic bronchitis, coronary heart disease, cancer, and stroke] to the extended model). 42

Sex‐stratified analyses were conducted alongside the overall population analysis as part of the study design to characterize potential mediating effects within distinct sex subgroups.

RESULTS

Characteristics of study participants

We included 2712 eligible participants aged 60 years and older from NHANES, comprising 1333 older females (49.1%) and 1379 older males (50.9%) (Table 1). The median age was 68 years (Q1‐Q3: 63 to 75) in both sexes. Overall, older males had higher median scores (indicating better performance) in the DSST, IRT, and DRT compared with females (all p < 0.001), whereas AFT scores were similar between sexes (p = 0.390). Median ln(E‐DII) scores were higher in older males (0.68 [−0.05, 1.17]) than in older females (0.23 [–0.37, 0.83]) (p < 0.001).

Sociodemographic characteristics differed by sex. Older males had slightly higher BMI (p < 0.001) and a greater proportion were living below the poverty level (p < 0.001). Educational attainment varied significantly (p < 0.001), with a higher proportion of older females being college graduates. Lifestyle factors showed marked sex differences: Older females were more likely to be current smokers (p < 0.001), whereas older males were more likely to be current alcohol consumers (p < 0.001). Regarding other comorbidities, chronic bronchitis was more prevalent among older males (p < 0.001), while coronary heart disease was more common in older females (p < 0.001). No significant sex differences were observed for cancer or stroke history.

Owing to the right‐skewed distribution of E‐DII, ln(E‐DII) was used as the primary exposure variable for all analyses. The full results of the analyses using the original E‐DII variable are presented in Supplementary Materials (Figures S3–S6).

Weighted multivariable regression analysis of sex differences in relationship between E‐DII and cognitive performance

In the total population, each one‐unit increase in ln(E‐DII) corresponded to a 0.77‐point decrease in AFT scores (indicating poorer verbal fluency; β = −0.7718, 95% CI: −1.1799 to −0.3637; p = 0.001178; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.01885), with no significant associations observed for DSST, IRT, or DRT. All interaction terms between ln(E‐DII) and sex were non‐significant (all p > 0.05) (Figure 2).

Sex‐stratified analyses indicated associations in males for AFT (β = −0.7530, 95% CI: −1.2042 to −0.3017; p = 0.00302; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.04835) and DSST (β = −2.4584, 95% CI: −3.6479 to −1.2689; p = 0.00057; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.00908) with a one‐unit increase in ln(E‐DII), corresponding to a 0.75‐point decrease in AFT scores and a 2.46‐point decrease in DSST scores (both indicating poorer performance). No significant associations were observed for DRT or IRT after Bonferroni correction. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between ln(E‐DII) and any cognitive test (AFT, DSST, DRT, or IRT) in older females, with all Bonferroni‐corrected p values exceeding 0.05 (Figure 2).

Sensitivity analyses confirmed robust results for AFT in the total population, with consistent significance across base, core covariate‐adjusted, and extreme ln(E‐DII)‐excluded models (β: −0.8199 to −0.7718, all p < 0.0022). In males, AFT (β: −0.8524 to −0.7530, all p < 0.0031) and DSST (base model β = −2.4584, core model β = −2.8281, extreme‐excluded β = −2.7324; all p < 0.0006) remained robust, with all associations maintaining statistical significance.

Weighted RCS analysis of sex differences in relationship between E‐DII and cognitive performance

In the overall sample, linear regression revealed a significant negative association between ln(E‐DII) and AFT (β = −0.772, 95% CI: −1.180 to −0.364; p = 0.0012; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.0471), with no significant associations observed for DSST, IRT, or DRT. All interaction terms between ln(E‐DII) and sex were non‐significant (all p > 0.05).

In older males, the linear model showed a significant negative association between ln(E‐DII) and DSST scores (β = −2.458, 95% CI: −3.648 to −1.269; p = 0.0006; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.0227), while the RCS non‐linear model (derived from the 3 degrees of freedom RCS model) further revealed significant non‐linear characteristics (p = 0.0008; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.0312), with two knots corresponding to ln(E‐DII) values of 0.182 and 0.927 (Figure 3). The RCS model demonstrated a significantly better fit than the linear model (F = 9.354, p = 0.0023), with improved deviance and pseudo‐R 2. In older females, no significant associations were detected between ln(E‐DII) and any outcome (AFT, DSST, DRT, or IRT) across all analyses.

Bootstrap validation (1000 resamples) yielded an indirect effect of −0.223 (consistent with the Sobel test result) with a 95% CI of −0.486 to 0.007, which overlaps with that from the Sobel test, supporting the robustness of the mediating effect. DISCUSSION

Diet‐induced inflammation has emerged as a critical modifiable factor in age‐related cognitive decline, with mounting evidence linking pro‐inflammatory dietary patterns to impaired cognitive function. 43 , 44 , 45 In this study, we investigated the sex‐specific associations between the ln(E‐DII) and performance across four cognitive domains – verbal fluency (AFT), processing speed (DSST), delayed recall (DRT), and immediate recall (IRT) in older adults – while exploring potential mediating roles of blood biochemical markers. Our findings reveal striking sex differences in how dietary inflammatory potential relates to cognitive health, with implications for personalized preventive strategies.

Sex‐specific associations between E‐DII and cognitive performance

Higher ln(E‐DII) scores (indicating a more pro‐inflammatory diet) were robustly associated with poorer cognitive performance in older males, particularly in tests of verbal fluency (AFT) and processing speed (DSST). These associations remained significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons and were confirmed in sensitivity analyses, underscoring their reliability. These findings align with accumulating evidence linking dietary inflammation to age‐related cognitive decline, particularly in domains that may be sensitive to neuroinflammatory processes. 29 , 34 , 46 , 47 Notably, the relationship between ln(E‐DII) and DSST in older males showed significant non‐linearity. The RCS model (df = 3) revealed two critical inflection points corresponding to ln(E‐DII) values of 0.182 and 0.927. Across these thresholds, the negative association between ln(E‐DII) and DSST score became increasingly prominent. This pattern suggests that the adverse impact of pro‐inflammatory diets on processing speed intensifies as dietary inflammatory potential rises, rather than showing a modest negative trend below a single threshold, while a modest degree of anti‐inflammatory dietary patterns (reflected by lower ln[E‐DII] values) may confer benefits to cognitive function. Such non‐linearity deepens our understanding of how dietary inflammation interacts with cognitive function in aging males, indicating that the “tipping point” for detrimental effects may involve a progressive escalation with higher ln(E‐DII). This has implications for timing dietary interventions to mitigate cognitive decline in this population.

These associations were evaluated using cognitive tests that are standard in NHANES, collectively capturing key domains relevant to aging and inflammation research: AFT (verbal fluency), DSST (processing speed), and IRT/DRT (immediate/delayed memory). 35 , 48 , 49 , 50 These domains were selected based on their neuroanatomical correlates, including frontoparietal networks and white matter connections (supporting DSST), temporal lobe structures and hippocampal networks (supporting AFT), and hippocampal regions (supporting IRT/DRT), all of which are associated with pathways vulnerable to inflammatory processes. 36 , 51 The prominence of associations with AFT and DSST in males may thus reflect differential sensitivity of frontoparietal networks (supporting DSST) and temporal lobe–hippocampal networks (supporting AFT) to dietary inflammatory cues, though this pattern requires further validation.

Notably, our finding that pro‐inflammatory diets disproportionately affect older males’ cognitive function aligns with preclinical research. Evans et al. 52 showed that high‐fat diets impaired cognitive behavior, and hippocampal microglial reactivity in male (but not female) C57BL/6J mice, mirroring our observation of male‐specific vulnerability in hippocampal and frontoparietal network‐linked domains. However, some animal studies 53 , 54 , 55 report greater diet‐induced cognitive impairments in females, likely due to differences in models, interventions, or assessed domains. These discrepancies underscore the complexity of translating preclinical findings to humans, where sociocultural (e.g., diet, healthcare) and biological (e.g., hormones, immunity, gut microbiota composition) factors further shape outcomes. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59

In contrast, no significant associations between ln(E‐DII) and continuous cognitive performance scores were observed in older females across any domain, which aligns with the literature on sex differences in inflammation's neurobiological impact. 58 Older females may exhibit distinct resilience to dietary pro‐inflammatory stimuli, with their effects manifesting only when the inflammatory burden reaches a certain threshold, as reflected by changes in continuous scores. This threshold effect likely arises from the interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors: genomic instability, hormonally mediated processes, differential immune responses, and gendered behavioral patterns (e.g., dietary adherence and healthcare‐seeking behaviors) that may buffer subclinical inflammation. 60 , 61 Additionally, sociocultural influences such as gender disparities in educational opportunities or lifelong cognitive stimulation could shape cognitive reserve, affecting how females tolerate inflammatory burden before manifesting detectable impairment. 61 , 62

Biochemical markers as sex‐specific mediators

To unravel the pathways linking dietary inflammation to cognition, we identified distinct mediating biomarkers by sex. In older males, serum GGT mediated 7.05% of the ln(E‐DII)‐DSST association. GGT plays a key role in glutathione metabolism, and elevated levels indicate impaired antioxidant capacity, a known driver of neuroinflammation and synaptic damage, 63 , 64 , 65 consistent with its role in oxidative stress cascades tied to biological ageing, with this association being observed in older males but not females. 66 This aligns with findings, which demonstrated that dietary oxidative balance exhibits sex‐specific non‐linear associations with cognitive performance, particularly in DSST, and highlighted that males may be more susceptible to dietary oxidative stress impacts on cognitive domains like processing speed. 67 This finding suggests that the dietary pro‐inflammatory potential may accelerate cognitive decline in older males, at least in part, by exacerbating oxidative stress, aligning with observations that GGT‐related processes impact DSST‐assessed cognitive domains. 68 A prospective cohort study 69 in middle‐aged to older Finnish men found that serum GGT was positively, log‐linearly, and independently associated with future risk of dementia over 22 years, with a higher hazard ratio after correcting for within‐person variability – supporting the role of GGT as a male‐specific mediator in inflammation‐related cognitive decline.

Notably, GGT‐mediated effects appeared specific to DSST (processing speed) rather than AFT (verbal fluency), indicating that oxidative stress, triggered by pro‐inflammatory diets, may selectively impact frontoparietal networks and white matter connections critical for processing speed, rather than temporal lobe and hippocampal networks underlying verbal fluency. GGT‐mediated effects were most pronounced for DSST, suggesting a preferential impact on processing speed pathways. This specificity resonates with GGT's involvement in pathways regulating synaptic plasticity and amyloid beta metabolism 70 and parallels links between elevated GGT, cognitive impairment, and age‐related neurodegeneration. 66 , 71 Together, these domain‐specific associations suggest that dietary inflammation may impact cognitive domains selectively, depending on the underlying neural networks’ potential sensitivity to specific inflammatory mediators (oxidative stress in males).

Implications for clinical practice and public health

Our findings reinforce the need for sex‐tailored dietary strategies to preserve cognitive health in aging. For older males, reducing dietary inflammatory potential – through increased intake of anti‐inflammatory foods (e.g., fruits, vegetables, fatty fish) 72 and reduced consumption of pro‐inflammatory items (e.g., processed meats, refined sugars) 73 – may help mitigate declines in executive function and processing speed, particularly when ln(E‐DII) exceeds 0.182 and 0.927. GGT levels may serve as a potential candidate biomarker to help assess the efficacy of such interventions.

For older females, the absence of direct linear or non‐linear ln(E‐DII)–cognition associations may highlight a clinically relevant threshold effect. This nuanced pattern underscores that broader anti‐inflammatory strategies – such as combining dietary modifications to reduce pro‐inflammatory intake with physical activity (a known modulator of systemic inflammation) 74 – remain valuable for mitigating cognitive impairment risk in this group.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, its cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether pro‐inflammatory diets precede cognitive decline or vice versa. Second, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, including from unmeasured factors (e.g., genetic variants and sleep quality). Additionally, E‐DII relies on self‐reported dietary data, which may introduce recall bias, particularly in older adults with cognitive impairment. Third, the modest mediation effect sizes (7.05%) indicate that other pathways (e.g., vascular dysfunction and gut–brain axis dysregulation), likely contribute to the observed associations.

Future research should (1) replicate these findings in longitudinal cohorts while accounting for sex‐specific covariates (e.g., menopausal status and hormone therapy), (2) investigate whether the ln(E‐DII) inflection point observed in older males corresponds to clinically meaningful cognitive thresholds, (3) explore additional biomarkers (e.g., CRP, IL‐6, and gut microbiota metabolites) to capture the full spectrum of inflammatory pathways, and (4) test sex‐specific dietary interventions to validate causal links and inform guidelines.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that the relationship between dietary inflammatory potential and cognitive function in older adults exhibits sex‐specific differences. Higher ln(E‐DII) scores are associated with poorer cognitive performance in older males, as reflected in both AFT and DSST, and with non‐linear effects specific to the DSST and oxidative stress (via GGT) acting as a key mediator. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between ln(E‐DII) and cognitive performance across any domain in older females. These insights advance our understanding of sex differences in cognitive aging and provide a foundation for personalized, inflammation‐targeted strategies to protect cognition health.
