Development and multicohort external validation of a preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET-derived deep learning score and multimodal model for predicting biochemical recurrence-free survival after radical prostatectomy
Authors: Tiancheng Li, Nina Xu, Xiaofang Yan, Guolin Wang, Zhenfeng Liu, Yinuo Liu, Kui Zhao, Xinhui Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and validate a preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET-derived deep learning score (DLS) and an integrated model combining DLS, D’Amico risk classification, and SUVmax for predicting biochemical recurrence-free survival (BRFS) after radical prostatectomy (RP).Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 697 patients with prostate cancer who underwent preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT before RP at three campuses within one healthcare network: Qingchun (training cohort, n = 445), Yuhang (validation cohort 1, n = 190), and Zhijiang (validation cohort 2, n = 62). Five convolutional neural networks were trained to predict 3-year biochemical recurrence from PET images, and the output of the best-performing network was defined as the DLS. Four preoperative prognostic models were evaluated: DLS alone, D’Amico classification alone, D’Amico plus SUVmax, and D’Amico plus SUVmax plus DLS. Performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-index, inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted time-dependent AUC, calibration, decision curve analysis, and nested model comparisons.ResultsVGG19 showed the best fixed-time classification performance, with 3-year ROC AUCs of 0.834, 0.755, and 0.723 in the training and two validation cohorts, respectively. A higher DLS was associated with shorter BRFS in all cohorts. Biopsy ISUP Grade Group was significantly associated with BRFS (global P < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, intermediate- and high-risk D’Amico groups, SUVmax, and DLS were independently associated with BRFS. The integrated model achieved C-indices of 0.846, 0.806, and 0.774 and 36-month AUCs of 0.853, 0.801, and 0.779 in the training and two validation cohorts, respectively, outperforming D’Amico classification alone and D’Amico plus SUVmax.ConclusionA fully preoperative model integrating an [18F]PSMA-1007 PET-derived DLS with D’Amico risk classification and SUVmax improved prediction of BRFS after RP. The model may support preoperative risk counseling and postoperative surveillance planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-026-01090-0.

Introduction

Biochemical recurrence (BCR) after radical prostatectomy (RP) is a significant concern, occurring in approximately 20–50% of patients within 10 years of surgery, and early BCR is associated with worse prognosis [1, 2]. Accurate preoperative risk assessment is important for patient counseling, surgical planning, selection of the extent of lymph-node dissection when clinically indicated, and early planning of postoperative surveillance [3–5]. The D’Amico classification is a widely used preoperative system that stratifies patients using preoperative PSA, biopsy Gleason score, and clinical T stage [6, 7]. However, such categorical systems rely on conventional clinical variables and may not fully capture intraprostatic spatial heterogeneity or molecular tumor aggressiveness.

Prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography (PSMA PET) has improved the detection and staging of prostate cancer [8]. Beyond lesion localization, baseline PSMA uptake metrics have been associated with adverse pathological findings and biochemical failure after definitive treatment [9–11]. These observations support PSMA PET as a potential source of preoperative prognostic biomarkers. Among available tracers, [18F]PSMA-1007 offers a relatively long physical half-life, high spatial resolution, and low urinary excretion, facilitating evaluation of the prostate and pelvic region [12–14].

Deep learning can automatically learn high-dimensional spatial representations from medical images [15, 16], potentially capturing information beyond a single intensity measurement such as SUVmax. CNN-based multimodal approaches have also demonstrated the potential to integrate imaging-derived representations with clinical or molecular variables for outcome prediction [17]. Nevertheless, the incremental prognostic value of deep features derived from preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET beyond an established preoperative clinical risk classification and conventional PET uptake remains insufficiently investigated.

Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to develop and externally validate a preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET-derived DLS for predicting BRFS after RP and to determine whether the DLS provides incremental prognostic value beyond the D’Amico risk classification and SUVmax.

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective multicohort study at The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, comprising three campus centers within the same healthcare network and geographic region. A total of 697 patients with histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma were included. All patients underwent preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT before RP between 2020 and 2024. The training cohort (n = 445) was obtained from the Qingchun campus, while the Yuhang (n = 190) and Zhijiang (n = 62) campuses served as external validation cohorts. Eligibility required: (a) histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma; (b) RP planned as primary curative treatment; (c) preoperative PET/CT within 60 days before surgery; (d) available preoperative PSA, biopsy Gleason pattern/ISUP Grade Group, and clinical T stage for D’Amico classification; and (e) PSA follow-up for BRFS assessment. Patients were excluded if they had received neoadjuvant systemic therapy or pelvic radiotherapy before PET/CT or surgery, had received adjuvant radiotherapy or androgen-deprivation therapy before BCR, had inadequate PET image quality, or had follow-up shorter than 12 months without BCR. Patients developing BCR within 12 months remained eligible. The study flow is shown in Fig. 1.

PET/CT acquisition

All patients underwent [18F] PSMA-1007 PET/CT on dedicated whole-body scanners after intravenous injection of approximately 3.7 MBq/kg of radiotracer. Following an uptake period of about 60 min, a whole-body PET scan (from mid-thigh to skull base) was acquired, followed by a low-dose CT for attenuation correction and anatomical localization. In the training and external validation cohort 1, imaging was performed using a Biograph Vision PET/CT system (Siemens Healthineers, Germany), whereas patients in the external validation cohort 2 were scanned on a uMI 780 PET/CT system (United Imaging Healthcare, China). Imaging protocols were standardized as much as possible, and scanners underwent routine quality control to ensure quantitative consistency. PET images were reconstructed using iterative algorithms (2–3 iterations, ≥ 20 subsets) with standard corrections for attenuation, scatter, randoms, and radioactive decay, resulting in a voxel size of approximately 2–4 mm. Images were reviewed for diagnostic quality, and patients with severe extravasation or artifacts that precluded quantitative analysis were excluded.

Image preprocessing, ROI definition, and deep learning modeling

The overall workflow of image preprocessing, deep learning model development, and prognostic analysis is illustrated in Fig. 2. To focus the analysis on intraprostatic imaging information, the whole-prostate region was delineated on preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT images. Whole-prostate regions of interest (ROIs) were delineated semi-automatically using LIFEx software (version 7.7.0) on the CT component, with PET uptake used as a reference, followed by manual refinement when necessary. The resulting whole-gland masks were mapped onto the co-registered PET images. PET images were converted to body-weight–normalized standardized uptake value units, resampled to an isotropic voxel spacing of 2 × 2 × 2 mm³, and normalized using z-score normalization within each whole-prostate ROI before model input generation. Axial PET slices covering the prostate ROI were extracted as inputs to a two-dimensional convolutional neural network.

Five convolutional neural network architectures—VGG19, DenseNet121, Xception, ResNet50, and InceptionV3—were initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights and fine-tuned using the PET dataset. Prostate-covering PET slices were resized to 224 × 224 pixels. Data augmentation included random rotations within ± 10°, horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5, horizontal and vertical translations of up to 10% of the image dimensions, and random intensity perturbations within ± 10%. Models were optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, binary cross-entropy loss, a batch size of 16, and a maximum of 100 epochs. Early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs and model checkpointing based on validation loss were applied. Class imbalance was addressed using inverse-frequency class weights. All slices from the same patient were retained within the same fold to prevent information leakage.

Architecture selection was performed using patient-level 10-fold cross-validation for classification of biochemical recurrence within 3 years. Patients who remained recurrence-free but were censored before 36 months were excluded from this fixed-time classification step. The architecture with the highest mean cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was selected as the final network architecture.

The selected network generated a logit for each prostate-covering PET slice. Slice-level logits were averaged across all prostate-covering slices for each patient, and the sigmoid function was subsequently applied to the mean logit to obtain a patient-level probability. This probability, ranging from 0 to 1, was defined as the deep learning score (DLS). For survival modeling in the training cohort, patient-level DLS values were generated from out-of-fold predictions obtained during patient-level 10-fold cross-validation. Thus, the DLS assigned to each patient in the training cohort was generated by a model that had not been trained using data from that patient. After architecture selection, the final network was trained using the complete training cohort and applied to the two validation cohorts without recalibration or additional fine-tuning.

The D’Amico risk group was determined according to the highest-risk preoperative feature. Low risk was defined as PSA < 10 ng/mL, biopsy Gleason score ≤ 6 or ISUP Grade Group 1, and clinical stage T1–T2a. Intermediate risk was defined as PSA 10–20 ng/mL, biopsy Gleason score 7 or ISUP Grade Group 2–3, or clinical stage T2b, in the absence of any high-risk feature. High risk was defined as PSA > 20 ng/mL, biopsy Gleason score 8–10 or ISUP Grade Group 4–5, or clinical stage T2c or higher. Four preoperative prognostic approaches were evaluated: DLS alone, D’Amico classification alone, D’Amico classification plus SUVmax, and D’Amico classification plus SUVmax and DLS.

To provide qualitative visualization of model attention, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to the PET-only input slices of the selected VGG19 model. Grad-CAM was computed with respect to the predicted probability of 3-year biochemical recurrence using the final convolutional layer of VGG19. The gradient of the target prediction was backpropagated to the selected feature maps, and channel-wise importance weights were obtained by global average pooling of the gradients. A weighted combination of the feature maps followed by rectified linear activation generated a coarse localization map. The resulting map was upsampled to the original input resolution of 224 × 224 pixels, min–max normalized, and overlaid on the corresponding PET slice.

Grad-CAM maps were generated for all prostate-covering slices. For each patient, the slice with the highest predicted recurrence probability was selected for representative visualization. Qualitative assessment examined whether regions of greater activation corresponded to intraprostatic areas showing relatively high or heterogeneous [18F]PSMA-1007 uptake within the delineated whole-prostate ROI. Grad-CAM was used only as a qualitative, post hoc visualization method and was not interpreted as quantitative or causal evidence of regional feature importance.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.1) and Python. Baseline characteristics were compared among cohorts using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. BRFS was defined as the time from RP to BCR (two consecutive PSA values ≥ 0.2 ng/mL) or last follow-up. DLS was analyzed continuously and was also dichotomized using the training-cohort median for Kaplan-Meier visualization. Biopsy Gleason scores were reclassified according to ISUP Grade Groups: Grade Group 1 (3 + 3), Grade Group 2 (3 + 4), Grade Group 3 (4 + 3), Grade Group 4 (Gleason 8), and Grade Group 5 (Gleason 9–10). The overall prognostic association of Grade Group was assessed using a global likelihood-ratio test. Final prostatectomy Grade Group was evaluated separately as a sensitivity analysis and was not included in the preoperative model.

Cox proportional hazards models were used for prognostic analyses, and proportional-hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. The principal multivariable model included D’Amico risk group, SUVmax, and DLS. Individual D’Amico components were not entered simultaneously with the composite risk group. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and IPCW time-dependent AUCs at 12, 24, 36, and 48 months. Incremental value was evaluated using likelihood-ratio tests for nested models, bootstrap differences in C-index and 36-month AUC with 95% confidence intervals, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis. Spearman correlation quantified the relationship between DLS and SUVmax in each cohort. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Predictive performance of the deep learning models and survival analysis

Among the evaluated CNN architectures, VGG19 showed the best discrimination, yielding ROC AUCs of 0.834, 0.755, and 0.723 for 3-year BCR in the training and two external validation cohorts, respectively (Fig. 3a-c). For risk stratification, patients were dichotomized into high- and low-risk groups using the median DLS in the training cohort as the cutoff (0.71), and the same threshold was applied to two external validation cohorts. This DLS-based stratification yielded significantly different BRFS between groups in the training and two external validation cohorts (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 4a-c).

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis

The results of the univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses are summarized in Table 2. In the training cohort, biopsy ISUP Grade Group was significantly associated with BRFS (global P < 0.001). Relative to Grade Group 1, the univariable HRs were 1.45 for Grade Group 2, 2.03 for Grade Group 3, 2.68 for Grade Group 4, and 3.46 for Grade Group 5. D’Amico risk classification was also significant (global p < 0.001). In the multivariable preoperative model, intermediate-risk versus low-risk disease (HR 1.72, 95% CI 1.09–2.72; p = 0.020), high-risk versus low-risk disease (HR 2.61, 95% CI 1.65–4.13; p < 0.001), SUVmax (per 1-unit increase: HR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.07; p < 0.001), and DLS (per 0.1 increase: HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.35–1.58; p < 0.001) remained independently associated with BRFS. Final prostatectomy ISUP Grade Group showed a stronger graded association in sensitivity analysis (global p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S2).

Relationship between DLS and SUVmax and incremental prognostic value

DLS was not significantly correlated with SUVmax in the training cohort (Spearman ρ = −0.032, P = 0.499), validation cohort 1 (ρ = −0.044, P = 0.550), or validation cohort 2 (ρ = −0.007, P = 0.960), suggesting that the DLS did not simply reflect SUVmax (Supplementary Fig. S1). Adding SUVmax to the D’Amico model increased the C-index by 0.064 (95% CI 0.038–0.090; likelihood-ratio p < 0.001), while adding DLS to the D’Amico+SUVmax model produced a further C-index increase of 0.074 (95% CI 0.048-0.100; likelihood-ratio p < 0.001). These results suggest that DLS contains prognostic information beyond a single maximum-uptake measurement.

Assessment and external validation of the multimodal models

A preoperative nomogram integrating D’Amico risk group, SUVmax, and DLS was developed to estimate individualized 24-, 36-, and 48-month BRFS probabilities (Fig. 5). The full multimodal model achieved C-indices of 0.846 (95% CI 0.817–0.875), 0.806 (95% CI 0.721–0.891), and 0.774 (95% CI 0.701–0.847) in the training and two validation cohorts, respectively, compared with 0.708, 0.682, and 0.671 for the D’Amico classification alone (Table 3). The corresponding 36-month AUCs for the full model were 0.853, 0.801, and 0.779. Calibration curves (Fig. 6) demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed BRFS probabilities at 24, 36, and 48 months, and decision curve analysis (Fig. 7) suggested greater net benefit for the full model across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. Time-dependent AUC curves for the four preoperative models are shown in Fig. 8. The full multimodal model consistently achieved the highest discrimination across all evaluated time points.

Discussion

In this multicohort study, a deep learning score derived from preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET was associated with biochemical recurrence-free survival after radical prostatectomy and provided incremental prognostic information beyond established preoperative risk stratification. The model combining D’Amico risk classification, SUVmax, and DLS consistently outperformed models based on D’Amico classification alone or D’Amico classification plus SUVmax across the training and two external validation cohorts. The integrated model achieved C-indices of 0.846, 0.806, and 0.774 and 36-month AUCs of 0.853, 0.801, and 0.779 in the training cohort and the two external validation cohorts, respectively. These findings suggest that deep imaging features extracted from PSMA PET may capture prognostically relevant tumor characteristics that are not fully represented by conventional clinical variables or simple uptake measurements.

Current preoperative risk assessment for localized prostate cancer is largely based on serum PSA, clinical T stage, and biopsy grade, as reflected in commonly used risk-classification systems and clinical guidelines [3–6]. Although these variables remain central to clinical decision-making, patients within the same conventional risk category may experience substantially different postoperative outcomes. Molecular imaging may help refine this assessment by providing an in vivo representation of tumor phenotype. PSMA PET provides information on tumor uptake intensity and spatial heterogeneity that may complement conventional clinical variables. The improvement observed after adding SUVmax to the D’Amico classification indicates that conventional PSMA uptake intensity contains prognostic information beyond routine clinical risk factors. The additional improvement after incorporating the DLS further suggests that the network extracted more complex imaging patterns beyond maximum uptake alone.

These findings are consistent with growing interest in artificial intelligence and quantitative molecular imaging for individualized risk assessment in prostate cancer [18, 19]. Previous predictive approaches have primarily relied on clinicopathologic variables, genomic biomarkers, or handcrafted radiomic features [20–24]. A machine-learning model based on presurgical clinical variables reported an AUC of 0.783 for predicting biochemical recurrence within 12 months after robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, significantly outperforming the D’Amico classification, which achieved an AUC of 0.692 in the same study [20]. Although direct comparison across studies is difficult because of differences in patient populations, outcome definitions, follow-up duration, and validation strategies, the present results support the addition of PSMA PET-derived information to conventional preoperative risk assessment.

The present study also builds on our previous PSMA PET radiomics work. In an earlier two-campus cohort, an [18F]PSMA-1007 PET-based radiomics model combining a handcrafted radiomic signature with clinical and PET parameters achieved C-indices of 0.81 and 0.78 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively [24]. Deep learning differs from conventional radiomics in that it automatically learns hierarchical image representations rather than relying on predefined feature families, potentially allowing more complex imaging patterns to be captured. This may include relationships among uptake intensity, spatial distribution, texture, and intraprostatic heterogeneity. In the present study, the DLS added prognostic information beyond both D’Amico classification and SUVmax, supporting the possibility that deep learning captures complementary tumor characteristics. However, the current study did not directly compare deep learning and radiomics in the same patients using an identical validation procedure; therefore, superiority of one approach over the other cannot be inferred.

Tumor grade remained an important determinant of postoperative outcome. Biopsy ISUP Grade Group demonstrated a significant graded association with BRFS, consistent with the established prognostic value of the five-tier Grade Group system and the clinically meaningful distinction between Gleason 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 disease [25]. Final prostatectomy Grade Group showed an even stronger association in sensitivity analysis, likely reflecting the more comprehensive assessment of tumor architecture in the surgical specimen and the recognized discordance, including undergrading, between biopsy and radical prostatectomy specimens [26]. Nevertheless, biopsy Grade Group is more relevant to the intended application of the model because it is available at the preoperative decision point. The use of preoperative variables throughout the primary prediction framework ensures that the estimated risk could be available before surgery rather than only after definitive pathologic assessment.

Grad-CAM analysis provided a qualitative view of the regions contributing to model predictions. In representative cases, model activation was predominantly located within the prostate, with greater activation in areas showing relatively high or heterogeneous [18F]PSMA-1007 uptake. This distribution is compatible with the possibility that the model used intraprostatic tumor-related information rather than relying exclusively on surrounding background structures. Nevertheless, Grad-CAM remains a coarse post hoc visualization technique. It does not quantify the contribution of individual regions, establish causal feature importance, or confirm that highlighted areas correspond to biologically distinct tumor subregions. Activation maps may also vary according to network architecture, selected convolutional layer, image preprocessing, and input definition. These visualizations should therefore be regarded as supportive qualitative evidence rather than mechanistic validation of the model.

From a clinical perspective, the integrated model may help refine preoperative counseling and postoperative planning for patients undergoing radical prostatectomy. Patients with a higher predicted risk of biochemical recurrence may warrant more detailed discussion of prognosis, careful evaluation of the need for additional staging or guideline-concordant pelvic lymph-node dissection, consideration for perioperative clinical trials, and closer postoperative PSA surveillance [3–5]. Conversely, patients with a lower predicted risk may potentially avoid unnecessarily intensive follow-up. However, the model was not developed to prescribe treatment independently, and its predictions should not be used in isolation to determine neoadjuvant, adjuvant, or salvage therapy. Clinical application would require integration with established risk factors, patient preferences, pathologic findings when available, and multidisciplinary assessment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, and the relatively short follow-up in external validation cohort 2 limited assessment of long-term outcomes. Second, all cohorts were derived from the same healthcare network and geographic region; therefore, the results support cross-campus and partial cross-scanner transportability rather than broad external generalizability. Third, incomplete CAPRA data precluded a direct comparison, and D’Amico classification was used as the principal preoperative benchmark. Fourth, Grad-CAM provides only coarse, post hoc, and qualitative localization of model attention; it does not quantify regional feature importance, establish causal relevance, or confirm that highlighted regions represent biologically meaningful tumor subregions. Prospective validation in geographically and institutionally independent cohorts with longer follow-up is warranted.

In conclusion, a preoperative [18F]PSMA-1007 PET-derived deep learning score provided prognostic information for BRFS after radical prostatectomy and improved risk discrimination when added to D’Amico classification and SUVmax. The results suggest that deep learning may extract complementary information from PSMA PET beyond conventional clinical risk factors and simple tracer-uptake measurements. This fully preoperative framework has potential as an adjunct for individualized risk assessment, but prospective validation in geographically and institutionally independent cohorts is required before clinical implementation.
