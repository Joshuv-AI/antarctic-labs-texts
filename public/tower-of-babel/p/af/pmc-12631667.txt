Rheology Behavior and Dispersion Mechanisms of Modified Wax Residue in Coal-Water Slurry
Authors: Xinyu Ren, Chuizhong Kong, Xudong Song, Juntao Wei, Jiaofei Wang, Qinghua Guo, Yonghui Bai, Guangsuo Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates the effective repurposing of a hazardous Fischer–Tropsch wax residue (containing 50% wax) from coal liquefaction as a performance-enhancing additive for coal water slurries. Through surfactant-assisted wet milling, we produced two modified wax residues: WR-1 (octylphenol ethoxylate OP-10 modified) and WR-2 (sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate SDBS modified), which reduced the contact angle by 10° and increased ζ-potential to 25 and 27.2 mV. Remarkably, incorporating 5% WR-1 increased the maximum slurry concentration to 68.17% while maintaining low viscosity, attributed to enhanced electrostatic repulsion (E T = 2 × 10–10 J) per DLVO theory. In contrast, WR-2 addition decreased the slurry performance due to unfavorable particle size effects. The optimized WR-1 system formed a stable three-phase network of wax residue, coal particles, and water, offering a practical solution for both waste valorization and slurry quality improvement.

Introduction

A technique called Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (F–T) uses CO and H2 to create liquid fuels. , During F–T synthesis, deactivated catalysts are replaced when they no longer meet the production demands. The waste catalyst is typically discharged along with residual wax, forming a wax residue. The wax residue contains 50% long-chain alkanes. Its high hydrogen content makes it prone to spontaneous combustion if stored improperly. Therefore, specialized handling procedures are essential to prevent environmental and health hazards.

In China, such wastes are typically disposed of in landfills, leading to both environmental contamination and inefficient resource utilization. Traditional incineration and other treatment methods will waste a large amount of paraffin resources; mechanical centrifugation and pyrolysis treatment is more energy-consuming and has a lower recovery rate; microwave and ultrasonic treatment is small, and the equipment investment and later maintenance costs are high. − Several thermochemical processes like pyrolysis, hydrocracking, and catalytic pyrolysis have been developed to treat wax residue, but their strict operating conditions make industrial use difficult. Co-gasification and cocombustion provide new ways to handle low-quality carbon materials, and processing wax residue with coal could be an effective method. Coal water slurry technology is widely used in coal gasification and combustion because it is energy efficient and causes little pollution, making it good for treating different wastes together. − Most studies have looked at using oily wastes, city sludge or biomass, but oily wastes make the slurry too thick while biomass makes it less efficient. Jiang et al. modified municipal sludge with inorganic salts (KCl, CaCl2, AlCl3), systematically studied the rheological properties and dispersion mechanism of salt-modified sludge-coal water slurry, and found that high-valence metal ions (Al3+) can significantly enhance the adsorption capacity of dispersants on the particle surface, reducing the slurry viscosity by more than 20%. Li et al. coprepared coal water slurry using corn straw biochar and coal, and found that the maximum slurry concentration reached 61.66%, which was 14.24% higher than that of the original biomass slurry. This study revealed that reducing the biochar particle size can enhance hydrophilicity (contact angle decreased from 75.96 to 41.95°) and electronegativity (ζ-potential decreased from −14.4 to −31.1 mV). Wax residue is different because its long-chain hydrocarbons make it very water-resistant, causing separation from water and making a stable slurry hard to form. Also, a high wax content creates complex flow behavior that makes wax residue hard to process using normal methods.

This paper introduces a wax residue modified wet milling method to address the adhesion issue during wax residue processing. The obtained wax residue suspension is blended with coal to prepare the slurry, enabling systematic investigation of how modified wax residue affects slurry performance. Through the DLVO theory-based quantification of interparticle forces and slurry formation mechanism analysis, this study provides fundamental support for advancing coal-water slurry technology.

Experimental Methods

Preparation of WR-CWS

Preparation of wax residue suspension: The wax residue was coarsely crushed into fragments of approximately 1–3 cm in size. After being weighed, the material was placed in a beaker, and 1% of the modifying agent (relative to the sample mass) was dissolved in deionized water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 13:9. The mixture was then subjected to wet grinding in a ball mill (operating at 12,000 rpm for 15 min) to obtain a 65% wax residue suspension.

Preparation process of wax resident-coal water slurry­(WR-CWS): This paper used dry pulping, which involves grinding coal powder and then adding it to the pulping water to fully combine it. The specific pulping steps are as follows: (1) Calculate the mass of coal powder, WR, and dispersant according to the concentration required for the experiment, weigh the coal mix powders with varying particle sizes, and add the WR suspension after grinding. (2) Mix and stir the deionized water and dispersant to dissolve all of the dispersant in the pulping water. (3) Add the weighed coal powder and WS suspension to the aqueous solution of additives, put it into a mechanical stirrer, and stir it for 15 min at a rate of 800 r/min to ensure all dispersion. To release the air, it was left for 5 min after the stirring was finished. The ratio of coal powder particle size in the pulping process was <75 mm:75–147 mm:147–250 mm:250–420 mm = 5:1:3:1. The mass ratio of WR to dry coal powder was known as the WR mixing ratio (α). Various values of α (5%, 10%) were chosen for the trials, whereas CWS served as the control.

Slurry Characteristics

Rheological characteristics are important indicators for evaluating the performance of an excellent slurry. Slurries are classified as Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids based on their rheological properties; the latter are further separated into dilatant and pseudoplastic fluids. While the pseudoplastic fluid in non-Newtonian fluids will continue to drop in viscosity when the shear rate rises, demonstrating the features of shear thinning, the apparent viscosity of Newtonian fluids remains constant when the shear rate changes; and the change of dilatant is opposite to the pseudoplasticity. In industrial applications, the CWS is presently mandated to possess shear-thinning pseudoplastic characteristics, which facilitate efficient pumping and atomization processes of the slurry. In this research, the parameters of slurry formation characteristics were determined using a HAAKE MASR60 rotational rheometer. The experiments employed both variable and constant shear rate methods at 25 °C. During the 300s variable shear rate stage, the shear rate linearly increased from 0 to 100 s–1, then stabilized at 100 s–1 for the subsequent 300s constant shear rate stage, where viscosity measurements were taken 10 times.

Regarding the rheological characteristic curve of CWS, the yield-power law model is used for fitting, and the formula is as follows 1 τ=τ0+Kγn τ -shear forces, Pa; τ0 -yield forces, Pa; K-consistency coefficient, Pa·s; γ-rate of shear, s–1; n-rheology index, n < 1 is the pseudoplastic fluid, n > 1 is the dilatant fluid, and n = 1 is the Newtonian fluid.

When the shear rate reaches 100 s–1, the viscosity of the CWS at this point is the apparent viscosity; when the apparent viscosity of the CWS is 1000 mPa·s, the solids content in the slurry is defined as the maximum slurry-forming consistency (ωmax). When the apparent viscosity of the CWS is low and ωmax is high, it indicates that the slurry has better slurry-forming properties.

Contact Angle Measurement

Contact angle measurements were conducted using a Dataphysics OCA20 instrument (Germany) under controlled conditions (26 °C, 65% RH). Approximately 1g of dried powder was compressed at 10 MPa for 15 min to prepare cylindrical tablets (2 mm thick, 10 mm diameter). The contact angle θ of deionized water droplets on the tablet surface was measured, with smaller angles indicating greater hydrophilicity.

ζ-Potential Measurement

Using a Litesizer 500 ζ-potential meter and electrophoretic scattering of light, the ζ-potential of the slurry was determined in this work. To ensure the accuracy of the potential measurement, the mass fraction of the slurry was diluted to 0.2–0.3% with deionized water prior to the measurement to ensure that the sample transmission was >10%; prior to loading the samples, the samples were used to wet wash the cuvette and the average value was obtained from three repetitions of the measurement.

Ion Concentration Measurement

With the use of inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), the cation concentrations in the CWS were identified, specifically, the PerkinElmer NEXION 300 model. Anion concentrations in the CWS were measured by ion chromatography (Diane ICS5000+).

Particle Size Distribution Analysis

The particle size distributions of coal powder and wax residue were determined using a Mastersizer 3000 automated laser particle size analyzer with a detection range of 0.017–2000 μm and a maximum detection angle of 150°. This instrument measures the particle size distribution based on laser scattering principles. For the testing methodology, Yangchangwan coal powder was measured using a dry dispersion method, while modified wax residue was analyzed via a wet dispersion approach.

Interaction Energy Calculation

The Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek (DLVO) theory, a classical framework for colloidal dispersions, elucidates the interactions among particles within suspensions by considering both van der Waals and electrostatic forces. The WR used in this paper is highly hydrophobic, and as the theory of dispersed systems continues to be investigated, it is found that hydrophobic forces also affect the stability of the dispersed system. Therefore, in this paper, the hydrophobic interaction energy is taken into account to explain the dispersion and agglomeration of particles in the slurry by quantifying the repulsive and attractive forces using the extended DLVO theory. In the calculations using this theory, the following assumptions are made: (1) all coal and WR particles are spherical; (2) the diameter of the spherical particles is taken as the average particle size of all particles. Results and Discussion

Experimental Results

Slurry Ability

Figure illustrates how the apparent viscosity and the maximum slurry concentration of a slurry vary with the solid content under different mixing conditions (YCW-CWS refers to the Yangchangwan coal water slurry without modified wax residue mixing). The solid content in the slurry significantly affects its viscosity. As the concentration of solids in the slurry rises, the interparticle spacing diminishes and the amount of free water decreases, resulting in enhanced interactions among the particles. This necessitates greater shear stress, causing the viscosity to increase with more solid particles. The highest slurry concentrations for CWS combined with 5% WR-1 and 10% WR-1 are 68.17 and 67.95%, respectively, as illustrated in Figure a, respectively, both higher than the maximum slurry concentration of the unmixed CWS (67.67%). This study suggests that the incorporation of WR-1 enhances the maximum slurry concentration in the coal water slurry (CWS), whereas the addition of WR-2 exhibits an opposing effect. As illustrated in Figure b, the apparent viscosity of CWS blended with WR-1 is markedly lower compared to that of the reference YCW-CWS. In contrast, upon adding WR-2 at concentrations of 67 and 69%, the apparent viscosity of the resultant slurry surpasses that of standard CWS, albeit not by a significant margin. These disparities are attributed to the distinct particle size distributions of the two additives. When these additives are mixed with coal to produce a slurry, the resultant packing density varies, serving as the primary factor underlying the differences in viscosity and maximum slurry concentration.

Maximum slurry consistency (a) and apparent viscosity (b) of coal water slurry under different blending conditions.

A reasonable particle size distribution allows for the preparation of high bulk density and low viscosity coal water slurry. Figure exhibits that the OP-10 modified wax residue (WR-1) exhibits a distinct unimodal distribution at 100 μm, forming excellent complementarity with the particle size distribution of Yangchangwan coal. This special size-matching relationship stems from the steric hindrance effect of polyoxyethylene ether chains in OP-10 molecules, which reorganize the wax molecular arrangement through hydrogen bonding to form a loose porous structure with specific particle sizes. When WR-1 particles are mixed with coal powder, optimal particle gradation can be achieved, increasing the packing density while squeezing out the bound water between coal particles, thereby increasing the free water content in the system. This ultimately manifests as reduced slurry viscosity and increased maximum slurry concentration (68.17%). In contrast, the particle size distribution of SDBS-modified wax residue (WR-2) shows significant left-shifting, with over 50% of particles being smaller than 10 μm. This size variation originates from the strong electrostatic interactions of sulfonic acid groups in SDBS molecules, which peel wax residues through charge adsorption, leading to excessive fragmentation. Although smaller particles theoretically could improve packing density, when the particle size becomes too small, it may cause particle agglomeration and poor particle size gradation. ,

Particle size distribution of YCW, WR-1, and WR-2.

Figure presents the rheological curves of coal water slurries with varying amounts of wax residue, illustrating the impact of different modified wax residues and mixing ratios on the slurry performance. The viscosity profile of the coal water slurry containing a wax residue, as depicted in Figure , exhibits a decrease with an escalating shear rate. This trend suggests that these slurries display shear-thinning behavior, also known as pseudoplasticity. This is because slurry water containing wax residue is a high-solid particulate suspension that easily forms stable Sample three-dimensional (3D) structures. When subjected to shear forces, water within the structural voids is released as free water, leading to shear thinning. Table presents the Herschel-Bulkley model parameters for various slurries with different concentrations (a = 0, 5, and 10%). The majority of these slurries exhibit correlation coefficients (R 2) exceeding 0.99, indicating a strong fit to the Herschel-Bulkley model. The rheological indices (n) of the slurries were consistently below 1 and demonstrated a decline with increasing solid content, suggesting an enhancement in the pseudoplastic behavior of the modified wax-residue coal water slurry. Furthermore, the yield stress (τ y ) and consistency index (K) exhibited an increase with an increase in solid content, highlighting the escalating significance of interparticle interactions. Consequently, elevated solid content requires higher shear stress. Despite potential alterations in surface structure postmodification, they retain a distinct composition from coal, comprising mineral and organic components. These structural disparities can influence interactions at particle–water and particle–particle interfaces. Consequently, the introduction of modified WR can impact the microstructure of the slurry, resulting in viscosity differences between WR-CWS and conventional CWS.

Contact Angle

The contact angle serves as a significant parameter for assessing the extent to which particle surfaces are wettable. Table shows the contact angles of coal, wax residue, and two types of modified wax residues. A smaller contact angle indicates a stronger surface wettability and a more pronounced hydrophilicity. Coal is a nonpolar substance, and its surface contains benzene rings, alkyl side chains, and other inorganic components, which result in its hydrophobic nature. Research indicates that the wettability of coal surfaces exhibits a direct correlation with both its moisture content and oxygen content. The Yangchangwan coal has a higher oxygen content and moisture content, leading to a relatively higher wettability, which gives it not only hydrophobic but also certain hydrophilic properties. As indicated in Table , the coal exhibits a contact angle of 55°, with the majority of coal samples presenting contact angles within the 40–70° range, suggesting a relatively hydrophilic nature of the experimental coal. Wax residue contains approximately 50% wax components, primarily consisting of long-chain alkanes, whose elemental analysis showing zero oxygen content confirms their strong hydrophobicity and low surface energy characteristics. As shown in Table , the contact angle of the unmodified wax residue is 110°, while the contact angles of the two modified wax residues are 100 and 97°, respectively. It is evident that the wettability of the modified wax residue has improved, mainly because the modification agent can adhere to the surface of the wax residue. The structural differences between coal and wax residue are significant, which also result in considerable differences in their wettability (Table ).

ζ-Potential

The ζ-potential values of various substances, such as YCW, YCW-CWS, WR, WR-1, and WR-2, are illustrated in Figure . The analysis reveals that both the YCW and WR surfaces exhibit negative electrical potentials. Compared to the potential on the surface of ordinary coal particles, the potential of coal particles in a coal water slurry is significantly higher. This is mainly because sodium lignosulfonate, an anionic dispersant, forms an adsorbed layer covering the surfaces of the coal particles. As a result, coal particles’ surface potential rises, the repulsive force increases, the coal particles are dispersed, and the whole coal water slurry system becomes more stable. , The ζ-potential of a coal water slurry under various wax slag mixing conditions is displayed in Figure . The findings suggest that all CWS suspensions exhibit a negative charge, and the degree of electronegativity remains relatively consistent, irrespective of the incorporation of various wax residues. This may be due to the relatively weak surface potential of the modified wax residue compared to coal particles. Upon the addition of a wax residue, there is no significant alteration in the average electronegativity of the particles.

The Stable Dispersion Mechanism of Slurry was Analyzed Based on the Principle of Extended DLVO

The van der Waals, electrostatic, and hydrophobic interaction energies, as well as the total interaction energy of the three coal–coal, coal-WR-1, and WR-1-WR-1 particles, are displayed in Figure . The electrostatic interaction force depicted in Figure a is positively charged, indicating a repulsive force that aids in particle dispersion. Conversely, Figure b,c illustrate negative van der Waals and hydrophobic interaction energies, indicating an attractive force that promotes particle aggregation. Specifically, due to the particles’ marked hydrophobicity, the hydrophobic forces become the predominant contributor to the overall attractive forces between them, outweighing the van der Waals forces. Notably, WR-1 particles exhibit a significantly higher hydrophobic interaction energy compared to coal particles, highlighting the correlation between the particle hydrophobicity and interaction energy strength. Analysis of the total energy plot reveals that the electrostatic interaction force is predominant among coal–coal, coal-WR-1, and WR-1-WR-1 particles. The electrostatic repulsive force between coal-WR-1 particles surpasses that between coal–coal particles, suggesting an enhancement in the electrostatic repulsion upon the addition of WR-1. Furthermore, the presence of dispersants such as sodium lignosulfonate and OP-10 is observed to elevate the surface potential of particles. This elevation enhances electrostatic repulsion between particles, ensuring stable dispersion of the slurry without agglomeration. These findings align with the results of ζ-potential experiments and stability analyses.

Modification Mechanism of WR and Dispersion Mechanism of CWS

Figure presents a schematic diagram explaining the mechanism of the modified wax residue water coal slurry (WR-CWS). OP-10s hydrophobic groups adsorb on wax residue surfaces via intermolecular forces, forming a modifying layer that improves wettability and prevents aggregation. Simultaneously, its ethoxy and hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bonds with water, creating a hydrophilic hydrated layer that further enhances the surface wettability. Additionally, the dispersant SL modifies the surface characteristics of coal particles by adsorbing onto their surface through hydrophobic interactions, enhancing binding and extending hydrophilic groups into the solution. The polar group of the dispersant orients toward water, forming a water film and increasing electrostatic repulsion between coal particles, which enhances dispersion and stability. When the modified WR suspension is added, excess OP-10 interacts with coal, further promoting a slurry dispersion. The WR fills the gaps between coal particles, forming a stable network structure with OP-10 and SL, bound together by significant intermolecular forces. This structure ensures the relative stability of the slurry’s internal structure and prevents particle agglomeration. −

Conclusions

This research primarily examines the slurry formation properties and stable dispersion mechanism of modified WR following coslurry with coal, addressing the challenge of grinding WR. WR-1 and WR-2 were combined with YCW coal to create a slurry, enabling an investigation into the grinding behavior of a modified WR-CWS. Additionally, the research delves into the dispersion mechanism of the modified slurry utilizing the expanded DLVO theory and examines the modification procedure of WR. The results of this investigation offer valuable insights into the following aspects:(1)The coal water slurry (CWS) with modified wax residues (WR-1 and WR-2) maintained pseudoplastic shear-thinning behavior. WR-1 increased the maximum solid loading to 68.17% (67.67% for the unmodified sample) at 5% blending ratio through optimized particle packing, while WR-2 showed opposite effects due to its distinct particle size distribution.(2)The electronegativity of CWS suspensions showed minimal variation with different wax residue additions, likely because the surface potential of modified wax residues was weaker than that of coal particles.(3)According to extended DLVO theory analysis, WR-1 significantly enhanced electrostatic repulsion between coal particles, with an interaction energy (E T) of 2 × 10–10 J, substantially higher than the interparticle repulsion of coal alone, thereby effectively preventing particle agglomeration.
