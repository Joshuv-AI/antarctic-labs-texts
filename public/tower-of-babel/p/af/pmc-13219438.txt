A highly limited amino acid library from asteroid Bennu yields wide-ranging protein folds
Authors: Stuart Bartlett, Arushi Gupta, Dominic Phung, Josh E. Goldford, Jiani Yang, Klara Hlouchova, Woodward Fischer, Yuk L. Yung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AI protein design software is used to explore the world of ancient protein structures, and very small, primordial amino acid libraries are found to produce a wide variety of key folds. Data from asteroid analyses and protobiotic chemistry experiments are utilised to constrain several primitive amino acid libraries of varying sizes. Protein design software is then used to construct sequences of these amino acids that could emulate a broad range of key protein folds including metabolic (all enzymes of the reverse tricarboxylic acid cycle), redox, electron-transfer and ribosomal, among others. AlphaFold2 is employed to predict 3D structures from these sequences, which are compared to their native counterparts using the Template Modelling Score. A library of only 6 amino acids-those of highest measured abundance on asteroid Bennu-can reproduce all folds in the test set. Two libraries of just 7 amino acids, constrained by the Miller-Urey experiment and Murchison meteorite data, and one library with 8 amino acids constrained by another Miller-Urey experiment, are also able to produce all folds considered. It is also demonstrated that a 6 amino acid alphabet-the 5 most abundant on Bennu supplemented by cysteine that could have been supplied by atmospheric haze chemistry-can yield a ferredoxin-like protein with a plausible Fe-S binding geometry. Such broad protein folding with very limited amino acid libraries has significant implications for the origins of life, synthetic biology and medical applications.

Introduction

Despite decades of intensive research, the origins of life remains one of the deepest, most stubborn and enduring mysteries in science. Approaches to this question can be approximately divided into bottom-up and top-down. In the former, attempts are made to recapitulate origins processes in experiments or simulations, perhaps by re-creating early Earth environments and testing for the formation of biomolecules1, or by creating abstract models of non-equilibrium complex systems2. Top-down approaches aim to de-construct the integrated, modular architectures of known life, to reduce and simplify its components and organisation, and thus travel back in evolutionary time3. Embarking on this journey takes one through eons of evolutionary transitions and global cataclysms, back to a time before there was atmospheric oxygen, then to before the age of eukaryotes, coming eventually to a world of simple, single cells. We cannot derive every detail of the organisms that inhabited the Earth at this primordial stage, approximately 4 billion years ago when the planet had barely stabilised from the late heavy bombardment. However, frontier methods in bioinformatics can give incredible insights into the so-called last universal common ancestor (LUCA)4 (note that opinions vary greatly as to whether there was one LUCA, or a set of LUCAs, or something even more complex5).

Since at that time the evolutionary machinery itself was in the process of emerging from a range of potential candidates (alternative genetic coding systems, ribosomal structures, metabolic systems, etc.), origins researchers can garner constraints and guidance by exploring various possibility spaces that were key to life’s emergence. One such possibility space is the world of proteins and the folded structures that endow them with their astounding functions. While there is ongoing, intensive debate regarding the sequence with which life’s functions arose (or indeed whether thinking of life’s emergence as a chronological, serial sequence of function discoveries makes philosophical sense6), there is widespread agreement that primitive proteins played a key, early role. This is because native minerals and putative inorganic ligands have, in general, limited catalytic power on their own. Peptides on the other hand, even rather small ones, can have significant catalytic potency7, and more broadly, enzymes are the engines that power life as we know it8–10.

In this work, we set out to chart the world of early protein folds using artificial intelligence (AI) tools, in particular, a set of enzymes such as one might need to drive a primitive metabolic cycle as well as other key examples including the TIM Barrel, Ferredoxin, Thioredoxin, ribosomal proteins and Ribonuclease H. Much work in the origins field has suggested that the so-called ‘reverse tricarboxylic acid’ (rTCA) cycle, or reverse Krebs cycle, could have served as the earliest metabolic system11,12. This is due to the fact that the precursor inputs were readily available in early Earth environments, it is biologically ubiquitous and universal, and certain inorganic substances appear able to drive some of its reaction steps13.

Given this hypothesis of the rTCA being the first metabolic system, we can endeavour to find the minimal set of protein folds required for this cycle to function. Enzyme function depends upon various physical chemical properties, and in this work we focus specifically on folds, perhaps the most defining feature. We targeted an intermediate, progenote stage of life’s emergence. Approximately speaking this would be after the true origin of life, but before all of the known functions of the LUCA were in place. It is assumed that at this stage peptides and small proteins were being synthesised, and a resolution of ‘the hydrolysis problem’ had emerged. We also assume that the symmetry-breaking event(s) which led to the fundamental property of homochirality-ubiquitous in life as we know it-had already come to pass14–17. We thus only consider left-handed or L amino acids (AAs) in this work, though there is increasing interest and caution regarding the so-called ‘Mirror world’, a stereo-inverted biochemical system of only D (right-handed) AAs, that should also be fully viable18.

Given the abundance of AAs in meteorites, asteroids and protobiotic19 chemistry experiments, it is clear that subsets of simple AAs would have been available ‘on tap’ in various early Earth scenarios20,21. What is not clear however, is the point at which the larger, more complex, challenging to synthesise AAs were incorporated into biochemistry. These are generally considered to be the ‘late 10’ AAs: cysteine, phenylalanine, histidine, lysine, methionine, asparagine, glutamine, arginine, tryptophan, tyrosine. Note however, that the membership of this list depends upon the availability of key precursors or elements. For example, phenylalanine and methionine can sometimes be detected in trace amounts in protobiotic chemistry experiments21,22. In addition, new studies suggest that cysteine may have been supplied to the surface of the Archaen Earth through atmospheric haze chemistry23.

Recent experimental work has shown that the canonical 20 AAs are highly favourable compared to other subsets of non-canonical AAs in terms of their ability to form folded structures, which are key to protein function24,25. Given this and other compelling evidence for canonical AAs outcompeting non-canonical AAs in terms of foldability, we can ask whether reduced libraries of canonical AAs-such as would be available in protobiotic environments-might also be able to form essential protein folds.

Several previous works have considered the feasibility of reduced AA libraries for the formation of functional proteins. For example, Walter et al.26 engineered an active chorismate mutase using an AA library of size 9, demonstrating that substantially reduced alphabets can still support both fold and function. Timm et al.27 designed a 13-residue peptide that coordinates a di-Ni cluster and catalyses hydrogen evolution, showing that simple metal-binding peptides can perform proto-hydrogenase chemistry.

In Gibney et al.28, 16-residue peptides were created from a library of just 8 AAs that can coordinate an Fe-S cluster and the authors also built a small four-helix bundle that binds both Fe-S clusters and hemes. This shows that simple, minimal sequences can organise multiple redox cofactors and carry out basic electron-transfer roles. Riddle et al.29 showed that a small β-sheet protein (the SH3 domain) can be built largely from a 5 AA library, whereas a 3 AA set failed. Despite drastic sequence simplification, the engineered domains folded rapidly and remained functional, suggesting that complex protein architecture doesn’t require the full modern alphabet.

Tretyachenko et al.30 constructed large sets of random AA sequences ( ~ 105 residues long) using either the full 20 canonical AAs or a 10 AA ‘prebiotic’ subset, and found that compact, protease-resistant structures can form from both libraries. It was found that the early-alphabet proteins were intrinsically more soluble and refoldable. They also showed that modern-alphabet proteins rely more on chaperones for solubility, while early-alphabet proteins may achieve structure via salts, metal ions, or oligomerisation-suggesting different routes to foldability early in life’s history.

Taking similar inspiration to our own, Solis31 explored whether primitive AA libraries could have formed the basis of a metabolic system early in life’s history. They considered whether a primitive 10 AA library can encode modern-like protein structures. Surveying thousands of proteins with an information-theoretic metric, it was found that the primitive set preserved fold information especially well for mixed α/β classes, implying that early polypeptides could fold before the full 20 AA alphabet emerged.

Murphy et al.32 showed that reducing the AA library to 10 − 12 residues preserves most sequence information needed to produce correct folds for many protein families. However, they observed a sharp drop in matching for smaller libraries, implying a practical lower bound of 10 − 12 letters for capturing diverse folds via sequence patterns. Longo et al.33 built ‘primitive’ β-trefoil proteins using reduced alphabets of size 12 or 13, strongly biased towards protobiotic AAs (74 − 79%), and showed they still fold and crystalise while preserving the β-trefoil architecture. Their most simplified variant folded only at high salt, suggesting primitive libraries can support foldable proteins in saline environments.

Compared to prior studies, our work targets library sizes at the lower end of what has previously been considered necessary or possible for broad structural matching, and we aimed to predict specific backbone structures over a wide range of protein families.

In order to design protein structures from primitive AA libraries, we use four main steps: 1) Library constraining using measurement data, 2) Sequence prediction using ProteinMPNN, 3) Structure prediction using Alphafold2, and 4) Structure comparison using the TM-Score. We see that almost no structural fold matching is achieved for libraries of size 4, though note the exception of Succinyl CoA Synthase, within which the enzyme fold with PDB code 1OI7 can be formed using only G, A, P and V (it was not possible to form this fold with a library size of < 4 AAs). Beyond these very small libraries, many folds can be formed with 5 AAs when using meteorite-derived constraints. Our main result is that the 6 AAs quantitatively observed in asteroid Bennu (Glycine (G), Alanine (A), Proline (P), Valine (V), Leucine (L) and Glutamic Acid (E)) are sufficient to produce all folds considered (Fig. 2a), which is an extraordinary diversity of structures for such a small AA library.

The Murchison (CM2) library (Fig. 2b) was able to reproduce all considered folds with only 7 AAs: Alanine (A), Glutamic acid (E), Glycine (G), Proline (P), Serine (S), Threonine (T) and Valine (V).

The volcanic Miller-Urey library of size 7 appears sufficient to reproduce all folds tested (Fig. 3a). This library includes Glycine (G), Alanine (A), Serine (S), Aspartic acid (D), Valine (V), Glutamic acid (E) and Phenylalanine (F). The last member, Phenylalanine, is rarely considered an ancient AA, and indeed it appears at a low concentration in the results of Johnson et al.22.

At a library size of 8, all folds can be formed by the H2S Miller-Urey set21 (Fig. 3b). Taken together, these results lend strong support to the hypothesis that primordial, abiotic sources of AAs could enable the emergence of an expansive range of protein folds.

Note that while a TM-score of greater than 0.5 is conventionally used to imply a strong structural match, we could also raise our threshold to TM-scores of at least 0.75 and the conclusions described above would be unaffected.

While this suggests a reduced upper bound on the required AA set required for a broad range of key protein folds, it does not rule out the possibility of complete fold matching with even smaller libraries. The complete space of protein sequences for even small AA alphabets is astronomically large (specifically, it is PN, where P is the library size and N is the number of residues in the protein), e.g., for 6 AAs and a peptide of length 100, there are ~ 6.5 × 1077 members of the sequence space. Given that we cannot possibly sample the sequence space of even small proteins, it is testament to the incredible power of ProteinMPNN that we could uncover such well matched folds with relatively small numbers of sequences (namely, 10 sequences for each specific protein structure tested). With deeper exploration of sequence space and tuning of the AI models, we may be able to reduce the upper bound of 6 AAs even further. It may also be possible to discover structural matching with sequences of different lengths to the original, native sequences34.

It is natural to question how a primitive six-member library such as the Bennu set (G, A, P, V, L, E)-mostly neutral or hydrophobic, with a single acidic residue (E), and lacking basic and aromatic residues-could support binding or catalysis. In ferredoxins, Fe-S clusters are generally ligated by cysteines. Thus, without a non-canonical substitute, inclusion of cysteine at specific positions would likely be required to bind and stabilize the Fe-S cofactor and enable redox function. While cysteine was long thought to be absent or scarce in early Earth scenarios, recent work suggests that it could have been supplied from the atmosphere via organic haze chemistry23.

As such, we examined whether a modified Bennu six-member library, with cysteine substituted for Glutamic acid (G, A, P, V, L, C), could re-capitulate Ferredoxin structures. An example structure (PDB code 1RFK) generated using just these 6 residues is shown in Fig. 5. The TM-score of the structural alignment between designed and native was 0.91. Both sequences have coordinating cysteines at positions 38, 43, 46, and 76 (or 41, 46, 49 and 81 factoring in a 3-residue frame shift due to Xs in the native sequence), and the local geometry of plausible Fe-S cluster placements suggest that the primitive version may indeed be capable of binding an Fe-S cluster. Discussion

By uncovering sequences and corresponding folded structures that match known key proteins, our results provide compelling support for the idea that metabolic, ribosomal and other protein folds can be formed using very small AA libraries. Given the results of protobiotic chemistry experiments and meteorite measurements, it is clear that such repertoires of AAs would have been available on early Earth (and perhaps also Mars and other astrobiological targets). Uncovering additional protein folds from these ancient libraries could provide invaluable insights into the forces that selected the universal genetic code. We may find that libraries as small as 5 or 4 AAs are sufficient to reconstruct more folds, with concomitant additional guidance as to the emergence events leading to the LUCA.

Our analysis suggests a very low upper bound on the necessary AAs required for key folds. Note that previous estimates of the minimum AA library required for early life were often higher, e.g., Longo et al.33 suggested 12 or 13. A key question however, relates to the selection process that eliminated non-canonical AAs at this stage of life’s emergence. Abiotic sources would have been providing a diversity of non-canonical AAs, and the selective forces that filtered them out from early life’s repertoire are unclear (apart from the propensity for foldability, as discussed in Makarov et al.24). A significant step forward will occur when AI protein sequence design and structure prediction models can incorporate non-canonical AAs. This is also pertinent to the question of enzyme function: our libraries would require additional, positively charged members to achieve their known functions, and these could have been non-canonical for some transient phase before life settled upon the canonical 20 AAs.

The fact that primitive AA libraries appear to be capable of producing a range of folded structures can also contribute to our understanding of how the ribosome originated. Since the ribosome is an RNA-peptide complex, its emergence would have required sources of both AAs and RNA precursors, with a complex process of co-evolution eventually condensing onto the final structure and genetic code35. Given that we successfully matched two ribosomal proteins using primordial libraries, this coevolutionary process between peptide and RNA worlds might not have had to wait for the availability of all 20 canonical AAs, and a fascinating new line of research is now opened up: the exploration of protoribosomal structures using highly limited AA libraries.

We do not claim that our protobiotic analogue structures match the functional efficacy of their modern counterparts. In proteins, form enables function, but the relationship is nuanced. Achieving the correct fold is only one requirement among several-binding geometry, catalytic residues, electrostatics, and dynamics all contribute to the functions of these molecular machines. For example, many enzymes rely critically on histidine side chains at the active site. This includes examples like PDB 1OI7 (Succinyl CoA Synthase), shown in Fig. 4a aligned with its primitive analogue. In the structure shown in that figure, instead of histidine, the primitive analogue has an alanine in its place, which clearly would not serve as a functional substitute for histidine. More broadly, our primordial libraries lack positively charged residues (K, R, H) and aromatics, a clear limitation for catalysis, metal or cofactor coordination, and specific binding.

However, this limitation may be a clue to an early division of labour among AAs. Our six-AA Bennu set (A, E, G, L, P, V) is sparse in charge and aromatic chemistry yet retains good hydrophobicity and size coverage, which helps it access a wide range of folds. This suggests that the remaining 14 canonical residues-including the more complex ‘modern 10’-were later selected primarily to expand binding, redox/electron-transfer, and catalytic capabilities. In other words, early life may have first explored readily accessible fold space with a minimal alphabet, and only subsequently incorporated additional AAs to endow those folds with the functional properties required for core biochemistry.

In the earliest stages of life, perhaps folded structures were forming from these limited libraries, but were performing different functions before their role as catalysts became central. Alternatively, perhaps small amounts of positively charged, non-canonical AAs replaced residues such as histidine in these primordial proteins, before life eventually discovered and locked in the 20 canonical AAs. Future work will explore substitution matrices and sequence alignments to see whether alternative residues in key places might serve as adequate functional substitutes for those found in known proteins. We will also test whether small amounts of histidine or other positively charged AAs (including non-canonical) could recover any lost function of these proteins.

In the case of cysteine, recent work suggests that atmospheric haze chemistry on the early Earth may have provided a source of this residue, which is crucial for redox proteins such as Ferredoxin23. We found that a modified six-member Bennu set that includes cysteine (G, A, P, V, L, C) was able to recapitulate several Ferredoxin structures (see example in Fig. 5). Furthermore, the ProteinMPNN sequence-generation step correctly positioned cysteines at all four canonical sites for Fe-S cluster coordination, lending support to the idea that this primitive design could act as a functional Ferredoxin.

There are many natural extensions that follow from these results. At the sequence level, future work could assess homology between each designed enzyme and its native counterpart, quantify the fraction of residues in natural sequences that fall within the AA subset of the corresponding reduced library, and perform k-mer or motif analyses to determine whether established sequence motifs are recapitulated in the designed proteins. Additional sequence-based comparisons could include computing sequence distances (e.g., Hamming or Levenshtein distances) between designed sequences and their closest natural homologs, as well as conducting BLAST searches to identify the nearest natural protein matches and report sequence similarity metrics.

Beyond sequence similarity, further analyses could focus on structural and functional contextualization. In particular, it would be informative to evaluate whether cofactor-binding pockets, electrostatic landscapes near active sites, and known protein-protein interaction interfaces are preserved in the designed primitive analogue structures. Tools such as Foldseek could be used to compare the designed structures to those contained in the Alphafold Protein Structure Database, thereby quantifying how redundant or novel the predicted folds are relative to the known natural structural space.

Finally, these analyses could be extended into an explicitly evolutionary and phylogenetic framework. Sequence-level comparisons could be performed across phylogenetic trees spanning proteins from diverse species, enabling direct tests of the hypothesis that earlier evolutionary branches exhibit higher homology to designed sequences and a stronger bias toward ancient AAs, while later branches show greater diversification and increased incorporation of newer AAs.

Ultimately, designed structures will require experimental validation, an essential next step in this work. We are in the process of producing and experimentally validating our best-matching sequences, and will also be carrying out functional assays to test whether primordial proteins can bind with relevant targets and catalyse their requisite reactions. Note that recent work has confirmed that Alphafold2-simulated folds do indeed form their correct 3D structures when produced in the laboratory36.

Beyond the origins of life, these results could provide simpler and cheaper means to synthesise functional proteins, with applications in synthetic biology, medicine, chemical engineering and artificial life.

Methods

Predicting 3D protein structure just from an AA sequence was a computationally formidable task until recent years, when advanced AI algorithms effectively ‘solved’ this problem through training on large databases of known examples. Models such as Alphafold 2 and 3, and ESMFold can compute accurate protein structures in minutes, with associated confidence scores37–39. This opens up an invaluable opportunity for origins researchers and the exploratory reconstruction of primitive proteins.

In the present work, we used the sequence design model, ProteinMPNN to infer possible sequences given various target folds40, and several reduced AA libraries from which the proteins could be built. Specifically, we used representative AA subsets constrained by the Miller-Urey experiment21,22,41 and meteorites, namely Murchison and the recently studied Bennu asteroid20. We started from a minimal set of the 4 most abundant AAs and re-introduced members of the library in order of measured abundance.

We considered all the enzymes present within the rTCA cycle: 2-Oxoglutarate Synthase, Aconitase, Pyruvate Synthase, Citrate Synthase, Fumarase, Fumarate Reductase, Isocitrate Dehydrogenase, Malate Dehydrogenase, and Succinyl-CoA Synthase. Also included in our test set were Ferredoxin, Thioredoxin, the TIM Barrel fold, ribosomal proteins, and Ribonuclease H. We used ProteinMPNN40 to design sequences using targets from at least two specific entries in the Protein Data Bank for each of these protein classes. This design process takes as a constraint the relevant reduced AA library under evaluation, candidate sequence generation with ProteinMPNN, and modelling of the resulting protein structures using Alphafold2. Finally, we validated the structural similarity between native structures and the modelled, primitive analogues using the ‘TM-Score’42,43.

Note that recent experimental work has confirmed the efficacy of Alphafold2’s structure predictions for sequences derived from primitive AA libraries36. This lends strong empirical credence to the predictions of our modelling pipeline.

Library constraining

In the following we use standard single-letter AA codes: Alanine (A), cysteine (C), aspartic acid (D), glutamic acid (E), phenylalanine (F), glycine (G), isoleucine (I), leucine (L), methionine (M), proline (P), serine (S), threonine (T), valine (V).

There are many (in fact an astronomically large number) of possible AA libraries that early life may have used. However, it is reasonable to assume that many of them can be ruled out due to kinetic and thermodynamic limits to abiotic synthesis. Certain AAs, such as A and G, are found in many protobiotic scenarios and are synthesised in various astronomical environments, such as interstellar clouds44. If we focus upon plausible early Earth conditions, we can use protobiotic chemistry and meteorite analyses as guidance to the specific AAs that might have been provided to early life abiotically. In the present work we used four AA libraries, two derived from meteorite measurements, and two from Miller-Urey experiments.

Our first library was constrained by recent, high precision measurements of samples from the asteroid Bennu, collected by the OSIRIS-REx mission. We used measured abundances from Extended Data Table 2 of Glavin et al.20 to construct an abundance-ranked list of AAs: G, A, P, V, L and E. The smallest library of 4 consisted of G, A, P and V, and the largest library of 6 consisted of G, A, P, V, L and E.

Our second library was derived from analyses of the well-studied Murchison meteorite. We used the measured abundances from Extended Data Table 2 of Glavin et al.20 to construct an abundance-ranked list of AAs: G, A, P, V, T, E, S, L and I. The smallest library of 4 consisted of G, A, P and V (identical to Bennu’s smallest library), and the largest library of 9 consisted of G, A, P, V, T, E, S, L and I.

Our third library made use of an analysis of Stanley Miller’s 1953-54 University of Chicago protobiotic chemistry experiments22. This apparatus “simulates the spark discharge synthesis by lightning in a steam-rich volcanic eruption”. We ranked the detected canonical AAs in order of abundance: G, A, S, D, V, E and F. The smallest library of 4 consisted of G, A, S and D, and the largest library of 7 consisted of G, A, S, D, V, E, F.

Similarly, we ranked the AAs detected from the Miller-Urey experiments described in Parker et al.21, the so-called ‘1958 Miller H2S-rich spark discharge experiment’, in order of abundance: A, G, V, S, T, I, E, L, D and M. As above, we started with a minimal library of 4 (A, G, V and S), incrementing by one until reaching a maximal library of 10: A, G, V, S, T, I, E, L, D and M.

Protein Sequence design

With a given AA library in hand, the next step in our pipeline was to design sequences given a target structure. All target structures were sourced from the Protein Data Bank, and we used ProteinMPNN to predict the sequences40. ProteinMPNN is freely available on Github, and the code is available as a Colab Notebook. In the present work, we simply entered the target structure using the relevant PDB code, constrained the model to only design chain “A”, left the sampling temperature at the default value of 0.1, and used the “omit AAs” flag to enter the AAs that the algorithm should ignore due to the reduced AA library.

For each class of proteins, e.g., Malate Dehydrogenase, we picked at least two specific entries from the PDB, and made 10 separate sequence predictions for each PDB entry. Structure prediction

With a given AA sequence in hand, the next step in our pipeline was to predict the protein structure arising from such a sequence. We used the popular Alphafold2 algorithm, freely available as a Colab notebook. We did not modify any of the parameters or options away from their default values in the code, hence this was simply a matter of entering the AA sequence, running the algorithm, and downloading the PDB output files corresponding to the folded structure.

Structure comparison

With a given primitive AA structure in hand in the form of a PDB file, the final stage in our pipeline was to compare this analogue structure to the original target. For this purpose, we use the commonly employed Template Modeling score (TM-Score)42,43. The TM-score has an online app as well as a C++ code that rapidly assigns a matching score between two protein structures. In the present work, we utilised the ’TM-Align’ C++ code to compute TM-Score values. These scores were used for measuring structural matching throughout our analysis.

In order to cross-validate our results, we also computed structural comparisons using the root mean square deviation (RMSD), and found very similar results to those obtained using the TM-Score, as presented in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. We also compared the RMSD and TM-Score to the ProteinMPNN Fit Score, Alphafold2 pTM and Maximum Substructure score for specific protein examples. As shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, and discussed in the Supplementary Information, the TM-Score and RMSD were found to be significantly more robust and reliable as structural comparators than the other three metrics listed above, as supported by the former’s widespread use in the field of protein engineering.

In order to control for potential biases or inaccuracies due to Alphafold2’s specific features, including its use of multiple sequence alignments (MSAs), we re-computed structures for all folds considered using the ESMFold model38. We found very similar results to those obtained with Alphafold2, as described in the Supplementary Information and presented in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4.
