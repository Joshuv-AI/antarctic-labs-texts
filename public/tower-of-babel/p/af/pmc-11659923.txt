Enhancing Fc‐mediated effector functions of monoclonal antibodies: The example of HexaBodies
Authors: Hilma J van der Horst, Tuna Mutis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummarySince the approval of the CD20‐targeting monoclonal antibody (mAb) rituximab for the treatment of lymphoma in 1997, mAb therapy has significantly transformed cancer treatment. With over 90 FDA‐approved mAbs for the treatment of various hematological and solid cancers, modern cancer treatment relies heavily on these therapies. The overwhelming success of mAbs as cancer therapeutics is attributed to their broad applicability, high safety profile, and precise targeting of cancer‐associated surface antigens. Furthermore, mAbs can induce various anti‐tumor cytotoxic effector mechanisms including antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement‐dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), all of which are mediated via their fragment crystallizable (Fc) domain. Over the past decades, these effector mechanisms have been substantially improved through Fc domain engineering. In this review, we will outline the different approaches to enhance Fc effector functions via Fc engineering of mAbs, with a specific emphasis on the so‐called “HexaBody” technology, which is designed to enhance the hexamerization of mAbs on the target cell surface, thereby inducing greater complement activation, CDC, and receptor clustering. The review will summarize the development, preclinical, and clinical testing of several HexaBodies designed for the treatment of B‐cell malignancies, as well as the potential use of the HexaBody technology beyond Fc‐mediated effector functions.Keywords: antibody therapy, B‐cell malignancies, complement‐mediated cytotoxicity, Fc‐engineering, HexaBody technology, multiple myeloma

INTRODUCTION

Antibodies (Abs), produced by B cells, are key components of our antigen‐specific adaptive immune system. 1 While it has long been known that they are crucial for defending our bodies against infections and diseases, 2 the idea of using Abs in cancer treatment originated from observations in the previous century, demonstrating that the immune system could recognize and attack cancer cells by identifying their surface antigens via Abs. 3 , 4 Since then, extensive research has discovered several cell surface antigens on cancer cells that are either shared with healthy cells or expressed at much higher levels compared to their healthy counterparts, fueling the idea of targeting such cancer‐associated surface antigens with antibodies for therapeutic purposes. 5

The development of the mouse hybridoma technology in 1975, which enabled large‐scale production of mAbs, is generally considered a major step toward the clinical application of murine antibodies. 6 However, it soon became evident that murine mAbs were immunogenic and were rapidly neutralized in the human body. 7 This led to the development of chimeric mAbs, in which a constant human domain was fused to a variable mouse domain. 8 Using this technology, the chimeric CD20‐targeting mAb rituximab became the first FDA‐approved mAb for cancer therapy in 1997. 9

To date, advanced antibody engineering technologies, such as human antibody‐gene expression libraries 10 and transgenic mice, have enabled the production of non‐immunogenic, fully human antibodies for treating various diseases, including cancer. 11 Antibody engineering has also been extensively applied to improve target cell specificity and affinity, 12 , 13 , 14 reduce biophysical liabilities, 15 , 16 increase the half‐life of mAbs, 17 and enhance their therapeutic potential through innovative designs, such as antibody fusion products, 18 antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs), 19 and bi‐ or tri‐specific antibodies. 20 , 21 These designs enable the precise targeting of cytotoxic T cells or toxic agents to one or more antigens on cancer cells.

While many recently developed strategies, such as ADCs and bispecific antibodies (bsAbs), enhance cancer cell elimination by associating target‐specific antibodies or fragments with various cytotoxic agents, engineering the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region of antibodies traditionally aims to enhance the naturally occurring Fc‐mediated effector functions, which include antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement‐dependent cytotoxicity (CDC). Over the past decades, a comprehensive understanding of Fc‐effector mechanisms has led to the development of effective strategies to enhance their activation. In this review, we will first briefly outline various traditional Fc‐engineering strategies, designed to enhance Fc‐mediated effector functions via enhancing binding affinity or binding avidity. We will then focus on HexaBody Technology, a novel Fc‐engineering approach developed from fundamental biological research on C1q binding, designed to enhance CDC.

THE STRUCTURE OF THE Fc DOMAIN

The Fc domain of an Ab is the distal part of the heavy chain constant region that specifically interacts with cell surface receptors, known as Fc‐gamma receptors (FcγR), and complement proteins. Depending on the isotype of the Ab, the Fc domain includes only the CH2 and CH3 regions (IgG, IgA, IgD), or an additional CH4 (IgE, IgM) region. The Fc domain is connected to the CH1 region on the Fab‐fragment by a flexible hinge region. The Fc‐region is extensively glycosylated, with the glycosylation pattern being class and isotype specific. Extensive reviews on these topics have been published. 22 , 23

Therapeutic antibodies are predominantly of the IgG isotype and of the IgG1 subclass, due to its favorable pharmacokinetics in combination with strong induction of Fc‐effector function. 24 , 25 Binding of the IgG1 Fc tail to FcγRs on immune cells can induce ADCC or ADCP. ADCC is primarily induced by natural killer (NK) cells which express only FcγRIIIa (CD16a). 26 ADCP can be mediated by macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, and dendritic cells. 27 In contrast to NK cells, these immune cells can co‐express several FcγRs, including FcγRI (CD64), FcγRIIa (CD32a), and FcγRIIb (CD16a), as well as the inhibitory FcγRIIb (CD32b). The induction of ADCP thereby depends on the binding to activating FcγR relative to the inhibitory FcγRIIb. 28 FcγR signaling involves phosphorylation of immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based activation motifs (ITAMs) or immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based inhibitory motifs (ITIMs), leading to target cell death through the release of granzymes or perforin, or to opsonization and phagocytosis of the target cell. 29 Binding of the antibody Fc tail to complement protein C1q can trigger CDC through a cascade of proteolytic cleavage steps, ultimately leading to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on the target cell surface. The membrane pores generated by MAC result in lysis of the cells, defined as CDC. 30

Fc‐ENGINEERING STRATEGIES TO ENHANCE BINDING AFFINITY

Fc‐engineering strategies to improve binding to FcγR or C1q can be mostly divided into two categories: site directed mutagenesis and glycoengineering, which will be briefly outlined.

Site‐directed mutagenesis

This first strategy involves the identification and modification of the specific binding regions within the Fc tail with the aim to enhance binding affinity. In the past decades, the identification of specific FcγR binding residues on Leu234‐Ser239 on the lower hinge and Asp265‐Glu269, Asn297‐Thr299 on the CH2 domain, 31 , 32 have led to the development of double or triple amino acid substitutions S239D/I332E, P247I/A339Q, and S298A/E333A/K334A, which significantly enhanced the binding of Abs to FcγRIIIa, thereby improving ADCC. 33 , 34 , 35 Additionally, efforts aiming at selectively enhancing FcγRIIa binding, while minimizing interactions with the inhibitory receptor FcγRIIb, have resulted in ADCP enhancing S239D/A330L/I332E and F243L/R292P/Y300L/V305I/P396L variants. 34 , 36 When discussing the improvement of C1q binding, it should be noted that binding affinity of a single IgG‐Fc domain to C1q is very low, with a dissociation constant (K d) around 10–4 M. 37 It has been shown that antibody binding to C1q, and consequently CDC, can be enhanced through the double mutant K326W/E333S and the triple mutant S267E/H268E/S324T. 38 , 39 These mutants alter residues within or near the primary binding sites, which are the residues 266–272 and 294–300 on one CH2 domain, and residues 325–331 on the other. 40 , 41 , 42 Site‐directed mutagenesis has also been utilized at the hinge region of the Fc‐tail, influencing its flexibility and thereby enhancing C1q binding and CDC. 43

Glycoengineering

This second strategy focuses on modifying the glycosylation pattern of the Fc tail. Notably, the Fc tail of IgG1 antibodies is glycosylated within the CH2 domain, specifically linked to a conserved asparagine at position 297. This so called N297‐linked glycosylation, or N‐glycosylation, contributes to the stability and dynamics of the CH2 domains. 44 , 45 , 46 N‐glycosylation thereby strongly impacts Fc‐effector functions and is especially vital for FcγR binding, and indispensable for interaction with low‐affinity FcγRs. 47 , 48 , 49 Consequently, glycoengineering has been shown to improve Fc‐effector functions ADCC and ADCP. The most significant glycan modification is afucosylation, which involves removing the fucose sugar from the N‐glycan. Afucosylated antibodies exhibit increased binding affinity for FcγRIIIa, thereby markedly improving both ADCC and ADCP. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Additionally, afucosylation has minimal impact on antibody stability and manufacturing processes, facilitating the development of several afucosylated or low‐fucosylated antibodies for clinical use. For instance, the afucosylated CD20‐targeting antibody obinutuzumab (GA101; Gazyva/Gazyvaro) demonstrated superior activity over the CD20‐targeting antibody rituximab, 54 , 55 and has received FDA‐approval for treatment of patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and follicular lymphoma.

While glycoengineering strategies primarily enhance ADCC and ADCP, some studies also highlighted the role of glycans in CDC. CDC was observed to be mainly affected by the presence of galactose, with Fc‐(hyper) galactosylation significantly enhancing C1q binding and improving CDC outcomes. 56 More recently, it was found that galactosylation enhances IgG1 hexamerization, the main topic of this review. 57

Fc engineering to improve Fc binding avidity

Another approach to enhance Fc tail interactions with FcγR and C1q is to increase Fc‐binding avidity through Fc‐multiplication. This strategy involves integrating multiple Fc‐tails into a single IgG1 molecule, thereby boosting its overall binding affinity. Both Fc‐duplication and multiplication have been demonstrated to increase FcγR binding avidity and thereby ADCC and ADCP. 58 , 59 , 60 , 61

Fc engineering to generate bispecific antibodies (bsAbs)

bsAbs are engineered antibodies that can simultaneously bind to two different antigens or epitopes. 62 Typically, bsAbs contain two different Fabs with a common Fc tail. Among several approaches to create bsAbs the most employed one is the knob‐into‐holes technology, which involves the generation of complementary “knob” and “hole” mutations in the CH3 domains of IgG molecules, allowing for the formation of a stable heterodimer. 63 , 64 A more recently developed technology is the “DuoBody” technology which involves separate expression of two parental antibodies, each containing matched CH3 mutations (F405L or K409R). After mixing, parental antibodies are subjected to controlled reducing conditions in vitro, which separate the antibodies into HL half‐molecules and allow reassembly and reoxidation to form highly pure heterodimerized bsAbs. 65 While these technologies are primarily used to generate bispecific T‐cell engagers targeting both cancer‐associated antigens and T cells, they can also be employed to design bsAbs with dual antigen‐ or epitope‐binding sites. These bispecific constructs can simultaneously engage multiple targets or epitopes, resulting in increased Fc density and enhanced avidity of Fc interactions.

Biology‐driven Fc‐engineering strategy: The HexaBody technology

In addition to conventional Fc‐engineering strategies, advancements in our understanding of CDC mechanisms over the last decade have introduced a novel and distinct Fc‐engineering approach: the HexaBody technology. This biology‐driven strategy employs a clustering approach based on insights from antibodies with high CDC‐inducing capacity. While the elimination of cancer cells via ADCC and ADCP requires the recruitment of immune effector cells toward target cells, in CDC antibodies activate the complement system to provide a much more rapid killing mechanism. Typically, the first event in the antibody‐dependent complement pathway activation is the binding of antigen‐bound antibodies to the multimeric pattern recognition molecule C1q. 66 This molecule is composed of 18 polypeptide chains organized in a tulip‐like structure, featuring six globular heads and a collagen‐like stalk. 67 Together with a heterotetramer of the proteases C1r and C1s, C1q forms the C1 complex, the first and defining component the complement pathway. Each C1q globular head contains a binding site for the Fc domain in the CH2 region of IgG. However, although a single IgG‐Fc domain binds to C1q with low affinity, 37 IgG1 antibodies can still naturally activate CDC to varying extents, depending on factors such as antigen density, size, and fluidity, even without Fc‐engineering modifications. 68 , 69 , 70 Until the last decade, the precise mechanism by which antigen‐bound antibodies effectively bind C1q and activate the complement cascade remained poorly understood.

In 2014, Diebolder et al. determined high‐resolution crystallographic structures of two antibodies known for efficient induction of CDC: human anti–HIV‐1 gp120 IgG1‐b12 and human antibody 2G12. 71 They observed that the IgG‐Fc segments of these antibodies were arranged in a hexameric ring. Remarkably, the orientation of a critical residue (Lys322) for C1q binding within this ring was spatially compatible with the arrangement of the six globular heads of C1q. Based on these findings, Diebolder et al. hypothesized and then experimentally confirmed that specific non‐covalent interactions between IgG Fc‐domains promote an ordered hexamer formation on the target cell surface. This hexameric arrangement provides a docking platform for the six‐globular‐headed C1q molecule, facilitating efficient complement activation. They further demonstrated that mutations of several Fc domain residues (Ile253, His433, or Asn434) significantly reduced complement activation and inhibited CDC. More importantly, they identified mutations that could enhance CDC. Substituting Glu with Arg at position 345 (E345R) and Glu with Gly at position 430 (E430G) readily promoted the formation of antibody hexamers and significantly enhanced CDC.

A subsequent study has shown that mutations of the residue E345 resulted in potent CDC irrespective of the specific amino acid substitution, suggesting that hexamer formation is not dependent on mutation‐specific interactions. 72 Detailed analyses indicated that E345 mutations most probably increased conformational flexibility or facilitated the movement of H433 of the Fc tail to optimize the noncovalent interactions with the opposing Fc. Among the various mutants, E430G and E345K were identified as particularly effective, significantly enhancing CDC—over 20‐fold compared to natural IgG1—while avoiding target‐independent complement activation in solution. These findings have driven the development of HexaBody technology, which aims to improve the CDC activity of therapeutic IgG1 antibodies by introducing specific point mutations (i.e., E345R and E430G) in the Fc domain of mAbs to enhance non‐covalent Fc interactions and facilitate the natural process of antigen‐bound antibody hexamerization on the cell surface. This leads to superior C1q binding and enhanced CDC (Figure 1). In contrast to Fc‐multimerization strategies, HexaBodies retain the characteristics of conventional IgG1 molecules.

The HexaBody Technology. The introduction of HexaBody mutations E430G and E345R into the Fc region of IgG1 molecules facilitates Fc:Fc interactions and subsequently the formation of IgG1 hexamers on the cell surface after binding to their target antigen. These hexamers create a docking site for the complement protein C1q, which, upon binding, triggers the complement cascade. This cascade results in the assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b‐9) and ultimately induces cell death through complement‐dependent cytotoxicity (CDC).

Application of the HexaBody technology for treatment of B‐cell malignancies

CD20 and CD38 are well‐established therapeutic targets for B‐cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia, B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (B‐NHL), and multiple myeloma (MM), respectively. The antibodies rituximab (targeting CD20) and daratumumab (targeting CD38) have significantly improved patient outcomes. 73 , 74 Hence, these findings provided a rationale for further investigation of HexaBodies for targeting B‐cell malignancies. Initial studies utilizing the HexaBody technology demonstrated enhanced CDC for antibodies targeting the CD20 and CD38 antigens. 71 Notably, HexaBody technology even enabled CDC activity of a type II CD20 antibody that was ineffective in CDC. 72 Subsequent preclinical studies further evaluated HexaBody‐CD38 for treatment of MM, and shifted from CD20 to CD37 as HexaBody‐target for B‐NHL.

DuoHexaBody‐CD37

Similar to CD20, the tetraspanin molecule CD37 is primarily expressed on mature B cells, making it a promising target antigen for the treatment of B‐cell malignancies. 75 Until now, several CD37‐targeting antibodies and immunoconjugates have been clinically evaluated, including BI 836826 (MAb 37.1), a chimeric IgG1 antibody with Fc mutations (S239D/I332E) that induces ADCC and exhibits pro‐apoptotic activity. 76 Despite its effectiveness in eliminating CLL cells in preclinical studies, dose‐limiting toxicities observed in combination with gemcitabine and oxaliplatin (NCT02624492) led to its discontinuation from further clinical development. Since natural CD37‐targeting antibodies show limited CDC, CD37‐targeting HexaBodies were developed to exploit the full potential of CD37 as target. Similar to HexaBodies targeting CD20, CD37‐HexaBodies significantly enhanced CDC. Furthermore, combining HexaBodies targeting both CD20 and CD37 demonstrated synergistic CDC activity, highlighting the benefits of dual‐targeting for increased avidity. 77 Building on this, and recognizing that CD37 can be targeted on different non‐overlapping epitopes, DuoBody technology was employed to enforce the heterodimerization of two distinct CD37‐targeting HexaBodies. This approach resulted in the generation of DuoHexaBody‐CD37, a biparatopic bispecific antibody designed to induce hexamerization. 78 DuoHexaBody‐CD37 retained its ADCC and ADCP capabilities, effectively depleting B cells while sparing other leukocyte populations in peripheral blood samples. Additionally, DuoHexaBody‐CD37 showed a significant anti‐tumor activity in vivo in xenograft models. 78

To further evaluate the clinical potential of DuoHexaBody‐CD37, CDC‐inducing capacity of DuoHexaBody‐CD37 was assessed in primary malignant B cells from lymph node and peripheral blood samples of a large cohort of CLL and B‐NHL patients. 79 DuoHexaBody‐CD37 induced potent, dose‐dependent CDC with minimal variability across samples, showing similar high levels of CDC in both newly diagnosed (ND) and relapsed/refractory (RR) patients. Only in 8 of 51 samples tested the lysis levels were lower than 60%, most of which (n = 7) were derived from DLBCL patients. Intriguingly, in all of these patients the low CDC levels were not due to the reduced CD37 levels, diminished C1q binding, or increased levels of the complement regulatory proteins CD46, CD55, or CD59. 79 These findings implied that DuoHexaBody‐CD37 effectively induced CDC, and in cases where it does not, resistance is not associated with lower complement activation. This translational study has also shown that combination with rituximab and/or ofatumumab could further enhance the CDC activity of DuoHexaBody‐CD37. These preclinical studies paved the way for the initiation of a First‐in‐Human clinical trial of DuoHexaBody‐CD37 (GEN3009) in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) B‐NHL. Nevertheless, the trial was discontinued in 2023, and further clinical development of DuoHexaBody‐CD37 was halted due to strategic reasons, rather than safety or regulatory concerns, according to statements of the developing company (https://ir.genmab.com/node/45356/html).

HexaBody‐CD38

Since its FDA approval in 2015, the fully human CD38‐targeting antibody daratumumab has significantly improved treatment outcomes for patients with MM. More recently, in 2020, isatuximab, a chimeric CD38‐targeting antibody was also approved. 80 Both of these unmodified IgG1 antibodies exhibit all Fc‐mediated effector killing mechanisms, to varying extents, including ADCC, ADCP, and CDC, as well as direct apoptosis or apoptosis upon crosslinking. The antibodies also demonstrate immunomodulatory effects through the depletion of CD38+ immune suppressor cells. 81 , 82 Anti‐tumor efficacy of these antibodies strongly correlates with the CD38 expression level on the tumor cells. 83 , 84 Hence CD38‐directing antibodies are generally more effective in MM patients whose malignant cells usually express high and uniform levels of CD38, in contrast to CLL, AML, and DLBCL patients, where the expression of CD38 on malignant cells is lower and more heterogeneous. 85 , 86 , 87 Nevertheless, even in MM, most if not all patients show disease progression. 88 Although the precise mechanisms of progression are yet to be discovered, it was found that the CD38‐targeting mAbs rapidly reduce CD38 expression on the tumor cell surface. 81 , 83 , 84 Thus, one of the underlying mechanisms of daratumumab resistance could be the strongly reduced sensitivity of MM cells to complement‐mediated tumor cell lysis due to low surface CD38 expression. 89 In an attempt to overcome this important drawback, HexaBody‐CD38 was developed. 90 Similar to CD37‐HexaBodies, the HexaBody‐CD38 (GEN3014) was generated by introducing the E430G mutation in the Fc domain of a human IgG1 mAb that binds CD38 with high affinity, and recognizes a unique epitope distinct from those targeted by daratumumab and isatuximab. Unlike DuoHexaBody‐CD37, HexaBody‐CD38 targets only one epitope on the CD38 molecule. In preclinical studies HexaBody‐CD38‐induced potent CDC not only in MM but also in acute myeloid leukemia and B‐NHL cells. 90 It also showed strong anti‐tumor activity in B‐NHL and AML PDX models in vivo. As expected, HexaBody CD38 also retained its activity to induce ADCC and ADCP. Interestingly, in all these preclinical assays, HexaBody‐CD38 outperformed the CDC activity of daratumumab, particularly in cells with lower levels of CD38 expression. Yet, sensitivity to HexaBody‐CD38 remained positively correlated with CD38 expression level and inversely correlated with expression of complement regulatory proteins. Based on these preclinical studies, a clinical dose‐Expansion Cohort of a Phase 1/2 Trial was initiated to assess the clinical safety of HexaBody‐CD38 (GEN3014) in patients with MM in a (NCT04824794). In a recently shared preliminary results of this trial, the tolerable, safe, and clinically active phase 2 dose was identified as 16 mg/kg. 91 In 11 MM Patients treated with this dose, GEN3014 showed a manageable safety profile: seven patients received ≥1 cycle of treatment. The overall responses were one complete response, two very good partial responses, two partial responses, one minimal response, and one stable disease. The study is ongoing and still open for enrollment.

The use of HexaBody technology to improve antibody clustering at the cell surface

Receptor clustering (dimerization or oligomerization) is a well‐known activation mechanism for numerous signaling pathways, including the pathways for hormones, cytokines, and growth factors. 92 , 93 On the cell membrane, dimerization/oligomerization of signaling molecules can be initiated by the formation of hydrogen bonds between proteins or by lipid–protein interactions, or by ligand‐induced clustering. The latter mechanism is particularly important for molecules in the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily. Accordingly, HexaBody technology was employed to induce clustering and subsequent activation of cell surface molecules through antibody hexamerization.

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 for the efficient activation of the death receptor (DR) pathway

Signaling through FAS receptor and the death receptor‐4 and ‐5 (DR4 and DR5) initiates the extrinsic apoptotic pathway. 94 DR4 and DR5 are receptors for the tumor necrosis factor‐related apoptosis inducing ligand (TRAIL). 95 In the past decades, the discovery of TRAIL and its capacity to induce apoptosis in tumor cells provided a strong stimulus for the development of TRAIL‐based cancer therapies such as the Recombinant TRAIL (e.g., dulanermin) or agonistic antibodies targeting DR4 (mapatumumab) or DR5 (conatumumab, tigatuzumab, drozitumab, lexatumumab, LBY‐135, and DS‐8273a). 96 These approaches were generally safe and well tolerated but they failed to provide significant clinical antitumor efficacy. 96 Soon after, it was discovered that DR signaling pathway could be more efficiently activated by crosslinking and clustering of the receptors on the cell surface, 97 , 98 which was not achieved by conventional antibodies. The fact that HexaBodies could potentially induce clustering of receptors via antibody hexamerization, prompted the development of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5, which consists of a mixture of two noncompeting DR5 mAbs, each carrying the E430G mutation. In an initial study this novel compound was tested in solid tumor models. In 104 different tumor cell lines derived from colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, gastric cancer, NSCLC, and melanoma, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 induced a superior caspase‐mediated tumor cell death by higher‐order DR5 clustering, and subsequent outside‐in signaling. Interestingly, the activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was achieved independent of Fc gamma receptor‐mediated antibody crosslinking. Intriguingly, however, the most optimal effect was observed in the presence of complement complex C1, but without inducing CDC, suggesting that C1 could bind and stabilize the HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 hexamers on the cell surface. The in vivo efficacy of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was confirmed in various PDX models. Nevertheless, resistance to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was observed for a fraction of tested cell lines and xenograft models. 99

In a subsequent preclinical study using MM patient samples, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 induced high levels of specific MM cell kill in bone marrow samples of MM patients, and outperformed the lysis obtained by the same mixture of DR5‐antibodies without the hexamerization‐enhancing E430G mutation, supporting the relevance of enhanced hexamerization in addition to dual epitope targeting for DR5 agonist activity. 100 Overall, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 performed with better efficacy in the earlier treated RR patients as compared to ND patients. Interestingly, the MM cells of patients who had become refractory to daratumumab monotherapy also responded to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5. This translational study also showed that the immunomodulating agent lenalidomide synergistically increased the HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐mediated kill of myeloma cell lines in the presence of effector cells, indicating the improvement of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐mediated ADCC.

Improving T‐cell co‐stimulatory signaling via HexaBodies

CD27 and OX40 are two key co‐stimulatory receptors in T cells. Similar to DR5, they belong to the TNF receptor superfamily. 101 , 102 Like other members of this family, signaling through CD27 and OX40 is enhanced by hexagonal receptor clustering. 103 Encouraged by the successful use of HexaBody technology to induce DR5‐mediated clustering and activation, the platform was recently adapted for CD27 and OX40 through a collaboration between Genmab and BioNTech to result in the products HexaBody‐CD27 (GEN1053/BNT313) and HexaBody‐OX40 (GEN1055/BNT315). Unlike other HexaBodies, the Fc domain of these T‐cell‐targeting antibodies was further modified to silence Fc‐mediated effector functions, aiming to prevent T‐cell depletion. In recently presented data HexaBody‐CD27 exhibited dose‐dependent CD27 agonist activity in reporter assays, independent of functionally enhancing with FcγRs or with C1q, enhanced activation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion of TCR‐stimulated human CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as antigen‐specific cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells. 104 HexaBody‐CD27 also enhanced expansion and IFN‐γ secretion of antigen‐specific CD8+ T cells in a human CD27 knock in mice model, without T‐cell depletion. In an additional study, combination of HexaBody‐CD27 with PD‐(L)1 blockade potentiated single‐agent activity leading to enhanced human T‐cell effector functions in vitro. 105 Based on these results, a first‐in‐human clinical trial has been initiated to evaluate HexaBody‐CD27 in patients with advanced solid tumors (NCT05435339). Similarly, HexaBody‐OX40 triggered OX40 agonist activity in T‐cell‐based reporter assays without relying on FcγR, The compound led to increased proliferation, activation, and cytokine release in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. The OX40 agonist effect in CD8+ T cells required the presence of CD4+ T cells. In a human OX40 knock‐in mouse model, HexaBody‐OX40 showed anti‐tumor effects, which were linked to peripheral T‐cell activation and proliferation, a higher percentage of tumor‐specific CD8+ T cells, and an increase in CD4+ T cells and Granzyme B+ cells within the tumor. 106

Together, these promising results highlight the potential of the HexaBody technology to enhance receptor clustering and strengthen transmembrane signaling in key co‐stimulatory pathways.

CONCLUDING REMARKS

In this review, we have outlined the rationale behind HexaBodies and summarized their preclinical and clinical applications as a unique Fc engineering technology. Despite the strategic discontinuation of the DuoHexaBody‐CD37 compound, HexaBodies remain promising for future developments and several HexaBodies are in preclinical development.

HexaBodies are inspired by the natural hexamerization of IgG antibodies, which generally distinguishes them from other protein‐, glyco‐, and Fc‐engineered antibodies. While many engineered antibodies undergo significant modifications to enhance binding to effector molecules with “always‐on” characteristics, HexaBodies typically retain a monomeric IgG1 structure unless further modified for specific purposes. This allows them to preserve regular Fc‐mediated functions, such as recruiting NK cells or neutrophils for ADCC and monocytes or macrophages for ADCP. Their conventional IgG1 configuration also offers significant advantages, including typical IgG1 pharmacokinetics, biopharmaceutical characteristics, and easier manufacturability.

To date, HexaBody technology has shown the ability to enhance CDC, but it can also be employed to induce (hyper) clustering of cell surface receptors (Figure 2) Since many TNF family receptors or other signaling pathways require receptor clustering at the cell surface, HexaBodies may find broad applications in the future. However, it is yet too early to predict the clinical success of HexaBodies, as ongoing trials are still in early stages. Future applications of the HexaBody technology could involve combining it with other Fc‐engineering strategies. One example is the recent development of a novel CD19 antibody that combines Fc affinity‐improving S239D/I332E mutations with the hexamer‐enhancing E345K mutation. This combination was shown to enhance ADCC, ADCP and CDC with isolated effector populations. 107 Additionally, building upon the HexaBody platform, the discovery of the mutation pair K439E and S440K in the Fc‐tail has led to the development of the HexElect platform. 108 While these mutations individually reduce hexamerization, together they promote antibody hetero‐hexamerization. This platform enables selective CDC only when both antibodies, each incorporating this mutation pair, are bound to the cell surface. As a result, HexElect technology enables a targeted CDC activation exclusively in the presence of co‐expressed antigens.

Schematic representations of alternative applications or strategies for HexaBody technology for antibodies preclinically evaluated. (A) To achieve potent CDC against CD37, HexaBody technology is combined with DuoBody technology. This involves introducing the F405L mutation in one CD37‐targeting HexaBody and the K409R mutation in another, facilitating heterodimerization of these two CD37‐targeting HexaBodies. The result is DuoHexaBody‐CD37, a bispecific antibody that enhances hexamerization by targeting two non‐overlapping epitopes on CD37, thereby maximizing CDC induction. (B) The application of the E430G HexaBody mutation to a mixture of two antibodies targeting death receptor 5 (DR5), a member of the TNF receptor superfamily (TNFRSF). Normally, DR5 clustering and activation of the apoptotic pathway are triggered by its natural ligand, TRAIL. Here, ligand‐independent receptor clustering is achieved through antibody hexamerization, which can directly activate the DR5‐mediated apoptotic pathway. (C) The E430G HexaBody mutation applied to antibodies targeting two additional TNFRSF members: CD27 and CD40, which are T‐cell costimulatory molecules. The resulting HexaBody‐CD27 and HexaBody‐CD40 antibodies induce agonistic activity and promote T‐cell activation independently of FcγR crosslinking, demonstrating their potential in enhancing immune responses.

In conclusion, the clinical application of HexaBody technology, either alone or in combination with complementary Fc‐engineering strategies, along with expanded preclinical and clinical experience with these innovative drugs, is expected to provide valuable insights in the near future. This progress could ultimately pave the way for the development of highly effective and safe antibodies with customized effector functions tailored to specific therapeutic needs.
