Comment on “Periodontitis and Oral Cancer” by Xia and Song
Authors: Efsun Somay, Sibel Bascil
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the recently published article by Xia and Song, which examines the spatiotemporal co-occurrence patterns between periodontitis and oral cancer using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data and advanced time-series and network-based analytical methods.1 The authors are to be commended for addressing an important question in global oral health by leveraging a large, internationally representative dataset and applying contemporary analytical approaches to explore long-term population-level trends. We would, however, like to offer several methodological and clinical clarifications regarding the interpretation and translational implications of the findings. These comments are intended to help contextualize the findings and their clinical interpretation within epidemiologic and clinical dental science frameworks.

First, it is imperative to underscore the inherent limitations of ecological inference, particularly when interpreting associations between diseases that are derived solely from population-aggregated data. The analyses in question rely exclusively on country-level estimates of periodontitis burden and oral cancer incidence, spanning an extensive observational period of over three decades.1 While such ecological methodologies are undoubtedly valuable for generating hypotheses and mapping global disease burdens, they inherently preclude inferences at the individual level. As a result, associations detected at the aggregate level should not be construed as evidence that individuals affected by periodontitis are at heightened risk of developing oral cancer, nor that periodontitis temporally precedes malignant transformation within individual patients.

The authors judiciously acknowledge the observational nature of their study; however, the implementation of vector autoregressive modeling and Granger causality testing could be misconstrued by some readers as suggesting biological or clinical causality. From an epidemiological perspective, temporal predictiveness at the population level does not establish an etiologic sequence, particularly when both conditions are shaped by shared upstream determinants such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, betel quid exposure, socioeconomic status, access to dental care, and diagnostic intensity.2,3 Furthermore, the prolonged study window from 1990 to 2021 encompasses marked changes in periodontal disease definitions, diagnostic criteria, surveillance methodologies, and the completeness of cancer registries across different regions. These evolving factors further confound efforts to attribute disease-to-disease relationships. Accordingly, the reported associations are best interpreted as reflecting parallel, co-evolving trends at the population level, rather than indicating a direct causal or premalignant relationship between periodontitis and oral cancer.

Second, it is pertinent to address the interpretation of effect sizes and population-attributable fractions as presented in the manuscript. Several of the reported relative risks are only marginally above unity, yet they are framed as having substantial population-level impact.1 Although such estimates may achieve statistical significance in large-scale ecological datasets, their clinical significance is likely limited, particularly when used to inform prevention strategies. Population-attributable fractions are highly sensitive to assumptions regarding exposure prevalence and model specification, which may lead to overestimation of public health importance when effect sizes are modest and derived from aggregated data.4 In the absence of a direct link between individual exposure and outcomes, these estimates are best regarded as general indicators of disease patterns rather than as guides for clinical action. From a clinical dentistry perspective, the current evidence does not support the use of periodontitis as a trigger for oral cancer screening, referral prioritization, or personalized prevention strategies. A clearer delineation between estimating population-level disease burden and stratifying patient-level risk would reduce the likelihood of inappropriate extrapolation of these findings to clinical settings.

Finally, we suggest that a cautious approach is essential when extrapolating the clinical and preventive implications of the findings. The manuscript highlights the potential value of integrated prevention strategies linking periodontal disease management with oral cancer control.1 Although interdisciplinary prevention represents a commendable objective, the current body of evidence does not substantiate the classification of periodontitis as a clinically significant precursor or predictive marker for oral cancer. Oral squamous cell carcinoma continues to be primarily attributable to well-established etiological factors, such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, betel quid chewing, and oncogenic viral infections.5, 6, 7 Periodontitis often co-occurs with these risk factors and may serve as an indicator of broader behavioral and socioeconomic determinants of oral health8,9; however, the available data do not support the existence of a direct, clinically actionable pathway linking periodontal inflammation to the pathogenesis of oral cancer.

From a clinical perspective, the primary contribution of the present study lies in its illumination of shared population-level determinants of oral diseases, rather than in the recharacterization of periodontitis as a premalignant entity. Maintaining this conceptual distinction is essential to prevent misinterpretation by clinicians and policymakers, particularly in resource-constrained settings where prioritizing oral cancer prevention strategies is paramount.

In summary, Xia and Song make a noteworthy contribution to global oral health epidemiology by systematically mapping long-term, population-level co-trends between periodontitis and oral cancer.1 Nevertheless, we believe that the interpretation of these findings warrants circumspection, particularly with respect to inferring causality, evaluating effect sizes, assessing statistical significance, and considering clinical applicability. Emphasizing the ecological and hypothesis-generating nature of the analyses will help ensure the results are appropriately contextualized within public health discourse, without unintentionally shaping clinical dental practice or policy recommendations beyond the current evidentiary base.
