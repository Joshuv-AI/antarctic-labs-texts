A Study on Quantification of the Effects of Heartfulness Meditation Intervention in Healthcare Professionals by Anchor-Based Method in Occupational Health Psychology
Authors: Deepak B. Sharma, Himanshu Sharma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:It’s more important to measure the subjective perception of change after undergoing any behavioral or psychological intervention. Global rating of change method—anchor item-based is a method by which the change can be quantified and “minimum subjective perceived effect” (MSPE) can be calculated.Aim:The current study was planned to quantify the effects of heartfulness meditation intervention in healthcare professionals by anchor-based method and compare the result with the pre-post-difference.Methods and Material:The current study was done using multimethod study design among the healthcare professionals. These were 1. Cross-sectional study design. 2. Quasi-experimental—Solomon four-nonequivalent control group study design for intervention.Results:Cohens’ d for perceived stress scale (PSS) in “Much less negative” category was -.96.Conclusion:The study highlights the use of anchor-based method to estimate the minimal subjective perceived difference, and it can also be compared with the groups pre-post-difference for academic purposes.

INTRODUCTION

Occupational health psychology (OHP) research helps us to understand how working conditions affect workers’ health. This knowledge should be used to design interventions to protect and improve workers’ health.[1] Seeing only the statistical significance for the pre-post-differences which are used to assess the intervention effect, may not be correct all the time particularly in behavioral and psychological studies. “Minimum subjective perceived effect” (MSPE) from the Likert scale scores can be effectively worked out through the use of an anchor item. There is a lack of clarity regarding the meaningfulness of the effect sizes calculated from the mean differences.[2] Global rating of change method—anchor item-based[3] is a method by which the change can be quantified and the minimum subjective difference can be calculated for different categories as having “Little change” and “Much more change” in terms of both positive and negative finding/scale.

Pictorial outlay of participants selections and intervention plan

This manuscript is based on 32 participants (intervention groups) from Group I and Group III.

Different healthcare professionals, viz. allopathic doctors, physiotherapists, nursing professionals and community health officers (CHOs), were included as study participants. Sampling was done as convenient sampling in two stages.

In Groups I and III, intervention was planned.

Intervention: Two heartfulness sessions of approximately half an hour each biweekly for 3 weeks, total six sessions were to be listened and followed which constituted of guided meditation and relaxation (20 minutes) and recording in journal.

Global rating of change method: Anchor item based for negative and positive scales[3]

Differences in perceived stress scale (PSS) scores[9] and Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS) scores[10] were seen in reference to two anchor items, one for measuring a negative trait and one for positive trait. Participant-centered anchor-based approach was used to estimate the important minimal subjective perceived difference where the participants themselves provided a response for subjective change. By using the global rating of change anchor item, individuals were categorized into those who perceived a “Little change” (Little more/Little less) and those who perceived “Much change” (Much more/Much less). Both the anchor items were on a 5-point Likert scale, and scoring was Much less positive (1) to Much more positive (5) for BRCS and Much less negative (5) to Much more negative (1) for PSS. The mean change in scores of the scales over two different measurements was used as an estimate of the minimal important difference who reported it for “Little more” or “Little less.” This was the minimum subjectively perceived difference. The construct of interest was measured two times, T1 and T2. At T2, a global rating of change question was asked to know the extent to which individuals subjectively felt an increase or decrease in the construct of interest since T1.

Percentage of maximum possible units (POMP)[311]. This was calculated as a measure of change in the scores of stress and resilient coping after the intervention.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The pre-post-differences as a test for the intervention effect may not be able to estimate the minimum subjectively perceived effect, particularly in behavioral and psychological studies. Possibilities of getting a wrong result may be more pronounced, if the intervention and post-readings are taken in a shorter duration of study. Rosenthal mentioned that effect sizes can be grouped as d family and the r family.[12] d family consists of standardized mean differences and the r family measures the strength of association.[13] Standardized measures of effect include measures such as r, Cohen’s d, and odds ratio.[1415] Effect size denotes the meaningful relationship between variables or the difference between groups and can be categorized into small as 0.2, medium as 0.5, and large as 0.8 or greater according to Cohen’s criteria. Values closer to 0 reflect a smaller effect size. Pearson’s r also specifies the direction.[16] Absolute effect size is the raw difference between the average outcomes of groups and lacks variability measures.[17] The minimum subjective perceived difference can be expressed as a raw score, as a standardized measure in terms of Cohen’s d and also as POMP measures. There is no universal minimal important difference.[18] Jaeschke et al.[19] defined minimal important difference as “the smallest change in an outcome that a patient would identify as important.” Reporting the effect size is one of the important results and is a good practice as it facilitates the interpretation in a better way as compared to the statistical significance only.[15] Confidence interval is always more meaningful in comparison to just reporting the statistical significance. Lee KD in the article has emphasized on using the effect size and confidence interval rather than reliance only on P value.[20] Factors such as treatment effect calculations and confidence intervals allow the clinicians to assess the application of research findings, in terms of magnitude and direction.[21] Recategorization of the participants by the anchor method helped in finding the minimum subjective perceived difference. The methods to detect minimum important difference (MID) are generally grouped into two categories, namely anchor-based and distribution-based approaches.[22] The anchor-based approaches use an external indicator, called an “anchor,” and differences can be determined either cross-sectionally or longitudinally.[23] Restructuring of scores of 32 participants was done based on the anchor-based method. For negative change (PSS), it was seen as “Much less negative”, “Little less negative” and “No change” and for positive change, the categories were “Much more positive”, “Little more positive” and “No change”. For PSS, the overall Cohens’ d was -0.39, which was significant and in “Much less negative” category Cohen’s d was -0.96 which was significant [Table 1]. For BRCS Cohen’s d was 0.26 in “Much more positive” and 0.04 in “Little more positive” categories. Overall Cohens’ d was 0.09. None of the results were significant [Table 2]. The difference between pre- and post-PSS scores for Group I was -2.5. The difference between pre- and post- PSS scores for Group III was -1.56. Cohen’s D was not significant in any of the groups. The POMP difference measure was -5.07 in the intervention group [Table 3]. The difference between pre- and post- BRCS scores for Group I was 0.18. The difference between pre- and post- BRCS scores for Group III was 0.31. Cohen’s D was not significant in any of the groups. The POMP difference measure was 1.56 in the intervention group [Table 4]. On redistribution, the effect size has changed and it is more pronounced for “Much more/less” than “little less/more,” which seems more logically sound and appealing to theory. The overall comparisons between these two measures were seen as the effectiveness of anchor-based method. One can identify ways to establish the interpretability of measures as anchor-based or distribution-based.[24] The error of putting a good effective intervention as noneffective just because of inadequate technique is Type III error.[25] In spite of a statistical significance of a healthcare intervention, it may be relatively insignificant to the health or quality of life of patients.[26] Statistical outcomes cannot be equated with clinical relevance. The MID may be used for cutoff of clinical relevance.[27] Wong H has stated that the minimum important difference should play no role in setting the sample size.[28] Ratings on the anchor item should be positively correlated with the change scores.[3] The anchor item ratings were positively correlated with BRCS change scores (r = .089, P = .630) and with PSS change scores (r = 0.313, P = .081), but these were not significant. The current MSPE size for perceived stress and non-significant effect for resilient coping suggested that both these constructs being behavioral attributes require a longer follow-up. Likert scale options need a substantial change to move from little to more effect. Subjectively the participants may respond to an overall feeling of good, bad or neutral after an intervention, but the difference in a 5-point Likert scale measured through the change in the options may require a substantial change for jumping to other higher or lower options.

Limitations

Studying the minimum subjectively perceived effect for “Resilient coping” and “Reduction in stress level” may require a follow-up based on multiple sessions and require multiple serial measurements.

CONCLUSIONS

Anchor-based method is a very useful method to estimate the minimum subjective perceived difference.
