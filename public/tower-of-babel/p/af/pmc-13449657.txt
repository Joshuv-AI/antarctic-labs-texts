Hair-related complications in a post-phalloplasty neourethra: A novel staged urethroplasty approach
Authors: Grace Maline, Hong-An Nguyen, Margaret Meagher, Jennifer Anger, Priya Lewis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 35-year-old transgender man with history of gender-affirming phalloplasty with radial forearm flap followed by inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) placement had recurrent urethral stones from hair growth. He presented with pain from his implant and voiding difficulty and was confirmed to have infected IPP. At the time of implant removal, a significant burden of stones and luminal hair were found and treated with laser lithotripsy. For definitive management of hair-related complications, he underwent a staged urethroplasty with a first stage Johanson technique followed by 6 months of electrolysis of hair follicles along urethral plate before second stage repair.Keywords: Phalloplasty, Urethroplasty, Neourethra, Urethral stone

Case report

Introduction

Urethral stones are a rare urologic phenomenon in the cisgender population, accounting for less than 1% of urinary stones and occurring almost exclusively in men.1 Presentations with urethral stones typically include infection, dysuria, and/or urinary retention.2 Foreign bodies (such as hair), urinary stasis, inflammation, and anatomical variations in the urinary tract may promote the formation of urethral stones.1 Primary urethral stones (those formed in situ) are most often the result of urinary stasis in the setting of urethral stricture or diverticulum or of prior hypospadias repair with skin that later becomes hair bearing. In transgender and other post-operative phalloplasty patients with a neourethra comprised of skin, regrowth of intraurethral hair is typical and a small amount of hair growth does not usually lead to stone disease. Pre-operative depilation decreases but may not fully eliminate neourethral hair burden.3 Although exact incidence of urethral stones after phalloplasty is unknown, stones represent a subset of the overall complication rate after phalloplasty, which ranges from 30 to 50% 4, 5, 6. For this reason, aggressive pre-operative hair removal (e.g. electrolysis) is necessary to reduce complications.7

Case presentation

In this case, we describe a 35-year-old transgender man (assigned female at birth) who underwent gender-affirming phalloplasty with a radial forearm free flap and placement of inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) seven and six years prior to presentation respectively. Prior to phalloplasty, he had not been referred for standard pre-operative electrolysis. Thus, two years after his initial phalloplasty, he presented with urinary retention and underwent laser lithotripsy with extraction of a urethral calculus from excess intra-urethral hair. He underwent repeat procedure two years later for the same presentation.

At most recent presentation, six years after his IPP placement, he presented with significant pain from the IPP. Examination revealed tenderness along the proximal shaft at the location of the cylinder, with exquisite tenderness of the pump, necessitating removal of all components of the device. Intra-operative cystoscopy revealed urethral stone and hair burden that expanded the entirety of the neourethra and nearly obliterated the urethral lumen (Fig. 1A–C). Approximately 5 cm of stone was removed with the laser, and urethral hair was removed with a grasper (Fig. 2), marking his third procedure for removal of urethral stones and hair. Culture of the IPP device grew Staphylococcus epidermidis, Propionibacterium avidum, and Cutibacterium namnetense.

Significant urethral hair and stone burden. Following removal of IPP, patient was found to have more neourethral hair and stone, requiring his third endoscopic removal of the depicted material.

During post-operative follow up, the decision was made to definitively address the excess urethral hair using a staged urethroplasty to open the neourethral plate to allow the patient to undergo electrolysis of hair follicles. The hair-bearing portion of the urethra was spatulated ventrally, followed by the creation of an inverted U-shaped scrotal flap at the scrotal junction to establish a perineal urethrostomy (first stage Johanson repair) (Fig. 3A–B). The lateral edges of the neourethra were sewn to the skin of the neophallus (Fig. 3C). Second stage urethroplasty with tubularization and closure of the neourethra was executed 13 months later following satisfactory electrolysis of neourethral plate (Fig. 4A). The patient is currently voiding well and planning another penile implant in the near future.

Discussion

Other case reports have demonstrated formation of urethral stones following phalloplasty. Viviano et al. describe a 35-year-old male presenting with urinary retention two years after phalloplasty for penile reconstruction due to traumatic amputation. His neourethra was similarly constructed from a hair-bearing donor site, which led to formation of a calculus secondary to a hairball nidus. The stone was removed endoscopically with holmium laser and no intervention for recurrent hair was described.8 Another case report by Pereira-Lourenço et al. describe a 22-year-old transgender male presenting two years after phalloplasty with a urethral stricture at the anastomosis between the pars fixa and the neourethra. Proximal to the stenosis was a urethral diverticulum with a significant urethral stone burden. The stone was treated endoscopically with holmium laser at the time of urethroplasty for diverticulum excision and stricture repair.9

Neourethral hair is a known sequela of hypospadias repair with skin-based flaps, including a scrotal skin flap. Usually, the repair is performed before the child undergoes puberty, resulting in a delayed presentation of urinary tract infections (UTI) due to intra-urethral hair. Kumar et al. describe a 66-year-old man presenting five decades after urethral reconstruction for hypospadias with a scrotal skin flap with dysuria and recurrent UTI for 10 years. Retrograde urethrogram revealed a distal urethral diverticulum consisting of 12-15 hair follicles and bulbar stricture. The patient underwent internal urethrotomy for bulbar stricture, creation of a diverticular neck, and epilation of hair follicles endoscopically using a holmium laser. Due to recurrence of symptoms, this was repeated 6 months later along with manual hair removal with grasping forceps. No further definitive hair removal treatment was described.10

Common methods for managing intraurethral hair growth include laser (CO2, holmium, neodymium:YAG, gallium-aluminum-arsenium diode), thioglycolate hair tricholysis, electrolysis, and grasper extraction.11 To our knowledge, we present the first case report describing open surgical revision involving two-stage urethroplasty for purposes of neourethral electrolysis for hair removal in the treatment of recurrent urethral stones. We have prior experience with this technique in a cisgender man with extensive urethral condyloma that failed endoscopic treatment. The urethra was opened in a first stage urethroplasty with perineal urethrostomy, treated and cured with topical podophyllin, then closed in a second stage repair approximately six months later.

Other options for extensive hair growth in a neophallus are possible, such as excision of the neophallus and skin grafting.12 However, this also requires staging and places the patient at a high risk of stricture and fistula. We believe that our technique will ultimately result in a better long-term outcome, as minimizing the disturbance of the original flap is ideal.12 Although the patient unfortunately had to sustain months of electrolysis with a “filleted” open ventral shaft and the need to sit to void, the patient had an untouched dorsal and lateral urethra, resulting in a normal anatomical appearance when standing (Fig. 4B).

Urethroplasty requires the use of hair-free skin—both naturally nonhair-bearing skin and skin rendered hairless after hair removal treatment.7 Permanent hair removal via pre-operative electrolysis is essential to reduce such urethral complications in phalloplasty.11 However, despite preventative measures, luminal hair growth continues to be a recognized complication of these procedures. The majority of men can be managed with periodic cystoscopic hair “plucking” and laser ablation of stones, though rarely more definitive treatment is needed. Patients who present with voiding difficulty and UTIs, such as in this case, should be evaluated for intraurethral hair growth and calculi. In this patient, we believe the infection within the urethra led to a secondary infection of the IPP. Based on this case, cystoscopy is now part of any evaluation of IPP pain or voiding difficulty in patients after phalloplasty.

Conclusion

This case represents a rare presentation of severe urethral stone burden in the setting of a hair-bearing urethral segment after phalloplasty with radial forearm free flap. We further describe a comparatively less invasive staged urethroplasty using interval electrolysis of the neourethral plate in contrast to total replacement with a skin graft. This intervention is expected to permanently resolve urethral stone burden for this patient going forward and prevent the need for future surgeries/interventions for urinary stones.
