Robot-assisted choledochal cysts resection in children across all ages: a larger cohort single-center experience with 318 Cases
Authors: Shuhao Zhang, Duote Cai, Yuebin Zhang, Yi Jin, Wenjuan Luo, Zongwei Huang, Qingjiang Chen, Zhigang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobotic-assisted surgery (RAS) is an emerging alternative to laparoscopic surgery (LAS) for the treatment of choledochal cysts. We aim to evaluate the safety and efficacy of robotic-assisted choledochal cyst resection (RACC) in children from neonates to 16-year-olds. In this retrospective study, 318 patients undergoing RAS between April 2020 and July 2025 were compared with 489 patients undergoing LAS between January 2015 and July 2025. The robotic cohort was stratified into six age-based subgroups: R1 (neonates), R2 (> 1 to ≤ 3 months), R3 (> 3 to ≤ 12 months), R4 (> 12 to ≤ 36 months), R5 (> 36 to ≤ 120 months), and R6 (> 120 to ≤ 192 months). The overall postoperative complication rate was significantly lower in the RAS group compared to the LAS group (p = 0.020), with significantly reduced rates of bile leakage and anastomotic stenosis (p = 0.032). The reoperation rate was also significantly lower in the RAS group (p = 0.042). Within the RAS group, the robotic installation time was significantly longer in the R1 group (p = 0.0328). Among patients older than 3 months, the total operative time was significantly longer in the R6 group (p = 0.0438), while the R3 group had a significantly shorter total operative time compared to other groups (p = 0.0018). Additionally, the robotic console time was significantly longer in the R6 group compared to other groups (p = 0.0248). RACC is safe and effective for children across all ages, demonstrating lower complication and reoperation rates compared to laparoscopic surgery. Robotic surgery involving neonates or older children with large cysts should be performed at experienced pediatric centers.Keywords: Robotic-assisted surgery, Choledochal cyst, Neonates, All ages

Introduction

Choledochal cyst (CC) refers to a primary dilatation of the biliary tract, characterized by single or multiple segmental expansions involving the intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic bile ducts. The incidence of CC is notably higher in Asian countries (approximately 1 in 1000) compared to Western populations (approximately 1/150,000 to 1/50,000), with a female-to-male ratio ranging from 3:1 to 4:1 [1, 2]. Laparoscopic-assisted choledochal cyst (LACC) resection has been the preferred surgical approach, while robotic-assisted surgery has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional laparoscopy, gradually gaining attention among pediatric surgeons. Woo et al. [3] and Meehan et al. [4] reported the first series of pediatric patients undergoing robotic-assisted choledochal cyst resection (RACC) in 2006 and 2007, respectively. Dawrant [5] was the first to report the application of robotic surgery in low-weight children with choledochal cysts. The da Vinci Xi Surgical System has slimmer robotic arms with more articulating joints that allow for closer port positioning and less external collision, which makes it better suited for pediatric applications [6], and improved flexible joints offering enhanced advantages in biliary dissection and biliary-enteric anastomosis. Consequently, RACC has gained growing preference among pediatric surgeons.

Since 2020, there has been a steady increase in international studies focusing on RACC [7, 8]. In 2022, Yi et al. [9] reported 10 cases of robotic surgery in children weighing less than 6 kg, including one patient weighing only 3.5 kg. Subsequent studies have even documented the use of RACC in neonates [10], with the youngest reported age and weight being 15 days and 2.6 kg, respectively. As the largest single-center study on RACC since 2006, the present research comprehensively evaluates the safety and efficacy of robotic surgery in children across all ages, using a control cohort of 489 LACCs. This study aims to contribute meaningful insights from the Chinese experience regarding the application of robotic surgery in the management of choledochal cysts.

Methods

Clinical characteristics and grouping

This study retrospectively reviewed the clinical data of 807 pediatric patients diagnosed with choledochal cysts who were admitted to the Department of General Surgery at Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2015 and July 2025. The cohort included 206 males and 601 females. The surgical approach was chosen voluntarily by the parents based on their family economic circumstances and educational background. According to the surgical approach, the patients were classified into two groups: the robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) group, which included 318 patients (92 males, 226 females) with a mean age of 46.89 ± 42.48 months, and the laparoscopic-assisted surgery (LAS) group, which included 489 patients (114 males, 375 females) with a mean age of 37.72 ± 38.50 months. All surgeries in both groups were performed by two experienced surgeons from the same surgical team. Additionally, the RAS group was further stratified into six age-based subgroups: R1 (neonates, n = 5), R2 (> 1 to ≤ 3 months, n = 35), R3 (> 3 to ≤ 12 months, n = 55), R4 (> 12 to ≤ 36 months, n = 77), R5 (> 36 to ≤ 120 months, n = 121), and R6 (> 120 to ≤ 192 months, n = 25). Patient demographics, surgical details, postoperative complications, and clinical outcomes were documented.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) The Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, was the hospital of initial presentation; (2) Preoperative Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography examination confirmed the diagnosis of choledochal cyst; (3) No history of abdominal surgery; and (4) absence of co-existing diseases.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) The Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, was not the hospital of initial presentation; (2) History of abdominal surgery; and (3) co-existing of other congenital diseases.

Docking positions and surgical procedure for choledochal cyst resection

RAS group: After anesthesia, patients were positioned in a reverse-Trendelenburg position. Pneumoperitoneum was established through an umbilical incision, followed by the placement of four trocars (Fig. 1A-C): an 8-mm umbilical port for the camera (port I), two 8-mm working ports (ports II and III), and a 5-mm assistant port (port IV). Then, the ligament of Treitz was identified, and a Roux-en-Y limb was established extracorporeally. The limb was then returned to the abdominal cavity and delivered tension-free to the porta hepatis via laparoscopy. The gallbladder and round ligament of the liver were suspended via the abdominal wall to expose operating area. The gallbladder was dissected and the cyst was meticulously dissected distally to its junction with the pancreatic duct. The distal end of the cyst was clipped with a Hem-o-lock clip. Proximal dissection was continued to approximately 1 cm below the confluence of the right and left hepatic ducts. Finally, a continuous full-thickness end-to-side hepaticojejunostomy was performed between the hepatic duct and the Roux limb using 5−0 absorbable suture. In neonatal patients, we preferred 6−0 absorbable sutures.

LAS group: After anesthesia, patients were similarly placed in the reverse-Trendelenburg position. Pneumoperitoneum was established via the umbilicus, and a 5-mm trocar was inserted as the main laparoscopic port. Three additional 5-mm trocars were placed: one in the right mid-abdomen at the level of the umbilicus on the midclavicular line, one in the right upper quadrant at the surface projection of the gallbladder, and one in the left upper quadrant approximately 2 cm below the costal margin along the midclavicular line. The subsequent surgical steps were identical to those in the RAS group.

Postoperative management and follow-up

Postoperatively, the patient underwent a five-day fasting period. Physiological fluid requirements were maintained intravenously, and a course of antibiotics was administered. Somatostatin was administered for curative treatment when patients developed elevated amylase levels during their postoperative hospitalization. The gastric tube was removed 3–5 days postoperatively, then water and liquid diet were added. After one day of liquid diet, a semi-liquid diet was initiated. Blood routine tests, biochemical function tests and ultrasound were then rechecked. The abdominal drainage tube was removed on postoperative days 7–9, following the confirmation of two criteria: absence of a significant increase in output after feeding and a daily drainage volume of less than 20 mL.

All patients were followed up at the outpatient clinic at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years after surgery. During follow-up, blood routine tests, biochemical function tests, and hepatopancreatic B ultrasounds were performed. The frequency of follow-up visits was adjusted based on the reexamination results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 6. Quantitative data were expressed as frequency (n). All data passed the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for normality and were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson χ2 test, while quantitative data were analyzed using parametric T-tests. A two-tailed test was adopted, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes in RAS and LAS groups

A total of 807 patients with CCs were included in this study, with 318 undergoing RAS and 489 treated via LAS. The LAS group consisted of 114 males and 375 females, while the RAS group included 92 males and 226 females. The mean age was significantly higher in the RAS group (46.89 ± 42.48 months) compared to the LAS group (37.72 ± 38.50 months; p = 0.0016). Preoperative imaging showed a significantly larger cyst diameter in the LAS group (36.43 ± 16.90 mm) versus the RAS group (29.69 ± 20.57 mm; p = 0.008). No significant difference was observed in total operative time between the two groups (LAS: 161.00 ± 29.03 min; RAS: 167.86 ± 50.89 min; p = 0.3657). However, the postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the RAS group (8.9 ± 2.1 days) compared to the LAS group (11.0 ± 2.4 days; p = 0.004). The surgical cost was significantly higher in the RAS group (74,799.45 ± 7,952.88 RMB) than in the LAS group (34,618.11 ± 15,131.30 RMB; p < 0.0001). The postoperative follow-up duration was significantly higher in the LAS group (1920.9306 ± 979.11 days) than in the RAS group (931.59 ± 489.06 days; p < 0.0001) (Table 1).

Discussion

Choledochal cyst (CC) is a severe congenital malformation, with approximately 80% of cases diagnosed before the age of 10 years [11]. Advancements in ultrasound technology have significantly increased the rates of both prenatal and early postnatal diagnosis of CCs [12–14]. Consequently, the age at diagnosis has continued to decrease, leading to a corresponding decline in the typical age for surgical intervention. Currently, minimally invasive surgery has largely replaced open surgery as the standard treatment for CCs. In well-equipped pediatric centers, RACC has gained widespread acceptance among pediatric surgeons, with numerous institutions confirming its safety and efficacy [15–17]. Since 2020, our center has performed over 3,400 robotic procedures, including more than 300 cases of CC, encompassing children across all ages-including neonates. Leveraging this extensive experience, we present a comprehensive evaluation of the safety and efficacy of robotic surgery for CCs across the pediatric age spectrum.

Comparative analysis of laparoscopic surgery and robotic surgery

Pediatric robotic surgery was introduced in 2000 [18] and has since been gradually applied to various pediatric surgical subspecialties. From the first robotic-assisted pyeloplasty in 2002 [19] to 226 cases of robotic-assisted pyeloplasty in a single center by 2025 [20], from the initial RACC in 2006 [3] to 50 cases of RACC in a single center by 2025 [17], and from 28 robotic-assisted patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) ligations in 2001 [21] to 425 robotic-assisted PDA ligations in a single center by 2025 [22], robotic surgery has experienced rapid development and is increasingly regarded as an ideal alternative to laparoscopic surgery. Additionally, robotic surgery has a shorter learning curve for complex reconstructive procedures [23], with RACC serving as a representative example. Some centers have even reported successful use of RACC in neonates.

Laparoscopic surgery has inherent limitations, such as limited magnification and unstable hand-held instruments, and an unsatisfactory surgical visual field, which may compromise the reliability of biliary-enteric anastomosis. Additionally, dissection of the biliary system carries risks of iatrogenic injury, including damage to accessory hepatic ducts or improper management of the distal cyst. In contrast, the robotic system provides a 3D high-definition magnified view, 7 degrees of freedom, and tremor filtration, enabling superior visualization, enhanced dexterity, and reduced iatrogenic injury during biliary dissection and anastomosis in deep surgical fields. Comparative analysis highlights the following advantages of robotic surgery: (1) Reduced iatrogenic injury: Robotic systems facilitate “precision minimally invasive manipulation” under the framework of minimally invasive surgery, leading to significantly lower overall complication and reoperation rates. Even in cases of common hepatic duct gangrene with severe inflammation, primary radical resection remains feasible (Fig. 2B); (2) More reliable distal cyst management: Flexible robotic arms and superior optics allow precise dissection and ligation of the distal cyst (Fig. 2D). In our previous surgeries, one laparoscopic case of recurrent pancreatitis was directly attributed to incomplete distal cyst excision (Fig. 2E); (3) Enhanced performance in biliary-enteric anastomosis with common hepatic duct diameter < 5 mm: The RAS group demonstrated significantly lower rates of bile leakage and anastomotic stenosis, attributable to the robotic system’s flexible arms and magnified vision. Surgeons can perform biliary-enteric anastomosis with high confidence, even in neonates and young infants with a cyst diameter < 5 mm (Fig. 2F-I); (4) Expedited postoperative recovery: Earlier abdominal drainage removal as well as the lower complication rate in the RAS group, contributed to shorter hospital stays.

Application of robotic surgery in pediatric patients across all ages

The recommended age for radical surgery in children with CCs varies [24, 25], and in young children, a major challenge in applying robotic surgery is the mismatch between limited intra-abdominal space and the relatively large size of robotic instruments. Based on our experience, robotic surgery is more suitable for infants aged 3–12 months. The shorter robotic installation time is attributed to the sufficient intra-abdominal space and body surface area. Additionally, the shorter total surgical time and robotic console time are due to the absence of pericystic adhesions caused by recurrent inflammation in young infants [26], allowing the surgeon to perform cyst dissection and biliary-enteric anastomosis more efficiently. In contrast, older children typically have a history of recurrent abdominal pain and are misdiagnosed as gastroenteritis. Recurrent inflammation of the cyst leads to vascular proliferation, and coupled with the large size of the cyst (Fig. 2C), surgeons need to devote a substantial amount of time achieving dissection and hemostasis, which results in significantly prolonged operative times. Therefore, it is recommended that surgeons, especially novices, should avoid selecting older children with large cysts.

Certainly, based on the experience of our center, by optimizing and standardizing preoperative preparation, robotic surgery remains feasible even under 3 months of age. Key technical tips include: (1) Trocar spacing: A 3-cm spacing between Trocars is feasible in neonates and does not impede instrument mobility. Positioning Trocars closer to the midaxillary line on the right side can further increase inter-arm distance; (2) Trocar placement: Trocars should be placed away from the gallbladder’s surface projection while avoiding overly low insertion that risks injury to iliac blood vessels and ilium. The classical triangular port arrangement (the line connecting the Trocars of the two working arms is perpendicular to the line connecting the umbilicus and the surgical site, Fig. 1A) is not strictly followed in this age group; (3) Trocar fixation: To prevent dislodgment in thin-walled abdomens of young infants, a rubber ring around the Trocar can be sutured to the skin. Traction on the abdominal wall also helps augment intraoperative space (Fig. 1D-E).

Regarding complications, no significant differences were observed across age groups within the RAS cohort. Bile leakage and anastomotic stenosis occurred in children across all ages, indicating that postoperative complication rates were not influenced by extreme youth or older age. However, the 8-mm diameter of the robotic Trocar is relatively large for young infants. Thus, a meticulous, multi-layered closure of the abdominal wall (incorporating the peritoneal, muscular, and skin layers) cannot be overemphasized to mitigate the risk of incisional herniation.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, variations in surgical techniques—such as suturing methods, anatomical approaches, and docking positions—may limit the generalizability of our findings to other pediatric centers. Second, the minimum follow-up duration for patients undergoing LAS or RAS was 242 days and 235 days, respectively. Recent study indicates that the median time to the onset of postoperative anastomotic stricture following choledochal cyst resection is 233.5 days [27]. Although the minimum follow-up duration in our study has exceeded this number, we still hold the opinion that it is insufficient to accurately assess the incidence of long-term complications such as anastomotic stricture. In future studies, we will further extend the postoperative follow-up duration and conduct multi-center research in collaboration with other pediatric centers.

In summary, robotic-assisted surgery is a safe and effective treatment for CCs in children across all ages. It demonstrates lower overall complication and reoperation rates compared to laparoscopic surgery, and patients aged 3–12 months are particularly well-suited for RACC. Neonatal robotic surgery demands higher proficiency in Trocar placement and operative technique, while older children with large cysts present challenges such as a broader dissection area and a heightened risk of hemorrhage due to vascular proliferation. Surgeons are advised to perform RACC in these specific groups only after attaining sufficient robotic experience, and it also requires the support from a skilled multidisciplinary team, including anesthesiologists, surgical assistants, and specialized nurses.
