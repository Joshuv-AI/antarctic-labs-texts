Implementation determinants, strategies and outcomes for scale-up of hospital-at-home: a systematic review
Authors: Stephanie Q Ko, Rachel H Choe, Yan Ling Lai, Nick Sevdalis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-at-Home (HaH) has been shown to be clinically and cost effective, but many programs struggle to scale. We conducted a systematic review of HaH studies reporting implementation contexts, strategies and outcomes with the primary aim to synthesise the implementation evidence on HaH. Our secondary aim was to develop a framework of HaH implementation outcome indicators for future studies to apply.MethodsWe searched the literature PubMed, Embase and Scopus, for publications from 2012 to 2022. We included studies related to HaH that included at least one implementation context and at least one implementation outcome variable, excluding clinical outcomes measures. At least two reviewers independently selected studies, abstracted data and assessed quality. We coded the implementation determinants to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR); implementation strategies to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC); and implementation outcomes to the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) frameworks.ResultsWe included 27 studies in the final review, which reported 24 CFIR contextual factors, 25 ERIC strategies, and all domains of the RE-AIM evaluation framework. The most commonly reported implementation determinant, identified in six studies, was information technology infrastructure, which included streamlining documentation processes and improving access to patient records. Five studies described the following implementation factors: (i) work infrastructure, focusing on organising manpower effectively; (ii) capability of innovation deliverers, referring to staff posessing the necessary skills to perform their roles; and (iii) local conditions, such as the geographical challenges associated with HaH. The most frequently referenced implementation strategies were conducting ongoing training (nine studies), creating new clinical teams (eight studies), and promoting adaptability (seven studies). All assessed implementation outcomes were aligned to the RE-AIM framework, with volume of admissions, patient-reported experiences or outcome measures, and home visit frequency being most commonly reported (four studies each). The majority of implementation measures lacked a common denominator, and use of validated tools across studies was lacking.ConclusionsWe identified a list of implementation determinants, strategies and outcomes that can be used to inform scale-up of HaH, and have developed a framework for consistent reporting of HaH implementation studies grounded onto RE-AIM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-01033-7.Keywords: Implementation science, Hospital-at-home, Homecare, Models of care, Scaling up, Systematic review

Background

Hospital-at-home (HaH) is a home-based inpatient substitutive care model, which has been expanding worldwide to augment hospital bed capacity as populations age [1]. It features multi-disciplinary healthcare teams that care for patients in their homes, who would otherwise have required a hospital bed. Several randomised controlled trials and systematic reviews [2, 3] to-date have shown that for suitable acutely-ill patients who require hospital-level treatment, HaH is associated with similar or better outcomes than an in-hospital stay, at generally lower cost and improved patient experience [4–7]. Consequently, various health systems worldwide, including United Kingdom (UK) [8], United States of America (USA) [9] and Asia [10] have invested heavily in developing HaH programs with the intention to attenuate future hospitalisation demand and the need to build costly new hospitals.

Despite these major national financial levers, the majority of existing HaH programs remain small. A recent account of HaH in the USA showed an average of 89 beds across over 300 HaH programs spanning 37 states [11]. While clinical and cost-effectiveness evidence exists, a critical gap remains in understanding how to scale these services optimally so that the impact demonstrated in research translates to real-world improvements in bed capacity and patient outcomes. A 2024 review by Cochrane [12] provided a narrative synthesis of perspectives on HaH implementation; however, it is still unclear which implementation strategies are most effective especially those needed to scale and sustain services beyond a pilot phase.

We conducted a systematic review of the global evidence base on HaH implementation at scale. Our primary aim was to extract and synthesise evidence on the context, strategies (if reported) and outcomes reported across studies, and map these to established implementation theories to identify what works and in which healthcare settings. Our secondary aim was to develop a framework for the consistent reporting of HaH-related outcomes, to enable cumulative implementation evidence across the emerging evaluative studies of HaH care models globally.

Methods

Implementation frameworks

For the primary aim of the review, we applied well-established implementation science frameworks to guide both the selection and analysis of studies. These frameworks were selected because together they address different dimensions of implementation: the phase of implementation activity, the contextual factors shaping it, the strategies used to support it, and the outcomes achieved. Each is briefly described below.

First, we used the EPIS (Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment) framework [13] to situate studies within the implementation lifecycle. EPIS is a phased framework that characterises implementation as a process unfolding across four sequential stages, from initial exploration of an innovation through to its sustained integration into routine practice. We applied EPIS to determine which phases of implementation the included studies addressed, enabling us to identify where the evidence base is concentrated and where gaps remain.

Second, we used the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [14] to examine the context in which HaH programs are implemented. CFIR organises determinants of implementation across five domains – the innovation itself, the outer setting, the inner setting, the individuals involved, and the implementation process – and has been widely applied to identify barriers and facilitators to healthcare implementation across diverse settings. We used CFIR to map factors reported across studies, including resources, leadership engagement, and patient-level considerations, that acted as barriers or facilitators to implementation and scale-up.

Third, for studies which reported on implementation strategies, we applied the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [15] framework to systematically categorize all intentionally applied strategies reported by programs to support implementation. ERIC provides a taxonomy of 73 discrete, operationally defined implementation strategies organised into nine conceptual clusters, developed through expert consensus to enable precise specification and comparison of implementation strategies across studies. This allowed us to characterise not only whether strategies were reported, but what types of strategies were employed.

Finally, to assess how well these programs were implemented, we used the RE-AIM framework [16]. RE-AIM evaluates five dimensions: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation fidelity, and Maintenance. It was developed specifically to address the gap between intervention efficacy and real-world scalability. We applied RE-AIM to provide a structured assessment of implementation success across the included studies.

These frameworks were used consistently across study selection and data extraction.

For the secondary aim of the review, drawing on the implementation knowledge base curated through this review, we developed an integrative outcome framework (HAH RE-AIM) to support the consistent evaluation of HaH implementation. The framework was informed by domains and definitions extracted from the included studies, and refined to enable comparability across HaH programs (e.g. adding a denominator). Each measure within the framework was assigned a standard definition, providing a universal reference that can be applied across diverse HaH programs.

This study was funded by PHRG Grant PHRGOC23Jan-0002. The review protocol was registered onto the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/mbxpy/).

Data sources and searches

A search was performed on December 14, 2022 using PubMed, Embase and Scopus. Search terms included concepts of hospital at home and implementation outcomes (including concepts of scale, sustainability, maintenance, reach, acceptability, appropriateness, penetration, and related terminology). We intentionally did not restrict studies by implementation context or strategies in the initial search, to broaden the inclusion of studies that were eventually analysed. These were agreed by all authors a priori and are available in eAppendix Table 1. Test searches were carried out and results were refined by S.K. before finalizing the search strategy. The search was restricted to studies published from 2012 to 2022. The search was restricted to studies published within the last ten years to ensure the relevance and currency of the evidence base. HaH services have evolved considerably over this period, with advances in medical technology, remote monitoring and care delivery models shaping how these services are designed and implemented. Older studies may not accurately reflect current clinical practices, workforce configurations or patient expectations, and therefore may be less applicable to current implementation efforts. While a formal systematic grey literature search was not performed, grey literature inclusion was limited to targeted identification of relevant reports and programme descriptions known to the study team through domain expertise and familiarity with the HaH landscape. One additional study identified through this process met inclusion criteria.

Study selection

The title and abstract of each study were each reviewed for inclusion by two independent reviewers (S.K., R.C., L.Y.L, L.S.Y. or A.P.) for consensus. Discordant opinions were reviewed by a third reviewer (S.K.). Duplicate studies were removed during the initial stage of reviewing titles and abstracts. Then, a full text review was conducted by two independent reviewers (S.K., R.C., or L.Y.L), who were familiar with HaH, for inclusion. If the reviewers did not agree on a study, a fourth independent reviewer (N.S.) made a final decision.

Studies needed to be about HaH. In this study, we followed the definition of HaH as presented in the World Hospital at Home Congress 2023, as an acute clinical service that takes staff, equipment, technologies, medication and skills usually provided in hospitals and delivers that hospital care to selected people in their homes or in nursing homes. It substitutes for acute inpatient hospital care. Included HaH programs in this study had to fit this definition, interpreted as (i) defined inclusion/exclusion criteria, (ii) vitals monitoring, (iii) daily medical team reviews.

Studies also had to include at least one implementation context parameter as per CFIR (such as changes in funding, organizational characteristics or processes), and one implementation outcome as per RE-AIM, excluding studies solely reporting clinical outcome measures. Implementation strategies were not mandated as an inclusion criterion, as the primary objective was to examine links between implementation context and outcomes. Nonetheless, many included studies described strategies used to address contextual barriers and facilitators, which were subsequently mapped to implementation science frameworks during data synthesis. We chose these frameworks as they were deemed to be broad enough to capture the respective constructs (i.e. contextual determinants and outcomes, for CFIR and RE-AIM, respectively), enabling systematic cross-study comparison and identification of patterns in factors associated with successful HaH implementation at scale.

Included studies could be observational or interventional of any design (i.e., quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods), with primary data published in the English language. Trial protocols, systematic or scoping reviews, editorials and abstracts were excluded as they did not report primary data, but reference lists were reviewed to look for potential relevant studies. Studies on programs specifically targeted at COVID-19, post-hospital discharge programs or individual components of home hospitalization (e.g., remote patient monitoring), were excluded.

Data extraction

From the selected studies, authors extracted study type, country, implementation stage (as per EPIS), diagnoses of patients managed under the respective HaH programs, and participant characteristics. For HaH programs, early supported discharge (ESD) models referred to acceptance of patients referred after a period of ward hospitalisation; admission avoidance (AA) models referred to acceptance of patients referred without prior ward hospitalisation, e.g. from the emergency department or primary care. Programs could do either or both models.

We coded implementation context, as per CFIR domains and subdomains and implementation outcomes as per the RE-AIM framework. Following this, authors examined whether any implementation strategies had been applied in response to identified contextual factors – that is, whether a barrier or facilitator to scale had promted a specific strategy that resulted in a measurable outcome, and coded these to the ERIC framework. For example, in one study [27] a lack of access to medical records and hospital protocols in the community setting was identified as a barrier (context: information technology infrastructure), which promoted information sharing via a shared computer medical file (strategy: change record systems) and a local helpline for General Practitioners (strategy: provide local technical assistance), resulting in increased acceptability (outcome). A detailed list of implementation context, strategies and outcome contextualized to HaH are provided in the supporting tables (eTable 2–4).

Risk of bias

Two reviewers (S.K., R.C.) independently assessed the risk of bias (ROB) of included studies using the mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT), version 2018 [17]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. The MMAT was selected as it is designed to appraise multiple study designs within a single review, including qualitative and mixed methods studies – reflecting the heterogenous nature of the HaH implementation literature. All included studies were first assessed against two screening questions, confirming the presence of clear research questions and that the collected data were sufficient to address them. Studies were then appraised according to their design category using the corresponding five methodological quality criteria, with each criterion scored as ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Can’t tell’. A final quality score was calculated as the proportion of criteria rated ‘Yes’, (maximum score of five) providing an indicative measure of methodological quality for each study (Table 1). Consistent with the MMAT guidance, these scores were not used as a basis for exclusion; rather, they served as a contextual reference to inform the interpretation of findings.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis was conducted through a structured coding process using Microsoft Excel. Each included study was assigned a study identification (ID) number and the context of the free text in the introduction, methods and results, coded against the relevant sub-domains of each framework – CFIR, ERIC and RE-AIM. From each sub-domain, relevant exerpts from studies were extracted and recorded, enabling direct traceability between the source data and framework mapping. Once coding was complete, the frequency of studies mapped to each subdomain was calculated to identify which implementation context, strategies and outcomes were most reported across the studies.

Deviation from study protocol

In our original protocol (https://osf.io/mbxpy/), we planned to include only scaling studies – defined as increase in number of beds or number of HaH programs within a hospital or healthcare group from beginning to the end of the study period. However, due to paucity of such studies (only six) and inconsistent reporting of the unit of scale up, we pragmatically decided to broaden our inclusion to a wider range of HaH implementation studies. We also originally stated a limited set of outcome and contextual factors in the protocol; this was broadened to the entire set of RE-AIM- and CFIR-covered factors to capture the richness of the data we found in the literature.

Results

The search yielded 7523 records. Of these, 2510 duplicate records were removed before screening and 4899 excluded following title and abstract screening. Forty-one reports were excluded as only abstracts were available. The remaining 73 records were considered in full text. Of these, 47 were excluded. Studies were excluded for the following reasons: no reported HaH evidence, absence of contextual factors or primary data, no implementation outcomes, and a specific focus on COVID-19 or a single component of home hospitalization. One article was identified from searching grey literature, and was included in the review. A total of 27 articles were included in the final review. The PRISMA flow diagram is present in eAppendix Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Characteristics of the studies are summarized in Table 1. The studies included various clinical conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, congestive cardiac failure, infective endocarditis, haematology and oncology conditions, neurosurgery, and palliative care across both adult and paediatric populations.

These studies encompassed populations from North America (USA, Canada), Europe (France, UK, Scotland, Sweden, Netherlands, Finland, Norway, Spain), and the Asia-Pacific region (Australia, Singapore). Most (n = 16) studies reported on HaH programs that included both AA and ESD models, eight included ESD only [27, 28, 31, 33, 37, 41, 47, 51], one AA only [46], and two not specified [16, 29].

The majority (n = 19) of the included studies [21, 29–46] were in the implementation phase, five studies [47–51] in the sustainment phase, and three studies [26–28] in the exploration and preparation phases according to the EPIS framework (Table 1). Of the included studies, six studies [34, 36, 39, 45, 49, 50] reported an increase in volume or scale of the studied HaH program. Of the 27 included studies, the majority employed quantitative descriptive study designs (n = 15), followed by qualitative studies (n = 10), one mixed methods study combining interviews and document analysis, and one randomized controlled trial.

Of the 27 studies, 25 had a clear research question and data sufficient to address it (screening questions). In two studies [21, 41] it was not clear whether the data were sufficient to answer the research question. The medial ROB score for all studies was four out of five (eAppendix Table 2). We used the ROB score as a guidance during interpretation of findings, acknowledging that studies with lower scores may carry greater uncertainty in their reported implementation outcomes.

Implementation context

Implementation determinants were extracted, identified as barriers and/or facilitators and coded onto the CFIR framework (Table 2 and eAppendix Table 3). Of 48 possible CFIR implementation determinants, 24 determinants featured in the included studies, with at least one featuring in each of the five CFIR domains. The most commonly featured implementation determinant was Inner setting, followed by Outer setting, then Characteristics of individuals (33, 20 and 14 times across included studies, respectively).

Three determinants were each reported in five studies: effective organisation of manpower and job roles to increase productivity (inner setting – work infrastructure) [28, 37, 39, 42, 43]; ensuring staff delivering care have capabilities to fulfil the role (characteristics of individuals – capability of innovation deliverers) [29, 31, 33, 41, 43]; and geographical challenges of HaH (outer setting – local conditions) [26, 35, 41, 42, 49].

The most commonly cited strategy clusters were: change infrastructure, support clinicians, and use evaluative and iterative strategies.

Implementation outcomes were categorized according to the RE-AIM framework (Table 4 and eAppendix Table 5). Discussion

In this systematic review, established implementation frameworks were used to map implementation determinants, strategies and outcomes involved in the implementation and scale-up of HaH worldwide. For the first time to our knowledge, we mapped available knowledge, identified gaps with current HaH implementation evidence and proposed a synthesised framework for HaH implementation evaluation indicators.

Synthesis of HaH implementation evidence: what we know globally

As HaH programs by design reflect the health system in which they operate, cross-program comparison has historically been constrained by heterogeneity in clinical scope, funding models and reporting practices. A key contribution of this review is demonstrating that systematic application of implementation science frameworks can help to reveal coherent and generalizable patterns. By applying CFIR, ERIC and RE-AIM to a diverse seires of 27 studies spanning general medicine, cancer care, end-of-life across Europe, North America and Asia Pacific, we show that implementation determinants, strategies and outcomes converge and provide a common language in ways that can support cross-context learning.

The implementation determinants identified across CFIR domains are largely consistent with the other CFIR-based healthcare studies [18–20] and qualitative evidence synthesis by Wallis and colleagues [12], who similarly identified system factors including policy and financing structures, and resources and skills, as central to successful implementation. Our review extends their narrative synthesis in two important ways. First, mapping determinants systematically to CFIR constructs, we provide a replicable checklist against which implementors in new contexts can audit their local conditions before attempting scale-up. For example, local attitudes were defined to include both patient and referring teams’ perceptions of HaH safety or effectiveness; and physical infrastructure was important to create a conducive permanent workspace for HaH teams despite the care being out of the hospital. In areas where health system implementors perceive there may be local differences in health service context, we recommend further evaluation of the domains this review identified as impacting implementation to define them better and identify strategies that best address them. Second, we identify information technology infrastructure as the single most frequently reported barrier (six studies), a finding that sits within CFIR’s inner setting domain. The recurrence of this barrier across geographically and clinically diverse settings suggests it represents a structural constraint on HaH scaling rather than a context-specific challenge, and points to IT interoperability as a priority target for implementation strategy development. Our review also identifies the key strategy clusters – training, new clinical team creation, and adaptability – as the most commonly deployed responses, suggesting generalisability of these strategy-barrier pairings beyond any single health system.

The ERIC framework helped identify generalisable scale-up strategies, ranging from internal planning of processes, training and organization, to advocacy for policy and financing framework changes. The dominance of strategies in the ‘change infrastructure’ and ‘support clinicians’ clusters aligns with the Cochrane synthesis finding that workforce configuration and organisational support are foundational enablers [12]. Crucially, this review identified studies – Mittaine-Marzac [50] and Hernández (2018) – that report what can be understood as partial implementation cycles: a contextual determinant prompts a strategy, which in turn produces a measurable outcome. Mittaine-Marzac [50] demonstrated that COVID-19 (implementation determinant: critical incidents) drove the development of a combination of strategies like staged scale-up, new clinical teams being established, and changes to record systems, which in turn led to an increase in volume of HaH admissions by 30% over a five-week period. Similarly, Hernández [36] reported an increase in capacity from 12 virtual beds in 2006 to 36 in 2016, facilitated by reimbursement changes and the use of evaluative and iterative strategies. These cases provide the closest available HaH evidence of associative links between CFIR-identified barriers, ERIC-mapped strategies, and RE-AIM outcomes. They represent exactly the kind of theory-informed, prospective evaluation that the field now needs to pursue at scale, moving toward designs capable of establishing causal rather than merely associative relationships between strategy selection and implementation success, as called for by Powell [25] in their research agenda for implementation strategy evaluation.

Weaknesses and gaps in HaH implementation evidence

Most strategies in the included studies were evaluated informally through clinician interviews or operational observation rather than formally tested, and only one study (Levine et al. 2022 [48]) measured strategy effectiveness using a validated tool. This pattern is not unique to HaH. Powell 25] have argued that implementation science as a field lacks prospective, controlled evaluations of individual implementation strategies, and that this gap constrains the development of an evidence-based menu of scale-up interventions applicable across contexts. Our review confirms that HaH is no exception: strategies are routinely selected on the basis of local logic and expert judgement rather than comparative evidence. The CFIR-ERIC matching tool offers a structured approach to strategy selection grounded in expert consensus, but its recommendations require empirical validation in HaH contexts – a gap this review’s framework is designed to help close by providing a structured starting-point for strategy specification and outcome measurement.

The weakest evidence in the included studies concerned implementation outcomes reporting. We applied RE-AIM to synthesise outcomes, as the most commonly used by clinical program implementors and evaluators [22, 23]. However, outcomes were ambiguously defined and conceived (the review team had to discuss the categorization extensively); often lacked denominators [24] (resulting in difficulty in comparisons within and between programs), and used non-standardised/validated survey tools (e.g. caregiver experience, referring physician satisfaction, or HaH staff satisfaction. This limits comparability and growth of a global evidence base. This echoes findings from D’Lima and colleagues (2022 [24]), whose systematic review of RE-AIM application across health research identified the absence of denominators and heterogeneity in outcome operationalisation as the primary barriers to cumulative implementation evidence. Kwan and colleagues (2019 [22]) further noted that RE-AIM is frequently applied post hoc and incompletely, particularly in the Adoption and Maintenance dimensions, which are the most directly relevant to scale-up decisions. Our review confirms this pattern specifically in HaH: adoption indicators (proportion of hospital units with HaH access; proportion of physicians referring) and maintenance indicators (staff satisfaction; programme longevity) were the least consistently reported dimensions across included studies. This gap is consequential: without standardised adoption and maintenance data, health system managers and policymakers face a lack of evidence at the scale-up phase.

Lastly, despite studies such as those by Mittaine-Marzac [50] and Hernández [36], most studies did not hypothesise links between challenges, strategies, and outcomes. We suspect that this is a weakness both of study design as well as study reporting, and limited use of implementation science methods in HaH evaluation as a health service. For example, although outer setting determinants were frequently identified, we cannot deduce which strategies addressed them most effectively. Likewise, staff satisfaction was a commonly reported implementation outcome (maintenance), but rarely linked to specific strategies. Future studies should clearly hypothesise and test these links to generate generalisable knowledge [25].

A global HaH implementation outcomes framework

To address the measurement gap identified above, we developed an integrative outcome framework – the HaH RE-AIM (Table 5) – grounded onto the RE-AIM framework and informed by the full implementation knowledge base curated through this review. RE-AIM was selected as the organising structure because it was the framework applied most commonly by clinical program implementors and evaluators in the included studies [22, 23], and because its five dimensions map directly onto the questions that health system leaders ask when considering whether and how to scale a service. Prior RE-AIM applications in home-based and community care settings have consistently identified denominators and standardised tools as the most significant barriers to comparability [24]; the HaH RE-AIM addresses both by proposing standard definitions and denominator specifications for each indicator. The framework is designed to be modular: a programme evaluating the impact of a clinician education campaign would apply the Adoption and Reach dimensions; a programme assessing long-term viability would apply Maintenance; implementation of a new clinical pathway may use all domains. It may also be used to facilitate comparative studies of HaH implementations, or longitudinally – e.g., they may be used to offer a longitudinal evaluation of how a single HaH program is faring over time, against hospital standards, or against other HaH programs regionally or globally. Next steps in the development of the HaH RE-AIM framework include developing standardised tools for patient, caregiver, staff and referrer satisfaction, to facilitate wider cross-setting comparisons and reduce unwarranted variation in measures across studies.

Future outlook of HaH research

The global HaH evidence base has matured to a point where further descriptive pilots are less of a priority. Answering Lai and Ko’s (2024) call to shift from “does HaH work?” to “how do we scale HaH successfully?“, this review provides three practical tools for that next generation of research. The CFIR-mapped checklist of implementation determinants gives program leaders a structured pre-scale-up audit instrument to identify which barriers are likely to impede expansion before investment is committed. The catalogued ERIC strategies provide a selection menu matched to those barriers, replacing ad hoc strategy choice with evidence-informed options. The HaH RE-AIM offers a common set of indicators that allow implementation trajectories to be tracked over time, compared across sites, and reported to policymakers in terms directly relevant to scale-up decisions. Together, these tools provide the infrastructure for a learning HaH implementation system, where programs contribute to a cumulative, globally comparable evidence base rather than being evaluated in isolation.

Limitations

First, while our search strategy and subsequent mapping to implementation science frameworks was thorough and rigorous, this led to a time lag between the final literature search and manuscript publication. Given the practical resourcing constraints and the intensive process required for framework-based extraction, coding, and quantitative synthesis of implementation determinants, strategies, and outcomes, studies published after 2022 were not included. Second, a formal systematic grey literature search was not performed and expert opinon was used; this approach may limit reproducibility and may have resulted in omission of other relevant unpublished or non-indexed work. Due to the degree of information required to identify implementation factors, we also had to exclude abstracts and conference proceedings. Third, restricting the search to studies only in the English language may have exluded studies done in some European nations where HaH has been well-developed. Fourth, while the use of predefined frameworks as inclusion criteria may have introduced some restrictiveness, we conducted a careful and exhaustive mapping of all identified implementation concepts to the established frameworks. Concepts that did not align with existing categories were proposed as additional categories where appropriate, ensuring that no relevant implementation concept was overlooked or inappropriately categorized. The comprehensiveness of CFIR and RE-AIM also made it unlikely that relevant studies were excluded from the search strategy. The grounding in established frameworks supports the comparability and transferability of findings across diverse HaH programs and settings. Fifth, intervention details were not always clearly reported, hence there may be implementation factors that were not identified during the coding and we had to make some assumptions as to whether the reported factors were contextual factors, strategies or outcomes.Finally, our assessment of risk of bias is limited. Although a final MMAT quality score was calculated for each included study, it is acknowledged that the MMAT was not designed or validated for score aggregation and these score should be interpreted as a contextual reference rather than a measure of methodological quality. Furthermore, lower quality studies contributed equally to the synthesis, which may introduce some uncertainty in the interpretation of identified patterns.

Conclusions

This systematic review synthesised implementation evidence from 27 HaH studies across diverse clinical areas and health systems, mapping determinants to CFIR, strategies to ERIC, and outcomes to RE-AIM. The resulting checklist of implementation determinants, catalogue of scale-up strategies, and HaH RE-AIM outcome framework provide a structured basis for more consistent evaluation and comparison of HaH programs globally. Future studies should move toward prospective, controlled evaluation of implementation strategies, with standardised outcome reporting to enable cumulative evidence development across settings.
