Perceptions of the effectiveness of non-pharmacological management of respiratory disorders among CRD patients
Authors: Yousef S. Aldabayan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) are among the leading cause of mortality worldwide. While pharmacological approaches are commonly used to manage symptoms, non-pharmacological management of CRDs is considered crucial in preventing disease progression and improving patient self-efficacy. To describe the perceived effectiveness of non-pharmacological management of CRDs among CRD patients and determine whether the CRD patients perceptions of the effectiveness of non-pharmacological management are associated with their demographic characteristics. An analytic cross-sectional study design was utilized. The non-pharmacological management practices of CRD patients and their perception of the effectiveness of these therapeutic measures were assessed using a researcher-developed questionnaire. Data collection took place in primary healthcare centers in Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia from October 2021 to March 2022. The proportion of CRD patients who perceived that non-pharmacological interventions were effective was computed. Binary logistic regression was performed to determine the association between the demographic characteristics of the respondents and their perception of the effectiveness of non-pharmacological management. Among the 390 respondents, 42% perceived that non-pharmacological measures were effective. Half of the respondents believed that smoking cessation, influenza vaccination, improving physical activity, and nutrition support helped alleviate CRD symptoms, while less than half of the respondents considered pulmonary rehabilitation, educational programs, and oxygen therapy effective measures to control symptoms. Women were found to be 3.24 times more likely to perceive non-pharmacological interventions as effective (P < .0000) compared to men. Those with university-level education were 66.6% less likely to consider non-pharmacological interventions to be effective (P < .0000) than those who completed preuniversity-level education. Age and marital status did not significantly influence perceptions of effectiveness. Differences in the perceptions of the effectiveness of various non-pharmacological measures to alleviate CRD symptoms existed among the CRD patients of Al Ahsa. The perception of effectiveness was significantly associated with the patient’s gender and educational attainment.

1. Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) are amongst the fastest-growing non communicable diseases worldwide,[1] largely due to the ubiquity of harmful behavioral practices and occupational and environmental inhalational exposure.[2] They are associated with significant rates of hospitalization and prolonged hospital stays, which negatively impact the long-term quality of life.[3–5] The 2 most common are chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, which share common symptoms (coughing, wheezing, and difficulty breathing) and which people may experience at the same time.[6] These 2 conditions contribute significantly to the growing global burden of non-communicable diseases.[7]

According to the 2023 Global Initiative for chronic obstructive lung disease classification, COPD is a heterogeneous lung condition marked by chronic respiratory symptoms brought on by abnormalities of the airways and/or alveoli that result in persistent, often progressive airflow obstruction.[8] The severity of COPD in an individual patient is influenced by exacerbations and comorbidities.[9] COPD is now one of the top 3 causes of death worldwide, with 90% of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries,[10,11] and its prevalence is expected to double by 2030 unless urgent steps are taken to reduce underlying risk factors, primarily tobacco smoking and passive exposure to environmental pollutants.[6,12,13] In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence rates for men and women are 2.5% and 5.7%, respectively.[14] Due to the relatively high smoking rate in Saudi Arabia (27.9% of Saudis over 40),[15] this burden is anticipated to rise further. Moreover, the frequent exposure of the population to nonsmoking factors (i.e., biomass fuel, dust, gases, and outdoor pollution) adds to the burden of CRDs such as COPD and asthma.[15–17]

There is currently no cure for CRDs such as COPD and asthma, but there are a variety of treatment approaches that can help slow the progression of the condition and control the symptoms,[18] including both pharmacological and non-pharmacological therapies.[19] Pharmacological therapy accounts for a substantial portion of the resources used in the long-term management of patients, increasing with illness severity[10,20] and especially during acute exacerbations of COPD.[21] The current treatment of COPD and asthma is mainly based on different combinations of bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, and antibiotics.[22–24] However, pharmacological therapy for COPD typically only addresses symptoms.[25–27] Furthermore, in an observational study conducted in Copenhagen, there was low use and adherence to maintenance medications for COPD in the general population, even in severe and very severe COPD cases.[28] Similar observation was made in Saudi Arabia by Kortuk et al, with poor treatment compliance among COPD patients linked to worsening of the illness and reduced quality of life, while depression, age, and educational level were independent predictors of compliance.[29]

Although medication is key to the effective management of the symptoms of CRDs, particularly COPD, non-pharmacological interventions are equally important for ensuring successful outcomes.[30,31] They are a crucial component of COPD management to prevent progression and improve self-efficacy and they are particularly important as complementary interventions in severe or very severe disease conditions when there is a loss of function and a decrease in quality of life.[30] Non-pharmacological interventions for COPD patients generally include preventive measures, such as smoking cessation and vaccination, pulmonary rehabilitation (i.e., physical activity and self-management), oxygen therapy, and surgical interventions.[32–37] While understanding of the importance of non-pharmacological interventions as a component of COPD therapy has evolved rapidly, knowledge regarding their effectiveness is lacking among COPD patients, whose judgment is largely based on their perceptions and experiences.

Against that backdrop, this study was conducted to evaluate perceptions toward non-pharmacological interventions for COPD management among Saudi adult patients in Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. The findings of this study will help improve the education and management of COPD patients, with benefits for their quality of life.

2. Methodology

2.1. Study design and setting

An analytic cross-sectional study was conducted among chronic respiratory disease patients in randomly selected primary healthcare centers with respiratory and smoking cessation clinics in Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia.

2.2. Study population and sampling procedure

A total of 390 patients previously diagnosed with 1 or both of the forms of chronic respiratory disease (bronchial asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) participated in the study from October 2021 to March 2022. Patients were deemed eligible to participate if they were above 18 years old, able to comprehend and verbalize, without mental disorders and voluntarily provided consent to participate. Using Open Epi, the minimum sample size (386) was calculated using the following parameters: total population of 316,841 (based on 2022 data from the Planning Department), 95% confidence level, and 80% power.

2.3. Data collection questionnaire and procedure

The study utilized a researcher-developed questionnaire validated before distribution to study respondents. It consisted of 2 sections: Demographic characteristics – age, gender, level of education, and marital status; Practices of non-pharmacological management of CRD (smoking cessation, influenza vaccination, pulmonary rehabilitation, physical activity, nutrition support, and oxygen therapy) and perceptions of its effectiveness. A patient was considered to practice a non-pharmacological intervention if they responded “yes” to any of the interventions listed. Those who responded in the affirmative to the practice question were then asked whether they perceived the intervention to be effective or not by choosing “yes” or “no,” respectively.

2.4. Ethical considerations

The research proposal was approved by the Research and Ethical Committee at King Fahad Hospital Hofuf, Al Ahsa, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (IRB KFHH no. H-05-HS-065). Informed consent was obtained from the patients after explaining the aim of the study. Participation in this study was completely voluntary. The ability to withdraw from the study at any time and the confidentiality, privacy, and anonymity of the patients and their responses were assured. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and followed ethical principles.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using Epi Info 7. Descriptive statistics were performed for the categorical and continuous variables. The proportions of respondents who perceived the non-pharmacological interventions as effective and ineffective were tabulated with the corresponding frequencies and confidence intervals. Binary logistic regression was applied to test the association between each demographic variable and the perceived effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions. P < .05 was considered significant, with a confidence interval of 95%.

No significant difference was found when participants were grouped by age; a higher proportion of the patients found non-pharmacological interventions to be ineffective than effective in both the young (<41.5) and old (41.5 and above) groups. The same trend was seen when the patients were grouped according to marital status; however, while both were a majority, a greater proportion (60%) of married respondents considered non-pharmacological interventions to be ineffective compared to the proportion of unmarried respondents. When grouped by educational attainment, those who completed preuniversity-level education were roughly evenly split on whether non-pharmacological interventions were effective, while among those with university-level education, only 24.53% perceived the effectiveness.

Smoking cessation, influenza vaccination, promotion of physical activity, and nutritional support were independently perceived to be effective by half of the study participants. However, only 30% of CRD patients considered pulmonary rehabilitation to be effective, along with 32.56% for educational activities and 43.33% for oxygen therapy.

Perceived effectiveness of various non-pharmacologic interventions.

Among the 390 study participants, 203 (52.05%, 95% CI: 47.10–56.97%) perceived smoking cessation as an effective strategy for alleviating CRD symptoms. The majority of male CRD patients (64.20%, 95% CI: 56.30–71.57%) considered this intervention effective; in contrast, the majority of female CRD patients (56.58%, 95% CI: 49.88–63.11%) considered smoking cessation ineffective in relieving and controlling CRD symptoms. This could be explained by the fact that only 19.3% of female CRD patients reported having a history of smoking, whereas 74.69% of male respondents were either current or former smokers. Meanwhile, nearly 6 out of 10 (58.79%, 95% CI: 50.87–66.38%) unmarried patients considered smoking cessation effective, while 66.38% (95% CI: 46.14–59.56%) of married patients considered this ineffective at relieving CRD symptoms. There were no significant differences in the proportions among age and education groups.

There was a roughly even split in perceptions of influenza vaccination; it was deemed to be effective by 199 patients (51.03%, 95% CI: 46.08–55.95%) and ineffective by 191 (48.97%, 95% CI: 44.05–53.92%). Over half of the male patients (55.56%, 95% CI: 47.55–63.35%) consider it effective, and the reverse was observed among females, where 52.19% (95% CI: 45.50–58.83%) perceive influenza vaccination as ineffective in the management of CRD. While no significant differences in perceptions were observed among age and marital status groups, it should be noted that nearly 60% of patients who had completed preuniversity-level education (95% CI: 49.46–68.87%) considered influenza vaccination an effective component of CRD management.

Pulmonary rehabilitation was perceived to be effective by 117 CRD patients (30.00%, 95% CI: 25.66–34.73%). However, 273 patients (70.00%, 95% CI: 65.27–74.34%) considered it to be ineffective as a component of CRD management. This intervention was consistently viewed as ineffective across age, gender, marital status, and educational attainment groups.

Educational programs that cover topics such as the use of inhalation devices, recognition of exacerbations, and so forth were perceived to be effective by 127 patients (32.56%, 95% CI: 28.10–28.10%) and ineffective by 263 CRD patients (67.44%, 95% CI: 62.63–71.90%). This intervention was consistently perceived to be ineffective across the demographic variables considered in this study, with males and patients with preuniversity-level education having the greatest perceptions of it as ineffective (80.86%, 95% CI: 73.95–86.61%).

Of the 390 patients, 208 (53.33%, 95% CI: 48.37–58.23%) recognized physical activity as an effective component of CRD management, while 182 (46.67%, 95% CI: 41.77–51.63%) considered it ineffective. A higher proportion of females found physical activity effective (57.89%, 95% CI: 51.20–64.38%) than those that did not. The same was seen for age and marital status, with younger and unmarried patients more likely to find physical activity effective than older and married patients.

A near-equal proportion of patients perceived nutrition support as effective (50.26%, 95% CI: 45.32–55.19%) and ineffective (49.74%, 95% CI: 44.81–54.68%) in the non-pharmacological management of CRD. This was seen across age, marital status, and educational attainment groups, but did not hold for gender. Nearly 60% (95% CI: 51.27–66.90%) of male patients viewed nutritional support as ineffective, while among females, 57.02% (95% CI: 50.32–63.53%) reported this intervention to be an effective component of CRD management.

Regarding oxygen therapy, 169 patients (43.33%, 95% CI: 38.50–48.29%) perceived this intervention as effective at relieving CRD symptoms, while 221 (56.67%, 95% CI: 51.71–61.50%) considered it ineffective. Oxygen therapy was perceived to be ineffective at relieving symptoms by a high proportion of male patients (69.75%, 95% CI: 62.05–76.71%) and older patients (65.75%, 95% CI: 58.34%–72.63%).

Binary logistic regression showed that female CRD patients were 3.24 times more likely (P < .0001) to perceive non-pharmacological interventions to be effective in the management of respiratory disease compared to male patients (Table 3). Meanwhile, patients who were more than 41.5 years old (older group) were 17.35% less likely to perceive non-pharmacological interventions to be effective than patients who were <41.5 years old (younger group; P < .3531).

4. Discussion

This study investigated perceptions of the effectiveness of commonly employed means of non-pharmacological management of chronic respiratory disease. Only half of the respondents considered smoking cessation, influenza vaccination, physical activity, and nutritional support to be effective. The perceived effectiveness of oxygen therapy was low (43%), and there were very low perceptions of the effectiveness of pulmonary rehabilitation and health education. Variations in perceptions of effectiveness across demographic variables were observed. When logistic regression analysis was conducted, the associations of gender and education with perceptions of effectiveness were highly significant.

In the current study, only 52% of participants agreed on the effectiveness of smoking cessation, though the management of COPD patients should place a high priority on smoking cessation because it is still the only intervention demonstrated to slow the rapid decline of lung function.[9,38–41] In their recommendations, international COPD guidelines have emphasized the importance of effective smoking cessation interventions.[9,42] Meanwhile, in a 2016 Cochrane review on smoking cessation, a combination of non-pharmacological (e.g., behavioral therapy) and pharmacological interventions was found to be more effective than any singular type of COPD management.[43] However, physicians do not always regularly offer evidence-based smoking cessation treatment to their patients,[39] and the prevalence of smoking in COPD patients remains high, with evidence that it exceeds the rate of smoking in the general population.[44]

Moreover, only 51% of the participants considered influenza vaccination as an important component of CRD management, though according to the centers for disease control and prevention, people with COPD or asthma are at high risk of serious problems from certain vaccine-preventable diseases.[45] Therefore, vaccination with antipneumococcal and anti-influenza vaccines is highly recommended for these patients.[20,46,47] A study by Malpuru et al concluded that influenza vaccination significantly lowers the risk of COPD patients hospitalization and critical illness due to influenza.[48] Furthermore, a 2017 Cochrane review of 12 studies on the importance of pneumococcal vaccines for COPD patients revealed that pneumococcal vaccination offers significant protection against community-acquired pneumonia and reduces the likelihood of COPD exacerbation.[49] In addition, several studies have shown the effectiveness of vaccinations in lowering the possibility of respiratory illnesses such as bronchitis or pneumonia, as well as flare-ups of COPD.[20,50,51] Nonetheless, their use in these individuals is suboptimal.[52,53]

Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) entails supervised instruction in exercise training, education, and behavioral change to improve physical function and modify behavior.[33,34,54,55] There was a low appreciation of the importance of pulmonary rehabilitation among the Saudi patients included in the study as only 30% reported it to be effective as a component of CRD management. Nonetheless, PR has been shown to ease breathlessness, boost exercise capacity, and improve the health-related quality of life of patients with COPD.[34,56] In an economic evaluation study by Mosher et al[57], PR after COPD hospitalization resulted in net cost savings among US COPD patients, along with an improved quality-adjusted life expectancy. Moreover, PR has been linked to a substantial decline in hospital admissions and mortality 1 year after hospitalization.[56,58] Despite consistent benefits being shown in large observational studies[58,59] and clinical trials,[56] the uptake of PR continues to be relatively low.[60] Lack of access to transportation and copayments have been identified as significant barriers to uptake and adherence, while others have identified poor reimbursement as a critical barrier to wider use.[55]

Less than half (43.33%) of the participants in the current study agreed that oxygen therapy was an effective strategy in CRD management. However, long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) for at least 15 hours a day has been shown to increase survival and significantly improve the quality of life of COPD patients experiencing breathlessness.[61–64] In a recent observational study conducted among COPD patients admitted in UK hospitals, mortality was lowest in patients who had oxygen therapy upon admission in controlled amounts (with target saturations of 88–92%). However, a review by Gauthier et al[65] showed that only 60% of COPD patients are adherent to LTOT, which can be considered suboptimal. Low adherence to LTOT is associated with increasing age and ambulatory oxygen utilization.

This study had several limitations. The results apply only to patients who consulted the primary health centers of Al Ahsa at the time of data collection. Selection bias could not be discounted as CRD patients who consulted the outpatient departments of hospitals in the area were not considered in this study. Furthermore, the study assessed only perceptions of effectiveness, and no clinical verification was performed; therefore, misclassification was probable. In addition, the utilization of non-pharmacological interventions is time-varying and may be affected by recent exacerbations of respiratory symptoms.

5. Conclusion

The effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions in the management of CRD was generally poorly perceived among patients in Al Ahsa. Among the interventions considered in this study, respondents believed that smoking cessation, influenza vaccination, improving physical activity, and nutrition support helped alleviate CRD symptoms. However, pulmonary rehabilitation, educational programs, and oxygen therapy were not deemed effective measures to control CRD symptoms. Differences in perceptions were noted among the CRD patients of Al Ahsa to be significantly associated with a patient’s gender and educational attainment.

5.1. Recommendations

Perceptions of effectiveness must be validated with clinical findings. Patients must be educated on the important role of non-pharmacological interventions in preventing exacerbations and disease progression early in the course of treatment. A community-based support system should be developed to reinforce appreciation and application of non-pharmacological interventions, for the holistic management of chronic respiratory disease.
