‘Seen, sorted and sent home’ – A patient’s desired outcome from urgent and emergency care
Authors: Sue Astbury
Published: 2026

Introduction

Urgent and emergency care is frequently discussed in terms of targets, performance metrics and system pressures. For patients and relatives, however, it is experienced at moments of fear, uncertainty and vulnerability, when care is urgently needed and trust in both individuals and systems is paramount. Drawing on my experiences as a patient and as a relative, this article reflects on how access, communication and transparency shape patient experience across urgent and emergency care pathways.

Communication and unintended distress

As a lay representative, I often accompany relatives to GP and hospital appointments, to offer support and gain insight into real patient experiences. Through this, I have become increasingly aware of how prolonged and frightening these journeys can be, even for confident, articulate adults. At these times, people are often overwhelmed, and their ability to process information is limited. The pathways offered, the language used and the information shared matter enormously.

One small but vivid example illustrates this. A worried, intelligent adult patient was nervously anticipating the insertion of a catheter, having been told hours earlier that they would need a cannula. They did not know one medical term from another. From a clinical perspective, this may appear inconsequential, but the confusion caused unnecessary anxiety at an already stressful moment. It was a small thing – but not to the person experiencing it. Importantly, this distress was unintended and unnoticed by staff. In urgent and emergency care settings, where fear is heightened, such moments can disproportionately shape the overall experience.

Experiences of emergency response

In contrast to coverage of paramedic pressures and delayed ambulance handovers, my experiences of requiring an ambulance have, fortunately, been positive. I have encountered responsive and reassuring crews, accurate estimated times of arrival, and appropriate, timely care. These interactions reinforced my confidence in emergency services and demonstrated the professionalism of staff working under intense pressure.

However, I have also witnessed the system from another perspective. I observed an off-duty GP making a 999 ambulance call with extraordinary patience. Despite clearly stating her role and providing a concise, structured clinical summary at the outset, she was still required to answer a lengthy list of questions from the call handler. I don’t know whether the ambulance was dispatched immediately or whether what felt like an interminable exchange delayed the response. In that particular case, it made no difference – but I worry about occasions when it might.

Trust, transparency and public confidence

It was against this backdrop that the recent Dispatches documentary on Channel 4 caused me genuine heartbreak. Accounts of callers being told that an ambulance was ‘on the way’, leading them to remain where they were rather than seek alternatives, were profoundly unsettling. I cannot speak for those individuals, whose experiences are theirs to tell. But when I make that call, all of my faith rests with the person on the other end of the line. I act solely on what I am told at my most frightened and vulnerable. To be lied to – or, at best, misled – undermines trust and transparency and would make me question my ability to rely on the service again. Public trust in urgent and emergency care systems is paramount.

Patient experiences of emergency and urgent hospital care

Across many encounters, it is often the ‘not knowing’ – when you might be seen, what will happen next, or whether anyone is aware of your presence – that causes distress beyond the presenting problem itself. I experienced this unexpectedly when I provided a lift for someone else’s hospital appointment, only to find myself walking – albeit slowly – into A&E. My memories are vivid: the fear of the sudden physical symptoms and my first encounter with the red doors, contrasted sharply with the apparent disinterest of the receptionist asking my reason for attendance. I sank into a chair, closed my eyes, and felt profoundly alone. I remember thinking, in a moment of pain, how long it might take for someone to notice if I did not open them again. For patients and relatives, many processes are invisible; what they experience instead is waiting, uncertainty and fear.

Thankfully, my story had a positive and relatively swift conclusion. I was seen, moved to same-day emergency care (SDEC), investigated, treated and sent home with a follow-up appointment. What I remember most clearly, however, is not the tests or treatments, but how people made me feel. The friendly smile and clear directions from security contrasted with the next interaction. I then felt reassured when given time to explain my symptoms and clear explanations of what was going to happen. Every member of the team shaped my experience. The service allowed same-day access to specialist care and diagnostics, when I might otherwise have taken up a hospital bed. I was, quite literally, seen, sorted and sent home. This stands in contrast to other experiences I have observed. One case, involving a normally well older patient, suddenly requiring multiple GP appointments – including a rare home visit – failed to lead to timely referral. In another case, a patient who rarely consulted their GP began requesting appointments with significant symptoms, including facial swelling, breathlessness on bending and a hoarse voice. Despite repeated consultations and photographs alongside pleas to seek hospital advice, escalation was delayed. Only later was a 2-week-wait pathway initiated for a different specialty, becoming the only route into secondary care without attending the emergency department. These experiences felt worrying and inefficient, and led to significant delay. These patients were neither accidents nor emergencies, but both cases became medically urgent during the delays. Neither the GP nor A&E felt like the right pathway in these instances. As a result, the patients’ faith in the healthcare system (for it to be there when it mattered) was damaged.

I cannot overstate the benefits I have seen, with my own eyes, when SDEC or acute medical units (AMUs) assessed medically complex relatives. Compared with the previous prolonged admissions involving debilitating bed rest, sleep deprivation and visible loss of function, I have seen the huge benefits of being assessed, treated and discharged home in preserving independence and preventing deterioration. Access to this service within the first 72 h and avoiding unnecessary, longer-stay hospital admissions felt life-changing.

I have been told of a prolonged emergency department stay – an 80+ year old who spent the night, in agony, in a corridor. Corridor ‘care’ feels like the polar opposite of what care should be. I hear of the moral distress experienced by NHS staff working in such conditions and am in awe of how they continue.

System pressures

Wider system pressures undoubtedly contribute to these experiences. Constant media coverage linking common symptoms to catastrophic diagnoses – while important for awareness – can fuel anxiety among the worried well. Combined with difficulties accessing GP appointments, mental health support and social care, it is perhaps unsurprising that emergency departments become a default option.

Modern acute medical pathways, including SDEC and short-stay AMUs, offer safe and efficient alternatives to traditional inpatient care for medically complex patients through prompt diagnostics and unnecessary admission avoidance. They could be used as an alternative to minimise repeated GP visits for serious, undiagnosed medically complex conditions. Yet patient awareness of these services remains limited, so understanding and access vary widely. Other developments such as community diagnostic centres may offer hope of improved access and reduced pressure on acute services at hospital sites, with improved access within the community. Either way, a genuine ‘one-stop shop’ approach that minimises repetition is preferable to fragmented care, where patients are faced with repeating their story across multiple appointments. This is inefficient and, sadly, often results in significant deterioration for the patient.

Conclusion

As urgent and emergency care services continue to evolve under increasing strain, patient experience must remain central. Safeguarding trust should be considered as fundamental to emergency and urgent care as response times and clinical outcomes.

As system pressures increase, the need to investigate medically complex adults is paramount. The benefits of being seen, sorted and sent home cannot be overestimated, particularly in older patients. The resulting efficiencies for the overall system are significant.

This is the care that I would wish for myself and my loved ones when acutely unwell: timely assessment, clear explanations, honest communication and the ability to return home safely. Seen, sorted, sent home.
