Escaped surveillance: tuberculosis masked by severe hyponatremia in an immunocompromised patient: a case report
Authors: Satyamedha Bathula, Hemalatha Pasupuleti, Aeman Waqar, Matthew DiCarlo, Jayashree Kadirvelu, Sang H Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis in immunosuppressed individuals frequently presents atypically and may produce false-negative screening results, delaying diagnosis. Hyponatremia and features of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion can mimic paraneoplastic processes, further complicating the clinical picture. This case describes the challenges of diagnosing tuberculosis in a patient receiving prolonged corticosteroid therapy.Case presentationA 74-year-old East Asian woman with long-standing myasthenia gravis treated with prednisone presented with 1 month of progressive weakness, weight loss, gastrointestinal symptoms, cough, shortness of breath, and persistent hyponatremia. Lung biopsy demonstrated necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, and acid-fast staining of sputum, stool, and ileal tissue confirmed tuberculosis with extrapulmonary ileal involvement. Background

Despite advances in diagnostics and treatment, tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health burden. An estimated 10.8 million people are infected worldwide, resulting in more than 1.25 million deaths [1]. Although the Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine offers protection against severe childhood disease, it does not entirely prevent pulmonary or disseminated TB, and the global burden remains substantial [1, 2].

Mycobacterium tuberculosis most commonly affects the lungs but may remain latent for years. Individuals with impaired immunity due to chronic illness, autoimmune disease, malnutrition, or prolonged corticosteroid therapy have an increased risk of progression from latent infection to active disease [2–7]. Active TB may present as pulmonary disease or disseminate hematogenously or through lymphatic pathways, involving organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, central nervous system, adrenal glands, or urogenital system [2–4]. A careful exposure history, including recent travel to TB-endemic regions, is essential [1, 2].

Screening for latent TB typically includes tuberculin skin testing (PPD), interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA), and chest imaging [2, 6, 7]. Classic pulmonary TB symptoms, including fever, night sweats, weight loss, and productive cough, generally heighten suspicion, and imaging helps define the extent of the disease [2]. Individuals with suspected TB should also be screened for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) due to frequent co-infection [2, 5].

However, immunosuppressed patients often present atypically. Screening tests may be falsely negative due to an inadequate immune response, thereby lowering clinical suspicion in low-prevalence regions such as the USA [5–7]. In this population, laboratory abnormalities, including hyponatremia and elevated inflammatory markers, and features of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) should raise concern for TB even when initial testing is unremarkable [8–13].

This case is unique in that TB first came to attention through severe hyponatremia. Because screening tests were negative and imaging resembled neoplastic disease, clinicians initially suspected malignancy. The patient’s clinical course demonstrates the need to perform confirmatory tests, including evaluation of inflammatory markers, microbiologic staining, and culture analysis, to avoid delayed diagnosis and severe complications.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old East Asian woman presented with 1 month of worsening weakness, shortness of breath, a dry cough, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, decreased urine output, and unintended weight loss. Her history included myasthenia gravis (MG) for two decades, chronic gastritis, intermittent cardiac arrhythmias, and a remote pelvic fracture. She had a remote smoking history of approximately three to five pack-years.

Her MG had worsened in the preceding month, and prednisone had been initiated after an inadequate response to pyridostigmine. Given recent travel to South Korea, initial screening for TB and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was performed using nasal swabs and a purified protein derivative (PPD) test; both results were negative. Her physical exam was notable for persistently elevated blood pressure (> 170/80 mmHg). Laboratory studies revealed stable parameters except for persistent hyponatremia (125–130 mmol/L). An electrocardiogram (ECG) was normal.

Given her weight loss, cough, dyspnea, and hyponatremia, clinicians initially considered cardiopulmonary disease, abdominal aortic pathology, and paraneoplastic or malignancy-associated antidiuretic hormone secretion. Chest X-ray (CXR) and computed tomography (CT) revealed a posterior right upper-lobe mass and extensive centrilobular nodules (see Figs. 1, 2). She was started on tolvaptan and nifedipine to address hyponatremia and elevated blood pressure.

A percutaneous lung biopsy showed necrotizing granulomatous pneumonitis without dysplasia or malignant features, raising suspicion for TB or fungal infection. Sputum and stool samples were obtained due to her gastrointestinal symptoms, and acid-fast staining of sputum, stool, and biopsy tissue confirmed TB with ileal involvement.

First-line therapy with rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol (RIPE regimen) was initiated, along with supportive care. Because rifampin reduces serum levels of tolvaptan and nifedipine, these medications were discontinued and replaced with beta-blockers. Table 1 summarizes her therapeutic course.

Five days into treatment, the patient developed severe abdominal pain. Inflammatory markers were also elevated, including a markedly increased C-reactive protein (CRP) level (45 mg/L), which decreased with treatment modification (see Table 2). Rifampin, isoniazid, and pyrazinamide were discontinued and replaced with linezolid, moxifloxacin, and amikacin. Liver enzymes improved within 48 hours. Discussion

Diagnostic methods and challenges in atypical presentation

Diagnosing tuberculosis in immunosuppressed patients is often difficult because their presenting symptoms are atypical or blunted, thus clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion [5–7]. In this case, several early diagnostic clues were subtle and easily missed, and chronic prednisone therapy likely suppressed the cell-mediated immune response required for a positive tuberculin skin test, contributing to a false-negative result [5–7]. Corticosteroids can also blunt typical inflammatory and cellular markers of infection, including leukocytosis, which may make early laboratory findings appear deceptively benign and delay consideration of infectious etiologies [5–7].

In low-prevalence regions, pulmonary masses on imaging further complicate the diagnostic picture and prompt clinicians to suspect malignancy [2, 5]. The combination of a right upper lobe mass and diffuse nodules, especially in this elderly patient with a remote smoking history, constitutional symptoms, and hyponatremia, could easily raise suspicion for malignancy or paraneoplastic syndrome rather than an infectious disease. The severe hyponatremia added to this impression, strengthening the possibility of malignancy-related antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion, and further lowered the initial suspicion for an infectious cause.

Hyponatremia, however, is an underrecognized but well-documented manifestation of tuberculosis [8–11, 13]. Multiple cohort studies have demonstrated significant associations between tuberculosis, hyponatremia, and SIADH [8–11]. Because SIADH also occurs in lung cancer and other malignancies, this overlap can easily obscure the diagnosis unless confirmatory microbiologic testing is obtained [8–13]. In our patient, the degree of hyponatremia was disproportionate to the rest of her presentation, which should prompt clinicians to broaden the differential rather than anchor on malignancy alone. In practice, these features mean that tuberculosis should remain in the differential diagnosis for unexplained hyponatremia, particularly in immunocompromised patients or those with epidemiologic risk factors, even when initial screening tests are negative.

The absence of classic pulmonary findings ultimately required definitive microbiologic confirmation. Acid-fast staining and culture of sputum, stool, and biopsy specimens were critical in establishing the diagnosis of pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis [2–4, 8–11]. This patient’s clinical course shows how relying too heavily on negative screening test results in immunosuppressed patients can delay the correct diagnosis. It reinforces the need for timely confirmatory testing, preferably before initiating or escalating corticosteroid therapy, to avoid delay, prevent dissemination, and reduce the risk of reactivation [5–7]. Pathophysiology of hyponatremia in tuberculosis

Hyponatremia in tuberculosis has several proposed mechanisms. Earlier literature suggested that adrenal suppression could result from direct invasion of TB into the adrenal glands or the hypothalamic–pituitary axis might cause adrenal insufficiency [3, 4, 8–11]. A more recent study by Yoshida et al. [14] contradicts this phenomenon, showing that cortisol levels increased continuously to compensate for stress from an infectious burden and hyponatremia, rather than decreasing. This indicates that adrenal failure is unlikely to be the main driver in most patients.

The more commonly accepted explanation involves inappropriate secretion of ADH due to both overstimulation and loss of feedback inhibition [8–12]. In TB infection, cytokines and a prolonged hypoxic respiratory state stimulate stretch receptors in the lungs via the vagal afferent pathway, signaling the hypothalamus and pituitary to increase ADH production [13]. At the same time, there is a disruption of osmoreceptor communication from the kidney to the brain, which impairs feedback inhibition of the ADH pathway, leading to continuous production of inappropriate amounts [8–12]. Altogether, these mechanisms cause euvolemic hyponatremia (see Fig. 6). Recognizing this physiology is essential, because in some patients, hyponatremia may serve as an indirect clue to disseminated or severe TB rather than a coincidental electrolyte disturbance [8–11, 13].

Acute-phase protein levels in the diagnostic process

Acute-phase reactants, such as CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), can aid in diagnostic evaluation [10, 13, 15–17]. ESR is sensitive but nonspecific, and many conditions can increase it [15, 16]. CRP, in contrast, correlates more closely with infection-related inflammation, even in patients with underlying autoimmune disease [13, 15, 17]. Several studies demonstrate a strong association between TB, elevated CRP, and hyponatremia [10, 13]. This relationship can guide early suspicion of TB in patients with negative findings on screening tests [10, 13, 17]. However, steroid therapy can alter CRP levels. Khaltaev et al. reported that low-dose steroids (< 5 mg/day prednisone) may suppress CRP, while higher doses may paradoxically increase it [16]. This pattern aligns with our patient’s elevated CRP despite chronic prednisone use. When combined with persistent hyponatremia and constitutional symptoms, elevated CRP should prompt performing confirmatory microbiologic testing [10, 13, 15–17].

Complications and clinical course

Delayed diagnosis in this case led to TB infecting the ileum by the time treatment began, causing TB ileitis, which progressed to stricturing, obstruction, and peritonitis [3–5, 18]. At that stage, medical therapy alone was insufficient, and surgical intervention became necessary. Findings on abdominal imaging and peritoneal fluid analysis supported this progression [3, 4, 18].

There was also concern for emerging drug resistance during periods when the first-line medication regimen was modified due to drug-induced hepatotoxicity. Resistance to first-line agents such as isoniazid can make tuberculosis far more challenging to treat and sometimes requires surgical approaches even in pulmonary disease [18, 19]. The patient’s clinical deterioration shows how delays in diagnosis and breaks in effective therapy—such as stopping first-line medications because of drug toxicity—can allow TB to progress and result in serious complications [5, 18, 19].

Limitations

This case also highlights several gaps in the current literature and in existing diagnostic frameworks for TB. Although the association between TB, hyponatremia, and ADH secretion is documented, standardized diagnostic criteria for immunosuppressed populations are lacking [8–13]. Many studies focus on hospitalized or critically ill populations and do not isolate the effects of corticosteroids from other causes of immune compromise [7, 10, 11, 16]. This makes it challenging to generalize findings to patients like ours.

In addition, acute-phase reactants have limitations. CRP is less reliable in children and young adults, and steroid dosages can alter values [16, 17]. ESR is nonspecific and slow to respond to inflammatory changes [15, 16]. Access to diagnostic tools such as interferon-gamma release assays or confirmatory microbiologic testing may also be limited in some clinical settings due to cost or turnaround time [2, 6, 7]. Taken together, these factors necessitate cautious interpretation of laboratory results and a sustained high index of suspicion when assessing immunosuppressed patients for possible TB [5–7].

Conclusion

Recognizing TB in immunosuppressed patients is challenging and often depends on clinicians picking up on subtle, nonspecific clues. This case illustrates how TB becomes difficult to detect when screening tests are falsely negative, and symptoms resemble malignancy. In patients receiving chronic corticosteroids, the combination of persistent hyponatremia, features of SIADH, constitutional symptoms, and atypical pulmonary imaging provided early indications of infection that warranted confirmatory testing. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for TB in immunosuppressed patients even when PPD or IGRA results are negative, integrating laboratory abnormalities such as hyponatremia and inflammatory markers into early decision-making. In this case, delayed recognition resulted in ileal TB, peritonitis, and the need for surgical intervention. In clinical practice, timely diagnosis and thoughtful adjustment of therapy are crucial to limiting dissemination and avoiding severe complications.
