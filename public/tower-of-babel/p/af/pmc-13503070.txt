Endovascular removal of a balloon-expandable uncovered superior mesenteric artery stent
Authors: David P. Kuwayama, Jeniann A. Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
No defined strategy exists for the endovascular removal of chronically indwelling stents. Any attempt carries risks including extraction failure, stent disruption, arterial injury, and embolization. We report the successful endovascular removal of a balloon-expandable, cobalt-chromium, uncovered stent from the superior mesenteric artery. Using bilateral axillary and unilateral femoral access, the stent was retrieved under continuous control with biopsy forceps, a distal angioplasty balloon, a body-floss wire, and a large-bore femoral sheath. We describe the extraction technique and discuss key considerations for endovascular stent extraction when such approach is contemplated.

Endovascular stenting is now considered as the first-line therapy for many occlusive vascular conditions. Although minimally invasive to deploy, stents are permanent implants with no well-defined removal strategy. The only established approach is open surgical extraction—either by eversion endarterectomy from the stent endpoint or by longitudinal arteriotomy along the stent. Although feasible for peripheral stents with modest operative morbidity, open exposure is significantly more difficult for stents in central locations such as the abdomen or thorax.

The superior mesenteric artery (SMA) origin is frequently stented for ostial atherosclerosis causing chronic mesenteric ischemia. Because of its location at the aortic diaphragmatic hiatus, an open approach requires retroperitoneal or thoracoabdominal exposure and aortic clamping for control. For the rare instance in which SMA stent extraction is necessary, an endovascular strategy may offer significant clinical benefit. Here, we describe a case where SMA stent extraction was clinically indicated and performed using entirely endovascular techniques. Written patient consent was obtained before publication.

Case report

A 49-year-old woman reportedly underwent SMA stenting for acute spontaneous SMA dissection at an outside facility; original records are unavailable. She recovered without complication and was clinically well for 14 years until, at the age of 63 years, she developed progressive intestinal angina and sitophobia. Computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrated a patent SMA stent with high-grade proximal in-stent stenosis. She subsequently underwent relining of the proximal stent with a 6.0 × 18 mm RX Herculink Elite (Abbott Vascular) balloon-expandable cobalt-chromium stent. Initially, she experienced immediate improvement in symptoms; however, within several weeks, she developed recurrent and now unrelenting abdominal pain unrelated to food intake. Repeat CT demonstrated widely patent SMA stents. In addition, she developed a full body macular rash, prompting referral to an allergist. Epicutaneous patch testing revealed reactivity to chromium and manganese; cobalt reactivity was not detected. A diagnosis of stent allergy was established, and the patient was initiated on oral prednisone therapy. However, despite 1 month of high-dose steroids, the rash and abdominal pain failed to resolve. She was referred to vascular surgery for consideration of stent explantation.

CT demonstrated a stent configuration favorable for endovascular retrieval. Specifically, the original stent appeared well sized, well expanded, and appropriately coapted to the native SMA lumen, mostly positioned within the SMA with only a short portion protruding into the aorta. In contrast, the newly placed stent appeared undersized without adequate luminal apposition to the original stent, and protruded significantly into the aortic lumen (Fig 1). At the time of surgery, the new stent had been indwelling for approximately 4 months. Bilateral open axillary and unilateral percutaneous femoral access was obtained. Two 6.5F TourGuide steerable sheaths (Medtronic) were advanced into the descending aorta through each axillary access. Via one sheath (the “balloon sheath”), a Rosen wire (Cook Medical) was advanced through the SMA stents into the distal SMA, followed by a 5 mm angioplasty balloon. Via the other sheath (the “forceps sheath”), a 2.0 mm EndoJaw biopsy forceps (Olympus) grasped the proximal stent (Fig 2, A). With backward traction on the forceps, the stent was withdrawn into the thoracic aorta over the balloon shaft (Fig 2, B). During this maneuver, the stent elongated and narrowed in caliber but did not fracture. While maintaining forceps control of the stent, the balloon was retracted into the aorta and inflated, trapping the stent on the balloon shaft; the stent was then released from the forceps (Fig 2, C). Directed SMA angiography was performed through the forceps sheath confirming no evidence of SMA occlusion, dissection, or distal embolization; Rosen wire access to the SMA was then withdrawn. The Rosen wire was removed from the angioplasty balloon lumen and swapped for a Lunderquist wire (Cook Medical), which was snared from the groin and delivered as a body floss wire (Fig 2, D). Two Perclose ProGlide suture-mediated closure devices (Abbott Vascular) were predeployed at the femoral access site followed by upsizing to a 14F DrySeal sheath (W. L. Gore & Associates). A gooseneck snare was advanced through the femoral sheath over the body floss wire and used to secure the stent (Fig 2, E). The patient tolerated the procedure uneventfully. She was discharged on clopidogrel and rivaroxaban on postprocedure day 2 with minimal abdominal pain and improved rash. No subsequent issues related to food tolerance or allergic symptoms were noted at 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

Endovascular retrieval of intravascular foreign bodies, including stents and stent grafts, has been successfully reported in an array of vascular territories using snare-based techniques, with technical success rates exceeding 90%.1, 2, 3, 4 Although intraoperative snare rescue of acutely dislodged stents has been described during mesenteric artery interventions,5 planned endovascular retrieval of a chronically integrated visceral artery stent has not previously been reported. The present technique demonstrates that endovascular SMA stent removal is feasible in appropriately selected patients, provided preoperative imaging confirms suitable stent anatomy.

Several patient-specific factors contributed to technical feasibility. First, the stent-in-stent configuration—an undersized, inner target stent seated within a larger, outer stent—facilitated retrieval; the outer stent protected the native SMA from rupture or dissection caused by traction applied to the inner stent. Second, the structure of the RX Herculink Elite stent (Abbott Vascular) contributed to favorable behavior in response to manipulation. Its balloon-expandable, open-cell structure allowed the snare to crimp it and caused traction to narrow its caliber (Fig 3), facilitating mobilization and extraction. Self-expanding stents would not exhibit similar crimping behavior, and it is unclear whether balloon-expandable closed-cell or covered stent designs would behave similarly. Third, the stent had been indwelling for only 4 months. A stent of longer indwelling duration might have proven significantly more difficult or impossible to extract due to neointimal formation, though a critical threshold duration for removability remains unknown.Fig 3Intraoperative angiogram demonstrating behavior of the balloon-expandable stent in response to traction. Specific elements of this approach facilitated safe retrieval. Removing the stent over a balloon provided continuous distal control, preventing embolization once the stent was mobilized. A body floss wire created a rail for directed extraction, further mitigating this risk. A large-bore femoral sheath provided a protected exit pathway, shielding the native vessel from injury during stent withdrawal. Extraction through axillary access was considered, but deferred in favor of femoral access because of the relative ease with which further femoral sheath upsizing could have been accomplished if necessary. Stent fracture was an anticipated risk. Our contingency plan was to maintain balloon occlusion distally to confine any fragment, then retrieve each fragment individually with the femoral snare. If retrieval had proven impossible, our bailout strategy would have been to reline fractured, deformed, or damaged stent with an appropriately sized covered stent, and resort to open stent removal for allergy treatment at a future date.

Conclusions

We describe a technique for endovascular removal of a chronically indwelling balloon-expandable uncovered SMA stent. This approach maintained continuous stent control, permitted interrogation of the native SMA for patency, and facilitated safe percutaneous extraction. Applicability to other stent types or vascular territories is highly patient-specific. Providers should carefully assess the likelihood of technical success before attempting similar procedures, as extraction failure may pose significant risk of patient harm.
