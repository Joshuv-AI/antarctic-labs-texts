Disaggregating animal welfare risks in aquaculture
Authors: Chiawen Chiang, Becca Franks
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aquaculture, fueled partly by claims of supporting food security, is experiencing unprecedented growth. Framing aquaculture as a monolith, however, overlooks its extreme taxonomic diversity. This paper assesses the welfare risks associated with that diversity, establishing seven species-level risk factors from involved parental care to long lifespans. Investigating these welfare risks across all aquatic species reported to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) revealed an uneven distribution of risk, with extreme species-level risks in chordates, crustaceans, and cephalopod mollusks. Compared to species with fewer risks, species with extreme welfare risks were found to cost more and contribute the least to global production. This work challenges the notion that prioritizing animal welfare is incompatible with addressing food security and creates the possibility of identifying certain plant and invertebrate species, like seaweeds and bivalves, that minimize welfare concerns while providing affordability and accessibility. Going forward, proactive welfare approaches are needed to inform consumer choice and shape just and sustainable aquaculture policy.

INTRODUCTION

Aquatic food production is undergoing a period of unparalleled expansion, outpacing land-based farming in both numbers (1) and diversity (2) while attracting international attention and resources. The industry is being heralded as the key to a “truly sustainable global food system” (3) and a “revolution” (4), and “vital to global food security” (5). However, these framings reduce an unusually heterogeneous sector into a monolith. Aquaculture, the farming of aquatic plants and animals, is one order of magnitude more species diverse than terrestrial farming (2) and involves a high variety of emerging and in-development cultivation methods (6). Comparing production methods involves weighing many trade-offs in, for example, economic viability such as infrastructure costs and maintenance, market price, and market potential, as well as societal concerns regarding alleviating food insecurity, minimizing environmental footprint, and safeguarding animal welfare. Work on terrestrial agriculture has shown that food production is highly nuanced and that disaggregating across various dimensions can lead to different conclusions than when heterogeneity is ignored or aggregated into a single unit (7, 8). Similarly, understanding specificities in aquaculture will be crucial for meaningful, just, and sustainable progress.

Animal welfare is an important dimension of concern for disaggregation: It is increasingly recognized as a priority both as a moral imperative (9) and as a global issue with impacts on environmental sustainability and human well-being (10, 11). In addition, animal welfare improvements can be associated with economic benefits such as reduced mortalities and injuries and improved productivity and reproduction (12, 13). For aquatic species, including chordates and invertebrates, concerns about welfare have been fueled by recent research revealing their complex social (14), emotional (15, 16), and cognitive (17, 18) lives (19), including signs of play and curiosity (20–22), mirror-self-recognition (23), tool use (24), and individual personalities (25). Work by fair-fish and its growing database has indicated that providing animals with a good life under aquaculture systems is an ongoing challenge: Their welfare needs to vary markedly by species, with most standard industry practices leading to unacceptable welfare conditions even in the best-case scenarios (26).

Yet, the growth of aquaculture remains unprecedented. The number of finfishes slaughtered for food has increased ninefold since 1990 and now exceeds the number of terrestrial vertebrates killed each year (1). Attempts to domesticate aquatic animals are occurring at a rate nearly 100 times faster than terrestrial animals (2). The vast majority of these efforts began only in the middle of the 20th century (6), meaning that a large number of species are being farmed today with a minimal history of artificial selection and in the absence of species-specific knowledge about their biological and behavioral needs (27).

While there are strong individual differences in how animals respond to captivity, there are also well-documented species-level differences, with some species tending to experience particularly poor welfare outcomes when held in captivity. Interspecies comparisons of welfare have begun to identify species-level characteristics that may play crucial roles in shaping the welfare of captive individuals (28, 29). Data-driven approaches that account for phylogenetic non-independence (30) have suggested that characteristics such as home-range size (31), mating system (32), and foraging style (33, 34) are all predictors of increased welfare risk at the species level.

General principles from the domestication literature and animal welfare science point to additional species-level considerations. Research has indicated that domestication attempts are successful when captive conditions have at least some possibility of meeting species-typical characteristics and the development and expression of species-typical behavior (35). For example, while it might be possible to accommodate certain types of grazing behavior in captivity, it would be impossible to accommodate hunting behavior that consists of traveling dozens of kilometers each day tracking down prey. When captive conditions are incompatible with a species’ natural life, e.g., their typical daily behavioral patterns, abiotic environment, ecological community, seasonal and annual migrations, etc., they face greater artificial selective pressure both in terms of the number of traits that must adapt to captivity and the severity of overall selection (35). For some traits, this artificial selection takes generations to achieve and in the interim, individual members of the species will be exposed to environments to which they are maladapted, including inappropriate diets, physical discomforts, mental distress, and restrictions to expressing species-typical behaviors. All of these factors are recognized by animal welfare science as critical to an individual’s well-being and may potentially harm their welfare (36).

In essence, modern aquaculture can be understood as a massive and rapid attempt to domesticate wild species into captive conditions under human care. This process involves exposing hundreds of wild species, consisting of billions of individual animals, to potentially harmful conditions. Moreover, as many domestication attempts ultimately do not yield valuable domesticates [i.e., are doomed to fail; (37)], attending to the species-level welfare risks is important for animal welfare considerations and also, potentially, for the ultimate success and longevity of aquaculture expansion goals.

To disaggregate welfare risk across aquaculture, therefore, we began by conducting a comprehensive literature review to identify a range of welfare risk factors reported from relevant fields: captive animal welfare, domestication, aquatic animal welfare, and animal welfare research more broadly. Second, we homed in on a subset of characteristics that were (i) consistently identified in the literature as the most risky and (ii) the most readily available for species-by-species evaluation. Next, to evaluate the species-level welfare risks associated with the vast taxonomic diversity within aquaculture, we used the welfare-risk parameters identified by our literature search to quantify the relative risk profile for each species in aquaculture. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations makes available the annual amount and diversity of aquatic animals used in global aquaculture operations since 1950. Using these data, we validated all FAO species listings with information on Fishbase (38) and Sealifebase (39), from which we extracted relevant species-level characteristics. Last, we conducted literature searches to fill in missing species-level traits. We combined these data with historical production trends and value data from the FAO to identify species at greatest relative welfare-risk and the least promising candidates for aquaculture expansion.

RESULTS

The literature review revealed 18 potential welfare risk factors, which we organized into six broad categories of direct welfare risk (e.g., reproduction, diet, and social behavior) plus an additional category of indirect welfare risk (e.g., the need to use cleaner fish). Six potential welfare risk factors (of the original 18) were poorly or patchily documented for the species in aquaculture despite having strong evidence of its impact on welfare, such as daily travel distance and the level of food manipulation. We deemed an additional five factors to have variable usefulness for predicting species-level risk (e.g., encephalization quotient). This left seven risk factors with both high welfare importance and high cross-species informational availability: (i) complex parental care and/or courtship, (ii) carnivory, (iii) cannibalism, (iv) solitary lifestyle, (v) migration, (vi) extreme depth, and (vii) a long lifespan. We used these seven risk factors to create a welfare-risk profile from 0 (no known welfare risks) to 7 (seven known welfare risks) for each species in the FAO data.

The FAO aquaculture data indicated that since reporting began in 1950, 488 new species have been subjected to domestication or farming attempts on top of the “baseline” 51 species that were reported in 1950. Of the total 539 species, only 397 remained in cultivation as of 2021. The other 142 species were farmed for a median duration of 6 years before being abandoned or halted. Moreover, 56 of the 397 currently farmed species were farmed for the first time in the most recent year (2021) and roughly a quarter (100) were farmed for the first time in the most recent 20 years (i.e., since 2011; data S3).

The chordate grouping had risks that spanned all seven welfare risk factors, with a range of 0 to 6 known risks per species and a group average of 2.78 (95% confidence interval, 2.65 to 2.92) known welfare risks. Crustaceans also had risks that spanned all seven welfare risk factors, with a range of 0 to 6 risks and a group average of 2.60 (2.25 to 2.95) known welfare risks. Overall, the mollusk grouping had a range of 0 to 6 risks and an average of 1.29 (0.31 to 2.26) known welfare risks. The common cuttlefish (Sepia officinalis), a cephalopod mollusk, has 6 known welfare risks: cannibalism, migration, complex courtship, solitary life stage, carnivory, and depth. The other invertebrates grouping had a range of 1 to 2 risks with an average of 0.8 (0.52 to 1.08) welfare risks. All species in the aquatic plants grouping were inferred to have no known direct welfare risk factors (Fig. 1).

DISCUSSION

Analysis of the FAO data revealed that aquaculture is continuing to diversify even after its exceptional growth was flagged 5 years ago (2), with 56 newly reported species in 2021 alone. For the species in aquaculture, our research identified seven reliable welfare risk factors—complex parental care or courtship, carnivory, cannibalism, solitary lifestyle, migration, extreme depth, and a long lifespan—that were unevenly distributed across the major groups. Chordates and crustaceans were found to carry substantial risk overall and extreme risk for some species within these groupings, whereas the other invertebrate and mollusk groupings tended to contain fewer welfare risks in general. However, cephalopod mollusks were identified as having substantially greater welfare risks than other mollusks and were comparable in their welfare risk profile to the most at-risk chordates and crustaceans. Aquatic plants were assumed to have no direct welfare risks across all categories.

Welfare risks were positively related to reported price per tonne, yet according to our tonnage findings, species with greater welfare risks (three or more) contributed less to overall global production compared to species with fewer welfare risks (two or fewer). These results indicate that the aquaculture industry could reduce animal welfare risks without compromising affordability and accessibility in global food production.

Popular discourse sometimes suggests that higher ethical standards for farmed animals are an elitist endeavor: that prioritizing welfare is economically unattainable for most individuals, hindering the incorporation of animal welfare considerations into agricultural policy. The results presented here challenge that notion, in particular in aquaculture. While improved welfare standards for farmed animals may be associated with increased costs in some cases and within the same species, we find that comparing across the aquaculture industry as a whole, this perception is unfounded. The inverse is true: Species with lower sources of welfare risk are significantly more affordable than species with major sources of welfare risk.

While the positive association between price and welfare risk was not predicted a priori, one possible driver may be variations in species complexity. Species complexity can manifest in various ways, for instance, as (i) high motility such as extensive migratory patterns versus sessility, (ii) involved parenting and mating behaviors versus asexual reproduction, and (iii) predatory or active foraging behavior versus autotrophy or filter feeding. These complex behaviors, needs, and physiology not only pose welfare risks for the species in question but are also likely to be more resource-intensive to address (even minimally for survival and growth) in artificial environments. Whether in terms of human resources (e.g., research effort, technical knowledge, and skills in animal husbandry and management), infrastructure (e.g., sophisticated enclosures and equipment, transport, and maintenance machinery), and natural resources (e.g., animal-based feed as opposed to single cell organisms or sunlight), cultivating these species likely incur additional burdens and challenges that result in higher operating costs.

Notably, lower welfare risk species can also be nutrient and protein rich. Seaweeds (40) and bivalves (41) have been proposed as alternative protein sources for their minimal environmental impact and affordability. Future research examining the intersection of these dimensions would add greatly to the ongoing conversation around aquaculture and projections about it expansion. Directing cultivation efforts to inherently lower-risk species in aquaculture could mean positive wins for both economic accessibility and overall farmed animal welfare.

The current aquaculture landscape is characterized by rapid change, suggesting that scientific research, civic engagement, and policy can wield substantial influence in steering the industry’s development (42). For instance, aquaculture cultivation methods are still undergoing research and testing (43). Simultaneously, efforts are being made to stoke demand for newly introduced species (44, 45), signaling a lack of existing consumer tastes. These factors indicate that corporate political influence and other economic considerations may be less entrenched in the aquaculture sector compared to terrestrial animal agriculture, in particular for emerging species. Given these dynamics, the findings presented here underscore the potential for diverse sectors to collaboratively shape the future of aquaculture and suggest three key implications.

First, in the realm of aquaculture research, a shift from reactive animal welfare research (i.e., accepting cultivation as a given and seeking modifications within the system) to proactive animal welfare research (i.e., discovering and flagging high risk species as poor candidates for domestication efforts) could help in leading the industry forward in a more ethical way. A proactive, animal-welfare first approach considers basic knowledge about species characteristics and autonomous behavior as foundational to assessing and protecting their well-being (46). In aquaculture, however, we identified several important species-level welfare considerations—natural home range, feeding habits and behaviors, intelligence, or behavioral plasticity—that appear to be extremely understudied for most species currently being farmed and targeted for domestication.

Filling in the gaps of this sort of knowledge could facilitate a fundamental rethinking of how to minimize the welfare risks in aquaculture. In a proactive welfare approach, documenting and weighing the importance of species-level welfare risk factors under natural conditions would be the first line of protection against negative welfare outcomes rather than determining how, or the extent to which, these factors may or may not lead to actual welfare harms once the animals are already in production systems.

To illustrate, the red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii; see Fig. 5A) engages in remarkable parental care that lasts 3 to 4 months (47). A female crayfish will actively search for her young if separated or disturbed and will call to them by elevating her body to reveal her pleopods, a vulnerable position only exhibited by maternal females, as a sign for her juveniles to return (48).

Reactive (versus proactive) welfare research might be interested in determining whether crayfish engaging in extended parental care experience welfare harms if offspring are separated early from parents. Researchers might ask if crayfish welfare could be improved through artificial proximity to their young or if the act of caring (fanning, cleaning, and feeding their young) is in itself rewarding for parents. The vast scope and welfare risks identified in our literature review suggest that this type of piecemeal, reactive research is unlikely to serve the multifaceted, diverse, and extensive demands of the current situation.

Instead, going forward, most current and targeted aquaculture species require proactive animal welfare approaches to determine whether sufficient welfare risks are present to warrant excluding the species from further cultivation efforts. While this approach may face formidable economic realities, the shift to proactive welfare protection has an important role to play in the policies and ongoing debate about the expansion of aquaculture. An example of a proactive approach is the recent ban in octopus farming in a jurisdiction in the United States (Washington State) (49) and a proposed national ban (50), in part because of welfare concerns as many species of octopus are primarily solitary and carnivorous, with the potential to be cannibalistic, and demonstrate high levels of behavioral flexibility and cognitive complexity (51, 52).

Second, the reliability and utility of research addressing aquaculture expansion will benefit from foregrounding the heterogeneity of the industry and considering a broad spectrum of trade-offs with intensified specificity. In particular, the welfare conditions and experiences of different species in farming vary markedly and merit consideration in decisions about policy and resource investment. Rather than extending global effort to domesticate species with inherent characteristics incompatible with farming (51, 52), resources can be channeled to identifying species that meet the nexus of low animal welfare risk, sustainability, and food efficiency (41). Accounting for the specific life-history details of the species involved in aquaculture is an important component of disaggregating the potential benefits and harms of the industry. Such close attention to life-history details may also flag risk to food security and environmental footprints. For instance, species that are long-lived (or slow-growing), have complex or high-energy dietary requirements, require large spaces to thrive, or are otherwise difficult to maintain, are unlikely to support these goals.

Consider, for example, the bumphead parrotfish (Bolbometopon muricatum; Fig. 5B), the largest in the parrotfish (Scaridae) family with a natural lifespan of up to 40 years (53). Parrotfish are grazers that forage on benthic organisms (e.g., corals and epithilic algae) (54). Bumphead parrotfish in particular have a diet composed of up to 50% live coral (54) and forage in large social groups over a distance of several kilometers throughout the day (up to 6 km on record) (55). They exhibit complex social behaviors such as aggregating in the hundreds during the full moon for spawning and engaging in ritual head-bumping among males (53). Ongoing initiatives to cultivate the bumphead parrotfish purport “to provide food security” (56) while aiding conservation efforts (57). Setting aside that raising fish for food is a fundamentally different project than raising fish for eventual release (one optimizes for production efficiency while the other for survival in the wild) (58), specific life-history details of bumphead parrotfish as long-lived, wide ranging species with niche dietary and foraging requirements indicate that they are an impractical species to cultivate for food. Current research is focused on shifting their natural feeding behavior from grazing on coral and algae to soy-based protein and wild fishmeal (57). In addition to (and potentially because of) these inefficiencies, farmed bumphead parrotfish are consumed by premium niche markets.

Attention to such specific characteristics and welfare needs of the species is needed to inform effective decision-making. Doing so at the species level is particularly salient for aquaculture considering its vast taxonomic diversity. While our work shows that mollusks as a phyla generally have lower risks relative to chordates and crustacea, existing knowledge indicates that cephalopod mollusks are particularly poorly suited to farmed conditions (51, 52). This further highlights the need to give serious consideration to species-level characteristics and their attendant welfare needs.

Third, on a broader scale, part of the challenge in aquaculture decision-making and policy is that public understanding of aquatic animals generally underappreciates their profound diversity. To begin with, the language to describe aquatic animals (e.g., a single word, “fish,” to refer to 30,000+ species of finned vertebrates, broadly construed) undermines the rich complexity in behavior, motivation, and life history that exists (59). While the seven broad categories we identified as welfare risk factors is a step toward disaggregating these differences, much complexity remains to be captured. Research and advocacy have an important role to play in terms of bringing the diversity of welfare risks into clear relief. Intentionally highlighting specifics about particular species can help promote a more accurate picture of the aquatic lives under consideration for proposed cultivation efforts or expansion.

For instance, the tiger puffer (Takifugu rubripes; Fig. 5C) and the burbot (Lota lota; Fig. 5D) are both the targets of current farming efforts and are also both bottom-dwellers. The nature of their relationship to the substrate, however, plays substantial differences in their life. Wild tiger puffers spend 6 to 13 hours of their daily activity burrowing under substrates at 11 to 33 meters below sea surface level (60). Their burrowing patterns are dependent on individual personalities and shifts throughout the seasons (60). On the other hand, burbot has been reported to create biogenic structures as deep as 351 m below freshwater lakes, constructing trenches that are many times the length of their own body and the full purpose of which still remains contested three decades after their initial discovery (61, 62).

Examining species-specific characteristics and their attendant welfare risks emphasizes the nuanced and multidimensional aspects of species needs. In turn, this knowledge can inform assessments of our likely capability to domesticate a species successfully and in a more ethical way. This proactive approach is especially important in light of the rapidity and regularity at which new species are now targeted for cultivation in the aquaculture space (45) and, more recently, the terrestrial animal agricultural space as well (63, 64).

For aquaculture to become a sustainable industry, aquaculture research and its related fields can learn from terrestrial agriculture research and the need to emphasize a disaggregation of species, production methods, and a host of other societal and economic parameters in a complex space of potential symbioses and trade-offs. Recognizing the multidimensionality of the disaggregation space is essential, involving accessibility (such as niche upscale markets versus subsistence farming), sustainability (including environmental impacts), nutritional profile (such as protein content), and animal welfare (highlighting species-level welfare risks in domestic environments) to name a few. These dimensions are not exhaustive or determinative, but in articulating and identifying animal welfare risks in aquaculture, the research presented here highlights the need for aquaculture disaggregation more generally and the novel strategies that are required to address animal welfare concerns in the 21st century.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Species identification

We used the FAO of the United Nations’ comprehensive database (FishStatJ) on global aquaculture production since 1950, extracting listings on production quantities and values. The FAO uses the Aquatic Sciences and Fisheries Information System (ASFIS) for coding common names, which is a unique identifier for each species, hybrid, or aggregation of species not individually listed. We matched each code with its scientific name from the FAO’s “CL_FI_SPECIES_GROUPS” file, resulting in 709 unique ASFIS codes which had at least 1 year with a positive production quantity. We retained only those entries with specific species-level identification, by omitting listings with “spp,” “nei,” or were more or less than two words. This refinement resulted in 553 species-level listings.

We validated each listing to update FAO’s original listing name to the scientific name accepted on Fishbase (38) and Sealifebase (39). In total, we changed 44 FAO listing names to the official species name recognized in Fishbase or Sealifebase. For two species, FAO provides two distinct listings: (i) the species Evynnis tumifrons is listed by FAO as both Evynnis japonica and Dentex tumifrons and (ii) the species Magallana bilineata is listed by FAO as both Crassostrea madrasensis and Crassostrea iredalei. We omitted all four of these FAO listings from consideration because they were not identified to a recognized species with a 1:1 specificity. Two additional FAO listings, Cherax cainii and Ex Unionidae, were also omitted because they produced multiple potential matches on Sealifebase (39). After resolving these discrepancies, we were left with 539 unique, validated species with at least 1 year of a positive production output from 1950 through 2021 for our analyses. For the complete list of species included for analysis, see data S1.

FAO categorizes all species by “major group” as follows: Pisces (356 species; classes Teleostei and Chondrostei), Amphibia and Reptilia (5 species; classes Amphibia and Reptilia), Crustacea, hereafter crustaceans, (53 species; classes Branchiopoda and Malacostraca), Mollusca, hereafter mollusks (85 species; classes Bivalvia, Gastropoda, and Cephalopoda), Invertebrata aquatica, hereafter other invertebrates (7 species; classes Ascidiacea, Holothuroidea, Scyphozoa, and Echinoidea), and Plantae aquaticae, hereafter aquatic plants (33 species; spanning 11 classes listed in data S2). Major group Pisces and major group Amphibia and Reptilia were combined into “chordates” for analysis purposes.

Welfare risk factors

We conducted a comprehensive review of literature spanning captive animal welfare, aquatic animal welfare, and domestication to identify a range of life-history characteristics that likely pose as welfare risk factors under captive conditions. Here, we define “characteristic” as any behavioral or physiological trait that may be linked to an increased risk of poor welfare in captivity. The literature search was conducted using Google Scholar, using combinations of keywords “domestication,” “farmed animal,” “captive animal,” “welfare,” “characteristics,” and “traits.” Many characteristics had overlaps and were potential risk factors of the same category. Consolidating these potential risk factors where possible, 18 distinct risk factors were identified, reflected by the total number of rows in Table 1. We organized these distinct risk factors into seven categories: general captivity exposure (two factors), indirect welfare impact (two factors), ranging behavior (four factors), social behavior (three factors), behavioral plasticity (two factors), reproduction and parental behavior (two factors), and food consumption behavior and dietary needs (three factors).

All welfare risk factors identified by comprehensive literature review.

We identified 18 potential welfare risk factors for consideration. We organized those factors into six broad categories of direct risk and one category of indirect risk. For each factor, we evaluated the evidence for its importance to predicting species-level welfare (ranging from low to high) and the accessibility of species-level information for the species recorded in FAO aquaculture database (ranging from low to high). Of the 18 original factors, seven factors (demarcated with bold font in the table) met the criteria of being both highly important to welfare and highly accessible across species.

To identify characteristics that are feasible for species-by-species evaluation, we assigned each distinct risk factor with an estimated level (from low to high) of (i) importance to welfare and (ii) informational accessibility. We ranked the importance to welfare through the following heuristic: Risk factors with strong logical validity and empirical evidence and/or several independent researchers/studies supporting the connection were marked as “high,” while risk factors that are logically reasonable with good empirical evidence and/or at least two independent researcher groups/studies supporting the connect and/or had mixed support were marked as “medium,” and last, risk factors that are logically plausible but with weak, mixed, or little empirical evidence and/or only one research group supporting the connection were marked as “low.” We ranked informational accessibility as high if it met the following conditions: (i) is straightforward to quantify or otherwise compare (i.e., there is minimal to no disagreement on the collection and interpretation of this risk factor) and (ii) is either available on Fishbase or an equivalent database or has been systematically studied across taxa. Risk factors were marked medium for informational accessibility if it meets only one condition. Risk factors were marked as low for informational accessibility if it meets neither condition.

We selected characteristics considered to be most relevant to welfare and most data-rich across all taxa for further analysis. In total, this procedure resulted in seven factors to collect information on a species-by-species basis (bolded rows in Table 1).

Species-by-species data collection

We collected species-level information for the 539 FAO listings according to the subset of characteristics listed in Table 1. We collected information from Fair-Fish-Database (26) where available, as it is the most reliable database of welfare-relevant information openly accessible. Otherwise, we supplemented missing data using the R package rfishbase (version 4.1.2) (38), literature reviews or experimental papers available on web of Science and Google Scholar, and hobbyist and traditional knowledge, in that order (see the Supplementary Materials for species references; data S2). For all characteristics, we marked species as high risk if there was positive evidence of meeting the criteria as defined in Table 2. Where available, we collected upper and lower estimates for the number of individuals per species per tonne from the Supplementary Materials sheet from Franks et al. (27).

Data processing and analysis

To situate species-level welfare risks within the broader aquaculture industry, we analyzed production and value trends provided by the FAO spanning from 1950 to 2021, the latest year for which data were available. We gauged the diversification of farmed species over time by comparing the annual count of validated species listings with positive production quantities reported to the FAO for all years. We calculated the number of years of attempted farming by summing the years each listing reported a positive production quantity, while listings without production quantities as of 2021 were considered unsuccessful domestication attempts. To evaluate the economic value of each species, we added global production quantities (in tonnes or metric ton) and value (USD in 1000) for the most recent production year for each species. Listing GAZ, which was identified as Portunus trituberculatus, was noted as NA due to the lack of reported value data from FAO. Adjusting for inflation with the R package priceR (version 1.0.1), we converted all values to US dollars using 2021 as the base year (65). To obtain price/tonne in 2021 US dollars, we divided the inflation-adjusted dollars by the global production quantity.

We treated species as the unit of analysis throughout our statistical modeling and only included animal species in our statistical models (although we retained plants in the plots for the sake of comparison). To test whether welfare risks differed between animal taxonomic groups (chordates, crustaceans, mollusks, and other invertebrates; n = 506), we conducted Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn-Bonferroni post hoc corrections for multiple pairwise comparisons. To evaluate whether welfare risk was associated with higher prices, we regressed the number of welfare risks for each animal species against the log of our estimation of the price/tonne in 2021 US dollars. We then evaluated the welfare risk–price relationship within each animal grouping: chordates, crustaceans, mollusks, and other invertebrates.

We conducted all data manipulation, plotting, and statistical analyses with R (66) in rStudio (67), using the R package tidyverse (68).
