A scoping review of equity toolkits for international academic partnerships
Authors: Chelsea E Modlin, Prakriti Shrestha, Larry W Chang, Joseph Ali, Nelson K Sewankambo, Chizoba Wonodi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhen there are frank asymmetries in power, resources, and capacity efforts to advance academic collaboration are essential for the conduct of ethical partnership-based health research, education, and policymaking. Numerous toolkits are available that provide structured templates for academic partnerships and other key stakeholders to systematically evaluate and reflect on how their practices enhance or inhibit equity. However, there is no comparative analysis available to examine similarities and differences between the multitude of toolkits available.MethodWe conducted a comprehensive scoping review of international partnership equity toolkits across the domains of global health research, education, and practice in four academic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and WHO Regional Indexes) and grey literature. The search took place June through August 2023. A primary search used broad keyword combinations followed by a secondary search of toolkit titles identified during the primary search. PRISMA extension for scoping reviews was followed. Key themes, motivations, development processes, application practices, and uses of these toolkits were examined by a combination of (1) templated extraction of data and (2) applying an inductive and iterative coding approach by two independent reviewers.ResultsWithin the academic databases, 7580 abstracts were screened (7580 primary, 198 secondary), 120 documents underwent full-text review (112 primary, 8 secondary) and 27 articles (26 primary, 1 secondary) met inclusion criteria. Within the grey literature, 104 articles and webpages (40 primary, 64 secondary) met inclusion criteria. Seventeen toolkits were identified. The majority were geared toward evaluating research (n = 15) rather than education (n = 2) or practice (n = 0) partnerships. Toolkits covered a broad range of objectives, development methods, and target audiences. Twenty-three countries were represented in the creation of the toolkits; however, less than half (8/17, 47%) of toolkits had representation from low- or low/middle-income countries. We identified six broad categories within the toolkits - oversight, partnership dynamics, ethical foundation, contextual factors, partnership procedures and activities, and capacity. Under these, themes such as stakeholder engagement, communication, partnership outputs, clarification of roles and responsibilities, funding, and management of data or other products generated by the partnership were common.ConclusionThere was variability in the development, intended audience, and application methods of the toolkits, yet there was also substantial thematic content overlap. Limited information on the application or use of toolkits is available. To date, there is no data on whether these toolkits resulted in equity-oriented changes in partnership practices or policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02632-x.Keywords: International partnerships, Global health, Research ethics, Equity

Introduction

Global health challenges transcend national borders and require collaborative, multinational solutions. International partnerships within health research, education, practice, or some combination of these focus areas, have emerged as a prominent strategy for improving health outcomes worldwide [1]. Often these international partnerships form between structurally unequal partners and institutions, with one or more partners situated in well-resourced parts of the world while others are in under-resourced areas, most often to address health issues that disproportionately affect communities in lower-income settings. The authors use the term ‘partners’ here to describe a variety of stakeholders, including researchers, educators, academic institutions, non-governmental organizations, and private sector organizations, who collaborate within a similar domain in global health activities. Each partner can bring complementary strengths and weaknesses while the act of collaboration provides an opportunity to pool resources and achieve outcomes that otherwise might not be possible.

However, the global structures that have generated inequities between partners and institutions can also be reflected in the inputs, processes, and outcomes of these partnerships [2–6]. Despite a foundation of collaboration, more recent scrutiny of academic partnerships has found that they can perpetuate, rather than eliminate, asymmetries in power, finances, resources, and academic recognition. Examples include inequities in funding allocation and resources for independent revenue [7], representation in the products of academic collaboration including authorship on manuscripts [8–11] or presenting at academic conferences [12], access to and ownership of data and intellectual property [13, 14] and the undue burdens of hosting students from high-income countries during rotations based in low- and low-/middle-income countries [15, 16].

With increasing recognition of the asymmetries global health partnerships can inadvertently maintain, a multitude of guidelines [17], principles [18–20], and recommendations [21, 22] for promoting equitable partnership practices have been published. These are mostly in multinational health research, although guidelines for health education partnerships are also available [23]. There is no consensus on a clear definition of global health partnership equity, but recent thematic reviews provide an amalgamation of topics that fall under the umbrella of partnership equity [24–26]. The authors here consider partnership equity to be the prioritization of mutually beneficial inputs, processes, outputs, and impact, including a pluralistic view on what ‘benefit’ means to stakeholders who have different backgrounds, values, and objectives.

While guidelines are important, operationalization of these principles and recommendations is key for eliciting actual changes to practice. However, this also poses significant challenges given the breadth and depth of issues that fall under partnership equity compounded by unique and specific considerations required for individual partnerships. Adding to the complexity is the variety of ways these partnerships can paradoxically both prevent and promote mutually beneficial health and academic agendas for both partners [27, 28], making it neither possible nor appropriate to identify a threshold that declares a partnership as ‘equitable’ or ‘inequitable.’

The need for operationalization has resulted in several structured equity-focused toolkits designed to evaluate partnership practices. While thematic comparisons of equity guidelines and principles have been conducted, a review of structured equity toolkits is not available. These toolkits differ in their approaches, methods, content, and intended audience making it difficult for global health stakeholders to identify which might best serve their needs. This scoping review aims to fill that gap and serve as a resource for those interested in taking a closer look at their partnership equity practices. Specifically, we aimed to identify and systematically review information on available global health partnership equity toolkits to answer the following questions:

Materials and methods

Given the broad range of research questions being asked, the interest in concept mapping, and intent to summarize and disseminate current findings, a scoping review method was selected over other review types [29]. This study was exempt from review by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board. There was engagement with global health researchers and educators located in both well- and under-resourced parts of the world at monthly intervals to discuss the study design, data collection, data analysis, and conclusions. To the extent possible, Arksey and O’Malley guidelines for scoping review methodology [29] and PRISMA guidelines for reporting scoping reviews were followed (PRISMA-ScR) [30]. Search strategies and strings for both the primary and secondary search strategies can be found in the supplemental material. The searches were conducted between June and August 2023.

Primary search strategy

Four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and WHO Regional Indexes) were searched from their earliest inclusion date through June 2023. One search string was used for each of the three types of partnerships of interest: research, education, and practice. The initial search strategy was constructed based on key references including existing equity tools familiar to the authors, related keywords, reviewing references for relevant publications, and recommended resources that were requested from colleagues with expertise in academic global health. Grey literature was accessed through keyword searches and a review of all links displayed on a Google search. To balance inclusivity with feasibility, cutoffs of two pages, five pages, seven pages, or ten pages on the Google search engine was piloted within three of the search strings. Five was used as there was a significant drop in new webpages after these thresholds.

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts followed by a full-text review utilizing Covidence systematic review software. Document types included primary empirical research, normative discussions, guidelines, policy briefs, frameworks, program reports, commentaries, editorials, editorial letters, or websites including blogs and online journal media. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Fig. 1. There were no exclusions based on document date or language. Non-English titles and abstracts were included in the review via English translations in Covidence. None of the abstracts written in languages other than English met the criteria for full-text review. During the analysis, the names of specific toolkits were identified and extracted to inform the secondary search strategy.

Summary of inclusion and exclusion criteria for document screening and a summary of coding methods and schemes for the research questions

Secondary search strategy

The same four databases were searched from their earliest inclusion date through August 2023 for any further documents specific to the toolkits identified in the primary search. Search terms were constructed based on toolkit titles and keywords. The same methods of review as the primary search strategy were used for the grey literature search, title and abstract screening, and full-text review. For toolkits with dedicated websites, the grey literature search also included reviews of these web pages and all links that were two or less clicks away from the main webpage.

Data extraction and analysis

Figure 1 outlines the two types of data extraction and analysis methods used. A templated data extraction form was used to extract the following from each document reviewed: title, author, author affiliation, name of toolkit, type of toolkit (research, education, or practice), type of document, year published, toolkit objectives, toolkit development methods, toolkit application methods, and intended audience. Data coding and analysis were facilitated by the use of NVivo 14 qualitative data analysis software (2023, Lumivero). Full-text documents were then reviewed using an inductive and iterative thematic analysis approach [31] which allowed for a systematic review of each text, absent of any preconceived framework, while also recognizing that there are diverse interpretations and complex realities of the process being investigated. Each document was reviewed by two independent coders using open coding to identify themes and classify preliminary categories grouped according to the study questions. Themes were grouped into broader categories acknowledging that there are many potential ways they could be arranged and that several themes overlap or are closely associated with one another. Following the formation of preliminary themes and categories, each document underwent a second axial coding for refinement and to identify associations and overlapping themes between categories. Coding schemes were iteratively compared between coders and revised during weekly meetings to generate the final codebook. Disagreements were resolved by reaching consensus.

Results

Search results

After duplicate removal, database searches of the academic and grey literatures that met inclusion criteria generated 66 relevant documents, 26 from full-text reviews and 40 from a citation, supplement, or website review. Seventeen partnership equity toolkits were found, 15 targeting research partnerships and two targeting education partnerships (Table 1). No toolkits focused on practice-related partnerships were identified. The secondary search of each toolkit by name yielded an additional one document from the academic literature and 64 documents from the grey literature and website reviews. High-income countries including the United States and the United Kingdom were most frequently involved in toolkit development with six toolkits each, but there was representation of at least one low- or low/middle-income country in the development process of eight out of the 17 toolkits (47%). The most frequent document type was the toolkits themselves (23%), followed by peer-reviewed articles (15%), supplementary files (13%), websites (10%), and press releases (10%). Publication dates of many documents and websites were not available, but there was an appreciable increase in the number of documents in 2022 and 2023 compared to years prior and a spike in documents published in 2018 related to the release of the Research Fairness Initiative (RFI) toolkit [32]. There were no toolkits that were implementation oriented.

What are the themes and topics included?

Collectively, the toolkits covered a diverse array of themes and topics. Broad categories included were: (1) Oversight, (2) Partnership Dynamics, (3) Ethical Foundation, (4) Contextual Factors (5) Partnership Activities and Procedures, and (6) Capacity. Oversight

This category included themes related to oversight of the partnership including logistical considerations such as bilateral ethical review of partnership activities [33–38] and having a formal institutional agreement or memorandum of understanding [34, 37–43]. Governance of the partnership was another topic that could be subcategorized as internal to the partnership (i.e. regulation by partnership leaders or staff) [37, 41, 44] versus outside of the partnership (i.e. a data safety monitoring board or continuous review from an institutional review board) [38, 39, 41, 44, 45]. Lastly, accountability for partnership equity practices was also included in this category [34, 36, 37, 42, 44, 46].

Partnership dynamics

The theme of leadership bridged both categories of oversight and partnership dynamics in that high-level management of a partnership holds significant influence over both aspects. Partnership dynamics also encompassed a variety of other facets that define relationships including trust [34, 38, 46], transparency [33]39– [35, 44]36– [38, 42], engaging with local partners [33, 35, 39, 45]44– [36]46– [38]41– [48], diversity [44, 45]47– [37], respect [43, 46], building relationships [37, 38, 44, 47]42– [43], and communication [33, 34, 39, 40, 44]47– [42, 43]. Power dynamics serves as a good example of the overlapping nature of many themes identified. ‘Power dynamics’ was often an independent theme asked about within a toolkit [41, 43, 44, 46, 47, 49], but the concept of differential power between partners permeates throughout multiple themes including leadership, access to financial and non-financial resources, publication and authorship, funding, and ownership of partnership products. The last theme in this category, establishing partnership priorities, relates to the early and continuous engagement with representatives and stakeholders from the less advantaged partner setting [33, 39, 44, 45]36– [38, 46] and was the most frequent theme identified in the toolkits.

Ethical foundation

This category brought together themes touching on broad ethical principles or frameworks including minimizing harm [33, 36–38, 41, 43], promoting benefit [33, 34, 45, 46]38– [41, 43], and prioritizing social justice [36, 44, 45]. Ethical ideals and foundations require interpretation related to local geographical and cultural contexts [34–36, 44, 45]46– [38, 43], making the theme of social justice also relevant for the next category.

Contextual factors

These themes are related to the plurality of the world and differences that arise based on variations in social, cultural, geographic, and political systems. Overlap with partnership dynamics include diversity within the partnership, engaging with local partners, and epistemic influences (i.e., how knowledge is defined, formed, and shared) [34, 35, 44, 45]36– [42]. Infrequently, toolkits reference the colonial legacies of global health and how these structures influence contemporary practices [36, 43]. Lastly, the themes of community engagement [33]45– [35, 44]36– [38, 42] and establishing partnership priorities span both geographic relevance and are directly related to the following category - partnership-specific procedures and activities.

Partnership procedures and activities

Several of the most common themes related to the pragmatic processes and activities of the partnership such as dissemination of outcomes and the potential for future innovation [33]45– [35]44– [38, 42], clarification of individual roles and responsibilities [33]39– [35]44– [38, 41, 42], data access and management [33]45– [35]44– [36–38, 41, 42], and funding [33, 39, 40, 44, 45]47– [38, 41–43]. This category also includes career development activities [33, 34]40– [38, 42, 48], intellectual property management [33, 34, 36, 39, 44]46– [38, 41], and setting standards for research activities [33–36, 38, 45].

Capacity

The final category pertains to each partner’s ability to engage and contribute to a partnership or limitations that prevent maximal impact. In addition to several themes that overlap with ‘partnership activities,’ other themes include the availability of financial [33, 34, 39, 44]47– [38, 41–43] and material [33, 34, 40, 48] resources, utilization of existing resources within under-resourced settings [33, 37, 38, 40], the longevity of the partnership [37, 38, 40, 41, 43, 44, 47], and efforts focused on building capacity for the less-resourced partner [33–35, 45]44– [38, 47].

What is the evidence for the successes, challenges, and limitations of using the toolkit?

Discussion regarding the potential benefits of using these toolkits were common, although data describing partnership changes as a result of using the toolkit were limited to qualitative feedback from toolkit users and almost exclusively available for only one of the toolkits, the RFI. Many toolkits grounded their importance and relevance to the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 17 which focuses on collaborative partnerships [32, 38, 45, 47, 50]. Others cited the universality of fairness and equity as foundational guiding principles supporting their creation [32, 34, 44]. Potential benefits of use included raising awareness of research inequities [34, 36, 38, 50], bidirectional communication and reduction in conflict [32, 43, 46, 50], increased transparency as a form of empowerment [32, 34, 38], improved reputation [32], organizational efficiency [32, 50, 51], higher competitiveness for funding [32, 41], enhanced awareness of implicit biases and assumptions [38, 46, 50], and improved partnership outcomes for less-resourced partners in particular [40, 41, 51]. Flexibility to adapt the toolkit as needed was a key feature frequently cited as a strength, for example, “research teams can use the tool in any way that suits them” (Equipar) [37] and “the tools are designed to be customised by the users” (Association of Commonwealth Universities Equitable Research Partnerships, ACU) [38]. Other toolkits highlighted how their content specifically addressed the needs of less-resourced partners and provided a more equitable platform for negotiation [34, 38, 40, 41, 51]. Despite the increasing prevalence of these equity-oriented toolkits, there was very little published comparison between them. Moreover, accountability was limited to, at most, producing a report of future or potential actions the partnership might take. There was no defined effectiveness outcomes identified, measured, or reported. Only the RFI provided examples of real-world use of their toolkit (see Supplemental Table 4 for these references) and offered context for comparison with other equity-related initiatives: “The main difference between the RFI and previous initiatives is the attempt to go beyond ‘good intentions’, as it is a compliance mechanism which builds on a growing body of evidence on all aspects related to partnerships” [52] although it is also notable that the RFI was published several years before the majority of other toolkits found in this review.

Many toolkits also included limitations in their development and scope. For the RFI, potential toolkit users reported barriers such as fear of losing reputability for making their partnership equity reporting public and difficulty meeting the time and administrative needs required [53]. The PAT describes the initial response to the toolkit of some users to be “one of surprise and some discomfort.” [50] The BRIDGE criteria found a lack of resources within a partnership could potentially limit the toolkit’s feasibility [51]. The 8Quity Tool reported that larger institutional and global structures made it difficult for toolkit users to translate identified inequities into actionable changes, concluding that “simple tools…may be helpful, even necessary, but are insufficient for the broader societal change required to address equity.” [45] The survey developed by Modlin et al. was limited by a small pilot sample size and low response rate [34] while the RRC focused on research collaboration limited to the United Kingdom [44]. Many toolkits raised the possibility of implementation barriers in other settings or parts of the world that were not involved in the toolkit’s development [34, 44, 47, 50, 51, 54]. Finally, most toolkits commented on an inability to cover all possible aspects of partnership equity, for example, “the FAIRNeSS framework does not aim to provide an all-encompassing overview of all possible indicators” [48] and, according to the Swiss Commission for Research Partnerships with Developing Countries (KFPE) “all the principles could not be universally applied to every partnership.” [42].

What are the relevant inputs and outputs that to toolkit requires?

The majority of toolkits utilized systematic literature reviews [33, 34, 39]51– [37] and/or stakeholder meetings [33, 39, 44, 51]36– [38, 42, 46] to develop their structure and content. Stakeholder meetings captured a broad spectrum of input, ranging from large multinational “exploratory consultations with 32 public and private sector organizations in 15 countries followed by a global consultation” [32] for the RFI to more localized institutional efforts such as a “series of internal organizational and external conversations involving research administrators and researchers” for the 8Quity Tool [45]. Fewer toolkits applied the personal experiences of authors and collaborators and 29% of toolkits underwent pilot testing [32, 34, 37, 45, 47].

Toolkit format, meaning how content and themes within each toolkit are structured and applied, also varied significantly. Checklist-style or discussion questions were the most common format used [33]45– [34, 44]46– [42, 43]. Often the expressed goal was to “[go] beyond a simple checklist to encourage in-depth discussion among partners at all stages” (PAT) [39] or “offer an opportunity to identify aspects of equity to be strengthened through open, non-judgmental dialogue… It needs to be reiterated that the primary intent is to flag areas that need attention such that a conversation can follow, ultimately leading to improving the partnership.” (Equity Tool for Valuing Global Health Partnerships, EQT) [46].

While no toolkit claimed to serve as a quantitative evaluation of equity, more in-depth mechanisms of evaluation such as interviews [33, 34, 38], document reviews [33], or stakeholder meetings [33, 38, 39, 46] were much less frequent than surveys and checklists. Two toolkits, 8Quity [45] and Equipar [37] directly linked to other partnership equity-related resources. The RFI [32] was the only toolkit that advocated for generating a tangible report as part of the equity-centered evaluation although other toolkits such as the PAT [39] and toolkit created by Modlin et al. [34] suggest that toolkit outputs can be used to generate an internal report to help document progress.

Information regarding the pragmatic application of the toolkit within real-world partnerships remains limited, except for the RFI for which there are at least seven publicly available reports of toolkit use. Responsibility for how and when a toolkit is administered is left up to the partnership themselves with recommendations generally only going as far as designating at least one person to compile results and involving partnership leadership in championing use of the toolkit [33, 34, 39, 44]. Similarly, the amount of time and resources needed to implement each toolkit was rarely reported and seemed to vary depending on the intended audience and level of in-depth or qualitative responses included. The RFI [33], EQT [46], Equipar [37], and survey developed by Modlin et al. [34] suggested using the toolkit at regular intervals to monitor progress and changes over time. The scalability of the toolkit and plan for dissemination to a wider array of partnerships were rarely addressed beyond discussion on how further implementation was needed.

Intended audience

Toolkits varied in their target audiences, a breakdown of which can be seen in the supplemental material. Most toolkits defined at least two specific groups (researchers, partnerships, institutions, governments, etc.) but the RFI [32], Partnership Assessment Tool (PAT) [50], and the Bridging Research Integrity and Global health Epidemiology (BRIDGE) [35] toolkits also included broader audience definitions. Examples include “anyone who engages as actor or funder of research and research partnerships” (RFI) or “all other parties involved in research partnerships” (PAT). Only the Rethinking Research Collaborative (RRC) [44] designed separate toolkits for different stakeholders. Other toolkits instead recommended adapting their content as appropriate for differing groups [34, 38, 39, 44, 46].

Most toolkits targeted global health research or education partnerships regardless of partnership focus, however three were developed for specific areas. The BRIDGE toolkit, while designed for “stakeholders involved in the commissioning, conduct, appraisal and publication of global health research” targets epidemiology research [35, 51], the Dementia Charter of Conduct pertains to dementia research [47], and the Framework for the Assessment of InteRNational Surgical Success (FAIRNeSS) tool is relevant for surgeons [48].

Discussion

Global health partnerships are a cornerstone approach to addressing many of the world’s health inequities. These partnerships can be beneficial – by advancing bi-directional scientific knowledge and global health innovation – but partnership practices can also risk being harmful, by perpetuating intellectual, scientific, and structural inequalities. A commitment to promoting more equitable partnership practices, including bilateral partner input and mutually beneficial processes and outputs, offers a promising approach for improving partnership outcomes and innovation. There is an abundance of partnership equity guidelines, but it is structured toolkits that aim to operationalize these principles into practice through systematic assessment. Growing academic interest in this area has led to an appreciable increase in the number of toolkits available within the past five years. Already, there has been at least one toolkit published [55] since the time of our scoping review with more, such as the Decolonizing Global Health Toolkit from the Emory Global Health Institute [56], in development. Given the proliferation of toolkits available, especially within the past few years, this review aimed to synthesize and compare the structure, content, and intended audience for existing research partnership equity toolkits so that researchers, research partnerships, institutions, and sponsors may access a tool that best suits their needs and interests.

The themes and topics identified in this scoping review overlapped with every theme identified in reviews of research partnership equity guidelines [24–26], suggesting close content similarity between guidelines and more operational toolkits. The broad range of themes helps highlight the spectrum of stakeholders that can contribute to inequities within a partnership or be affected by it. For example, partnership funding was a common topic. At an individual and partnership level there can be agreements about how funding is allocated but there is less control over how and when funding for a partnership is available, which is typically at the discretion of international funding agencies. These agencies, most often situated in well-resourced areas of the world, not only frequently establish the research topic or agenda, but sometimes place limitations on how funds are allocated or how partnership resources – like data – are utilized [7, 57–59]. Any attempt to fully investigate how partnership funding can be more equitably accessed and distributed cannot be solved by discussions or evaluations at the partnership level and must include evaluation of global health funders as well.

Similarly, authorship of products and papers produced by a partnership is a common theme. There is a well-documented lack of representation of investigators from under-resourced settings as lead authors of research conducted in their local communities [8–11, 60–62]. While there is an interpersonal and professional aspect to determining who achieves authorship at the partnership level, there are also systemic factors at the institutional level (capacity, mentorship, fostering early career development, and access to material and financial resources needed for publication) and systems level (lack of global representation within global health editorial boards, journal biases toward lead authors from certain institutions or countries, publishing in English as a ‘universal’ scientific language) that can influence the ability to independently generate publications as a lead author [61]. For toolkits mostly designed to be implemented at the individual or partnership level, interfacing with these larger structures is an appreciable gap in implementing meaningful change.

Given the broad range of topics and systems that can influence equity, it is not surprising that the concept of global health partnership equity is not confined to a strict definition, set of principles, or series of topics. This ambiguity was apparent in the toolkits, which often intersected with themes and principles from health equity [44], social justice [45], and community engagement [32, 36] – overlapping but arguably different areas of global health ethics and practice. This raises a question of scope when considering what constitutes a topic or indicator of partnership equity. Conversely, there were few toolkits that directly referenced decolonial theory [40], anti-racism, and gender equality [45], with minimal measurement of how historical inequities influence contemporary partnership structures and power dynamics between partners, despite this being one of the core motivations for developing several of the toolkits. More broadly, while equity is the most common value drawn upon within guidelines and toolkits, there are also contemporary discussions and debates about other values that also meaningfully contribute such as solidarity [63, 64], reciprocity [65, 66] and dignity [67, 68].

Regarding the challenges and limitations of using these toolkits, accountability and responsibility for toolkit-driven outcomes are loosely defined and most toolkits offer little guidance on how they should be implemented. Conversely, outcomes may become of more interest as the need to demonstrate partnership equity becomes a more prominent metric. Influential entities such as academic journals [69, 70], health ministries [71], and research funders [57, 72] are increasingly requiring partnerships to exhibit how they employ equitable practices and promote the advancement of priorities and capacity of under-resourced areas. The availability of widely accepted metrics or outcomes may facilitate this process. However, a move towards indicators and monitoring equity-related outcomes also requires close attention to ensure these toolkits and their outcomes disrupt, rather than propagate, systemic structures that contribute to health system inequities.

Similarly, implementation of these toolkits has not been systematic or well-described. The one exception is feedback on the RFI toolkit, although completion reports are limited in number and still do not reflect any actual changes to partnership practices. In many ways, how the toolkit results are interpreted is just as important as the results themselves. For example, where the processes and functionality of international partnerships have been evaluated and reported, Plamondon et al. conducted a critical interpretive analysis and concluded that these evaluations still tend to highlight the contributions of partners from well-resourced settings and emphasize the performance of the partners from under-resourced settings as a core area for improvement [3]. One possible interpretation of this is a representation of how entrenched contextual norms still influence well-intentioned efforts and inadvertently perpetuate inequitable conclusions. Without evidence for their efficacy or effectiveness to date, there is no current standard by which an outcome can be attributed to use of these toolkits. Any trend towards operationalization raises important questions about defining and using equity metrics and which indicators are quantifiable versus which need to remain qualitative to retain the complex nuances of partnership inequities.

There was significant variation in how each toolkit was created, how it was designed to be implemented, and who the toolkit was developed for, with some broadly focused on any type of international research collaboration while others targeted specific disease processes or populations. Many toolkits relied on systematic literature review as a primary source of topics and given most of these toolkits have emerged within the past few years, it raises an interesting question about the diversity of references and funding sources that contributed to each toolkit that our group is currently investigating via bibliometric and funder mapping. If the same set of references, particularly those written by investigators from well-resourced areas, are informing multiple toolkits that subsequently inform further guidelines, practices, and tools, this may serve as an example of epistemic injustice in academic global health. Attempts to define and systematically operationalize equity result in a conceptualization that ends up ignoring those from under-resourced backgrounds or groups whose experience does not align with the growing consensus of what research partnership equity means. This is particularly important as several toolkits cited a lack of engagement with stakeholders from under-resourced populations as one of their major limitations. Efforts to characterize what the broader area of ‘global health ethics’ entails have similarly identified limited engagement with scholars and representatives from low- and low-/middle-income countries as an epistemic issue [73].

Limitations

The similarity in themes between these toolkits and research partnership equity guidelines is likely influenced by the overrepresentation of research partnership-related toolkits in this review and topics that are more specific to research such as ownership of data and intellectual property. Themes more relevant to education and practice partnership such as exceeding the scope of ability or training [74] are not reflected in this dataset. Other limitations of this scoping review include that it relied on the academic literature with support from a grey literature search, the latter of which was only conducted using Google search. This may have limited the types of toolkits that contributed to the analysis and excluded toolkits published via other mechanisms, such as an institutional white paper or a webpage written in a language other than English since the search terms were in English although the search did not exclude any abstract based on language.

Recommendations and future research

Currently, the assumption that use of these toolkits results in the strengthening of equitable partnership practices is not much more than a hypothesis. Data is needed to support or refute the presumption that these toolkits have any meaningful impact. This requires researchers, partnerships, and institutions who use these toolkits be willing to commit to iterative evaluation and change in partnerships practices, share their experiences, and find a balance with pragmatic concerns about publicizing partnership-specific challenges. This review also found that many toolkits reiterate many of the same themes and are designed to be adaptable to various settings. Rather than continuing to develop more toolkits, future research should move toward implementation, efficacy, adaptability, scalability, and comparability. Other recommendations include more meaningful involvement of larger, powerful stakeholders such as financial sponsors and academic journals to delineate and justify expectations and accountability for the promotion of more equitable partnership practices. Broader questions for consideration include: Can comparisons between partnerships be made, or will this lead to an inappropriate use of toolkits to ‘rank’ partnerships? To what standards do we hold partnerships accountable, and who enforces those standards or holds researchers, partnerships, and institutions responsible? How realistic is it to focus on partnership processes when there are other actors who influence global health research equity such as institutional leadership or policy, funders, journals, and governments? Should similar processes be demanded of them and are some these toolkits equipped to function in that capacity? Hopefully, these questions will begin to be addressed as several of these toolkits transition to pilot studies and scaled-up implementation.

Conclusion

Toolkits generated to evaluate and improve equitable inputs, processes, and outcomes of global health partnerships have a wide array of development processes, application methods, and intended audiences. The content covers a broad range of topics which overlap considerably with partnership equity guidelines. If toolkits are to be successful, there is a need for a clearer conceptualization of the layers of accountability and responsibility for equity-related outcomes including contextualizing the relationships between a partnership, partner institutions, and larger global health structures like funders and national governments. It remains to be seen as to whether the operationalization of these toolkits results in a positive impact on partnership practices.
