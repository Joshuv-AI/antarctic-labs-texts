XRE-type transcriptional regulator ProR controls prodigiosin synthesis in Serratia marcescens JNB5-1
Authors: Mi Tang, Yanan Hao, Yixuan Zhao, Yanan Li, Jiajia You, Taowei Yang, Xuewei Pan, Zhiming Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gram-negative bacterium Serratia marcescens produces various secondary metabolites, including prodigiosin, althiomycin, and serratamolide. Among these, prodigiosin exhibits multiple biological activities such as antibacterial, anticancer, and immunosuppressive properties. Nevertheless, our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens remains incomplete. Here, an HTH_XRE family transcription factor, BVG90_RS12600 (designated ProR), was identified as a positive regulator of prodigiosin production. Disruption of proR drastically reduced prodigiosin yield, with the ΔproR mutant producing only 2.68% of that produced by the wild-type strain JNB5-1. Integrated analysis employing comparative transcriptomics, RT-qPCR, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA), and transcriptional fusion reporters elucidated the molecular mechanism whereby ProR regulates prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens. Mechanistically, ProR binds to the promoter region of psrA, which encodes a key transcriptional activator of prodigiosin synthesis, thereby upregulating PsrA expression. This, in turn, enhances transcription of the prodigiosin biosynthetic (pig) operon, establishing ProR as a positive regulator in the prodigiosin biosynthesis pathway. Our findings provide a framework for systematically elucidating the complex regulatory network governing prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens and for rational engineering of optimized microbial chassis for its overproduction.Keywords: XRE-Type transcriptional regulator, Prodigiosin synthesis, Regulatory mechanism, Serratia marcescens

Introduction

Prodigiosin (PG), the primary red linear tripyrrole pigment in the prodiginine family, has garnered considerable attention recently owing to its broad spectrum of bioactivities, including antimalarial, antibacterial, antifungal, antiprotozoal, and immunosuppression [1]. This compound is predominantly biosynthesized by Serratia marcescens [2], Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 [3], Serratia rubidaea [4], Streptomyces coelicolor [5], Streptomyces griseoviridis [6], and Serratia nematodiphila [7], with S. marcescens and Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 being the most extensively studied system for prodigiosin biosynthesis [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, the limited understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying prodigiosin biosynthesis may partially hinder the advancement of fermentative prodigiosin synthesis. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate and elucidate the molecular mechanisms how novel regulatory factors influence prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens, thereby laying a solid foundation for guiding the construction of high-efficiency S. marcescens cell factories for prodigiosin production.

The biosynthesis of prodigiosin in S. marcescens originates from a 14-gene operon (pigA-N) and diverges into two convergent biosynthetic streams. The first, governed by pigB, pigD, and pigE, transforms 2-octenal into the pyrrole intermediate 2-methyl-3-n-amyl-pyrrole (MAP). Concurrently, a suite of genes (pigA, pigH, pigI, pigJ, pigM, and pigN) facilitates the synthesis of the bipyrrole unit 4-methoxy-2,2′-bipyrrole-5-carbaldehyde (MBC) from l-proline. These two streams converge through the action of PigC (pigC), which catalyzes the final coupling to form prodigiosin [1]. Additionally, studies have identified multiple transcriptional regulators in S. marcescens that directly or indirectly modulate prodigiosin biosynthesis, including positive regulators OxyR [16], OmpR [17], EepR [18], PigP [19], and FlhDC [20], and negative regulators UvrY [21], SpnR [22], HexS [23], RssB [24], and SmaR [25]. S. marcescens JNB5-1, isolated from a soil sample, exhibits a relatively strong prodigiosin biosynthesis capabilities [26]. Previous studies by our research group have demonstrated that transcriptional regulators MetR [26], RcsB [27], and PsrA [2] participate in regulating prodigiosin biosynthesis in strain JNB5-1. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms responsible for prodigiosin biosynthesis in this strain remain incompletely understood.

In this study, we identified the XRE-family transcriptional regulator ProR (BVG90_RS12600, prodigiosin synthesis activator) through a Tn5G transposon screen in S. marcescens JNB5-1 and demonstrated that it acts as a positive regulator of prodigiosin biosynthesis. Mechanistic investigation revealed that ProR modulates prodigiosin synthesis by regulating the expression level of PsrA, a known positive regulator of prodigiosin biosynthesis, in strain JNB5-1. Our findings offer crucial mechanistic insights into the ProR-mediated regulatory network for prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens and establish a framework for engineering high-performance microbial cell factories for its production.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

The strain S. marcescens JNB5-1, which produces prodigiosin, was originally isolated from a soil sample. SK6-61, a mutant with severely impaired prodigiosin biosynthesis, was identified by screening a Tn5G transposon insertion mutant library. ΔproR, ΔpsrA, and ΔslyA represent the gene deletion mutants of proR, psrA, and slyA gene, respectively. The pET28a plasmid is an E. coli expression vector harboring the pBR322 replicon and was used for the expression of ProR protein. The pUCP18 plasmid is a broad-host-range shuttle vector, which carries the pRO1600 and pMB1 replicons, and was employed for functional complementation assays. The pDN19lacΩ plasmid is a promoterless lacZ fusion vector containing the oriV replicon and was utilized for constructing transcriptional fusion reporters. For plasmid construction, E. coli DH5α and S17-1 were employed; for protein expression, E. coli BL21(DE3) was used. Cultivation was performed in LB medium under the following conditions: E. coli strains, 37 °C; S. marcescens strains, 30 °C. Antibiotics were included at specified concentrations when necessary. Antibiotics selection for E. coli strains used spectinomycin (25 μg/mL), streptomycin (25 μg/mL), kanamycin (50 μg/mL), ampicillin (50 μg/mL), or gentamicin (10 μg/mL). For S. marcescens strains, concentrations were: spectinomycin (50 μg/mL), streptomycin (50 μg/mL), ampicillin (150 μg/mL), apramycin (50 μg/mL), or gentamicin (50 μg/mL). The bacterial strains and plasmids employed in this work are detailed in Table S1.

Determination of the transposon insertion locus in mutant strain SK6-61

A Tn5G transposon mutant library was generated in S. marcescens JNB5-1 using established methods [26]. Screening of this library identified the mutant SK6-61, which displays a dramatic reduction in prodigiosin biosynthesis. The Tn5G insertion site in mutant SK6-61 was identified using inverse PCR according to established methods [28,29]. The protocol involved digesting genomic DNA with the restriction enzyme TaqI, self-ligating the digested DNA, and amplifying the ligated product with primers OTn1 and OTn2 (Table S2). To map the insertion locus, the amplified DNA fragment underwent sequencing followed by homology analysis against the NCBI GenBank database. The proR gene, identified through this process, was amplified with primers ProR-F1 and ProR-R1 (Table S2) and cloned into plasmid pUCP18, resulting in the recombinant plasmid pXW2010. Then plasmid pXW2010 was electroporated into strains SK6-61 and ΔproR for functional complementation assays. In functional complementation assays, induction was carried out by adding isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.5 mM when cultures of SK6-61/pXW2010 and ΔproR/pXW2010 reached early-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.8). This induction protocol was employed because proR expression in pXW2010 is regulated by the inducible Plac promoter. To validate that ProR regulates prodigiosin synthesis in strain JNB5-1 through mediating PsrA expression, the psrA gene was overexpressed in the ΔproR mutant, resulting in the recombinant strain ΔproR/pXW2016. The construction was performed as follows: the intact psrA gene along with its native promoter was amplified using primers PsrA-F1 and PsrA-R1 (Table S2) and subsequently cloned into the plasmid pUCP18. This recombinant plasmid, designated pXW2016, was then electroporated into the ΔproR strain to generate the final recombinant strain, ΔproR/pXW2016.

Construction of proR deletion mutant ΔproR

The psrA and slyA gene deletion mutants ΔpsrA and ΔslyA were previously constructed by our research team [2,26]. The proR gene was knocked out as the method described previously, yielding the mutant ΔproR [26]. Briefly, proR flanks and aacC3 were amplified. OE-PCR (overlap extension PCR) inserted aacC3 between proR flanks, and the product was cloned into pUTKm. The proR gene was deleted in S. marcescens JNB5-1 via conjugative transfer of a recombinant plasmid from E. coli S17-1, generating the mutant strain ΔproR. Successful deletion was verified by PCR and sequencing. The primers for the construction of the proR deletion mutant (ΔproR) are detailed in Table S2.

Growth curve assays

The growth curves for strains JNB5-1, SK6-61, SK6-61/pXW2010, SK6-61/pUCP18, ΔproR, ΔproR/pXW2010, and ΔproR/pUCP18 were determined using established methods [26]. Briefly, cells harvested at exponential phase (OD600 = 0.8) were used to inoculate fresh LB medium at a 3% (v/v) inoculum size and continuous incubation proceeded at 30 °C. Growth curves for all seven strains were generated based on OD600 measurements taken at specified time points (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h). The assays were independently replicated three times.

Prodigiosin production assays

The prodigiosin production of strains JNB5-1, SK6-61, SK6-61/pXW2010, SK6-61/pUCP18, ΔproR, ΔproR/pXW2010, ΔproR/pUCP18, ΔslyA, ΔpsrA and ΔproR/pXW2016 was determined using the acidic ethanol and absorbance measurement method [19,26]. Briefly, samples collected at 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h were dissolved in pH 3.0 acidic ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at A534. A standard curve (Y = 1.1936X-0.001; Y, A534; X, concentration in units of 10 mg/L) was established. Prodigiosin levels for all strains across time points were quantified by converting A534 values using this curve, with three independent replicates performed.

Real-time quantitative PCR assay

Gene expression in the prodigiosin pathway was analyzed in JNB5-1 and ΔproR strains harvested at mid-log phase (OD600 = 6.0). Total RNA was extracted with the RNAprep Pure Kit (Tiangen), treated with DNase I, and reverse-transcribed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme). RT-qPCR analyses were performed on 200 ng/μL cDNA with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR mastermix kit (Vazyme) and primers from Supplementary Table S2. The assays were independently replicated three times.

Construction of transcription reporters

To investigate the relationship between ProR and the pig operon, the psrA gene, and the slyA gene, the promoter regions of the pig gene cluster, the psrA gene, and the slyA gene were amplified by PCR using primers detailed in Table S2. These amplified fragments, which are presumed to contain the promoter elements, represent specific intergenic regions: the 391-bp fragment upstream of the pig gene cluster, the 104-bp fragment upstream of psrA gene, and the 180-bp fragment upstream of slyA gene. Following amplification, each promoter fragment (PpigA, PpsrA, and PslyA) was cloned into the pDN19lacΩ reporter vector (carrying lacZ) to generate recombinant plasmids pDN19lacΩ-PpigA, pDN19lacΩ-PpsrA, and pDN19lacΩ-PslyA. These plasmids were then electroporated into the relevant S. marcescens or E. coli strains (Table S1). β-Galactosidase assays were used to assess the expression of the pig cluster, psrA, and slyA genes in different bacterial strains. To identify the core ProR-binding sequences within the psrA promoter, a series of lacZ transcriptional fusions harboring psrA promoter fragments of varying lengths was constructed. These included the PpsrA104, PpsrA103, and PpsrA102 truncations (spanning nucleotides −104, −103, and −102 to −1, respectively), along with specific point mutants in the PpsrA104 background: PpsrA104 (A−103A−102), PpsrA104 (A−78A−77), and PpsrA104 (A−55A−54). Primer sequences used for cloning are provided in Supplementary Table S2. β-Galactosidase activities were subsequently measured in strain JNB5-1 harboring these constructs. The assays were independently replicated three times.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

EMSA was conducted according to established protocols [30]. The proR gene was amplified using primers ProR-F2 and ProR-R2 (Table S2) and cloned into the pET28a vector, yielding the recombinant plasmid pET28a-ProR for overexpression of His-tagged ProR protein. E. coli BL21 harboring pET28a-ProR was cultured in 50 mL of LB medium in a 250 mL flask. When the culture reached the early exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 0.8), protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS buffer, and resuspended in PBS followed by ultrasonication. After centrifugation, the crude lysate was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and loaded onto an AKTA purifier system (GE Healthcare, Sweden) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Proteins were eluted using a linear gradient between buffer A (50 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 7.4). Fractions containing the target protein were collected and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. To determine whether ProR binds directly to the pig operon, psrA, and slyA promoters, the respective promoter regions were PCR-amplified using primer pairs PigA-F1/PigA-R1, PsrA-F1/PsrA-R1, and SlyA-F1/SlyA-R1 as listed in Supplementary Table S2. The precise binding site of ProR on the psrA promoter was mapped by generating a series of truncated DNA fragments with primers as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. This included PpsrA180 (covering positions −180 to −1 relative to the psrA translational start site ATG), PpsrA104 (−104 to −1), PpsrA103 (−103 to −1), PpsrA102 (−102 to −1), PpsrA105’ (−180 to −76), PpsrA104’ (−180 to −77), and PpsrA103’ (−180 to −78). Binding reactions (20 μL) containing purified DNA fragments (100 ng), serially diluted ProR protein, and EMSA binding buffer (4 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM DTT, 0.2 mg/ml BSA, 1 mM EDTA) were incubated for 30 min. Electrophoretic separation of the reaction mixtures was performed on 5% native PAGE gels, with subsequent visualization of DNA bands by ethidium bromide staining.

Comparative transcriptomic analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism how ProR regulates prodigiosin production in strain JNB5-1, the total RNA isolation from JNB5-1 and ΔproR was conducted using method as described previously [31], and subsequent RNA-seq-based transcriptome analysis was employed to map genome-wide expression variations and pinpoint differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Briefly, prior to collection, strains JNB5-1 and ΔproR were cultured in LB medium at 30 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 6 h until an OD600 of ≈3.0 was reached. Subsequently, total RNA was extracted from 1 mL of culture using the RNAprep Pure Kit (Tiangen). The resulting RNA samples were shipped on dry ice to Azenta (Suzhou, China) for RNA-seq analysis. Following ribosomal RNA depletion, the mRNA fraction was used for cDNA synthesis. The resulting cDNA library was subsequently subjected to sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Reads were aligned to the S. marcescens UMH8 genome (GenBank: NZ_CP018927.1) for downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis between the ΔproR and JNB5-1 strains was performed with stringent thresholds (P-value ≤0.05, |log2FC| ≥1). The resulting set of significant DEGs was functionally characterized using KEGG_B_class pathway enrichment analysis implemented in the Omicshare tool (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/Home/Soft/pathwaygsea).

Statistical analysis

Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA was employed to assess statistical significance among experimental groups.

Results

Identification of ProR as a novel prodigiosin biosynthesis regulator in strain JNB5-1

To identify novel regulatory factors of prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens, we constructed a Tn5G transposon mutant library in strain JNB5-1, using E. coli/pRK2013 Tn5G as the donor. A screen of this library identified mutant SK6-61, which exhibited a dramatic reduction in prodigiosin production (Fig. 1A). Shake-flask fermentation experiments further confirmed this phenotype, revealing that when the proR disrupted mutant SK6-61 was cultured in liquid medium, its prodigiosin-deficient phenotype was more pronounced than that on solid plates compared to the wild-type strain JNB5-1 (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1). After 24 h of cultivation, SK6-61 produced only 1.37 mg/L of prodigiosin, which is merely 0.02-times of the wild-type JNB5-1 yield (55.44 mg/L) (Fig. 1B).

Inverse PCR analysis of the disrupted gene in mutant SK6-61 revealed that the Tn5G transposon insertion site was precisely mapped between 81 bp and 82 bp in the coding region of the gene BVG90_RS12600, encoding an XRE-family transcriptional regulator with uncharacterized function (Fig. 1C). To further characterize the genomic context of the disrupted gene in mutant SK6-61, we designed the primer pair ProR-F3/ProR-R3 based on the genome sequence of the S. marcescens UMH8 (GenBank accession number NZ_CP018927.1). PCR amplification was performed using the genome of strain JNB5-1 as a template, and the resulting amplicon was compared with the UMH8 genome. The results showed that the amplified 4204 bp DNA fragment shared high homology with the UMH8 genome, with a similarity of 99.11%. Among these, the Orf2 protein encoded by orf2 of strain JNB5-1 showed high homology to the BVG90_RS12600 protein encoded by the BVG90_RS12600 gene of strain UMH8, with a similarity of 99.00% (Fig. 1C and S2). Functional complementation assays of mutant SK6-61 demonstrated that the recombinant strain SK6-61/pXW2010, harboring the full-length orf2 gene from the genome of strain JNB5-1, exhibited substantial restoration of prodigiosin biosynthesis capacity compared to both the parental mutant SK6-61 and the empty vector control strain SK6-61/pUCP18 (Fig. 1B). These results indicated that Orf2 probably function as a prodigiosin synthesis activator (ProR) in strain JNB5-1.

Functional analysis of the genes flanking proR revealed that in the JNB5-1 genome, the proR gene is located between orf1, which encodes an aldose 1-epimerase family protein, and orf3, which encodes a type II toxin-antitoxin system RelE/ParE family toxin (Fig. 1C). All three genes are transcribed in the same orientation, and the intergenic region between proR and orf3 is less than 100 bp, suggesting that proR and orf3 may constitute an operon. Moreover, previous studies have shown that the most common genetic organization of type II toxin-antitoxin systems is indeed an operon structure [32,33]. These findings indicate that ProR is likely a bifunctional protein, acting both as a transcriptional regulator involved in the positive regulation of prodigiosin biosynthesis and as an antitoxin protein. Given that the objective of this study is to identify novel regulatory factors influencing prodigiosin synthesis in S. marcescens and to elucidate their molecular mechanisms of regulation, determining whether ProR functions as an antitoxin will be systematically investigated in future research.

To further validate the functional role of ProR in regulating prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens, a proR deletion strain (ΔproR) and its complementary strain (ΔproR/pXW2010) were constructed, and their ability to synthesize prodigiosin were evaluated. Results showed that deletion of proR severely impaired prodigiosin biosynthesis, whereas complementation strain exhibited restored prodigiosin production capacity (Fig. 1B). Collectively, these findings establish ProR as a novel transcriptional activator of prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1.

ProR positively regulates the expression level of the pig operon associated with prodigiosin synthesis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism how ProR regulates prodigiosin synthesis, the growth capacity of strains JNB5-1, SK6-61, SK6-61/pXW2010, SK6-61/pUCP18, ΔproR, ΔproR/pXW2010, and ΔproR/pUCP18 was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2A, the growth patterns of the seven strains were indistinguishable. Further analysis of prodigiosin biosynthesis capacity at the single-cell level revealed that the proR mutant strains SK6-61 and ΔproR showed a significant decrease in prodigiosin production compared to that of the wild-type strain JNB5-1 and the complemented strains SK6-61/pXW2010 and ΔproR/pXW2010 (Fig. 2B). Results indicate that ProR regulates prodigiosin synthesis probably independently of cellular biomass, possibly by positively regulating the expression level of the pig gene cluster responsible for prodigiosin biosynthesis, thereby influencing the production of this secondary metabolite in the strain JNB5-1.

Prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens is regulated by 14 genes within the pig cluster (pigA to pigN; Fig. S1). Transcription of this cluster initiates from a promoter upstream of pigA, generating a single polycistronic mRNA encompassing all 14 genes. To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying ProR-mediated positive regulation of prodigiosin synthesis in strain JNB5-1, RT-qPCR was employed to quantify pigABCDEFGHIJKLMN transcript levels in strain JNB5-1 versus proR deletion mutant ΔproR. Results revealed that deletion of proR significantly downregulated pigA-pigN genes expression to levels ranging from 0.04-fold to 0.14-fold of those in the strain JNB5-1 (Fig. 2C). In additional, a transcriptional fusion plasmid (pDN19lacΩ-PpigA) harboring the pig operon promoter::lacZ was constructed by ligating the promoter to a promoterless lacZ gene. This plasmid was then introduced into strains JNB5-1 and ΔproR to generate JNB5-1/pDN19lacΩ-PpigA and ΔproR/pDN19lacΩ-PpigA, respectively. Measurement of β-galactosidase activity revealed a significant reduction in transcriptional activity from the pig operon promoter in the ΔproR mutant compared to the strain JNB5-1 (Fig. 2D, p < 0.001). Collectively, these data support a model wherein ProR positively regulates prodigiosin biosynthesis in JNB5-1, probably through transcriptional activation of the pig operon.

To address whether ProR directly controls the pig operon, its capability to bind the operon's promoter was assessed by EMSA. Results indicated that no direct binding of ProR to the pig operon promoter was observed in EMSA experiment (Fig. 2E). This data implies an indirect regulatory mechanism, possibly involving a mediator gene that transduces ProR signals to the prodigiosin biosynthetic pathway in strain JNB5-1. It is also possible that a certain cofactor exists, which stimulates ProR to activate the expression of the downstream pig operon.

Transcriptome analysis of the proR mutant strain

To identify ProR-regulated genes mediating prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1, total RNA was isolated from both the strains JNB5-1 and ΔproR during the mid-logarithmic growth phase at an OD600 of approximately 3.0. RNAseq was subsequently employed to characterize transcriptional differences between the proR-deficient strain ΔproR and the wild-type strain JNB5-1. Comparative transcriptomic data revealed that in the proR deletion mutant ΔproR, 245 genes exhibited significantly upregulated expression levels, while 159 genes, including the prodigiosin biosynthesis gene cluster pigABCDEFGHIJKLMN, showed significant downregulation in their expression profiles (Fig. 3A, Tables S3 and S4, |log2 [fold change (ΔproR vs JNB5-1)]| ≥1, P-value≤0.05). Functional categorization via KEGG_B_class annotation revealed that 404 significantly differentially expressed genes grouped into 21 major cellular processes, including global and overview maps, amino acid metabolism, and membrane transport (Fig. 3B). This result suggest that ProR may function as a multifunctional transcriptional regulator involved in modulating various cellular processes in strain JNB5-1.

In S. marcescens, multiple transcriptional regulators control the expression of the pig operon and ultimately regulate prodigiosin synthesis by modulating the expression levels of other transcriptional regulators associated with prodigiosin production [20,23,26,27]. Therefore, we hypothesize that ProR may also indirectly regulate prodigiosin biosynthesis in strain JNB5-1 by targeting the expression of any prodigiosin-related transcriptional regulators. To validate this hypothesis, the comparative transcriptomics data was analyzed, and the results showed that 13 transcriptional regulator-encoding genes (including proR, psrA, slyA, and cueR) were significantly downregulated, while 11 transcriptional regulator-encoding genes (including leuO, dsdC, narL, agaR, btsR, and phnF) were significantly upregulated in the proR-deficient mutant ΔproR (Fig. 3C, |log2[fold change (ΔproR vs JNB5-1)]| ≥ 1, P-value ≤ 0.05). As PsrA and SlyA are established positive regulators of prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens [2,26], we posited that ProR may controls prodigiosin production in strain JNB5-1 through transcriptional modulation of PsrA or SlyA.

ProR regulates prodigiosin synthesis by modulating the expression level of PsrA

To investigate whether ProR modulates prodigiosin biosynthesis in strain JNB5-1 through modulating the expression level of PsrA or SlyA, the prodigiosin production capabilities of strains JNB5-1, ΔproR, ΔpsrA (psrA-deleted mutant), and ΔslyA (slyA-deleted mutant) was analyzed after 24 h of fermentation. The results demonstrated that the deletion of proR, psrA, and slyA genes in strain JNB5-1 significantly impaired the bacterial capacity for prodigiosin synthesis (Fig. 4A, p < 0.001). These findings align with previous literature reports identifying PsrA and SlyA as positive regulators of prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens [2,26]. Further, EMSA demonstrated that ProR specifically binds the psrA promoter (Fig. 4C), while with no observable affinity for slyA (Fig. 4B), establishing PsrA probably as a direct transcriptional target of ProR in S. marcescens. This observation agreed with the finding that SmProR (ProR from strain JNB5-1) significantly boosted expression of the PSmpsrA-lacZ transcriptional fusion in E. coli (Fig. 4D, p < 0.001), while exhibiting negligible regulatory effects on the PSmslyA-lacZ reporter system (Fig. 4D). As a control, the expression of the PSmpigA-lacZ transcriptional fusion in E. coli was also assessed. The results showed that SmProR had no significant effect on its expression (Fig. 4D), which is consistent with the EMSA results (Fig. 2E), further demonstrating that ProR does not directly regulate the expression of the pig gene cluster. These results suggest that ProR modulates prodigiosin biosynthesis in strain JNB5-1 probably through transcriptional regulation of PsrA. To further validate this hypothesis, the recombinant plasmid pXW2016, which carries the psrA gene driven by its native promoter, was introduced into the ΔproR mutant, resulting in the recombinant strain ΔproR/pXW2016. Analysis of prodigiosin production in strains JNB5-1, JNB5-1/pXW2016, JNB5-1/pUCP18, ΔproR/pXW2010, and ΔproR/pXW2016 revealed that, compared to the JNB5-1, ΔproR/pXW2010, ΔproR/pXW2016, and JNB5-1/pUCP18 strains, the JNB5-1/pXW2016 strain exhibited a significantly enhanced capacity for prodigiosin synthesis (Fig. 4A, p < 0.005). In contrast, no statistically significant differences in prodigiosin production were observed among the JNB5-1, ΔproR/pXW2010, ΔproR/pXW2016, and JNB5-1/pUCP18 strains (Fig. 4A). Taken together, our findings establish that ProR activates prodigiosin biosynthesis in strain JNB5-1 through direct binding to the psrA promoter and subsequent transcriptional activation of the pig cluster.

Mapping ProR binding site in the psrA promoter region

To delineate the ProR binding site within the psrA promoter, a set of progressively truncated DNA fragments (PpsrA180, PpsrA104, PpsrA103, PpsrA102, PpsrA105’, PpsrA104’, and PpsrA103’) were incubated with purified ProR protein and subjected to EMSA. These fragments cover distinct regions relative to the psrA start codon (ATG): PpsrA180 (−180 to −1 bp, 180 bp), PpsrA104 (−104 to −1 bp, 104 bp), PpsrA103 (−103 to −1 bp, 103 bp), PpsrA102 (−102 to −1 bp, 102 bp), PpsrA105’ (−180 to −76 bp, 105 bp), PpsrA104’ (−180 to −77 bp, 104 bp), and PpsrA103’ (−180 to −78 bp, 103 bp) (Fig. 5A). EMSA revealed that ProR bound to PpsrA180, PpsrA103, PpsrA105’, and PpsrA104’, but not to PpsrA102 or PpsrA103’ (Fig. 5B), indicating that the binding site of ProR probably resides between positions −103 and −77 upstream of the psrA start codon.

To further characterize the binding site, we constructed a series of lacZ-based transcriptional reporter fusions carrying specifically mutated or truncated variants of the psrA promoter: PpsrA104 (a 104-bp fragment spanning positions −104 to −1 relative to the ATG start codon), PpsrA103 (a 103-bp fragment spanning positions −103 to −1 relative to the ATG start codon), PpsrA102 (a 102-bp fragment spanning positions −102 to −1 relative to the ATG start codon), PpsrA104 (A−103A−102) (carrying a GG-to-AA substitution at positions −103 and −102), PpsrA104 (A−78A−77) (carrying a CC-to-AA substitution at positions −78 and −77), and PpsrA104 (A−55A−54) (carrying a GG-to-AA substitution at positions −55 and −54). β-Galactosidase activity measurements in JNB5-1 demonstrated that disrupting the putative ProR binding site markedly decreased psrA transcription, while the activity remained unchanged with other mutations (Fig. 5C). These results confirm that the region between −103 and −77 bp, which contains a palindromic sequence (5′-GGCCTTGGTGGTTCAACCACCAAAGCC-3′), constitutes the ProR binding site within the psrA promoter (Fig. 5A). Collectively, our results support a model wherein ProR binds the psrA promoter to upregulate PsrA, a key positive regulator of the pig cluster. Consequently, elevated PsrA levels activate transcription of the pig genes, enhancing prodigiosin biosynthesis (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Transcriptional regulators, which are protein molecules that modulate the expression of target genes by binding to the 5’ untranslated regions, are increasingly being recognized for their pivotal roles in regulating cellular processes [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. The xenobiotic-responsive element (XRE) family of transcriptional regulators are present across bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes. Previous studies have revealed that XRE family transcriptional regulators play crucial roles in regulating various core cellular processes in microorganisms. For instance: the transcriptional regulator SrpA has been found to regulates bacteriophage infection, biofilm formation, protein secretion, and T6SS-mediated antibacterial activity in Pseudomonas aeruginosa [31]. The transcriptional regulator LfsT has been identified to regulates bacteriophage infection, spermidine transport, and the type III secretion system (T3SS) in P. aeruginosa [40]. The transcriptional regulator BioX has been confirmed negatively regulates biotin biosynthesis in Riemerella anatipestifer [41]. In Streptomyces coelicolor, the transcriptional regulator Sco1979 functions as a repressor of the biosynthesis of actinorhodin (ACT), undecylprodigiosin (RED), and the calcium-dependent antibiotic (CDA) [42]. Also, in Microbacterium sp., the transcriptional regulator SadR has been identified to regulates sulfonamide metabolism [43]. Although previous studies have reported the roles of XRE family transcriptional regulators in regulating other microbial cellular processes, our understanding of their functions in Serratia, particularly in S. marcescens, remains limited. Fineran et al. experimentally demonstrated that the transcriptional regulator PigP is involved in regulating prodigiosin biosynthesis, carbapenem production, cellulase activity, and swimming motility in Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 [44]. Here, we provide experimental evidence that ProR, an XRE-family transcriptional regulator, functions as a positive regulator in the prodigiosin biosynthetic network of S. marcescens (Fig. 1).

Previous studies have identified multiple transcriptional regulators crucial for mediating prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens. These include positive regulators OxyR [16], OmpR [17], EepR [23], PigP [19], FlhDC [20] and PsrA [2] and negative regulators UvrY [21], SpnR [22], HexS [45], RssB [24], SmaR [25], MetR [26], and RcsB [27]. Additionally, several structural proteins, such as the IgaA/UmoB family protein GumB [46], copper efflux pump CopA [47], and cAMP receptor protein CRP [48], have been also shown to participate in regulating prodigiosin synthesis. Among them, the transcriptional regulator PsrA positively regulates the expression of the pig gene cluster by binding directly to the regulatory binding site (RBS, 5′-TGTAATAATAGACCACA-3′) and activator binding site (ABS, 5′-ACTTTGCCTGTTTGAGT-3′) within the promoter region of the pig operon, thereby enhancing prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1 [2]. Using Tn5G transposon mutagenesis, we have identified ProR, a member of the XRE family of transcriptional regulators, as a novel positive regulator of prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens. Integrated results from RT-qPCR (Fig. 2C), β-galactosidase assays (Fig. 2, Fig. 4, Fig. 5C), comparative transcriptomics (Fig. 3), and EMSAs (Fig. 2, Fig. 4, Fig. 5B) reveal that ProR activates the expression of PsrA, a positive regulator of prodigiosin synthesis, by binding to a 27-bp palindromic motif (5′-GGCCTTGGTGGTTCAACCACCAAAGCC-3′) in its promoter. The elevated PsrA level subsequently upregulates the pig gene cluster, thereby positively regulating prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1 (Fig. 5D). It is worth noting that while EMSA results suggest the presence of two binding complexes between ProR and the psrA promoter region (Fig. 4, Fig. 5B), subsequent promoter truncation analysis identified only one core region essential for transcriptional activation (Fig. 5A, B and 5C). This discrepancy may arise from cooperative interactions between the two binding sites, where binding to the secondary site strictly depends on prior occupancy of the primary site [49,50]. Future studies using DNA footprinting experiments could precisely map all binding sites of ProR within the psrA promoter region.

RT-qPCR analysis of the prodigiosin biosynthesis gene cluster (pigABCDEFGHIJKLMN) in wild-type strain JNB5-1 and its ΔproR mutant revealed that deletion of proR significantly downregulated the expression of pigA through pigN, reducing transcript levels to between 0.04- and 0.14-fold of those in JNB5-1 (Fig. 2C). While this result may appear inconsistent with the expectation that all 14 genes within the same operon should exhibit similar expression levels, it does not contradict our hypothesis. Rather, it underscores the complexity of post-transcriptional regulation in gene clusters. Despite being co-transcribed as a polycistronic unit from a common core promoter, the final abundance of mature mRNA for each gene is modulated by a suite of precise post-transcriptional mechanisms, a phenomenon well-documented in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic polycistronic systems [[51], [52], [53]]. Consistent with this, prior studies have also reported differential expression among the 14 pig genes within the same operon [54,55]. In the present study, we attribute the observed expression variation primarily to two factors: (i) differential mRNA stability, wherein sequence features such as 5′/3′ UTRs and coding regions affect interactions with RNA-binding proteins and susceptibility to nucleases, leading to distinct transcript half-lives; and (ii) variations in internal ribosome entry sites or translation efficiency, whereby differences in ribosome-binding site strength or start codon context result in unequal translation initiation rates across the polycistronic mRNA. These findings not only align with established regulatory principles but also highlight a key direction for future research: elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms that fine-tune the balanced expression of individual members within this operon.

Previous studies have shown that various transcriptional regulators can control prodigiosin synthesis in S. marcescens by sensing different environmental cues. For example, Gristwood et al. found that the two-component system PhoBR senses phosphate limitation to stimulate prodigiosin synthesis in Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 [56]. Fineran et al. reported that the transcriptional regulator PigT negatively regulates prodigiosin synthesis in Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 in response to gluconate concentration [57], while the transcriptional regulator SmaR senses N-acyl-homoserine lactone quorum-sensing signals to stimulate prodigiosin synthesis in Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 [44]. Sun et al. demonstrated that the MarR-family transcriptional regulator OhrR senses organic hydroperoxide levels to promote prodigiosin synthesis in Serratia sp. ATCC 39006 [8], and our team previously confirmed experimentally that the transcriptional regulator PsrA regulates prodigiosin synthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1 by sensing intracellular l-proline concentration [2]. These findings suggest that ProR-mediated positive regulation of prodigiosin synthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1 may also be triggered by certain environmental factors. Based on comparative transcriptomic analysis of the most differentially expressed genes in the proR deletion mutant, we found that the three most significantly up-regulated genes were ttrB, ttrC, and ttrA (encoding tetrathionate reductase subunits TtrB, TrtC, and TrtA) (Table S3), while the most significantly down-regulated genes, aside from psrA, were BVG90_RS12635 (encoding a fatty acyl-AMP ligase) and BVG90_RS12655 (encoding an ATP-binding protein) (Table S4). This indicates that ProR may regulate downstream gene expression and prodigiosin synthesis in response to changes in intracellular concentrations of ATP, sulfur, fatty acids, or other gene-modulated metabolites. In future work, we will take these hypotheses as starting points and employ integrated approaches such as isothermal titration microcalorimetry (ITC) assays, EMSAs, and β-galactosidase assays to systematically identify the signal molecules that activate ProR.

Previous studies have suggested that transcriptional regulators may function as global regulators, playing critical roles in mediating diverse cellular processes [2,31,58,59]. Comparative transcriptomic analysis between the wild-type strain JNB5-1 and the ΔproR mutant identified 245 significantly upregulated and 159 significantly downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, KEGG_B_Class analysis of the cellular processes affected by these differentially expressed genes indicated their involvement in 22 major pathways (Fig. 3B). This result demonstrates that the transcriptional regulator ProR in S. marcescens is likely involved in regulating cellular processes beyond prodigiosin biosynthesis. The present work thereby lays a robust foundation for future research aimed at elucidating the additional cellular processes governed by ProR in S. marcescens and uncovering the molecular mechanisms through which it regulates these processes.

In summary, this study identifies the previously uncharacterized transcriptional regulator ProR as playing a critical role in regulating prodigiosin biosynthesis in S. marcescens JNB5-1. The systematic elucidation of its underlying molecular mechanism provides a solid foundation for engineering high-efficiency S. marcescens cell factories for prodigiosin production.
