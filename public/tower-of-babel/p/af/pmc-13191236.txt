Enhancing physical activity enjoyment through cognitively demanding VR: Individual differences in engagement and cardiovascular response
Authors: Olivia L. Brand, Erik D. Thiessen, Cassondra M. Eng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As virtual reality (VR) and digital health technologies become more widespread, innovative strategies are needed to counteract the rise in sedentary behavior. This study examines the impact of exercise-based VR with varying cognitive demands on physical activity enjoyment and acute cardiovascular responses in young adults, with a particular focus on individual differences in habitual physical activity. College students (ages 18–25) were randomly assigned to one of two 60-minute VR exercise conditions: Low Load (n = 25) or High Load (n = 25). To preserve ecological validity, participants engaged with experimentally modified versions of a commercially available VR exergame for acute aerobic training. Key outcomes included self-reported enjoyment of physical activity, frequency of physical activity, and engagement in heart rate zones. Results showed that participants in the High Load condition experienced greater increases in enjoyment from pre- to post-exercise compared to those in the Low Load condition. Notably, sedentary individuals in the High Load group reported the greatest increases in physical activity enjoyment, suggesting that cognitive engagement may enhance exercise appeal for less active populations. All participants, regardless of condition, reached target cardiovascular intensity zones. These findings highlight the potential of cognitively demanding VR to promote physical activity engagement among sedentary young adults. By integrating cognitive stimulation into immersive exercise environments, VR technologies may serve as scalable, accessible tools for behavioral change. This study contributes to a growing body of research on the psychophysiological mechanisms of VR and underscores the importance of individual differences in designing effective digital health interventions.

Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) refers to a computer-generated, sensorimotor-contingent environment that replaces users’ perceptual input with a fully simulated world, enabling naturalistic interaction and locomotion (Parong & Mayer, 2021; Slater, 2022). VR is distinct from related immersive technologies, such as augmented reality (AR), which overlays digital elements onto the physical environment, and mixed reality (MR), which blends physical and virtual objects in real-time. These modalities are sometimes grouped under the broader label extended reality (XR), but VR uniquely provides complete perceptual immersion, making it especially suitable for manipulating environmental cues relevant to physical activity. The qualities of VR most relevant to physical activity include immersion, presence, and flow. Immersion is the experience of being enveloped in an environment with a constant flow of sensory input (Cummings & Bailenson, 2016). Presence is defined as the degree to which a person perceives the digital environment as their current reality (Slater, 2003). When VR tasks are designed with optimal challenge-skill balance through the simulated environment, users may also experience flow, a state of deep absorption associated with intrinsic motivation and enjoyment (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Guerra-Tamez et al., 2025). Together, these experiential mechanisms may explain why VR-based exercise often produces greater enjoyment and engagement compared with conventional physical activity (Bian et al., 2023). These variables enhance the users’ engagement and strengthen the effectiveness of exposure-based interventions in ways that non-immersive media, such as video or text, cannot achieve (Segura-Ortí et al., 2019; Standen et al., 2021). Medical applications of VR have increased as a training and intervention tool to remediate symptoms in populations with chronic and acute pain, anxiety disorders, gait disorders, phobias, and post-traumatic stress disorder due to increased accessibility, efficiency, and patient engagement (Parsons et al., 2017; Vourvopoulos et al., 2023). With the rise of VR, active video games, and technology use, alongside increasing reports of physical inactivity and cognitive fatigue, there is an urgent need to leverage emerging technologies to promote both cognitive and physical activity.

The development of tools that support physical activity engagement is especially crucial, as exercise reduces stress and boosts immunity. The growing reliance on technology has contributed to increased sedentary indoor behavior and decreased physical activity, raising concerns about its negative impact on cardiovascular health (Sarcinella et al., 2024).

Prior research has shown that exercise-based video games projected in 2D environments, increase physical activity enjoyment and cardiovascular fitness in older populations (Monteiro-Junior et al., 2014), increase caloric expenditure in children (Graf et al., 2009) and adolescents (Graves, Stratton, Ridgers & Cable, 2007), and decrease waist circumferences in children (Mhurchu et al., 2008). Yet, comparatively little is known about the beneficial effects on exercise enjoyment and cardiovascular health outcomes of gameplay in immersive exercise-based 3D VR environments with college students.

College students transitioning back to in-person learning report feeling sluggish, unable to concentrate, and anxiety related to physical fatigue and mental health (Huang et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2021). With increased autonomy, reduced parental monitoring, and greater self-management demands, accessible and scalable strategies for maintaining well-being are essential for this population. While exercise can enhance concentration and reduce anxiety in college-aged students, motivating this population to be physically active remains a challenge, especially for those with sedentary habits or psychosocial barriers, such as low self-efficacy or discomfort exercising in social settings. (Cabral et al., 2023). One potential solution for college students to engage in physical activity is exercise-based VR, particularly for students struggling with sustained focus, organization, distraction resistance, and planning. A recent study of undergraduate students revealed that only 54.6% (206/377) of students engage in physical activity on a weekly basis with lack of time, unwillingness, and lack of facilities being the leading reasons for physical inactivity (Alhammad et al., 2023). Exercise-based VR can provide an avenue for increasing physical activity enjoyment resulting in increased motivation to engage in physical activity. Furthermore, students can engage in exercise-based VR within their own home, and the decreasing cost of VR makes this form of exercise attainable for many students.

Promising studies have shown immersive exercise paradigms such as cycling to have beneficial effects on efficiency, physical activity engagement with elderly populations and geriatric wellness and rehabilitation populations (Bermúdez i Badia et al., 2023; Gaggioli et al., 2017). However, the ability to use exercise-based VR as a tool to increase physical activity enjoyment and cardiovascular health in college students is understudied. At the same time, a growing number of cognitively oriented digital “brain games” aim to improve mental or physical health through tasks designed to stimulate cognitive processes (e.g., Gaggioli et al., 2017). These applications are relevant because they highlight growing interest in digital tools that engage cognitive resources, yet most operate independently of physical movement. Emerging work has proposed that interventions combining cognitive engagement with physical activity may be particularly effective because they integrate attentional demands with embodied movement (Eng et al., 2023). Given the well-established role of exercise in both preventive and therapeutic health, identifying optimal digital platforms for supporting movement is increasingly important in a landscape where technology shapes how individuals initiate and sustain physical activity (Bhundoo et al., 2025). Thus, physical activity performed within an immersive VR context may provide synergistic benefits (Eng et al., 2025). Exercise serves as both a preventative measure and a therapeutic intervention for health, underscoring the importance of understanding optimal platforms in which it can be delivered; this is especially pressing in a digital landscape that has shifted how individuals across the lifespan can access and experience exercise (Bhundoo et al., 2025).

With the decreasing cost of VR and sedentary behavior increasing, this study investigates the optimal exercise-based VR to promote physical activity enjoyment and cardiovascular health for college students, with an emphasis on the role of individual differences in sedentary behavior.

Methods

Participants and procedure

Participants of this study are part of a larger intervention study examining the effects of exercise-based VR on cognitive skills, cardiovascular health, exercise enjoyment, and neurological development. The cardiovascular health and exercise enjoyment assessments acquired during the research lab visit are the focus of the current study.

Fifty participants ages 18–25 (M = 20.41, SD = 1.43) were recruited from a private university in a Mid-Atlantic city in the United States. Participants were 54% Asian, 36% White, 6% Black or African American, 2% Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, and 2% Multiracial. 12% of the sample identified as Hispanic or Latino. The Carnegie Mellon University Institutional Review Board approved the experimental protocol. Eligibility information was obtained through the standard pre-screening questionnaire embedded within the university’s secure SONA research recruitment system. Participants completed this anonymized self-report questionnaire, which assigns users a randomly generated study ID to prevent investigator bias and protect confidentiality. The questionnaire includes items assessing medical conditions relevant to physical activity, neurological and psychiatric history, sensory impairments, and seizure-related conditions. Participants were excluded if they reported any medical condition preventing physical exercise, neurological or psychiatric disorders, sensory impairments, or a history of seizure activity or epilepsy. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: Low Load (n = 25) or High Load (n = 25). The cognitive load of the conditions differed solely in visuospatial processing demands; all other features of the VR experience, including physical movements, session duration, and audio, were held constant. In the Low Load Condition, participants were, on average, 20.43 years old (SD = 1.33), and participants in the High Load Condition were, on average, 20.39 years old (SD = 1.55). In the Low Load Condition, participants were 72% female and 28% male. In the High Load Condition, participants were 76% female and 24% male. Sex and prior familiarity with video games and VR were assessed in the pretest demographics questionnaire. Body mass index (BMI) was not collected, as the study focused on exercise-induced affective outcomes rather than baseline body composition. Instead, baseline resting heart rate was recorded as a more immediate physiological indicator of participants’ initial physical state. Participants were randomly assigned to conditions, and preliminary checks indicated that sex, gaming experience, and baseline heart rate did not differ significantly between groups. In accordance with standard recommendations for randomized experimental designs, these characteristics were not included as covariates in the primary analyses.

Frequency and intensity of weekly physical activity were assessed through a validated questionnaire for young adults (Wallace & Buckworth, 2002) and administered at Pretest. The main dependent variable to quantify individual sedentary behavior profiles was the frequency of physical activity. Participants reported how often they, on average, exercised for at least 20 min weekly ranging from 0 - never, 1 - <1 time/week, 2 - 1 time/week, 3 - 2 times/week, 4 - 3 times/week, 5 - 4 times/week, 6 - 5 times/week, 7 - 6 times/week, and 8 - greater than 7 times/week. This information was used to create a “Sedentary Behavior Profile” for each participant, so we could assess any possible differential impact as a function of participants’ prior exercise habits.

Study design and conditions

At Pretest prior to VR, participants in both conditions were administered a series of standardized assessments and questionnaires: exercise enjoyment, frequency of physical activity, and physical activity enjoyment. Participants used wireless portable Meta Quest 2 VR Headsets with 3D positional audio and associated hand-held controllers. To preserve ecological validity, participants played an experimentally modified popular commercially available exercise-based VR game, Beat Saber (Beat Games, Oculus VR, 2019). Beatsaber is a VR rhythm game where players slice boxes to the beat of songs as they fly towards them. This study employed a between-subjects design in which participants were randomly assigned to one of two Conditions: High Load or Low Load (see Fig. 1). In the High Load Condition there were arrows on the blocks and participants sliced the blocks left, right, up, down, or diagonal depending on whether the direction of the arrow on the target block was facing left, right, up, down, or diagonal; thus, increasing the cognitive load as participants needed to simultaneously maintain attention to an additional visuospatial cue (the direction of the arrow) and coordinate their motor response accordingly. This design increased cognitive load by requiring participants to attend to an additional visuospatial cue and map it onto a specific motor response. This form of selective-attention demand parallels classic cognitive-control paradigms such as the Eriksen Flanker Task (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974; Kim & Kim, 2021; Rueda et al., 2004), where increased load arises from processing and resolving competing visuospatial information. In contrast, the Low Load Condition involved slicing blocks in any direction; thus, keeping the exercise demands constant but decreasing the cognitive load.Fig. 1Overview of the two experimental conditions. Participants for both conditions then played a total of nine songs determined through pilot testing for 60 min to align with CDC age-appropriate guidelines for daily aerobic activity. Cybersickness risk was minimized through extensive pilot testing to ensure that the finalized VR paradigm did not induce sensory conflict. As part of the consent procedures, participants were advised about the risk of cybersickness, which can occur when visual motion cues are not matched by bodily movement, and were instructed to notify the researcher immediately if they experienced any discomfort so that the session could be stopped. Participants were also encouraged to take breaks at any point during the session. All participants were shown a tutorial to familiarize themselves with the VR environment until directions were understood, followed by a practice song during which they were also able to determine the appropriate difficulty according to their individual skill level. Both cardiovascular and performance metrics were collected for the remaining eight songs. The difficulty of each song was adjusted according to the individual’s self-determined skill level, and participants were able to adjust this throughout the intervention. If participants were unable to complete a song, they were permitted to try again to mimic real-world exercise routines and VR game mechanics. At Posttest, exercise enjoyment and physical activity enjoyment were assessed again.

Outcomes

Physical activity enjoyment scale

Physical activity enjoyment was measured using the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES; Motl et al., 2001). The Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale is a 16-item questionnaire that assesses how participants feel about physical activity using a 5-point bipolar rating scale. Eleven items are reverse scored. Higher PACES scores reflect greater levels of enjoyment.

Cardiovascular health

Cardiovascular health was recorded using a wireless physical activity monitor (Fitbit Inspire HR; Healthy Metrics Research, Inc.). Heart rate (HR) and calories expended after each song were obtained from the device. The wireless monitor estimates caloric expenditure by integrating basal metabolic rate (BMR; the rate at which individuals burn calories at rest to maintain vital body function)–which is derived from height, weight, age, and sex–with heart rate and accelerometer-based activity data. These demographic factors are automatically incorporated by the device's proprietary algorithm; no additional statistical adjustments were made by the researchers. ΔHR was calculated as the pre-VR resting HR subtracted from the recorded heart rate immediately following the completion of each song. The main dependent variables were computed by averaging the VR training-induced changes in HR across songs using a simple arithmetic mean and summing total calories expended across the session.

Data analytic approach

Alpha was set at 0.05 for all tests. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and partial eta-squared (ηp2) for effect size estimates were reported for analyses. 95% CIs for Pearson Correlation Coefficients were calculated utilizing the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient observed within the sample and the number of paired observations of the sample. Data analysis was performed using R (R Core Team, 2023). All continuous variables were examined for normality using visual inspection of Q–Q plots and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis prior to conducting parametric analyses.

Descriptive analyses. Energy expenditure (total calories burned) and mean heart rate during VR training were summarized descriptively for each condition. VR training-induced mean heart rate was measured as the mean heart rate during each song between the High Load Condition compared to the Low Load Condition.

Inferential analyses. To assess changes in physical activity enjoyment, PACES scores were assessed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVAs) with difference scores from Pretest to Posttest as the dependent variable and Condition as the explanatory variable. Because random assignment resulted in no baseline differences across conditions, difference scores provided a direct and unbiased estimate of change and were therefore preferred over ANCOVA. To examine whether the cognitively engaging VR Condition might be especially useful for students who exhibit sedentary behavior was investigated, a difference score for each student was calculated by subtracting the PACES pretest scores from their PACES posttest scores. Difference scores estimated changes in physical activity enjoyment after VR training, such that higher and positive scores indexed greater increases in physical activity enjoyment. Then, the association between changes in physical activity enjoyment and weekly exercise frequency was examined, by Condition.

Results

Preliminary analyses were conducted to confirm that randomization produced comparable groups. Analyses of variance (ANOVAs) and χ² tests examined condition differences in age, sex, exercise frequency, and pretest physical activity enjoyment. Neither sex nor exercise frequency differed significantly between groups (ps > 0.17), and no significant differences emerged for age, pretest PACES scores, or baseline heart rate (ps > 0.23). Skewness and kurtosis estimates for the main variables were within ±2 standard errors, and Q-Q plots showed no systematic deviations from normality, supporting the use of parametric analyses.

Manipulation Check: First, to verify that the cognitive-load manipulation functioned as intended, participants reported perceived cognitive effort after VR training using a single-item rating adapted from the Borg CR10 RPE format (Borg, 1998; Paas et al., 2008; Staiano et al., 2024). Participants in the High Load condition (M = 8.88, SD = 1.05) reported significantly higher perceived cognitive effort than those in the Low Load condition (M = 6.08, SD = 2.38), F(1, 48) = 28.95, p < .001, η² = 0.38, confirming that the manipulation produced the intended differences in cognitive load.

Effects on cardiovascular health

There were no significant differences in energy expenditure between the groups, measured as the total amount of calories burned during VR training between the High Load Condition (M = 266.76, SD = 20.99 Cal) and the Low Load Condition (M = 235.00, SD = 18.26 Cal), F(1, 48) = 0.326; p = .571; ηp2 = 0.007. There were also no significant differences in VR training-induced mean heart rate, measured as the mean heart rate during each song between the High Load Condition (M = 113.80, SD = 20.65 BPM) compared to the Low Load Condition (M = 110.74, SD = 10.52 BPM), F(1, 48) = 0.437; p = .512; ηp2 = 0.009. Moderate exercise intensity is between about 50% - 70% of maximum heart rate (HRmax=(208−age)×0.6)(American Heart Association, 2023), indicating both conditions elicited moderate-intensity physical activity. For example, for a 20-year-old, the estimated maximum age-related heart rate would be calculated as 208 – 20 years = 188 BPM. The 60% level would be: 188 × 0.6 = 112.8 BPM. Thus, although no significant differences in cardiovascular outcomes emerged between conditions, the findings suggest that both VR conditions elicited moderate-intensity physical activity, indicating that exercise-based VR may serve as a feasible approach for supporting cardiovascular health.

Effects on physical activity enjoyment

There were no significant differences in PACES scores at Pretest between the High Load Condition (M = 4.07, SD = 0.52) compared to the Low Load Condition (M = 3.84, SD = 0.76), F(1, 48) = 1.46; p = .232; ηp2 = 0.03. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVAs) were conducted with difference scores from Pretest to Posttest as the dependent variable and Condition as the explanatory variable. Ratings of Physical Activity Enjoyment changes from pretest to posttest revealed a significant effect of condition, in that changes in students’ PACES scores were significantly higher in the High Load Condition (M = 0.26, SD = 0.28) compared to the Low Load Condition (M = 0.07, SD =0.32), F(1, 48) = 5.01; p = .030; ηp2 = 0.10. The outcome for each condition of the physical activity enjoyment measure followed a normal distribution. There was one extreme outlier (i.e., value that deviated >3 SD away from the group mean) with a change score of 1.06 in the High Load Condition. With the removal of this outlier, there was still evidence of a main effect of condition on changes in physical activity enjoyment, F(1, 47) = 9.37; p = .004; ηp2 = 0.17. Discussion

This study provides preliminary findings regarding whether cognitive demand during exercise-based VR influences physical activity enjoyment in college students.. Four main findings emerged. First, participants in the high cognitive load condition reported significantly greater increases in physical activity enjoyment compared to those in the low cognitive load condition. This suggests that cognitively engaging VR may increase the likelihood of sustained exercise engagement by making physical activity more enjoyable. Second, enjoyment gains in the high-load VR condition were strongest among students who reported low baseline physical activity. This may indicate that VR can be an effective ‘onboarding’ tool to increase engagement with physical activity. Third, although cardiovascular fitness outcomes (e.g., HRmax) did not differ significantly by condition, participants in both cognitive load conditions achieved the target heart rate zones. This suggests that exercise-based VR, regardless of the level of mental engagement, may elicit sufficient physical exertion to benefit cardiovascular health.. Additionally, while a 60-minute session was selected to align with CDC age-appropriate guidelines for daily aerobic activity, this duration may have introduced physical fatigue, visual strain, or cybersickness, potentially influencing enjoyment or ecological validity. Although cybersickness is a known adverse effect of immersive VR (Chang et al., 2020; Weech et al., 2019), no participants withdrew from the study (0% attrition), and none reported cybersickness. This absence of adverse effects is noteworthy given that cybersickness is a known risk of VR engagement. The complete retention in our sample may reflect the reduced visual-vestibular mismatch inherent in exercise-based VR, where users’ real-world movements align closely with in-game actions, thereby decreasing the sensory conflict that typically produces cybersickness.

These findings have both pragmatic and theoretical implications. Of greatest pragmatic interest is the potential effectiveness of VR as a gateway to physical activity for users with sedentary tendencies. This finding is particularly noteworthy given increasing sedentary behavior in college populations. The result highlights the potential of cognitively stimulating VR to engage sedentary individuals, who may find it more challenging to initiate or sustain physical activity. As young adults transition from structured high school athletics to autonomous health behaviors, scalable, enjoyable tools like VR may support both physical and mental well-being. Prior research notes that college students face barriers such as low motivation, limited time, and social anxiety related to physical activity. Exercise-based VR addresses these issues by offering personalized, adaptable challenges and a private, nonjudgmental context for movement (Lima et al., 2022). Its portability and minimal space requirements also support deployment in dorms or apartments and could serve as a gateway to group fitness.

At a theoretical level, the results can be situated within the broader literature on VR engagement, presence, and immersion. VR is distinctive in its ability to generate a strong sense of presence, the subjective experience of “being there,” through the integration of visual, auditory, and proprioceptive cues (Segura-Orti et al., 2019; Standen et al., 2021). However, perceptual realism alone is insufficient to produce meaningful cognitive or affective outcomes. Effective VR engagement also depends on the user directing cognitive and physical resources toward autonomously motivated goals, as articulated by Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In the present study, cognitive engagement was manipulated through visuospatial load, with the manipulation applied uniformly across all participants. Theories of flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990) would predict that a fixed-load task may be optimally engaging for novices or sedentary participants, whose skill level aligns with the challenge presented, but insufficiently stimulating for individuals with greater physical or gaming experience. The greater increases in enjoyment observed among sedentary users may reflect this alignment between challenge and skill.

These considerations highlight the need for future VR exercise programs to dynamically calibrate cognitive challenge to the user’s performance and motivational profile. Doing so may more fully leverage the presence, immersion, and flow mechanisms of engagement, and may increase the effectiveness of VR interventions for a wider range of users. Future research should also examine how these mechanisms translate into longer-term physical activity behavior and distal fitness outcomes.

Limitations

These findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the study’s small sample size and limited participant diversity. A priori power analysis was not conducted for this study, as effect-size estimates for this type of exercise-based VR paradigm were not yet available. As a result, the sample size may not have been optimized for detecting small effects. Future research should incorporate prospective power analyses using the effect sizes generated here to ensure adequate statistical power for confirmatory tests. All participants were U.S. college students, which restricts generalizability. Additionally, only one commercially available VR game was used; future research should investigate whether similar outcomes are observed with other exercise-based VR platforms to assess reproducibility and variation in engagement or cardiovascular benefit. The novelty of first-time exposure to this VR paradigm may have inflated enjoyment ratings, and future work should examine whether these effects persist across repeated sessions once the novelty wears off. Lastly, although heart rate was recorded after each VR song to index cardiovascular engagement during the session, we did not obtain a post-session resting HR measure. As a result, we were unable to examine changes in resting HR or HR recovery, which would offer additional insight into cardiovascular response. Future studies should include repeated resting HR assessments and HR variability metrics to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of cardiovascular engagement in exercise-based VR.

Future directions

The present findings highlight several avenues for future research. First, tailoring cognitive demand to the user’s skill level may enhance engagement, consistent with Self-Determination Theory and Flow Theory, which emphasize optimal challenge and autonomous motivation. Adaptive VR systems that calibrate visuospatial or attentional demands in real time could more effectively sustain engagement for users with different baseline activity levels.

Second, the cardiovascular outcomes observed here were modest and did not differ between conditions, suggesting that future work should explore how exercise-based VR can be modified to elicit higher-intensity activity or varied interval structures. Longitudinal designs will clarify whether repeated VR exercise sessions lead to sustained improvements in enjoyment, engagement, or physical activity behavior—particularly for sedentary users, who appeared to benefit most in this exploratory analysis.

Finally, future studies should incorporate more comprehensive measures of physical and affective response, such as repeated resting HR assessments, heart rate variability, or validated cognitive-load instruments. These additions would provide a fuller understanding of how VR-based cognitive challenge interacts with physical exertion over time.

Broader impact

This study contributes to a growing body of work on how immersive technologies can increase motivation for physical activity. By tailoring cognitive demand to user preference and baseline activity levels, exercise-based VR may be especially impactful for students in cold climates, dense urban settings, or those with social barriers to traditional exercise. Although preliminary, our findings support the feasibility of cognitively stimulating VR as an engaging, scalable intervention to promote health and well-being in digital-native college populations.
