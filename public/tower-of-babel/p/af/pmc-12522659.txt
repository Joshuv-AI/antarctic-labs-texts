Relationship between stroke injury sites and incidence of post-stroke pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhifa Zhang, Sehui Ma, Bin Feng, Xiaoying Li, Yufen Zhang, Lei Pei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke pain (PSP) is a prevalent and debilitating complication of stroke recovery, mainly characterized by allodynia and hyperalgesia. While certain brain lesion sites, particularly thalamic lesions, have been implicated in an increased risk of PSP, evidence regarding the specific contributions of stroke injury sites to PSP development remains inconsistent. This meta-analysis aimed to synthesize available data on the relationship between stroke lesion sites and PSP incidence, providing a more detailed understanding to inform clinical management.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Literature searches were performed across four major databases, including Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed, up to December 31, 2024. Studies were included if they assessed the association between stroke lesion sites and PSP and reported 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for relevant outcomes. A random-effects model was used to pool incidence estimates, and heterogeneity across studies was evaluated. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on different PSP subtypes, including post-stroke shoulder pain (PSSP) and central post-stroke pain (CPSP).ResultsFrom an initial pool of 14,631 screened studies, 25 met the eligibility criteria, involving a total of 3,812 stroke patients. The meta-analysis revealed a higher incidence of PSP (49% [95% CI: 0.41–0.56]) in patients with left-sided cerebral stroke compared to those with right-sided stroke. Subgroup analyses indicated significant associations between left-sided stroke lesions and both PSSP (50% [95% CI: 0.39–0.61]) and CPSP (41% [95% CI: 0.37–0.45]). Additionally, thalamic lesions were significantly associated with PSP in 36% (95% CI: 0.17–0.58) of cases. Despite some heterogeneity (I2 = 96%), no significant publication bias was detected based on funnel plot analysis and Egger’s test (P = 0.164).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis provided significant association between left-sided cerebral stroke and the increased incidence of PSP, particularly in patients with PSSP and CPSP. Thalamic stroke was notably associated with the development of PSP. These results underscore the importance of considering stroke injury sites in the assessment and management of PSP during stroke rehabilitation. Background

Stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability and mortality worldwide, profoundly impacting both patients and healthcare systems. Among its numerous complications, post-stroke pain (PSP) is a particularly debilitating condition that hinders rehabilitation and diminishes patients’ quality of life [1, 2]. PSP encompasses diverse syndromes, such as central post-stroke pain (CPSP), nociceptive pain (e.g., musculoskeletal pain, shoulder pain), spasticity-related pain, and headaches, with prevalence rates ranging from 10% to 45.8% due to variations in clinical presentation, diagnostic criteria, and pathophysiological mechanisms [3, 4].

The neurobiological basis of PSP is multifactorial, involving maladaptive plasticity, central sensitization, neuroinflammation, and disrupted pain modulatory networks. Neuroimaging studies demonstrate that lesions in specific brain regions, including the thalamus (particularly the ventroposterior nucleus), insular cortex, primary and secondary somatosensory cortices, rostral anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, periaqueductal gray (PAG), and rostral ventromedial medulla are implicated in distinct pain syndromes [5–7]. These regions are integral to pain processing and modulation, and their damage can impair sensory perception, reduce descending pain inhibition, and promote central sensitization, leading to persistent pain. For example, thalamic strokes affecting the ventroposterior nucleus are strongly associated with CPSP, a neuropathic pain condition caused by disrupted pain-processing pathways. Similarly, lesions in the primary somatosensory cortex or posterior insula are shown to alter sensory and pain signal processing, enhancing pain perception. Kandić et al. linked anterior cingulate cortex damage to musculoskeletal pain, while Chen et al. (2021) identified PAG lesions as a significant predictor of pain persistence and severity.

Despite these insights, the precise relationship between stroke lesion topography and PSP incidence remains unclear. Existing studies often rely on retrospective data, have small sample sizes, and lack standardized diagnostic criteria, leading to inconsistencies in PSP subtypes classification. Additionally, many studies fail to account for confounding factors such as stroke severity, comorbid conditions, and rehabilitation interventions, which influence pain outcomes [8–10]. These limitations highlight the need for a systematic approach to investigate how specific stroke injury sites contribute to PSP.

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes data from high-quality studies with well-characterized patient cohorts and standardized diagnostic criteria for PSP. By focusing on studies with comprehensive clinical evaluations and control for confounding variables, this review provides a nuanced understanding of PSP’s neurobiological underpinnings. The findings aim to inform the development of diagnostic tools and therapeutic strategies to improve patients outcomes and quality of life.

Methods

This study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (Registration No. CRD42019139928).

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science using keywords and MeSH terms related to 'pain' and 'stroke' (full search strategies available in Supplementary Material 1).

Study selection

Eligible studies met the following criteria: 1) Evaluation of the association between stroke lesion site and PSP; 2) Provision of 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for effect size calculation; 3) Use of computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for stroke diagnosis; 4) Inclusion of human subjects and publication in English; 5) For duplicate studies, the version containing the most comprehensive data was selected; 6) Interventional studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), were excluded during screening, as the focus was on observational studies evaluating the association between stroke lesion sites and PSP incidence.

Data extraction

Two independent investigators (SHM and YFZ) extracted data based on the above mentioned eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third investigator (ZFZ). The extracted data included patient source and research type, sample size, number of PSP cases, PSP identification and assessment, pain type, stroke type and lesion sites, and history of stroke and pain. Authors were not contacted to obtain unpublished data.

Quality assessment of studies

The quality of cohort and case–control studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11], with scores ranging from 0 (the lowest) to 9 (the highest). Only studies with a score of 4 or higher were included. Cross-sectional studies were evaluated using an 11-item questionnaire recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), with studies scoring 0–3 excluded due to low quality. Independent assessments were conducted by two other investigators (BF and XYL). As the included studies were exclusively observational (cohort, case–control, and cross-sectional), no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, and thus, no RCT-specific risk of bias tool (e.g., Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool) was applied.

Statistical analysis

The effect size for this meta-analysis was defined as the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for the relationship between stroke lesion sites and the presence of PSP. Proportions were used to report the frequency of pain in subgroups based on lesion location, while the primary outcome of interest was OR, which calculated for each study. The statistical analysis was performed using Stata 12.0 software (StataCorp., College Station, TX, USA). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistics, and values above 50% indicated significant heterogeneity. A random-effects model was applied to calculate pooled ORs and CIs across studies. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the impact of study quality and patient characteristics on the overall results. Publication bias was evaluated using both Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test, and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Meta-regression analysis was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including study characteristics, such as sample size, stroke type, and lesion location [12]. In addition to pooled ORs, proportions were also calculated for specific subgroups, such as left versus right hemisphere strokes, to further investigate the incidence of PSP in relation to lesion sites. This approach allowed for a deeper understanding of stroke-related pain mechanisms and improved assessment tools are critical for timely diagnosis and personalized management of PSP.

Results

Characteristics of the included studies

A comprehensive Literature search identified 14,631 articles. Following a systematic screening process, 12,122 articles were excluded during the title and abstract review phase. A detailed evaluation of the full texts led to the selection of 196 articles for further assessment. Ultimately, the meta-analysis included 25 original studies comprising a total of 3,812 stroke patients. All 25 included studies were observational (cohort, case–control, or cross-sectional), with no RCTs meeting the eligibility criteria. A flow diagram detailing the study selection process, along with reasons for exclusion, is presented in Fig. 1. The clinicodemographic characteristics of the included studies, such as patient age, sex, stroke severity, stroke type, and other relevant factors, are documented in Supplementary Material 2 (Table S1) [13–37]. Proportions are reported to describe the frequency of PSP in specific subgroups (e.g., left-sided vs. right-sided stroke), while ORs were calculated to quantify the association between stroke lesion sites and PSP incidence.

Association between stroke injury sites and incidence of PSP

The meta-analysis investigated the incidence of PSP in stroke patients, concentrating primarily on stroke location. A total of 1,392 cases were included in the analysis of stroke-related pain, and significant heterogeneity was found across the studies (I2 = 96%, P < 0.01). A random-effects model was used to pool the data, revealing that 49% had left-sided cerebral strokes (95% CI: 0.41–0.56) among patients with PSP, suggesting a potential lateralization pattern in stroke-related pain, as illustrated in Fig. 2. A funnel plot and Egger’s test (t = −1.45, P = 0.164) confirmed no significant publication bias (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis

A more detailed subgroup analysis was performed, considering variables, such as stroke type (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic), hemisphere of injury (left vs. right), stroke severity (mild, moderate, severe) to provide insights into how these factors may influence the development of PSP.

Stroke type

In ischemic stroke patients, the incidence of PSP was significantly higher compared to hemorrhagic stroke patients (55% vs. 38%, P < 0.05). The association between ischemic stroke and PSP was particularly pronounced in patients with moderate-to-severe stroke, where 63% of patients with moderate stroke and 70% of patients with severe ischemic stroke reported PSP. Conversely, in hemorrhagic stroke patients, 42% of those with mild stroke reported PSP, while the incidence dropped to 30% in those with moderate or severe stroke.

Hemisphere of injury

Left-sided stroke was associated with a higher incidence of PSP (49%, 95% CI: 0.41–0.56) compared to right sided stroke (36%, P = 0.02). This trend was more pronounced in ischemic stroke, where 58% of patients with left-sided ischemic stroke reported PSP compared to 45% with right-sided ischemic stroke (P = 0.02). The stronger association with left-sided stroke, particularly for PSSP, may reflect disruptions in the left hemisphere’s motor and sensory pathways, which are critical for motor planning and sensory processing, especially in right-handed individuals. Right-sided stroke, however, exhibited a higher incidence of PSP in patients with mild stroke (47%) compared to those with moderate or severe stroke (35%).

Stroke severity

Stroke severity played a significant role in the incidence of PSP. Among mild stroke patients, only 36% of patients reported PSP. However, in moderate stroke patients, 52% of patients developed PSP, and this increased to 68% in those with severe stroke (P < 0.001). These results highlight that the incidence of PSP rises with increasing stroke severity.

These results suggest that while left-sided stroke, particularly in ischemic patients with moderate-to-severe stroke, is more strongly linked to PSP, right-sided stroke should not be overlooked. Subgroup analyses for comorbidities (e.g., depression, diabetes) were not conducted due to inconsistent reporting across the included studies.

Association between stroke injury sites and incidence of post-stroke shoulder pain (PSSP) and CPSP

The analysis of PSSP and CPSP in relation to stroke location provided valuable insights into how stroke injury sites influence the development of these specific pain syndromes. For PSSP, the analysis included 760 cases, revealing substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 97%, P < 0.01) (Fig. 4), suggesting variability in study design, inconsistent definitions of PSSP, differences in imaging resolution, and varying time intervals between stroke onset and pain assessment. Despite this, the pooled results from a random-effects model indicated that 50% of PSSP patients had left-sided cerebral stroke (95% CI: 0.39–0.61), as shown in Fig. 5. This suggests a strong association between left hemispheric stroke and PSSP, warranting further investigation into the mechanisms linking stroke lateralization to shoulder pain.

For CPSP, the analysis of 632 cases revealed no heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 0, P = 0.49), indicating greater consistency in diagnostic criteria and study methodologies for this subtype (Fig 6). The pooled results from a fixed-effects model demonstrated that 41% of CPSP patients had left-sided cerebral stroke (95% CI: 0.37–0.45), as depicted in Fig. 7. While left-sided stroke remains a key incidence factor for CPSP, the association was less pronounced compared to PSSP, suggesting that CPSP may be more evenly distributed in both hemispheres.

Thalamic stroke and stroke-related pain

The relationship between thalamic stroke and PSP was also analyzed. Among patients with stroke-related pain, although significant heterogeneity was found (I2 = 96%, P < 0.01) (Fig. 8), 36% experienced thalamic stroke (95% CI: 0.17–0.58) (Fig. 9), indicating significant variation in study design (e.g., cohort vs. cross-sectional), differences in PSP definitions and assessment methods, variability in imaging techniques (CT vs. MRI), and inconsistencies in the timing of pain assessment post-stroke. Meta-regression analysis identified sample size, stroke type, and lesion location as partial contributors to heterogeneity, though further exploration is needed. These findings suggest that thalamic stroke may contribute to a subset of stroke-related pain, while variability in study design and patient characteristics requires further investigation to clarify the strength and consistency of this association.

Secondary analysis of stroke locations in hemispheres

To investigate the relationship between specific stroke locations in each hemisphere and PSP, secondary analysis was conducted. The results revealed distinct patterns of association between stroke location and different types of PSP, including PSSP and CPSP.

Left hemisphere cortical stroke

Cortical stroke in the left hemisphere was strongly associated with PSP, particularly PSSP. Among patients with left cortical lesions, 61% developed PSP (95% CI: 0.54–0.68), and 48% developed PSSP (95% CI: 0.40–0.56). This indicates a significant relationship between left-sided cortical stroke and the development of shoulder pain, suggesting that disruptions in the left hemisphere’s sensory and motor pathways, including the primary somatosensory cortex and motor cortex, may play a key role in PSP development. Notably, the incidence of PSSP was higher in patients with left-sided ischemic stroke (65%) compared to hemorrhagic stroke (46%) (P = 0.04).

Left Hemisphere subcortical Stroke

Left-sided subcortical stroke was associated with a moderate incidence of both PSP and CPSP. Among patients with left subcortical lesions, 42% developed PSP (95% CI: 0.35–0.50), while 33% developed CPSP (95% CI: 0.27–0.40). These results indicate that subcortical lesions in the left hemisphere may be less strongly associated with PSP compared to cortical lesions, while contribute to both peripheral and central pain syndromes.

Right hemisphere cortical stroke

Right cortical stroke, while being less frequently associated with PSP or CPSP than left-sided stroke, exhibited notable associations. Approximately 37% of patients with right cortical stroke reported PSP (95% CI: 0.28–0.46), and 29% developed CPSP (95% CI: 0.20–0.38). These findings suggest that cortical lesions in the right hemisphere may play a less pronounced role in the development of PSP, while should not be disregarded, particularly in the context of other factors such as stroke severity and patient age.

Right Hemisphere subcortical Stroke

In contrast, subcortical stroke in the right hemisphere was more strongly associated with CPSP. Of the patients with right subcortical lesions, 52% developed CPSP (95% CI: 0.44–0.60), a notably higher incidence than in those with left subcortical lesions (38%, P = 0.02). The thalamus and internal capsule were particularly implicated, and 58% of right thalamic stroke patients reported CPSP (95% CI: 0.50–0.66), suggesting a potential relationship between thalamic damage and central pain syndromes.

These findings underscore the importance of specific stroke locations in each hemisphere in determining the likelihood of developing various types of PSP. Left hemisphere cortical lesions, especially those involving the motor and sensory cortices, were most strongly associated with PSSP. Conversely, right hemisphere subcortical lesions, particularly in the thalamus, were strongly linked to CPSP. This detailed analysis of stroke location in the left and right hemispheres provides additional evidence for the role of neuroanatomical disruptions in the development of PSP and highlights the importance of considering stroke localization when diagnosing and managing PSP.

Quality assessment and sensitivity analysis

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the NOS for cohort studies and the AHRQ standards for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The quality assessment results for RCTs (incidence of bias table) are summarized in Supplementary Material 2 (Table S2), and those for cohort studies are presented in Supplementary Material 2 (Table S3). All studies achieved a minimum quality score of 4. Quality assessment results for cross-sectional studies are detailed in Supplementary Material 2 (Table S4).

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled ORs. The results indicated high stability of the meta-analysis, with no significant variation in the ORs when any single study was removed, confirming the robustness of the findings. However, sensitivity analysis of the PSSP subgroup indicated lower stability, likely due to the high variability among some included studies.

Incidence of publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s funnel plots and Egger’s linear regression test for each analysis. The funnel plots (Figs. 3, 7, 8, and 9) showed no significant asymmetry, suggesting minimal publication bias. Egger’s test results confirmed this, with p-values of 0.164 for overall PSP, 0.838 for PSSP, 0.881 for CPSP, and 0.753 for thalamic stroke-related PSP, indicating no statistically significant publication bias across these analyses. These findings support the reliability of the pooled estimates, although the high heterogeneity in some analyses (e.g., PSP and PSSP) warrants cautious interpretation.

Discussion

PSP significantly impairs daily life and necessitates careful consideration particularly in the diagnosis and post-stroke care [38]. Understanding the incidence factors of PSP is crucial for clinical diagnosis and treatment. Established incidence factors for PSP include female gender, excessive alcohol consumption, stroke severity, depression, diabetes, and vascular disease, influencing lower limb blood vessels [7]. Hemorrhagic stroke and initial mobility limitations are also noted as influential factors [39]. Additionally, PSP is more prevalent in older individuals from 3 to 18 months post-stroke onset [40]. However, the role of stroke injury sites as a potential confounder in PSP diagnosis remains elusive, despite some reports suggesting a higher incidence of pain in patients with right-sided hemisphere stroke [41]. This warrants further investigation using studies with larger sample sizes for validation.

Findings of the current study

This meta-analysis investigated the relationship between stroke location and PSP, focusing on PSSP and CPSP. We found that 49% of patients with left-sided strokes experienced PSP, compared to 36% with right-sided strokes, suggesting a hemispheric difference in pain susceptibility. Subgroup analyses revealed that cortical strokes were associated with a 48% incidence of PSP, while subcortical strokes had a 42% incidence. Thalamic strokes were particularly linked to CPSP, with 58% of right thalamic stroke patients reporting CPSP, highlighting the thalamus’s critical role in pain processing. The stronger association of left-sided strokes with PSSP contrasts with some prior reports of higher pain incidence in right-sided strokes, particularly for CPSP [42–47]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in pain subtypes, with left-sided cortical strokes driving PSSP and right-sided subcortical strokes contributing to CPSP.

Hemispheric lesion and PSP

This study revealed that patients with left hemisphere stroke were more likely to develop PSSP, a subtype of PSP characterized by moderate to severe pain. Specifically, 50% of PSSP patients had left-sided cerebral strokes, compared to 36% with right-sided strokes. This suggests a hemispheric difference in pain susceptibility, potentially due to disruptions in the left hemisphere’s motor and sensory pathways, particularly in right-handed individuals [48]. In contrast, prior studies have reported a higher incidence of pain, including CPSP, in right-sided stroke [6]. Our findings partially align with these reports, as right-sided subcortical strokes were associated with a higher incidence of CPSP compared to left-sided subcortical strokes [49].

Regional stroke lesions and PSP

Thalamic lesions are strongly associated with CPSP, particularly Dejerine-Roussy syndrome, which accounts for approximately one-third of PSP cases and is characterized by intense, episodic pain with allodynia and hyperalgesia [50–52]. Our findings indicate that 58% of right thalamic stroke patients developed CPSP, likely due to disruptions in spinothalamic pathways and the ventroposterior nucleus. Cortical strokes, particularly in the parietal lobe, were associated with PSSP (48%), reflecting disruptions in sensory and motor pathways. Small vessel infarcts, often involving the thalamus or pons, also contribute to PSP, particularly CPSP [53, 54]. Other regions, such as the amygdala, hippocampus, and insula, may modulate pain perception through emotional and affective processing, though their roles are less well-defined [55, 56]. Future research should use advanced neuroimaging to map functional connectivity between these regions to guide targeted interventions, such as neuromodulation, for PSP management.

Challenges in PSP identification

This meta-analysis identified significant associations between specific stroke locations and PSP subtypes, such as left-sided cortical strokes with PSSP (50%) and right-sided thalamic strokes with CPSP (58%). However, establishing a universal causal link across all PSP subtypes remains challenging due to heterogeneity in study designs and the influence of confounders. PSP identification is complicated by its subjective nature and the absence of a standardized assessment scale, which may lead to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis [2]. Post-stroke impairments, such as motor dysfunction, language deficits, cognitive decline, and depressive symptoms, often overshadow pain symptoms, as patients may prioritize reporting functional limitations over pain [57]. For example, hemiparesis or aphasia may limit patients’ ability to communicate pain, while depression may amplify pain perception or mask it as a somatic complaint. Variability in the timing of pain assessment post-stroke further complicates diagnosis, as pain may emerge months after stroke onset. Future studies should develop standardized PSP assessment tools and account for these confounding impairments to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Potential confounders of PSP

This meta-analysis highlighted the relationship between stroke injury sites and PSP, while also identifying potential confounders, such as gender, alcohol consumption, and stroke severity. Our subgroup analysis found no significant difference in PSP incidence between men and women (47% vs. 50%), though prior studies suggest women may experience a higher prevalence of certain PSP types, possibly due to hormonal or sensory factors [58]. Alcohol consumption, a known risk factor for stroke, was inconsistently reported across studies, limiting our ability to assess its impact on PSP. Stroke severity was a significant determinant, with 68% of severe stroke patients developing PSP compared to 36% of mild stroke patients [59]. The potential interaction between severe strokes, female gender, and alcohol use may amplify PSP risk, though this hypothesis requires confirmation through studies with standardized reporting and advanced statistical analyses, such as multivariate regression, to explore these complex relationships. Future research should prioritize gender-specific analyses and consistent documentation of confounders to improve PSP management strategies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the observational nature of the included studies introduces the risk of bias, including information bias, selection bias, and confounding factors that were not consistently controlled across studies. Although subgroup analyses were conducted, factors, such as comorbidities and rehabilitation status were not consistently accounted for, which might limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the use of language-restricted databases might lead to the exclusion of relevant non-English studies, potentially omitting valuable insights. Furthermore, while stroke locations were broadly classified into cortical and subcortical regions, a more detailed categorization, such as differentiating specific subcortical structures (e.g., the basal ganglia or internal capsule), could yield a more precise understanding of the relationship between lesion sites and PSP [60, 61].

Future directions

Future research should aim to address the gaps identified in this study, particularly with regard to controlling for individual-level variables, such as comorbidities, and stroke severity in subgroup analyses. Additionally, studies should aim to incorporate advanced imaging techniques to precisely map lesion locations and their correlation with PSP incidence [62]. The application of multivariate meta-regression models and individual participant data meta-analysis could further refine the understanding of how specific stroke characteristics, such as precise lesion location and size, contribute to pain development. These approaches would allow for more accurate and individualized predictions of PSP onset and severity, facilitating the development of more targeted therapeutic interventions.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis significantly advances our understanding of the relationship between stroke lesion sites and the incidence of post-stroke pain (PSP), with a particular focus on central post-stroke pain (CPSP) and post-stroke shoulder pain (PSSP). Our findings demonstrate that left-sided stroke and lesions involving the thalamus are more strongly associated with PSP, particularly CPSP. The observed hemispheric differences, particularly the stronger association of left-sided strokes with PSSP, may reflect disruptions in motor and sensory pathways, especially in right-handed individuals. Thalamic lesions, particularly in the right hemisphere, were strongly linked to CPSP, likely due to their impact on spinothalamic pathways and the ventroposterior nucleus. These findings provide a neurobiological framework for understanding PSP subtypes and emphasize the need for targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies tailored to lesion topography. By integrating stroke location into clinical assessments, healthcare providers can better identify at-risk patients and implement targeted rehabilitation strategies, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and quality of life.
