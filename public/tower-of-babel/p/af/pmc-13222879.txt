Multi-level semi-supervised GAN enables accurate segmentation of breast ultrasound images
Authors: Yuting Zhang, Jia Wang, Ming Jiang, Hongyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultrasound imaging is a crucial modality for breast cancer diagnosis, with lesion segmentation being an essential component of computer-aided diagnosis systems. However, the high cost and effort associated with pixel-level annotation significantly hinder the advancement of breast ultrasound (BUS) image segmentation algorithms. In this paper, we propose a multi-level semi-supervised generative adversarial network (MLSS-GAN) that incorporates a generator and a novel multi-level classifier acting as the discriminator. While some previous approaches have incorporated multi-level adversarial learning, our discriminator employs an encoder for feature extraction and two decoders designed for distinct image-level and pixel-level adversarial tasks, enabling more comprehensive segmentation supervision. Specifically, one decoder performs image-level classification, while the other conducts pixel-level classification. This multi-level adversarial strategy compels the generator to produce images that closely resemble real BUS images at different feature representations, thereby enriching the classification feature space. To assess the effectiveness of the proposed method, comprehensive comparisons with both fully supervised and semi-supervised approaches are conducted on the BUSI dataset. Experimental results demonstrated the superior performance of our method, especially under conditions with only a limited number of annotated BUS images.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related death among females1. Ultrasound imaging offers several advantages, including low cost, rapid acquisition, and noninvasive procedures, making it widely used for breast cancer diagnosis. Accurate segmentation of tumor regions is an essential component of clinical assessment2. However, the manual interpretation of breast ultrasound (BUS) images depends heavily on the expertise and experience of clinicians3. Such expertise is not always available, particularly in underdeveloped regions. To enable rapid and efficient breast cancer screening, computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems have been developed for automated tumor detection4. Among the tasks in CAD systems, segmentation of breast tumor areas from the background is critical for both diagnosis and subsequent treatment planning5.

In recent years, deep learning models have achieved remarkable success in medical image segmentation. Most of these models adopt a fully supervised paradigm, leveraging networks such as Fully Convolutional Network (FCN)6 and U-Net7 to extract multi-scale features. Despite their success, fully supervised methods rely on large-scale annotated datasets, which are expensive and time-consuming for clinicians to produce. As an alternative, semi-supervised learning methods have been explored, requiring only a small fraction of annotated images while utilizing large amounts of unannotated data4. To address the challenges of limited annotation, we propose a multi-level semi-supervised generative adversarial network (MLSS-GAN). MLSS-GAN is built upon the hypothesis that features in an ideal representation reflect the underlying causes of the data. If label y is among the salient factors generating data x, then a representation suitable for modeling p(x) should also be valuable for modeling the conditional probability p(y|x) 8. The relationship between the marginal probability p(x) and the conditional probability p(y|x) can be expressed by Bayes’ rule:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} p(y|x) = \frac{{p(x|y)p(y)}}{p(x)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Our motivation objective is to leverage unannotated data to discover hidden features that facilitate accurate prediction. In the proposed MLSS-GAN, the architecture consists of a generator and a multi-headed discriminator, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The generator synthesizes realistic BUS images, aiming to approximate the true data distribution p(x) observed in the partially annotated dataset. The discriminator classifies images as real or generated (fake), thereby learning more discriminative features for BUS images. Unlike previous generative adversarial networks (GANs) segmentation methods9–11, which introduce image-level adversarial losses, our discriminator incorporates both image-level and pixel-level adversarial signals within a unified multi-head discriminator architecture. Furthermore, it learns to map BUS images to semantic segmentation masks, using image-level information to guide pixel-level predictions.

Related work

BUS image segmentation

In recent years, deep learning models have emerged as powerful tools for BUS image segmentation. Yap et al.13 systematically evaluated the performance of U-Net14 and FCN6 for breast lesion segmentation, reporting that these architectures substantially outperform traditional image processing approaches. Building upon this, Almajalid et al.15 improved the segmentation accuracy of U-Net by integrating contrast enhancement and speckle reduction techniques, thereby refining input image quality prior to segmentation. To address the limitations of plain convolutional units in deep networks, Zhuang et al.16 incorporated residual units into the U-Net framework, which not only strengthened edge representation but also mitigated the degradation problem associated with increased network depth. Furthermore, Shareef et al.17 introduced the Small Tumor-Aware Network (STAN), which fuses contextual information with high-resolution image features to enhance the segmentation of tumors spanning a wide range of sizes. Xue et al.18 proposed a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) augmented with a global guidance block and boundary detection modules, leading to significant advancements in BUS lesion segmentation performance. Dong et al.19 proposed a feature-guided CNN to address the challenges of noise in ultrasound image analysis. In addition, Luo et al.20 enhanced the classical U-shaped network architecture by designing novel context refinement blocks within a multi-level context refinement network, thereby optimizing feature integration and effectively reducing semantic gaps to improve segmentation accuracy.

Semi-supervised segmentation

Semi-supervised image segmentation methods have been developed to address the challenge of limited annotated data. Recent studies have explored the use of GANs within semi-supervised learning frameworks to improve semantic segmentation performance. Broadly, there are two principal approaches for employing GANs in semi-supervised segmentation. The first approach leverages GANs to generate additional synthetic images, thereby enriching the feature space available to the segmentation network. For instance, Souly et al.21 utilized GANs to generate y training images, which enhanced the discriminative power of the segmentation model. The second approach involves generating pseudo-labels for each pixel in annotated images. Hung et al.9 adopted an FCN–based discriminator to produce dense probabilistic maps at the pixel level, facilitating more accurate segmentation without full supervision. In addition, Mittal et al.10 introduced a feature matching loss, which is critical for stabilizing GAN training, particularly when only a small number of annotated samples are available. Furthermore, Wang et al.11 improved segmentation accuracy by utilizing feature density, thereby enhancing model training in sparsely populated regions of the feature space. However, their approaches primarily focus on the pixel-level aspects, without jointly exploiting image-level adversarial signals within the discriminator.

GANs in medical image segmentation

Data scarcity poses a particularly acute challenge in the field of medical imaging. To address this issue, a variety of GAN–based strategies have been developed. For example, Li et al.22 proposed an adversarial training framework for brain tumor segmentation, where a discriminator was employed to distinguish between generated and real segmentation masks. The adversarial loss from the discriminator was iteratively fed back to the generator, reducing the discrepancy between synthetic and ground-truth labels while simultaneously reinforcing spatial contiguity through higher-order loss terms. Similarly, Lahiri et al.23 introduced an unsupervised adversarial loss within a structured prediction architecture, achieving superior performance compared to fully supervised neural networks for retinal vascular segmentation, even when annotation ratios were extremely low. Moeskops et al.24 consistently leveraged adversarial training to enhance CNN-based brain MR image segmentation by designing a loss function that encourages the generator to produce segmentations that are difficult to distinguish from manual annotations. This adversarial loss was optimized jointly with the conventional cross-entropy loss and validated on multiple datasets and network architectures. Despite these advances, most existing methods focus exclusively on pixel-level discrimination, thereby overlooking the potential benefits of incorporating image-level information into the segmentation process.

Our method also adopts a generative adversarial framework to address the issue of limited annotated data. In addition, image-level information is leveraged to uncover latent features that contribute to more accurate segmentation. The proposed model consists of a generator and a multi-level classifier, which serves as the discriminator within the GAN framework. Specifically, the classifier incorporates a single encoder for feature extraction and two decoders for multi-level adversarial learning: one dedicated to image-level classification and the other to pixel-level classification. This multi-level classification strategy compels the generated images to closely resemble real ultrasound images across different feature map scales, thereby enriching the feature space and further enhancing segmentation performance.

Methodology

The goal of this study is to develop a unified framework for semi-supervised BUS segmentation. Analyzing the influence factors in semi-supervised segmentation, we propose a multi-level semi-supervised, named MLSS-GAN, which consists of a generator and a multi-level classifier, acting as the discriminator in the GAN framework. Image-level and pixel-level losses are simultaneously used to leverage the unannotated data to assist the annotated data for segmentation.

The unannotated data serve as a regularizer to improve the generalization capability of the model. In MLSS-GAN, a generator is employed to synthesize data from random noise inputs, which are subsequently evaluated by a multi-level classifier acting as the discriminator. This classifier is designed not only to distinguish between real and synthetic data, but also to perform segmentation on ultrasound images. The generator is trained to align the distribution of synthesized data with the real data distribution p(x) derived from the partially annotated dataset. This process enables the discriminator to learn more discriminative features for BUS image representation and to filter out irrelevant features. Besides, the annotated data are utilized to establish the relationship between these features and the segmentation task.

To enhance segmentation performance, the conventional GAN architecture is modified by replacing the original discriminator with a U-shaped network that assigns a label to each pixel. Multi-levels discriminator

MLSS-GAN is integrated into the GAN framework, where it functions as the discriminator to distinguish between real and fake data at both the image and pixel levels, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Besides, MLSS-GAN serves as a classifier, assigning semantic labels to each pixel as well as to the entire image.

In this work, we enhance MLSS-GAN by adopting a modified U-shaped network as the discriminator. The U-shaped architecture comprises a feature encoder and a decoder module 5. Specifically, the encoder is substituted with a pretrained ResNet-50 25, which alleviates the computational cost of training from scratch and addresses the degradation problem through the use of identity connections. Our discriminator utilizes two decoders for multi-level adversarial learning. Specifically, one decoder is dedicated to image-level classification and discrimination, while the other focuses on pixel-level classification and discrimination. The pixel-level decoder module is responsible for reconstructing high-level semantic features extracted by the encoder. Materials and experiments

Datasets

In our experiments, we employ the BUSI dataset12, a publicly available benchmark commonly used for breast cancer classification, detection, and segmentation tasks. The BUSI dataset consists of 780 ultrasound images collected from 600 women aged between 25 and 75 years, with an average image resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$500 \times 500$$\end{document} pixels. The dataset comprises 133 normal cases, 487 benign cases, and 210 malignant cases, each accompanied by corresponding ground truth annotations. In the BUSI dataset, the class label of each lesion image (benign or malignant) is consistently identical to its corresponding mask label. All pixels within the lesion mask are assigned its class, while pixels outside the mask are annotated as background. For normal images, where no lesion is present, the entire mask is annotated as background. Additionally, the “fake” label is employed exclusively for generated images in adversarial training, consistent with adversarial segmentation frameworks established in recent studies10,21.

Experimental settings

MLSS-GAN is implemented in Python (v3.10) using the PyTorch framework (v1.12.1). Training is conducted on a workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU and an Intel Core i9-12900K CPU. The batch size is set to 6. Data augmentation includes horizontal and vertical flipping. All grayscale BUS images are resized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256 \times 256$$\end{document} pixels and converted to RGB format to ensure compatibility with the pretrained ResNet-50 backbone. Moreover, all input images are normalized to the range [-1,1]. The weights of ResNet-50 in the discriminator are initialized with parameters pretrained on the ImageNet dataset 31. Both the generator and discriminator are trained for 500 epochs using the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer, with initial learning rates set to 0.003 and 0.0003, respectively. The higher learning rate for the generator is chosen to balance the adversarial dynamics in GAN training. This setting prevents the generator from lagging behind, reducing instability and vanishing gradient issues. Our approach aligns with established practices in recent studies10,21 and is supported by our preliminary experiments. The learning rates are decayed by a factor of 0.1 after 100 epochs. This strategy stabilizes training by enabling rapid early progress and finer convergence as the learning rate decreases25,32,33. The decay point at 100 epochs was chosen empirically, as model performance typically plateaued after this stage in our experiments. Applying decay at this point improves both stability and generalization32.

The dataset is randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:1:2 ratio. We evaluate the proposed MLSS-GAN against seven fully supervised and seven semi-supervised learning methods. For semi-supervised experiments, 20% of the training images are randomly selected as the annotated set, while the remaining images are treated as unannotated. For fully supervised learning, all images and their corresponding annotations are utilized.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce MLSS-GAN, a novel multi-level semi-supervised GAN designed for breast mass segmentation in ultrasound images. Unlike conventional deep learning approaches that typically rely solely on pixel-level supervision or require extensive annotated datasets, MLSS-GAN leverages both image-level and pixel-level discrimination within a unified adversarial framework. This design enables the model to exploit annotated data more effectively, thereby alleviating the need for costly and labor-intensive manual annotations.

Extensive experiments on the BUSI dataset demonstrated that MLSS-GAN achieves superior segmentation performance compared to existing fully supervised and semi-supervised methods. When trained with only 20% annotated data, MLSS-GAN achieves results comparable to or even exceeding those of state-of-the-art fully supervised models, highlighting its robustness in low-annotation scenarios. The ablation studies further validated that both the unsupervised loss and the multi-level discriminator contribute significantly to performance improvements. Specifically, the unsupervised learning approaches encourage the network to learn richer global semantic representations, while supervised learning approaches ensure fine-grained localization of lesions. Notably, the addition of image-level discrimination substantially enhances the model’s capabilities. This improvement is particularly advantageous for complex cases with heterogeneous tissue appearance and indistinct tumor margins, as shown in Fig. 5.

Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. The current validation is limited to the BUS dataset, and the generalizability of MLSS-GAN to different imaging modalities (such as MRI or CT) warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present MLSS-GAN, a novel multi-level semi-supervised GAN for breast mass segmentation in ultrasound images. By introducing a discriminator capable of both image-level and pixel-level adversarial learning, MLSS-GAN fully exploits the potential of annotated data and significantly reduces the need for expensive annotation. The proposed framework was validated on the BUSI dataset, where it consistently outperformed both state-of-the-art fully supervised and semi-supervised models. Our results demonstrate that multi-level adversarial learning not only enhances feature representation but also provides a practical solution for high-precision BUS image segmentation with limited annotation.

A limitation of this study is that patient-wise data splitting is not possible, as the BUSI dataset12 lacks patient identifiers. This poses a potential risk of images from the same patient appearing in different subsets. However, all images are independently reviewed by our breast specialists, who confirm that each image exhibits sufficient heterogeneity in lesion and tissue to minimize this risk. Moreover, our random splitting strategy is consistent with recent BUSI-based studies40,53,54. Looking forward, MLSS-GAN offers promising prospects for large-scale deployment in computer-aided diagnosis systems, especially in scenarios with limited annotated data. Future work will focus on further improving computational efficiency, extending the framework to other imaging modalities and multi-center datasets, and exploring its integration with clinical workflows for enhanced diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.
