Changes in White Blood Cell and Neutrophil Counts in Patients With Schizophrenia Treated With Lithium Carbonate, Adenine, and Ninjin‐Yoei‐To: A Preliminary Study
Authors: Teruyuki Matsuoka, Sumihiro Yamano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimClozapine is often unavailable to patients with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia when white blood cell (WBC) and neutrophil counts do not meet the required thresholds. This study aimed to examine changes in WBC and neutrophil counts and the factors associated with these changes.MethodsThirty‐five patients with schizophrenia who received adenine, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, or lithium carbonate were included in the treatment group. Eighty‐one patients with schizophrenia comprised the control group. A two‐way mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare changes in WBC and neutrophil counts before and after treatment. Stepwise multiple regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with changes in WBC and neutrophil counts.ResultsIn the two‐way mixed ANOVA, WBC and neutrophil counts in the treatment group were significantly lower than those in the control group at baseline (p < 0.001), and the counts at the final examination were significantly higher than the baseline values in the treatment group (p < 0.001). Stepwise multiple regression analyses revealed that adenine and lithium carbonate were significant predictors of changes in WBC (adjusted R2 = 0.173, p < 0.001) and neutrophil counts (adjusted R2 = 0.175, p < 0.001).ConclusionLithium carbonate and adenine may help increase the WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia. These agents may be useful for patients with low WBC and neutrophil counts prior to initiating clozapine treatment.

Introduction

Clozapine is prescribed for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia. Because it is associated with various side effects, including agranulocytosis and diabetic ketoacidosis [1], strict criteria for its use have been established. For example, in Japan, one requirement is a minimum white blood cell (WBC) count of 4 000/mm3 and a neutrophil count of 2 000/mm3 on a blood test performed within 4 weeks prior to enrollment.

However, clozapine is often unavailable to patients with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia because the WBC and neutrophil count criteria are not met. One reason is that antipsychotic medications often cause leukopenia and neutropenia [2]. Additionally, since metabolic syndrome is associated with higher WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia [3], improved eating habits following hospitalization may lower these counts. Although neutrophil counts and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratios are generally higher in patients with schizophrenia than in healthy controls, negative symptoms are not correlated with, or are negatively correlated with, the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio [4]. Therefore, reduced physical activity due to negative symptoms may also lead to lower WBC and neutrophil counts.

In patients with clozapine‐induced leukopenia, various methods have been employed to increase WBC counts. These include oral medications such as lithium carbonate [2, 5, 6], adenine [6, 7, 8], Ninjin‐yoei‐to [8], and cepharanthine [6, 8], as well as granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) injections [2]. These treatments are generally used off‐label. Exercise has also been reported to increase WBC counts [2, 9]. However, a previous systematic review [10] and clinical guidelines [11] have only provided a weak recommendation for the use of lithium carbonate in the treatment of clozapine‐induced neutropenia.

These interventions, medications, and exercise may be beneficial for increasing WBC and neutrophil counts even before initiating clozapine. Reducing or discontinuing antipsychotic drugs and increasing body weight might also improve these counts, as both antipsychotic use and weight loss may contribute to lower values. However, it remains unclear which methods are most effective in increasing WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia.

This study aimed to examine the effects of lithium carbonate, adenine, and Ninjin‐yoei‐to on WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia before or after clozapine administration, as well as to identify the factors associated with these changes.

Methods

Participants

Medical charts from our hospital between April 1, 2020, and September 30, 2024, were retrospectively examined. Notably, 35 patients with schizophrenia who visited or were admitted to our hospital during the period received adenine, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, or lithium carbonate before or after taking clozapine. These patients were included in the treatment group; none of them had physical conditions that could decrease WBC and neutrophil counts. Conversely, patients with schizophrenia who had been treated during the same period, had undergone routine blood testing, and had no physical illness that could affect WBC or neutrophil counts were also retrospectively examined. A total of 81 such patients were identified and included as the control group. This retrospective study was approved by the hospital's Research Ethics Committee (R7‐8).

Assessment

Data were retrospectively obtained from medical records in December 2025. Age, sex, diagnosis, exercise before the blood test, and observation period were extracted. Each attending psychiatrist diagnosed schizophrenia based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition (ICD‐10). WBC and neutrophil counts, chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose, and body weight at baseline and at final examination were recorded. Blood tests were performed in the morning, and WBC and neutrophil counts were tracked monthly from 3 months before to 3 months after the initiation of drug administration (7 consecutive months). Baseline in the treatment group was defined as the time of the first administration of adenine, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, or lithium carbonate. For the control group, blood test data collected over 7 consecutive months during the study period were analyzed. The chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose was calculated using a published conversion table (https://www.js‐pp.or.jp/cp/) [12]. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score [13] was used to evaluate physical comorbidities. The CCI consists of 19 items designed to assess comorbidities such as heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, lung disease, connective tissue disease, gastrointestinal disease, diabetes, tumor, leukemia, or acquired immune deficiency syndrome; a higher score indicates a greater risk of mortality [13].

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons between the two groups were conducted using the t‐test, chi‐square test, and Fisher's exact test. Patients treated with lithium carbonate only were included in the lithium‐treated group. These patients treated with lithium carbonate were further categorized into the high and low serum lithium level groups based on a maximum serum lithium level of 0.4 mEq/L, which may represent the minimum level required to increase WBC and neutrophil counts [14]. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the dose of drugs, serum lithium levels, and changes in WBC or neutrophil counts. A two‐way mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare WBC and neutrophil counts before and after treatment. Variables showing significant differences between the groups were included as covariates. The two‐way mixed ANOVA was conducted for all patients. Since the observation period varied, this analysis was also performed in patients with available blood test data for 7 consecutive months. Stepwise multiple regression analyses were conducted to identify the factors related to changes in WBC and neutrophil counts. Dependent variables were the changes in WBC and neutrophil counts. Independent variables included age, sex, changes in chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose and body weight, CCI score, presence of exercise before the blood test, administration of adenine, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, or lithium carbonate, and observation period. Stepwise entry‐value probabilities were set to 0.05 for entry and 0.10 for removal. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Comparison Between Treatment and Control Groups

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the treatment and control groups. Age, chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose at baseline, number of antipsychotic drugs at baseline, types of antipsychotic drugs at baseline and final examination, changes in chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose, presence of exercise before blood test, and observation period differed significantly between the two groups. However, there was no significant difference in the chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose at the final examination. The number of patients who exercised before the blood test was very small, with only three in the treatment group. The number of patients taking clozapine at the final examination was 13 in the treatment group and 2 in the control group. Among 13 patients taking clozapine in the treatment group, 10 patients were treated with adenine, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, or lithium carbonate before taking clozapine, whereas 3 were treated after taking clozapine. In the treatment group, 6 were outpatients, and 29 were inpatients. Among the inpatients, 17 had been discharged during the study period. In the control group, 15 were outpatients, of whom 13 were hospitalized during the period, whereas 66 were inpatients, of whom 29 remained hospitalized during the period. However, all patients with available blood test data for 7 consecutive months in both groups had been hospitalized during the 7 months.

The comparison between the high (n = 22) and low (n = 6) maximum serum lithium carbonate level groups did not show significant differences in the changes in WBC counts (946 ± 1 736/mm3 vs. 683 ± 1 183/mm3, p = 0.732) and neutrophil counts (885 ± 1 489/mm3 vs. 1133 ± 1 464/mm3, p = 0.720). In patients treated with lithium carbonate only (n = 18), no significant differences were observed in the changes in WBC counts (844 ± 1 955/mm3 vs. 1 500 ± 1 697/mm3, p = 0.658) and neutrophil counts (836 ± 1 696/mm3 vs. 2 400 ± 1 980/mm3, p = 0.242) between the high (n = 16) and low (n = 2) maximum serum lithium level groups.

The maximum serum lithium carbonate levels negatively correlated with the changes in neutrophil counts (r = −0.408, p = 0.031) and WBC counts (r = −0.324, p = 0.092), although the maximum lithium carbonate doses did not correlate with the changes in WBC counts (r = −0.212, p = 0.279) and neutrophil counts (r = −0.162, p = 0.411). The maximum adenine doses significantly correlated with the changes in neutrophil counts (r = −0.519, p = 0.040) but not with the changes in WBC counts (r = −0.375, p = 0.153). In patients treated with lithium carbonate only (n = 18), significant correlations were observed between the maximum serum lithium carbonate levels and changes in WBC counts (r = −0.477, p = 0.046) and neutrophil counts (r = −0.583, p = 0.011), whereas no significant correlations were observed between the maximum lithium carbonate doses and changes in WBC counts (r = −0.438, p = 0.069) and neutrophil counts (r = −0.398, p = 0.102). Additionally, no significant correlations were observed between the maximum adenine doses and changes in WBC counts (r = −0.329, p = 0.589) and neutrophil counts (r = −0.633, p = 0.252) in patients treated with adenine only (n = 5).

Comparison Between WBC and Neutrophil Counts Before and After Treatment

In the two‐way mixed ANOVA comparing WBC counts before and after treatment, age, baseline chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose, changes in chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose, presence of exercise before blood test, and observation period were used as covariates. A significant main effect of group was observed (F(1, 109) = 17.657, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.139), while the main effect of time was not significant (F(1, 109) = 0.000, p = 0.999, partial η2 = 0.000). A significant interaction effect between time and group was found (F(1, 109) = 20.886, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.161). The Bonferroni post hoc test showed that WBC counts in the treatment group were significantly lower than those in the control group at baseline (p < 0.001) and significantly higher at the final examination compared to baseline (p < 0.001) (Figure 1a). WBC counts in the control group decreased significantly at the final examination compared to baseline (p = 0.002) (Figure 1a). Similar results were observed for neutrophil counts (Figure 1b). A significant main effect of group (F(1, 109) = 14.988, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.121) and interaction effect (F(1, 109) = 21.898, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.167) were found, while the main effect of time was not significant (F(1, 109) = 0.365, p = 0.547, partial η2 = 0.003).

Discussion

WBC and neutrophil counts increased significantly after administration of lithium carbonate, adenine, or Ninjin‐yoei‐to in patients with schizophrenia. These increases became evident approximately 2 months after drug initiation. Among the variables examined, administration of lithium carbonate or adenine was more strongly associated with increases in WBC and neutrophil counts than age, sex, reduced antipsychotic dose, increased body weight, or exercise prior to blood testing.

Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of lithium carbonate, adenine, G‐CSF, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, and cepharanthine in increasing WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia receiving clozapine. A prior systematic review [10] and clinical guidelines [11] reported that lithium carbonate may be beneficial for clozapine‐induced neutropenia, although the guidelines note that lithium carbonate does not necessarily prevent agranulocytosis and that such use is off‐label. One study showed that 28 of 30 patients with schizophrenia were able to continue clozapine with adenine administration [7], while another found that 23 of 30 patients successfully resumed clozapine following G‐CSF injection [15]. Two case reports describe patients who continue clozapine treatment with combinations of lithium carbonate, adenine, and cepharanthine [6], or adenine, cepharanthine, and Ninjin‐yoei‐to [8]. In the present study, 13 of 35 patients in the treatment group received clozapine, and 10 of these 13 were able to initiate clozapine following improvements in WBC and neutrophil counts after receiving lithium carbonate, adenine, or Ninjin‐yoei‐to. These findings suggest that such agents, particularly lithium carbonate and adenine, may also be effective in treating neutropenia before the initiation of clozapine.

In this study, the maximum serum lithium carbonate levels and maximum adenine doses demonstrated a negative correlation with the changes in WBC and/or neutrophil counts. This may be because the dose was increased when WBC and neutrophil counts did not rise at the lower doses; however, even after dose escalation, no increase in WBC and neutrophil counts was observed. A previous review reported that a minimum serum lithium carbonate level of 0.4 mEq/L may be necessary to increase WBC and neutrophil counts, although no clear dose‐dependent relationship was identified [14]. Therefore, low‐ to moderate‐dose lithium carbonate or adenine may be sufficient to increase WBC and neutrophil counts, and if no response is observed at that dose, increasing the dose may be ineffective.

WBC and neutrophil counts tended to decrease over 6 months and showed a significant decrease at final examination in the control group. The decreased WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia may be due to antipsychotic medication use [2], improved metabolic syndrome [3], and reduced physical activity [4]. These factors may have contributed to a gradual decrease in WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia, although a change in body weight was not associated with changes in WBC and neutrophil counts in the present study.

A previous meta‐analysis reported no significant difference in the risk of neutropenia between clozapine and other antipsychotic agents (risk ratio: 1.45; 95% CI: 0.87–2.42) [16]. This finding suggests that reducing antipsychotic doses may help improve WBC and neutrophil counts. However, in this study, dose reduction did not improve these counts in all participants. This may be because both the mean baseline chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose and the extent of dose reduction were relatively small (711 ± 381 mg and – 110 ± 402 mg, respectively). However, a decrease in chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose was identified as one of the predictors of increased WBC counts in the 99 patients in the lithium‐treated (n = 18) and control (n = 81) groups. Therefore, reducing the dose of antipsychotic drugs may lead to an increase in WBC and neutrophil counts.

Antipsychotic drugs are known to cause leukopenia and neutropenia. Although physical illness was not associated with WBC or neutrophil counts in this study, such conditions should still be ruled out. A previous case report described the use of G‐CSF for suspected antipsychotic‐induced cytopenia, which was later diagnosed as acute promyelocytic leukemia [17]. Therefore, physical comorbidities should be considered as possible causes of leukopenia or neutropenia in patients with schizophrenia.

This study had some limitations. First, the number of participants, particularly those in the lithium‐treated group and those who received Ninjin‐yoei‐to or engaged in exercise before blood testing, was small. Therefore, the analyses were conducted by combining patients who received adenine, Ninjin‐yoei‐to, or lithium carbonate. The results of the lithium‐treated group (n = 18) were similar to those of the all treatment group (n = 35), although a slight difference was observed. However, the synergistic effects of each drug were not investigated. Moreover, the treatment and control groups may have been heterogeneous and insufficiently powered. Second, cepharanthine and G‐CSF were not administered at the study site. Third, some factors, including adherence to drugs, meals, and activity levels, may have varied depending on whether the patient was hospitalized or received outpatient care. Moreover, the number of antipsychotic drugs at baseline and the types of antipsychotic drugs at baseline and final examination were significantly different between the treatment and control groups. These factors may have influenced the results. Fourth, potential confounding factors associated with fluctuations in WBC and neutrophil counts may have influenced the results of this study. Further research involving a larger cohort of patients is needed to better elucidate factors associated with increased WBC and neutrophil counts in schizophrenia.

Conclusion

Oral administration of lithium carbonate, adenine, and Ninjin‐yoei‐to may increase WBC and neutrophil counts in patients with schizophrenia, particularly from 2 months after initiation. Lithium carbonate and adenine, in particular, appear to be effective in increasing these counts. Initiating lithium carbonate or adenine may be beneficial for patients with reduced WBC and neutrophil counts prior to clozapine treatment.
