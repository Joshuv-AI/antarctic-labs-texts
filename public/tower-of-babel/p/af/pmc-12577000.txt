Epigenetic regulatory mechanisms of autoimmune skin diseases: novel biomarkers and therapeutic prospects
Authors: Xingyue Lai, Jilin Huang, Hongjia Li, Cuicui Chang, Rouxi Li, Xiaofang Li, Xuhong Yan, Liang Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune skin diseases (AISDs) are disorders characterized by disrupted immune tolerance and aberrant responses against skin antigens, including atopic dermatitis (AD), psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), bullous pemphigoid (BP), and other common conditions. Dysregulated expression of immune-related genes—particularly those governing B and T cell functions—holds promise as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, though their regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely defined. This review systematically synthesizes recent evidence elucidating the central role of three core epigenetic mechanisms—DNA methylation, histone modifications (including emerging metabolically linked alterations), and noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs)—in driving AISD pathogenesis. We highlight the dynamic interplay between epigenetic dysregulation, immune dysfunction, and skin barrier impairment. Deciphering these epigenetic networks and identifying key regulators (e.g., DNMTs, histone-modifying enzymes, ncRNAs) may enable high-precision diagnostics and novel therapies to restore immune homeostasis. Such advances will deepen mechanistic insights and transform clinical management paradigms.

Background

Autoimmune skin diseases (AISDs), such as psoriasis, atopic dermatitis (AD), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and bullous pemphigoid (BP), are a group of complex and debilitating disorders characterized by a loss of immune tolerance, chronic inflammation, and impaired skin barrier function. These diseases collectively contribute to significant morbidity and healthcare burdens worldwide, increasing patients' risks of systemic complications including cardiovascular, infectious, and psychiatric disorders. Current treatment modalities—primarily immunosuppressive agents and biologics—often provide insufficient long-term control and are associated with considerable side effects, highlighting a pressing need for more targeted and effective therapeutic strategies.

The pathogenesis of AISDs involves a multifaceted interplay among genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, dysregulated immune responses, and structural defects in epidermal differentiation. For instance, mutations in the filaggrin gene (FLG) disrupt keratinocyte maturation and compromise the physical barrier, facilitating the penetration of allergens and microbial products. This breach initiates innate immune activation, leading to the release of alarmins and polarizing cytokines that drive adaptive responses dominated by Th2, Th17, or type I interferon pathways, depending on the disease context. Additionally, microbiome dysbiosis—particularly Staphylococcus aureus dominance in AD—further amplifies inflammation through toxin-mediated activation of mast cells and T cells.

Despite advances in understanding immune and barrier dysfunction, the molecular mechanisms linking environmental exposures to persistent immune activation remain incompletely defined. Epigenetics has recently emerged as a pivotal interface through which external stimuli—such as UV radiation, pollutants, and infections—program gene expression and stabilize pathogenic cell states without altering DNA sequences. Key epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone posttranslational modifications (PTMs), and noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), dynamically regulate transcriptional programs central to immune cell function, keratinocyte differentiation, and inflammatory signaling. For example, genome-wide hypomethylation in CD4⁺ T cells promotes overexpression of interferon-stimulated genes in SLE, while hypermethylation of anti-fibrotic genes contributes to fibrosis in systemic sclerosis.

Notably, novel histone modifications—such as lactylation, crotonylation, and citrullination—are increasingly recognized as metabolic sensors that integrate immunometabolic rewiring with transcriptional outputs in autoimmune settings. Furthermore, dysregulated microRNAs (e.g., miR-146a in SLE) and long noncoding RNAs (e.g., HOTAIR and NEAT1) fine-tune inflammatory cascades and serve as potential biomarkers. However, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the cross talk between different epigenetic layers, their cell-type-specific roles, and their potential as therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Autoimmune skin diseases (AISDs), which caused by the abnormal attack of the immune system on the skin tissue. Encompassing conditions, such as atopic dermatitis (AD), psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and bullous pemphigoid (BP), represent a subset of debilitating disorders characterized by a loss of immune tolerance and aberrant targeting of cutaneous antigens [1]. The pathogenesis of AISDs involves a multifactorial interplay between genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, epidermal barrier defects (e.g., FLG mutations), and dysbiosis of the cutaneous microbiome [2]. And AISDs are difficult to treat and often have a heavy financial impact on patients [3]. These diseases impose a substantial clinical burden, contributing significantly to global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and elevating the risk of severe comorbidities including sepsis, cardiovascular disease, and psychiatric disorders [4].

The standard treatment for AISDs includes glucocorticoids, immunomodulators, immunosuppressants, and biologics (such as rituximab and omalizumab) to control disease activity [5]. At the same time, there are also targeted therapies for B cells [6] and cytokine targeted therapies (such as Dupilant, anti-IL-4Rα) [7]. However, achieving long-term effective remission remains challenging, with patients frequently experiencing medication response variations, various adverse reactions caused by prolonged immunosuppression, and high recurrence rates—particularly increased susceptibility to infections [8]. In view of this phenomenon, the need to develop new therapeutic strategies is highlighted.

Emerging evidence highlights the central role of epigenetic mechanisms—such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and noncoding RNAs—in the pathogenesis of AISDs [9]. Epigenetic alterations help explain how environmental factors interact with genetic predisposition to disrupt immune homeostasis and epidermal barrier function, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation. This review systematically outlines the dysregulation of key epigenetic pathways in AISDs and discusses their implications for immune polarization, barrier integrity, and systemic inflammation. Furthermore, we evaluate the translational potential and challenges of epigenetics-based therapies for these debilitating conditions.

The developmental trajectory of epigenetics

Conrad Waddington first introduced the term "epigenetics" in 1942, defining it as "the mechanism by which genotypes generate phenotypes" and proposing the conceptual "epigenetic landscape" model [10]. This framework set the stage for understanding gene regulation beyond DNA sequence changes. Landmark discoveries soon followed: in 1953, Wyatt GR identified 5-methylcytosine as the first chemical DNA epigenetic mark [11], while Allfrey et al. demonstrated in 1964 that histone modifications such as lactylation could directly modulate transcription [12]. The proposal of the nucleosome structure in 1974 [13] and the subsequent "maintenance methylase" model by Holliday and Pugh [14] further elucidated how chromatin organization and DNA methylation patterns could be stably inherited. Collectively, these findings established DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin dynamics as core pillars of epigenetic regulation by the mid-1970s.

In recent decades, epigenetics has entered a transformative phase with profound clinical implications. Key milestones include the launch of the Human Epigenome Project in 2003 [15], the FDA approval of the first epigenetic drugs in 2006 [16], and the landmark 2007 discovery that somatic cells can be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) via transcription factors—highlighting the reversibility of epigenetic states [17]. Technological advances, particularly in single-cell multi-omics (e.g., scATAC-seq + scRNA-seq) [18], now enable integrated profiling of chromatin accessibility and gene expression within individual cells [19].

It is worth noting that the understanding of epigenetics in AISDs is also changing. Beyond the technological leaps in mapping epigenomes, several paradigm-shifting discoveries have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of AISDs. Foremost among these is the identification of genome-wide DNA hypomethylation in SLE T cells, which established epigenetics as a core mechanism of loss of self-tolerance [20]. More recently, the concept of “inflammatory memory” in psoriatic keratinocytes has provided a compelling explanation for the recurrent nature of the disease, suggesting that epigenetic reprogramming of skin cells underlies disease persistence [21]. Furthermore, groundbreaking research has uncovered how gut microbiota-derived metabolites, such as indoxyl sulfate, can remotely prime skin inflammation through epigenetic remodeling of Th17 cells, thereby elucidating a molecular basis for the gut–skin axis [22]. Concurrently, the emergence of precise epigenetic editing technologies (e.g., CRISPR-based systems) holds significant promise for novel gene therapies targeting neurodegenerative, autoimmune, and musculoskeletal diseases. In 2025, it was found that DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (such as dexamethasone) can epigenetically restore the cGAS-STING natural immune pathway, which is often silenced in tumor cells, through demethylation. This proves that epigenetics is a major progress in disease treatment [23]. Similarly, epigenetics is increasingly being used to predict disease [24]. Collectively, these advances not only illuminate the critical roles of disease-associated genes and pathways but also open new avenues for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

These historical and contemporary advances collectively underscore the evolving role of epigenetics—from foundational regulatory mechanisms to clinical translation. In the following sections, we focus on the epigenetic mechanisms underlying AISDs and explore their implications for novel treatment strategies.

Pathogenesis of AISDs

AISDs represent a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by loss of immune tolerance to self-antigens, resulting in chronic inflammation and structural damage to skin components. These conditions encompass a broad spectrum of clinical entities including psoriasis, vitiligo, alopecia areata, SLE, and pemphigus variants. The pathogenesis of AISDs involves a complex interplay between genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, immune dysregulation, and barrier dysfunction. SLE is a complex polygenic autoimmune disorder characterized by immune system dysfunction that leads to immune tolerance breakdown, resulting in the production of high-affinity autoantibodies targeting healthy tissues throughout the body [26]. Clinically heterogeneous, SLE manifests with multi-organ involvement including skin, kidneys, joints, and nervous system. Cutaneous manifestations typically present as butterfly-shaped erythema, discoid lupus erythematosus, papules, and other cutaneous features [27]. The pathogenesis of SLE involves a breakdown of immune tolerance, primarily driven by the expansion and dysregulation of circulating T follicular helper (cTfh) cells. These cells play a central role in promoting B cell hyperactivation, autoantibody production, and germinal center formation, ultimately leading to multi-organ autoimmune injury. This process is further modulated by co-stimulatory molecules such as PD-1 and ICOS, cytokines including IL-21, key transcription factors like BCL-6, as well as genetic and epigenetic alterations [28].

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease characterized by a complex interaction between genetic susceptibility, cytokine dysregulation, and environmental triggers [29]. Psoriasis has many manifestations, including skin and arthritis. The skin is the most prominent manifestation, characterized by scaly skin [30]. The core pathogenesis involves aberrant activation of the IL-23/Th17 inflammatory axis, and IL-23 promotes the differentiation of Th17 cells, which subsequently produce cytokines such as IL-17A and IL-22. These cytokines directly contribute to keratinocyte hyperproliferation, aberrant differentiation, and sustained inflammation. Furthermore, hyperactivation of signaling pathways including NF-κB and JAK/STAT, dysregulation of epigenetic mechanisms, and chemokine-mediated immune cell infiltration collectively perpetuate local and systemic inflammation. Genetic predispositions (e.g., the HLA-C allele) and environmental triggers (such as infection, trauma, and stress) further exacerbate this process, thereby promoting the development of psoriatic plaques and systemic complications [31].

Vitiligo is an autoimmune disease of the skin that targets pigment-producing melanocytes and results in patches of depigmentation that are visible as white spots [32]. The pathogenesis of vitiligo involves a multifactorial interplay among genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation. Genetically determined alterations impair both immune modulation and melanocyte functionality. Oxidative stress, characterized by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), induces melanocyte damage and promotes the exposure of autoantigens. Within the immune system, CD8⁺ T cells are activated and directly target melanocytes, while key cytokines such as IFN-γ facilitate the recruitment of additional effector T cells via the CXCL9/CXCL10–CXCR3 axis. Concurrently, impaired regulatory T cell (Treg) function exacerbates the loss of immune tolerance, amplifying the autoimmune response. Ultimately, these processes lead to apoptosis of melanocytes and subsequent cutaneous depigmentation [33]. Now more and more evidence suggests that the onset of vitiligo is epigenetically related [34].

AD is a common inflammatory skin disorder characterized by recurrent eczematous lesions and intense itch [35]. The pathogenesis of AD is characterized by impaired skin barrier function, often due to loss-of-function mutations in the FLG, coupled with dysregulated immune activation dominated by type 2 inflammation. This immune response is driven primarily by cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31, which promote Th2 polarization, leading to chronic inflammation, intense pruritus, and further disruption of the epidermal barrier. Additionally, activation of the Th17/Th22 axis, cutaneous dysbiosis (e.g., colonization by Staphylococcus aureus), genetic susceptibility (e.g., certain HLA alleles), and environmental triggers collectively contribute to sustained inflammatory responses and disease persistence [36].

SSc is characterized by skin and internal organ fibrosis, diffuse fibroproliferative vascular modifications, and autoimmunity [37]. SSc is a complex autoimmune disorder whose pathogenesis involves an interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and epigenetic modifications. These factors collectively contribute to aberrant immune activation, leading to widespread vasculopathy and tissue fibrosis. Central to disease progression are endothelial cell injury, recurrent vasospasm, and loss of capillaries, accompanied by dysregulated innate and adaptive immune responses [38]. In recent years, epigenetics has been found to have great significance in the pathogenesis and treatment strategies of SSc [39].

Bullous pemphigoid is a chronic, subepidermal autoimmune blistering disease characterized by tense blisters on erythematous or normal skin that predominantly affects the older population [40]. The pathogenesis primarily stems from a breakdown in immune tolerance to autoantigens located at the epidermal basement membrane zone, notably type XVII collagen (BP180) and BP230. This process involves a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental triggers (such as trauma, medications, and infections), and epigenetic modifications. These factors collectively contribute to the activation of autoreactive T and B cells, leading to the production of autoantibodies against BP180 and BP230. These autoantibodies initiate both complement-dependent and complement-independent pathways, resulting in inflammatory cell infiltration, damage to keratinocytes, detachment of the basement membrane, and blister formation. Epitope spreading further promotes chronicity of the disease, while dysfunctional regulatory T cells exacerbate the autoimmune response [41].

Epigenetic regulation in cutaneous immunity and autoimmune dermatoses

As epigenetic research deepens, its central pivotal role in regulating immune responses in autoimmune dermatoses is becoming increasingly prominent. The maintenance of homeostasis of skin immunity, a highly environmentally dependent complex system, is closely linked to the precise regulation of epigenetic mechanisms. Studies have shown that epigenetic dysregulation (e.g., abnormalities in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and ncRNAsis a key driver of immune dysfunction in autoimmune diseases. These modifications are deeply involved in the pathogenesis and progression of multiple AISDs by dynamically influencing gene expression profiles. The following section systematically analyzes the three core epigenetic mechanisms described above.

DNA methylation

DNA methylation is a reversible epigenetic modification that adds methyl groups to cytosine in CpG dinucleotides. It regulates cellular differentiation, genome stability, and environmental responses by silencing gene expression, and its aberrations are closely associated with disease development [42]. Methylation of genes through the addition of a methyl group to the promoter region of a gene can significantly affect T-cell function and a variety of immune responses [43]. This epigenetic modification inhibits gene activity by preventing the transcription factors AP-1, NF-κB from binding to DNA [44]. A clear example of precise methylation regulation is demonstrated by the protein QSER1, which, in collaboration with TET1, protects DNA methylation valleys (DMVs)—large hypomethylated regions encompassing developmental genes—from de novo methylation by DNMT3A and DNMT3B. This protective mechanism is crucial for maintaining transcriptional and developmental programs, and its disruption is implicated in cancers [45]. Methylated DNA recruits methyl-binding proteins (e.g., MeCP2), which further bind histone deacetylases (HDACs) and heterochromatin proteins to form dense silenced chromatin structures effectively blocking access to the transcriptional machinery [46]. Methylation of DNA is able to recruit additional silencing complexes, such as HDACs, which actively remove acetyl groups from histone proteins, further promoting chromatin compression, thereby enhancing repression of gene expression. This combination of methylation and histone modification tightly regulates gene activity by stabilizing the repressive chromatin environment [47]. In addition to this, DNA methylation also affects the activity of chromatin-remodeling complexes that play a key role in regulating chromatin structure, which in turn controls gene transcription [48]. The SWI2/SNF2-type chromatin-remodeling proteins like CLSY family in Arabidopsis exemplify this dual role, functioning in both RNA-directed DNA methylation and active DNA demethylation pathways, highlighting their importance in dynamically balancing the DNA methylome [49].

Regulation of skin immunity and barrier genes by DNA methylation

DNA methylation critically regulates epidermal development and barrier functional integrity by modulating the expression of key keratinocyte differentiation genes (e.g., KRT9, FLG). Notably, FLG-mutant individuals exhibit locus-specific methylation alterations that further compromise stratum corneum water retention capacity, representing a core epigenetic mechanism underlying AD barrier defects [50]. Environmental stressors such as UV radiation can induce genome-wide methylation reprogramming, which exacerbates aberrant expression of differentiation-related genes. In AISDs, DNA methylation serves as a central epigenetic regulator within the pathogenic "epigenetic immune–barrier" axis. It orchestrates immune tolerance, inflammatory responses, and skin barrier integrity through targeted gene silencing/activation. Although terminally differentiated keratinocytes exhibit overall methylation stability, their differentiation is dynamically controlled by methylation changes at specific loci (e.g., TRIM29) [52]. Crucially, promoter methylation of key immune genes exerts profound functional consequences. Hypermethylation of Ifnb1 promoter CpG sites (mediated by UHRF1) sterically blocks IRF3 transcription factor binding, suppressing type I interferon production. This impairment in anti-viral immunity may facilitate autoimmune cross-reactivity [53]. Genome-wide methylation alterations (e.g., ALOX15 hypermethylation) constitutively activate NF-κB signaling, upregulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and triggering IL-6/TNF-α release. These cytokines recruit and polarize macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, driving chronic inflammation and dysregulating innate immunity [54].

Collectively, these findings establish DNA methylation as a master regulator of the "epigenetic immune–barrier" axis, where locus-specific and genome-wide alterations—manifesting as both hypermethylation and hypomethylation—orchestrate barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation, providing a critical epigenetic foundation for the pathogenesis of AISDs.

AISDs and hypomethylation

SLE is a typical systemic autoimmune disease that manifests as skin rash, arthritis, lymphadenopathy, and multiple organ lesions [55]. Genome-wide hypomethylation in CD4⁺ T cells is a characteristic epigenetic alteration observed in patients with SLE. This hypomethylation leads to the overexpression of key autoimmune-associated genes, including ITGAL (encoding CD11a), TNFSF7 (CD70), CD40LG (CD40L), and PRF1 (perforin). Consequently, T-cell autoreactivity is enhanced, promoting B-cell production of pathogenic autoantibodies. Environmental factors, such as trichloroethylene (TCE), may trigger this epigenetic dysregulation [56]. Genome-wide analyses identified 3785 significantly hypomethylated sites in CD4⁺ T cells, with enrichment in genes involved in interferon pathways (e.g., MX1, IFI44L) and Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling [57]. Notably, the degree of hypomethylation positively correlated with disease activity, as assessed by the SLEDAI score [58]. Notably, type I interferon (IFN)-associated genes (e.g., IFIT1, IRF7) exhibit a pattern of hypomethylation across CD4⁺ T cells, B cells, and monocytes. This hypomethylation occurs independently of serum IFN levels, suggesting the presence of an epigenetic "memory" [59]. The underlying mechanism may involve hypomethylation enhancing the binding capacity of transcription factors (e.g., IRF5, IRF7), thereby amplifying IFN signaling and establishing a self-perpetuating inflammatory feedback loop. Furthermore, mRNA expression of DNA methyltransferases DNMT1 and DNMT3A was significantly reduced in SCLE patients. Conversely, expression of methyl-binding domain proteins MBD1, MBD3, and MBD4 was elevated and positively correlated with the degree of global DNA hypomethylation. DNMT1 is essential for maintaining cellular proliferation within the basal lamina; its deletion results in epidermal thinning, premature differentiation, and aberrant expression of differentiation markers (e.g., FLG). DNMT3A/3B mediate de novo methylation, silencing non-epithelial genes to ensure lineage-specific differentiation. During this process, active demethylation occurs at promoters of genes such as S100P and EPHA2, lifting transcriptional repression [60]. Concurrently, histone modifications and DNA methylation synergistically regulate epidermal barrier formation: the basal lamina is enriched for the activating mark H3K4me3, while repressive marks (H3K27me3, H3K9me3) are removed during terminal differentiation, co-activating epidermal differentiation complex (EDC) genes (e.g., FLG). Defects in TET2-mediated active demethylation suppress EDC expression, confirming that epigenetic homeostasis is critical for barrier integrity [61]. Critically, miR-126 promotes demethylation of autoimmune gene regulatory sequences (e.g., ITGAL/CD11a, CD70 promoter) in T cells by targeting the 3'UTR of DNMT1 mRNA. This leads to abnormally high expression of these genes, ultimately driving the autoimmune response in T and B cells—a central mechanism in SLE pathogenesis [62].

The pathogenesis of AD is closely related to multilocus-specific hypomethylation. The promoter region of the FcεRIγ subunit gene is significantly hypomethylated in monocytes from AD patients, resulting in abnormally high mRNA and protein expression (P < 0.01), which enhances the assembly of the FcεRI receptor on the cell surface and IgE-mediated inflammatory signaling. Clinical cohort analyses confirmed that FcεRIγ promoter methylation levels were negatively correlated with protein expression, suggesting that hypomethylation is a key epigenetic mechanism for the amplification of allergic responses in AD [63]. Genome-wide analysis identified 16,840 differentially methylated CpG regions between AD patients and controls. Remarkably, over 97% of these CpG sites exhibited hypomethylation in children with AD. In an independent cohort comprising 224 AD children and 44 control subjects, differential hypomethylation within the golli-mbp locus, specifically at the top-ranked cg24700313 cluster, was validated using pyrosequencing. We conclude that loss of methylation within the golli-mbp locus represents an epigenetic factor associated with disease severity in childhood AD [64]. Notably, hypomethylation of the thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) has also been strongly implicated in AD pathogenesis (OR = 2.32; 95% CI = 1.06–5.11) [65].

And in psoriasis, integrated analysis of genome-wide methylation data from patient skin tissues and blood-derived genotypes revealed a pivotal role for DNA methylation in mediating genetic risk. The study demonstrated that genetic variants within the MHC region—particularly proximal to HLA-C—modulate disease susceptibility by inducing hypomethylation at three critical genomic loci: the gene body of SIK3,C1orf106 and the promoter region of DMBX1. Notably, methylation at the SIK3 locus mediated 47% of the genetic effect (β = − 0.13, 95% CI [− 0.16–0.11]) on psoriasis pathogenesis. These methylation sites function as epigenetic bridges, translating MHC region genetic risk into functional pathological manifestations. This mechanistic insight identifies novel targets for precision therapeutics in psoriasis [66]. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) showed that hypomethylation of the promoter region of the IL23R gene (30% reduction in methylation level) enhances chromatin openness and promotes binding of the transcription factor STAT3, which in turn upregulates IL-23 receptor expression. This leads to increased sensitivity of keratinocytes and DCs to IL-23, further activation of Th17 cells and release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-17A and IL-22 [67].

AISDs and hypermethylation

Unlike hypomethylation—which drives overexpression of autoimmune-related genes—hypermethylation silences key repressive genes (e.g., tumor suppressors and anti-inflammatory regulators) in autoimmune diseases. Both mechanisms critically contribute to disease pathogenesis. Psoriasis exemplifies this epigenetic imbalance, with lesional skin exhibiting 811 hypomethylated and 3,510 hypermethylated CpG sites [68]. These aberrantly methylated sites are enriched in inflammatory pathway genes (IL-17, IRF7, IL7, CXCL1), accelerating disease progression by enhancing Th17 cell differentiation signaling pathways and upregulating keratinocyte proliferation drivers. Early formation of Munro's microabscesses in psoriasis is significantly associated with DNA hypermethylation. Genome-wide profiling identified 647 differentially methylated sites (DMSs), of which 81% (526 sites) exhibited hypermethylation predominantly enriched in gene body regions. This epigenetic alteration inhibits DNA binding of AP-1 transcription factors (e.g., FOS/JUN), thereby activating inflammatory pathways (TGF-β/IL-17) and dysregulating focal adhesion-associated genes (e.g., PIP5K1A, CLASP1). Collectively, these mechanisms drive neutrophil migration into the epidermis and promote microabscess pathogenesis [69].

In systemic sclerosis (SSc), the fibrotic process involves epigenetic silencing of key regulatory genes. Hypermethylation of the FLI1 promoter region leads to transcriptional silencing of this gene, which encodes a potent inhibitor of collagen synthesis. Consequently, the loss of FLI1-mediated repression results in unrestrained collagen production, driving dermal fibrosis. Concurrently, aberrant DNA methylation at specific CpG sites within genes of the PI3K-Akt pathway contributes to pathogenesis. For example, hypermethylation at cg15381320 and hypomethylation at cg09931511 in genes such as JAK1 and EGFR dysregulate cellular proliferation and inflammation, further promoting disease progression [70]. In dermal fibroblasts from SSc patients, TGF-β signaling via the SMAD-dependent pathway upregulates the expression of DNA methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT1. This leads to hypermethylation of the promoter region of the anti-inflammatory gene SOCS3 (as validated by methylation-specific PCR and MeDIP assays). Consequently, SOCS3 silencing alleviates its inhibitory effect on the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, driving pathological fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation, excessive collagen secretion, and tissue fibrosis. Critically, targeted interventions—using the DNMT inhibitor 5-azacytidine or genetic knockdown of DNMT3A—reversed SOCS3 promoter hypermethylation, suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation, and significantly ameliorated skin fibrosis in murine models [71].

Genome-wide analyses of chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) revealed subtype-specific epigenetic dysregulation. Patients exhibiting the thyroid autoimmune phenotype (characterized by anti-TPO antibody positivity and elevated serum IgE) demonstrated 59 hypermethylated sites. Notably, hypermethylation of HLA-DPB2 and HLA-DRB1 was identified, potentially impairing the expression of these key MHC-II genes. This aberrant hypermethylation may compromise MHC-II-mediated antigen presentation efficiency, ultimately potentiating pathogenic autoantibody production [72].

Emerging posttranslational modifications of histones

Histone modifications are chemical alterations occurring at the N-terminal tails of histone proteins. They involve the addition of various chemical groups (e.g., acetyl, methyl, phosphoryl, ubiquitin) to specific amino acid residues. These modifications modulate histone–DNA interactions and the euchromatin-to-heterochromatin ratio, thereby regulating chromatin structure and function [73]. Significant progress has recently elucidated the key roles of histone posttranslational modifications in the pathophysiology of diverse diseases [74]. Histone lactylation, predominantly targeting lysine residues on H3 and H4 N termini, is tightly regulated by the opposing activities of histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs). This modification relaxes chromatin structure, facilitating transcriptional activation and gene expression; conversely, hypolactylation typically correlates with gene repression [75]. Histone methylation, involving methyl group addition to lysine or arginine residues, represents another critical modification. Unlike lactylation, which generally promotes activation, the transcriptional outcome of methylation is residue-dependent, capable of either activating or repressing transcription [76]. Consequently, the dynamic interplay between histone lactylation and methylation is essential for normal immune cell function and is frequently dysregulated in disease. Lactylation

Posttranslational modification (PTM) of proteins, known as lactylation, is achieved by altering the structure of the histone H3K18 site, a modification that is closely related to cellular metabolism and epigenetic regulation. Lactylation is a recently discovered PTM of proteins, which refers to the enzymatic addition of a lactide group to the ε-amino group of lysine residues of histones (e.g., H3K18) and non-histone proteins. The reaction is catalyzed by histone acetyltransferase p300 [77], and the modification is directly regulated by lactate concentration. Under conditions of hypoxia, inflammation or high glycolysis (e.g., the Warburg effect), lactate accumulation drives elevated levels of lactylation [78], whose dynamic reversibility is maintained by HDAC1-3 and SIRT1-3-mediated deprotonation [79]. In addition, through plasma cell and B cell activity and autoantibody production, this modification, together with m6A lactylation, directly alters the structure of histone tails, which in turn affects the chromatin conformation and transcriptional activity of SFTSV and other genes [80].

Although there is limited evidence to directly study lactylation modifications in AISDs, multiple clues support their involvement in AISDs. Hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming are often present locally in AISDs [81], leading to the accumulation of lactic acid, which provides a substrate for lactylation. Keratinocytes have enhanced glycolysis in inflammatory states [82], and their lactylation modifications may modulate chemokine (e.g., IL-8) expression to promote neutrophil infiltration. B-cell differentiation and autoantibody production may be affected by lactylation-mediated metabolic–epigenetic coupling, similar to its role in macrophage M2 polarization [83].

Citrullination

Peptidylarginine deiminase (PAD) catalyzes citrullination, a posttranslational modification (PTM) that converts arginine residues on histones to citrulline. This modification alters histone charge, thereby affecting chromatin organization and gene expression (84). Studies have shown that in addition to altering protein structure, citrullination enhances peptide binding to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules and activates CD4+ T cells [85]. This modification significantly alters the charge, conformation and function of the protein, which in turn affects its immunogenicity. Five PAD isozymes (PAD1-4, PAD6) exist in humans, of which PAD1 is highly expressed in epidermal keratinocytes and PAD2/4 have enhanced activity in inflammatory tissues [86]. Physiologically, PAD1-mediated citrullination of keratin K1 facilitates epidermal terminal differentiation by promoting dense packing of keratin filaments. Pathologically, however, aberrant citrullination exposes self-antigenic epitopes, triggering autoimmune responses.

Proteins such as citrullinated fibrinogen α/β chains and corrugated vimentin (cVim) in the skin can be raised by DCs, activating T/B cells and inducing the production of antibodies against citrullinated proteins (ACPAs), which in turn lead to joint and cutaneous vascular inflammation through the formation of immune complexes activated by complement- and Fcγ receptor-positive cells [87]. In psoriasis, reduced activity of PAD1 diminishes citrullination of keratin K1. This impairment disrupts keratin–loricrin cross-linking and compromises filaggrin dissociation. Consequently, the structural integrity of the stratum corneum is compromised, leading to epidermal barrier dysfunction and initiation of psoriatic pathogenesis [88]. In psoriatic patients, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) contain citrullinated antimicrobial peptide LL37. Citrullinated LL37 (citLL37) specifically activates immune cells, stimulating secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ. This demonstrates that citrullination enhances LL37 antigenicity, thereby promoting Th1/cytotoxic T-cell responses in psoriasis. These findings provide a mechanistic foundation for targeting the NETs-citLL37 pathway (e.g., via PAD inhibitors) in therapeutic strategies [89]. Clinical evidence indicates elevated neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation in psoriatic skin lesions, with levels positively correlating with disease severity. In psoriasis, PAD4-mediated NETs amplify local inflammation by stimulating fibroblasts to release IL-6, IL-8, and adhesion molecules [90]. Additionally, HLA-DRB1 allele carriers exhibit heightened susceptibility to citrullinated antigens, and aberrant environmental factors activate pulmonary and cutaneous peptidylarginine deiminase (PAD) enzymes, driving systemic autoantigen production.

In SLE, genetic variants within the deubiquitinase (DUB) domain of A20 (e.g., rs2230926) significantly promote protein citrullination and NETs formation by upregulating PAD4 expression, thereby directly contributing to SLE pathogenesis [91]. Notably, citrullination of LL37 (citLL37) enhances its capacity as a T-cell autoantigen, facilitating adaptive autoimmune responses. However, this modification concomitantly impairs its critical function as a “danger signal” by abolishing DNA binding, which is essential for activating innate immune cells (pDCs) and promoting B cell maturation, thereby mediating SLE development [92]. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), PAD4 acts as a key mediator downstream of TNFα. It exacerbates the progression of chronic inflammatory arthritis by promoting the production of autoantibodies (potentially independent of its citrullinating function) and amplifying inflammatory responses. Research has revealed a likely complex positive feedback network involving TNFα, PAD4, citrullinated antigens, and autoantibodies, suggesting that inhibiting PAD4 represents a potential therapeutic target for RA [93]. In both multiple sclerosis (MS) patients and animal models of demyelination, the expression and activity of PAD4 are significantly elevated in normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) and PAD4 translocates into the nucleus. Concurrently, the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α, primarily derived from activated astrocytes, triggers the nuclear translocation of PAD4. This leads to the citrullination of arginine residues on histone H3 (H3cit). The TNF-α/PAD4/histone citrullination axis represents a core pathological mechanism in MS. Protein citrullination mediated by PAD represents a central pathological mechanism linking multiple autoimmune diseases, including psoriasis, SLE, RA, and MS. This modification drives aberrant immune activation—through altering protein structure/function, exposing self-antigenic epitopes (e.g., keratins, LL37, fibrinogen, vimentin), generating citrullinated histones (e.g., H3cit), and promoting NETs formation. Collectively, these effects lead to T/B-cell activation, autoantibody production, inflammatory cytokine release, and ultimately, tissue damage and disease progression. Consequently, targeted inhibition of PAD (particularly PAD4) or its upstream signaling pathways (e.g., TNF-α) offers a promising novel therapeutic strategy for intervening in this shared pathological cascade across these disorders.

Crotonylation

Histone crotonylation, a recently identified posttranslational modification involving the addition of crotonyl groups to specific lysine residues, alters chromatin architecture and modulates gene expression. First reported by Yingming Zhao's team in 2011, this modification enhances transcriptional activity by reducing histone positive charge [95]. The diminished electrostatic interaction between histones and negatively charged DNA promotes chromatin relaxation, facilitating transcription factor binding and gene activation. Enzymatic regulation involves p300/CBP and MOF as primary crotonylation writers, whereas HDAC3 and SIRT1-3 function as erasers that remove crotonyl groups during decrotonylation [96].

Protein crotonylation, a novel posttranslational modification linked to metabolic and inflammatory pathways, plays critical yet complex roles in skin pathophysiology and host–pathogen interactions. In a high-sugar and high-fat environment, this modification is promoted in keratinocytes (HaCaT) through upregulation of ACOX2 expression. This process inhibits the STAT3/SNAI2 pathway, hindering epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and migratory capacity, and ultimately disrupts skin barrier integrity. This suggests that crotonylation of HaCaT may be the pathogenesis of some skin diseases [97]. In psoriasis, impaired crotonylation at lysine 49 (K49) of the S100A7 protein enhances its binding to the RAGE receptor, thereby activating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. This cascade drives keratinocyte hyperproliferation and glycolytic metabolic reprogramming. Targeting restoration of S100A7 crotonylation or blockade of its interaction with RAGE effectively suppresses disease progression, representing a novel therapeutic target for psoriasis intervention [98]. In SLE, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) exhibit significant dysregulation in protein lysine crotonylation. A total of 612 differentially crotonylated sites were identified, corresponding to 272 modified proteins. This aberrant crotonylation promotes the infiltration of immune cells into tissues by enhancing their transendothelial migration capacity, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., MMP9 expression was upregulated by 5.04-fold). That dysregulation of crotonylation represents a critical component in the immunopathogenesis of SLE. This discovery provides a rationale for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting the relevant modifying enzymes [99]. Crotonylation also directly regulates IL-1β gene promoter activity via CBP/p300. In BP patients, the IL-23/IL-17 axis drives macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and promotes IL-1β maturation and release, and crotonylation modification of CBP/p300 may be involved in its transcriptional activation. Inhibiting crotonylation of CBP/p300 may be a potential therapeutic approach for BP [100].

Aberrant lysine crotonylation in proteins plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of multiple inflammatory skin diseases—such as psoriasis, SLE, and BP—by disrupting key signaling pathways (e.g., STAT3/SNAI2, AKT/mTOR, NLRP3 inflammasome) and cellular functions (including EMT, migration, proliferation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release). This mechanism highlights its potential as a novel therapeutic target for these conditions.

Succinylation

Protein succinylation is a PTM involving the covalent attachment of a succinyl group from succinyl-CoA to a lysine residue. This modification significantly alters protein charge, structure, and function. Specifically, the negatively charged succinyl group converts the cationic lysine into an anion. Recently, the succinyltransferase OXCT1 was identified as responsible for mediating LACTB succinylation at lysine 284 (K284). This modification inhibits LACTB's protease activity, thereby relieving its suppression of mitochondrial function [101]. The dynamic equilibrium of succinylation is co-regulated by succinyl-CoA (the donor substrate), succinyltransferases (e.g., KAT2A, CPT1A, OXCT1), and desuccinylases (e.g., SIRT5, SIRT7). Succinylation plays a central role in cellular processes, particularly energy metabolism and oxidative stress response, by widely participating in metabolic reprogramming (e.g., glycolysis, TCA cycle, fatty acid metabolism) and gene expression regulation [102].

Succinylation may contribute to immune tolerance breakdown by altering the conformation or charge of self-antigens, thereby exposing cryptic epitopes [103]. For instance, in SSc, DNA methylation suppresses expression of the anti-fibrotic gene FLI1, leading to excessive collagen deposition. Similarly, succinylation could modify structural proteins in the skin—such as laminin and collagen—potentially increasing their immunogenicity and susceptibility to autoantibody recognition. In the pathogenesis of vitiligo, SIRT7 impairs melanin production in melanocytes by inhibiting the succinylation of Ezrin (EZR) at lysine 60 (K60). Targeting this pathway represents a novel potential therapeutic strategy for repigmentation [104]. In multiple sclerosis, aberrant posttranslational modifications of myelin basic protein (MBP)—such as deamidation—can trigger autoimmune responses by enhancing antigen immunogenicity. This suggests that succinylation may similarly promote pathogenic autoimmunity through altered presentation of skin antigens [105]. Mechanistically, succinylation modulates T cell metabolic reprogramming by regulating key enzymes (e.g., pyruvate kinase M2 [PKM2], succinate dehydrogenase [SDH]). For instance, SIRT5 deficiency drives succinyl-CoA accumulation, which inhibits fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial ATP production; this metabolic shift may favor the differentiation of pro-inflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells — a pivotal driver in psoriasis and SLE [106]. Furthermore, succinylation amplifies inflammatory cascades by modifying transcription factors (e.g., NF-κB, STAT). In rheumatoid arthritis, cross talk between O-GlcNAcylation and IL-17 signaling regulates disease severity, implying potential roles for succinylation in skin inflammation via analogous posttranslational cross talk [107]. Notably, succinyl proteomics of Staphylococcus epidermidis reveals that this modification governs bacterial energy metabolism and quorum sensing. Since skin dysbiosis activates TLR pathways and exacerbates autoimmune disorders (e.g., psoriasis), succinylation-mediated microbial adaptations may indirectly fuel cutaneous inflammation [108].

Collectively, succinylation disrupts immune homeostasis through tripartite mechanisms: neo-antigen generation via structural protein modification (e.g., skin laminin/collagen, myelin); metabolic reprogramming of immune cells (e.g., Th17 differentiation); and microbiota-dependent inflammation amplification—establishing it as a master orchestrator of autoimmunity across multiple organ systems.

SUMOylation

SUMOylation is a reversible posttranslational modification involving the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) to lysine residues on target proteins. Ubiquitous in eukaryotes, this process critically regulates diverse cellular functions, including gene expression, signal transduction, and genome integrity maintenance [109]. A key mechanism involves the SUMOylation of transcription factors and chromatin-remodeling proteins, which modulates cytokine gene expression during immunomodulation. For instance, SUMOylated histone H4 recruits HP1 and HDACs, inducing transcriptional repression and influencing chromatin accessibility. Furthermore, SUMOylation affects the activity of histone-modifying enzymes such as HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC4, SIRT1, EZH2, and KDM5B, thereby altering chromatin structure, function, and gene expression patterns [110]. Distinct from ubiquitination (primarily targeting degradation), SUMOylation regulates protein stability, subcellular localization, interactions, and transcriptional activity to exert its broad functional roles [111].

SUMOylation dysregulation contributes to several fibrotic and autoimmune skin disorders. In keloid fibroblasts, SUMO1 overexpression enhances Smad4 SUMOylation, increasing its stability and transcriptional activity to drive TGF-β-mediated proliferation and collagen overdeposition; this pathology is attenuated by the SUMO inhibitor ginkgolic acid (GA). SUMOylation also modulates inflammation: IκBα SUMOylation inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation, while PIAS proteins (SUMO E3 ligases) directly regulate NF-κB inflammatory activity. Aberrations in this pathway are implicated in the chronic inflammation of pemphigus and BP. Notably, SUMOylation of the BP180 autoantigen influences its stability and immunogenicity, potentially promoting autoimmune responses through altered epitope exposure [100, 112]. In epidermolysis bullosa acquisita (EBA), where anti-type VII collagen titers correlate with severity, SUMOylation may regulate collagen-metabolizing enzyme activities [113]. Finally, UBC9-mediated impairment of SUMOylation disrupts CD4⁺ T-cell metabolism (inhibiting glycolysis), reduces peripheral Treg proliferation, and compromises immune tolerance—a mechanism potentially underlying T-cell infiltration in cutaneous autoimmune diseases.

Collectively, these findings underscore SUMOylation's pivotal role in modulating inflammatory responses, tissue remodeling, and immune tolerance, highlighting its significance as a regulatory node in skin fibrosis and autoimmunity that presents potential therapeutic targets.

Hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib)

Khib, a highly conserved and increasingly recognized posttranslational modification, plays important roles in diverse bacterial processes, including metabolism. Recent studies further suggest its potential significance in the pathogenesis of various diseases. The acetyltransferase EP300 is a key enzyme for Khib formation, catalyzing the addition of the hydroxyisobutyryl moiety to target proteins. Homologous enzymes, Esa1p in yeast and Tip60 in humans, have also been identified and may function as the major Khib transferases, responsible for modifying specific lysine residues on their respective target proteins. Conversely, HDAC2 and HDAC3 have been identified as major dehydroxyisobutyrylating enzymes that reverse Khib modification by removing the hydroxyisobutyryl group [84, 114].

Analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from SLE patients identified 1295 differential Khib sites (865 upregulated and 630 downregulated) across 416 proteins. These sites exhibited significant enrichment within the complement cascade, coagulation pathway, and platelet activation pathway [115]. Functionally, Khib synergizes with crotonylation (Kcr) to regulate leukocyte transendothelial migration. Furthermore, Khib modification enhances ATPase activity, promotes antigen binding to MHC class II molecules, and activates autoimmune responses. An imbalance in Khib/Kcr modification at the K79 locus alters cytoskeletal dynamics via neutralization of positive charges, exacerbating leukocyte infiltration and tissue damage [116]. In psoriasis patients, comparison of lesional versus non-lesional skin revealed Khib upregulation on 72 proteins and downregulation on 44 proteins, primarily implicating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [117]. Additionally, miR-155 deregulates NF-κB inhibition and drives inflammatory factor release (IL-6/IL-1β) by suppressing IRF2BP2/KLF2. Khib modification may amplify this inflammation by targeting proteins within this pathway.

Collectively, Khib modification emerges as a critical regulator of autoimmune inflammation, orchestrating leukocyte migration through cytoskeletal charge modulation (K79 site), amplifying antigen presentation via MHC-II potentiation, and driving pathogenic signaling cascades (e.g., PI3K-AKT, miR-155/NF-κB)—positioning its enzymatic machinery (EP300/Tip60 vs. HDAC2/3) as promising therapeutic targets for inflammatory disorders.

Palmitoylation

Palmitoylation is a post-modification known to regulate gene expression by affecting the structure of histones, which in turn alters chromatin configuration. This modification increases the accessibility of specific gene promoters to transcription factors and protects modified proteins from degradation, thereby prolonging the half-life of modified proteins within the cell [118]. Research has revealed that the palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC5 catalyzes the palmitoylation of the LRR domain in NLRP3. This modification promotes the formation of the NLRP3-NEK7 complex, thereby activating caspase-1. Consequently, it drives the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, ultimately mediating the onset of inflammation. This process may underlie the pathogenesis of certain cutaneous inflammatory disorders [119]. In addition, palmitoylated histones regulate gene expression patterns that control immune cell function and development. Foxp3 undergoes DHHC2-catalyzed S-palmitoylation at cysteine 267 (Cys267). This modification enhances Foxp3 binding to the nuclear transport protein KPNA2, facilitating its nuclear localization. Consequently, it stabilizes the expression of Treg-suppressive genes such as CTLA4 and IL10 [120]. Furthermore, PD-L1 palmitoylation, catalyzed by DHHC3, sustains its plasma membrane localization and stability. This modification consequently suppresses T cell function and inhibits the expression of effector genes such as IFNG and TNF [121].

Palmitoylation represents a critical posttranslational modification whose dysregulation underlies core pathogenic mechanisms in AISDs. Within cutaneous tissue, aberrant palmitoylation directly impairs both functional integrity and antigenicity of structural proteins essential for epidermal cohesion and dermal–epidermal junction stability. In pemphigus—characterized by autoantibodies targeting epidermal adhesion proteins like desmoglein—desmoglein palmitoylation status governs its membrane localization and stability, where modification dysregulation may expose cryptic antigenic epitopes and trigger pathogenic autoantibody production [122]. Palmitoylation of BP180 (type XVII collagen) regulates its interaction with laminin, and abnormal modification can weaken dermal–epidermal adhesion, thereby promoting subepidermal blister formation in pemphigoid [123]. Within the immune system, palmitoylation extensively modulates immune cell signaling wherein TCR co-receptors (CD4/CD8) and immune regulators including PD-1 undergo palmitoylation-dependent regulation; abnormal modification may drive T-cell hyperactivation or tolerance defects, consequently promoting autoimmunity [124]. Regarding inflammatory responses, palmitoylation amplifies tissue-destructive inflammation. Specifically, ZDHHC17-catalyzed palmitoylation at cysteine 419 (Cys419) of the NLRP3 inflammasome facilitates NEK7 binding and enhances inflammasome activation, with inhibition of this modification attenuating inflammatory damage [125]. Additionally, ZDHHC1/11-mediated palmitoylation of STING critically regulates the DNA-sensing pathway, which is mechanistically implicated in systemic autoimmune diseases such as lupus erythematosus [126].

Notably, protein palmitoylation plays significant roles in AISDs. The palmitoyltransferase Zdhhc2 has been identified as a critical driver of inflammatory responses in psoriasis. Experimental models demonstrate significantly elevated Zdhhc2 expression within psoriatic lesions. Genetic ablation of Zdhhc2 via CRISPR/Cas9 technology effectively attenuated key pathological manifestations, including epidermal hyperplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration, and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably IFN-α. This study establishes Zdhhc2 as a key regulator mediating psoriasis pathogenesis through its control of plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) function, thereby highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the palmitoylation pathway for psoriasis intervention [127]. ZDHHC13-mediated protein palmitoylation constitutes a critical mechanism protecting the skin against AD. Deficiency in ZDHHC13 impairs palmitoylation, leading to compromised skin barrier function, diminished antimicrobial peptide secretion, dysbiosis of the cutaneous microbiota (e.g., overproliferation of Staphylococcus aureus), and attenuated TLR-mediated immune signaling. Consequently, these disruptions exacerbate the development and progression of inflammatory skin disorders, including AD. Therapeutic targeting to restore ZDHHC13 function thus represents a potential strategy for AD intervention [128].

Collectively, these findings underscore that dysregulated protein palmitoylation critically underpins the pathogenesis of AISDs by impairing structural integrity at the epidermal and dermal–epidermal junction, thereby exposing cryptic epitopes and weakening adhesion, and by exacerbating pathogenic immune responses through aberrant signaling in immune cells and hyperactivation of inflammatory pathways. Notably, the identification of palmitoyltransferases, such as ZDHHC2 driving psoriasis pathology via pDC regulation and ZDHHC13 serving as a protective factor against atopic dermatitis by maintaining barrier function and immune homeostasis, highlights the enzyme-specific nature of these mechanisms and reveals palmitoylation modulation as a promising therapeutic avenue for targeted intervention in AISDs.

Noncoding RNAs

ncRNAs are key factors in the regulation of a variety of cellular processes including proliferation, apoptosis, cell cycle and DNA methylation. Important ncRNA types involved in immune regulation include microRNAs (miRNAs) and long-chain noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), which regulate gene expression at the posttranscriptional level [129]. miRNAs act mainly by promoting the degradation of target mRNAs or by repressing their translation. Ribosome profiling revealed that ≥ 84% of miRNA-mediated suppression stems from target mRNA degradation, with only a minority directly mediated by translational repression. By concurrently measuring mRNA levels and protein output, this study demonstrated that mammalian miRNAs primarily achieve gene silencing by reducing mRNA stability (predominantly via the deadenylation pathway), rather than relying on translational repression mechanisms [130]. Interestingly, miRNAs themselves undergo rapid turnover upon binding to target RNAs with extensive complementarity, a phenomenon termed target-directed miRNA degradation (TDMD) [131]. On the other hand, lncRNAs often act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), by binding miRNAs to regulate gene expression. Research demonstrates that lncRNA UICLM is highly expressed in colorectal cancer with liver metastasis. By competitively binding to miR-215, it functions as a ceRNA to alleviate the suppression of the oncogene ZEB2, thereby promoting tumor metastasis [132]. These mechanisms profoundly influence immune processes such as anti-viral responses, tumor immunity and autoimmune diseases can ultimately promote both protective immunity and pathological immune dysregulation. Thus, miRNAs and lncRNAs can significantly influence immune activation, tolerance and resolution of inflammation.

MiRNAs and AISDs

The biosynthesis of miRNAs requires transcription by RNA polymerase II, intranuclear processing by Drosha/DGCR8 complex, and final cleavage by Dicer enzyme to mature bodies. A single miRNA can target hundreds of genes, forming a complex regulatory network that is involved in key physiological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune response [133].

In skin biology, miRNAs maintain homeostasis by regulating keratinocyte differentiation and immune cell activation. Through sequence-specific binding to target mRNA 3'UTRs, they induce transcript degradation or translational repression to modulate immune responses. Critically, miRNAs regulate nearly all immune cells—including T cells, B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells—as demonstrated by miR-223, which actively promotes neutrophil differentiation with significantly elevated expression during this process [134]. Beyond cellular regulation, miRNAs control immune pathways by targeting key signaling molecules, particularly in inflammation: miR-146a suppresses inflammatory responses via targeting IRAK1 and TRAF6 [135], while miR-155 inhibits inflammatory macrophage activation [136]. These mechanisms establish miRNAs as precise transcriptional regulators of immune cell-driven inflammatory and immune responses.

In the pathogenesis of psoriasis, miR-381-3p, mediated by keratinocyte-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) that facilitate communication between keratinocytes (KCs) and T cells, alleviates the inhibitory effects of UBR5 and FOXO1 on Th1/Th17 cell polarization. Consequently, it serves as a critical driver of psoriatic inflammation. Therefore, blocking the KC-sEV-mediated delivery of miR-381-3p or targeting its downstream pathways (e.g., the UBR5/RORγt and FOXO1/T-bet axes) can effectively inhibit pathogenic Th1/Th17 immune responses, offering novel therapeutic strategies for psoriasis [137]. Significantly diminished plasma levels of EV-encapsulated let-7b-5p and miR-30e-5p were validated in a psoriasis cohort. Crucially, this reduction was associated with psoriatic arthritis (PsA), identifying miR-30e-5p as a candidate biomarker for PsA [138]. Psoriatic skin exhibits a distinct microRNA (miRNA) expression profile. Specifically, the keratinocyte-specific miR-203 and the leukocyte-derived miR-146a are upregulated in psoriatic lesions. Research has demonstrated that miR-203 post-transcriptionally suppresses the expression of suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) at the protein but not mRNA level. This suppression leads to sustained activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting aberrant keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, inflammatory responses, and the formation of psoriatic plaques. These findings reveal miRNA dysregulation as a novel regulatory layer in psoriasis pathogenesis, driving disease development by disrupting the functional cross talk between keratinocytes and immune cells [139].

MiRNAs serve as critical posttranscriptional regulators of immune pathways, and their aberrant expression contributes significantly to SLE pathogenesis. The miR-146a regulates IFN-I pathway and photosensitivity. The miR-146a functions as a key negative regulator of the IFN-I pathway by directly targeting IRAK1 and TRAF6, thereby inhibiting IFN-α production. Significantly reduced miR-146a expression in SLE peripheral blood leads to persistent activation of the IFN-I pathway, which negatively correlates with disease activity. This sustained IFN-I overproduction promotes autoantibody generation and induces cutaneous photosensitivity reactions, exacerbating characteristic skin lesions like malar erythema [140, 141]. Current research hypothesizes that ultraviolet B (UVB) exposure induces aberrant miRNA expression in skin keratinocytes, promoting IFN-I secretion via Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathway activation. Critically, the low miR-146a expression in SLE fails to effectively inhibit this response, leading to photosensitive skin rashes [142]. This is supported by clinical data showing photosensitivity in 75% of SLE patients, correlating positively with serum IFN-α levels. Restoring miR-146a function could suppress pathological IFN-I signaling, reduce autoantibodies, and ameliorate photosensitivity. The miR-125a deficiency promotes inflammation via RANTES. The miR-125a expression is downregulated in SLE T cells. This reduction elevates expression of its target gene, the transcription factor KLF13, resulting in overproduction of the chemokine RANTES (CCL5). Elevated RANTES mediates the recruitment of inflammatory cells (including Th1 cells and monocytes) to target tissues. This chemotaxis is directly involved in the pathogenesis of lupus dermatitis (manifesting as cutaneous vasculitis-like lesions) and nephritis by inducing local inflammation and facilitating immune complex deposition in the dermis and glomerular basement membrane [143]. Enhancing miR-125a or blocking the RANTES/CCR5 axis could attenuate inflammatory cell infiltration, mitigating skin and renal damage. Plasma exosomes from SLE patients carry dysregulated miRNAs, such as miR-155 and miR-19b, facilitating intercellular communication and immune perturbation. Upregulated in SLE B cells, miR-155 targets and represses SHP-1 (a tyrosine phosphatase). This suppression promotes B cell proliferation and pathogenic autoantibody secretion [144]. Downregulation of miR-19b leads to elevated KLF13 expression. Increased KLF13 inhibits regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, exacerbating both cutaneous and systemic inflammation [145]. Inhibiting miR-155 could normalize B-cell tolerance, while restoring miR-19b may enhance Treg function and dampen inflammation. LncRNA and AISDs

Their core functions include serving as scaffolding molecules to recruit chromatin modification complexes (e.g., PRC2, DNMTs) and silencing target genes by H3K27me3 methylation or DNA methylation. For example HOTAIR regulates histone modifications by binding to EZH2 [149].

Studies have demonstrated that patients with SLE exhibit broad dysregulation of lncRNA expression, which contributes to aberrant autoimmune activation by influencing T-cell differentiation, B-cell function, and interferon signaling pathways [150]. For instance, certain lncRNAs promote overexpression of pro-inflammatory genes in T cells and suppress regulatory T-cell (Treg) differentiation by modulating epigenetic regulators such as DNA methyltransferase DNMT1 and the histone-modifying enzyme EZH2 [151]. Additionally, imbalanced expression of X chromosome-linked lncRNAs—including XIST—may help explain the elevated prevalence of SLE in females. This imbalance is linked to incomplete X chromosome inactivation, resulting in abnormal immune gene expression, such as that of CD40LG [152]. Notably, IFN signatures, a hallmark of SLE, are directly influenced by lncRNAs. For example, linc00513, located within an SLE risk locus, contains functional single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; rs205764/G and rs547311/A) that enhance its promoter activity. This upregulation facilitates STAT1/STAT2 phosphorylation, amplifies type I interferon signaling, and correlates positively with disease severity [153]. Another lncRNA, NEAT1, is highly expressed in cutaneous and renal lesions of SLE patients, where it activates the ERK/JNK pathway to induce secretion of chemokines such as IL-6 and CXCL10. This process recruits inflammatory cells, exacerbating tissue damage [154]. Furthermore, lncRNA00892 upregulates CD40L expression by interacting with heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNP K), thereby enhancing T-cell–B-cell contact and promoting the production of autoantibodies, which contributes to the pathogenesis of SLE [155]. Similarly, lncRNA MIAT functions as a ceRNA by sequestering miR-222, which alleviates its suppression of complement factor H-related protein 5 (CFHR5). This mechanism disrupts complement regulation and promotes immune complex deposition. Targeting the reduction of lncRNA MIAT may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for SLE [156].

Beyond SLE, lncRNA dysregulation also plays a role in other AISDs. In psoriasis, the level of lnc-HULC is significantly elevated in the blood of patients with psoriasis and can serve as a diagnostic biomarker for these patients [157]. lncRNA Inc-SPRR2G-2 (SPRR2G) is significantly overexpressed in psoriatic lesions and keratinocytes. It promotes keratinocyte proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and increases the expression of inflammatory factors by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway and downregulating the RNA-binding protein KHSRP. Inhibiting the expression of SPRR2G may help reduce the occurrence of psoriasis [158]. Notably, Lnc-GDA-1 has been shown to regulate FOXM1 expression and mediate the proliferation and inflammation of psoriatic keratinocytes through the STAT3/NF-κB signaling pathways, which may represent a promising therapeutic target for psoriasis [159]. The study found that lncRNA AGXT2L1-2:2 is highly expressed in psoriatic lesions and IL-17A-stimulated keratinocytes. By interacting with estrogen-related receptor alpha (ERRα), it promotes keratinocyte proliferation and inhibits apoptosis. Additionally, this lncRNA upregulates the expression of ERRα and activates the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, thereby exacerbating the pathological process of psoriasis. Inhibiting this lncRNA may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for psoriasis [160].

Similarly, lncRNAs play a key role in other skin diseases. In SSc, NRIR is upregulated in monocytes, augmenting type I interferon signaling, while CTBP1-AS2 and OTUD6B-AS1 are overexpressed in dermal fibroblasts, where they promote fibrosis via TGF-β signaling. In pemphigus vulgaris, reduced expression of ANCR (anti-differentiation noncoding RNA) accelerates keratinocyte differentiation and compromises epidermal barrier integrity [161]. In patients with vitiligo, lncRNAs such as AFAP1-AS1 and MALAT1 contribute to the pathogenesis of the disease by interacting with and forming regulatory networks with target gene mRNAs. These lncRNAs, AFAP1-AS1 and MALAT1, can serve as potential biomarkers for vitiligo [162]. In the future, targeted regulation of LncRNA may become a new strategy for the treatment of various AISDs.

Multi-omics techniques and three-dimensional genome analysis of epigenetics in AISDs

Recent advances suggest that DNA methylation, histone PTMs and ncRNAs operate in interconnected networks rather than in isolation. Multimodal approaches play an important role in deciphering epigenetic networks in AISDs. Our scATAC-seq analysis reveals a distinct chromatin accessibility dysregulation in SLE patient immune cells and uncovers pivotal transcription factors underlying the aberrant immune activation, offering a foundation for epigenetic-targeted interventions [163]. Using scChIP-seq, we discover that STAG2 inactivation in melanoma causes dysregulation of cohesin-mediated chromatin looping, leading to epigenetic activation of IRF9 and a consequent enhancement of PD-L1 signaling, which collectively promote immune evasion [164]. Similarly, scRNA-seq technology plays an indispensable role in this process. We used scRNA-seq of AISDs reveals distinct, disease-specific T-cell signatures that correlate with melanoma immunotherapy response, implicating underlying epigenetic drivers of these stable transcriptional programs [165]. The application of scRNA-seq in AISDs has refined our understanding of cellular heterogeneity, allowing for the precise characterization of immune and stromal cell landscapes. This approach has led to the discovery of novel pathogenic cell states, such as a unique CD8⁺ T-cell subset in psoriasis defined by the concurrent expression of CXCL13, IL-17A, and GNLY. [166]. Similarly, some studies have used single-cell multi-omics to investigate epigenetic mechanisms in vitiligo. This dysregulation is underpinned by epigenetic rewiring that drives a concerted program of pro-inflammatory polarization, cytotoxicity, and loss of immunoregulatory function [167]. Future research leveraging multi-omics approaches will decipher the epigenetic landscape of AISDs, thereby uncovering the mechanistic links spanning the genome, cellular behavior, and tissue pathology. This will facilitate the identification of superior diagnostic markers and novel therapeutic targets.

It is important to note that a better understanding of chromatin structure and three-dimensional genome structure can lead to a better understanding of the epigenetic mechanisms in AISDs. Serving as a master regulator of epigenetic signaling, chromatin accessibility is critical for the pathological activation and differentiation of immune cells in SLE [168]. A key example is the overexpression of the transcription factor CREMα in SLE T cells, which remodels the chromatin landscape at loci encoding [169]. Recent studies have shown that alterations in the three-dimensional structure of the genome can regulate immune response and transcription of inflammatory genes, with a direct effect on AISDs. Dysregulation of the three-dimensional genome in psoriatic CD4⁺ T cells, including topological association domain (TAD) boundary shifts, chromatin loop rewiring, and super-enhancer activation, serves as a key driver of pathogenic immune gene expression and disease progression [170]. In patients with SLE, CD4⁺ T cells exhibit significant alterations in three-dimensional genomic architecture, wherein the transcription factor SPI1 facilitates the expression of interferon pathway genes, such as DDX60L, by modulating histone modifications and chromatin loop formation. This work provides novel mechanistic insight into the epigenetic dysregulation in SLE and highlights potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets [171]. The pathogenesis of vitiligo may involve mechanisms that disrupt the three-dimensional genome architecture in melanocytes. Hi-C analyses have revealed that the disruption of specific enhancer–promoter loops, particularly those regulating the MITF gene, can lead to aberrant melanogenesis and loss of cellular function, providing a novel epigenetic perspective on the disease [172]. In summary, three-dimensional genome organization plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of AISDs. By integrating multi-omics technologies and functional genomics approaches, it is possible to elucidate how noncoding variants regulate gene expression through spatial chromatin interactions, offering new insights into disease mechanisms and the development of targeted therapies [173].

The therapeutic prospect of targeting epigenetics

The pathogenesis of AISDs involves a complex interplay between genetic and environmental factors. Genetic variations confer susceptibility—for instance, mutations in inflammasome pathway genes—while environmental triggers exacerbate inflammatory responses through epigenetic reprogramming [174]. Serving as a critical bridge between these elements, epigenetic regulation has emerged as a promising therapeutic target. In recent years, notable breakthroughs in epigenetic modulators—such as DNA methyltransferase inhibitors and histone deacetylase inhibitors—as well as advances in gene-editing technologies, have opened novel therapeutic avenues for the treatment of these disorders.

Modulation of methylation for the treatment of AISDs

In recent years, epigenetic mechanisms, particularly DNA methylation, have emerged as pivotal regulators in the treatment of AISDs. For example, in AD, reduced expression of DNA methyltransferase 1 (Dnmt1) in T cells results in IL-4 overproduction and enhanced IgE-mediated inflammation; restoring Dnmt1 activity can mitigate this Th2-type immune bias [70]. A related mechanism involves demethylation of the SHP-1 gene promoter, which disrupts T-cell function. Notably, the demethylating agent 5-azacytidine reversed this effect in a preclinical model of psoriasis. Furthermore, genome-wide DNA hypomethylation in T cells is correlated with autoreactivity. Correction of such aberrant methylation using the inhibitor procainamide has been shown to improve symptoms in patients with SLE [175]. Additionally, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) was found to alleviate SLE manifestations by elevating global DNA methylation levels through upregulation of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) [176]. Beyond DNA methylation, m6A RNA methylation also contributes to the pathogenesis of inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis and AD. It modulates keratinocyte inflammatory responses by regulating fatty acid metabolism, including the stability of ELOVL6 and lipid metabolic pathways [177]. Together, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting methylation processes across different epigenetic layers and provide new directions for the treatment of AISDs.

Modulation of histone modifications for the treatment of AISDs

Histone modifications represent a promising therapeutic frontier in AISDs, given their central role in regulating immune responses, inflammation, and skin barrier integrity. Emerging PTM—such as lactylation, citrullination, crotonylation, succinylation, SUMOylation, hydroxyisobutyrylation, and palmitoylation—are critically involved in the pathogenesis of various AISDs by altering chromatin structure, gene expression, and immune cell function. Targeting these modifications offers novel strategies for restoring epigenetic homeostasis and immune balance.

For instance, HDAC inhibitors have shown efficacy in modulating histone lactylation patterns. Entinostat, an HDAC1 inhibitor, reduces IL-17A⁺ γδ T-cell infiltration and ameliorates psoriasis-like inflammation in mouse models by suppressing Th17 differentiation and keratinocyte activation [178]. In melanoma, HDAC inhibitors such as vorinostat have been shown to induce PD-L1 upregulation in tumor cells, thereby enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 blockade therapy. Preclinical studies demonstrate that this combination significantly delays tumor progression in murine models [179]. Notably, vorinostat, the first HDAC inhibitor approved for clinical use, promotes the expression of tumor suppressor genes via histone hyperlactylation and currently serves as a standard-of-care treatment for relapsed or refractory CTCL [180]. These findings highlight the potential of HDAC inhibition in counteracting aberrant histone lactylation in AISDs.

Citrullination, mediated by PAD, contributes to autoantigen exposure and NET formation in psoriasis and SLE. Preclinical studies demonstrate that Cl-amidine, a pan-PAD inhibitor, significantly reduces histone H3 citrullination, NET formation, and skin inflammation in a psoriasis model, underscoring the therapeutic potential of PAD inhibition [181]. A recent study demonstrated that inhibition of PAD4 reduces the deposition of NET in psoriasis, suggesting that PAD4 inhibitors may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for this condition [182]. Downregulation of PAD1 expression leads to a marked reduction in keratinocyte layers, accompanied by decreased expression of filaggrin and cornified envelope components (such as loricrin and transglutaminases). These changes result in increased epidermal permeability and a sharp decline in transepidermal electrical resistance. Targeted restoration of PAD1 activity may therefore enhance skin barrier function [183]. Thus, modulating specific PAD isoforms represents a promising strategy for treating skin diseases characterized by autoimmunity, neutrophil activation, or barrier dysfunction.

Research findings demonstrate that deficient crotonylation at S100A7 lysine 49 (K49) plays a critical role in promoting keratinocyte proliferation and metabolic reprogramming in psoriasis. Targeting aberrant S100A7 crotonylation may thus represent a promising therapeutic strategy for intervening in psoriasis and related disorders [98]. Lysine crotonylation (Kcr) may facilitate antigen presentation in patients with SLE and co-regulate leukocyte migration-mediated tissue injury, potentially playing a critical role in the pathogenesis of SLE. Targeted inhibition of Kcr could suppress antigen presentation, thereby representing a promising therapeutic strategy for SLE [116]. Collectively, protein crotonylation holds considerable promise as a therapeutic target for AISDs. Similarly, SUMOylation of TRPV1 attenuates pruritus by suppressing its interaction with the H1 receptor, thereby alleviating dermatological symptoms [184]. Furthermore, modulation of SUMOylation can regulate the extent of skin fibrosis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for SSc [185]. A recent profiling study in psoriatic lesions revealed aberrant Khib, with this modification upregulated on 72 proteins and downregulated on 44 others. Functionally, Khib-modulated proteins are implicated in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting Khib for psoriasis treatment [117].

Palmitoylation, catalyzed by palmitoyltransferases such as ZDHHC13 and ZDHHC2, regulates skin barrier integrity and immune responses. ZDHHC13-mediated palmitoylation of keratinocytes maintains epidermal structure and suppresses IL-23/Th17-driven inflammation, suggesting that enhancing ZDHHC13 activity could benefit AD patients [128]. Conversely, inhibition of ZDHHC2 attenuates psoriatic inflammation by modulating plasmacytoid dendritic cell function [120]. The STING inhibitor C-176, which acts by suppressing STING palmitoylation, attenuates psoriatic inflammation and enhances responsiveness to anti-IL-17A therapy, representing a promising therapeutic strategy for psoriasis [186]. These studies illustrate the enzyme-specific roles of palmitoyltransferases and their potential as therapeutic targets.

There are still a number of potential mechanisms of histone modification that have not yet been investigated clearly, but many studies have shown that modulating histone modification improves AISDs.

Modulation of noncoding RNAs for the treatment of AISDs

It has been found that lncRNAs (e.g., HOTAIR, MALAT1) promote the release of inflammatory factors (IL-6, IL-8) by modulating NF-κB, TLR and other signaling pathways to drive autoimmune responses. The ncRNAs can be used as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, e.g., inhibition of HOTAIR reduces the invasion and metastasis of melanoma, and provides autoimmune skin disease Intervention Ideas [187]. The antimicrobial peptide LL37 (highly expressed in psoriasis and rosacea) breaks the body's immune tolerance to autonucleic acids by mediating the binding of its own ncRNAs(e.g., U1 RNA) to scavenger receptors on the cell surface, activating intracellular nucleic acid pattern recognition receptors (e.g., TLR), and triggering the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IFN-β1. Blocking the interaction of LL37 with scavenger receptors can inhibit the inflammatory cascade response and provide new therapeutic strategies for diseases such as psoriasis [188]. Studies have shown that lncRNA HCP5 upregulates the oncogene RARRES3 through sponge adsorption of miR-1286 and inhibits the progression of cutaneous melanoma. lncRNA HCP5 could be a targeted therapeutic point for the subsequent treatment of cutaneous melanoma [189].

Recently gene editing and cellular therapies also have significant potential for reversing aberrant epistatic states of autoimmune-related genes by fusing dCas9 with epimerase modifying enzymes (e.g., DNMT3A, TET1) to achieve precise methylation/demethylation modulation of specific gene loci [190], and non-viral vector lipid nanoparticles can reduce the immunogenicity of CRISPR-Cas9 to improve in vivo Targeting Efficiency Clinical Trial [191]. NCT02810951 as an autologous cell gene therapy using genetically modified autologous fibroblasts (FCX-007) for recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB) confirmed that Editing of COL7A1 gene can restore the anchoring fiber structure of the skin [192].

Overall, these findings underscore the expanding role of noncoding RNAs as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets in autoimmune skin disorders, highlighting mechanisms from inflammatory activation to epigenetic regulation. Furthermore, emerging technologies—such as precise gene editing via CRISPR-dCas9 systems and non-viral delivery methods—offer promising avenues for correcting disease-associated genetic and epigenetic alterations, bridging molecular insights with clinical applications.

Emerging therapeutic strategies

Interestingly, some epigenetic targets for the regulation of autoimmune skin diseases can also regulate the tumor microenvironment [193]. Epigenetic drugs (e.g., DNMTi, HDACi) or CRISPR epigenetic editing tools can be used to treat AISDs and may influence the course of cancer by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment [194]. Emerging evidence indicates that autoimmunity (characterized by T cell overactivation) and cancer (marked by functional suppression of T cells) represent two extremes of immune dysregulation. The metabolism–epigenetics axis serves as a central regulatory mechanism governing this balance. Consequently, precise modulation of the metabolic and epigenetic states of specific T cell subsets—through pharmacological agents, nutritional interventions, or genetic engineering techniques—offers a promising strategy to restore immune homeostasis. Such approaches may suppress pathogenic T cell responses in autoimmune disorders and inhibit tumor progression in cancers, thereby advancing the development of precision immunotherapies (195).

Conclusion

AISDs arise from a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and profound immune dysregulation, with B and T lymphocytes playing central roles in disease pathogenesis. This review underscores that epigenetic mechanisms—including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and noncoding RNAs—serve as master regulators that orchestrate immune responses, skin barrier integrity, and chronic inflammation in AISDs. These modifications provide a dynamic interface through which environmental factors influence gene expression without altering the DNA sequence, thereby contributing significantly to disease onset, progression, and heterogeneity.

We have detailed how dysregulated DNA methylation—both global hypomethylation and locus-specific hypermethylation—alters the expression of immune and barrier genes, facilitating autoimmune activation. Emerging histone posttranslational modifications—such as lactylation, citrullination, crotonylation, and succinylation—have been implicated in neutrophil activation, autoantigen exposure, and metabolic reprogramming in keratinocytes and immune cells. Furthermore, noncoding RNAs (miRNAs and lncRNAs) fine-tune inflammatory pathways and cellular functions, offering both diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

The translation of these epigenetic insights into clinical practice holds significant promise for improving patient outcomes. Epigenetic biomarkers may enable earlier and more precise diagnosis, while therapies targeting DNA methyltransferases, histone-modifying enzymes, or specific noncoding RNAs could restore immune homeostasis and barrier function with greater specificity than current immunosuppressive regimens. For patients, this could mean reduced disease flares, fewer side effects, and personalized treatment strategies that address the root epigenetic dysregulation rather than merely alleviating symptoms.

Despite these advances, critical challenges remain. The functional roles of many novel histone modifications in AISDs are still emerging, and the interactions between different epigenetic layers are not fully elucidated. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies to track epigenetic changes during disease progression, single-cell multi-omics to resolve cell-type-specific mechanisms, and preclinical models to validate novel epigenetic therapeutics. Ultimately, integrating epigenetic approaches into the management of AISDs may revolutionize therapeutic paradigms, offering hope for lasting remission and improved quality of life for patients worldwide.
