NDUFA5 deficiency promotes renal inflammation in diabetic nephropathy via mitochondrial ROS signaling
Authors: Guofang Gong, Li Meng, Mengyao Wang, Xiuli Si, Yunzhuo Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDiabetic nephropathy (DN) involves mitochondrial dysfunction, but the role of complex I subunit NDUFA5 is unclear. This study investigated its protective mechanisms. Using tubule-specific Ndufa5 knockout mice, streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice, and high glucose-treated human kidney-2 (HK-2) cells, we assessed the effects of NDUFA5 manipulation. Renal NDUFA5 expression was reduced in diabetes. Its knockout exacerbated mitochondrial damage, ROS overproduction, and renal injury, while AAV9-mediated overexpression ameliorated these defects. NDUFA5 overexpression also suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistically, NDUFA5 attenuated inflammation by inhibiting mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) generation. Thus, NDUFA5 protects against DN, at least in part, by preserving mitochondrial integrity and reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.Subject areas: health sciences, biological sciences, molecular biology, functional aspects of cell biology

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), a severe chronic complication of diabetes mellitus, is characterized by progressive renal dysfunction and ultimately progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Tubulointerstitial inflammation has been identified as a critical mediator of renal functional deterioration and ESRD progression.1,2,3,4 Notably, tubular cells constitute approximately 90% of the renal cortex. Emerging evidence suggests that proximal tubular injury plays a pivotal role in the initial phase of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and significantly contributes to its subsequent progression.5,6,7,8,9

Considering that the kidney exhibits the second-highest ATP-dependent energy demand in the human body, following only the heart, the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis plays a crucial role in preserving optimal renal function.10 The proximal tubules, predominantly situated in the renal cortex, are characterized by their high mitochondrial density. Oxidative stress, defined as the imbalance between elevated mitochondrial-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and their subsequent detoxification by antioxidant defense systems,11 has been recognized as a crucial pathophysiological mechanism underlying DKD progression. This oxidative imbalance exerts multifaceted deleterious effects on renal function, primarily through the activation and amplification of pro-inflammatory signaling cascades.12,13

As the initial enzyme complex in the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), complex I (CI), also known as NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase, facilitates electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone. This process is coupled with proton translocation, generating an electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, which contributes approximately 40% of the proton-motive force required for ATP synthesis by ATP synthase.10,14 Impairments in CI disrupt mitochondrial function in DN, a condition frequently characterized by cellular energy depletion.15,16 However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these defects remain largely elusive. In eukaryotes, CI, with an approximate molecular weight of 1 MDa, is composed of 45 distinct subunits. Among these, 7 are encoded by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), while the remaining 38 are encoded by nuclear DNA (nDNA). Among the 38 nDNA-encoded subunits, 31 are classified as “supernumerary,” representing evolutionary acquisitions that are thought to play regulatory roles. For example, the core subunit V1 of NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase (NDUFV1) has been shown to enhance mitochondrial integrity and function, resulting in diminished oxidative stress and reduced apoptotic cell death in acute kidney injury (AKI).17 Inhibition of NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] iron-sulfur protein 2 (NDUFS2) markedly impaired cell proliferation, CI-dependent respiration, glycolytic activity, ATP levels, and cell membrane integrity in HEK293 cells, a human embryonic kidney cell line.18 Lastly, NDUFS4 has been demonstrated to enhance mitochondrial cristae structure, promote mitochondrial dynamics, and alleviate albuminuria in DKD.

Following the precedent of other CI supernumerary subunits (e.g., NDUFV1 and NDUFS4) implicated in renal pathophysiology, we focused on NDUFA5 (NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] 1 alpha subcomplex subunit 5). Located in the peripheral arm of CI, NDUFA5 is a critical nuclear-encoded regulator of mitochondrial redox homeostasis. Its pathogenic relevance is underscored by direct evidence linking NDUFA5 downregulation to oxidative phosphorylation defects, elevated ROS, and renal injury in a nephrotoxicity model.19 Functional genetics in Candida auris further confirms its indispensability for mitochondrial integrity, ATP synthesis, and ROS control, with loss leading to bioenergetic collapse.20 Collectively, these studies nominate NDUFA5 as a master regulator of mitochondrial metabolism. However, its specific role in DN remains unexplored.

This study aimed to investigate the renoprotective role of NDUFA5 in DN. We demonstrate that enhancing NDUFA5 expression attenuates kidney interstitial inflammation, improves mitochondrial integrity and function, and reduces oxidative stress.

Results

Generation and validation of tubule-specific NDUFA5 knockout mice

To investigate the role of NDUFA5 in renal tubules, we generated a conditional knockout model using Ggt1-Cre drivers（Figure 1). Genotyping confirmed successful establishment of Ndufa5fl/fl; Ggt1-Cre+ mice (referred to as cKO) (Figure 2A). Western blot revealed a significant decrease in NDUFA5 protein in cKO mice, which was effectively rescued by AAV-Ndufa5 administration (Figure 2B), confirming the model’s suitability for functional studies.

NDUFA5 knockout disrupts renal morphology and increases urinary albumin excretion

Histopathological examination by H&E and PAS staining demonstrated substantial renal abnormalities in cKO mice compared to controls. The cKO mice exhibited disorganized renal architecture, marked tubular dilation with luminal destruction, and prominent hydropic degeneration of tubular epithelial cells. These pathological changes were significantly ameliorated in cKO mice receiving CV232-NDUFA5, which exhibited preserved tubular integrity and attenuated hydropic degeneration, particularly in proximal tubules (Figure 3A). Consistent with the structural damage, urinary albumin excretion (UAE) was significantly elevated in cKO mice relative to controls, indicating compromised tubular reabsorption (Figure 3B). Notably, CV232-NDUFA5 effectively reduced UAE levels in cKO mice, further supporting the functional rescue of renal integrity.

NDUFA5 loss disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis and promotes oxidative stress

We further elucidated the subcellular mechanisms linking Ndufa5 deletion to renal dysfunction. Knockout mice exhibited severe mitochondrial aberrations—including cristae loss, swelling, and reduced aspect ratio—by TEM (Figure 4A), suggesting impaired ETC integrity. This structural decline correlated with reduced ATP levels, indicating bioenergetic deficiency. Notably, CV232-NDUFA5 normalized mitochondrial ATP production, confirming NDUFA5’s essential role in energy maintenance (Figure 4B). Furthermore, NDUFA5 deficiency induced a pronounced redox imbalance, with elevated ROS, depleted glutathione, and reduced SOD activity (Figures 4C and 4D). These findings collectively indicate that NDUFA5 loss disrupts ETC architecture, leading to bioenergetic failure and oxidative damage, ultimately driving renal pathology.

NDUFA5 attenuates renal injury in STZ-induced DN mice

Having established the essential role of NDUFA5 in tubular homeostasis, we investigated its pathophysiological significance in DN. Western blot result showed that NDUFA5 expression in STZ mice was significantly lower than that in healthy control, but it increased after CV232-NDUFA5 treatment (Figure 5). The result confirmed the successful overexpression of NDUFA5 in STZ mice. We then examined the effect of NDUFA5 overexpression on renal injury. Compared with STZ mice, NDUFA5 overexpression decreased the fasting blood level, kidney/body weight, 24 h-urinary protein level, serum creatinine (Scr), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) level (Figures 6A–6E). Moreover, NDUFA5 overexpression significantly mitigated STZ-induced renal tubular injury, as indicated by HE and PAS (Figure 6F). Together, these results demonstrate that the reduced expression of NDUFA5 in kidneys might be involved in renal injury induced by STZ and reinforcement of NDUFA5 may play a beneficial role against renal damage in DN.

NDUFA5 ameliorates mitochondrial abnormality and restores redox homeostasis in diabetic kidney

We then examined whether the benefits of NDUFA5 in DN were mediated through mitochondrial protection. Mitochondrial dysfunction in renal cells, characterized by impaired bioenergetics, has been identified as a critical factor driving the pathological alterations associated with DN progression to ESRD.21,22,23 Furthermore, mitochondria serve as the primary cellular source of ROS, potentially inducing oxidative stress through excessive ROS generation.8,24 TEM analysis revealed significant ultrastructural alterations in renal mitochondria following STZ treatment, characterized by mitochondrial fragmentation, swelling, cristae disruption, and rupture of both inner and outer membranes. Notably, these STZ-induced pathological changes were substantially ameliorated by NDUFA5 overexpression (Figure 7A). Consistent with these morphological observations, we detected a significant reduction in ATP levels within renal tissues of STZ-treated mice compared to wild-type (WT) controls, which was effectively prevented by NDUFA5 overexpression (Figure 7B). The mitochondrial ETC, while generating ATP, is also a major source of ROS through electron leakage and subsequent reaction with molecular oxygen. To further elucidate the antioxidant defense mechanisms, we quantified the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, GSH-PX and SOD, in renal tissues. Our data demonstrated that NDUFA5 overexpression significantly restored the impaired activities of these enzymes in STZ-treated mouse kidneys (Figures 7C and 7D). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that upregulation of NDUFA5 expression in renal tissue represents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction in DN.

NDUFA5 represses renal inflammation in diabetic kidney

Inflammatory response is a common event that plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of DN. We therefore determined the effect of NDUFA5 overexpression on renal inflammation in DN mice by measuring the inflammatory factors interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) in DN kidneys. Western blot results revealed that compared with WT mice, an evident rise in the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, and NF-κB, was observed in the renal cortex of STZ-induced DN mice. In the presence of exogenous Ndufa5, the proinflammatory factors induced by STZ were largely prevented (Figure 8A). Consistent with the protein results, the mRNA amounts of IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1 in the STZ group (Figures 8B–8D) were dramatically upregulated in comparison with the WT group, while these effects were markedly abated following NDUFA5 overexpression. IHC was carried out to investigate the levels of IL-1β and MCP-1 in the renal tissues. Expressions of IL-1β and MCP-1 were elevated in tubular cells of STZ kidneys (Figure 8E). Collectively, these results demonstrated that NDUFA5 can reduce renal inflammation induced by STZ in mice.

Knockdown of NDUFA5 induces mitochondrial abnormality and ROS in HK-2 cells

To investigate the effect of NDUFA5 on mitochondrial stabilization in HK-2 cells, we directly knockdown Ndufa5 with si-NDUFA5. Western blot results displayed that si-NDUFA5 significantly decreased the expression of NDUFA5, thus confirming Ndufa5 knockdown efficiency (Figure 9A). Then we evaluated the effect of si-NDUFA5 on mitochondrial morphology and ROS. The mitochondrial network was intact in normal HK-2 cells with a tubular appearance as determined by MitoTracker staining; Ablation of Ndufa5 in HK-2 cells led to mitochondrial fragmentation (Figure 9B). Meanwhile, ATP was significantly reduced by si-NDUFA5 (Figure 9C). Furthermore, to comprehensively assess the oxidative stress induced by si-NDUFA5, we evaluated both the production of ROS (MitoSOX, DCFH-DA) and the cellular antioxidant defense system (GSH, SOD). Si-NDUFA5 significantly increased both mitochondrial superoxide levels (MitoSOX assay) and total intracellular ROS (DCFH-DA assay) (Figure 9D), pinpointing mitochondria as the primary source of oxidative stress. Concurrently, the endogenous antioxidant defense was compromised, as evidenced by markedly decreased GSH levels and inhibited SOD activity (Figures 9E and 9F). Therefore, it can be concluded that NDUFA5 plays a crucial role in mitochondrial maintenance.

High glucose inhibits the translocation of NDUFA5 from the cytosol to mitochondria in HK-2 cells

We further explored the mechanism underlying NDUFA5 reduction in DN. The NDUFA5 proteins, encoded by nDNA, contain distinct targeting sequences that facilitate their translocation from the cytosol to mitochondrial surface receptors, followed by their precise integration into specific mitochondrial subcompartments.25,26,27 To capture the early dynamics of this process, we first conducted a time-course experiment. Consistent with the rapid onset of mitochondrial stress responses,28 treatment of cells with HG induced a time-dependent mislocalization of NDUFA5, which became most pronounced and statistically significant at 100 min (Figure 10A). Therefore, this key early time point was selected for detailed analysis. Quantification of Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the fraction of NDUFA5 co-localization with mitochondria was reduced significantly in HG-treated cells compared to the control (Figure 10B). This indicates that HG disrupts the proper targeting of NDUFA5 to mitochondria. These results suggested that the abnormality of NDUFA5 mitochondrial translocation might be involved in tubular injury induced by HG.

NDUFA5 overexpression attenuates ROS and inflammatory response in HK-2 cells treated with high glucose

Next, we examined the effect of NDUFA5 overexpression on ROS and the inflammatory response induced by HG. First, we confirmed the efficiency of NDUFA5 overexpression by western blot. NDUFA5 expression was significantly elevated both in WCL and mitochondria after transfection of the NDUFA5 overexpression plasmid (Figures 11A and 11B). Crucially, NDUFA5 overexpression not only reversed the HG-induced overproduction of mitochondrial superoxide (MitoSOX) and total cellular ROS (DCFH-DA) (Figure 11C), but also restored the compromised antioxidant defense system, as evidenced by the recovered levels of GSH and SOD activity (Figures 11D and 11E). Meanwhile, the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, and NF-κB, was all significantly inhibited by NDUFA5 (Figure 12).

Discussion

Renal tubular epithelial cells exhibit a high mitochondrial density to support their elevated metabolic energy requirements, rendering them particularly susceptible to disruptions in mitochondrial bioenergetic function. Among the protein complexes comprising the mitochondrial ETC, CI stands out as the largest and most structurally complex. The loss or dysfunction of CI is frequently associated with a range of adverse cellular and physiological consequences. For example, deficiency of NDUFS2 in dopaminergic neurons is associated with Parkinson’s disease-like phenotypes in mice.18 Moreover, ablation of NDUFA5 in neurons causes a mild encephalopathy.29 This subunit plays a critical role in establishing the electrochemical gradient necessary for ATP synthesis. In the present study, we found that NDUFA5 expression was significantly reduced in kidneys from STZ mice. Furthermore, knockdown of NDUFA5 in HK-2 cells induced mitochondrial ultra-structure damage and ATP synthesis reduction. These loss-of-function results indicate that NDUFA5 deficiency is sufficient to directly cause mitochondrial structural and functional abnormalities. The NDUFA5 gene, encoding a protein with a molecular mass of 13 kDa, is ubiquitously expressed across mammalian species and is mapped to chromosome 7q32 in the human genome. NDUFA5 mutations have been shown to be associated with chronic encephalopathy,29 hepatosteatosis,30 and human pancreatic islets from patients with type 2 diabetes.31

We reasoned that the forced expression of the NDUFA5 subunit might overcome the mitochondrial maladaptation in DKD and provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of its progression. Overexpression of the NDUFAS4 in podocytes improved mitochondrial respiration and prevented mitochondrial fragmentation in podocytes of diabetic mice.32 Consistent with our hypothesis, the experimental data revealed that the upregulation of NDUFA5 expression exerts renoprotective effects in STZ-induced diabetic mice. Specifically, NDUFA5 overexpression significantly improved renal function parameters, as evidenced by reduced BUN, Scr, and UAE. Furthermore, it attenuated proximal tubular epithelial cell injury and suppressed renal inflammatory responses. Notably, as an essential component of mitochondrial CI, NDUFA5 overexpression effectively alleviated mitochondrial fragmentation and restored cellular energy metabolism through enhanced ATP synthesis. Together, these loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments demonstrate that the reduction of NDUFA5 is a contributing factor, rather than a mere consequence, in driving mitochondrial dysfunction and injury in diabetic tubules. It is noteworthy that a CNS-specific NDUFA5 knockout mouse model, while exhibiting CI deficiency, did not show significant oxidative damage.29 This discrepancy with our findings likely underscores the tissue- and context-dependent nature of mitochondrial stress responses. The renal tubule, with its high metabolic demands and exposure to hyperglycemic and inflammatory insults in diabetes, may be particularly predisposed to mounting a pronounced mtROS-mediated inflammatory cascade upon NDUFA5 loss, a response that might be attenuated or different in the CNS under non-disease conditions.

These observations in vivo and in vitro are not contradictory but may reflect a hierarchical dysregulation of NDUFA5 during DN. We propose that the initial cellular response to hyperglycemia involves impaired mitochondrial targeting of NDUFA5, directly compromising CI function. In the chronic in vivo setting, sustained metabolic stress may then lead to a secondary downregulation of total NDUFA5 protein, further entrenching mitochondrial dysfunction. To solidify this model, future work should seek definitive evidence to separate the contributions of initial mislocalization from subsequent protein downregulation across the progression of the disease.

Subsequently, we investigated the role of NDUFA5 in mitochondrial function and inflammatory responses under in vitro conditions. As a nDNA-encoded protein, NDUFA5 undergoes post-translational translocation across the mitochondrial membrane.27,33 Our experimental data demonstrated that HG conditions significantly impaired NDUFA5 mitochondrial translocation, resulting in mitochondrial network fragmentation and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Importantly, mitochondrial-targeted overexpression of NDUFA5 effectively ameliorated HG-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and suppressed inflammatory activation. This directly proves that restoring its mitochondrial localization is sufficient to reverse high-glucose-induced damage, underscoring the functional significance of correct subcellular targeting. These findings collectively indicate that proper mitochondrial localization of NDUFA5 is essential for maintaining mitochondrial integrity and regulating inflammatory processes under hyperglycemic conditions.

As comprehensively elucidated in recent reviews, mitochondrial dysfunction—particularly of the ETC—is not merely a bystander but a driver of inflammatory responses through multiple, interlinked mechanisms.34 The release of mtDNA and other DAMPs from damaged mitochondria directly activates innate immune receptors, including cGAS-STING and TLR9, initiating a sterile inflammatory cascade.28,35 Concurrently, excessive mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) has been shown to induce renal injury by activating proinflammatory signals such as the NLRP3 inflammasome.36,37,38,39 We found that inhibition of mtROS attenuated the inflammatory response (e.g., cytokine release and infiltration by macrophages) in the kidneys of STZ mice and in HK-2 cells maintained under HG conditions. mtROS are predominantly generated at complexes I and III of the ETC through the interaction of electrons originating from NADH or FADH2 with molecular oxygen (O2).40 We observed that NDUFA5 overexpression reduced mtROS in HK-2 cells under HG. Therefore, it seems likely that NDUFA5 can suppress the expression of inflammatory cytokines by inhibiting mtROS. Our study bridges a specific knowledge gap within this established paradigm. We identify NDUFA5 deficiency as a novel upstream molecular lesion that instigates the pathogenic cascade of ETC dysfunction → mtROS overproduction → renal inflammation in the context of hyperglycemia.

In summary, the present findings demonstrate for the first time that NDUFA5 deficiency is a direct cause of mitochondrial damage and energy failure in renal tubules, as rigorously shown by our loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments. Furthermore, by situating NDUFA5 within the mtROS-inflammatory axis, our data provide strong support for a mechanistically plausible causal link between this subunit’s loss and the progression of renal inflammation in diabetes. The potential mechanism may involve abnormal mitochondrial translocation and the regulation of mtROS.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, while we establish a strong correlation between NDUFA5 loss, mitochondrial damage, and inflammation, the precise molecular cascade linking impaired NDUFA5 mitochondrial translocation to the specific activation of inflammatory effectors (e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome or NF-κB pathways) remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies employing pathway-specific inhibitors or omics approaches are needed to map this causal sequence.

Second, our in vivo model could not distinguish whether NDUFA5 dysfunction stems primarily from an overall reduction in protein abundance or a specific defect in its mitochondrial targeting—a distinction with implications for therapeutic design.

Third, and most critically, the central hypothesis that tubule-specific loss of NDUFA5 is sufficient to drive diabetic kidney injury remains to be directly tested. While our composite evidence is compelling, definitive proof requires the generation and analysis of a diabetic NDUFA5 tubule-specific conditional knockout model.

Fourth, the study primarily employs a type 1 diabetes model. Although hyperglycemia is a common driver, confirming whether the NDUFA5-dependent mechanism operates similarly in type II diabetic contexts (e.g., db/db mice) is an essential future step for generalizing the findings.

Finally, only male mice and a male-derived HK-2 cell line were used. As sex is known to influence mitochondrial function and inflammatory responses, future studies incorporating female subjects are needed to assess potential sex-specific effects of NDUFA5 in DN.

Cell line

The human kidney-2 (HK-2) cell line, an immortalized proximal tubular epithelial cell line derived from normal adult human male kidney, was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination and used within passages 5–15.

Method details

Generation of mice with tubule-specific deletion of Ndufa5

Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing. The targeting strategy is illustrated in Figure 1. Two gRNAs flanking exon 3 were used alongside a donor vector containing loxP sites and homology arms, which was co-injected with Cas9 mRNA into C57BL/6 fertilized eggs. This resulted in the insertion of loxP sites into the adjacent introns.

Founder (F0) mice carrying the floxed allele were bred to wild-type C57BL/6 mice to establish heterozygous Ndufa5fl/+ offspring, which were then intercrossed to generate homozygous Ndufa5fl/fl mice. To achieve tubule-specific deletion, Ndufa5fl/fl mice were crossed with Ggt1-Cre transgenic mice expressing Cre recombinase under the kidney-specific Ggt1 promoter. Experimental mice (Ndufa5fl/fl; Ggt1-Cre+, referred to as cKO) and control littermates (Ndufa5fl/fl; Ggt1-Cre-, referred to as Control) were obtained from these crosses.

Genotyping was performed via PCR on tail-derived genomic DNA. The floxed Ndufa5 allele was identified using primers P1-F/P1-R (annealing at 60°C), yielding a 236bp product (wild-type: 175bp). The Ggt1-Cre transgene was detected with primers Cre-F/Cre-R, producing a 430bp fragment.

Tissue-specific deletion of exon 3 in renal tubules was confirmed by deletion detection PCR using renal genomic DNA and primers Del-F/Del-R. Successful recombination produced a 255bp amplicon, whereas unrecombined DNA yielded no product.

STZ animal model

Recombinant AAV9 expressing mouse Ndufa5 (NM_026614) was constructed using the CV232 vector (serotype 9, EGFP reporter) by GeneChem (Order #GOSV0 378247). The viral stock (6.64×1013 v.g./mL in PBS) was administered via tail vein injection (100 μL/mouse, 3.32×1012 v.g.) to STZ-induced diabetic C57BL/6 mice (n = 5/group). After confirmation of successful diabetes induction (fasting blood glucose≥16.7 mmol/L on two consecutive measurements), diabetic mice were randomly allocated into treatment groups. One week later, mice received a single tail-vein injection of AAV9-CV232-NDUFA5 or an equal dose of the AAV9-CV232 empty vector for therapeutic intervention.

Biochemical and histopathological analyses

Fasting blood glucose levels were determined using a glucometer (Accu-Chek, Roche, Germany) after a 6-h fast. The kidney/body weight ratio was calculated from the wet weight of bilateral kidneys and the final body weight measured at sacrifice. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine of serum (Scr) levels were quantified using a biochemical auto-analyzer (Hitachi 7600 modular chemistry analyzer, Hitachi Ltd, USA). The UAE were measured by urinary albumin quantitative assay kit (C035-21, NJJCBIO, Nanjing, China). The fixed kidney tissues were embedded in paraffin, and tissues were cut into 4 μm sections for H&E, PAS and immunohistochemical staining, respectively. The histopathological damage was evaluated and recorded using a light microscope (400× amplification; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy

The mitochondrial morphology was assessed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Assays of ATP, glutathione(GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD)

ATP was measured through an ATP Content Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit and glutathione (GSH) assay kit were purchased from Jiancheng of China. All experiments were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cell lines and transfection

HK-2 cells were cultured with DMEM containing different concentrations of D-glucose (5, 30 mM) and with or without other interventions. In addition, for gene disruption, HK-2 cells were pretransfected with NDUFA5 siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) or with recombinant AAV9 expressing Ndufa5 (NM_026614) (MOI = 1×104 v.g./cell) under the control of the CV232 vector (serotype 9) was constructed by GeneChem (Order #GOSV0378247). n = 5/group.

Mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions preparation

HK-2 cell pellets were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in lysis buffer. Cells were homogenized on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged in three sequential steps: 1000g, 12,000g, and 100,000g. The 12,000g pellet was considered the “mitochondrial” fraction, and the 100,000g supernatant the “cytosolic” fraction. These fractions were subjected to western blot analyses.

Immunofluorescence

IF staining was used to detect the expression of NDUFA5, and the injury of mitochondrial in HK-2 Cells. Primary antibody NDUFA5 (Proteintech, USA)was used to detect NDUFA5 in HK-2 cells. The MitoTracker Red(MTR, Invitrogen, USA) was used to detect morphology of mitochondrial in HK-2 cells. Cells were stained with 500 nM MTR for 15 min before measurement. The fluorescence intensity indicating NDUFA5 localization at mitochondria was quantified using ImageJ software.

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using fluorescent probes. For total cellular ROS, cells were loaded with 10 μM 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime, Beijing, China) in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37°C. For mitochondrial superoxide, cells were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX Red (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) under the same conditions.

Western blot

Cell and tissue lysate homogenates were prepared. Total proteins were extracted from kidney tissues and HK-2 cells. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked for 1 h and then incubated separately with different kinds of primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The protein bands were detected by a chemiluminescence image analysis system (Tanon Science and Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China), and their intensities were measured with ImageJ software.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (Sigma Aldrich, USA). cDNA was generated using cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara). Real-time PCR was performed using an SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase HPlus) Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RR820; Takara). The relative quantification of mRNA levels was calculated based on the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. The primers were as follows: GAPDH:5′-GTC TCC TCT GAC TTC AAC AGC G- 3′ (forward) and 5′- ACC ACC CTG TTG CTG TAG CCA A- 3′ (reverse). IL-1β: 5′- TCG CCA GTG AAA TGA TGG CT - 3′ (forward) and 5′- TGA AGC CCT TGC TGT AGT GG - 3′ (reverse). TNF-α: 5′- CAA GGA CAG CAG AGG ACC AG - 3′ (forward) and 5′- GCC GTG GGT CAG TAT GTG AG - 3′ (reverse). MCP-1: 5′- CAG GTG ACA GAG ACT CTT GGG A - 3′ (forward) and 5′- GGC AAT CCT ACA GCC AAG AGC T - 3′ (reverse).

Quantification and statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± SD with the results of at least three independent experiments. The number of independent biological replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t test was utilized.For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Statistical significance was defined by asterisks as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Values of p > 0.05 were considered not significant (ns).
