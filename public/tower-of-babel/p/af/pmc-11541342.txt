Olink proteomics and lipidomics analysis of serum from patients infected with non-tuberculous mycobacteria and Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Li Wang, Guoling Yang, Liang Guo, Lan Yao, Yidian Liu, Wei Sha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infections are difficult to diagnose and treat, significantly burdening global health. The host immune status is generally believed to be associated with the onset and progression of NTM and MTB infections, but its specific impact remains unclear.MethodsIn the present study, proteomics and lipidomics analysis of serum from normal controls (n = 26) and patients with MTB (n = 26), rapidly growing NTM (RGM, n = 15), and slowly growing NTM (SGM, n = 21) were conducted using the Olink technique based on a highly sensitive and specific neighborhood extension assay and the lipidomics technique.ResultsIFN-γ, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13, etc. were simultaneously upregulated in MTB, RGM, and SGM, while lipids FAHFA 22:3, FAHFA 26:4, FAHFA 24:4, FAHFA 20:5, FAHFA 18:2 simultaneously downregulated. IL8, CCL3, CXCL5, and MCP-2, etc. were simultaneously upregulated in RGM and SGM compared to MTB, as well as PCs, LPCs, PEs, and LPEs. Compared with RGM, IL7, CD27, CCL17, CXCL12, and LPC 28:7-SN2 were downregulated in SGM. Pathway analyses revealed that tuberculosis, sphingolipid signaling pathway, and adipocytokine signaling pathway were regulated at the protein level and metabolite level. Diagnostic panels comprising immune-associated proteins and lipids greatly enhance diagnostic specificity and sensitivity.ConclusionThis integrated multi-omics analysis provides a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular landscape of NTM and MTB, which may provide molecular targets for specialized therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-024-01943-z.Keywords: Non-tuberculous mycobacteria, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Biomarkers, Proteomics, Olink, Lipidomics, Metabolomics

Introduction

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) lung infections are an increasing clinical problem worldwide, even surpassing tuberculosis in some developed countries or regions [1, 2]. Due to the increasing incidence of diseases caused by NTM [3], its prevalence is becoming a cause for concern [4, 5]. It is demonstrated that approximately 13.7/100,000 people in the southeast of the United States were infected with NTM each year, with pulmonary infection being 9.4/100,000 per year [6].

The diagnosis and treatment of NTM are complicated and challenging [7]. To date, more than 140 NTMs have been identified, and NTM can be categorized into rapidly growing NTM (RGM) and slowly growing NTM (SGM). Pulmonary infections caused by these two types of NTM differ significantly in disease progression rate and prognosis. Rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGMs), such as M. abscessus, progress more rapidly, while slowly growing mycobacteria (SGM), such as M. avium complex and M. kansasii, progress relatively slowly [8]. In addition, due to the symptoms of NTM pulmonary infections being similar to those of tuberculosis, some NTM pulmonary infections, especially SGM, can be easily misdiagnosed as Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infections in clinical practice. Therefore, to make an accurate diagnosis and treatment of NTM and MTB pulmonary infections, there is an urgent need for pulmonologists to search for reliable diagnostic markers that can aid in the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of RGM, SGM, and MTB infections, and systematically understand the differences and associations between these three infection states.

There is a growing recognition that multiple molecular mechanisms interact to form a complex system that specific pathogens use to interfere with host biochemical processes [9]. Therefore, systems-level studies are a potent complement to traditional molecular biology research in investigating pathogens and their interactions with the human host. The study of MTB and NTM at the proteomic level can help discover potential diagnostic marker proteins, identify proteins that may be considered potential targets for drug development, and enhance our understanding of pathogen-host interactions [10]. The advancement of high-throughput proteomics allows for the simultaneous quantitative analysis of a large number of proteins. However, the massive amount of data makes analysis more challenging, and false-positive results pose a challenge for subsequent validation [11]. Recently, Olink technology has begun to gain popularity as it offers a wide range of test panels for various disease processes. It requires only a small volume of samples, which is especially important in cases where clinical samples are scarce. In addition, it captures multiple proteins across the entire dynamic range (> 10 logs). A previous study demonstrated that Olink Proteomics has excellent reproducibility and stability in detecting proteins in plasma samples [12].

Lipid metabolism is considered to play a key role in the pathogenicity of several intracellular bacteria [13]. The complex lipid in the cell wall of MTB is a significant effector molecule that interacts positively with the host, regulates host metabolism, stimulates immune responses, and, in turn, influences the physiology of both host cells and MTB [14, 15]. Lipids from the host also serve as a source of nutrients for MTB and play a crucial role in determining the outcome of infection by regulating lipid homeostasis. Due to the similarities in the biological structure and mode of invasion of NTM and MTB, the above description of MTB is equally applicable to NTM. Although new technologies and infection models have greatly contributed to the understanding of various aspects of host-pathogen interactions at the lipid level, the impact of such interactions on pathogen lipid regulation is still in its early stages. This is primarily because of the limited background knowledge in this research area. Therefore, in the present study, Olink proteomics and lipidomics are used to identify differences in immune-related proteins and lipids among patients with NTM and MTB, as well as normal donors. This investigation aims to explore the altered host-pathogen interactions and identify potential markers.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

In the present study, 26 serum samples from normal donors, 26 samples from patients newly diagnosed with MTB, 15 samples from patients newly diagnosed with RGM, and 21 samples from patients newly diagnosed with SGM were collected at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital between 2022 and 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a clear diagnosis (positive sputum microscopy, sputum culture, or PCR); (2) patients aged between 18 and 75 years; and (3) patients with normal immune function (assessed by routine blood counts and lymphocyte subpopulation counts). The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) patients with combined NTM and MTB infection; (2) patients with more than one NTM species infection; (3) patients with extrapulmonary NTM or MTB infection. (4) patients with diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and other metabolic diseases; (5) HIV-positive individuals; (6) patients with other infections; (7) patients with other severe lung diseases; and (8) patients who had received anti-tuberculous treatment at the time of enrollment. The study design is shown in Fig. 1.

Overview of the study design. MTB: Mycobacterium tuberculosis. NTM: Non-tuberculous mycobacterial. RGM: Rapidly growing mycobacteria. SGM: Slowly growing mycobacteria. DEPs: Differentially expressed proteins. DEMs: Differently expressed metabolites. PPI: Protein-protein interaction

The present study was conducted by the Hippocratic Declaration and other ethical guidelines. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (approval number: K21-257). All individuals enrolled in this study have signed informed consent forms.

Olink proteomics analysis

Serum samples were analyzed using the Olink® IMMUNO-ONCOLOGY panel (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The Proximity Extension Assay technology used in the Olink protocol has been well described [16], allowing for the simultaneous analysis of 92 proteins with just 1 µL of sample per sample. Pairs of oligonucleotide-labeled antibody probes bind to targeted protein. If the two probes are in proximity, the oligonucleotides will hybridize in a pair-wise manner. The addition of DNA polymerase initiates a DNA polymerization reaction that generates a distinct PCR target sequence. The DNA sequences generated were subsequently identified and quantified using a microfluidic real-time PCR instrument (Signature Q100, LC-Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). The data were then quality controlled and normalized using internal extension and inter-plate controls to adjust for intra- and inter-run differences. Final assay reads are expressed as Normalized Protein Expression (NPX) values, which are arbitrary units in a logarithmic range. Higher values indicate higher protein expression levels. All assay validation data are available on the manufacturer’s website (www.olink.com). The Olink proteomics panel used in the present study is provided in Table S1.

For Olink data analysis, differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using a P-value threshold of 0.05. The expression of the two proteins was correlated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks of DEPs were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.1).

Lipidomics analysis

The collected samples were thawed on ice, and the metabolites were extracted using a lipid extraction buffer. Meanwhile, mixed quality control samples were prepared by adding 10 µL of each extract separately. Samples were collected by the LC-MS system following machine commands. All chromatographic separations were performed using an ACQUITY UPLC System (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), and a Kinetex UPLC C18 column (Phenomenex, UK) was used for the reversed-phase separation. Metabolites eluting from the column were detected using a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer, TripleTOF6600 (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). The XCMS [17] software was used to preprocess the acquired mass spectrometry data for peak picking, peak grouping, retention time correction, secondary peak grouping, isotope labeling, and adduct labeling. The differently expressed metabolites (DEMs) were filtered by foldchange (FC) > 1.20 or < 0.833 with p-value < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 and R software (version 4.3.0). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Immune protein alterations caused by mycobacterium infection

Olink proteomics analysis revealed that 25 DEPs were identified in the MTB group (16 upregulated and 9 downregulated), 29 DEPs in the RGM group (24 upregulated and 5 downregulated), and 54 DEPs in the SGM group (46 upregulated and 8 downregulated) compared to the normal group (Fig. 2a, Table S2). A Venn diagram showed that 16 proteins exhibited a consistent trend of differential expression in the MTB, RGM, and SGM groups compared to the control group. This suggests that these proteins represent alterations in the host immune landscape that are shared among all three Mycobacteria (Fig. 2e, f). These 16 proteins consist of CCL3, CD83, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL13, CXCL9, Gal-9, HGF, IFNG (IFN-gamma), IL12, MMP12, MUC-16, PD-L1, TNF, TRAIL, and VEGFR-2.

PPI network analysis revealed that these DEPs were enriched in Gene Ontology (GO) biological process terms such as positive regulation of CD4-positive, CD25-positive, alpha-beta regulatory T cell differentiation involved in immune response, T cell chemotaxis, and chronic inflammatory response (Fig. 2i, Table S3). Furthermore, these DEPs included four out of the five proteins in the GO molecular function term CXCR3 chemokine receptor binding, namely CXCL13, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL9 (Table S4).

C-X-C motif chemokine ligands (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13) belong to the CXC subfamily of chemokines. They are ligands that target CXCR3, inducing chemotaxis and promoting the differentiation of immune cells [18]. It has been demonstrated that CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, as well as CXCR3, are significantly elevated in patients with active tuberculosis compared to patients with other types of lung disease and healthy controls [19–22]. The immune response occurs through the CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11/CXCR3axis by recruiting immune cells such as cytotoxic lymphocytes, natural killer cells, NKT cells, and macrophages. The ligands are typically expressed at low levels under steady-state conditions but are upregulated in response to cytokine stimulation. CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 are primarily secreted by monocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and carcinoma cells in response to IFN-gamma [22]. Correlation analysis of our results showed that IFN-gamma was positively correlated with CXCL9 (r = 0.77, p-value < 0.001, Fig. 2g), CXCL10 (r = 0.74, p-value < 0.001), and CXCL11(r = 0.64, p-value < 0.001). Therefore, it can be assumed that IFN-gamma induces activation of the CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11/CXCR3 axis in all three infection groups.

The results of the ROC analysis showed that many immune-related proteins are promising diagnostic markers for Mycobacterium (Fig. 2h). IL8 was demonstrated to be an effective diagnostic marker for MTB infection, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9186. It is interesting to note that CXCL9 showed robust diagnostic potential for both RGM and SGM.

A Venn diagram showed that 7 proteins were co-upregulated in both the RGM and SGM groups compared to the MTB group. These proteins include adenosine deaminase (ADA), CCL3, CD40, CXCL5, Gal-1, IL8, and MCP-2 (Fig. 3d, g). PPI analysis showed that these 7 DEPs were involved in GO biological processes such as granulocyte activation, neutrophil chemotaxis, and eosinophil chemotaxis (Fig. 3e, Table S5).

Interestingly, interleukin-8 (IL-8), a member of the CXC chemokine family and a major mediator of the inflammatory response, was most significantly increased in both the RGM group (FC = 1.56, p-value = 0.01) and the SGM group (FC = 1.31, p-value = 0.03) compared to the MTB group. Therefore, it can be speculated that NTM, especially RGM, induced the host to produce more IL-8, enhanced the IL-8 pro-inflammatory signaling cascade, and augmented the host inflammatory response.

Compared with the SGM group, 7 downregulated DEPs were identified in the RGB group, including CCL17, CXCL12, IL7, LAP TGF-beta-1, CD27, PGF, and TWEAK. PPI analysis showed that these 7 DEPs were involved in GO biological processes such as induction of positive chemotaxis, positive regulation of B cell differentiation, and extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway (Fig. 3f, Table S6). Furthermore, CCL17 exhibits chemotactic activity for T lymphocytes, but not for monocytes or granulocytes [23]. Therefore, it can be assumed that the differences between RGM and SGM mainly focus on the chemotactic activity of T and B cells and extrinsic apoptosis.

In addition, the results of the ROC analysis revealed several promising Mycobacterium differential diagnostic markers (Fig. 3i). It was demonstrated that MCP-2 was a potent differential diagnostic marker for RGM and MTB with an AUC of 0.8051, whereas MCP-4 was a potent differential diagnostic marker for SGM and MTB. CCL17 was demonstrated to be a promising differential diagnostic marker for RGM and SGM with, an AUC of 0.7587.

According to the Venn diagram, 10 DEMs (PI 40:6, CE 22:6, SM 17:0, SMGDG O-11:0, PC 40:7, PC 17:0, LNAPE 18:1, LPC 40:8-SN2, PC O-39:7, and LPC O-24:1) were co-upregulated, and 6 DEMs (FAHFA 22:3, FAHFA 26:4, FAHFA 24:4, FAHFA 20:5, FAHFA 18:2, and CAR18:2) were co-downregulated in the MTB, RGM, and SGM groups compared with the control group (Fig. 4d).

Venn diagrams showed that 29 DEMs were co-upregulated in the RGM and SGM groups compared to the MTB group, revealing differences in the lipid profiles of individuals infected with NTM and MTB (Fig. 5d). The most abundant types of these 29 DEMs were phosphatidylcholines (PCs) and lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs) with 65.5% (19/29), followed by phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) and lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LPEs) with 20.7% (6/29, Fig. 5e). In addition, digalactosyl diacylglycerol (DGDG) O-19:0 was up-regulated the most significantly in both the RGM group (FC = 2.51, p-value = 0.020) and the SGM group (FC = 2.89, p-value = 0.02) compared to the MTB group. Remarkably, the correlation heatmap revealed a strong positive correlation between LPCs, LPEs, and DGDGs (Fig. 5f). Conclusively, the differences in serum lipid profiling between NTM patients and MTB patients mainly revolved around LPCs, LPEs, DGDGs, PCs, and PEs.

Compared with the SGM group, 8 upregulated and 45 downregulated DEMs were identified in the RGB group (Fig. 5c). LPC 28:7-SN2 was the most significant DEM with an FC = 0.55 and a p-value < 0.001.

ROC analysis revealed that Cer 20:0 was a reliable marker for distinguishing RGM from MTB, with an AUC of 0.8747. It was demonstrated that both LPE O-18:1 and LPE O-18:2 were promising differential diagnostic markers for distinguishing SGM from MTB, with AUC values of 0.7657 and 0.7467, respectively. Moreover, LPC 28:7-SN2 is a potential marker for the differential diagnosis of RGM and SGM with an AUC value of 0.7891.

Pathway analysis

Pathway analysis was conducted at both the protein and metabolite levels for the four comparison groups, with annotation provided by KEGG. The shared pathways that are regulated at the protein and metabolite levels among the four comparison groups are illustrated in Fig. 6b. After excluding the irrelevant KEGG pathways, only three shared pathways that were regulated at the protein and metabolite levels remained: tuberculosis, sphingolipid signaling pathway, and adipocytokine signaling pathway.

Subsequently, protein-lipid metabolite interaction maps (Fig. 6c) demonstrated the regulated DEPs and DEMs and shared pathways. Tuberculosis, sphingolipid signaling pathway, and adipocytokine signaling pathway were all enriched in the three Mycobacterium-infected groups compared to the normal group. As illustrated in Fig. 6c, the composition of tuberculosis-related DEPs and DEMs was highly similar across the three comparison groups. All three comparison groups consist of TNF, IFN-gamma, and 1-Phosphatidyl-D-myo-inositol, suggesting that these DEPs and DEMs might be regulated in all patients infected with Mycobacterium. In addition, LAP TGF-beta-1 was regulated in both MTB and SGM patients, while CASP-8 was regulated only in SGM patients. The pathways we enriched, and the proteins and lipids in these pathways, are associated with disease pathogenesis and host-microbe-related effects.

Establishment of protein-lipid diagnostic panels

It has been demonstrated that many DEPs and DEMs serve as effective diagnostic and differential diagnostic markers for MTB, RGM, and SGM, as mentioned earlier. However, the differential diagnostic efficacy of DEPs and DEMs for different groups of Mycobacterium infections needs to be improved. Therefore, we furthermore conducted additional research to combine proteomic and lipidomic data to create predictive panels with increased specificity and sensitivity. The DEPs and DEMs with the highest AUC values were used to develop predictive models.

As shown in Fig. 7, the MTB diagnostic model utilizing FAHFA 18:2 and IL8 demonstrated an AUC value of 0.9754, with a specificity and sensitivity of 96% and 92.3%, respectively. This performance was significantly superior to that of a single marker. Meanwhile, diagnostic panels for RGM and SGM constructed using protein and lipid markers had AUC values of 0.9867 and 0.9562, respectively (Fig. 7b, c).

Establishment of protein-lipid diagnostic panels. a Establishment of the diagnostic panel for MTB. The upper panel is the ROC curve for single diagnostic markers and the lower panel is the ROC curve for a protein-lipid diagnostic panel. b Establishment of the diagnostic panel for RGM. c Establishment of the diagnostic panel for SGM. d Establishment of MTB-RGM differential diagnostic model. e Establishment of MTB-SGM differential diagnostic model. f Establishment of RGM-SGM differential diagnostic model

Differential diagnostic panels constructed from proteins and lipids significantly enhance diagnostic efficacy among patients with different Mycobacteria. The MTB-RGM differential diagnostic model using Cer 20:0 and MCP-2 showed an AUC value of 0.92, with a specificity of 96% and a sensitivity of 86.7%. The MTB-SGM differential diagnostic model using LPE O-18:1 and DCN showed an AUC value of 0.80, with a specificity of 72% and a sensitivity of 85.7%. Moreover, the RGM-SGM differential diagnostic model using CCL17 and LPC 28:7-SN2 showed an AUC value of 0.86, with a specificity of 92.9% and a sensitivity of 81%. Overall, the panels constructed from proteins and lipids were satisfactory for the diagnostic and differential diagnostic efficacy of Mycobacterium infections, except for the relatively low specificity (< 80%) of the MTB-SGM differential diagnostic model.

Discussion

In this study, a “protein-metabolite co-analysis” strategy was applied to analyze serum alterations in patients with NTM (subdivided into RGM and SGM) and MTB. This strategy not only helped us to analyze the alterations in serum immune proteins and lipid metabolites in patients but also provided clues to potential associations between proteins and metabolites. Using this strategy, we revealed host alterations after NTM and MTB infection at the protein and metabolite level, revealed differences in host responses to different species of pathogenic microbes, and constructed promising protein-metabolite diagnostic panels.

Chemokines play an important role in Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. Several reports have shown that serum CXCR3 and its ligands CXCL9 and CXCL11 can be used as biomarkers for TB diagnosis or surveillance [20, 21], which is consistent with our findings. In addition, our findings revealed that both NTM and MTB infections resulted in elevated CXCL9 and CXCL11 in patients’ serum, and these chemokines are also potential diagnostic markers for NTM infection. In addition, it has also been reported in the literature that in chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCR3 are widely used as biomarkers in many areas such as inflammation [25–27], tumor [28, 29], and autoimmune diseases [30, 31]. It is suggested that the elevation of these chemokines in NTM and MTB-infected patients is not disease-specific. Therefore, inflammatory, neoplastic, and autoimmune diseases should be excluded before using CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13 as diagnostic markers for NTM and MTB, which undoubtedly dramatically reduces the value of the clinical application of these chemokines.

Fatty Acyl esters of Hydroxy Fatty Acids (FAHFAs) are a class of bioactive lipids and branched-chain FAHFAs, involved in the regulation of glucose metabolism and inflammation, with acylation of an internal branched-chain hydroxy-palmitic or -stearic acid [32, 33]. It is reported that some FAHFAs may improve insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance in mice by enhancing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, and insulin-stimulated glucose transport, as well as insulin inhibition of hepatic gluconeogenesis and reduction of adipose tissue inflammation [34]. In addition, some FAHFAs enhance GSIS, activate G protein-coupled receptors40, and attenuate lipopolysaccharides-induced expression, secretion, and phagocytosis of chemokines and cytokines in immune cells. However, little is known about the biological effects of other FAHFAs, especially in Mycobacterium infections [34], which have not been reported in the literature. Branched-chain FAHFAs are formed by the acylation of medium-chain (16 to 18 carbons) hydroxy fatty acids. It has been widely reported in the literature that Mycobacterium infections are closely associated with host lipids, especially FAs, metabolism [35]. The present study is the first time that differences in FAHFAs were observed in the serum from NTM and MTB patients. It also hinted at potential directions for research on Mycobacterium infections.

Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) is a bioactive lipid that exerts pleiotropic effects mediated by its receptors, G protein-coupled signaling receptors, Toll-like receptors, and ion channels to activate several second messengers [36]. LPC is increasingly considered a key factor positively in pathological states, especially inflammation, by altering various functions of multiple cell types, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, monocytes, macrophages, and T cells. In addition, LPC is involved in various cellular responses, such as activation of second messengers and bactericidal activity in neutrophils [37]. It has been reported that LPC can inhibit Salmonella by promoting phagosome maturation through ROS-induced activation of the NF-κB pathway [38]. The researcher also demonstrated that LPC could control the intracellular growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis by promoting phagosome maturation [37]. In the present study, LPCs and LPEs were identified as potent differential diagnostic markers for NTM and MTB.

It is reported that sphingomyelin biosynthesis is essential for phagocytic signaling during MTB host cell entry [39]. Inhibition or knockdown of early sphingolipid biosynthesis enzymes greatly reduces MTB uptake by a wide range of phagocytic cell types without affecting other forms of endocytosis. Although the phagocytic receptor dectin-1 normally accumulates at sites of pathogen contact, sphingolipid biosynthesis mutant cells are unable to separate the regulatory phosphatase CD45 from the accumulating receptor. Blockade of sphingolipid production also impairs downstream activation of Rho GTPase, actin dynamics, and phosphatidylinositol turnover on nascent phagocytic cups. In conjunction with our studies, sphingolipid biosynthesis is a potential target for anti-MTB and anti-NTM therapies.

The diagnosis and differential diagnosis of Mycobacterium infections, especially the differential diagnosis, have consistently stumped researchers and clinicians. Serum biomarker technology based on proteomics or metabolomics detection is an effective method for the diagnosis of tuberculosis and several studies have been conducted to investigate it [10, 40–42]. For example, Lubbers reported [42] that complement component C1q could serve as a serum biomarker to detect active tuberculosis. Unlike other studies that used single proteomics or metabolomics, we innovatively co-analyzed proteomic and metabolomic data from patient sera, thus providing a more comprehensive landscape of alterations, which is one of the highlights of the present study. The multi-omics diagnostic panels we have built have a strong efficacy in the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of the three Mycobacterium infections and are promising for application in clinical practice. Our only pity is that the specificity of the differential diagnostic panel between MTB-SGM is not optimal. It might also be related to the high similarity of clinical symptoms and progression of the two diseases. Differential diagnosis of these two diseases is indeed difficult and further studies are warranted.

Another highlight was the study of immune-related proteins and lipid metabolites, which are critical in the pathogenesis and progression of Mycobacterium infections [43]. By reducing the scope, we increased the depth of our analysis and discovered clues that had not been found before. Moreover, the present study is our innovative simultaneous analysis of three Mycobacterium species, MTB, SGM, and RGM. Our study not only bridged the gap in NTM research but also revealed similarities and differences in serum proteomics and metabolomics of MTB and NTM patients, providing a better understanding of Mycobacterium infections.

Our study certainly has some shortcomings. Firstly, the markers we screened and the diagnostic panel we constructed required an independent cohort to validate their accuracy and sensitivity. Secondly, the pulmonary pathological signatures of the patients were not included in the present study, so it is difficult to link protein/lipid markers to host-pathogen interactions and disease pathology. Thirdly, more work, such as molecular biology experiments, is necessary to follow up on the clues we found to provide a stronger explanation of the host-pathogen interactions in patients with MTB and NTM infections.

Conclusion

In the present study, we revealed the differences and associations in immune-related proteins and lipids among MTB patients, RGM patients, SGM patients, and normal donors using Olink proteomics and lipidomics. Serum immune proteins and lipid metabolites in patients with each of the three Mycobacterium species infections are distinctive but interrelated. The present study also identified potential diagnostic and differential markers and constructed predictive panels.
