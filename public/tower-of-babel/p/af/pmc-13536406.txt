Integrated analysis of single-cell and bulk RNA-sequence data reveal plasma dendritic cell-related diagnostic model for Sjögren’s syndrome based on machine learning
Authors: Jinhao Zhang, Chunye Zhang, Huan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposePlasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) play a critical role in linking innate and adaptive immunity in the pathogenesis of Sjögren’s syndrome (SS). This study aimed to characterize pDC functional features in SS by integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data and to develop a pDC-related gene signature–based diagnostic model.Materials and methodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed using quality control, clustering, and single-cell weighted gene co-expression network analysis (scWGCNA) to identify pDC-associated gene modules. Bulk transcriptomic datasets were then used to screen diagnostic biomarkers through 204 combinations of 15 machine-learning algorithms, and a nomogram model was constructed. Cell–cell communication and drug–gene interaction analyses were subsequently performed.ResultsA gene module strongly correlated with pDCs was identified, from which ten core biomarkers were selected to establish the diagnostic model. The model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with AUCs of 0.999 in the training set and 0.866 in an independent validation set. Cell–cell communication analysis revealed active macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) signaling between pDCs and monocyte subsets. Drug network and molecular docking analyses suggested that methotrexate and hydroxychloroquine may interact with proteins encoded by several biomarker genes.ConclusionThis study developed a robust SS diagnostic model based on pDC-related gene signatures and revealed potential pDC–monocyte interactions. The identified biomarkers and candidate drugs may facilitate auxiliary diagnosis and targeted therapeutic development for SS.Keywords: Sjögren’s syndrome, Plasmacytoid dendritic cells, Diagnostic model, Machine learning, Single-cell sequencing

Introduction

Sjögren’s Syndrome (SS) is a typical autoimmune disease, clinically manifested as xerostomia and xerophthalmia, which may be accompanied by organ damage. Its typical pathological manifestation is the infiltration of autoreactive lymphocytes in target organs, leading to impaired organ function.1 SS commonly occurs in middle-aged women, and its morbidity is second only to rheumatoid arthritis.2 However, the pathogenesis of SS is still unclear. The abnormal activation of T cell was proved usually happened in early stage, while auto-reactive B cell plays a crucial role in the damage of target organ at late stage. Although many researchers have focused on the role of T and B lymphocyte dysfunction in disease pathogenesis. Recently, the functional role of innate immune cells gradually attracted our attentions. Among innate immune cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) have emerged as indispensable contributors to autoimmune pathogenesis due to their exceptional capacity to produce type I interferons (IFN-I).3 Sustained activation of pDCs and excessive IFN-I production was observed in SLE. pDCs are specialized sentinels of viral infection. They express high levels of Toll-like receptors 7 and 9 (TLR7/9) that recognize nucleic acids. While this function is critical for host defense, aberrant or sustained activation of pDCs can drive pathological immune responses. SS is increasingly recognized as an interferon-driven disease. Studies proved that IFN-ahpha was upregulated in SS patients and correlated with disease activity and systemic involvement.4 In SS, pDCs was observed decrease in peripheral circulation and accumulated in salivary glands.5 Meanwhile, pDCs mainly localized surrounding ductal epithelial cells and lymphocyte infiltration foci, suggesting its role in shaping local immune responses. IFN-I secreted by pDCs promotes epithelial cell activation, antigen presentation, and secretion of proinflammatory factors, including B cell-activating factor (BAFF). In turn, epithelial cell apoptosis and the release of self-nucleic acids can stimulate pDCs through TLR7/9 signaling. This pDC-epithelial cell loop could induce the autoimmune inflammation and promote the progress of disease.6

Although pDCs is the primary producer of IFN-I. However, its role in the pathogenesis of pSS remains inclusive. The functional state and molecular heterogeneity of pDCs in SS have not been fully characterized, particularly at single-cell resolution. Moreover, the interactions between pDCs and other myeloid populations, such as monocytes, and their contribution to immune communication networks remain unclear. More studies were needed to reveal pDC-associated molecular features. Here, we performed an integrated analysis of single-cell and bulk RNA-seq datasets to characterize molecular features associated with pDCs in SS. Aiming to explore the functional role of pDC in diseases and construct a diagnostic model related to pDC. We further examined the functional states of pDCs and their interactions with monocyte subpopulations using pathway enrichment and cell–cell communication analyses, and assessed potential therapeutic associations through drug–gene interaction analysis.

Materials and methods

scRNA-seq data download, processing, and analysis

In order to extract the sequencing information of monocytes in SS patients, The single-cell sequencing dataset GSE157278 containing the gene expression of various cell-types in Sjogren’s patients was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), and the EULAR SS disease activity index (ESSDAI) score of SS patients was calculated and all patients fulfilled 2016 ACR/Eular classification criteria for SS. The following steps were taken:1) R software package “Seurat” was used to convert 10 × scRNA-seq data into Seurat objects;7 2) quality control was performed by calculating the percentage of mitochondrial or ribosomal genes and excluding low-quality cells (setting nFeature_RNA >500, nFeature_RNA <2500 and percent. mt < 10 as cutoffs for excluding low-quality cells, empties or doublets); 3) the “FindVariableFeatures” function was used to screen out the top 2000 highly variable genes after QC, and Unique Molecular Indentifier (UMI) and gene correlation analysis was performed to infer the data quality; 4) principal component analysis (PCA) was performed based on the 2000 genes, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction and cluster identification.8 The R software package “SingleR” was applied to cluster annotation to identify different cell types.9 Then, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) was used to reduce the dimensionality of single-cell data by decomposing the cell-gene matrix into its low-dimensional representation.10 Finally, the “FindAllMarkers” function was used to identify feature genes of each subpopulation, minpct = 0.5 and logfc = 0.5 were set as the threshold to retain differentially expressed genes, and the genes of each cell subpopulation were subjected to gene ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis to compare their biological functions and explore their potential roles and effects in the pathogenesis of SS.

Single-cell weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Single cell RNA (scRNA) data was used to generate metacells (metacells) to prepare for subsequent analysis.11 The “ConstructNetwork” function in the R package scWGCNA was used to construct a gene co-expression network, which refers to a network structure formed based on the interaction between genes in a large-scale gene expression dataset. This network structure can help to analyze cell types, functions, and interactions in single-cell data.12 Then, the “GetHubGenes” function was used to obtain the core genes in the top 25 eigengene-based connectivity (KME value) rankings in each module, and the R package “enrichR” was used to enrich the core genes of each module in enriched GO terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, to compare the differences in biological functions between different modules. Finally, the “igraph” package was used to construct a target module gene network graph.

Cell communication

To identify signaling pathways between monocyte subpopulations and other cell population. The “CellChat” package was used to analyze cell–cell communication. The annotated cell gene expression profile data was analyzed using the “CellChat” software package, The “netVisual_aggregate” function was used to visualize the signaling pathway, with the ligand defined as the outgoing signal and the receptor defined as the incoming signal.

Patient gene expression profile data source and preprocessing

For diagnostic prediction models and genetic clinical typing, GSE84844, GSE40611 and GSE23117 containing transcriptome sequencing and complete clinical diagnosis information was download form GEO database, GSE84844 was composed of 30 SS patients and 30 healthy controls, GSE40611 included 24 SS patients, 16 non-sicca patients, and 25 control patients, and GSE23117 included 5 SS patients, 3 intermediate patients, or severe SS patients, 4 healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). All datasets were normalized, GSE40611 and -0pppGSE23117 was integrated as one gene expression profiles after removing batch differences. The normalization process was conducted using the “limma”package.13 The ComBat function from the “sva” package of the R software was used to eliminate batch differences.14 Differential expression analysis between control and disease groups was conducted using limma on the gene expression data from the training set and the test set, respectively. Genes with logFC >1 and FDR <0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The top 100 genes ranked by absolute logFC from each dataset were then selected, and overlapping genes between the two datasets were identified.

Functional enrichment analysis

The “ClusterProfiler” package15 was used to perform functional and pathway enrichment analyses of the marker genes in each subgroup, including GO analysis of biological processes (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular components (CC). KEGG revealed the enrichment of marker genes in signaling pathways. The P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method or False Discovery Rate. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) software package was used for gene set enrichment scoring in C5 (GO gene set). The t value represents the activity level of the gene set in the sample, and the pathways were screened based on the P-value and t value magnitude. AUCell is a method for evaluating cell-related signaling pathway activity in single-cell RNA-seq data.16

Signature generated from machine learning integrative approaches

We integrated 15 different machine learning algorithms, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), neural networks, logistic regression, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), decision trees, XGBoost, ridge regression, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Lasso), Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), Random Forest, Elastic Net, Stepwise Logistic Regression, and Naive Bayes. Based on these methods, a consensus model was generated.17 A total of 207 algorithm combinations were executed to match the prediction model based on the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework. Predictive models were developed on the GEO cohort and validated on an external independent dataset (GSE40611 and GSE23117 were integrated as one gene expression profile after removing batch differences), with accuracy, recall, and F-score calculated for model evaluation. The F-score is a third measure combining accuracy and recall, being the harmonic mean of accuracy and recall.18 By calculating the average importance score of each gene in all models, we obtained the ranking of gene importance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). P-values <0.05 were considered significant. ROC curves were calculated to assess the diagnostic model’s reliability, with AUC >0.7 and P-value <0.05 considered significant. Wilcoxon rank-sum and chi-square tests were used to compare median and proportion between groups. The predictive performance was evaluated using the ROC curve’s AUC. The DeLong test was used to compare different models’ ROC curves, while the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to assess the fit of the constructed chart.

Results

Identification of cell types in SS

The workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, the single-cell data were preprocessed. we excluded cells expressing less than 500 genes or more than 30 % of the total expressed genes, treating them as mitochondrial genes in our research. Furthermore, cells with fewer than 250 genes and fewer than 3 cells were also eliminated due to their inferior quality. After performing PCA dimensionality reduction, 15 principal components were extracted for subsequent analysis. The FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions were used with a resolution of 0.1 to cluster cells into 12 subgroups. The abundance of these subgroups in patient and normal tissues was calculated (Fig. 2A). The cell subgroups were annotated using SingleR and manual annotation, resulting in eight cell names: CD4+ effector memory cells, monocytes, pDC, CD4+ naïve T cells, NK cells, B cells, CD4+ central memory cells, macrophages, and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2B).

Myeloid in SS

This study aimed to identify the myeloid subpopulations most closely associated with the pathogenesis of SS and to elucidate their functional characteristics. Through differential expression analysis, seven key genes closely related to disease development were identified (SAMD9, IFIT1, IFI44L, CXCL19, CMPK2, CENPK, CD69) (Fig. 2C and D). The activity levels of pathways associated with these genes across the three myeloid subpopulations were evaluated using the AUcell scoring system. The results demonstrated that, compared with macrophages, monocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) exhibited significantly higher pathway activity scores (Fig. 2E). To further analyze cellular heterogeneity, dimensionality reduction and clustering based on non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) were applied to subdivide the monocyte and pDC subpopulations. The analysis revealed that monocytes could be further classified into four functional subclusters (CM0–CM4), while pDCs formed a distinct cell cluster (Fig. 2F).

Identification of pDC-related key modules

Based on above UCell scoring results, WGCNA was used to identify genes involved in function of pDCs and monocytes in SS. A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed with a soft threshold of 10 (Fig. 3A), and the gene set was divided into five gene modules (Fig. 3B). The correlation between modules and subgroups was demonstrated using a bubble plot, and the correlation between the green module genes and pDCs was the highest, suggesting that these are specific genes that are differentially expressed between pDCs and other monocyte subgroups (Fig. 3C). GO enrichment results suggested green module genes were associated with the positive regulation of actin nucleation, nuclear envelope lumen, and nucleoside kinase activity (Fig. 3D). The top module KME scoring genes of each module were visualized (Fig. 3E). Given the important role of pDCs in the pathogenesis of Sjögren’s syndrome, our subsequent research will focus on pDCs. The 66 green module genes’ relationship network was showed in network diagrama (Supplementary Table 2), with 25 genes ranked according to their importance (Fig. 3F). These genes were then used to build diagnostic models.

Integrative construction of a consensus signature, screening of pDCs-related diagnostic features and construction of nomogram model

Sixty-six biomarkers from the green module were fitted into 204 algorithm combinations. The accuracy, recall, F-score, and AUC values of each model were calculated in all training and validation datasets (Fig. 4A) (Supplementary Figure 1). Based on the gene importance ranking (Fig. 4B), we selected 10 characteristic genes with the highest diagnostic value (MYL12A, TMEM109, HSP90B1, APP, TLR7, VIMP, SEC61B, PPM1K, CD2AP, and PIM2). Using these 10 characteristic genes, we constructed a diagnostic nomogram (Fig. 4C and D). The DCA curve showed the clinical utility of the risk model (Fig. 4F). In addition, the calibration curve demonstrated the good calibration and consistency between the predicted results of the well-calibrated nomogram (Fig. 4E). The constructed predictive model showed that the nomogram had a good predictive ability in both the training and validation sets (AUC = 0.999, AUC = 0.866) (Fig. 4G and H). The independent ROC curves of the 10 genes in the training set had AUC values greater than 0.700 (Fig. 4I–R). The results indicated that our diagnostic model was effective in distinguishing between SS and healthy individuals.

Function analysis: enrichment of cell signaling pathways and cell–cell communication

The results of single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) demonstrated a significant overexpression of the MYC, E2F, and G2M pathways in pDCs. This suggests that pDCs in pSS are not only immunologically active but also in a state of enhanced cellular proliferation and metabolic activity, which may underpin their sustained pathogenic function. CM0 and CM4 showed strong enrichment in HALLMARK_TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB, HALLMARK_INFLAMMATORY_RESPONSE. This positions them as the primary inflammatory executors within the monocyte compartment. CM1 were significantly enriched in Interferon Alpha Response, Interferon Gamma Response and oxidative phosphorylation. This co-enrichment suggests that CM1 cells are potent responders to interferon signals while maintaining a high metabolic capacity, potentially supporting their effector functions. CM2 and CM3 subsets exhibited intermediate or distinct functional profiles, potentially representing transitional or other specialized states (Fig. 5A). Functional and pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the marker genes in each subcluster. These results suggest that the pDCs may be related to genetic material splicing and processing, and we speculate that it may regulate other immune cells differentiation and affect protein translation and synthesis (Fig. 5B).

To investigate how these functionally distinct cells synergistically drive disease, we performed systematic cell–cell communication analysis. The results revealed that pDCs received signaling pathways from CM0, CM1, CM2, CM3, and CM4 cells. Notably, the MIF signaling pathway exhibited the highest level of communication probability (Fig. 5C). The cell interaction pattern map depicted that pDCs predominantly belonged to PATTERN3, which was primarily associated with the IL16 pathway (Fig. 5D). The MIF signaling network heatmap provided insights into the functional relevance and significance of each cell group in the MIT signaling pathway, highlighting the essential role of pDCs in signal transmission, reception, and mediation (Fig. 5G). CM1 cells served as the primary signal providers, while both CM1 and pDCs cells acted as the main signal receptors. The potential signaling pathways between these cell types encompassed MIF, GALECTIN, ANNEXIN, TNF, RESISTIN, IL16, CCL, and FLT3 (Fig. 5F). The left plot of the signaling role scatter plot indicated that CM1 cells predominantly acted as the primary signal providers and receptors across most pathways, whereas the right plot showcased pDCs cells as the primary signal providers and receptors specifically in the MIF pathway (Fig. 5E).

Drug-target interaction network

The disease-drug-gene interaction network for above model genes was constructed. Two trugs, Methotrexate and Hydroxychloroquine, were identified to have potential binding site with model gene proteins (VIMP, PPM1K, PIM2 respectively). As shown in Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 3, two chemical drugs associated with model genes in Sjogren’s syndrome were identified (Methotrexate, Hydroxychloroquine). Then, we predicted the molecular binding sites of these two drugs with the model gene proteins that have been studied (VIMP, PPM1K, PIM2), where the smallest S value was observed for Methotrexate binding to all three proteins (Supplementary Figure 2), indicating its potential targeted regulatory ability. The molecular docking binding patterns are shown in Fig. 6B–D. Among them, the strongest binding performance was observed for PIM2 with Methotrexate, with an S value of −7.5145.

Discussion

Our study integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses to reveal the functional role of myeloid cells, especially pDC, in the pathogenesis of SS. Based on scRNA-seq and machine learning, we identified pDC-related gene with strong diagnostic potential and revealed intercellular communication patterns between pDCs and monocyte subsets. Functional enrichment and ssGSEA revealed that MYC, E2F, and G2M checkpoint pathway enriched in pDCs in SS. The results indicated that pDCs exhibit enhanced proliferative and metabolic activity. However, previous study found that the levels of IFN-I, IFN-II secreted by SS pDCs after stimulation showed significantly higher than healthy control.19 These findings indicated that rather than uncontrolled proliferation, pDCs in SS are more likely at a transcriptionally active and metabolically primed state. Such a state might enable pDCs sending out sustained immune signaling. This functional state is consistent with the chronic activation of pDCs observed in target tissues of SS patients and supports their role in maintaining IFN-driven immune responses. Our study highlighted extensive interactions between pDCs and monocyte subpopulations. The macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) pathway was identified as the dominant signaling axis between pDCs and monocyte subclusters, with pDCs acting as both signal receivers and mediators. Although studies proved that the activation of MIF pathway can induce pro-inflammatory cytokine production in both monocytes and dendritic cells.20,21 However, more studies are needed to prove the paracrine MIF signaling between pDCs and monocytes.

Based on the integration of scWGCNA with large-scale machine learning, we can identify clinically relevant pDC-associated biomarkers. Among the ten genes derived from a pDC-correlated module, TLR7 and APP have already been proved to participate in innate immune sensing and inflammatory signaling, which reinforce the biological relevance of the diagnostic model. The high accuracy of the nomogram suggests that pDC-related molecular features capture disease-specific signals that may complement current diagnostic approaches. The results of drug–gene interaction and molecular docking analyses indicated the potential interactions between key biomarkers and commonly used immunosuppressants in clinical practice, such as methotrexate and hydroxychloroquine. Methotrexate showing high binding affinity to core pDC biomarkers such as PIM2. Although, the efficacy of methotrexate in treating SS glandular symptoms remain controversial. However, it is usually used to manage systemic manifestations, particularly articular involvement.22 In terms of mechanism, our results suggest that methotrexate might exert its anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the high metabolic and transcriptional activity of pDCs. PIM2, a serine/threonine kinase involved in cell survival and protein synthesis, was found to be highly active in antibody-secreting cells.23,24 PIM2 was identified as one of biomarkers which demonstrated excellent diagnostic ability for RA through a nomogram.24 Although, the functional role of PIM2 in pDCs is still unknown. In SS, where pDCs are hyper-activated to secrete excessive Type I interferons (IFN-I), PIM2 likely acts as a critical regulator to sustain this high secretory burden and prevent activation-induced cell death. Further studies are needed to prove the hyperthesis. The strong binding between methotrexate and PIM2 (S value = − 7.5145) indicates that methotrexate may suppress secretion of type I interferons (IFN-I) and other pro-inflammatory cytokines in SS through regulating PIM2 expression. This provides a novel hypothesis for how methotrexate could influence pDC-mediated innate immune activation beyond its classical role in folate metabolism. However, these results are hypothesis-generating and further experimental validation are needed. Take together, the results indicate that pDC-associated molecular networks may intersect with established therapeutic mechanisms in SS.

Considering the high prevalence of overlap syndromes in clinical practice, it is crucial to discuss whether our pDC-associated biomarkers are specific to SS or extensible to other autoimmune conditions. pDCs and the subsequent IFN-I signature play a crucial role in pathogenesis of multiple systemic autoimmune diseases, especially SLE.25 The credible of our diagnostic model (AUC = 0.866 in the validation set) suggests that it successfully acquire the characteristic molecular derangements of pDCs in SS. Considering that IFN-I pathways are a shared hallmark of autoimmune diseases, it is plausible that the pDC-associated biomarkers, especially TLR7 and interferon-response genes, may also be upregulated in SS patients complicated with SLE or RA.26 Our findings regarding the pDC-monocyte axis, specifically the MIF signaling pathway, likely define a specialized inflammatory circuit that becomes further exacerbated in the context of overlapping autoimmune features. Consequently, further studies involving multi-disease cohorts are essential to parse the functional nuances of pDCs in primary SS versus secondary SS.

The limitation of this study is that all results were relied on publicly available datasets and the absence of experimental validation of inferred pathways. Nonetheless, our findings emphasize the central role of pDCs in SS and demonstrate the value of integrating single-cell transcriptomics with machine learning to identify diagnostically and biologically meaningful signatures that may inform precision diagnosis and targeted intervention.
