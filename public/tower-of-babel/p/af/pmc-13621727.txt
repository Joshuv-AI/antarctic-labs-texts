Regulatory mechanisms driven by functional 3’-UTR variants in alcohol use disorder and related traits
Authors: Andy B. Chen, Xuhong Yu, Jennifer M. Rupp, Xiaona Chu, Kriti S. Thapa, Hongyu Gao, Jill L. Reiter, Xiaoling Xuei, Andy P. Tsai, Gary E. Landreth, Yue Wang, Tatiana M. Foroud, Jay A. Tischfield, Dongbing Lai, Pengyue Zhang, Howard J. Edenberg, Yunlong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenetic variants in the 3’ untranslated regions (3’-UTRs) of mRNAs can alter binding of RNA-binding proteins and microRNAs and thereby influence regulation by affecting RNA stability, localization, and translation. Despite their potential impact on the risk for complex traits, including alcohol use disorder, the contribution of 3’-UTR variants has not been systematically explored. We evaluate the impact of 3’-UTR variants within loci associated with substance use and neurological disorders using a massively parallel reporter assay (MPRA) in neuroblastoma and microglia cells.ResultsOf the 13,515 variants tested, 400 and 657 variants significantly alter gene expression in neuroblastoma and microglia cells, respectively. These functionally impactful variants account for more heritability of alcohol-related traits than non-functional variants. We develop a computational framework, MPRA-mediated Gene Expression Association (MGExA), that combines MPRA-derived variant effects with GWAS summary statistics and identify 31 genes whose expression changes may contribute to alcohol-related traits. CRISPR inhibition of 7 of these genes in neuronal cells leads to gene expression changes associated with neurodegenerative disorders and the oxidative phosphorylation pathway. Pharmacoepidemiological analysis of drugs that had similar effects on gene expression linked RBM14 and KANSL1 to risk for alcohol use disorder.ConclusionsWe identify genetic variants in 3’-UTR regions that affect gene expression. By integrating these functional genomics data and pharmacoepidemiological assessment with GWAS analysis, we identify genes whose expression differences could contribute to alcohol related traits. This approach provides a framework for moving from GWAS data to identifying biologically and clinically relevant genes associated with complex disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04176-x.

Background

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified loci associated with alcohol use disorder [1–4] (AUD), alcohol consumption [1, 2, 5–9], and related traits [10–18]. Though these strategies have expanded our understanding of the genetics of alcohol-related traits, they are limited in what they reveal about the underlying mechanisms. The significant variants are usually in noncoding regions and may either affect gene expression or merely mark a broad locus that contains variants that affect the trait. Identifying genes within these loci that contribute to complex traits is essential for informing downstream analyses and uncovering disease mechanisms.

The 3’ untranslated regions (3′-UTRs) of mRNAs are rich in regulatory elements, including binding sites for RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) that modulate mRNA stability, localization, and translational efficiency [19]. Disruption of these elements by genetic variants can alter gene expression patterns and contribute to disease susceptibility. Despite their regulatory importance, the roles of 3′-UTR variants in complex diseases remain underexplored. In this study, we used a massively parallel reporter assay [20, 21] (MPRA), PASSPORT-seq [22–24], to test the potential function of variants in the 3’-UTR of genes within loci associated with alcohol consumption, alcohol use disorder, and neurological disorders.

Several methods, including PrediXcan [25], TWAS [26], and SMR [27], employ a Mendelian Randomization (MR)-like framework to prioritize genes that potentially contribute to a phenotype. They impute gene expression from genotype using models trained on tissue-level transcriptome datasets and then test the association between imputed expression levels and the phenotype. These imputations, however, rely on statistical associations between variants and gene expression, rather than direct evidence of variant functionality. MPRAs offer a complementary approach by experimentally evaluating the regulatory activity of individual variants. We developed MPRA-mediated Gene Expression Association Analysis (MGExA), a novel method that integrates MPRA-derived functional data with GWAS summary statistics to identify genes whose imputed expression is associated with the phenotype. Unlike traditional approaches that rely solely on statistical associations, MGExA incorporates direct evidence of variant regulatory activity, providing a more biologically grounded framework for gene prioritization.

To validate the functional relevance of the identified genes, we employed CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) [28] to assess their downstream effects. Additionally, we leveraged Connectivity Map (CMap) and insurance claims data to explore potential biological pathways and mechanisms of action. Our results suggest that MGExA not only enhances statistical power for discovering genes that contribute to risk for AUD and related traits, it also facilitates a genetically informed investigation into the mechanisms underlying AUD risk.

Results

MPRA identifies functional variants impacting gene expression

To identify genes within GWAS loci that are likely to functionally impact AUD and related traits, we tested 13,515 candidate SNPs selected from 3’-UTRs of genes within loci associated at suggestive levels (p < 10–5) with alcohol consumption, AUD, and neurological disorders (Additional file 1: Fig. S1, Tables S1 and S2) to determine whether they affected gene expression using PASSPORT-seq [22–24], a massively parallel reporter assay (MPRA) specifically designed for 3’-UTR variants (Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and Methods). The overall flow of the protocol is shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. For each oligo, we compared the normalized number of DNA and RNA (cDNA) reads using a generalized linear model (see Methods) to determine if the oligo altered gene expression. Of the 24,780 oligo sequences tested, 2,908 altered gene expression (FDR < 0.05) in SH-SY5Y cells, a neuroblastoma cell line; of those, 876 sequences decreased expression by more than twofold, while only 81 increased it by that extent (Fig. 1A, Additional file 2: Table S3). In SV40-immortalized human microglia cells, 5,460 sequences altered gene expression (FDR < 0.05), with 1,220 decreasing and 71 increasing expression by more than twofold (Additional file 2: Table S3). The imbalance between decreasing and increasing expression is consistent with the expectation that 3’-UTR sequences are enriched with cis-acting elements that regulate RNA degradation [19, 29]. We used a generalized linear mixed effect model to determine whether there was a difference between reference and alternative alleles in gene expression. Of the 13,515 SNPs tested, 972 (7.2%) showed significant differences between alleles (FDR < 0.05): 400 in neuroblastoma cells and 657 in microglia cells (Fig. 1B & C, Additional file 2: Table S3). We next evaluated potential trans-acting mechanisms by examining whether 3′-UTR variants alter RNA-binding protein (RBP) interactions. To this end, we used RBP binding affinity predictions from regSNPs-splicing [30] and regSNPs-intron [31], which estimate allele-specific changes in RBP binding based on position weight matrix (PWM) models. These approaches compute the relative binding probability of RBPs for reference and alternative alleles using sequence motif preferences, enabling systematic assessment of variant-induced binding changes across 201 RBPs curated from RBPDB [32] and cisBP-RNA [33].

To identify RBPs with potential functional relevance, we tested whether variants predicted to alter RBP binding affinity were enriched among variants showing measurable MPRA activity. Using this enrichment-based criterion, we identified 14 RBPs in microglia and 2 RBPs in neuroblastoma cells that showed nominal associations (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A, Additional file 2: Table S4). For example, among SNPs predicted to affect binding of PPRC1, a higher proportion of variants exhibited moderate MPRA activity changes (p < 0.05) in microglia compared to SNPs without predicted binding changes (32% vs. 16%). We further examined whether predicted changes in RBP binding showed consistent directionality with MPRA activity across multiple variants (Additional file 2: Table S4). Notably, variants predicted to increase binding of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (HNRNPK) were associated with increased reporter activity in both neuroblastoma and microglia assays (Fig. 2B). HNRNPK is a multifunctional RBP involved in mRNA stability and translational regulation [34], and has been implicated in neuronal processes including synaptic transmission [35]. Notably, HNRNPK can enhance gene expression through binding the 3’-UTR of mRNA, primarily by stabilizing transcripts and antagonizing inhibitory factors such as microRNAs [36, 37]. This concordant directionality suggests that, for a subset of variants, altered RBP binding may contribute to regulatory effects observed in the MPRA.

Functionally impactful variants explained more heritability than expected

We analyzed the subset of SNPs that were included in both MPRA and GWAS analyses to test whether the 3’-UTR SNPs that differentially affected gene expression explained a higher fraction of heritability for the number of alcoholic drinks per week [6] (DPW) or AUD [2] than the median of 1,000 randomly selected SNP sets chosen from among all the tested SNPs (Additional file 2: Table S5 and Fig. 2). For DPW [6], the 223 GWAS SNPs that significantly affected gene expression in our MPRA data in neuroblastoma cells explained 1.4% of the overall heritability, an enrichment of 2.1-fold (p = 0.034). The 387 SNPs that affected gene expression in microglia cells explained 2.3% of the heritability, an enrichment of 2.3-fold (p = 0.003). Similarly, for AUD, the 158 and 285 functional SNPs identified in neuroblastoma and microglia cells that were included in the GWAS analyses explained 1.5% and 1.3% of the heritability, respectively, which is 8.4 times (neuroblastoma; p = 0.001) and 5.0 times (microglia; p = 0.004) the median heritability of randomly selected sets of all the SNPs tested in the MPRA experiment (Additional file 2: Table S5).

Integrating GWAS and MPRA reveals genes whose 3’-UTR activity is associated with alcohol consumption and AUD

To examine how the genetic variants in the non-coding regulatory regions of a gene might contribute to a trait of interest, we designed a computational framework that we call MPRA-mediated Gene Expression Association Analysis (MGExA). MGExA multiplies the functional effect of each SNP (determined by the MPRA) by its effect size from a GWAS of a trait and sums the results for each gene to calculate a Z-score (Wald statistic) for the association between the trait and the gene expression level determined by the aggregated effects of tested SNPs (Methods, Eq. 3, and Additional file 1: Fig. S4A) [2, 6]. We applied MGExA to 565 and 664 genes that contained at least one MPRA-evaluated 3’-UTR variant with p < 0.05 in neuroblastoma and microglia cells, respectively. Among these, 3’-UTR activities of 38 genes in neuroblastoma and 50 genes in microglia were significantly associated with DPW [6] (FDR < 0.2); 12 were common to both cell lines (Table 1, Additional file 2: Table S6). Of these, 17/31 genes in neuroblastoma cells (7 could not be tested because of differences in the variants available between datasets) and 14/45 in microglia (5 not tested) were also associated (FDR < 0.2) with AUDIT-C (which measures a related aspect of drinking behavior) in the Million Veteran Program (MVP) study [2]; four of these were associated in both cell types (Fig. 3A & B). The direction of the effect in 16 out of the 17 genes identified based on MGExA in the neuroblastoma cells and in all 14 genes from microglia was consistent across both studies (Table 1; Fig. 3A & B). CRISPR inhibition of target genes identifies potential mechanisms

To identify the downstream effects of the genes identified by MGExA, we used a KRAB-dCas9-based CRISPRi assay followed by single-cell RNA-seq (Perturb-seq [28]) to measure global gene expression changes after knocking down the target genes in neuroblastoma cells. We used lentivirus to transduce 5 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting each of the candidate genes along with 18 negative template control (NTC) sgRNAs (Additional file 2: Table S7) into neuroblastoma cells (see Methods, Additional file 1: Fig. S5). 8,842 cells were transduced with a single target gene sgRNA, and 1,331 cells were transduced with a negative template control (NTC). We used Seurat’s Mixscape function [38] to analyze the 23 genes with reads detected in at least 50% of the NTC cells. Seven had robust perturbation responses: CUL3, INO80E, KANSL1, PLEKHM1, RBM14, SNRNP40, and TAOK2 (Fig. 4A & B). Knockdown of four target genes significantly affected at least 10 downstream genes (adjusted p < 0.05; Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test): CUL3 (17 differentially expressed genes), KANSL1 (199 genes), RBM14 (505 genes), and SNRNP40 (49 genes) (Additional file 2: Table S8). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the downstream targets of the perturbed genes identified thirty-one significantly enriched (FDR < 0.05) pathways from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [39] (Additional file 2: Table S9); 16 pathways were enriched by at least two perturbed genes (Fig. 4C). Neurodegenerative disease pathways, including Alzheimer’s, Huntington’s, and Parkinson’s disease, were enriched following CUL3 and KANSL1 inhibition, while Huntington’s and Parkinson’s disease pathways were also enriched upon RBM14 inhibition. Additionally, oxidative phosphorylation was enriched by CUL3, RBM14, and KANSL1 inhibition, suggesting that oxidative stress may be involved in response to alcohol consumption and AUD [40, 41]. The ribosome gene set was also enriched following inhibition of KANSL1, PLEKHM1, TAOK2, RBM14, and SNRNP40. GSEA was also performed using MSigDB hallmark [42] and Reactome [43] gene sets (Additional file 1: Fig. S6 & S7, Additional file 2: Table S9). Pathways related to cell cycle and protein synthesis were commonly enriched among all three databases. Taken together with the enrichment of ribosome genes, these finding point to potential alterations in cell growth and stress response. Validating the identified AUD risk genes using drug perturbation gene signatures in real-world pharmacoepidemiologic data

To explore the translational impacts of the perturbed genes in AUD, we identified candidate drugs based on the similarity between the gene expression changes induced by drug treatment in neuronal cell lines (neural progenitor cells (NPC), SH-SY5Y, NEU, and U251MG) from the Expanded Connectivity Map (CMap) LINCS Resource 2020 dataset [44] (Additional file 2: Table S10, Additional file 1: Fig. S8) and the knockdown expression profiles of the candidate genes (see Methods). Since CMap queries require defined sets of up- and down-regulated genes, we focused our analyses on three genes (RBM14, SNRNP40, and KANSL1) whose inhibition resulted in at least 10 up- and 10 down-regulated differentially expressed genes. Nineteen candidate drugs (Additional file 2: Table S10) produced gene expression perturbations in CMap that were similar to those from the knockdown of at least one gene (FDR < 0.05). We then determined whether patients taking these drugs have an altered risk of developing AUD. For each candidate drug, we identified two cohorts: the first cohort consisted of people who used the candidate drug and did not have AUD before taking the drug; the second cohort consisted of people who took a comparator drug, defined as a compound in the same Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification [45] subgroup or prescribed for the same disease(s) (see Methods and Additional file 1: Fig. S9). The comparator drugs (Additional file 2: Table S11) were selected to avoid confounding by indication [46], ensuring that differences in AUD risk could be attributed to the specific drug of interest rather than the underlying condition being treated. For example, fluoxetine is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (ATC code N06AB); other drugs in this class are zimeldine, citalopram, paroxetine, sertraline, alaproclate, fluvoxamine, etoperidone, and escitalopram, so the comparator cohort for fluoxetine was comprised of individuals who had been prescribed any of these drugs.

For each candidate drug and comparator drug pair, we calculated two measures: the perturbation concordance score (PCS), which quantifies the alignment between drug-induced expression differences and gene knockdown effects, and the hazard ratio, HRAUD, for developing AUD in individuals taking the candidate drug versus the comparator drug (see Methods, Additional file 2: Table S12). A linear regression analysis revealed a significant positive association between PCS and HRAUD for KANSL1 perturbation (p = 0.006), indicating that KANSL1 may be an AUD protective gene. This is consistent with the MGExA results, which show that higher KANSL levels are associated with lower alcohol consumption (KANSL1 is in the 3rd quadrant in Fig. 3A). In contrast, RBM14 and SNRNP40 perturbations showed negative associations between PCS and HRAUD. Although these associations did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.11 and p = 0.25, respectively; Fig. 4D), the negative trend suggests that higher expression of these genes may increase drinking risk, consistent with the MGExA results (Fig. 3B and D).

Discussion

In this study, we utilized massively parallel reporter assays in neuroblastoma and microglial cells to evaluate the functional effects of variants in 3’-UTRs of genes associated with alcohol consumption, AUD, and other neurological disorders. We found 972 variants in which the alternative allele altered gene expression in at least one cell line; among these, 81 altered expression in the same direction in both (Additional file 2: Table S3). We identified RBP motifs that help explain the variant effects observed by MPRA (Additional file 2: Table S4). We used a permutation analysis of heritability to demonstrate that these variants explained a disproportionate fraction of the heritability of alcohol consumption and alcohol use disorder, suggesting that these traits are controlled in part by the expression levels of the genes these variants regulate. Given that estimates of SNP heritability in the GWAS we used were around 5%−10% [2, 6], the 1%−2% covered by MPRA-significant variants located in the 3’UTR are a meaningful signal.

To understand how these variants are implicated in AUD, we used an approach inspired by Mendelian Randomization and PrediXcan [25], which we called MGExA, to identify candidate genes by combining variant associations with a trait (derived from GWAS) with variant effects on gene expression (derived from MPRA). Using MGExA, we identified 31 genes whose 3’-UTRs were associated with alcohol consumption or alcohol use disorder (Table 1). An advantage of this approach is that MPRA reveals causal variant effects on gene expression. MPRA data are cell-type specific, while most eQTL data are at the tissue level. However, because MPRAs are performed in cell lines, their regulatory environment may not recapitulate the environment found in vivo.

Interestingly, only 5 among the 31 genes (16%) whose 3’-UTR activities contribute to either alcohol consumption or alcohol use disorder contain 3’-UTR SNPs that were genome-wide significant (p < 5 × 10–8) in either GWAS [2, 6]. The increased power of our MGExA analysis is in part the result of combining the effects of multiple functional variants affecting the same gene with GWAS summary statistics. For example, the weighted contributions of each SNP to the trait and the effect size in the MPRA and GWAS analysis for Diazepam Binding Inhibitor-Like 5 Pseudogene 2 (DBIL5P2), TAO Kinase 2 (TAOK2), INO80 Complex Subunit E (INO80E), and Dopamine Receptor D2 (DRD2) are depicted in Additional file 1: Fig. S4B&C. Each gene’s 3’-UTR contains multiple SNPs evaluated in both the MPRA and GWAS for drinks per week. The combined effects suggest that their gene expression levels positively (for DBIL5P2) or negatively (for TAOK2, INO80E, and DRD2) associate with the phenotype. None of these SNPs nor any SNPs in LD with them (r2 > 0.8) had GWAS signals that reach genome-wide significance.

For gene identification by MGExA, we used a permissive FDR cutoff of 0.2 because our goal was to identify candidate genes for downstream analysis, not make any statistical claims, and downstream analyses are more powerful with a larger input of candidate genes. Of the genes identified by MGExA as associated with alcohol consumption or alcohol use disorder, 29 are expressed in microglia or neurons in the single cell transcriptome data from the Human Brain Cell Atlas [47]. Several, including Alcohol Dehydrogenase 4 (ADH4) [48, 49], AGBL Carboxypeptidase 2 (AGBL2) [50], and Microtubule Associated Protein Tau (MAPT) [51], have previously been shown to be associated with alcohol-related phenotypes. Other MGExA-identified genes, such as Signal Transducer And Activator Of Transcription 6 (STAT6) [52] and TAOK2 [53] have been found to be involved in the response to ethanol in animal models. MGExA also identified genes associated with addiction to other substances, including nicotine (KAT8 Regulatory NSL Complex Subunit 1; KANSL1) [54]) and opioids (DRD2 [55]). MGExA genes were also associated with other disorders of the brain such as Alzheimer’s disease (Protein Tyrosine Kinase 2 Beta; PTK2B [56]), and schizophrenia (SREBF2 [57]).

To understand the mechanistic role of the MGExA-identified genes in alcohol use, we used CRISPR to inhibit expression of the genes in neuroblastoma cells and identified pathways that were enriched by each gene perturbation (Figs. 4C, S6, S7, Additional file 2: Table S9). Several sets of genes were differentially expressed, including sets enriched in Alzheimer’s, Huntington’s, and Parkinson’s diseases pathways, confirming that these genes may be involved in brain function. Oxidative phosphorylation was also enriched by several genes. Alcohol metabolism is associated with an increase in oxidative stress by causing production of reactive oxygen [40, 41]. Prolonged oxidative stress may be in part responsible for the neurodegenerative symptoms of excessive alcohol consumption and AUD. Oxidative stress is also associated with neuroinflammation [58], and several immune and inflammation related pathways, including chemokine and toll-like receptor signaling, were enriched. Growth and proliferation related pathways like ribosome, cell cycle, and protein metabolism were also enriched by several genes. These may indicate cell growth changes or the stress response.

To validate the association between candidate genes and AUD, we used healthcare outcomes from insurance claims data to see if proxies derived from CRISPR inhibition effects would be associated with AUD risk. As proxies, we identified drugs that had effects on gene expression similar to those we found using CRISPR inhibition and evaluated their effects on risk for AUD. Many of these drugs are prescribed for brain-related disorders, including valproic acid for epilepsy, and duloxetine and fluoxetine for depression. Proxies for KANSL1 inhibition were associated with AUD diagnosis risk, suggesting that it may act as a protective factor. We believe this approach provides a more direct link between the perturbed gene and AUD and may enable us to better evaluate the mechanisms contributing to AUD risk. Our goal differs from drug repurposing approaches [59] that seek to identify potential therapies.

There are several limitations to our approach. The cell lines we use aim to mimic the regulatory environment of neurons and microglia; however, they may still be different than cells in the brain. For our MPRA, we selected a single incubation time, although effects may vary across different time points. Future studies could explore the temporal dynamics of 3’-UTR variant effects. MPRA evaluates a segment of the regulatory region (51 nucleotides in our case), which excludes the context of the full 3’-UTR and may miss haplotype effects and the impact of more distant secondary structure. MGExA integrates MPRA and GWAS results to identify genes that are associated with alcohol traits using publicly available GWAS summary statistics instead of using individual-level genotypes, and therefore does not incorporate haplotype information. We evaluated the downstream effects of these MGExA-identified genes by pooled CRISPR inhibition screening, using 5 guide RNA sequences per gene to account for the possibility of failed guides; some guide sequences had a relatively low success rate which decreased the number of total perturbed cells to analyze, but the analysis takes that into consideration. Our approach to validating the involvement of these genes in alcohol traits using drug screening results provides a novel way to link genes with their involvement in disease risk, but with some limitations. It treats differential effects of two drugs in a cell line as a proxy for differential effects of two drugs on AUD risk observed in patient healthcare data, though this link may not be straightforward. Furthermore, the candidate-comparator drug pairs we test are limited in number, so the association we observe is likely underpowered. We used the candidate-comparator approach to minimize confounding by indication, but there may still be other confounders that are unaccounted for.

Conclusions

In summary, we identified 3’-UTR variants that affect gene expression and integrated those results with GWAS to identify genes that may contribute to propensity for alcohol consumption or AUD. Using CRISPR inhibition of genes and insurance claims data, we validated the link between these genes and AUD risk. Overall, we believe this approach can uncover potential mechanisms that help us understand and treat alcohol use disorder.

Methods

Identification of candidate 3’-UTR SNPs

We identified candidate SNPs from the 3’UTRs of genes located within loci defined by the region around lead SNPs from the GWAS catalog [60] (v1.0, release 2019–08–24, accessed 2019–08–27) that were at least marginally associated (p < 10–5) with alcohol phenotypes [61] and other traits that mapped to the Experimental Factor Ontology [62] category “neurological disorders” (Additional file 1: Fig. S1, Additional file 2: Table S1 and S2; ref. [60, 61]). A total of 13,515 candidate SNPs that had minor allele frequency larger than 5% in at least one 1000 Genomes super population were selected from within these LD regions by identifying SNPs within 3’-UTRs defined by dbSNP [63] build 151.

Massively Parallel Reporter Assay for 3’-UTR variants

PASSPORT-seq [22–24], a massively parallel reporter assay (MPRA) similar to MPRAu [29], was used to evaluate the effect of 3’-UTR SNPs on the expression of a luciferase reporter gene contained in the pIS-0 vector [64]. If the inserted 3’-UTR sequences have no effect on transcription, the RNA abundance should be proportional to the DNA abundance. A pool of 24,780 oligonucleotides was synthesized by Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA), with each oligonucleotide containing either the reference or alternative allele of 13,515 SNPs flanked by the 25 nucleotides upstream and downstream of its genomic position plus vector-specific sequence at both termini (5’ end: 5’-GCCGTGTAATTCTAGGAGCTC; 3’ end: CGTTCTAGAGTCGGGGCGG-3’) to allow assembly into the pIS-0 plasmid. The oligo pool (10 fmol) was amplified by the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for 15 cycles with Invitrogen Platinum SuperFi DNA Polymerase mastermix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using 0.25 µM each of PCR primers HJ7207 (5’-GCCGTGTAATTCTAGGAGCTC-3’) and HJ7208 (5’-GCCCCGACTCTAGAACG-3’) in a 50 µL reaction. The products of four independent PCRs were combined and purified using MinElute columns (Qiagen, Germantown, MD).

To construct the plasmid library, the pIS-0 vector [64] (RRID: Addgene_12178, Addgene, Cambridge, MA) was first linearized using SacI-HF and BmtI-HF restriction enzymes (New England Biolabs (NEB), Ipswich, MA), separated in a 0.8% agarose gel, excised, and purified using a QIAquick gel extraction kit (Qiagen). The linearized vector was assembled with the amplified oligo pool using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA assembly kit (NEB) by mixing 50 ng of the linearized pIS-0 vector and 2 µL of the amplified oligonucleotide pool in a 20 µL reaction volume (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). Four independent assembly reactions were conducted, and 3–4 independent transformations were carried out from each assembly reaction, using 1.5 µl of each reaction product. Transformation was performed into 50 µL of chemically competent NEB 5-alpha competent E. coli cells (NEB), which were spread on two 150-mm LB-agar plates containing 100 µg/ml ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Bacteria were harvested by adding 4 mL LB to each dish and scraping with an L-shaped cell spreader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plasmid DNA was isolated from the bacteria using PureLink HiPure Plasmid Filter Miniprep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and pooled to produce the final plasmid library.

The MPRA was performed in two cell lines. All cells were purchased from the indicated supplier and tested for mycoplasma contamination using a mycoplasma detection kit (30–1012 K, American Type Culture Collection ATCC; Manassas, VA). Authentication was not performed within our laboratory since cells were utilized soon after purchase. SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells (CRl-2266, ATCC) were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of EMEM (ATCC) and F12K medium (10,025-CV, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (ATCC) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. Cells were plated in six 10-cm dishes at a density of 5 × 106 cells per dish. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, cells were transfected with X-tremeGENE HP DNA transfection reagent (Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO) using 10 µg of plasmid library and 40 µL reagent in 1000 µL Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A second cell line, SV40-immortalized human microglia cells (Cat.No: T0251, Applied Biological Materials Inc, Richmond, QC, Canada), was cultured in Prigrow III medium with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (16,000–044, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in type I collagen-coated (1:30; Sigma-Aldrich, C3867) 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells per wells (Corning™ 3516, Corning, NY). After 24 h, cells were transfected with 1 µg of plasmid library using 3 µl Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L3000008, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA; 3:1 reagent-to-DNA ratio) in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium (31,985–070, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Six independent transfections of each cell line were conducted. After 42 h, the cells were harvested. This timepoint, which we used previously [23], was chosen to allow for adequate reporter transcription while minimizing second-order effects from extended incubation. DNA and RNA were isolated from each transfection, using the AllPrep DNA/RNA mini kit (Qiagen). Poly-A RNA was isolated using Dynabeads oligo(dT)25 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mRNA treated with gDNA wipeout buffer (Qiagen) before preparing sequencing libraries. Sequencing libraries were prepared as described previously [23] with some modifications. Poly(A)RNA (500 ng) from each independent transfection was reverse transcribed to cDNA using QuantiTech Reverse Transcription kit (Qiagen) using a reporter-specific first strand primer (HJ7211, Additional file 2: Table S13 contains primer sequences) that contains the R1 Illumina adapter sequence, a 9–12 nt barcode (with staggered starts to reduce sequencing errors caused by low library complexity) and a 10 nt unique molecular index (UMI, a random sequence to reduce PCR bias). The resulting cDNA was then PCR-amplified with a primer containing the R2 Illumina adapter sequence and one of 6 barcodes, one for each independent transfection (HJ7214-HJ7219, Additional file 2: Table S13). Similarly, the DNA was PCR-amplified with another first strand primer (HJ7212) and one of 6 barcodes (HJ7214-HJ7219). The barcoding scheme is shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S2B. Both RNA and DNA samples underwent a second PCR using primers HJ7220 and HJ7221, after which the resulting libraries were pooled and prepared for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq SP (v 1.5) flow cell lane for 121 nt single-end reads.

Bioinformatics processing and statistical analysis for MPRA data

The pooled samples were differentiated using the barcodes. The FASTQ files were demultiplexed using cutadapt [65] (v2.7), and the UMI for each read was appended to the read name using umi_tools [66] (v1.0.0). After trimming the barcodes, UMI, and primer sequences, the remaining oligonucleotide insert sequences (51 nt) were mapped to the reference and alternative alleles of the SNPs. Finally, the reads containing mismatches or duplicated UMIs were discarded (20% of the reads), and the number of unique reads for each sequence was counted using umi_tools.

To calculate the amount of RNA expressed relative to its corresponding plasmid DNA for each oligonucleotide sequence, a generalized linear model implemented within the edgeR package [67, 68] (v. 3.26.8) in R (v. 3.6.0) was used to estimate the coefficient associated with a sequence’s cDNA count compared to what is expected based on its plasmid DNA count (analogous to the ratio of cDNA counts to plasmid DNA counts). The “glmFit” function was used to calculate the coefficients, and the “glmLRT” function was used to perform likelihood ratio tests.

RNA binding protein analysis

Potential changes in RBP binding motifs induced by 3′UTR variants were evaluated using the RBP binding affinity framework implemented in regSNPs-splicing [30] and regSNPs-intron [31]. Briefly, position weight matrices (PWMs) for RBPs were obtained from RBPDB [32] and CISBP-RNA [33], and sequence matching scores were computed for each oligonucleotide sequence in the MPRA library. To estimate binding likelihood, these matching scores were compared against empirical distributions derived from known bound and background sequences, allowing calculation of the probability of RBP binding for each oligonucleotide. For each variant, the reference and alternative alleles were evaluated separately, and differences in both matching scores and binding probabilities were used to quantify the magnitude of binding change as well as the probability of a binding state transition (i.e., gain or loss of RBP binding).

To identify RBPs whose binding changes may contribute to MPRA-derived regulatory effects, we assessed the association between predicted RBP binding state changes and variant activity. For each RBP, variants were classified based on whether they exhibited a change in binding state (defined as a shift in binding probability across a 0.5 threshold), and whether they showed significant MPRA effects (p < 0.05). These classifications were summarized in contingency tables, and Fisher’s exact test was applied to evaluate enrichment of functional variants among those with predicted binding changes. The alternative hypothesis tested whether variants altering RBP binding were more likely to exhibit regulatory activity in the MPRA assay.

Heritability enrichment analysis of MPRA-derived significant SNPs

Heritability enrichment analysis was conducted using LDAK [70] (v. 5.0) by comparing the heritability contribution of SNPs significant in our MPRA (FDR ≤ 0.05; ref. [69]) with the heritability contribution of 1000 sets containing the same number of candidate SNPs randomly drawn from the full 13,515 SNPs. LDAK estimates the expected heritability contribution of each GWAS SNP using the GWAS summary statistics, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and minor allele frequency for each SNP. A permutation p-value was determined by calculating the fraction of the 1000 values that were greater than the share of heritability of the FDR-significant SNPs.

Generation of an SH-SY5Y cell line that stably expresses KRAB-dCas9

SH-SY5Y cells stably expressing KRAB-dCas9 were generated following the protocol for CRISPRi by Sigma-Aldrich. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well culture plates at 2 × 105 cells per well (10% confluency) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was replaced with growth medium containing 8 µg/mL polybrene, and cells were transduced with a KRAB-dCas9 lentiviral construct at multiplicity of infection (MOI) 0.2. After 24 h, media was replaced with fresh growth medium, and the cells were incubated for another 24 h. To begin antibiotic selection, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing Blasticidin (5 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). Antibiotic-containing medium was replaced every other day for 8 days, and the resulting cell line was cryopreserved for future experiments.

Perturb-seq single cell CRISPR-induced inhibition of target genes

We performed Perturb-seq [28] with candidate genes derived from MGExA. For each of these 31 genes, 5 guide RNA sequences were generated to target the region near the transcriptional start site of each gene. Additionally, 18 positive controls and 18 negative controls were also included, for a total of 191 sgRNAs in the CRISPRi library. The pooled lentiviral construct for the library was generated by Sigma-Aldrich.

KRAB-dCas9 SH-SY5Y cells were seeded at 3 × 106 cells per dish. After 24 h incubation, cells were transduced with the pooled lentiviral constructs at a target MOI of 0.2. After another 24 h, medium was changed, then after 24 h incubation, antibiotic selection began using medium containing 1.5 µg/mL Puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich). Medium with antibiotic was changed every other day for 7 more days. Cells were then harvested for single cell sequencing library preparation using 10X Genomics Chromium X.

Approximately 33,000 cells were loaded to target 20,000 cells in each well of a chip N, and two wells were used. The libraries were constructed following the 10X Genomics protocol for Chromium NEXT GEM Single Cell 5’ HT Reagent Kits (Dual Index) with Feature Barcode technology for CRISPR Screening. To identify the sgRNAs present in each gel bead, CRISPR primers were included in the reverse transcription step to amplify sgRNA as well as mRNA. After cDNA amplification, cDNA from sgRNA was separated from mRNA using size selection with SPRISelect and prepared for sequencing. The final cDNA and CRIPSR libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The cellranger multi algorithm of Cell Ranger 7.0 (http://support.10xgenomics.com/) was used to process the sequence data. Of the 30,972 cell barcodes identified, 18,507 contained at least one sgRNA barcode. Of these, 8,842 cells were identified to have been transduced with one target gene sgRNA, and 1,331 cells were identified to be transduced with NTC.

To identify which cells were successfully perturbed, we used Seurat’s mixscape function [38] closely following their recommended vignette. Briefly, the sequencing results for cells with only one sgRNA were loaded into Seurat and normalized using SCTransform [73]. The perturbation signature of each cell was calculated using the CalcPerturbSig function using ndims = 40 and num.neighbors = 20 with “pca” as the dimensional reduction method. The RunMixscape function was then run with default parameters using default pooling mode followed by fine mode. Default mode treats all five sgRNA IDs for one target gene as the same class, while fine mode treats each sgRNA ID as its own class. Of the 23 genes with reads detected in at least 50% of NTC cells, 7 genes had cells that were successfully perturbed. To evaluate differential expression for each target gene, the expression of perturbed cells was compared to NTC cells using Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test with the FindMarkers function. Perturbation responses across cells were visualized using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), a method that maximizes the separation between labels. The MixscapeLDA function was used to do dimensional reduction, and RunUMAP was used to visualize the resulting UMAP clusters.

Identifying relevant pathways using gene set enrichment analysis

We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [74] using the fgsea [75] package in R for each set of expression changes from either target gene perturbation or MPRA-derived gene activity stratification using pathways from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [39], Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) hallmark gene sets [42], and Reactome [43] collected by MSigDB [76]. For each analysis, we used a list of genes ordered by -log10(p-value of differential expression analysis) multiplied by the direction of effect as the gene set input. We applied Benjamini–Hochberg correction to report the false discovery rate of each pathway.

Identifying candidate drugs for real-world validation

To validate the perturbed genes using real-world data, we used Expanded CMap LINCS Resource 2020 to identify candidate drugs. For each perturbed gene, we created upregulated and downregulated gene set lists consisting of differentially expressed genes with FDR < 0.05. Using the three perturbed genes (KANSL1, RBM14, SNRNP40) that had 10 or more genes in both gene set directions, we ran a query using the CLUE web portal to identify drug compounds with differential gene expression signatures highly similar or dissimilar to the gene sets. For gene sets larger than 150, we used the top 150 genes by p-value. Using the CLUE web portal query results, we identified drug compounds with FDR < 0.05, tested in neuronal cell lines (NPC, SH-SY5Y, NEU, and U251MG), and passed quality control filters (qc_pass = 1, tas > 0.2). Further, we used drug compounds whose direction of similarity matched the perturbed gene’s predicted association with alcohol use. For example, KANSL1 expression is predicted to be AUD protective, so KANSL1 knockdown will be associated with increased AUD risk. To identify drug compounds that are AUD protective, we selected drugs that are negatively correlated with KANSL1 knockdown.

To qualify for further analysis, we removed drug candidates that were available over-the-counter or were cancer drugs, since these would not be tracked by pharmacy claims data. For each drug candidate, we defined a candidate cohort and a comparator cohort. The candidate cohort consisted of people who were prescribed the candidate drug. For the comparator cohort, we identified drugs that were prescribed for the same diseases by identifying compounds that are in the same Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification subgroup as the candidate drug. If they did not yield a large enough cohort for analysis, we identified similar drugs in other subgroups commonly prescribed for the same diseases.

Real-world validation through insurance claims data

To validate these candidate drugs, we used the Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database (years: 2007–2021) to determine if any of the drugs change patients’ propensity to develop AUD. Optum’s Clinformatics® was derived from a database of administrative health claims for members of large commercial and Medicare Advantage health plans and contains records on demographics, enrollment, diagnoses and pharmacy claims. The data was licensed to Indiana University, and The Indiana University Institutional Review Board (IRB) designated this study as exempt. We used RxNorm [77] and the data source’s drug lookup table to define drug exposure. We used the R package comorbidity [78] to define AUD and comorbidity. For both cohorts, we selected people who were not diagnosed with AUD before drug exposure. For each candidate drug, we compared it to each of its selected comparators individually, so each candidate could have multiple comparisons.

Using a covariate-adjusted Cox model, we compared the hazard ratio of AUD diagnosis, HRAUD, between each candidate and comparator cohort. We used covariates for age (binned < 45, 45–64, and > 64), gender, race (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, unknown), and the following comorbidities: anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, blood loss anemia, cardiac arrhythmias, congestive heart failure, coagulopathy, chronic pulmonary disease, deficiency anemia, depression, diabetes (uncomplicated, complicated), fluid and electrolyte disorders, hypertension (uncomplicated, complicated), hypothyroidism, liver disease, obesity, other neurological disorders, paralysis, pulmonary circulation disorders, psychoses, peptic ulcer disease excluding bleeding, peripheral vascular disorders, renal failure, rheumatoid arthritis or collaged vascular disease, substance use disorder, valvular disease, and weight loss.

Ascertaining the effects of the perturbed gene on AUD risk

To demonstrate the relationship between the perturbed gene to the candidate drug’s association with AUD risk, we calculated the perturbation concordance score (PCS). To do so, we first calculated the fold-change of gene expression between the candidate drug and comparator drug by subtracting their CMap differential gene expression signatures. We focused on gene signatures found in neural progenitor cells because they were tested with the most drugs of interest. Then we calculated the Pearson’s correlation between this fold-change and the gene expression changes resulting from CRISPR knockdown of the candidate genes in our Perturb-seq experiment. PCS quantifies the alignment between drug-induced expression differences and gene knockdown effects. A large PCS indicates that the candidate drug’s perturbation is more similar to the gene knockdown than that of the comparator drug, whereas a small PCS indicates the opposite (Additional file 2: Table S12). We then performed a regression analysis using a linear model to determine the association of PCS with HRAUD​. We used the lm function in R (v4.1.1) to derive the p-value significance for each perturbed gene. A positive association between PCS and HRAUD​ suggests that a drug inducing expression changes similar to a candidate gene knockdown are associated with a higher risk of AUD, implying that the candidate gene may play a protective role, whereas a negative association suggests it may act as a risk gene.
