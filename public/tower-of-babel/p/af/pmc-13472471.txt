Histopathological enigma in a recurrent gingival swelling
Authors: Ajay Kumar Verma, Anjali Narwal, Mala Kamboj, Neha Sahjlan, Shikha Tewari, Anju Devi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A patient presented with a recurrent gingival swelling in the upper left anterior region which was localized, erythematous, and involved the interdental papilla. The patient had a history of biopsy from the same site 5 to 6 months prior. The lesion was surgically excised and subjected to histopathological evaluation. The case highlights the importance of histopathology in diagnosing rare gingival lesions with recurrence.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral odontogenic fibroma (POdF) is a rare, benign odontogenic tumor arising from odontogenic ectomesenchyme with epithelial rests.[1] The World Health Organization (WHO) recognized it in 1992 as the extraosseous counterpart of central odontogenic fibroma, distinguishing it from reactive lesions such as peripheral ossifying fibroma.[2] In the 2005 WHO classification, it was reaffirmed as a distinct entity under benign ectomesenchymal tumor, which included two distinct histologic patterns: the epithelium-poor (simple) type and the epithelium-rich (WHO) type.[3] However, the 2017 WHO classification eliminated these histologic subtypes because of poor reproducibility.[4]

POdF constitutes approximately 1.2%–4.7% of all odontogenic tumors and demonstrates a wide age distribution ranging from infancy to advanced age, with a peak incidence in the third decade of life. It is an uncommon benign encapsulated ectomesenchymal neoplasm that shows a clear female predilection, with reported female-to-male ratios varying from 1.1:1 to 7.5:1.[5] Clinically, it typically presents as a firm, slow-growing, sessile gingival mass, predominantly in the anterior maxilla or mandible, particularly the mandibular canine-premolar region.[1] Despite its rarity, accounting for <0.1% of oral biopsies, its distinctive histological pattern of connective tissue with odontogenic epithelium remains the diagnostic hallmark.[2]

CASE REPORT

In the present case, a 48-year-old male presented to the department of periodontics with a chief complaint of gingival redness in the upper left anterior tooth region for the past 3 months. Intraoral examination revealed a localized erythematous swelling involving the interdental papilla in relation to teeth 23 and 24. The swelling was mild, nontender, and sessile, with no surface ulceration [Figure 1].

Intraoral image showing mild erythematous swelling involving the interdental papilla region in relation to teeth 23 and 24

On reviewing history, the patient reported having undergone a biopsy from the same site approximately 5–6 months earlier at a private dental clinic. However, according to the patient, no histopathological examination was performed at that time and no documentation of the biopsy findings was available. The lesion subsequently recurred following excision from the same site. The patient also gave a positive history of systemic hypertension, which was well controlled with medication.

An intraoral periapical radiograph revealed horizontal alveolar bone loss [Figure 2]. Based on the clinical findings and history, a provisional diagnosis of localized gingival enlargement was considered. The lesion was surgically excised under local anesthesia and submitted for histopathological examination.

Intraoral periapical radiograph showing horizontal alveolar bone loss in relation to teeth 23 and 24

Histopathological examination demonstrated a moderately fibrocellular connective tissue stroma with numerous strands and islands of odontogenic epithelium with peripheral palisading tall columnar-like cells embedded within it. Multiple areas of dystrophic calcifications were noted in proximity to the epithelial islands. The overlying parakeratinized stratified squamous epithelium was intact. Overall histopathological features were consistent with POdF with calcifications [Figure 3a-d].

DISCUSSION

Clinically, such lesions can closely mimic reactive gingival enlargements, as seen in the present case. This underscores the diagnostic value of histopathology in all excised soft-tissue lesions from the oral cavity, regardless of their innocuous appearance. According to the WHO classification of odontogenic tumors, POdF is categorized under benign mesenchymal odontogenic tumors with odontogenic epithelium, further highlighting the importance of accurate histopathological identification.[6]

POdF is characterized by a nonencapsulated fibrocellular stroma containing inactive appearing odontogenic epithelial islands, strands, or cords. The epithelial elements are usually cuboidal to tall columnar in appearance resembling dental lamina remnants or rests of Malassez. These epithelial elements vary in quantity, ranging from epithelium-rich to epithelium-poor variants and are often situated deeper within the lesion, distinct from the surface mucosa.[7] In our case, multiple inactive epithelial nests embedded within a moderately cellular fibroblastic stroma underscore the neoplasm’s odontogenic origin.

As POdF may clinically resemble several reactive gingival enlargements, lesions such as pyogenic granuloma, peripheral giant cell granuloma, peripheral ossifying fibroma, and peripheral ameloblastoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis.[58] Definitive distinction relies primarily on histopathological evaluation. In contrast to peripheral ameloblastoma, the odontogenic epithelial islands in POdF are typically inactive, resting, and relatively smaller in size.[5] Odontogenic epithelial rests are a consistent feature of POdF but are not characteristic of peripheral ossifying fibroma which typically demonstrates reactive bone formation.[59] Peripheral giant cell granuloma demonstrates numerous multinucleated giant cells, whereas pyogenic granuloma shows prominent vascular proliferation resembling granulation tissue, features that are not seen in POdF [Table 1].[589]

The odontogenic epithelial islands are distinct from surface epithelial budding, which represents basal projections from the surface mucosa into the underlying tissue, appearing as epithelial sprouts or bud-like extensions near the epithelial lining.[5] This distinction has been emphasized in previous clinicopathological studies, thus highlighting its diagnostic relevance.[8]

A notable feature of POdF is the presence of dystrophic calcifications, which may take the form of dentinoid, cementum-like globules, or bone-like trabeculae. Histopathological studies have documented calcified deposits in roughly 70%–80% cases of POdF.[10]

A clinicopathological analysis of 151 POdF cases reported a 50% overall recurrence rate particularly in cases with incomplete surgical excision or presence of histological predictors such as basal cell budding.[811] Furthermore, calcifications directly adjacent to epithelial rests were associated with significantly lower recurrence.[8] However, in the present case, a history of recurrence following a previous biopsy highlights the unpredictable clinical behavior of this lesion and highlights the necessity for complete excision and long-term follow-up.

Postoperatively, the healing was uneventful. At the 3-week follow-up, the surgical site showed complete resolution of erythema with satisfactory soft-tissue healing. No signs of recurrence were noted over a 3-month observation period.

CONCLUSION

POdF is a rare but distinct benign odontogenic neoplasm that can clinically resemble common reactive gingival enlargements, creating a potential risk of misdiagnosis and inadequate management. Definitive diagnosis relies on careful histopathological evaluation demonstrating fibrocellular connective tissue, odontogenic epithelial remnants, and associated calcifications. Early recognition followed by complete surgical excision and long-term follow-up is essential to minimize recurrence and ensure appropriate treatment outcomes. This case further underscores the importance of submitting all excised gingival lesions for microscopic examination, even when they appear clinically innocuous.
