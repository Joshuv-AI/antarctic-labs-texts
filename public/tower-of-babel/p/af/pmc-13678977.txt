Reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling extends reproductive span through somatic gonadal collagen-mediated maintenance of oocyte and embryo quality in Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Neha Kaushik, Soumya Rastogi, Vaibhav Jain, Sonia Verma, Deepak Pandey, Mona Sharma, Ashutosh Halder, Simon Gregory, Neeraj Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe somatic gonad plays a central role in supporting germline development and maintaining oocyte and embryonic integrity, both of which are essential for sustained reproductive function. With age, deterioration of somatic gonadal structures compromises germline support, leading to declines in oocyte morphology and increased embryonic abnormalities. The insulin/IGF-1 signaling (IIS) pathway regulates longevity and reproductive aging, and its reduction (rIIS) in the daf-2(e1370) mutant extends reproductive span in Caenorhabditis elegans. While prior work has largely focused on germline stem cells and oocyte maturation, the molecular mechanisms by which IIS preserves somatic gonadal architecture and thereby maintains oocyte and embryonic morphological integrity remain poorly understood. Elucidating how reduced IIS influences the somatic gonad can provide insights into the coordination of systemic aging pathways with tissue-specific mechanisms that sustain reproductive health.MethodsWe identified somatic gonad–enriched genes from temporal transcriptomic datasets of reduced IIS animals, highlighting collagens consistently upregulated with age. Using RNAi knockdowns in both systemic and somatic gonad–specific RNAi-sensitive strains of Caenorhabditis elegans, we examined effects on self-reproductive span, oocyte and embryonic morphology, and somatic gonad integrity. Age-associated morphological changes were assessed by differential interference contrast microscopy, and regulatory dependencies were tested via quantitative polymerase chain reaction following knockdown of key transcription factors.ResultsSeveral collagens were found to be essential for maintaining somatic gonadal architecture and preserving oocyte morphology during aging. Gonad-specific knockdown of these collagens caused structural deterioration, impaired oocyte and embryo quality, and accelerated reproductive decline. These defects occurred without a major reduction in total progeny number, suggesting that age-related reproductive loss arises primarily from tissue deterioration rather than immediate fertility impairment. Expression and function of these collagens were largely dependent on key transcription factors downstream of IIS.ConclusionOur findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for somatic gonadal collagens in preserving reproductive function under IIS. By maintaining gonadal architecture and sustaining oocyte and embryonic morphological integrity, these collagens act as downstream effectors of IIS-mediated reproductive longevity. Targeting gonadal collagen networks may therefore represent a potential strategy to mitigate age-related reproductive decline and warrants further investigation in higher-order models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03069-7.Keywords: Insulin signaling, Somatic gonads, Oocyte, Embryo, Collagens, Reproductive aging, Transcriptomics, Caenorhabditis elegans

Background

Fertility declines markedly with advancing maternal age, accompanied by an increased risk of adverse reproductive outcomes such as miscarriages, chromosomal abnormalities, and infertility [1]. Although many of these age-related reproductive defects originate from intrinsic errors in oocyte meiosis, such as aneuploidy, spindle dysfunction, and chromosomal cohesion loss [1, 2], growing evidence suggests that the somatic environment surrounding the oocyte plays a critical and underappreciated role in reproductive aging [3, 4]. Somatic cells of the gonad—including sheath cells, and components of the extracellular matrix—fulfill indispensable roles in oocyte development. They provide metabolic nourishment via gap junctions, offer structural support through ECM-based frameworks, and deliver critical signaling cues that uphold germline maintenance and promote oocyte quality [5–7]. These interactions ensure mechanical integrity of the reproductive niche while facilitating essential communicative pathways necessary for proper oocyte growth and maturation. However, the interactions between developing oocytes and ovarian somatic cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM), which may contribute to age-related oocyte decline, are not yet fully understood.

Over the past two decades, Caenorhabditis elegans has emerged as a valuable model for studying human reproductive aging [8–10]. In the C. elegans hermaphrodite, the gonad consists of two U-shaped tubular arms in which germline stem cells (GSCs) reside at the distal end and undergo mitotic proliferation. As germ cells move proximally, they enter meiotic prophase, and following spermatogenesis during the L4 stage (with sperm stored in the spermatheca), a sperm-to-oocyte switch occurs, committing germ cells to oocyte development [11–13]. Developing oocytes in the proximal arm are enveloped by contractile gonadal sheath cells and supported by extracellular matrix components. Both gonadal arms connect to a common uterus that carries fertilized embryos until they are laid. These coordinated somatic and germline interactions ensure proper oocyte maturation and reproductive success. In both humans and C. elegans, fertility and reproductive capacity significantly decrease during early to mid-adulthood, leading to an extended post-reproductive phase [14–16]. In this study, we focused exclusively on self-reproduction, which is inherently limited by self-sperm number and quality in addition to oocyte factors. While many studies have focused on germline/oocyte quality due to numerous similarities in the reproductive systems of C. elegans and humans [8–10, 17–20], we were instead interested in identifying factors within the local niche of developing oocytes—the somatic gonad. Caenorhabditis elegans serves as an excellent model system for studying the effects of the somatic components of gonads on reproductive aging in humans through several conserved mechanisms. Proximal sheath cells in C. elegans are interconnected by gap junctions, similar to the connections between cumulus and mural granulosa cells in humans [21, 22]. In C. elegans, proximal gonadal sheath cells and, in humans, cumulus granulosa cells form gap junctions with the developing oocytes, which are essential for transferring signals and nutrients [21, 23]. Endocrine signals from the soma regulate germline proliferation and oocyte maturation in C. elegans, whereas in humans, hormonal signals from the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒gonadal (HPG) axis manage follicle development and oocyte maturation, with granulosa cells providing supportive factors [24–27]. The extracellular matrix (ECM) in both species offers crucial structural support and signaling cues for oocyte development [28–30]. In mammals, somatic cell dysfunction contributes to follicular atresia and impaired oocyte development [31]. Similarly, in C. elegans, sheath cell deterioration is associated with structural collapse and loss of oocyte integrity during aging [32]. Aging disrupts these somatic support systems, compromising their ability to maintain the reproductive niche. The conservation of these mechanisms across species underscores the utility of C. elegans as a powerful model for dissecting the molecular and cellular interactions within the gonadal microenvironment that govern reproductive aging. Understanding how somatic support cells maintain their function over time and how this is regulated at the molecular level is critical for identifying strategies to preserve fertility.

The insulin/IGF-1 signaling (IIS) pathway is a well-established regulator of aging and reproduction in organisms ranging from worms to humans [9, 33–38]. In mammals, IIS through the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a pivotal role in follicular recruitment, granulosa cell function, and oocyte maturation [39]. Insulin and IGF1 have been shown in vitro to enhance granulosa cell proliferation and follicle survival, while also promoting oocyte maturation and reducing atresia [40]. In C. elegans, reduced IIS via mutations in daf-2 delays reproductive aging, preserves germline quality, and extends reproductive span through the activation of DAF-16/FOXO [9, 10, 19, 41, 42]. However, the role of IIS in regulating the structural integrity and function of the somatic gonad, especially the extracellular components such as collagen, remains underexplored.

In this study, we focused on a set of collagen genes that were consistently upregulated in the somatic gonads under reduced IIS conditions. Collagens are major components of the ECM and are crucial for maintaining tissue architecture and biomechanical stability. Using transcriptomic analyses and functional assays, we demonstrated that these collagen genes are required to maintain somatic gonadal morphology and preserve oocyte and embryonic integrity during aging. Their knockdown, specifically in the gonadal sheath cells, led to accelerated reproductive decline and loss of tissue structure, independent of immediate changes in fertility output. The regulation of these collagens is largely governed by key transcription factors downstream of rIIS, which help maintain the reproductive niche. Our findings highlight the importance of ECM integrity in reproductive aging and establish somatic gonadal collagens as key downstream effectors of IIS-mediated reproductive maintenance. These results offer new insights into the somatic contributions to fertility preservation and suggest potential targets for mitigating age-associated reproductive decline.

Methods

Strain maintenance

The N2 Bristol (wild-type) and daf-2 (e1370) strains were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC), University of Minnesota, MN, USA. The somatic gonad sheath cell-specific RNAi strains daf-2 (e1370);inx-8::rde-1;rde-1(-) were generated in-house via standard crossover techniques between inx-8::rde-1;rde-1(-) and daf-2 (e1370) in Arnab Mukhopadhyay’s Lab at the National Institute of Immunology, Delhi. Throughout the manuscript, different strains of the empty vector [E. coli HT115 (DE3) carrying the L4440 vector] are referred to as the “control”. Caenorhabditis elegans were maintained on nematode growth medium plates at 20 °C and fed the OP50 Escherichia coli strain unless otherwise stated. The animals were regularly passaged to prevent starvation and overcrowding during routine maintenance.

Egg collection and population synchronization

Caenorhabditis elegans were cultured on standard NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50 (Caenorhabditis Genetics Center, Minneapolis, MN, USA) until they reached the gravid adult stage. Worms were harvested into 15 mL centrifuge tubes using M9 buffer, which was prepared by dissolving 6 g Na₂HPO₄, 3 g KH₂PO₄, 5 g NaCl, and 0.25 g MgSO₄ in 1 L of double-distilled water (all reagents from Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India). The worm suspension was centrifuged at 1200 × g for 60 s in a swing-bucket rotor centrifuge (Eppendorf, Model #5810R), and the resulting pellet was washed three times with fresh M9 buffer. For synchronization, the washed worm pellet was treated with a bleaching solution (7 parts ddH₂O: 2 parts sodium hypochlorite: 1 part 5 N NaOH; Merck Life Sciences, Mumbai, India) and vortexed vigorously for 6–8 min (Tarsons Spinix vortex mixer) to dissolve the adult carcasses and release eggs. The released eggs were washed 5–6 times by centrifugation at 2000 × g to remove residual bleach and alkali. A suction pump (Rocker410, Rocker Scientific, Taiwan) was used to remove the supernatant, and the eggs were resuspended in fresh M9 buffer. Eggs were then transferred into 10 mL of M9 buffer and incubated on a rocking platform (Rockymax, Tarsons) at 21–22 °C for 18–20 h to allow synchronized L1 larvae to hatch and arrest. Hatching efficiency was assessed to ensure viability. Healthy, synchronized L1 larvae were plated onto RNAi feeding plates and cultured until the L4 stage. Unhealthy or developmentally asynchronous worms were removed before the remaining population was grown to the desired time point for downstream RNA extraction.

RNA sequencing and differential expression analysis

Gonads were isolated from Caenorhabditis elegans hermaphrodites (N2 and daf-2) at defined adult stages (young adult, day 2, day 6, and day 10) as previously described [43]. Dissected tissue included the complete gonad arm comprising germ cells (mitotic and meiotic regions), oocytes, sheath cells and the distal tip cell (DTC). No physical or genetic separation of individual gonadal cell types was performed prior to RNA extraction. Briefly, worms were placed on a glass slide under a dissection microscope and severed posterior to the pharynx using a sterile 23 G needle to extrude the gonadal arms, which were subsequently separated from the carcass near the spermathecae. Isolated gonads (five per tube, collected within 10 min) were transferred into ice-cold M9 buffer using a Stripper™ pipettor, centrifuged briefly, and homogenized in 100 µL TRIzol reagent. For whole-animal transcriptomic comparison, N2 and daf-2 hermaphrodites were collected at Young Adult and Day 10 stages (10–15 animals per biological replicate) and homogenized in 500 µL TRIzol. All samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, quality-checked for integrity, and used for library preparation followed by Illumina-based RNA sequencing. The resulting transcriptomic dataset represents the composite gene expression profile of the entire dissected gonad arm rather than a specific somatic cell type. Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked and trimmed prior to alignment to the C. elegans reference genome (WBcel 245). Gene-level read counts RPKM (reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads) values were generated. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [44] and genes with a P value < 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1.5 were considered differentially expressed for Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. Comparisons were conducted between daf-2 and N2, across adult time points (Young Adult, Day 2, Day 6, and Day 10), and between gonadal and whole-worm samples. Collagen-encoding genes (col family) were extracted from the differential expression dataset for focused downstream analysis.

RNAi plate preparation

Nematode growth medium (NGM) was prepared by mixing 3 g of NaCl (Merck Life Sciences Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India, Catalog #1.93606.0521), 2.5 g of peptone (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India, Catalog #RM001), and 17 g of agar (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India, Catalog #24970) in 1 L of double-distilled water. After autoclaving and cooling to 55–60 °C, the following components were added: 0.5 mL of cholesterol (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India, Catalog #TC1101) [10 mg/mL in ethanol (Merck Life Sciences Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India, Catalog #1.00983.0511)], 1 mL of 1 M CaCl2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States, Catalog #C614-500), 1 mL of 1 M MgSO4 (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India, Catalog #GRM684), and 25 mL of 1 M KPO4 (pH 6.0) were used. The 1 M KPO4 solution was prepared by mixing 10.83 g of KH2PO4 (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India, Catalog #54358) and 3.56 g of K2HPO4 (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India, Catalog #GRM1045) in 100 mL of double-distilled water. The NGM was then supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin (Bio Basic Inc., Markham, Ontario, Canada, Catalog #AB0028) and 2 mM IPTG (BR Biochem Lifesciences Pvt. Ltd., New Delhi, India, Catalog #BC0168). After the medium was added, the plates were dried at room temperature for 2–3 days. The E. coli bacterial strain HT115, containing the gene of interest for RNAi in the L4440 vector construct, was cultured overnight in Luria Bertani (LB) medium (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India, Catalog #M1245) at 37 °C in a shaker incubator. The LB medium was supplemented with 12.5 µg/mL tetracycline (Bio Basic Inc., Markham, Ontario, Canada, Catalog #TB0504) and 100 µg/mL ampicillin. The following day, the overnight-grown primary culture was inoculated into fresh LB medium containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin at a 1:100 ratio for secondary culture, which was incubated at 37 °C with shaking until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, as measured via a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan, Model #UV-1900). The bacterial cells from the secondary culture were pelleted by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in a 1/10th volume of M9 buffer containing 1 mM IPTG and 100 µg/mL ampicillin. IPTG in both the plates and the M9-suspended culture was used to induce T7 polymerase expression in the HT115 bacteria, which transcribes the dsRNA in the plasmid. A 120-µL aliquot of the M9 culture suspension was seeded onto 60 mm RNAi plates and dried to develop a bacterial lawn at room temperature over approximately 2–3 days.

Sheath cell–specific RNAi

To functionally assess the role of candidate collagens, RNAi knockdown was performed using a sheath cell–specific RNAi strain daf-2 (e1370);inx-8::rde-1;rde-1(-) and inx-8::rde-1;rde-1(-). This approach restricts RNAi activity to gonadal sheath cells, allowing targeted depletion of collagen expression in this cell type without broadly affecting other somatic gonadal cells such as the DTC. RNAi was initiated at the L4 stage unless otherwise specified. Knockdown efficiency was validated indirectly through phenotypic assays, including reproductive span analysis and brood assays.

Gonads dissection and sample collection

Gonadal tissues were dissected from Caenorhabditis elegans at key time points. For dissections, hermaphrodite worms were placed on a glass slide under a dissection microscope and carefully incised just posterior to the pharynx using a 23 G needle. The extruded gonads were further trimmed near the spermatheca to isolate them from the remaining body. Dissections were carried out using a Stripper™ pipettor (Cooper Surgical Inc., Trumbull, CT, USA; Catalog #MXL3-STR-CGR) fitted with 75 μm tips (Catalog #MXL3-75). Isolated gonads were immediately collected into the caps of 1.5 mL PCR tubes prefilled with chilled M9 buffer. A maximum of five gonads of same-aged worms were processed per tube within 10 min to ensure RNA integrity. Tubes were briefly centrifuged to pellet the tissue, after which 100 µL of TRIzol was added. Samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for RNA extraction.

RNA isolation

Samples stored at − 80 °C were subjected to three cycles of freezing and thawing, with intermittent vortexing, to ensure complete lysis of worm tissue. For RNA isolation, gonads were collected from animals of the same age, with 5 gonads pooled per tube across four tubes. Subsequently, 400 µL of chloroform (Merck Life Sciences, Mumbai, India; Catalog #1.07024.0521) was added to the homogenized lysate, and the tubes were gently inverted to mix. After a 3-minute incubation at room temperature, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the aqueous and organic phases. The upper aqueous layer was carefully transferred to a new tube, ensuring the interphase and organic layer remained undisturbed. An equal volume of isopropanol (Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, Canada; Catalog #BP2618-500) was added to precipitate RNA, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed once with 1 mL of 70% ethanol. A final centrifugation step (12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C) was performed, after which the pellet was air-dried. The RNA pellet was resuspended in nuclease-free water, heated to 65 °C for 10 min with occasional tapping to facilitate dissolution. RNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorimeter (Invitrogen, CA, USA), and integrity was evaluated on an Agilent Bioanalyzer using the RNA 6000 Nano kit (Agilent Technologies, USA; Catalog #RNA6000Nano).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

To verify the effectiveness of RNAi-mediated knockdown of transcription factors and collagens, transcript levels were analyzed using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Catalog #18080051). RNA was mixed with oligo dT primers and dNTPs, incubated at 65 °C for 5 min, and then immediately cooled to 4 °C for 1 min. The reaction was supplemented with dithiothreitol (DTT), RNase OUT, 5× reverse transcriptase buffer, and 1 µL of Superscript III enzyme per sample. Reverse transcription was carried out at 42 °C for 50 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 70 °C for 15 min. qRT-PCR was performed using the Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR QPCR Master Mix (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA; Catalog #600882) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gpd-2 reference gene and analyzed using the comparative ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Additional File 1.

Data analysis

Protein–protein interaction network and clustering analysis

Protein–protein association networks were generated using the STRING database (v12.0) with a medium confidence interaction score threshold of 0.4 [45]. In this network, edges represent specific and meaningful functional associations between proteins. The network incorporated both known interactions (from curated databases and experimentally determined evidence) and predicted associations (based on gene neighbourhood, gene fusions, and gene co-occurrence), as well as additional evidence from text mining, co-expression, and protein homology. Clustering of the network was performed in STRING using the k-means algorithm. The clustered network was subsequently imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.3) for visualization [46]. A prefuse force-directed layout was applied, and clusters were manually adjusted to improve clarity and separation.

Heat map

Heatmaps were generated using the online tool Morpheus (Broad Institute, USA). Normalized average RNA-seq expression values (RPKM) of three biological replicates were first transformed as log₂(RPKM + 1) prior to visualization. The processed data matrix was uploaded to Morpheus, and hierarchical clustering was performed using Euclidean distance with average linkage. Expression values were visualized using a sequential color scale to represent relative expression levels across time points. Gene order and clustering parameters were kept consistent across genotypes to enable direct comparison of temporal expression patterns.

Venn diagrams

Overlapping genes among different datasets were visualized using the Venny 2.1.0 tool (https://csbg.cnb.csic.es/BioinfoGP/venny.html) [47]. Gene sets were uploaded into the platform to generate Venn diagrams, which identified shared and unique genes across the lists. The graphical outputs were further refined manually using Microsoft PowerPoint for enhanced clarity and presentation. Statistical significance of the overlaps between gene sets was assessed using hypergeometric distribution analysis and representation factor calculations.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis

GO analysis was carried out using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) tool [48]. Gene sets identified from the transcription factor enrichment analysis (reported previously) were uploaded to the DAVID web platform. The Caenorhabditis elegans genome was selected as the reference species, along with the appropriate gene identifier format. The Gene Functional Classification module was used to perform functional annotation and categorize genes into enriched biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. DAVID’s clustering algorithm was applied to group functionally related terms, and the five most significantly enriched GO terms (based on p-values) were visualized using bar plots.

Dendrogram formation

To generate a dendrogram for all the collagens present in C. elegans, the collagen sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database and prepared in a suitable format, such as FASTA. The MAFFT aligner was utilized with the command FFT-NS-i (Standard), executed as follows: MAFFT --reorder --anysymbol --auto input. Next, the sequences were aligned via MAFFT through the command line interface. Once aligned, the output was formatted correctly for iTOL, typically in FASTA format with each sequence on a separate line. The aligned sequences were subsequently uploaded to iTOL via the website, where iTOL automatically generated a dendrogram on the basis of sequence similarity. The dendrogram’s visualization underwent customization via iTOL’s interactive tools to adjust the tree layout, color coding, and labeling options. Clades and subclades were defined as sets of collagens sharing a most recent common ancestor (MRCA) node with a branch length ≤ 0.05 substitutions/site. The branch lengths were ignored for better representation. Following customization, the dendrogram was saved and exported in PNG format for presentation purposes.

RNAi knockdown collagens

The RNA interference (RNAi) technique was employed to induce loss-of-function RNAi phenotypes by feeding worms with E. coli (HT115) expressing double-stranded RNA in the L4440 vector. RNAi clones targeting collagen genes were sourced from the Ahringer (genomic DNA) Library [49] and verified by sequencing. Initially, primary cultures of RNAi colonies were cultivated overnight at 37 °C, with selective growth facilitated by tetracycline (12.5 µg/mL) and ampicillin (50 µg/mL) antibiotics. The secondary cultures were subsequently grown in LB media until they reached an optical density (OD) of 0.6–0.8, followed by inoculation with a 1/100th volume of primary cultures and 50 µg/mL ampicillin. Upon reaching the specified OD range (approximately 3–6 h), the cultures were pelleted (5000 rpm for 10 min), and a 1/10th volume of M9 buffer was added to the pellets along with 50 µg/mL ampicillin and 238.3 µg/mL IPTG to induce dsRNA expression. Given the expected redundant nature of collagen functions, RNAi cultures targeting collagens with similar sequences were mixed in equal ratios. These cultures, including individual and mixed collagen RNAi, were plated onto 30 mm agar plates supplemented with 50 µg/mL ampicillin and 1 mM isopropylthiogalactoside (IPTG). Worms were synchronized by hypochlorite treatment, and L1 larvae were arrested by overnight starvation. Subsequently, starved L1 larvae were transferred to 60 mm control RNAi NGM plates and cultured at 20 °C until they reached the L4 stage/young adult stage. Upon reaching the L4/YA (larval4 / Young adult) stage, the worms were shifted to RNAi plates, which included individual, grouped, and control RNAi conditions.

Brood size and reproductive span

Brood and reproductive span was assessed by monitoring self-fertility of individual hermaphrodites without mating. As such, these measurements reflect self-sperm-limited reproduction and do not account for mated reproductive span. We cannot exclude contributions of self-sperm quality to the observed reproductive defects, even with post-L4 RNAi initiation. In the Brood assay, synchronized L1-stage N2 and daf-2 worms were initially placed on control RNAi plates. Upon reaching the L4 young adult stage, the worms were transferred to 30 mm plates containing control, individual, and mixed collagen RNAi. Daily egg counts were conducted under a dissecting microscope, with worms transferred to fresh plates each day for subsequent counting until cessation. Egg hatching was monitored after cessation for three consecutive days. For each strain, six plates with one worm per plate were assessed. This was considered one technical replicate, and three technical replicates per biological replicate were performed. For each experiment, at least 3 biological replicates were performed. Reproductive spans were calculated similarly, egg hatching was recorded, and the plates were monitored even after cessation for 3 consecutive days to detect whether any egg was hatched.

Gonad and oocyte morphology

Worms were grown from the L1 larval stage onward on control RNAi. At the L4 larval stage, some worms were again shifted to fresh control RNAi plates, whereas others were on the gene of interest (collagens) RNAi conditions. This shift at the L4 stage was due to severe developmental problems starting from the L1 RNAi on the gene of interest. Differential interference contrast (DIC) images of the gonads and oocytes were captured on days 2, 6 and 10 of adulthood. Oocyte images were categorized into three groups on the basis of their morphology, including features such as cavities, shape, size, and organization. The oocyte phenotypes were classified as normal (no small oocytes, no cavities, and no disorganized oocytes), mild (a few cavities in the gonad or slightly disorganized oocytes or small oocytes), or severe (many cavities in the gonad or disorganized or misshapen oocytes). Similarly, embryo morphology was categorized into three groups based on observable structural features within the uterus, including eggshell integrity, shape, cytoplasmic organization, and cleavage pattern. Embryos were classified as normal when they exhibited intact eggshells, regular oval morphology, uniform cytoplasm, and clearly defined blastomeres undergoing normal cleavage without fragmentation. Mild defects included slight irregularity in shape, subtle cytoplasmic granularity, minor asymmetry in blastomere size, or delayed but detectable cleavage while overall structural integrity was maintained. Severe defects were defined by collapsed or distorted eggshells, extensive cytoplasmic disorganization, fragmented or uneven blastomeres, developmental arrest, or markedly abnormal cleavage patterns. Oocyte and embryo morphology was evaluated on Days 2, 6, and 10 of adulthood following RNAi-mediated knockdown of gonadal (ShGn) or non-gonadal (NoGn) collagen genes. For each condition and time point, at least 20 animals were analyzed per biological replicate. A minimum of three independent biological replicates were performed, each consisting of independently grown and processed worm populations examined on separate experimental days. The percentage of animals displaying oocyte or embryo defects was calculated for each replicate and used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Genes were classified as significantly differentially expressed if they exhibited an adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 and an absolute log2-fold change of ≥ 1. To evaluate the statistical significance of gene overlaps in Venn diagrams, hypergeometric p-values were calculated using the online tool available at http://www.geneprof.org/GeneProf/tools/hypergeometric.jsp, with exact values displayed in the respective figure panels. To assess the impact of collagen gene knockdowns on reproductive span and brood size, experiments were conducted with four to five biological replicates. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays were performed in technical triplicates. Gene expression changes and other experimental comparisons were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Welch’s t-test, which accounts for unequal variances between sample groups. Reproductive span analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier analysis, defining cessation of egg laying as the event and applying the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test for p-value. Transcription factor RNAi effects in N2 and daf-2 were reanalyzed using multiple t-tests with false discovery rate control (two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli, Q = 5%), without assuming equal standard deviations. For DIC-based morphological assessment, animals were categorized at each adult day as either normal or exhibiting oocyte/gonadal defects according to predefined criteria. The number of animals in each category was recorded for control (empty vector) and RNAi-treated groups. Comparisons between control and test RNAi conditions were performed independently for each time point using the chi-square (χ²) test to evaluate differences in the distribution of phenotypic categories. All data analyses and graphical representations were carried out using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was determined using the following thresholds: p ≤ 0.05 (*), p ≤ 0.01 (**), and p ≤ 0.001 (***) until otherwise stated.

Results

Enrichment and temporal stability of collagens in gonads under rIIS condition

Reproductive aging is characterized by a decline in oocyte quality, yet the underlying tissue-specific regulators remain poorly understood. To explore how reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling (rIIS) modulates reproductive longevity, we dissected gonad arms from wild-type and daf-2 animals and profiled their gene expression across adulthood in our earlier study to characterize IIS-dependent transcriptional programs across shared gonadal (ShGn) and non-gonadal (NoGn) tissues [43]. Because the dissected tissue includes germ cells, sheath cells, and the distal tip cell (DTC), the resulting dataset reflects the composite transcriptome of the gonad arm. To investigate gonad-specific traits that may contribute to increased reproductive longevity beyond the systemic effects of rIIS, we performed gene ontology (GO) analysis of differentially expressed genes in both ShGn and NoGn groups (Figure S1A, B). While several categories were enriched in both upregulated and downregulated states, a comparison of upregulated categories revealed a striking and uniquely gonadal enrichment in the “structural constituent of cuticle,” comprising predominantly collagen genes (32 collagens out of 38 genes) (Fig. 1A, B). Our selection was based on multiple factors—including enrichment score, count, statistical significance, dataset specificity, and biological relevance in the context of oocyte quality and reproductive span under rIIS. By these criteria, collagen enrichment in gonads stood out as a unique molecular signature with potential functional significance. Remarkably, no significant enrichment categories were observed among downregulated genes in either ShGn or NoGn datasets (Figure S1C, D). To further assess the specificity of this signal, we performed a hypergeometric test, which confirmed that collagens are significantly enriched in gonadal tissues but not in non-gonadal tissues. Analysis of collagen gene distribution revealed a striking enrichment among activated genes. Collagen genes were markedly enriched in the activated category of the gonadal versus non-gonadal (NoGn) datasets (representation factor > 5, p = 9.98 × 10⁻¹⁵), indicating a robust and conserved induction of collagen expression across tissues. By contrast, repressed genes in the same group showed no significant enrichment (p = 0.460). A similar trend was observed in the non-gonadal dataset, where activated genes displayed a moderate but significant enrichment of collagens (p = 0.003), whereas repressed genes remained non-enriched (p = 0.242). These results demonstrate that collagen gene expression is preferentially associated with gene activation rather than repression, with the strongest enrichment evident in genes in the gonads (Fig. 1A).

We further analyzed these collagens (ShGn) to assess their temporal expression, examining changes from the young adult (YA) stage through Day 10 in the gonads of both wild-type (N2) and rIIS (daf-2) worms. Our observations revealed a significant decrease in collagen levels on Day 2, with further reductions at Days 6 and 10 in the wild type. In contrast, under reduced insulin signaling conditions, most collagens maintained their levels on Day 2, exhibited a slight decline on Day 6, and increased by Day 10, with some collagens surpassing even YA levels (Fig. 1B).

Next, we examined how the expression of gonad-enriched collagens (ShGn) changed from day 0 to day 10 in both gonadal and whole-worm samples (the latter representing the complete transcriptome, not just non-gonadal tissue). For whole-worm data, only two time points (day 0 and day 10) were available; therefore, collagen expression was directly compared between these time points. The fold-change ratio of day 10 to day 0 (YA) was then plotted. As anticipated, most collagens in the rIIS condition maintained their expression levels even on Day 10 (fold change ≥ 1) in the gonads. In contrast, a significant decrease in collagen expression was observed in the wild-type gonads. Interestingly, at the whole-worm level, most of these collagens did not maintain their levels in either the wild-type or the daf-2 worms. This finding underscores the preservation of a distinct subset of collagens downstream of rIIS signaling, specifically within gonadal tissues (Figure S2).

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was generated using STRING to explore associations among the differentially expressed genes. K-means clustering partitioned the network into three distinct functional modules. The largest cluster (27 genes) was defined by collagen biosynthesis, chain trimerization, and modifying enzymes, representing the central extracellular matrix (ECM) module. The second cluster (5 genes) showed a mixed profile, encompassing genes linked to neuropeptide binding along with collagens. The third cluster (4 genes) included collagen triple helix repeat proteins (20 copies) together with a carboxypeptidase activation peptide domain–containing protein, suggesting a distinct ECM-modifying group. STRING further predicted five functional partners based on co-expression: ZK813.7, D1086.6, and D1086.11 (uncharacterized proteins), ule-3 (uterine lumen expressed/localized), and vit-4 (a vitellogenin precursor essential for yolk protein deposition during embryogenesis), (Fig. 1C). These predicted partners may represent additional reproductive or ECM-related functional links.

Collagens primarily affect the reproductive span under rIIS conditions

To investigate the impact of collagen genes on the reproductive capacity of Caenorhabditis elegans, we employed RNA interference (RNAi) knockdown strategy to selectively knockdown the expression of individual collagen genes at systemic level. This approach allows us to assess how changes in collagen gene expression influence key reproductive parameters, such as brood size and reproductive span. Given the often-redundant functions of collagen genes, targeting a single gene may not consistently produce significant effects on phenotype readout, as compensatory functions of similar genes can result in minimal observable changes [50]. Additionally, redundancy may lead to off-target effects, further complicating the interpretation of the results. To address this challenge, we implemented simultaneous knockdowns of multiple collagen genes exhibiting high sequence similarity, suggesting that targeting multiple functionally similar genes on the basis of sequence similarity may decrease the likelihood of unintended consequences arising from off-target effects. Moreover, our goal was to understand the role of collagens in regulating reproductive aspects rather than focusing solely on initial levels of expression. By targeting groups of collagen genes, we aimed to obtain more reliable and informative results regarding their collective influence on reproductive parameters. To facilitate this process, a dendrogram was constructed encompassing all the collagen genes identified in the C. elegans genome, highlighting the collagens analyzed in our study. We observed that most collagen clades in the dendrogram showed very short internal branch lengths, with node-to-node substitution rates ranging from 0.01 to 0.05 (excluding terminal branches). This limited the possibility of defining strictly distinct clades. Therefore, candidate collagens were instead organized into functional groups by combining closely related paralogs (short internal branches, ≤ 0.05 substitutions/site, with a few exceptions) together with their known co-expression in gonadal or non-gonadal tissues. Furthermore, larger clades were subdivided into subclades to limit the number of clones per group, thereby minimizing dilution effects in the RNAi mixtures. Importantly, our primary objective was not to resolve evolutionary relationships among all collagens or to perform detailed intragroup comparisons. Instead, we aimed to generate experimentally tractable groups for testing combined versus individual RNAi effects. To achieve this, we adopted an integrative and pragmatic strategy that considers short internal branches, tissue specific expression data, and experimental feasibility, thereby providing a quantitative and biologically meaningful basis for grouping. This approach guided the selection of collagen genes for simultaneous knockdown (Fig. 2), while genes that could not be grouped were tested individually.

Our findings demonstrate that RNAi-mediated knockdown of individual somatic gonadal collagen genes (ShGn) significantly reduces reproductive span, particularly under reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling (rIIS) conditions. It should be noted that all reproductive span measurements reflect self-fertility and may be influenced by self-sperm availability in addition to oocyte and somatic gonadal factors. In ShGn I, pooled RNAi resulted in a strong reduction in reproductive span. Among individual genes, col-93 and col-122 exhibited highly significant shortening comparable to the pooled treatment, whereas col-98 and col-139 had no detectable effect (Fig. 3A). In ShGn II, pooled knockdown significantly shortened reproductive span. Individually, all members except col-81 and col-179 produced robust reductions (Fig. 3B). Similarly, in ShGn III, pooled RNAi significantly reduced reproductive span. col-107 did not affect reproductive span, whereas col-8, col-19, and col-140 exhibited significant effects comparable to the pooled condition (Fig. 3C). In ShGn IV, col-80 significantly reduced reproductive span, approaching the magnitude of the pooled knockdown, while col-39 and col-143 did not show any effect (Fig. 3D). For collagens where group formation was not feasible, individual knockdown of col-65, col-76, and col-184 produced strong reductions in reproductive span, whereas col-103, col-11, and col-119 had no detectable impact (Figure S3C). In contrast, knockdown of non-gonadal collagen groups (NoGn I and II) did not significantly affect reproductive span under rIIS conditions (Figs. 3E–F and S3D) highlighting the distinct and tissue-specific functional relevance of gonadal versus non-gonadal collagens in regulating reproductive aging. Importantly, no significant effects were observed in the wild-type background following systemic knockdown (representative graph shown in Figure S3A). To determine whether these effects were specific to the somatic gonad, we performed gonad-specific (sheath cell–restricted) RNAi knockdown. This approach allowed us to directly assess the requirement of these collagens within sheath cells, independent of other gonadal cell types. Consistent with systemic RNAi results, all ShGn groups except ShGn IV showed robust reductions in reproductive span under rIIS, whereas NoGn knockdown had no significant effect, mirroring the systemic RNAi results (Figure S3B). Remarkably, simultaneous knockdown of all genes within a group (pooled RNAi) resulted in a reduction in reproductive span comparable to that observed with the strongest individual members, with no clear additive effect (Fig. 3 and S3), suggesting partial redundancy within ShGn collagen subgroups.

Brood size was variably affected by individual and mixed RNAi knockdowns of gonadal collagens. Among the four gonadal collagen groups, ShGn II group, which comprised the largest number of genes, exhibited only a modest reduction in brood size under mixed RNAi, and this effect was restricted to the rIIS condition. By contrast, ShGn III group collagens, col-107 and col-8, significantly reduced brood size when tested individually under wild-type conditions, highlighting their independent roles. Several other individual collagens also influenced brood size in specific conditions: col-76, col-111, col-65, and col-184 impaired brood size under rIIS, whereas col-103, col-119, and col-65 did so under wild-type conditions (Figure S4B2).

For the non-gonadal collagens, only NoGn I group showed an effect in the mixed RNAi condition. Within this group, col-50 and col-54 reduced brood size both in wild-type and rIIS conditions when tested individually, suggesting consistent reproductive roles (Fig. 4E-F). Additionally, the individually tested col-62 also reduced brood size under both wild-type and rIIS conditions, further underscoring its functional importance (Figure S4C). Overall, these findings indicate that gonadal collagens exert context-specific effects on brood size, with certain collagens acting predominantly in the wild-type background and others under reduced IIS conditions. In contrast, non-gonadal collagens exhibited more consistent effects, particularly when tested individually. These results highlight that the majority of GnSh collagens primarily affect the reproductive span under rIIS conditions, whereas a subset also influences brood size under both wild-type and rIIS conditions.

Analysis of brood size revealed that sheath-specific knockdown of ShGn I, II, and IV significantly reduced progeny production under reduced IIS conditions (Figure S4D). Particularly, ShGn IV did not affect brood size under systemic knockdown (Figs. 4D), indicating a sheath cell–specific effect. Somatic gonadal collagens influence reproductive outcomes under rIIS conditions

We dissected the gonads, which primarily contain distal tip cell (DTC), somatic gonad sheath cells, germ cells, and unfertilized oocytes, and then investigated the expression and localization of the collagens identified in our study within these dissected gonadal components. To contextualize our findings, we compared our data on ShGn collagens with previously published studies that focused on germline [51], gamete-specific expression [52], and whole-gonadal data [53]. The analysis revealed that the majority of ShGn collagens (28 out of 32) overlapped with gonadal datasets but showed minimal overlap with germline- or gamete-specific datasets (Fig. 5B left-side). In contrast, for NoGn collagens, only a small subset (9 out of 32) overlapped with gonadal datasets, with no notable overlap with germline or gamete-specific datasets (Fig. 5B right-side).

Next, we knocked down the collagen genes expressed in the somatic gonads via a somatic gonad-specific RNAi strain (daf-2(e1370);inx-8::rde-1;rde-1(-)). The oocytes and embryos were examined morphologically via differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy (magnification of 40X) on day 2 of adulthood. Oocytes were assigned a score (normal, mild, or severely defective morphology) based on the presence of cavities, abnormal shape/size, and/or disorganized structure. Similarly, embryos were categorized into three groups based on morphological features within the uterus, including eggshell integrity, shape, and cleavage pattern. Exposing this strain to RNAi clones targeting somatic gonad-expressed genes starting from the L1 stage resulted in delayed or arrested gonadal development, with most of the cases classified as having a severe phenotype. Similarly, the collagen genes expressed by NoGn showed mostly arrested gonadal development even at day 2 (Figure S5). Therefore, we opted to knockdown these genes at the L4 stage. On day 2, worms grown on the empty vector displayed proper arrangement of stacked oocytes without any cavities. However, the severity index increased with age, as observed on days 6 and 10 (Fig. 5A and C). Knockdown of collagen genes expressed in the somatic gonad resulted in a drastic increase in oocyte severity starting from day 2, which further increased with age. In contrast, NoGn-expressing collagen gene knockdown did not result in the same age-dependent decline in oocyte quality (Fig. 5A and C). These findings highlight a preferential functional role for somatically expressed gonadal collagens over systemically expressed collagens in preserving gonadal and oocyte quality during aging.

To determine whether the observed oocyte defects affected subsequent developmental outcomes, we examined embryo morphology following sheath cell–specific collagen knockdown. Animals subjected to ShGn knockdown displayed an increased frequency of morphologically abnormal embryos, characterized by irregular cellular organization and compromised structural appearance compared to controls. In contrast, NoGn knockdown animals did not exhibit comparable embryonic defects (Figure S6A and S6B). These results indicate that depletion of somatic gonadal collagens is associated with measurable impairments at the embryonic level.

Tissue-specific regulation of collagen genes by key transcription factors

To investigate the mechanism by which somatic gonad-specific collagens are regulated through key transcription factors (TFs) downstream of insulin signaling, we curated a set of nonredundant gene datasets regulated by DAF-16 [54–58], SKN-1 [56, 59, 60], and HSF-1 [56, 61, 62]. These datasets were derived from our previous study, where we compared our gonad-specific findings with literature on organism-level datasets [56]. In the current work, these curated datasets were further compared with the complete repertoire of collagens expressed in C. elegans. This analysis revealed that most collagens are regulated predominantly by HSF-1, followed by SKN-1, with DAF-16 playing a comparatively smaller role. DAF-16 was found to regulate 73 collagens (42%), with a representation factor (RF) of 2.4-fold and a p value of 3.38 × 10− 14; SKN-1 regulated 101 collagens (58%), with an RF of 8.01-fold and a p value of 3.50 × 10− 69; and HSF-1 exhibited the highest regulation, controlling 115 collagens (66%), with an RF of 2.7-fold and a p value of 4.59 × 10− 31 (Fig. 6A).

Further analysis of the potential combinations of transcription factors (TFs) involved in regulating collagen genes revealed that HSF-1, in conjunction with SKN-1, primarily governs the expression of collagen genes (Figure S7A). Among the 32 gonad-enriched genes identified in our study, HSF-1 was the dominant regulator, controlling 29 genes (91%), with an RF of 3.72 and a p value of 3.48 × 10–15. SKN-1 regulated 13 genes (41%) with an RF of 5.64 and a p value of 1.27 × 10 − 7, whereas DAF-16 showed the least significant enrichment, regulating 15 genes (47%) with an RF of 2.69 and a p value of 1.12 × 10 − 4 (Fig. 6B and S6B). In both the complete collagen repertoire (Fig. 6A) and the gonad-enriched subset (Fig. 6B), HSF-1 consistently emerged as the dominant regulator, whereas DAF-16 regulated a smaller subset of collagens. As many of these genes are also expressed in other tissues, their gonadal expression may be masked in whole-worm transcriptional datasets.

To test this possibility, we assessed collagen gene expression in the gonads of daf-2 worms at the peak reproductive period (day 2), following RNAi knockdown of TFs starting at the L4 stage. Surprisingly, our findings revealed a contrasting regulatory hierarchy in the gonadal context: DAF-16 was identified as the predominant regulator, with 10 of 28 collagen genes (36%) exhibiting significant fold changes under reduced IIS relative to wild-type, including col-42, col-76, col-81, col-98, col-119, col-122, col-125, col-179, col-180, and col-181). All of these genes were positively regulated by DAF-16, as evidenced by their significant downregulation following DAF-16 knockdown compared to control RNAi (Fig. 6C, lower panel). Additionally, RNAi-mediated knockdown in the wild-type background led to the upregulation of multiple genes within this set. This was followed by SKN-1, which regulated 9 out of 28 genes (32%) (col-8, col-119, col-122, col-124, col-140, col-143, col-178, col-181 and col-184). Four of these genes (col-8, col-143, col-181 and col-184) were significantly upregulated following SKN-1 knockdown compared to control RNAi indicating their negative regulation by SKN-1 (Fig. 6C, middle panel). Similarly, HSF-1 was found to regulate 9 out of 28 genes (32%) (col-42, col-98, col-107, col-111, col-122, col-140, col-143 and col-178). Three found to be negatively regulated (col-42, col-107 and col-111) (Fig. 6C, top panel).

Among the transcription factor–positively regulated genes that influenced reproductive span, DAF-16–regulated collagens included col-76, col-179, and col-181; SKN-1–regulated collagens included col-122 and col-178; and HSF-1–regulated collagens included col-101, col-122, col-140, col-143, and col-178. (Fig. 3 & S3C). These findings suggest that TFs regulate collagen genes in a tissue-specific manner and that their roles are fine-tuned based on the physiological needs of different tissues. Gonad-specific regulation by these TFs could be an adaptive response to ensure proper collagen function in the context of reproduction, aging, and gonadal health, which may involve regulatory mechanisms distinct from those of other tissues.

RNAi-mediated knockdown of transcription factors accelerates age-dependent gonadal degeneration

To determine the relative role of transcription factors downstream of reduced IIS in maintaining gonadal integrity, we performed RNAi knockdown of daf-16, skn-1, and hsf-1, individually or in combination, and assessed gonadal morphology at days 2, 6, and 10 of adulthood. DIC images (Fig. 7A) showed that empty vector (EV)-treated gonads were well organized at day 2, with progressive signs of disorganization, shrinkage, and vacuolation evident by day 10. In contrast, knockdown of daf-16, skn-1, or hsf-1 resulted in detectable degeneration as early as day 2, with marked structural loss by day 6 and predominantly severe defects by day 10. Mix RNAi also produced a similar progression, with gonads highly disorganized from day 2 and severely degenerated by later time points.

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 7B) further confirmed that ~ 75% of gonads were normal at day 2 in EV controls, with a gradual shift toward mild and severe categories by day 10. In all RNAi-treated groups, the proportion of normal gonads was substantially reduced at day 2, with progressive increases in severe degeneration by days 6 and 10. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that daf-16, skn-1, and hsf-1 are each essential for maintaining gonadal health during aging, but their contributions are not additive, consistent with their action in interconnected protective pathways.

Discussion

Genetic mutations in several signaling pathways that regulate longevity have been implicated in delaying reproductive aging; however, these mutations are often accompanied by a significant reduction in total progeny production [9, 10, 15, 16]. Previous studies, together with our data, have demonstrated that insulin/IGF-1 signaling (IIS) mutants exhibit a distinct phenotype characterized by an extended reproductive period with a nearly wild-type brood size under permissive temperature conditions [9, 17, 63]. Moreover, early progeny production has been shown to neither accelerate nor delay the duration of the reproductive period, suggesting that early fecundity is not a direct determinant of reproductive longevity [9]. Based on these findings, we focused our investigation on the IIS pathway as a key modulator of reproductive aging.

Interorgan communication involving signals from the germline and somatic gonads has been shown to influence systemic aging and promote the longevity of somatic tissues [64–66]. Conversely, signaling originating from somatic tissues can regulate germline function and oocyte maintenance [10, 15]. These secondary signaling molecules, many of which remain uncharacterized, may act on the somatic gonad, which in turn influences germline function in a nonautonomous manner. Alternatively, the somatic gonad itself may be a direct target of longevity-regulating pathways, acting autonomously to maintain its own function and support the germline. Together, these findings suggest that the somatic gonad is an indispensable organ that potentially functions as a local niche that integrates systemic and tissue-intrinsic signals to preserve germline health. Therefore, in this study, we characterized the somatic gonadal components and investigated how they differ functionally and transcriptionally from other somatic tissues in the context of reproductive aging.

Reproductive aging is a multifaceted process influenced by intrinsic germline deterioration and the decline of somatic support systems. In both Caenorhabditis elegans and humans, oocyte quality begins to decline well before reproductive cessation, suggesting that the local tissue environment may play a key role in reproductive health during aging. Our study reveals a crucial function for somatic gonad-specific collagens, regulated by key TFs under reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling (rIIS), in preserving the structural integrity of the gonadal niche and consequently supporting oocyte quality during aging. Our findings add an important but previously underexplored perspective. While mechanisms intrinsic to the germline in insulin- or insulin-like growth factor signaling-mediated reproductive aging, such as cathepsin B regulation [19], have been described, the role of the somatic gonadal extracellular matrix as a downstream target of this pathway, represents novel insight. We show that collagen genes enriched in gonadal sheath cells are regulated differently from those in the rest of the body and that their sustained expression under reduced insulin signaling conditions is associated with preserved oocyte morphology and a delay in reproductive aging (Figs. 3 and 5C-D). While the bulk RNA-seq dataset reflects mixed gonadal cell populations, the phenotypic outcomes of sheath cell–specific RNAi establish that sheath-derived collagen is sufficient to modulate reproductive aging. We do not exclude the possibility that collagens expressed in other gonadal cell types, including the DTC, may also contribute to the overall phenotype [67].

To assess the functional impact of the identified collagen genes, we employed both individual and pooled RNAi treatments. The knockdown efficiencies were comparable between the two approaches (Figure S4A). These findings suggest that each gene is effectively silenced, even when targeted collectively. On this basis, a stronger reproductive phenotype was anticipated with pooled knockdown, as multiple genes were simultaneously suppressed. However, the observation that pooled and individual knockdowns yielded similar phenotypic outcomes challenges the notion of simple functional redundancy among these collagens. Rather, these findings suggest that individual collagens may play distinct, not compensable roles within a shared structural or regulatory network. The absence of an additive effect points to a threshold or bottleneck mechanism, wherein the disruption of a single critical collagen is sufficient to impair the reproductive span, and additional knockdowns do not exacerbate the phenotype. These findings support a model in which collagen genes operate in a modular or hierarchical fashion rather than performing fully interchangeable roles despite high sequence similarity among them.

Our findings from collagen gene knockdowns indicate that somatic gonadal collagens are critical regulators of reproductive aging, acting not merely as structural components but as active modulators of fertility and reproductive span. In C. elegans, basement membrane collagens such as type IV are localized to the gonad and are required for normal tissue integrity and development, indicating that collagens provide essential structural and signaling support within reproductive tissues [68, 69]. The reduction in reproductive span following collagen knockdown therefore likely reflects a loss of gonadal architecture and a compromised oocyte microenvironment, both of which are critical for maintaining oocyte quality and sustained fertility during aging. Similar principles are observed in mammals, where ECM composition and collagen remodeling within the ovary influence follicle health and age-associated decline in reproductive potential [28, 70]. Importantly, the pronounced effects under reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling (rIIS) suggest that gonadal collagens act at the interface of local tissue architecture and systemic longevity pathways. IIS and related signaling cascades are known regulators of reproductive aging in C. elegans, influencing germline maintenance and oocyte quality [10, 71]. The minimal impact of non-gonadal collagens further underscores the tissue-specific role of gonadal ECM in reproductive maintenance, consistent with broader evidence that collagen expression is dynamically regulated with age and directly influences longevity outcomes [72]. Together, these results support a model in which the somatic gonadal ECM preserves germline health and buffers the reproductive system against age-associated decline, thereby positioning collagens as critical modulators of reproductive lifespan.

Although the collagens identified in this study belong predominantly to the expanded col- gene family in C. elegans, understanding how these proteins relate to evolutionarily conserved extracellular matrix components is essential for interpreting their broader biological significance. While most C. elegans col- genes encode nematode-specific cuticle collagens without direct one-to-one orthology to vertebrate fibrillar collagens, they form a structurally and functionally important ECM network that maintains tissue integrity [73, 74]. Only a limited subset, such as emb-9 and let-2 (type IV–like) and cle-1 (type XVIII–like), show clear structural conservation with mammalian basement membrane collagens [75]. Importantly, despite structural divergence, collagen remodeling and ECM homeostasis emerge as conserved determinants of tissue maintenance and aging across species [76]. Thus, although direct subclassification of C. elegans col- genes according to human collagen types is not feasible, the underlying biological principle that regulated ECM composition preserves tissue architecture and reproductive capacity appears evolutionarily conserved.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) plays a critical role in maintaining tissue architecture and facilitating paracrine signaling in both nematodes and mammalian gonads. In humans, ECM remodeling within the ovarian follicle is essential for cumulus expansion, oocyte maturation, and follicle rupture [28]. Studies of aging human ovaries have revealed that extracellular matrix remodeling within ovarian follicles is associated with increased matrix rigidity and altered collagen cross-linking, which disrupts cell–matrix interactions and contributes to impaired follicular function [77]. While these changes are typically associated with reproductive decline, our findings in C. elegans reveal a contrasting yet complementary perspective. In daf-2 mutants, which exhibit reduced insulin signaling and delayed reproductive aging, we observed elevated expression of specific collagen genes in gonadal sheath cells. Rather than promoting fibrotic rigidity, this upregulation could maintain ECM homeostasis and preserve the structural integrity of the gonadal environment during aging [78]. These findings suggest that the impact of collagen on reproductive function is not simply a matter of abundance but rather of regulated expression and context-specific remodeling. In daf-2 mutants, increased collagen expression may reflect a protective, TFs–mediated program that sustains tissue elasticity and support rather than pathological stiffening, as observed in aged human ovaries [79]. Thus, our data support a model in which the maintenance of a functional extracellular matrix, rather than its overaccumulation or degradation, is critical for preserving oocyte quality and delaying reproductive aging.

An intriguing finding from our study is that gonad-specific collagens predominantly affect reproductive span rather than brood size (Figs. 3 and 4). It is important to note that all reproductive assays measured self-fertility, which is limited by both self-sperm availability and oocyte quality. We cannot exclude that self-sperm quality contributes to the observed phenotypes; mated reproductive span assays in the future would help distinguish somatic gonadal from sperm-related contributions. This distinction parallels observations in humans, where oocyte quality, rather than quantity, is the major determinant of middle-aged female fertility loss [80]. Aging females often exhibit a sufficient ovarian reserve yet produce poor-quality oocytes with meiotic and chromosomal anomalies. The selective impact of collagens on oocyte integrity without dramatically affecting early fecundity suggests that the somatic ECM functions as a longevity buffer, extending the window of reproductive competence. This concept is reinforced by our observation that collagens expressed in nongonadal somatic tissues do not exhibit similar protective effects on oocyte structure, further emphasizing the specificity of the gonadal niche.

The collagens identified in our study are predominantly expressed in the somatic region of the gonads rather than in the germline or gametes. The minimal overlap of NoGn collagens with gonadal datasets further supports the tissue-specific enrichment of ShGn collagens. Together, these observations indicate that the identified ShGn collagens form a unique set associated with the somatic part of the gonads, reinforcing the reliability and reproducibility of our dataset (Fig. 5). Furthermore, we found that somatic collagens in sheath cells, which interface with oocytes through gap junctions like those in human granulosa cells, are indispensable for maintaining the gonadal structure. Their knockdown led to accelerated oocyte morphological decline (Fig. 5C & S6). Importantly, these effects are more pronounced under aging conditions, reinforcing the notion that the somatic ECM is a limiting factor in reproductive longevity. Moreover, many cuticle-associated collagen genes in C. elegans are known to act redundantly, particularly in epidermal morphogenesis. Our findings suggest that gonad-enriched collagens under rIIS conditions may play nonredundant and essential roles in maintaining the reproductive span. This context-specific requirement indicates that functional redundancy among collagens is not universal [81], and tissue-specific collagen networks may exhibit hierarchical or bottleneck dependencies.

Transcription factors knockdown accelerates age-associated gonadal degeneration, yet simultaneous depletion through combined RNAi does not exacerbate the phenotype beyond individual knockdowns. This lack of additivity suggests that these factors operate within a shared protective network rather than in independent, parallel pathways. DAF-16/FOXO, SKN-1/NRF2, and HSF-1 are well-established regulators of stress resistance and proteostasis, and previous studies including ours indicate that their transcriptional programs converge on overlapping sets of target genes involved in antioxidant defense, protein homeostasis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [56, 82–84]. Thus, loss of any one factor may compromise a critical node of this network, lowering gonadal resilience to a threshold beyond which additional depletion does not produce further decline. A parallel non-additive effect was observed in our collagen knockdown experiments as mentioned above, where pooled RNAi against multiple collagen genes did not worsen reproductive phenotypes relative to individual knockdowns. Integrating these findings, we propose that transcription factors downstream of reduced IIS maintain gonadal integrity, at least in part, by regulating ECM homeostasis through collagen expression. DAF-16, SKN-1, and HSF-1 have all been implicated in extracellular matrix regulation. Our data support a model in which these transcription factors converge on a collagen-centered protective program that preserves gonadal architecture during aging. Loss of either the upstream regulatory nodes (daf-16, skn-1, hsf-1) or downstream structural effectors (specific collagens) results in accelerated degeneration, and combined perturbations do not intensify the phenotype because each alone is sufficient to collapse the protective network. Recent endogenous tagging studies in C. elegans provide a framework to directly assess collagen localization and abundance in vivo [85]. Comparing tagged collagens in wild-type and daf-2 mutants would help clarify how IIS-dependent transcriptional regulation translates into structural ECM remodeling within the gonad. Our findings establish a functional and regulatory link between longevity signaling and collagen expression; future spatial analyses will further refine the underlying mechanism.

To contextualize these findings, we developed a model of transcriptional regulation that highlights the roles of DAF-16, SKN-1, and HSF-1 in somatic gonad-specific collagen expression during development and aging (Fig. 8). This model illustrates how, in early development, DAF-16, SKN-1 and HSF-1 regulate collagen expression to support the structural integrity of reproductive tissues. Notably, in aged wild-type animals, collagen expression decreases, and the regulatory influence of DAF-16, SKN-1, and HSF-1 may decrease, which correlates with compromised oocyte and embryo quality and reproductive capacity. In contrast, aged daf-2 mutants sustain collagen expression through persistent TFs–mediated regulation, thus maintaining the sheath cell structure as well as oocyte and embryo quality. This tissue-specific regulatory rewiring suggests that collagens may act as a nexus where longevity pathways intersect with reproductive aging. TFs-driven regulation of gonadal collagens could represent an adaptive mechanism to extend reproductive capacity under conditions of reduced IIS, ensuring that reproductive aging is delayed in alignment with somatic longevity. Such fine-tuned control may help reconcile the apparent trade-off between lifespan and reproduction by prioritizing gonadal structural resilience.

Transcriptional regulation of somatic gonad-specific collagen expression by DAF-16, SKN-1, and HSF-1 during development and aging. Upper panel (Early development): Proper expression of gonad-specific collagens during early development supports the structural integrity of sheath cells and oocytes, promoting reproductive tissue health. In wild-type animals, SKN-1 and HSF-1 are known to regulate collagen expression, whereas the role of DAF-16 remains unclear. Under reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling (rIIS), both DAF-16 and SKN-1 contribute to collagen regulation, but the involvement of HSF-1 is unknown. Left panel (aged wild-type): In aged wild-type animals, collagen expression is diminished, potentially compromising the structure of reproductive tissues and oocyte quality. The regulatory mechanisms underlying this age-related decline are unclear, as DAF-16, SKN-1, and HSF-1 exhibit minimal regulatory influence during advanced aging, and their roles in collagen regulation at this stage are unknown (indicated by question marks). Right panel (aged daf-2 mutants): In contrast, daf-2 mutants maintain collagen expression at advanced ages, largely due to the sustained activity of key transcription factors downstream of rIIS. This maintenance supports sheath cell structure and oocyte quality

While our study highlights the importance of collagen-mediated niche maintenance, it remains unclear whether these collagens function purely as structural elements or also modulate cell signaling. Recent studies in C. elegans and mice suggest that ECM components can directly influence stem cell behavior and niche signaling via integrins and mechanosensitive pathways [86, 87]. Future work is necessary to explore the translational relevance of these collagen findings in mammalian reproductive systems. In mouse models of PCOS, increased expression of Col1a1, Col1a2, and Col3a1 in ovarian stromal cells correlates with fibrosis and altered ovarian architecture [88]. TGF-β–driven stromal collagen deposition similarly underlies the characteristic fibrotic stroma in PCOS-affected ovaries [89]. In human ovarian tissue, collagen I and III form organized stromal layers and collagen IV localizes to the follicular basal lamina, underscoring the dynamic ECM remodeling during ovulation [90]. Moreover, in endometrial models, miR-29c–mediated downregulation of COL4A1 impairs epithelial adhesion—highlighting how collagen IV can influence reproductive tissue function and fertility [91]. Regulation of COL4A1 by FOXL2 in granulosa cells further illustrates its role in follicle development and niche stability [92]. In a POI model induced by cyclophosphamide in rats, the ovarian cortex and medulla exhibited markedly reduced collagen content throughout follicular regions, as assessed by Masson’s trichrome staining. This ECM disruption—including likely loss of collagen IV—parallels impaired follicular development [93]. Together, these studies support the notion that dysregulated collagen expression and ECM remodeling represent conserved mechanisms affecting reproductive health across species.

Conclusion

Given the increasing trend of delayed childbearing and the increasing incidence of ovarian disorders such as polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), both of which compromise oocyte quality, our findings underscore the importance of understanding the molecular mechanisms that safeguard oocyte integrity. Elucidating these pathways is crucial for developing strategies to delay reproductive aging and improve fertility outcomes. The current study emphasized that somatic gonad-specific collagens are essential for preserving reproductive health during aging in Caenorhabditis elegans. Through detailed transcriptomic and functional analyses, we showed that reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling (rIIS) sustains the expression of a specific set of collagen genes in somatic gonad sheath cells. These collagens help maintain the structural integrity of the gonadal environment and support oocyte quality as an organism age. Our findings demonstrate that the regulation of these collagens is tissue specific in the gonads under rIIS conditions. Functional experiments using RNAi confirmed that depletion of these collagen genes or TFs upstream of these collagens in the somatic gonad accelerated the age-related decline in oocyte and gonadal morphology, highlighting their role in reproductive longevity. Together, these results underscore the importance of extracellular matrix components in the aging gonad and establish a mechanistic link between insulin signaling, somatic collagen expression, and reproductive maintenance. These parallels with mammalian ovarian biology suggest that targeting ECM homeostasis and local IIS signaling may hold promise for mitigating age-associated fertility decline in women.
