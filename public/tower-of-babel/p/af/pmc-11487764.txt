Predictive value of stress hyperglycemia ratio on one-year mortality in chronic kidney disease patients admitted to intensive care unit
Authors: Shuoyan An, Zixiang Ye, Wuqiang Che, Yanxiang Gao, Jingyi Ren, Jiahui Li, Jingang Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR) reflects the acute blood glucose variation in critically ill conditions. However, its prognostic value in chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains understudied. This study aimed to investigate the association between SHR and one-year mortality in CKD patients hospitalized in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU).MethodsPatients with diagnosis of CKD in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database were enrolled. Incidence of all-cause mortality within one-year follow-up was used as the primary endpoint.Results1825 CKD patients were included in the study. A “U-shaped” relationship between SHR and one-year mortality as identified using multivariate restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. Then study population were categorized into three groups: Group 1 (SHR < 0.70), Group 2 (0.70 ≤ SHR ≤ 0.95) and Group 3 (SHR > 0.95). Group 2 showed significantly better one-year outcomes compared to the other two groups (p = 0.0031). This survival benefit persisted across subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, CKD stage, anemia and various clinical conditions.ConclusionSHR proved to be a meaningful biomarker for predicting one-year mortality in ICU-admitted CKD patients, with a “U-shaped” correlation. The identification of the optimal SHR range (0.70–0.95) provided clinicians with a valuable tool for detecting high-risk populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-024-03823-4.Keywords: Stress hyperglycemia Ratio1, Chronic kidney disease2, Mortality3

Background

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) showed a notable increase in recent years, which was associated with increased cardiovascular adverse events and other morbidities, leading to higher mortality [1]. Critically ill patients were frequently observed to have CKD. When admitted to Intensive Care Unit (ICU), CKD patients had twice risk of death compared with non-CKD patients [2]. Therefore, identifying reliable prognostic markers for CKD patients in ICU settings is crucial.

Stress hyperglycemia, which represented transient elevation of glucose level in acute phase has emerged as a new biomarker correlated with adverse clinical outcomes, particularly in ICU admissions [3]. Several studies have proved that stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) outperforms absolute hyperglycemia as a predictor of mortality in conditions such as coronary artery disease (CAD) and pneumonia [4, 5]. In CKD patients, approximately two thirds were with impaired glucose tolerance and lower insulin sensitivity [6]. Furthermore, glucose metabolism indices were also associated with prognosis in CKD [7]. Based on this background, we hypothesized an association between SHR and clinical outcomes in CKD patients requiring ICU admission due to severe illness. The present study aimed to investigate this association between SHR and one-year all-cause mortality in ICU-admitted CKD patients.

Methods

Data sources

The present study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database [8]. MIMIC-IV was a comprehensive and freely accessible database which contained de-identified electronic health record data from patients admitted to ICU in Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, Massachusetts) during 2008 to 2019. It included following clinical data: demographics, diagnosis of International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, physiological indicators, laboratory results and treatment. Moreover, the MIMIC database included comprehensive data on death status and date of death, derived from hospital and state records, using a custom rule-based algorithm that incorporates name, date of birth, and social security number. One author (ASY, certification number: 39674606) was certificated to access this database and obtained the data required for the study. Our study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration. Since information in this database were unidentified, there was no requirement for patient informed consent.

Study population and definition

Patients who diagnosed with CKD and without missing data of admission blood glucose (ABG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were included. Younger than 18 years old, hospitalized for less than 6 h and with more than 30% missing data were exclusion criteria. Only the first admission was included for patients with multiple admissions. Demographic data, comorbidities, lab results, vital signs, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score and follow-up outcomes were obtained from MIMIC-IV database by pgAdmin PostgreSQL tools (version 1.22.1). The first result of blood glucose after ICU admission was used as the ABG. If HbA1c was tested more than once, the first was used to compute the SHR. The calculation formula of SHR was as follows: [(ABG (mg/dl))/(28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7)] [5].

Statistical analysis

Multivariate restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analysis was employed to visualize the relationship between SHR and one-year mortality, using 5 knots. Then participants were categorized to different groups. Categorical variables were represented using numbers and percentages. and compared by Fisher’s exact probability method. The continuous variables were summarized using the median and interquartile ranges and assessed by Wilcoxon rank sum test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to identify predictors of mortality, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 included the variables from Model 2, plus additional confounders that either demonstrated statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariate regression or were deemed clinically important. Subgroup analysis was conducted according to different baseline characteristics with the interaction P value calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted by R (version 4.1.3, Austria). P-value less than 0.05 (two-sided) was defined as indicative of a statistically significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1815 patients with CKD were included. Figure 1 showed the screening process. The mean age was 73 [64.00, 81.00] years old, and male accounted for 64.2%. The proportion of advanced CKD with eGFR < 30 ml/min/1.73m2 was 34.1%. During the one-year follow-up, 509 patients died. According to the multivariate RCS analysis, SHR exhibited a “U-shaped” association with one-year mortality (Fig. 2). Subsequently, we divided the study population into three groups: Group 1 (SHR < 0.70), Group 2 (0.70 ≤ SHR ≤ 0.95), and Group 3 (SHR > 0.95). Baseline characteristics and the hospital mortality between the three groups were shown in Table 1 and supplementary Table 1. Group 2 had a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation (AF), while heart failure (HF) was less prevalent. Patients in Group 1 were younger and had the highest proportion of diabetes. Group 3 had more patients receiving renal replacement therapy (RRT). White blood cell (WBC) counts and admission glucose levels were higher in Group 3. Vital sign parameters and SOFA scores did not differ significantly among the three groups. More patients in the Group 1 and Group 3 deceased during hospitalization (p = 0.0028).

Follow-up results

After one-year follow-up, 509 patients (28.0%) died, including 68 (31.9%) in Group 1, 119 (22.6%) in Group 2 and 322 (29.9%) in Group 3, respectively. Kaplan-Meier curves of the three groups were shown in Fig. 3. Group 2 patients (0.70 ≤ SHR ≤ 0.95) showed lower mortality rates compared to those in Group 1 (SHR < 0.70) and Group 3 (SHR > 0.95), with a significant difference observed (p = 0.0031). SHR between 0.70 and 0.95 emerged as an independent predictor of lower mortality in univariate Cox regression analysis (Model 1, Table 2). This association remained significant after adjusting for confounding factors in both Model 2 and Model 3 (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between SHR and one-year mortality across different demographic and clinical conditions. As shown in supplementary Table 2, no significant interactions were observed among different subgroups, including age, gender, CKD stage, anemia (hemoglobin < 11 g/dL), hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and sepsis. Given the greater impact of CKD stage 3 and above on poor prognosis, we retained patients in this stage (totaling 1,601 patients, accounting for 88.2% of the overall CKD study population) for further analysis. The findings were consistent with those of the entire CKD study population. Among patients with CKD stage 3 and above, SHR also demonstrated a U-shaped relationship with one-year all-cause mortality (Supplementary Fig. 1a). When SHR was divided into three groups using cutoff values of 0.75 and 0.90, patients with SHR between 0.70 and 0.95 exhibited lower one-year mortality rates compared to the other two groups (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, SHR between 0.70 and 0.95 remained independently associated with one-year mortality (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Stress hyperglycemia, an indicator of inflammatory and neuro-hormonal derangements during acute conditions, has been shown to be a more reliable biomarker than admission glucose level and fasting blood glucose [9, 10]. SHR was initially induced by Roberts, G. W et al. to reflect the stress hyperglycemia response [9]. Subsequently, SHR has proven to be predictive in various medical conditions including CHD, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), pulmonary infection, congestive heart failure (CHF), and cerebrovascular disease [4, 5, 11]. In our study, we used a large public database and investigated the correlation between SHR and prognosis in ICU patients with CKD. Our findings revealed a “U-shaped” association between SHR and one-year all-cause mortality, with SHR between 0.70 and 0.95 demonstrating more favorable clinical outcomes.

Previous studies extensively explored the relationship between SHR and CAD. In the COACT registry study, SHR was divided into four quartiles, with the highest quartiles showing a 31% higher likelihood of major adverse cardiovascular events [12]. Additionally, SHR showed a “U-shaped” association with long-term clinical outcome in ACS [13] and could improve the prognostic accuracy of TIMI and GRACE risk scores [14, 15]. Moreover, SHR correlated with CAD severity [16] and extent of thrombus burden [17].

Besides CAD, SHR was also correlated with various other clinical conditions. In the ACROSS-China study, patients in the highest SHR quartile exhibited a twofold increased risk of stroke recurrence [18]. Additionally, SHR was positively correlated with hemorrhagic transformation in acute ischemic stroke [19]. Among individuals with both CHF and type 2 diabetes, a “U-shaped” association was observed between SHR and the occurrence of composite cardiac events [11]. In a study using MIMIC database, SHR demonstrated a J-shaped correlation with ventricular arrhythmia (VA) where SHR > 1.31 was independently associated with VA in ICU patients [3]. Higher SHR was also linked to an increased risk of pulmonary infection in STEMI patients undergoing PCI [5]. Cui et al. identified cutoff values of 1.08 and 1.20 for risk stratification in AMI patients with and without diabetes [20]. Furthermore, Gao et al. categorized patients based on SHR tertile, and found that higher SHR independently correlated with increased mortality in myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries [21]. Recently, Le L et al. [22] reported a U-shaped relationship between SHR and one-year mortality using the MIMIC database across all ICU patients. In contrast, our study focused specifically on CKD patients, confirming the predictive role of SHR in this population and identifying the optimal SHR range associated with favorable prognosis.

CKD patients were particularly susceptible to developing dysglycemia. Increasing evidence suggested that kidney could act as a gluconeogenic organ, producing approximately 20% of endogenously derived glucose [23]. In the context of type 2 diabetes, kidney insulin resistance was also observed [24]. In CKD patients, both gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance were prevalent. With exacerbation of kidney function, metabolic acidosis and inflammation worsen, exacerbating glucose metabolic disorders [25]. During critical illness in CKD patients, heightened sympathetic nervous system activity and a surge in inflammatory factors occur. These inflammatory mediators can intensify insulin resistance and contribute to hyperglycemia by regulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and increasing hepatic gluconeogenesis [26, 27]. Hyperglycemia further stimulated more release of inflammatory factors, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein [28], creating a vicious cycle of hyperglycemia and inflammation that ultimately lead to adverse clinical outcomes. Given the intertwined nature of glucose and inflammatory system, SHR might partly reflect the acute stress response in CKD patients admitted to the ICU. This could elucidate the association between high SHR and poor clinical outcome.

In contrast, some patients in severe conditions might develop hypoglycemia, leading low SHR level. Hypoglycemia in these cases could be attributed to malnutrition, digestive impairment and adrenal insufficiency, all of which contributed to higher mortality rate [11]. However, a notable finding from our study was a higher proportion of diabetic patients in the lowest SHR group, consistent with previous research [13]. Diabetic patients often used antidiabetic medications that might not be appropriately adjusted during critical illness or in response to dietary changes, potentially resulting in hypoglycemic events and poorer prognoses.

HbA1C resulted from glucose binding non-enzymatically to hemoglobin throughout the red blood cell’s lifespan, reflecting average glucose levels over the past three months in the general population. However, in CKD patients, assessing glycemia via HbA1C is complicated by various CKD-related factors such as anemia, use of erythropoietin-stimulating agents, shortened erythrocyte lifespan due to uremia, and erythrocyte loss during hemodialysis, all of which can lead to lower-than-expected HbA1C levels [29]. Factors like sickle cell trait, oxidative stress, inflammation, and acidosis further contributed to these complexities [30]. Therefore, SHR may be overestimated in CKD patients. Considering these factors, we conducted subgroup analyses based on eGFR and hemoglobin levels. The results indicated no interaction between different subgroups based on eGFR and hemoglobin levels, which supported the reliability of our findings.

There were a few limitations in our study. Firstly, it was a retrospective analysis based on data obtained from a single center’s public database, which inevitably caused a selection bias. Second, CKD patients with missing data of ABG or HbA1C were excluded, potentially biasing the results since patients without diabetes were less likely to test these parameters. Thirdly, the specific causes of CKD were unclear, making it uncertain whether SHR had meaningful association with different etiologies, such as chronic nephritis, diabetes, and autoimmune diseases, among others. Fourthly, only all-cause mortality data could be extracted from the database, and specific causes of death were not studied.

Conclusion

SHR exhibited a “U-shaped” correlation with one-year all-cause mortality in ICU-admitted patients with CKD, indicating a lower risk of death within the SHR range of 0.70–0.95. SHR served as an effective prognostic predictor for critically ill CKD patients. Both high SHR (> 0.95) and low SHR (< 0.70) identify a high-risk population with increased mortality, necessitating more aggressive treatment of the underlying disease and close monitoring during hospitalization.
