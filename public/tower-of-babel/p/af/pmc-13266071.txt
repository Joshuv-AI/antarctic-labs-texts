Cellular heterogeneity in hypertrophic burn scars in response to carbon dioxide laser therapy
Authors: Yung-Yi Chen, Christopher Mahony, Jason Turner, Charlotte G Smith, Abdulrazak Abdulsalam, Ezekwe Amirize, Amberley Prince, Adrian Heagerty, Claudia Roberts, Adam Croft, Yvonne Wilson, Naiem Moiemen, Janet M Lord
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAblative fractional carbon dioxide (AFCO2) laser therapy is used for treating pathological scarring. However, mechanisms underlying reduction in hypertrophic scarring are poorly understood.MethodsWe investigated cellular mechanisms of AFCO2 laser therapy by performing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on skin biopsies from burn survivors with hypertrophic scars before and after three sessions of AFCO2 therapy. Scar reduction was assessed subjectively and objectively.ResultsThose with a good response (GR) to laser therapy have scars less than 6 years from injury, whereas poor responders (PR) have scars over 6 years since injury. ScRNA-seq analysis of skin biopsies reveals that genes enriched in GR are associated with extracellular matrix and structure organisation (COL14A1, POSTN, SPARC); whereas genes enriched in PR are related to enhanced immune inflammatory responses (CXCL14, JUN, TNC). Notably, expression of the pro-fibrotic gene Engrailed-1 (EN1) remains elevated in PR scars compared to GR. The ECM-regulatory gene TIMP-1 (Tissue Inhibitor of Metalloproteinases 1) is also significantly upregulated in PR scars following treatment relative to GR scars, although at the protein level TIMP1 levels decrease in PR but increase in GR after therapy. The regenerative-associated gene, TRPS1 (Transcriptional Repressor GATA Binding 1) expression demonstrates opposing regulation post treatment, with upregulation observed in GR scars but downregulation in PR scars, underscoring distinct transcriptional trajectories associated with differential therapeutic outcomes. Finally, distinct intercellular communication networks and differentiation trajectories are observed after AFCO2, with regenerative mesenchymal fibroblasts predominating in GR but inflammatory fibroblasts associated with PR.ConclusionsWe conclude AFCO2 laser therapy is more effective if done early after injury and distinct fibroblast recruitment is associated with a good response, specifically regenerative fibroblasts.Subject terms: Cell biology, Medical research

Introduction

Burn injury is one of the most common traumas worldwide, with an estimated 11 million people requiring medical attention each year1. The impact of burn injuries is clinically significant because they often give rise to exuberant scarring that results in permanent physical function loss and psychological issues due to the stigma of disfigurement. Scarring post-burn occurs in 32–72% of patients2, and more than 100,000 patients develop problematic hypertrophic scarring (HTS) each year in the UK alone.

In clinical practice, several modalities are commonly used to treat post-burn HTS, such as topical emollients, compression garments, intra-lesional steroid injections or even the use of anti-neoplastic drugs such as fluorouracil and bleomycin3–6. Laser therapy was developed in the 1980s and has been used for treating HTS with minimum side effects but variable positive outcomes7,8. There are three main types of laser therapy being used for treating established scars9–12. The 585 and 595 nm pulsed-dye lasers (PDL) are used to selectively target blood vessels in the scar tissue and reduce scar erythema8,13. Q-switched neodymium -yttrium- aluminium-garnet (Q-switched Nd:YAG) lasers emit light with a wavelength of 1064 nm, have deeper tissue penetration and are commonly used to improve atrophic acne scars and correct pigmentation in burn scars14. Ablative fractional carbon dioxide (AFCO2) laser therapy induces haemostasis and stimulates fibroblasts to produce new collagens14–19. It functions by emitting a laser beam at a wavelength of 10,600nm, whose energy is quickly absorbed by water, which then evaporates to induce tissue damage and break down scar matrix. According to the 2020 International Consensus Recommendations by a panel of 26 dermatologists and plastic and reconstructive surgeons, lasers are now considered a first-line therapy for managing traumatic scars20.

Clinical evidence has shown that AFCO2 treatment is an effective way to treat HTS. Miletta et al. showed that AFCO2 laser therapy provides significant, sustained improvement of elasticity, thickness, appearance, and symptoms of mature HTS in adults21. Patel et al. further demonstrated that AFCO2 laser therapy improved hypertrophic burn scars in paediatric patients22. Laubach et al. explored the biological response of human skin to fractional photothermolysis (FP) treatment and found that FP stimulates epidermal regeneration, increases collagen production, and promotes the removal of epidermal melanin through microscopic epidermal necrotic debris, suggesting FP induces a wound healing response in the dermis23. However, despite being an effective approach to alleviate HTS with positive results overall, clinical outcomes remain variable, and the detailed mechanisms of action behind AFCO2 laser therapy remain unclear24,25.

Wound healing is a multifactorial, long-term biological process involving four major phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and tissue remodelling, with skin fibroblasts playing a major role. Variation in this process can potentially lead to alternative outcomes, such as HTS. Recent studies have shown that dermal fibroblasts are highly heterogeneous post-wounding and the diversity of fibroblasts at the wound site contributes to skin repair and regeneration, which ultimately impacts on scarring outcomes15–19. One fibroblast subtype, the Engrailed 1 (En1) expressing cells, was found to be associated with scarring in mice20. Moreover, inflammation-associated fibroblasts, characterised by their ability to produce and respond to inflammatory cytokines, have been linked to fibrosis and pathogenesis in several chronic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease21. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the complex biological mechanisms underlying laser therapy to reduce hypertrophic scarring in humans, specifically examining the cellular infiltrate at the treated scar site that may contribute to the variable scar reduction outcomes after AFCO2 laser therapy.

We show that AFCO2 laser therapy is more effective in patients whose scars are less than 6 years old. In addition, scRNAseq analysis of skin biopsy before and after laser therapy shows differential transcriptomic responses and distinct recruitment of fibroblast and immune cells in good and poor responders, with the former showing a more regenerative profile and the latter more inflammatory.

Methods

Study participants and study schedule

Adults with established hypertrophic scars following a burn injury were recruited to the study. As numerous confounding factors could potentially influence treatment outcomes, to mitigate variability and ensure consistency, we adopted a comprehensive list of inclusion and exclusion criteria for patient recruitment, based on previously established recruitment criteria (Supplementary Table 1)22. Participants gave their written informed consent, and the study was approved by the North of Scotland Research Ethics Committee, REC ref. 19/NS/0125). They received 3 sessions of AFCO2 laser treatments, at 3-month intervals over a 12-month period. During the study, various validated scar assessment procedures were used to evaluate scar characteristics before each treatment and at the endpoint (baseline, M3, M6, Y1). Skin biopsy samples were collected from participants before treatment (baseline), and 3 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year after the 1st laser treatment22. Additionally, blood samples were collected (baseline, week 3, M3, M6, Y1) and analysed using the Sysmex XT1000 to monitor immune system activity throughout the study.

AFCO2 settings

The Lumenis® UltraPulse® CO2 laser device with DeepFXTM and/or SCAARFXTM headpiece was used. The treatment included a single pass of the following settings: (1) SCAARFX™: Energy: 110-150mJ, density: 3%, shape: 2, size: 10, pulses 1; and (2) DeepFX TM: Energy: 17.5-20.0mJ, density 5%, shape: 2, size: 10, pulses: 1. Repeat rate 0.5 seconds; Frequency 300 Hz; no active cooling was used during the treatment.

Clinical Scar assessment measures

Clinical scar parameters, such as skin thickness, pliability, vascularity, and pigmentation, were obtained from participants before each laser treatment and at the 12-month endpoint to evaluate treatment outcomes. Assessments used to collect scar parameters include the modified Vancouver Scar Scale (mVSS), Dermascan® ultrasound, Cutometer, and DSMIII® Colourimeter. In addition, Vectra 3D photography and POSAS-O were used as auxiliary measures.

Modified Vancouver Scar Scale (mVSS)

An adapted version of the modified Vancouver Scar Scale (mVSS) was used to evaluate the clinical efficacy of the laser treatment23–25. The mVSS is a numerical assessment for skin characteristics, including pliability (range, 0-5), height (range, 0-3), vascularity (range, 0–3), and pigmentation (range, 0–3). The mVSS of each patient was individually assessed by an experienced observer who was blinded to the study. The accessor scores each characteristic based on the difference between the site of evaluation and the normal skin.

Dermascan® high-frequency ultrasound

Dermascan C® USB (Cortex Technology ApS, Denmark)26 provides 20mHz B-scanning at 60 ×150 micron resolution, with 13 mm penetration27, which allows the visualisation of soft tissue at high resolution, providing a measurement of the skin thickness possible. Skin thickness is defined as the distance between the stratum corneum and the inner surface of the burn scar in the dermis.

Cutometer® skin elasticity metre

The Cutometer® skin elasticity metre (MPA 580, Courage and Khazaka GmbH, Germany)23,28 is used to evaluate the skin elasticity parameters, measuring the vertical deformation of skin in millimetres (mm) when pulled by a controlled vacuum into a circular aperture. Skin elasticity is expressed as absolute parameters (Ua, Ue, Uf, Ur, and Uv) or relative parameters (R-, F-, and Q-parameters)29,30. R0 and R2 were used in this study: R0 (=Uf) was used to describe the stiffness (pliability/firmness) of the skin by measuring the maximum of kinematic deformation. R2 (=Ua/Uf) was used to describe the visco-elasticity of the skin by measuring the ratio of final retraction and maximum deformation.

DSMIII colorimeter®

DSMIII colorimeter measures the colour feature of the skin31. It combines two colour system readings into one measurement: narrow-band spectrophotometry (melanin and erythema) and tristimulus reflectance colorimetry (CIE L*a*b* system, CIELAB), which allows us to quantitatively measure the skin vascularity (erythema index), pigmentation (melanin index), and colour shift during the AFCO2 therapy over time.

Patient observer scar assessment scale—observer (POSAS-O)

The POSAS-O measures the quality of the scars by evaluating six dimensions, including pain, itchiness, pliability, thickness, colour, and irregularity from observers’ perspectives. Each dimension was scored from scale 1 to 10 based on the differences between the site of evaluation and the normal skin, with 10 indicating the largest difference. Additionally, the observer records their overall opinion score, from 1 to 10, regardless of the total score across the six evaluation dimensions.

Human skin biopsy samples

5 mm skin punch biopsy samples were obtained from participants undergoing AFCO2 laser treatment (baseline, week 3, month 6, and Y1). All biopsies collected were processed immediately and bisected, with one half transferred to 10% neutral buffered formalin for downstream histological and immunohistochemical assessments, and the other half was fresh frozen in CS10 CryoStor® cell cryopreservation media at −80 °C for future analysis using single cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) to determine cellular heterogeneity and transcriptomic differences in the scars.

Histological and immunohistochemical staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded skin biopsy sections from two good and two poor responders across all time points were subjected to histochemical and immunohistochemical staining. Masson Trichrome Blue (RRSK20-100, Atom Scientific, UK) was used to assess collagen organisation, and sections were independently evaluated by two dermatologists for structural comparison. Immunohistochemistry was performed with antibodies against collagen I (ab88147, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), collagen III (ab23445, Abcam), and TIMP1 (ab216432, Abcam), followed by DAB detection. Secondary antibody–only controls were included to assess background staining. All stained sections were scanned using a Zeiss AxioScan Z1 slide scanner (Zeiss, Germany), and quantification of collagen I, collagen III, TIMP1, and controls was performed in QuPath by measuring the percentage of DAB-positive area after background subtraction.

scRNAseq analysis

Skin biopsy samples (n = 16) were thawed and washed with Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) supplemented with 5% foetal calf serum (FCS) once before incubating with 1 mg/ml collagenase P (Sigma Aldrich, UK) and 100μg/ml DNase I (Sigma Aldrich, UK) for 3.5 h at 37 °C in a humidified CO2 incubator. Cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Dissociated cells were then labelled with live and dead dye before live cell sorting using a BD LSR FORTESSA™ X-20 Cell Analyser (BD Biosciences, USA). Single cells were captured and barcoded using 10× Chromium platform and the sequencing libraries were prepared following manufacturer’s instructions using dual index Chromium Next GEM G Single Cell 3’ kit v3.1 (10×Gemonics, California, USA). Libraries were quantified using an Agilent Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA chip and pooled together to get similar numbers of reads from every single cell before sequencing. Overall, approximately 50,000 cells per sample were used for constructing libraries and sequenced using Novogene single-cell RNA sequencing services. Using Cell Ranger pipeline v.3.1.0 (10× Genomics), raw sequencing data were aligned to the hg38 reference genome and converted into a cell–feature matrix. This was followed by the unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting. In addition to 16 skin scar samples, 3 healthy control skin samples (age 44–67 years) sequencing data were integrated into our database for downstream analysis. We filtered out cells that had unique feature gene counts of less than 200 and cells that were contaminated with more than 15% mitochondrial counts. Transformed data were processed and refined before further data visualisation and differential analysis using Seurat (Version 4.3.1)32, Monocle 2 (version 2.18.0)33, and CellChatDB (v.3.0)34 within the R programming environment. Full details of the analysis can be found in the online data supplement.

Data analysis

Single-cell processing and clustering

Following initial quality control processing, cells were clustered by constructing a k-nearest neighbour (KNN) graph using Euclidean distances in principal component space (PCA). This was subsequently converted to a shared nearest neighbour (SNN) graph to identify densely connected communities. Clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm to optimise modularity, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for visualisation based on selected principal components and cluster assignments.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication networks were inferred using CellChatDB (v3.0). Ligand-receptor interactions were mapped to predict signalling pathways involved in cellular crosstalk. Key communication patterns were visualised to highlight the functional relationships between distinct cell populations.

Pseudobulk gene expression analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were aggregated at the sample level by summing raw gene counts across biological replicates. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, treating each replicate as an independent sample to enhance statistical robustness. Normalisation and batch correction were applied to mitigate the effects of sequencing depth and sample composition.

Trajectory inference and pseudotime analysis

Cell differentiation trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle 2 (v2.18.0), based on unsupervised clustering of gene expression dynamics. Cells were ordered along a pseudotime axis to reveal transcriptional changes associated with lineage progression. Differential gene expression along the trajectory was assessed to identify key regulators of cellular state transitions.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.1.0) and R software. Non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank, and Friedman test with multiple comparisons) and parametric tests (one-way ANOVA with post hoc analysis) were used for comparisons. For scar assessment, all patients (n = 14) data were included, with four time points per patient (baseline at day 1 prior to treatment, and post-treatment follow-up at week 3, month 6, and year 1). For scRNAseq, biopsies from two good responders and two poor responders were analysed, each with four time points defined as above. Replicates were defined as independent patient samples collected at each time point. For immunohistochemistry, each biopsy had four sections stained, and the data were averaged for analysis.

Laser treatment is more effective on less-established scars

All participants were given 3 sessions of AFCO2 laser treatment at 3-month intervals. Before each treatment and at the end point at year 1 (Y1), the physical characteristics of the scars were examined using different assessment tools, including quantitative scar assessment devices, such as DermaScan® Ultrasound, the DSMIII® colorimeter, a cutometer, and qualitative scar assessment questionnaires, such as modified Vancouver Scar Scale (mVSS) and the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale version 2.0—Observer’s Scale (POSAS-O). Vectra 3D photography was also used to record the appearance of the scars before and after treatment.

Overall, AFCO2 laser therapy resulted in a significant colour shift (ΔE) between month 3 (M3) and Y1 endpoint (5.93 ± 0.76 vs. 19.64 ± 2.70, p = 0.0005) and between M6 and Y1 (8.82 ± 2.31 vs. 19.64 ± 2.70, p < 0.005). It also produced a significant colorimetric change from baseline to endpoint at Y1, for lightness (43.29 ± 2.88 vs. 59.49 ± 2.43; p < 0.001) and yellowness (11.04 ± 1.10 vs. 8.39 ± 1.46; p < 0.05), Supplementary Fig. 1. However, other scar assessment comparisons between baseline and Y1 endpoint, including mVSS overall score (5.14 ± 0.31 vs. 4.64 ± 0.29), skin thickness (2.64 ± 0.12 mm vs. 2.67 ± 0.19 mm), skin stiffness R0 (0.60 ± 0.07 vs. 0.66 ± 0.08), skin visco-elasticity R2 (0.85 ± 0.02 vs. 0.86 ± 0.02), erythema index (10.88 ± 0.81 vs. 9.27 ± 0.95) and melanin (pigmentation) index (34.02 ± 1.19 vs. 33.72 ± 1.35), failed to reach significance, Table 2.

As our participants’ scars varied greatly in how long they had been established, we considered this a potential factor in laser efficacy. To investigate whether scar age was related to treatment outcomes, we first ranked scar outcomes from the best to worst response (rank 1–14) using the sum of the total scar assessment scores. The overall scores revealed that the scar treatment outcomes were closely correlated with the scar age (r = 0.6974, p = 0.0071) and that the participants with better overall treatment outcomes had younger scars (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Data 1). Therefore, to further identify differences in participant response to treatments, we divided them into those with younger scars, <6 years since wound closure (n = 7), and those with older scars, >6 years since wound closure (n = 7). Demographics of these two groups are shown in Table 1. Our analysis showed that young scars had significantly better scar reduction than old scars in skin thickness (14.12 ± 7.08% vs. −23.00 ± 13.30%, p < 0.05), mVSS overall score (−0.48 ± 5.93% vs. −25.48 ± 8.99%, p < 0.05), and vascularity (−1.88 ± 9.78% vs. −57.29 ± 19.24%, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1c–e and Supplementary Data 1). There was also a trend towards better reduction in pigmentation for younger scars (4.64 ± 3.41% vs. −58.98 ± 5.82%, p = 0.072) (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Data 1), whereas no significant differences were observed in skin stiffness (R0), Supplementary Fig. 2a. Additionally, AFCO2 laser treatment achieved satisfactory colorimetric adjustment, including colour shift (ΔE), for all participants, with no further differences observed between young and old scars (Figs. 1g-j, Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 1).

scRNAseq of skin biopsies reveals differences in cell content associated with differential scar reduction

Hypertrophic scars at different maturation stages may contain factors, such as distinct cell populations and microenvironmental influences, that shape responses to laser treatment. To explore the biological mechanisms potentially underpinning the differences in clinical scar outcomes post-laser therapy, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis (scRNAseq) was carried out on longitudinal full thickness skin biopsies obtained from two patients with young scars (good responder, GR, mean donor age 48 ± 11 years, mean scar age 3.5 ± 0.5 years) and two with old scars (poor responder, PR, mean donor age 43.5 ± 16.5 years, mean scar age 37 ± 19 years), at 4 time points (Day 0, Week 3, Month 6, Year 1). Three healthy control (HC, mean age 54.67 ± 6.49 years) samples were also analysed. In total, 116,759 cells were analysed, with an average of 20,431 reads per cell. Integrating the data from all samples and using unsupervised uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), we identified nine major skin cell types based on established lineage-specific marker genes35–37 from all 3 groups (GR, PR and HC), these were: fibroblasts (PDGFRA+); vascular endothelial cells (VEC; VWF+, SELE+, CLDN5+); lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC; LYVE1+); pericytes (ACTA2+, RGS5+, PDGFRB+); keratinocytes (KRT; KRT14+); melanocytes (Mel; MLANA+); Schwann cells (Schwann; NRXN1+); lymphoid cells (Lymphoid; PTPRC+, CD3E+) and myeloid cells (Myeloid; PTPRC+, CD3E−, HLA-DRA+, CD68+), Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. 5a.

Next, we examined the relative proportions of each cell type across the three groups for pooled biopsy data (GR, PR, and HC). We found that the proportions of fibroblasts and immune cells were much higher in scars compared to healthy control biopsies, consistent with the pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic nature of hypertrophic scar pathogenesis. Compared to HC, both GR and PR exhibited a significantly increased myeloid population. In PR, there was also a higher proportion of lymphoid cells. Additionally, fibroblast abundance was significantly higher in GR, with a more moderate difference in PR relative to HC, Supplementary Fig. 5b and Fig. 5c, d. To take a closer look at the differences in cell frequency in response to laser therapy between GR and PR, we further compared scar samples at baseline (D0) for GR and PR, with pooled post-treatment samples for GR (D21, M6, Y1) and PR (D21, M6, Y1), Fig. 2d. In GR, we found significant increases in immune cell populations (lymphoid and myeloid) after AFCO2, Fig. 2e. In contrast, PR exhibited a modest but not significant increase in pericyte, lymphoid and myeloid cells following AFCO2 treatment, Fig. 2f. Interestingly, the lymphoid population remained consistently elevated in PR compared to GR, both before and after AFCO2 treatment, indicating a sustained state of inflammatory cell infiltration, Supplementary Fig. 5e, f.

Differential response to laser therapy is associated with distinct gene expression

To further delineate the biological differences between GR and PR that may influence their response to AFCO2 treatment, we analysed their gene expression profiles. As shown in the hierarchical clustering heatmap, analysed using Pseudobulk differential expression gene (DEG) software package in R (DESeq2), we found that gene expression profiles in skin samples differed significantly between groups, Supplementary Fig. 5g and Fig. 3a. Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis further revealed that biological pathways including extracellular matrix and structure organisations were significantly enriched in both GR and PR post-AFCO2. Pathways unique to GR scars following AFCO2 (GR Treatment) also included collagen fibril organisation and collagen binding, while pathways unique to PR scars post AFCO2 (PR Treatment) were associated with immune response-activating cell surface receptor signalling pathways and immune response-regulating cell surface receptor signalling pathways (Fig. 3a, b). Genes associated with ECM organisation, such as COL14A1, POSTN, COL1A2, SPARC, KLK4, LUM, and ADAMTS19, were enriched in GR fibroblast populations (Fig. 3c, d). Genes associated with immune activation and regulation, such as SKAP1, ITK, FYB1, HLA-DRB5, HLA-DQA2, HLA-DQA1, CTLA4, MS4A1, KLRC2, KLRC4, TRGC2, BANK1, PLPP4, and GCSAM, were enriched in PR lymphoid and myeloid cell populations, suggesting the continued activation of these immune cells as a crucial factor in driving poorer responses to laser treatment, Fig. 3e, f.

As previous studies suggested that fractional CO2 laser treatment increased type III to type I collagen ratios in mature burn scars38,39, we further examined the expression of COL3A1 and COL1A1 in our scar biopsies. Our data indicate that the overall expression of COL3A1 and COL1A1 is significantly higher in GR compared to PR, however no significant differences were observed in COL3A1-to-COL1A1 ratio before and after treatment, Fig. 3g & i. These data suggest a coordinated regulation of collagen synthesis rather than a selective shift in composition following AFCO2 treatment. Classical wound healing typically follows a sequential pattern, where Collagen I dominates early to provide structural integrity, followed by Collagen III upregulation in later tissue remodelling stages40. However, our findings deviate from this established trajectory, indicating that AFCO2 treatment or intrinsic differences between GR and PR may be influencing collagen regulation in an unconventional manner. As this ratio is known to impact skin stiffness, this may explain why we saw no differences in this parameter after laser therapy in either group. Furthermore, the expression of the pro-fibrotic factor, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP1)41, was also examined. Our analysis revealed a significant increase in TIMP-1 expression in PR post-AFCO2 compared to GR (fold change, 1.52 ± 0.25 vs. 1.025 ± 0.06, p = 0.0379), Fig. 3h, i. We attempted to verify these changes at the protein level by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using biopsies from one PR and one GR across all time points. As tissue was limiting IHC was only done for collagen 1 and III and TIMP1. When comparing baseline (day 1) with the endpoint at year 1, both collagen I and collagen III increase in GR (Supplementary Table 2. However, when baseline is compared with the average of all post-treatment time points (week 3, month 6, year 1), only collagen I increases in GR, while collagen III remains unchanged (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2. In PR, collagen III decreases after treatment in both comparisons, whereas collagen I increases in both comparisons (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2). For TIMP1, the protein expression did not reflect that seen for transcriptomic analysis, with increased expression seen in GR and no difference for PR, though expression in PR was higher at day 1 pretreatment baseline (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2).

Additionally, EN1, LEF1, TRPS1, CDH1, FOXO1, and KLF4 have been associated with mechanisms mediating excessive scarring or regeneration. Therefore, we also compared these gene expressions between GR and PR. Our findings indicate that although AFCO2 treatment significantly reduced EN1 expression in PR scars, it remained substantially higher than in GR scars. Similarly, CDH1 expression decreased significantly following AFCO2 treatment in PR scars, with no significant change observed in GR scars. FOXO1 expression was significantly upregulated, while the KLF4 gene was downregulated across both PR and GR scars post-treatment. Notably, AFCO2 treatment elicited opposite effects on TRPS1 expression, with a significant upregulation in GR scars and downregulation in PR scars after AFCO2 treatment, Supplementary Fig. 5h.

Collectively, these data suggest that differential skin response to laser therapy is associated with distinct gene expression, with fibroblasts and immune cells key populations that may influence different AFCO2 laser treatment outcomes. However, the transcriptomic data need to be confirmed at the protein level. We next went on to further investigate the gene expression patterns of these fibroblast and immune cell populations.

Distinct gene expression patterns identified in fibroblast and immune cell populations from good and poor responders

To further investigate the identified key populations, we compared the gene expression patterns between GR and PR cell populations post-AFCO2. Our analysis revealed that, consistent with our findings in cell populations, the gene expression patterns in the immune cell populations, including lymphoid (n = 11,864 genes) and myeloid (n = 12,586 genes) populations, and fibroblasts (n = 11,279 genes) exhibited the most pronounced differences, Fig. 5a–c and Supplementary Fig. 6. In lymphoid cell populations, the main genes distinguishing the two groups included FKBP5, RUNX2, PDE3B, SESN3, EPSTI1 and LINC01619 in PR and CCL4, PLCG2, S1004A, RPS29 and XCL2 in GR, Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b. In myeloid cell populations, the main genes differing between the two groups included HLA-DQA2, HLA-DRB5, BANK1, CCL4L2, CCL3L1 and LYZ, Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 6c, d. Additionally, key genes that differentiated PR and GR in fibroblast populations included APOD, PHLDA1, MT1X, RGS2, PLA2G2A, ID2, PTGS2, ADAMTS1, HSPA1B, ZFP36L2, JUN, GSN, KLF2, FOS, MGST1, GPX3, COL23A1, and TNC in PR; and COL1A1, COL3A1, SPARC, SDK1, CTHRC1, LUM, XIST, COL14A1, ASPN, POSTN, PDGFRL, and PCOLCE in GR, Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 6e, f.

Pseudobulk analysis was then used to identify cell-type-specific differences. In fibroblasts, despite similar biological pathways in ECM organisation being observed in GR and PR, there were specific gene differences. Pathways associated with ECM structure constituents and collagen binding were specifically identified in GR Treatment, and genes upregulated in these pathways included SPARC, COL14A1, and CTHRC1, Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6e, f. In addition, the pathways significantly enriched in PR Treatment were also associated with myeloid leucocyte migration regulation and leucocyte chemotaxis, Fig. 5d. Of note, specific immune response related pathways were identified in PR Baseline biopsies prior to AFCO2, including interleukin-12 production and regulation of interleukin-12 production, suggesting a potential role of immune response in differentiating GR and PR treatment outcomes, Fig. 5d. In lymphoid cells, genes enriched in PR Treatment were associated with positive regulation of leucocyte activation, positive regulation of T cell activation, positive regulation of leucocyte cell-cell adhesion, antigen processing and presentation, MHC-II protein complex assembly, Fig. 5e. In myeloid cells, significantly enriched pathways in PR Treatment included positive regulation of leucocyte activation, positive regulation of T cell activation, positive regulation of leucocyte cell–cell adhesion, MHC class I protein complex assembly and antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen, Fig. 5f.

In summary, our data suggest that PR and GR responses are associated with distinct cell population frequencies and patterns of gene expression following laser therapy, with genes enriched in GR scars favouring ECM structural organisation, whereas genes enriched in PR scars favoured immune activities. The elevated expression of inflammatory gene signatures in PR scars suggested altered immune regulation, potentially keeping these scars sequestered in the inflammatory phase following laser treatment, rather than transitioning to the later stages of tissue remodelling.

Enhanced ECM intercellular communication networks between fibroblasts and immune cells were observed in PR scars, with fibroblasts as the main orchestrator

As wound healing is a complex biological process that involves several cellular communication networks to support its progression, we wondered whether there were any specific networks that underpin the differences between GR and PR scar reduction after laser therapy. Therefore, we investigated the intercellular communication networks within the GR and PR scars. Using the CellChatDB algorithm, we inferred the secretion-signalling (SS), cell–cell contact (CC), and ECM intercellular communication networks. Overall, we found that AFCO2 treatment decreased the number and the strength of the SS- and CC-intercellular communication networks in GR. On the other hand, we found the number of SS- and CC-networks in PR increased after AFCO2 treatment, as well as the strength of the CC-communications, Supplementary Fig. 7a, b. Comparing the ECM intercellular network between GR and PR post-treatment (GRTx and PRTx), we detected 73 enriched ligand–receptor genes that linked to 144 significant ligand-receptor pairs in GRTx, and 78 enriched ligand–receptor genes that linked to 164 significant ligand–receptor pairs in PRTx. The communication networks, including the number of interactions and relative information flow, were higher in PR scars after AFCO2, Fig. 6a, b. Given that the collagen network plays a crucial role in ECM communications, we conducted further analysis on this network. Our analysis revealed an increased network traffic between fibroblasts and immune cells, including myeloid and lymphoid cells, in PR, Fig. 6c, d.

Enhanced profibrotic and immune cell activation network traffic observed in PR scars, with fibroblast, myeloid, and vascular endothelial cells being the primary communicators

The wound healing process comprises four major phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and tissue remodelling. During the inflammation phase, the site of injury sends out signals to recruit immune cells. In response, immune cells from the circulation transit across blood vessels and enter the microenvironment to exert their function. The increased immune activity we found in PR indicates that communication between cells involved in this phase may also be increased. Therefore, we also studied the secretion-signalling (SS) and cell-cell contact (CC) communication networks within GR and PR.

In the CC network, we detected 180 enriched ligand–receptor genes that linked to 142 significant ligand-receptor pairs in GRTx, and 201 enriched ligand–receptor genes that linked to 158 significant ligand-receptor pairs in PRTx, which can be further categorised into 52 signalling pathways. Again, we observed that the overall number of CC communication networks was higher in poor responders compared to good responders, although the communication strength was lower, Supplementary Fig. 7c. Among these networks, MHC-II, SELE and MHC-I were the major signalling pathways involved, with myeloid cells and VEC being the primary players in CC communication, Supplementary Fig. 7d. Comparing the overall information flow of each signalling pathway, increased information flow of CD80, L1CAM and VISTA were observed in PR and we further identified CD226 and CLEC pathway as significant to PR and CD39 as significant in GR, Supplementary Fig. 7d. Interestingly, after AFCO2 treatment, we found an increase in both incoming and outgoing interaction strength of myeloid cells in poor responders, suggesting that the treatment potentially induced myeloid activities, Supplementary Fig. 7e.

In the SS network, we detected 266 enriched ligand–receptor genes that linked to 299 significant ligand-receptor pairs in GRTx; and 276 enriched ligand–receptor genes that linked to 301 significant ligand-receptor pairs in PRTx, which can be further categorised into 66 signalling pathways, including but not limited to CCL, CXCL, MIF, PDGF, PERIOSTIN, TWEAK, and MSTN pathways. Similar to the ECM communication network, we observed an increase in the overall number of SS communication networks in PR scars post-AFCO2 treatment. However, the communication strength was lower than GR, Supplementary Fig. 7c. Among them, CCL, CXCL, and VISFATIN, were the major signalling pathways taking part in the SS communication network, with vascular endothelial cells (VEC) being the major player mediating the communications (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Comparing the overall information flow of each signalling pathway, we found the upregulation of the MSTN pathway after AFCO2 treatment was specific to PRTx, mediating communication traffic between endothelial cells, fibroblasts and myeloid cells, Supplementary Fig. 8b. Moreover, we found increased information flow in the VISFATIN signalling pathway in PRTx, including NAMPT-(ITGA5 + ITGB1) and NAMPT-INSR, which contribute to increased communications between immune cells, fibroblasts and VEC (Supplementary Fig 8c, d). Increased CXCL signalling pathways, including CXCL2-CXCR2 and CXCL12-ACKR3, that mediate the communications between VEC, fibroblast, and immune cells were also observed in PR, Supplementary Fig. 8e, f.

In summary, these findings again indicated that GR and PR responses to laser therapy are biologically different, and they possess different intercellular communication patterns. We found increased communication traffic in both SS and CC networks in PR, with fibroblast, VEC, and myeloid cells being the main communicators.

Single-cell trajectory analysis reveals distinct fibroblast developmental trajectories between GR and PR

Fibroblasts play a central role in the pathogenesis of hypertrophic scars, and research in mice has shown that they exhibit high degrees of heterogeneity during the scarring process42. Above, we have demonstrated that gene expression in the fibroblast population differed greatly between GR and PR, Fig. 3d and Fig. 4c, d. Therefore, we decided to take a closer look at this population. First of all, we further divided fibroblasts into five subsets based on feature marker expression35,36, including secretory papillary fibroblasts (sPF; PTGS2+), secretory reticular fibroblasts (sRF; MFAP5+PTGS2+), pro-inflammatory fibroblasts (InflamF; APOD+), myofibroblasts (MyoF; TAGLN+ACTA2+), and mesenchymal fibroblasts (MscF; MFAP5+ASPN+), Fig. 7a. Then, we compared their cellular composition between GR and PR, before and after treatments. Our analysis revealed that the MscF subset was significantly enriched in the GR, while the InflamF and sPF populations were most prominent in PR (Fig. 7b). Notably, comparison of these subsets before and after AFCO2 treatment revealed that AFCO2 treatment led to a significant reduction in InflamF proportions in GR, whereas InflamF levels increased prominently in PR, Fig. 7b, c.

Since different fibroblast subsets exhibit different properties, our analysis has identified a high abundance of MscF in GR and InflamF in PR. To investigate further, we examined the expression of these genes across these subsets, and we found that two genes displayed opposing expression patterns between GR and PR. The ECM-modulating gene, COL14A1, which is enriched in MscF, showed a significant increase in GR following AFCO2 treatment, while in PR, its expression was markedly decreased. On the other hand, the inflammatory-associated gene, CXCL14, which is enriched in InflamF, displayed a significant reduction in GR after AFCO2 treatment, whereas in PR, the treatment further induced its expression, Fig. 8f, g. Taken together, we showed GR and PR have distinct proportions of fibroblast subpopulations, with GR comprised of higher levels of regenerative MscF and PR comprised of higher levels of inflammatory InflamF and sPF populations. GR and PR underwent different developmental trajectories during the response to laser treatment. Higher levels of regenerative MscF from GR mapped to earlier stages of the response, together with distinct gene expression patterns across time, may explain the reason driving the differences.

Discussion

To summarise, our study demonstrated that AFCO2 laser treatment is more effective on less well-established scars, with better scar reduction as assessed by skin thickness, vascularity, and mVSS overall scores. Importantly, our data showed that young scars and old scars showed different gene expression patterns in response to AFCO2 laser treatment, suggesting a relationship with the response to laser therapy. Genes enriched in young scars and thus a positive response, were associated with extracellular matrix and structure organisation; whereas genes enriched in old scars and associated with a poorer response were related to enhanced immune and inflammatory responses. Limited analysis of protein expression showed differences in collagen I, collagen III, and TIMP1 responses between GR and PR, though the latter did not mirror the transcriptomic data. Intercellular communication networks were enhanced in poor responders, with increased crosstalk between fibroblasts, immune cells, and vascular endothelial cells. Moreover, different proportions of fibroblast subpopulations were observed with differential responses to laser therapy, specifically, a good response was associated with higher proportions of regenerative MSCF in the treated skin, whereas a poor response was associated with higher proportions of inflammatory InflamF. Single-cell trajectory analysis further revealed that good and poor responders underwent different trajectories across time; the genes involved in the good response developmental trajectory were associated with regenerative features, while in poor responders, genes were associated with excessive fibrosis. These differences provide important new insights into the cellular responses to AFCO2 laser treatment that may ultimately influence treatment outcomes and allow future targeting of therapy to improve clinical results.

Clinically, the optimum timing for treating hypertrophic burn scars using AFCO2 laser therapy remains unclear. In recent years, there have been a few studies investigating the efficacy of early laser treatment on hypertrophic burn scars. For example, in a retrospective review, Tan et al. assessed the potential of early intervention on improving HTS using AFCO2. They analysed 223 HTS patients treated by AFCO2 laser within 12 months of injury and concluded that the early AFCO2 treatment is safe and effective43. A recent randomised control trial (RCT) was conducted in the US to investigate the use of AFCO2 laser treatment on acute traumatic injuries to prevent scar formation and showed that early AFCO2 laser treatment, 3 months after injury, resulted in a significant reduction of scar formation44. Moreover, another recent RCT trial carried out in Western Australia investigated the impact of the AFCO2 laser on patient-reported outcome measures (POSAS), subjective scar appearance, dermal architecture, and gene transcription in early burn scars. It concluded that the AFCO2 laser-treated scars significantly altered scar thickness and texture and presented a better appearance after treatment. They also reported a significant decrease in thick collagen fibres and a significant increase in finer collagen fibre density post-AFCO2. Using bulk RNAseq analysis, they further suggested that AFCO2 induces sustained changes in fibroblast gene expression, indicating potential beneficial mechanisms of AFCO2 in HTS45. Similar to the above-mentioned studies, our results showed that early laser treatment gave better scar reduction in skin thickness, vascularity, and mVSS overall scores and suggested that the presence of specific fibroblast subtypes was associated with a good response.

Laser therapy is commonly used in post-burn scar clinics and has been shown to relieve scar tension, break down local scar matrix and mobilise fibroblasts to generate new collagen for better tissue remodelling outcomes9,14,46–49, but the cell and molecular factors influencing laser efficacy remain poorly understood. Using a transgenic mouse model, Phan et al. identified the Wnt effector transcription factor Lef1 as a regenerative factor in fibroblasts of developing skin that transforms adult skin to regenerate rather than scarring50. Mascharak et al. showed that postnatal Engrailed-1 (En1) activation in fibroblasts leads to excessive fibrosis and scarring in mice20. Recent studies on keratinocyte dedifferentiation pathways provide further evidence on the involvement of the forkhead box O1/Krüppel-like factor 4 (Foxo1/Klf4) pathway with skin regeneration after mechanical stretch and identified E-cadherin (Cdh1) as a crucial factor for skin regeneration51,52. To find out whether GR scars also exhibited similar regenerative features in humans, we analysed specific gene expression, including the profibrotic EN1 and the regenerative LEF1, TRPS1, CDH1, FOXO1 and KLF4 in GR and PR scars. Our findings indicate that AFCO2 treatment significantly reduced EN1 expression in PR scars; however, EN1 levels in PR remained substantially higher than those in GR scars. Similarly, CDH1 expression decreased significantly following AFCO2 treatment in PR scars, with no significant change observed in GR scars. FOXO1 expression was significantly upregulated, while the KLF4 gene was downregulated across both PR and GR scars post-treatment. Notably, ACFCO2 treatment elicited opposite effects on TRPS1 expression, with a significant upregulation in GR scars and downregulation in PR scars after AFCO2 treatment, Supplementary Fig. 5h.

Aberrant inflammation during wound healing is associated with impaired healing and pathological scarring52,53. In our study, we showed significantly higher proportions of immune cells in PR compared to GR biopsies at baseline and after treatment. In fibroblasts, specific immune response-related pathways were identified in PR Baseline prior to AFCO2, including interleukin-12 production, regulation of interleukin-12 production, and positive regulation of protein secretion. Previous studies have shown that IL-12 is anti-angiogenic54 and serves as a chemoattractant for macrophages55. Using IL-12/IL-23 knockout mice, Matias et al. further revealed that IL-12 and IL-23 influence wound healing by modulating early inflammatory responses and subsequent angiogenesis56. Therefore, the observation of IL-12 in PR prior to AFCO2 indicates an underlying inflammatory predisposition, which may underlie the increased myeloid cell migration after treatment. In addition, although PR showed a modest but not significant increase in immune cell infiltration after treatment, genes enriched in lymphoid cells in PR after laser treatment were associated with immune activation, e.g., regulation of leucocyte migration, negative regulation of myoblast differentiation. In myeloid cells, enriched pathways identified in PR after laser treatment included pathways associated with cell activation, including antigen processing and presentation via MHC-II, as well as mediators of immune responses. In addition, studying DEG profiles between GR and PR scars, we also noticed increased expression of HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DQA2 in immune cell populations in PR scars, which may be other potential factors that modulate the increased inflammatory responses in PR scars. To mitigate this prolonged inflammation, potential multi-modal treatments or the use of steroids could help to control excessive inflammation and improve therapeutic outcomes.

Fibroblasts are the main cell population in the skin dermis. Our data suggest that in young and old scars, the composition of fibroblast sub-populations induced by the laser treatment is different, with young scars (good responder) comprised higher proportions of regenerative MscF which would promote new skin formation57, and old scars (poor responder) comprised of higher proportions of inflammatory InflamF and sPF which would be more conducive to fibrosis and thus prevention of scar reduction. Studies have indicated that inflammatory fibroblasts produce inflammatory cytokines and may be involved in inflammation progression and in the development of other pathogenic conditions, such as pain58,59. Our data echoes these findings, showing that pathways enriched in PR after laser treatment were also associated with myeloid leucocyte migration.

The increased proportion of InflamF in PR suggests a potential prolonged inflammation phase after AFCO2. Because it is now understood that fibroblast subsets are responsible for different biological functions60, the stimulation of fibroblasts in young and old scars by AFCO2 treatment could therefore lead to alternative outcomes. Comparing differential gene expression profiles and intercellular communication patterns between good and poor responders, we found that poor responder biopsies were enriched with pro-inflammatory features and exhibited enhanced cross-talk between fibroblasts and immune cells. Moreover, we found that good responders and poor responders demonstrate different gene expression features that are responsible for distinct biological functions. Genes enriched in GR were predominantly associated with ECM structure and organisation, whereas genes enriched in PR were linked to enhanced immune responses. In PR, the expression of the pro-fibrotic factor, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP1), was significantly elevated following laser treatment (Fig. 3i); and the expression of the pro-inflammatory CXCL14 and CCL19 in InflamF was also increased (Fig. 7f). However, at the protein level TIMP1 was increased in GR but not PR so it will be important to confirm the transcriptomic data. Similarly, COL3A1 expression was induced in PR after AFCO2 treatment, but at the protein level, we saw a decrease in collagen III. While collagen III contributes to ECM regulation and tissue remodelling, its dysregulation has been associated with excessive ECM deposition and heightened immune cell infiltration61, we would therefore have expected an increase in PR scars. However, PR did exhibit increased intercellular communications between fibroblast and cells, including myeloid, lymphoid, and vascular endothelial cells, using ligand–receptor pairs unique to old scars. These enhanced cross-talks included: (1) fibroblast and myeloid cells interactions using MSTN signalling (TGFBR1–ACVR2A and TGFBR1–ACVR2B); (2) vascular endothelial–myeloid cells communication via VISFATIN (NAMPT) pathways (NAMPT–ITGA5 and NAMPT–ITBG1); and (3) vascular endothelial–fibroblast interactions through CXCL2–CXCR2 signalling. Given these findings, targeting these fibroblast–immune interactions and ECM regulatory pathways may offer novel therapeutic strategies for modulating scar formation and fibrosis. Interventions aimed at balancing TIMP1, CXCL14, and COL3A1 activity, or disrupting excessive ligand-receptor signalling, could also help refine approaches to scar remodelling and immune modulation.

Finally, inferring intercellular communication networks, we found that regulators of wound healing, including POSTN, TWEAK, and PTN, were significantly elevated in GR. Despite periostin+ fibroblast being often associated with pro-fibrotic features36, it has also been previously reported to have several positive functions in scar formation, including promoting proliferation and differentiation of fibroblasts, re-epithelialisation, and wound closure62. Others have reported that periostin can promote tissue repair by enhancing the function of adipose-derived stem cells63. TNF-related weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) is another factor that exhibits beneficial roles in scarring. It has been shown to facilitate angiogenesis and regulate the production of mediators that are crucial for tissue regeneration64. In contrast, signalling associated with profibrotic and T cell activation pathways, including CD22665, MSTN66, and IL1667, was significantly increased in PR. Many studies have described the involvement of MSTN in excessive scarring, and it has been indicated as a potential target for skin healing66. For example, a study using full thickness burns rodent model found a fourfold increase in Mstn68. Other studies using Mstn-null mice showed higher tissue regeneration and reduced fibrosis69,70. Therefore, to improve PR outcomes, drugs targeting the MSTN pathway could also be used as a supportive treatment, in addition to laser therapy.

This study focuses almost exclusively on transcriptomic analysis, with immunohistochemistry only performed for collagen 1 and 3 and TIMP1; thus, validating changes at the protein level remains a limitation of our study. Due to repeated treatments and biopsies, obtaining sufficiently large samples for protein measurement potentially posed challenges for participant recruitment and retention. Future studies should attempt to confirm the key gene expression changes observed between GR and PR at the protein level. In addition, our studies were all in scars older than 2 years, and as recent studies indicate that laser therapy is efficacious at earlier times after wound closure, i.e., 3-6 months, studies of the skin response at these time points could confirm our findings.

To conclude, our study demonstrated that young scars and old scars are biologically distinct in their response to AFCO2 laser treatment. The differences in gene expression profiles and unique intercellular communication networks between them ultimately determine the fate of different treatment outcomes. Our findings suggest that young scars favour wound healing and regeneration, whereas old scars are prone to maintained inflammation and fibrosis, which potentially limits the ability to reduce scars with AFCO2. Therefore, better scar reduction may be seen if AFCO2 laser therapy is applied sooner after wound healing.
