Emergence, evolution and temporal spread of ceftriaxone-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae in China from 2002 to 2022: a retrospective genomic surveillance study
Authors: Chi Zhang, Yang Yang, Bangyong Zhu, Xiaozheng Tang, Na Zhong, Youwei Wang, Zhijun Zeng, Huichao Chen, Xinyi Lu, Cansheng Zhu, Ruizha Shadehan, Wenling Cao, Daniel Golparian, Weiming Gu, Magnus Unemo, Junping Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResistance to the last treatment option for gonorrhoea, ceftriaxone is a major public health concern globally, which poses a serious threat to the currently recommended ceftriaxone monotherapy. We elucidate the emergence, evolution and temporal spread of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China, using whole-genome sequencing of 357 ceftriaxone-resistant isolates from eight provinces during 2002–2022. We describe distinct differences between ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China and global isolates. The resistance levels for the 357 isolates for cefixime, azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, benzylpenicillin, tetracycline, and spectinomycin were 92.4%, 17.0%, 100%, 84.9%, 92.7%, and 0.8%, respectively. Before 2019, ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in China mainly harboured non-mosaic penA alleles like penA-13, penA-12, penA-18 or mosaic penA-10. From 2019, ceftriaxone-resistant isolates with mosaic penA-60.001 increased rapidly, becoming the dominant penA allele. Meanwhile, penA-60.001 was horizontally transferred from MLST ST1903 strains to strains of other MLST STs that were prevalent in China. Notably, the MLST ST7365 isolates with penA-60.001 allele had higher ceftriaxone MICs than isolates of the typical ST1903 FC428 clone. This may be attributed to a main clade of MLST ST7365 strains in China exhibiting elevated ceftriaxone MICs, and the temporal phylogeny showed this clade emerged around 1994. In summary, we reveal the unique genomic and evolutionary patterns of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China, and the 357 not previously reported isolates substantially increase the number of global ceftriaxone-resistant isolates. Our results suggest potential evolutionary pathways and directions of ceftriaxone-resistant strains, and provide valuable data for examining genomic epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance determinants and formulating strategies for surveillance and interventions.KEYWORDS: Neisseria gonorrhoeae, ceftriaxone-resistance, whole-genome sequencing, FC428 clone, temporal phylogeny

Introduction

Gonorrhoea and antimicrobial resistance in Neisseria gonorrhoeae remain major global public health concerns. The third-generation cephalosporin ceftriaxone (in monotherapy or in dual therapy with azithromycin) is the only remaining option for empiric first-line therapy [1]. However, resistance to also ceftriaxone has emerged and N. gonorrhoeae is a high-priority pathogen according to the 2024 World Health Organization (WHO) and other international and national public health organizations [1–4]. In China, among the highest levels of ceftriaxone resistance in N. gonorrhoeae globally has been reported [3,4]. In 2022, the levels of ceftriaxone-resistant and cefixime-resistant gonococcal isolates were 8.1% and 16.0% respectively, which was three times higher than in 2017 [4]. The high prevalence of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal isolates poses a threat to the efficacy of the currently recommended ceftriaxone 1 g monotherapy for gonorrhoea in China and internationally [1,3]. Nevertheless, genomic studies characterizing the gonococcal strains spreading in China have been limited. A multicenter study provided genomic baseline data on gonococcal strains circulating in China during 2012–2013 [5]. However, this study included only a small number of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates (n = 14) [5]. Accordingly, data is lacking regarding the emergence, evolution and temporal spread of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China. In particular, the continuous spread and evolution of the ceftriaxone-resistant FC428 clone have not been comprehensively characterized [6,7]. This clone has also been transmitted internationally, underscoring its global significance [8]. In addition, several other ceftriaxone-resistant lineages have emerged in China. Together, these diverse resistant strains have substantially reshaped the gonococcal population in China.

To comprehensively elucidate the emergence, evolution and temporal spread of ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae strains in China, we examined ceftriaxone-resistant isolates (n = 357) from eight provinces, collected during 2002–2022, using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and phylogenomic analysis. We additionally describe distinct differences between ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae strains in China and those from other countries, and suggest future evolutionary paths.

Materials and methods

Gonococcal isolates and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

A total of 385 not previously described ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae isolates from China were included. These isolates originated from eight provinces in China and were isolated from 2002 to 2022. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using agar dilution method, according to the Clinical Laboratory and Standards Institute (www.clsi.org). This method is widely used in clinical laboratories for determining MIC values. The current European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, www.eucast.org) clinical resistance breakpoints (v15.0) were used: ceftriaxone and cefixime MIC > 0.125 mg/L, ciprofloxacin MIC > 0.06 mg/L, spectinomycin MIC > 64 mg/L, benzylpenicillin MIC > 1 mg/L, and tetracycline MIC > 0.5 mg/L. For azithromycin, the EUCAST epidemiological cut-off value (MIC > 1 mg/L) was used to indicate resistance. The EUCAST breakpoints are considered the most up-to-date and widely recognized criteria for assessing antimicrobial resistance, particularly for cephalosporins like ceftriaxone. This combination of the CLSI testing method and EUCAST interpretation criteria ensures that our results align with current international practice for antimicrobial resistance surveillance. All isolates were cultured and preserved as part of routine diagnostics (standard care) and surveillance, and no patient identifiable information was available. Accordingly, no ethical approval was required.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

DNA was extracted from each isolate using a QIAamp 96 DNA QIAcube HT kit with the QIAcube HT instrument (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The genomes were sequenced using an Illunima NovaSeq X Plus system (Illumina, San Diego, USA) with paired-end 150-bp sequencing libraries (Annoroad Gene Technology, Beijing, China). Isolates were excluded if the following quality metrics were not satisfied: a minimum average genome coverage of 50x and >85% of nucleotides with Phred quality score ≥30. Mapping and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) identification was performed as previously described [5]. Alleles for multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) and N. gonorrhoeae Sequence Typing for Antimicrobial Resistance (NG-STAR) were extracted using in-house scripts, and sequence types (STs) were generated using the PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/) and NG-STAR (https://ngstar.canada.ca/) databases, respectively. New alleles and STs were submitted to corresponding databases. NG-STAR clonal complexes (CCs) were designated as previously described [9].

Phylogenomic analysis

Raw sequencing data of additional Chinese and global gonococcal isolates were collected together with available metadata from PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/), Pathogenwatch database (https://pathogen.watch/) and previous studies (Supplementary Tables 1–3). For each analysed isolate, a full-length whole genome relative to FA1090 (NC_002946.2) was generated. The whole genomes of all ceftriaxone-resistant isolates, MLST ST8123 isolates, and MLST ST7365 isolates were classified into corresponding datasets and aligned for phylogenomic analysis. Sites with missing data in more than 5% of the isolates in the alignments were removed. After removal the recombinant sites using Gubbins (v2.3.4) [10], the filtered alignments were used to construct maximum-likelihood phylogeny with RAxML-NG (v1.2.0). Fastbaps (v1.0.8) were used to partition the maximum-likelihood phylogeny into BAPS clusters [11]. The temporal phylogenetic analyses were performed with BEAST2 v2.7.6 [12]. Specifically, TempEst v1.5.3 was used to determine whether the dataset exhibited sufficient temporal signal to support molecular clock analysis. The estimated root-to-tip correlation of MSLT ST7365 and MLST ST8123 temporal phylogenetic analyses were 0.5089 (R2 = 0.259) and 0.5328 (R2 = 0.2839) respectively, indicating moderate temporal signal and the results could be interpreted with caution. The alignment file, with recombinant regions removed using Gubbins, was used as input for BEAST2 (v2.7.6). Firstly, the XML files were generated with BEAUti v2.7.6. The BEAST analysis was performed using a GTR substitution model, a coalescent model with a constant population size, and a strict molecular clock model with chains of 200 million steps. Then, Tracer v1.7.2 was used to assess convergence of the BEAST results. All parameters exhibited effective sample size (ESS) values greater than 800, which means that the results converged. Finally, Tree Annotator was used to generate the maximum clade credibility trees. The ggtree v3.16.0 was used to annotate and visualized the temporal phylogenies generated by BEAST [13]. Finally, we compared the 357 Chinese isolates from the present study (Supplementary Table 4) with 51,502 publicly-available gonococcal genomes downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive (short-read archive) in a global phylogenomic tree analysis, as previously described [14].

Results

Characteristics of gonorrhoea patients and isolates

A total of 357 ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal isolates passed the quality control and their geo-temporal distribution is shown in Figure 1. Demographic characteristics of gonorrhoea patients and phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility of these 357 isolates are shown in Table 1. Most patients were males (94.3%, 332/352), and the median age was 31 years (inter-quartile range (IQR) 25–38). The ceftriaxone-resistant isolates had ceftriaxone MICs of 0.25 (n = 200), 0.5 (n = 117), 1 (n = 39), and 2 (n = 1) mg/L, and additionally exhibited high resistance levels for cefixime (92.4%, 305/330), azithromycin (17.0%, 58/342), ciprofloxacin (100%, 357/357), benzylpenicillin (84.9%, 303/357), and tetracycline (92.7%, 319/344). However, only 0.8% (3/356) of the isolates were resistant to spectinomycin (Table 1). Temporal trends in antimicrobial resistance phenotypes and genotypes

Figure 2(a,b) shows the temporal trends in resistance levels for cefixime, azithromycin, spectinomycin, benzylpenicillin, tetracycline, and ciprofloxacin among the 357 ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal isolates. The resistance to cefixime increased from 62.1% (18/29) in 2011–2015 to 98.3% (233/237) in 2019–2022. The resistance to azithromycin annually fluctuated between 3.3% (1/30, 2020) and 27.5% (38/138, 2021) in 2011–2022. The resistance levels for benzylpenicillin and tetracycline consistently remained above 80% and all the isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin. Spectinomycin-resistant isolates were rare, with only three (0.8%, 3/356) found in 2021. Figure 2(c,d) display the MICs of ceftriaxone and cefixime, respectively, in isolates by year. The isolates from 2019 had higher MICs of both ceftriaxone and cefixime.

Twenty-four different penA alleles were identified, and Figure 2(e) shows the temporal distribution of penA alleles represented by more than 10 isolates. Before 2019, ceftriaxone-resistant isolates mainly harboured non-mosaic penA alleles like penA-13, penA-12, penA-18, penA-43 penA-21 or mosaic penA-10 allele. Mosaic penA-60.001 allele was first found in 14.3% (3/21) of the ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in 2017, but from 2019 it was in vast predominance (74.5% (178/239) of all ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in 2019–2022). Gonococcal isolates with penA-60.001 had higher ceftriaxone and cefixime MICs compared to isolates with other penA alleles (p < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test, Supplementary Table 5), which explains the higher MICs from 2019 and onwards (Figure 2(c,d)).

However, among the isolates with penA-60.001, the FC428 clone (characterized by penA-60.001 and MLST ST1903) [7] has been declining over time. The penA-60.001 allele appears to have been horizontally transferred from MLST ST1903 strain(s) to MLST ST8123 and ST7365 strains, which were prevalent in China, and subsequently further spread in China (Figure 2(f)).

Overall, 58 MLST STs were identified, and STs represented by more than five isolates are shown in Figure 2(g). Since all MLST ST1903 isolates contained penA-60.001, the trend in the prevalence of MLST ST1903 isolates corresponded to that of the FC428 clone. In 2019, the FC428 clone had rapidly spread in China. From 2020, the proportion of MLST ST7365 isolates increased, while the previously prevalent MLST ST7360 and ST14283 strains disappeared. Ceftriaxone-resistant MLST ST8123 strains were identified during 2011–2022.

Overall, 141 NG-STAR STs were identified, with ST1143 (n = 44) and ST233 (n = 41), which only differ in their porB allele, accounting for 23.8% (85/357) of the isolates. Regarding NG-STAR CCs, CC199 has maintained an annual proportion from 6.5% (2/31, 2011–2015) to 50.0% (11/22, 2019) over the past decade. From 2018, there was an increase in the proportion of CC233 and/or CC2473, with more than 65.3% (32/49) of the ceftriaxone-resistant isolates belonging to these CCs in 2022, while the proportion of previously prevalent CCs, such as CC348, CC127, CC38, CC497, CC428 decreased (Figure 2(h)).

Notably, regarding novel antimicrobials, no known gyrB (in GyrB D429 or K450) or gyrA (in GyrA A92) target resistance determinants for zoliflodacin or gepotidacin, respectively, were observed [1,14,15]. However, 5.6% (20/357) of the ceftriaxone-resistant isolates carried the ParC D86N mutation, which has been associated with a potential predisposition for gepotidacin resistance, although its functional impact was not directly validated [1,15].

Genomic epidemiology of ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae isolates worldwide

We subsequently examined 658 global ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae isolates in a phylogenomic tree (Figure 3). These included the 357 isolates from the present study, 210 isolates previously reported in China, and 91 representative international isolates (24 from Europe, 16 from North America, 46 from Asia, five from Oceania) (Supplementary Table 1). Fastbaps partitioned the maximum-likelihood phylogeny into 12 clades (A-L). The most abundant MLST STs, penA alleles and NG-STAR CCs closely correlated to the clades. Seven clades were highlighted due to their inclusion of representative ceftriaxone-resistant isolates (Figure 3).

Briefly, clade J and A corresponded to the two internationally disseminated MLST STs, MLST ST1901 and ST7363, respectively, with frequent resistance to cefixime but also lower levels of ceftriaxone resistance. Most isolates in clade J were MLST ST1901, including ceftriaxone-resistant isolates from North America and Europe in the 2010s, harbouring mosaic penA-34 [16–18]. The penA-34 allele appears to have originated from two recombination events: first, recombination from commensal Neisseria into non-mosaic penA-5, resulting in penA-10; then, recombination from penA-1 into penA-10 generating mosaic penA-34 [19]. The isolates from China clustered in a distinct branch of clade J, comprised penA-5 and mosaic penA-10, and were isolated relatively recently (2017–2022). This indicates that MLST ST1901 isolates with mosaic penA-34 were not widely prevalent in China, when they earlier widely spread in Europe and North America. However, we have identified MLST ST1901 strains within this clade that harbour the mosaic penA-60.001 allele, which demonstrated horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of penA-60.001 to MLST1901. These three strains, isolated from China and the United States, all exhibited high ceftriaxone MICs (>0.5 mg/L) [20,21]. Another branch in clade J comprised MLST 10,314 isolates with penA-103 and penA-60.001. Most isolates in clade A were MLST ST7363 or ST14283, which differ at only one MLST locus. The MLST ST7363 isolates included representative ceftriaxone-resistant isolates from Japan, and WHO-X, exhibiting high-level ceftriaxone resistance in Japan, and WHO-Z, a multidrug-resistant strain from Australia [15]. The ceftriaxone-resistant MLST ST14283 strains have only been found in China, and primarily were NG-STAR CC348 with mosaic penA-10.

Forty-two per cent (278/658) of the isolates were located in clades H and I, and these isolates exhibited high genomic similarity. These clades included the FC428 strain first reported in Japan in 2015 [7], as well as the FC428 and FC428-like isolates subsequently reported in China, Southeast Asia, Europe, North America, and Oceania, representing the clonal expansion of FC428 both in China and globally. In addition to the typical FC428 clone (featured by penA-60.001 and MLST ST1903), the two clades demonstrated extensive HGT of penA-60.001 to MLST ST7365, ST1600, and ST13871 strains. Isolates with these three STs were genetically similar to ST1903 isolates, differing at only one MLST locus. ST7365 and ST1600 were the major MLST STs in China, while ST13871 isolates were more commonly found in Southeast Asian countries, including Vietnam, Cambodia, and Singapore (Supplementary Table 1). Interestingly, among the ceftriaxone-resistant penA-60.001 isolates, the cephalosporin MICs of MLST ST7365 isolates were higher than those of MLST ST1903 isolates (ceftriaxone: p = 0.018, cefixime: p = 0.020, Mann–Whitney U test, Supplementary Table 5). Notably, MLST ST7365 isolates had another branch harbouring the penA-13 allele, which was first identified in 2012 and has remained prevalent in China since then.

Most of the isolates in clades F and L were MLST ST7360 or ST8123. Apart from a recent study that reported eight ceftriaxone-resistant MLST ST8123 isolates in the UK associated with travel to the Asian-Pacific region [22], no other studies outside of China have reported ceftriaxone-resistant isolates with MLST ST7360 or ST8123. Isolates with the non-mosaic penA-12 and penA-13 alleles accounted for the majority of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in China before the emergence of the FC428 clone [7]. However, a newer branch harbouring penA-60.001 has emerged in MLST ST8123 isolates, indicating that penA-60.001 has been horizontally transferred to strains with a genomic background distant from MLST ST1903 (differing from MLST ST8123 at three locus).

Clade B comprised MLST ST7827 isolates, which accounted for a major proportion of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in China. MLST ST7827 is dominated by non-mosaic penA-13, however, this clade developed a new branch with mosaic penA-60.001 in 2021–2022.

Temporal phylogenetic analyses of MLST ST7365 isolates

In this study, we observed among the ceftriaxone-resistant penA-60.001 isolates, the cephalosporin MICs of MLST ST7365 isolates were significantly higher than those of MLST ST1903 isolates. Furthermore, MLST ST7365 (13.9%, 37/266) ranked second after MLST ST8123 among the ceftriaxone-resistant MLST non-ST1903 isolates. The above features make MLST ST7365 a noteworthy ST type. Therefore, we collected genomic sequences of 164 MLST ST7365 isolates from China and globally from public databases for temporal phylogenomic analysis using BEAST (Supplementary Table 2). The substitution rates of the nonrecombinant region of the genome were estimated from the BEAST analysis as 6.95 × 10–6 substitutions per site per year (95% highest posterior density [HPD] ranging from 6.43 × 10–6 to 7.45 × 10–6). MLST ST7365 isolates predominantly harboured non-mosaic penA alleles. Isolates originating from outside China mainly carried penA-1 and penA-2, all of which were susceptible to ceftriaxone (MIC < 0.125 mg/L). The temporal phylogeny showed a clade (highlighted in Figure 4) harbouring the penA-13 allele emerged around 1994 (95% CI: 1991–1996). Nearly all isolates within this clade originated from China and Vietnam and exhibited elevated MICs. Within this clade, a group of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates that underwent HGT of penA-60.001 were emerged around 2016 (95% CI: 2015–2017), and most of them exhibited higher ceftriaxone MICs (≥0.5 mg/L), likely due to additional ceftriaxone resistance determinants in isolates from this clade (Figure 4).

Temporal phylogenetic analyses of MLST ST8123 isolates

In the present study, ceftriaxone-resistant MLST ST8123 isolates persisted from 2015 to 2022 and accounted for the highest proportion (19.9%, 53/266) among the MLST non-ST1903 ceftriaxone-resistant isolates. Additionally, it was the most common MLST ST among non-penA-60.001 isolates. MLST ST8123 isolates have been rarely previously reported. We collected genomic sequences of 206 MLST ST8123 isolates from China and globally from public databases for temporal phylogenomic analysis using BEAST (Supplementary Table 3). The substitution rates of the nonrecombinant region of the genome were estimated from the BEAST analysis as 4.04 × 10–6 substitutions per site per year (95% highest posterior density [HPD] ranging from 3.62 × 10–6 to 4.46 × 10–6). Most MLST ST8123 isolates harboured non-mosaic penA alleles, primarily including penA-12, penA-2, penA-5, penA-13, penA-18, and penA-120. In contrast to MLST ST7365 strains, where penA-13-harbouring isolates exhibited higher ceftriaxone MICs, non-mosaic penA alleles in MLST ST8123 strains did not result in a significant increase in ceftriaxone MICs. The temporal phylogeny revealed a clade (highlighted in Figure 5) harbouring the penA-12 allele that emerged around 2006 (95% CI: 2004–2007). This clade includes strains recently isolated in Europe, which are also associated with the Asian-Pacific region. Meanwhile, a group of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates that acquired penA-60.001 via HGT emerged around 2014 (95% CI: 2013–2015).

Ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal Chinese isolates in a global phylogenomic tree

The Chinese ceftriaxone-resistant isolates sequenced in the present study (n = 357) were spread across large parts of the gonococcal species phylogeny (Supplementary Figure 6). Accordingly, this clearly illustrates that the ceftriaxone-resistant strains with mosaic penA-60.001 as well as other both mosaic (e.g. penA-10) and non-mosaic penA alleles (e.g. penA-12, penA-13, penA-18) are now found in both the antimicrobial resistance-associated lineage A and more antimicrobial-susceptible lineage B [14,18,23]; in several different genomic backgrounds, illustrating that both ceftriaxone-resistant strains and ceftriaxone resistance-mediating penA sequences are spreading.

Discussion

Our genomic data elucidates the emergence, evolution and temporal spread of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China and internationally. The study substantially increases the number of well-characterised global ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal isolates. We describe previously identified internationally spreading ceftriaxone-resistant clones, including MLST ST1901 (with mosaic penA-34 variants), MLST ST7363 (mosaic penA-10 variants), and MLST ST1903 (mosaic penA-60.001). Additionally, we identified several important ceftriaxone-resistant clones that had not been previously reported, revealing the unique genomic and evolutionary pathway of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China. This pathway was divided into two stages, before 2019 and from 2019 and onwards, each exhibiting distinct characteristics.

Before 2019, FC428 clone (MLST ST1903, penA-60.001) appeared sporadically in China [6,24–26], instead, strains harbouring non-mosaic penA-12, penA-13, penA-18 and penA-43 alleles accounted for 40–70% of all ceftriaxone-resistant isolates. This was in sharp contrast to ceftriaxone-resistant and ceftriaxone-DS isolates prevalent during the same time period in other parts of the world outside China. Specifically, MLST ST1901 isolates, which carries the mosaic penA-34 allele, predominated among ceftriaxone-resistant and ceftriaxone-DS isolates in both the USA and Europe [16–18]. Concurrently, the cefixime-DS strains prevalent in Japan were predominantly MLST ST7363 isolates, which carry the mosaic penA-10 allele [27]. In contrast, during this period in China, ST7363 strains carrying the mosaic penA-10 allele circulated at a lower frequency, while ST1901 strains with the mosaic penA-34 allele were extremely rare [5,21,28,29].

Ceftriaxone resistance caused by non-mosaic penA alleles in gonococci has not received sufficient attention and the mechanisms remain unexplored experimentally. According to the national study from 2012 to 2013 in China [5], 53.8% (234/435) of isolates harboured penA-12, penA-13, or penA-18. However, only 6.0% (14/234) of these isolates were resistant to ceftriaxone [5]. The limited accuracy of molecular assays in predicting ceftriaxone resistance in isolates with non-mosaic penA alleles may have resulted in misidentification of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates [30], exacerbating their neglect and hidden spread in China and neighbouring countries. The ceftriaxone resistance in these gonococcal strains is likely caused by a cumulative effect of several known and possibly some unknown resistance determinants and their complex interactions, rather than being determined solely by the non-mosaic penA. Comparative genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics studies would be valuable to explore these ceftriaxone-resistance mechanisms in detail, and the isolates reported in the present study will provide an exceedingly valuable resource for this.

From 2019 to 2022, the proportion of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates harbouring penA-60.001 increased substantially, exceeding 68% each year. However, the proportion of MLST ST1903 isolates was declining annually, being gradually replaced by the predominant MLST STs in China, such as MLST ST8123, ST7365, ST1600, and ST7827. This indicated that the HGT of penA-60.001 occurred rapidly in China and we define this pattern as the conversion from the FC428 clone to FC428-like isolates. This pattern had two main effects. First, it greatly accelerated the dissemination of penA-60.001, because ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains shifted from monoclonal to multiclonal expansion. Second, it further elevated the MICs of both ceftriaxone and cefixime in some emerging penA-60.001-harbouring clones, e.g. MLST ST7365 with penA-60.001. This underscores the importance of employing multi-omics approaches to study ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal isolates with both non-mosaic and mosaic penA alleles. During this period, the predominant strains in Europe were primarily MLST ST1583, ST7822, and ST9363; however, ceftriaxone-resistant strains were rare among these STs [31,32]. Additionally, reports of the FC428 clone surged globally, particularly in the Western Pacific region [33].

Understanding the propensity for HGT of especially penA-60.001 but also other penA alleles among gonococcal isolates with different MSLT STs will support the development of effective monitoring strategies for key ceftriaxone-resistant clones. In China, the predominant MLST STs have included ST8123, ST7827, ST7365, ST1600, ST7363, ST7367, and ST7360 [5,21,28,29]. These MLST STs differ substantially from, for example, the well-studied gonococcal populations in the European Economic Area [31,32]. The present study elucidates that while the HGT of penA-60.001 can occur widely among strains across multiple genomic lineages, its distribution among isolates with the aforementioned STs varied substantially. This indicates that efficacy of HGT of penA-60.001 into gonococcal strains of specific MLST STs were substantially higher. However, since ceftriaxone-susceptible gonococcal isolates were not included in the present study, the actual proportion of penA-60.001 within each MLST ST cannot be determined. Nevertheless, ceftriaxone-susceptible gonococcal strains with penA-60.001 have been very rarely described. Future studies should collect more baseline data on both ceftriaxone-resistant and ceftriaxone-susceptible gonococcal strains across China to continuously monitor the dissemination trend of particularly penA-60.001 but also other penA alleles in ceftriaxone-resistant strains.

Currently, FC428-like isolates are temporarily confined to the Asia-Pacific region and ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in Europe has remained at low levels [31,32]. Most isolates harbouring penA-60.001 that have been detected in Europe have been linked to travel to the Asia-Pacific region or contact with patients from that area [33]. Accordingly, the occasional ceftriaxone-resistant FC428-like isolates in Europe have been primarily imported. However, we should be vigilant to the possibility of HGT of penA-60.001 with predominant MLST STs in Europe, which would rapidly accelerate the spread of ceftriaxone resistance in Europe, i.e. as we in the present study elucidate has happened in China.

A limitation of this study is the limited representativeness of the included strains. Geographically, the strains were predominantly from southern provinces of China, and in terms of drug susceptibility phenotypes, strains susceptible to ceftriaxone were not included. Future studies should aim to include strains from northern China and ensure a more balanced representation of strains with diverse resistance profiles. This will help expand the database of N. gonorrhoeae strains in China and facilitate the identification of new mechanisms and determinants of antibiotic resistance.

In conclusion, the present study reveals the unique genomic and evolutionary patterns of ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains in China, and the 357 not previously reported ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal isolates from 2002–2022 substantially increases the number of well-characterised global ceftriaxone-resistant isolates. Our results suggest potential evolutionary pathways and directions of ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae strains, and provide valuable data for examining genomic epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance determinants and formulating strategies for surveillance and interventions.
