Solvent Vapor Conditioning of Polymer–Polymer Organic Solar Cells
Authors: Michael K. H. Ho, Jun-Hua Bai, Rui-Qi Png
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite rapid advances in all-polymer organic solar cells (OSCs), challenges remain in process optimization of their donor–acceptor heterostructure. Here, using all-polymer OSCs based on the state-of-the-art PM6:PYIT donor–acceptor system, we show that hot mixing of the donor and the acceptor polymer solutions can retain the desired disaggregated state of the acceptor polymer, significantly extending the pot life from 10 min to over 15 h, without harming the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the fabricated solar cells. This enhanced processing stability enables systematic solvent vapor conditioning (SVC) to be conducted on the polymer films. We show that treatment by certain solvent vapors at activity ratios of 0.01–0.1, corresponding to subsaturation vapor pressures of 1–10%, respectively, can improve not only the open-circuit voltage (V OC), short-circuit current density (J SC), and fill factor (FF) of these cells, and thus their PCE, but also, surprisingly, improve their shunt resistance by suppressing the dark leakage current density. For example, SVC with chloroform at an activity of 0.1 for 1 min improves PCE from 13.2% to 14.7% and shunt resistivity from 4 to 24 kΩ cm2. In contrast, exposure to saturated chloroform vapor degrades all cell parameters. The SVC leads to a small swelling of the films, estimated to be less than a few percent. But Vis–NIR spectroscopy reveals changes to the state of J-aggregation of the PYIT, suggesting a refinement of packing and/or conformational order of the polymer chain segments in the absence of long-range diffusion. Our results open a scalable and inexpensive route to improving polymer–polymer OSCs, while highlighting their extreme sensitivity to residual solvent vapors in the manufacturing atmosphere. Finally, the suppression of leakage current is also advantageous to improving the performance of indoor PV cells under low-light conditions.

Introduction

Polymer–polymer OSCs have attracted much recent attention due to their remarkable performance combined with several inherent advantages. , Different from traditional polymer–molecule OSCs, where recrystallizable small molecules, such as PCBM or Y6, are used as acceptors, , polymer–polymer OSCs may exhibit greater morphological and operational stability due to inherent entanglements of the polymer chains. However, the constituent donor and acceptor polymers need to be self-organized into the appropriate bulk heterostructure for efficient photon absorption, exciton dissociation, and charge carrier collection to optimize their V OC, J SC, and FF cell parameters. , This is a challenging task even for polymer–molecule OSCs. − For example, characterization of the cell parameter landscape as a function of composition and film thickness under “constant morphology” conditions has only been achieved recently to reveal the effects of phase contiguity. ,

This challenge becomes more severe in polymer–polymer OSCs. The interior and interface of polymer–polymer heterostructures may be more complex than those of polymer–molecule heterostructures. Yet, a simple codeposition of suitable donor and acceptor polymers, e.g., PM6 and PYIT (Figure ), from their biblend solution does produce a bulk heterostructure film with satisfactory photovoltaic performance. The heterostructure may be optimized using cosolvents or ternary and multinary photoactive systems. − Sequential deposition of donor and acceptor polymer layers has also been proposed. , But the results appear highly variable, which suggests a stronger morphological dependence on kinetic factors that may be difficult to control.

Chemical structure of donor and acceptor polymers used in this work.

In this work, we explore the ability of a simple process, which we call “solvent vapor conditioning (SVC)”, to regulate the polymer–polymer morphology of the film. This approach differs from the well-known solvent vapor annealing (SVA), where the polymer film is exposed to solvent vapor, typically at or near its saturation (or equilibrium) vapor pressure (p 0), sometimes at elevated temperatures. This promotes molecular reorganization or recrystallization through an extensive solvent vapor-induced swelling–deswelling cycle. In some cases, e.g., P3HT:PCBM films, SVA emulates the effect of thermal annealing. , However, SVA may often degrade semiconductor performance through excessive phase coarsening or recrystallization, especially of those films that are already nearly optimal. For example, the carrier mobility of organic thin-film transistors is often degraded by SVA, so is the PCE of OSCs. − Devices that show improvement often start from a greatly suboptimal state. − Thus, SVA has not been adopted as a standard processing step for performant devices.

Here, we show that treating polymer films with suitable solvent vapors at a controlled activity in the range of 0.01 ≲ p/p 0 ≲ 0.1, much less than unity, can surprisingly improve the PCE of PM6:PYIT cells. Thus, we distinguish this enhanced SVC method from the standard SVA method to emphasize the conditioning effect. The highly subequilibrium vapor pressures employed cause minimal swelling of the polymer films and avoid condensation of liquid solvents. To achieve this demonstration, we developed an improved mixing protocol for the PM6:PYIT biblend polymer solution that increases its pot life from 10 min to over 15 h. The extended solution stability allows us to perform multiple experimental splits to evaluate the effects of SVC. The list of solvents tested is given in Table .

Experimental Section

Materials

PM6 (M n 31 kDa, M w 71 kDa) and PYIT (M n 18 kDa, M w 29 kDa) were obtained and used as received from 1-Material and Derthon Optoelectronic Materials, respectively. PEDT:PSSH was obtained from Heraeus Precious Metals GmbH (Clevios P VP Al 4083) as a 1:6 w/w (weight/weight) PEDT:PSSH aqueous solution and purified in-house by dialysis against a 1-M semiconductor-grade HCl solution and then Millipore water through a 12-kDa molecular-weight-cutoff membrane to remove ionic impurities. , EIL1-Ox:OAc was synthesized and purified in-house.

Solar Cell Fabrication and Measurement

ITO–glass substrates were cleaned by the standard SC-1 recipe. 40 nm-thick films of PEDT:PSSH were spin-cast in air onto the ITO substrates and baked at 140 °C for 10 min in air on a digital hot plate to give the bottom hole collection layer (HCL). PM6 and PYIT were separately dissolved in chloroform containing 0.5 v/v% (volume/volume) 1-chloronaphthalene to a concentration of 12 mg mL–1 in glass vials protected from light. The solutions were heated under nitrogen at 50 °C for 30 min in isothermal aluminum wells on a digital hot plate.

In the standard mixing protocol (denoted “RT-mixing”), the two polymer solutions were cooled to room temperature for 3 min, mixed at the desired ratio, reheated to 50 °C for 30 min in the isothermal well, and then cooled to room temperature for 3 min for spin-casting of the PM6:PYIT biblend films. In the improved mixing protocol (denoted “hot-mixing”), the two solutions were mixed directly at 50 °C in the isothermal well, heated to 55 °C for 10 min, followed by 50 °C for 20 min, and then cooled to room temperature for spin-casting of the films. The PM6:PYIT composition ratios were investigated over the range of 1.2:1–1:1.2 w/w (weight/weight). Previous studies have indicated that device performance exhibits a wide tolerance to the donor:acceptor composition ratio, particularly in the acceptor-rich region.

The PM6:PYIT films were spin-cast over the HCL in air under dim lighting (ca. 1 lx) to give 105 nm-thick films. Spin conditions: 2-s acceleration to 3000 rpm for 20 s, followed by 2-s acceleration to 4000 rpm for 40 s. To avoid unintentional SVC effects, the spin-cast basin was cleaned after every spin. Moreover, to avoid the deleterious effects of residual 1-chloronaphthalene (vide infra), the biblend films were baked immediately at 95 °C for 5 min on a digital hot plate in nitrogen. The SVC experiments were then performed on these films. After SVC, the films were immediately postbaked at 60 °C for 2 min on a digital hot plate in nitrogen to eliminate sorbed solvents.

Then, 10 nm-thick films of EIL1-Ox:OAc (8:2 mol/mol), where EIL1 is the polyelectrolyte poly­((9,9-bis­(3-trimethylammoniopropyl)­fluorenyl-2,7-diyl)-alt-(benzo­[2,1,3]­thiadiazole-4,8-diyl)), counterbalanced with a mixture of oxalate (Ox) and acetate (OAc) anions at an Ox:OAc mole ratio of 4:1 to induce electron doping of the EIL, synthesized in-house, were spin-cast in air from a 2 mg mL–1 solution in 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol to give the top electron collection layer (ECL). A 120 nm-thick top Ag electrode was thermally evaporated through a shadow mask at a base pressure of 1 × 10–6 mbar to define eight 4.3 mm2 pixels on each device substrate. Ag paint was applied to the top Ag and bottom ITO electrodes to suppress contact resistance at the measurement probes. The solar cells were mounted in a sealed electrical access chamber under nitrogen. Current–voltage (JV) characteristics were collected using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200), first in the dark and then under 100 mW cm–2 simulated AM1.5 solar irradiance (Oriel Sol2A).

Results and Discussion

Improved Solution Mixing Protocol

The development of a reproducible protocol for the fabrication of PM6:PYIT films is critical for systematic SVC investigations. The standard room-temperature (RT) mixing protocol, which stipulates mixing the PM6 and PYIT solutions upon cooling to room temperature, leads to the formation of visible polymer particles after about 10 min. Thus, the solutions have to be used immediately for film deposition. This limited processing window hampers the production of large batches of identical PM6:PYIT films for systematic investigations. To understand the cause of the instability, we employed Vis–NIR spectroscopy to probe the state of order and/or aggregation of the polymers in their respective solutions in chloroform (CF). We studied the semidilute regime, exemplified by a concentration of 12 mg mL–1, which is that used for mixing and spin-casting (Figure ). Its high optical density, however, precludes the use of conventional quartz cuvettes, necessitating the sandwiching of these solutions between fused silica plates to reduce the path length to ca. 2.5 μm. For comparison, we also studied the dilute regime, exemplified by a concentration of 0.13 mg mL–1. The analysis of these spectra reveals key markers of polymer π-stacking and aggregation, which facilitate the molecular interpretation of subtle SVC-induced changes in film morphology.

Vis–NIR spectra of PM6 and PYIT solutions. (a) PM6 and (b) PYIT solutions in chloroform. Spectra of semidilute solutions are shown for 12 mg mL–1 at 22 °C, sandwiched between two 1-in fused silica plates with an estimated path length of 2.5 μm, as a function of elapsed time after cooling down from 50 °C in nitrogen. Spectra of dilute solutions are also shown for 0.13 mg mL–1, as a function of temperature, measured in a cuvette and corrected to a path length of 0.200 mm. CF, chloroform.

The dilute solution of PM6 at room temperature shows a single π–π* absorption manifold characterized by a 0–0 peak at 620 nm and an apparent 0–1 vibronic shoulder at 577 nm (solid curves, Figure a). The relative intensity of the 0–0 peak decreases with increasing temperature, presumably due to conformational twisting of the polymer backbone. The spectra are substantially concentration-independent across the dilute to semidilute concentration regimes, while those in the semidilute regime are also independent of elapsed time (dashed curves, Figure a). Thus, PM6 exhibits stable spectral characteristics, which provide a stable reference to analyze biblend spectra.

In contrast, the dilute solution of PYIT at room temperature shows a multitude of π–π* absorption manifolds (solid curves, Figure b). The lowest-lying manifold has a 0–0 peak at 781 nm and an apparent 0–1 vibronic shoulder at 733 nm, while higher-lying manifolds have prominent bands at 628 and 516 nm. The relative intensity of the 0–0 peak in the lowest-lying manifold also decreases with increasing temperature. Unlike PM6, however, the spectrum of PYIT varies strongly with concentration. The spectrum redshifts and broadens as concentration increases into the semidilute regime, where the spectrum also evolves with time at room temperature after cooling down from an initial preparation temperature at 50 °C. The key spectral features redshift to 792, 654, and 532 nm after 210 min. The redshift indicates the formation of π-stacked aggregates with weak J-character. ,− J-aggregates arise from staggered stacking of transition dipoles that characterize the chromophores, while H-aggregates arise from parallel stacking of these transition dipoles. The changes here are similar to those observed upon cooling of Y6 solutions in 2-methyltetrahydrofuran, except that the dilute solution of PYIT exhibits a more red-shifted absorption than the Y6 “monomer”, presumably due to polymerization linking of the chromophores. These spectroscopic signatures provide a handle to interpret molecular changes during SVC (vide infra). The π-stacking aggregation in the semidilute regime, however, is a prelude to undesirable polymer precipitation. Indeed, the solution shows visible polymer particles within 10 min. Thus, PYIT is strongly aggregated in CF at the typical film-casting concentrations.

Given the propensity of PYIT to aggregate rapidly at room temperature, we developed a hot-mixing protocol at 50 °C to suppress premature π-stacking. Vis–NIR spectroscopy of the biblend solutions confirms that this strategy is successful (Figure ). The RT-mixed solution at a total polymer concentration of 12 mg mL–1 (1:1 w/w PM6:PYIT) exhibits partial aggregation of PYIT at room temperature, albeit less than in pure PYIT solutions (Figure a). However, the hot-mixed solution retains essentially the unaggregated form of PYIT even after cooling to room temperature, suggesting molecular-scale or near-molecular-scale mixing through favorable interactions between the two polymers in solution (Figure b). We derived this conclusion from the PYIT spectrum obtained by subtracting the PM6 spectrum, which is known to be invariant in the semidilute regime. Thus, mixing with PM6 suppresses PYIT aggregation, but this suppression is not complete unless the solutions are mixed hot.

The hot-mixing protocol significantly elongates pot life a hundredfold, reaching at least 16 h at room temperature. This suggests that PM6 can afford robust “protection” of the molecularly mixed state of PYIT against extensive aggregation, enabling large-scale batch processing. In contrast, the RT-mixed solutions show visible precipitation that resists dissolution even on reheating, after just 10 min. Advantageously, the hot-mixed cells show similar V OC, J SC, and FF, and hence similar PCE (13.2%; Table ), as the RT-mixed cells, within the known batch-to-batch variability (standard error of mean PCE, ±0.2% absolute). Both the RT- and hot-mixing protocols give similar final Vis–NIR spectra of the spin-cast PM6:PYIT. Thus, we performed all SVC experiments on the hot-mixed films.

SVC: Experimental Setup

The spin-cast PM6:PYIT films were exposed to selected solvent vapors in a home-built copper chamber (inset, Figure a). This has a closed-cup design where the solvent is held in a ring reservoir surrounding the device substrates, minimizing the quiescent headspace above the substrates and the solvent vapor equilibration time. The vapor pressure (p) of the volatile solvent in the headspace (e.g., chloroform) can be regulated relative to its equilibrium vapor pressure (p 0) by mixing the volatile solvent with an ideal nonvolatile diluent solvent. Raoult’s law gives p/p 0 = x, where x is the mole fraction of the volatile solvent, which may be approximated by its volume fraction. This allows our simple apparatus to vary p/p 0 over a wide range of 0.01 ≤ p/p 0 ≤ 1 by varying x, where the pure solvent (x = 1) provides unit activity and the equilibrium vapor pressure (i.e., p/p 0 = 1). We employed 1-chloronaphthalene as the low-volatility diluent solvent, as it is already an additive in the polymer formulations. After SVC, a postbake (60 °C, 2 min) was applied to remove any sorbed solvent from the photoactive layer.

Theoretical Considerations

Swelling is a competition between entropy and enthalpy of mixing. The equilibrium vapor sorption for free-swelling polymers is given by Flory–Huggins theory as follows: 1 ln⁡ppo=ln(1−φp)+φp+χφp2 where φp is the polymer volume fraction, (1−φp) is the solvent volume fraction, and χ is the polymer–solvent interaction parameter, and for grafted polymer brushes, 2 ln⁡ppo=ln(1−φp)+φp+χφp2+3ρg2φp where ρg is the polymer graft density, which accounts for the elastic energy stored in the swollen brush film. The swelling of polymer thin films attached to a substrate occurs in the film thickness direction. This should similarly incur an elasticity term, the precise form of which is not crucial to the present discussion. The film swelling ratio (h/h 0), which is the ratio of swollen thickness h to original thickness h 0, is given by 1/φp. The idealized behavior revealed by the equations above is that h/h 0 is determined only by the solvent vapor activity (p/p 0), χ, and any relevant elasticity parameter. Film swelling increases with solvent vapor activity; the vapor at unit activity of a good solvent (χ ≤ 0.5) would dissolve the film. Conjugated polymers, however, exhibit complex behaviors where the apparent χ value varies with h/h 0. Using regioregular P3HT as an example, chloroform and tetrahydrofuran are reported to give χ ≈ 0.27 and 0.51, respectively, in the weak swelling regime (where h/h 0 < 1.1), but 0.99 and 1.04, respectively, in the strong swelling regime (where 1.1 < h/h 0 < 1.4). The apparent quality of the solvent becomes poorer as swelling progresses. For realistic values of 0 < χ < 1 and p/p 0 of 0.3, we may expect an overall film swelling ratio of 1.05 < h/h 0 < 1.15, or about 10%. The crystalline π-stacked lamellae swell even less.

Vapor transport is relatively fast in our chamber. For a typical vapor diffusion coefficient through air of 0.1 cm2 s–1 and a diffusion length of 1 cm, the diffusion time is only 10 s. Even for a solvent system with a relatively low p = 10 mbar, the amount of solvent required to saturate a 100 nm-thick film to reach h/h 0 = 1.1 is contained within a 0.1 cm headspace. On the other hand, the sorption time may be of the order of 10–100 s for typical molecular films and several 102 s for P3HT:PCBM films. Thus, the rate of film swelling in our experiments is not limited by vapor transport but by film sorption. Vapor transport becomes limiting only for low-volatility solvents, such as 1-chloronaphthalene, where the evaporation rate of 0.2 nm s–1, estimated from empirical correlations, requires a transport time of several minutes to deposit sufficient solvent flux to the film surface.

Effect on Cell Performance Parameters

To investigate the effects of SVC on device performance, we exposed PM6:PYIT (1:1 w/w) films to different solvent vapors at controlled activities (Table ). Exposure to 1-chloronaphthalene vapor for 30 min degrades both V OC and FF, but improves J SC slightly, causing PCE to drop marginally from 13.2% to 12.9% (S/N 2 cf S/N 1). Thus, 1-chloronaphthalene has to be removed after fabrication to avoid it harming the film. Exposure of the film to chloroform vapor at 1% activity (i.e., 2.3 mbar) for 3 min does not significantly affect the V OC, FF, and J SC parameters of the cell (S/N 3 cf S/N 1), but increasing the chloroform vapor activity to 10% (i.e., 23 mbar) for 1 min markedly improves both V OC and FF, raising PCE to 14.7% (S/N 4; p-value ≪ 0.01). Correspondingly, the open-circuit series resistance (dVdI|VOC) advantageously reduces from 4.9 Ω to 4.4 Ω. The JV characteristics are compared in Figure a. These improvements suggest a reduction of recombination through better morphology of the bulk heterostructure. ,, However, raising the vapor activity to 100% (i.e., 230 mbar) by employing pure chloroform in the solvent reservoir severely degrades all cell parameters even after a 1 min exposure (S/N 5). This suggests degradation of the donor–acceptor morphology. Such a degradation is also known in P3HT:PCBM films, where exposure to pure solvent vapors also degrades cell performance, attributed to vapor-induced segregation of PCBM to the surface of the films.

Atomic force microscopy images (Figure b) show that chloroform SVC up to 10% activity does not alter the surface morphology nor the surface roughness (root-mean-square roughness, 10.7 Å over 3 × 3 μm2), but SVC at the equilibrium vapor pressure of chloroform causes the surface morphology to become finer and smoother (root-mean-square roughness, 9.4 Å). Thus, the initial improvement is not related to any visible change at the mesoscopic length scale of the film morphology. In particular, the peaking of V OC, FF, and PCE parameters at an intermediate SVC activity and time confirms that the optimal morphology occurs at an intermediate state of the transformation (Figure ). The morphology of the polymer–polymer bulk heterostructure improves and then degrades, presumably through overcoarsening or crystallization. Hence, the optimal condition is dictated by kinetic rather than thermodynamic control.

Remarkably, even the vapor of nonsolvents, e.g., cyclohexane, can alter cell performance. The solubilities of PM6 and PYIT in cyclohexane are less than 10 μg mL–1. Yet, exposure to cyclohexane vapor improves PCE at low activity and short exposure but degrades PCE at high activity and long exposure (S/N 6–8). The SVC phenomenon depends on the solvent. Chlorobenzene degrades PCE through FF, while both 1,2-dichlorobenzene and 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene do not (S/N 9–11). Similar SVC effects are also observed for both PM6- and PYIT-rich films (i.e., 1.2:1 and 1:1.2 w/w, respectively). In general, we find enhancements in the cell parameters for chloroform SVC at low activity and short exposure, but a reversal in the equilibrium vapor (S/N 12–15, 17–20). 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene may also be useful (S/N 16, S/N 21).

Effect on Shunt Resistance

SVC can surprisingly and advantageously increase the shunt resistance of the cells (dVdI|V=0) , reducing their parasitic power dissipation, especially at low irradiances away from the standard test conditions. The shunt resistance in OSCs arises largely from film defects. For simplicity, it can be characterized by the dark leakage current density J d at a fixed bias, defined here to be 0.60 V, near the maximum power point of the cell.

Standard cells with the balanced PM6:PYIT composition exhibit a broad distribution of J d from 20 μA cm–2 to over 500 μA cm–2, with modes at 50 and 500 μA cm–2, corresponding to shunt resistivities of 12 kΩ cm2 and 1.2 kΩ cm2, respectively (Figure a). Chloroform SVC at 1% activity upshifts the J d distribution, but SVC at 10% and higher activities narrows and downshifts the distribution, eliminating the high-leakage tail (Figure b). The log-mean shunt resistivity improves from 4 kΩ cm2 to 24 kΩ cm2 after chloroform SVC at 10% activity (Table ). Cyclohexane SVC at 1–10% activity can also surprisingly suppress the high-leakage tail (Figure c), but pure 1-chloronaphthalene vapor at long time worsens this tail (Figure d). Similarly, both PM6-rich and PYIT-rich films also benefit from SVC (Figure e,f, respectively). The suppression of leakage current is immensely beneficial to improving low-light performance of indoor PV cells.

These results reveal that sorption of solvent vapors, including those of nonsolvents, can “heal” shunt-causing film defects, presumably by inducing favorable nanoscopic-scale reorganization in the film, even at far subsaturation vapor pressures.

Mechanism of SVC

To probe the nature of this reorganization, we employ Vis–NIR spectroscopy to measure the response of PM6:PYIT (1:1 w/w) films to solvent vapors. Compared to their semidilute solutions, PM6:PYIT films show a further redshift of both the PM6 and PYIT π–π* bands and a relative intensification of the PYIT 0–0 peak (Figure a). The shift is larger for PYIT (792 → 802 nm; 20-meV redshift) than for PM6 (620 → 623 nm; 10-meV redshift). This suggests more extensive π-stacking interaction between PYIT segments in the film than in solution. , Electronic spectroscopy is the spectroscopic technique of choice here, as it is more uniformly sensitive across the bulk of the film. In contrast, X-ray scattering is primarily sensitive to the crystalline domains.

Exposure of the film to certain nonsolvents, for example, 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol, even at unit activity, does not alter the spectrum of the film (Figure a). These nonsolvents are termed “inactive” with respect to film morphology. Thus, deposition of the EIL overlayer using 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol does not alter the PM6:PYIT underlayer. Exposure of the film to the vapor of good solvents alters the π–π* band shapes. The subtle changes are readily observed by taking spectral differences. For example, exposure to 1-chloronaphthalene induces a blueshift of the 802 nm band but a redshift of the 528 nm band of PYIT, accompanied by intensification of the 623 and 577 nm bands of PM6. These changes indicate swelling of both PM6 and PYIT domains, leading to in-plane ordering of the former but disordering of the latter. A postbake locks in a permanent spectral redshift for both PYIT and PM6, indicating better π-stacking and/or conformational order in the final baked film. This reorganization appears to be associated with phase coarsening and film defect formation, as it degrades FF, V OC, and shunt resistance.

On the other hand, exposure to chloroform vapor at 1–10% activity induces changes to the film in two stages. The first stage on the shorter time scale (1 min) relates primarily to swelling of PYIT, while the second stage on longer time scale (10 min) relates to reorganization of both PM6 and PYIT, similar to 1-chloronaphthalene (Figure c,d). The first stage benefits FF, V OC, and shunt resistance, presumably by improving PYIT connectivity while closing pinholes in the film, while the second stage harms FF, V OC, and shunt resistance.

Exposure to cyclohexane also induces changes to the film. Thus, this nonsolvent is termed “active” with respect to film morphology. Cyclohexane vapor at 1% activity induces similar changes as 1-chloronaphthalene, although baking reverses most of the changes (Figure e). While cyclohexane is unable to dissolve PM6 nor PYIT, it can still swell their side chains on account of similar solubility parameters, , as in the case also of regioregular P3HT films. This side-chain swelling evidently produces sufficient free volume and pressure to alter the packing and/or conformation of the PM6 and PYIT backbones, leading to the observed spectral and performance changes. Raising the cyclohexane vapor pressure to the equilibrium value leads to a significant permanent changeredshift of PYIT and blueshift of PM6. Clearly, the vapor of nonsolvents can also strongly alter the morphology of polymer semiconductor films through their side chains.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated that solvent vapor conditioning at subequilibrium vapor pressures can be employed to improve the power conversion efficiency and shunt resistance of all-polymer solar cells. For example, exposure to chloroform vapor at 10% activity for a short duration of 1 min has produced a new performance benchmark for PM6:PYIT solar cells (PCE, 14.7%). This improvement is associated with a subtle reorganization, primarily in the PYIT domains by the sorbed solvent, which is not visible in mesoscopic images. In fact, swelling of the alkyl side chains by active nonsolvents can alter backbone order and/or packing morphology sufficiently to alter device performance. Thus, solvent vapor conditioning is a rich space that deserves more attention for its positive and negative effects. As a corollary, the rigorous monitoring and control of solvent vapors in the fabrication atmosphere is of critical importance for reproducibility and performance optimization. Such monitoring and control must extend to submillibar pressures for lowly volatile solvents, which requires fast air change with solvent scrubbing. Finally, the results here illuminate why annealing of P3HT:PCBM films gives different results depending on whether the annealing is performed in a closed glovebox or open air. Part of the variability in reported literature results must be attributed to variation or loss of control of the composition of the fabrication atmosphere.
