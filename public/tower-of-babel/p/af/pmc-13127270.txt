Successful Cyclosporine Salvage Therapy in Refractory Lupus Nephritis: A Case Study From Ethiopia
Authors: Daniel Rebuma Bekele, Birbirsa Sefera Senbeta, Alemayehu Abebe Guji, Mohammed Kedir Shukri, Kidus Tesfaye Bezabih, Yonas Damtew Bejiga, Kedir Negesso Tukeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLupus nephritis is a severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), often requiring immunosuppressive therapy. Standard regimens include corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), or cyclophosphamide. Calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) have shown therapeutic promise, but their use in low-income settings remains constrained by limited availability and cost.ObjectiveTo highlight the potential role of cyclosporine as salvage therapy in class V lupus nephritis when conventional treatments fail or are inaccessible, particularly in resource-limited settings.MethodsWe report the clinical course of a 28-year-old Ethiopian woman with biopsy-proven class V lupus nephritis and nephrotic syndrome. The patient presented with fatigue and generalized edema. Initial therapy with MMF and corticosteroids was administered, but no clinical improvement was observed. Cyclosporine-based triple therapy was subsequently introduced, alongside supportive care.ResultsFollowing initiation of cyclosporine, the patient achieved partial remission characterized by reduction in proteinuria and stabilization of clinical symptoms. Generalized edema improved, and fatigue lessened. No major adverse effects were reported during the observation period.ConclusionsCyclosporine may serve as a pragmatic therapeutic option for class V lupus nephritis in resource-limited settings where standard regimens are ineffective or unavailable. This case underscores the importance of considering CNIs as salvage therapy. Further controlled studies are warranted to evaluate efficacy, safety, and to define the role of cyclosporine in treatment algorithms for lupus nephritis.Keywords: Case report, Cyclosporine, Disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug, Ethiopia, Lupus nephritis, Systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Lupus nephritis (LN) represents one of the most critical and debilitating manifestations of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), an autoimmune disorder with multi-organ involvement. LN occurs in 35–60% of adult SLE patients and is present at diagnosis or within the first few years of disease in up to 80% of children, frequently determining long-term morbidity and mortality for the affected population.1, 2, 3, 4 In Ethiopia and other countries in sub-Saharan Africa, LN emerges as a major cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD), reflecting the global epidemiological trends, where SLE disproportionately affects women of childbearing age and individuals of African or Asian descent.

The pathophysiology of LN is characterized by the formation of nephritogenic autoantibodies—most notably anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA)—that form circulating immune complexes. These complexes deposit within the renal glomeruli, triggering local inflammation through complement activation and subsequent infiltration of lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils.2 This immune response leads to a spectrum of kidney histopathology, classified according to the revised International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) criteria into six major classes, ranging from minimal mesangial (Class I) to advanced sclerosing (Class VI) disease.5

Across sub-Saharan Africa, and specifically in Ethiopia, precise LN incidence and prevalence data are limited, in part due to underdiagnosis, irregular access to renal biopsy, and lack of comprehensive health registries. Nevertheless, hospital-based case series have documented LN in approximately 38% of chronic kidney disease patients undergoing renal biopsy, with nearly 80% of these cases affecting women aged 20–43 years.4 These findings echo global figures but are compounded by socioeconomic challenges that increase disease severity, delay diagnosis, and result in poorer outcomes compared to higher-income regions.

In Ethiopian and central African cohorts, hypertension and nephrotic-range proteinuria are the most common clinical presentations of LN, followed by hematuria, worsening renal function, and serological abnormalities such as high ANA, anti-dsDNA, low complement, and anemia. Biopsy findings reveal a high prevalence of proliferative forms—Class III and IV—associated with more aggressive disease and a higher risk of progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) if untreated or undertreated.

Despite advances in therapy, LN prognosis in sub-Saharan Africa remains dismal for many patients, with 10–15% progressing to ESRD within a decade of diagnosis, and as many as 44% of patients with severe proliferative subclasses developing ESRD within 15 years.4 This is largely attributed to delays in diagnosis, non-adherence, recurring infectious complications, and systemic barriers to consistent implementation of standard-of-care regimens.

The clinical backdrop for the case of successful cyclosporine salvage therapy for LN in Ethiopia is thus defined by a high disease burden, structural barriers to care, and an urgent need for effective, innovative, and sustainable treatment approaches to improve patient outcomes.2,4,5 LN is a significant consequence of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) that causes inflammation and damage to the kidneys, potentially leading to renal failure.2,3,6 Symptoms may include edema, elevated blood pressure, changes in urine output, weariness, and joint pain. Blood and urine tests, imaging examinations, and, in many cases, a kidney biopsy are required to make the diagnosis. Immunosuppressive drugs, including corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), cyclophosphamide, and calcineurin inhibitors (CNI) such as voclosporin and tacrolimus, are often used in treatment.2,7 Antimalarials, blood pressure drugs, and lifestyle changes are also used. The prognosis varies by individual, but early diagnosis and intensive treatment improve results and reduce the risk of end-stage renal disease.3,6 Regular monitoring is required to successfully manage the illness (Table 1 showing key clinical data in Ethiopia and Africa).

Tacrolimus and voclosporin are effective CNIs for treating lupus nephritis (LN),6, 7, 8 but their availability is limited in low-income countries like Ethiopia. This case study discusses a 28-year-old Ethiopian woman diagnosed with class V lupus LN and nephrotic syndrome who achieved partial remission after six weeks of cyclosporine treatment at Saint Paul Hospital Millennium Medical College, highlighting an alternative treatment option in regions with limited access to other CNIs.

Case Description

Here we report a case of a 28-year-old female from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, who presented to our hospital with fatigue, progressive body swelling that started from the face and generalized within a month. She also had a low-grade fever and reddish urine. Otherwise, she did not have cough, shortness of breath, skin lesion, joint pain or swelling, vertigo, blurring of vision, or other systemic complaints. She did not have orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, abnormal body movement, body weakness, or yellowish discoloration of the skin or eyes. She did not utilize herbal medications. She has no known personal or family history of chronic medical conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, or heart illness; no history of interaction with confirmed or suspected cases of tuberculosis, no prior history of upper respiratory tract infections, ulcers, or congestion in the nose. Her childhood history was uneventful. On physical examination, she was well-looking with a body weight of 62 kilograms, height of 162 centimeters, and body mass index of 23.85 kg/m2. The patient was clinically stable but had facial edema; her blood pressure was 150/100 mm Hg, pulse rate of 112 beats per minute, which was regular, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute, afebrile with body temperature of 36.5°C, and she was maintaining her saturation of 94% with room air. Her conjunctivae were pink, and non-icteric sclerae were noted. The abdomen was protuberant, with bilateral generalized edema noted. The other system was non-revealing. There was coarse crepitus in the bilateral infra-scapular area of the lungs.

Investigations

Investigations revealed nephrotic-range proteinuria with both qualitative and quantitative markers of SLE. Anti-dsDNA and ANA serologic markers were increased 10 and 6 times, respectively. There was also moderate hypoalbuminemia. Liver enzymes were raised, indicative of hepatitis. The kidney function was normal. Abdominal sonography suggested renal parenchymal disease (Table 2).

Management and Follow-Up

LN in Ethiopia manifests within the broader spectrum of CKD and is associated with high morbidity, often complicated by additional factors such as co-existing infectious diseases, limited access to advanced diagnostic tools, and economic constraints that undermine continuity of care. Children and young adults face particularly severe forms, aligning with the global pattern of more aggressive SLE and LN presentation in people of African heritage.1 The above reality underscores an urgent need for intensified surveillance, early aggressive intervention, and context-specific adaptation of global treatment guidelines (Table 3)—including the flexibility to introduce salvage therapies such as cyclosporine when standard regimens fail or are not tolerated. Clinical symptoms indicated systemic lupus erythematosus, as did positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) and anti-double-stranded deoxyribonuclease antibody (anti-dsDNA), and a kidney biopsy revealed class V lupus nephritis with nephrotic syndrome clinical signs. The patient was started on typical nephrotic syndrome treatments, including prednisolone initiated at 1 mg/kg/day (60 mg daily as his weight was 62 kg), and subsequently tapered according to KDIGO guidelines,12 and MMF 1000 mg PO BID.13 After one month, the proteinuria worsened, with a 24-hour urine protein raised to 9864 mg, edema remained, and new-onset hypertension developed; therefore, nifedipine 20 mg PO BID was added to the initial therapy. Cyclosporine therapy was initiated for the management of lupus nephritis after a comprehensive evaluation excluded systemic infection, with no clinical, laboratory, or radiological evidence of active infectious disease at the time of treatment.

Cyclosporine was initiated at 100 mg PO BID, then reduced to 50 mg PO BID. Serum cyclosporine level monitoring was not available in our setting; therefore, dose adjustments were guided by clinical response and safety monitoring. In resource-rich environments, trough levels are typically maintained between 100 and 200 ng/mL to balance efficacy and nephrotoxicity risk. MMF was continued. Proteinuria decreased significantly to 1455 mg/24-h urine collection 6 weeks following cyclosporine beginning (partial remission), as did edema and general clinical status, with no adverse effects from immunosuppression documented to date (Table 4).

Discussion

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease that can significantly impact various organs, particularly the kidneys, leading to lupus nephritis (LN). LN presents with diverse clinical and histopathological features and is associated with a challenging prognosis, as conventional treatments yield a complete remission rate of only 20–30% after six months.14 Diagnosing LN primarily relies on renal biopsy, with no effective non-invasive biomarkers available,14,15 as was used in the diagnosis of our patient. The use of immunosuppressants, especially glucocorticoids, can lead to adverse effects that further hinder patient outcomes; hence, there are new biological agents targeting autoimmune and inflammatory pathways offering promising treatment avenues.

Recent advancements in understanding the disease's pathogenesis have facilitated improvements in therapeutic strategies, notably the development of specific immunomodulatory agents that effectively target pathogenic pathways, with good medium-term efficacy in clinical trials, leading to proteinuria remission and kidney function preservation, while reducing reliance on corticosteroids and toxic therapies.16,17 Intravenous cyclophosphamide is the standard treatment for proliferative lupus nephritis; however, these medications are either limited or not available, leading to suboptimal treatment of similar patients in this setting, and hence, cyclosporine is used with better improvement. Though the use of this drug is reported, as from a study that evaluated cyclosporine A as a safer alternative, involving 40 patients assigned to either cyclophosphamide or cyclosporine A treatment regimens, with the primary outcomes measured being remission and therapy response, which showed 26% remission and 43% response. The Cyclofa-Lune study (2010) demonstrated that Cyclosporine A (CsA) and intravenous cyclophosphamide (CYC) achieved comparable remission rates in proliferative lupus nephritis, though their safety profiles differed, with CYC linked to systemic toxicities and CsA primarily to nephrotoxicity but better overall tolerance, supporting CsA as a viable alternative for patients at risk of CYC-related complications. Complementing this, the 2018 study on low-dose CsA in resistant cases showed significant improvement in proteinuria and renal function stabilization with manageable side effects, highlighting CsA’s potential not only as an alternative but also as an effective salvage therapy, reinforcing its flexible role across different stages of lupus nephritis management.18,19 A case series from Maiduguri, North-Eastern Nigeria, described five patients with lupus nephritis, highlighting their clinical and histopathological features while underscoring the diagnostic difficulties and therapeutic constraints in resource-limited settings. Notably, no cyclosporine salvage therapy was reported, with most patients managed using steroids and cyclophosphamide.20 The use in our setup is of the first kind, with both significant remission and response in the patient, suggesting its use in the wider public, especially in places where standard medications are lacking. Furthermore, in contrast to our case, the review from Groote Schuur Hospital, University of Cape Town, emphasizes the challenges of delayed recognition and restricted access to advanced therapies in lupus nephritis.21 While standard regimens such as corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, and MMF are routinely employed, cyclosporine is only referenced within broader immunosuppressive strategies, with no published case report documenting its successful use as salvage therapy—underscoring the distinctiveness of our report. Nigeria and South Africa provide contextual evidence of lupus nephritis burden and management, but not parallel cyclosporine salvage therapy reports, making our case unique.

The effectiveness and safety of a combination of either low-dose cyclosporine or tacrolimus (Tac) added to MMF maintenance therapy in patients with proliferative lupus nephritis who experienced relapses22 showed no severe adverse effects, though our case used cyclosporine with MMF, and fortunately had no significant adverse events as well. Furthermore, refractory Class V LN presents a treatment challenge in general, even with combination therapy with standard medications.19,22 This case showed effective partial remission with cyclosporine following MMF failure. Further evaluation is needed to establish whether the partial remission has progressed to complete remission in the long term and across different patient profiles, which, if maintained, would be used as a salvage therapy in similar cases in a similar setting.

In summary, Cyclosporine, tacrolimus, and voclosporin are all CNIs used in lupus nephritis, but their outcomes differ: cyclosporine is effective in reducing proteinuria and achieving partial responses, though complete remission rates are generally lower and side effects such as hypertension and cosmetic changes are common; tacrolimus, particularly in combination with mycophenolate, achieves faster and more durable complete renal responses, though it carries risks of diabetes and neurotoxicity; voclosporin, the newest agent, has demonstrated superior complete renal response and rapid proteinuria reduction compared to standard MMF plus steroids, with a more favorable blood pressure profile and the advantage of fixed dosing without drug-level monitoring.23, 24, 25 In our setting, where access to standard medications is limited, cyclosporine was used as a practical alternative to ensure timely immunosuppressive therapy. This report is limited by the short duration of follow-up, the single-case design, and the use of concomitant therapies, which make it difficult to isolate the specific contribution of cyclosporine. As such, the findings cannot be generalized beyond this individual patient context. However, it may pave the way for further studies exploring cyclosporine as a potential alternative in resource-limited settings where access to standard therapies is constrained.

Conclusion

Lupus nephritis remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to standard immunosuppressive agents such as corticosteroids, MMF, cyclophosphamide, and newer CNIs like voclosporin and tacrolimus is restricted. This case demonstrates that cyclosporine, an older but accessible CNI, can serve as a viable salvage therapy for refractory class V lupus nephritis when conventional options are unavailable. Clinically, this highlights the importance of considering cyclosporine as part of the therapeutic armamentarium in low-resource environments, offering hope for patients otherwise facing poor outcomes. Future research should focus on well-designed clinical trials and multicenter studies to validate cyclosporine’s efficacy and safety in diverse populations, explore optimal dosing strategies, and assess long-term renal and cardiovascular outcomes. Such evidence will be critical to inform treatment guidelines and expand therapeutic options for lupus nephritis across Africa and other resource-constrained regions.

Patient Perspective

As someone living with lupus nephritis in a setting where medicines are scarce, I felt hopeless when the usual treatments were unavailable and my condition worsened. Starting cyclosporine gave me a second chance—it was accessible, and it worked when nothing else did. For me, this drug was more than just therapy; it restored hope, dignity, and the belief that my life could continue despite limited resources. Knowing that doctors are willing to adapt and that research is being done makes me feel that patients like me in Africa and other resource-constrained regions are not forgotten.
