Emerging substances of abuse and cheap highs among Nigerian youths
Authors: Nenubari Naabiae, Gideon S Alex, Fortune C Orish, Naomi A Mogborukor, Danladi C Husaini, Andrew Orowigho Oghenerukevwe, Orish Ebere Orisakwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubstance use among Nigerian youths has become a significant public health concern. The narrative review examines the trends and emerging substance use among Nigerian youths, highlighting the use of conventional and unconventional substances. Data were collected from academic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, media outlets and national newspapers using keywords such as “unconventional substance use in Nigeria,” “youth drug abuse,” and “emerging substances”. The results showed a disturbing trend of using unusual substances: fermented urine, lizard dung, pit-latrine gas, gutter water, boiled sanitary pads, and nitrous oxide (laughing gas). To account for this trend, socioeconomic deprivation, peer influence, curiosity, and attempts at self-medication of depression are considered. The health consequences are serious and involve neurotoxicity, respiratory failure, endocrine disruption, and even death. The proliferation of unconventional “cheap highs” reflects deep socioeconomic and mental health crises among Nigerian youths. This further calls for multi-sectoral interventions, public awareness campaigns, policy reforms targeting substance use, and expanded rehabilitation services.Keywords: Emerging substance of abuse, Cheap highs, Unconventional substance use, Public health

Introduction

The Nigerian youth population, which accounts for approximately 70% of the country’s total population is facing an unprecedented crisis [1, 2]. The escalating menace of harmful practices, illicit drug abuse, and mental health issues have become a pressing concern, threatening the very fabric of Nigerian society [3]. The intake of these substances can affect both the physical and mental health of the users and the public. Hard drugs are potent and toxic substances that cause significant risk to an individual’s physical and mental well being. It is illegal to be in possession of illicit drugs like methamphetamine, heroin, cocaine. The non-medical use of legally available drugs such as painkillers, sleeping pills, and the abuse of illicit substances such as cannabis, cocaine which have become widespread among Nigerian youths [4, 5]. Conventional substance of abuse refers to use of psychoactive substances that are widely recognized, established and produce dependence and addiction, while unconventional substance use is the intake of unknown or unpredictable substances in non-traditional or innovative ways. These unconventional substances and practices include the use of lizard dung, hydrogen sulphide, premium motor spirit, rubber solution, fermented and camel urine [6]. This narrative review appraisal aims to provide a comprehensive account of the current state of harmful practices, illicit drug abuse, and mental health issues among Nigerian youths. By examining the epidemiology, etiology, and consequences of these issues, this review seeks to provide a framework to inform policymakers, healthcare professionals, and other stakeholders about the urgent need for effective interventions and strategies to address this unfolding social malady.

Methodology

A narrative review was adopted for this study to explore the prevalence, patterns, and emerging trends of substance abuse among Nigerian youths, with a special focus on unconventional and improvised substances. However, relevant study was included in the research. Data were obtained electronic databases including PubMed, SCOPUS, Google Scholar, AJOL, Grey literatures and Media Outlets. The literature search included the following Keywords “substance use in Nigeria,” “youth drug abuse,” “emerging drugs,” “cheap highs,” “non-conventional substance use,” and “unconventional psychoactive substances in Nigeria.” The search primarily focused on studies from 2010 to 2025 in order to capture the focus, emerging trends. Substances of abuse reported outside Nigeria were excluded.

Results and discussion

Prevalence of substance of abuse

Twenty three studies were selected in this study as shown in Table 1. According to WHO, about 296 million people aged 15–64 had used psychoactive drugs in 2021 and about 39.5 million people are estimated to be affected by drug use disorders including harmful pattern of drug use or drug dependence [7]. The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) in the United State reported that young adults are the biggest abusers of prescription opioid pain relievers [8], Attention deficit hyperactive disorder, stimulants, and anti-anxiety drugs. Moreso, recent data have revealed alarming rates of cocaine (6%), opioid (5.5%), benzodiazepine (4.5%), methamphetamine (0.8%) and heroine (0.5%) misuse among young adults [9]. Mkpuru Mmiri (so called because it looks like ice block) known as methamphetamine hydrochloride is a new drug that has taken dominance among Igbo community in Eastern Nigeria [10]. According to a community-based study in Southeast Nigeria, the prevalence of Methamphetamine (Mkpurummiri) was 21.8%. Mkpuru mmiri which is majorly administered via the inhalational route 64.9% [11], for the treatment of depression (86.5%), has become a substance of abuse with health effects which range from sleep deprivation, depression to death in some instances. Similarly, a questionnaire study among Nigerian University students showed that the proportions of female and male students were 58.9% and 41.1%, respectively. Twenty percent (20%) of them had consumed alcohol while 16% had taken marijuana and /or opioids including tramadol and codeine [12]. In central Nigeria, 33% of students reported that they have used psychoactive substances, with alcohol and tramadol being the most consumed. Those aged 15–19 years were found to have a higher prevalence of substances use than other ages [13]. According to a study carried out in north central Nigeria, the percentage of non-conventional substance use included lizard dung (9.2%), hydrogen sulphide gas (Gas from pit toilet) (24.4%), Premium Motor Spirit (PMS) (16%), rubber solutions (20.9%), nail polish (17.9%), pawpaw leaves (17%), pawpaw seed (12%), Moringa (Zogale) leave (19.4%), Zakami seed 11.7%. Psychoactive substance use is a social and public health issue that has become a global epidemic affecting various countries, including Nigeria. Cannabis commonly known as ‘weed’ and Shisha smoking known as ‘water pipe’ emerged as one of the substance most abused in night clubs which is a global public health issue as reported, in another study in Ibadan Nigeria. The overall prevalence of shisha smoking was 7.1% [14]. The harmful practice of mixing and consuming multiple harmful illicit substances is notable among Nigerian youths, and it is rapidly spreading to other regions. A study in Lagos metropolis showed that the rate of use of proscribed addictive substances such as cannabis, heroine, and cocaine, ranged between 4.0 and 4.8% [15]. Commonly abused substances included cough syrup with codeine, cocaine, sildenafil, heroine, tramadol, diazepam, pentazocine, cannabis, Rohypnol, and amphetamines. The effect of this substance use has been that the abusers act irrationally, lack self-control, and display odd behaviours. Unlike some substance use, this case is not geographically confined, but rather a widespread case across Nigeria. The abuse of these substances was discovered to be driven by three primary motivations. Firstly, individuals seek enhanced energy to perform physically demanding labor. Secondly, they pursue euphoric feelings and thirdly, they strive for prolonged sexual performance. Nitrous oxide, a medically approved anesthetic, is also being misused by recreational users who inhale high concentrations through balloons or plastic bags, risking psychological addiction and brain damage due to oxygen deprivation [16]. Cases of laughing gas abuse in Nigeria have also been reported to be associated with health risks such as severe lung damage, respiratory arrest, slow cardiac function and potentially resulting to death [17]. Proper education and awareness creation, national re-orientation and promoting a culture that prioritizes health, well-being and safety will be useful in preventing the public health implications of this substance use among youths in Nigeria.

Papers included in this study

Emerging substance of abuse

Fermented human urine

Fermented urine abuse represents one of the concerning of unconventional substance use in Nigeria as shown in Table 1. Youths reportedly store human urine for several days, allowing it to ferment and release ammonia, which is then inhaled or ingested to achieve intoxication. This practice, predominantly found in northern Nigeria, is driven by poverty, curiosity, and the unavailability of conventional drugs [18, 19]. The resulting exposure to ammonia and microbial contaminants causes neurotoxicity, gastrointestinal infections, and hepatic dysfunction. The National Drug Law Enforcement Agency, raised an alarm that youths in Borno State are drinking fermented human urine, preserved over 10 days, as an alternative to hard drugs. Of those arrested, 53 had been convicted while 736 were undergoing counselling and rehabilitation [20].

Latrine waste and sanitary pads

Equally unsettling is the use of pit-latrine gas and boiled sanitary pads as intoxicants. Pit-latrine fumes, rich in hydrogen sulfide and methane, are inhaled for euphoric sensations [21, 22]. Hydrogen sulfide acts as a chemical asphyxiant, reducing oxygen transport and leading to dizziness, hallucination, and, in severe cases, respiratory failure. The boiling of sanitary pads has been reported in Indonesia, Zimbabwe and recently in Nigeria [23, 24]. This substance produces chemical extracts believed to contain chlorine and synthetic cellulose compounds that induce mild psychotropic effects. Users are typically driven by peer influence and affordability, unaware of the potential for toxic shock, endocrine disruption, and long-term neurological impairment. These practices reveal the extreme adaptive behaviors of youths in response to social neglect and the inaccessibility of formal drugs.

Gutter water

Gutter water also known as (monkey tail or gutter juice) is a concoction of alcohol, codeine syrup, cannabis extract, and occasionally tramadol often brewed illicitly in local environments. As presented in Table 2, its consumption is associated with energy enhancement, prolonged sexual performance, and social belonging among urban youths [25, 26]. However, this mixture poses a high risk of poly-drug toxicity, liver and kidney damage, and acute psychosis due to the unpredictable synergy of multiple psychoactive compounds. The emergence of such homebrewed cocktails highlights the transition from organized drug supply to locally improvised production, complicating law enforcement and health surveillance efforts. Furthermore, the normalization of “gutter juice” within nightlife and club culture contributes to its rising social acceptance despite grave health consequences.

Unconventional and emerging substances use

Laughing gas (Nitrous oxide)

Nitrous oxide abuse, the inhalation of laughing gas is a relatively new phenomenon among Nigerian youths, mirroring global recreational trends. Once restricted to medical and industrial use, nitrous oxide is now misused for its short-lived euphoric and dissociative effects. When inhaled in high concentrations from balloons or canisters, it induces a transient sense of relaxation and altered consciousness [27]. However, repeated use can cause oxygen deprivation, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive impairment, and in severe cases, asphyxiation or cardiac arrest. Nigerian media reports have documented a growing number of cases, particularly among university students and partygoers [28]. This trend reflects a Westernized influence on substance experimentation, amplified by social media, where nitrous oxide is portrayed as a harmless recreational aid. In reality, chronic misuse leads to neurological degeneration due to vitamin B12 inactivation and sustained hypoxia.

Lizard dung and sewer gas

The use of lizard dung and sewer gas is perhaps the most bizarre and toxic manifestation of emerging drug culture. Youths reportedly burn dried lizard feces or inhale fumes from sewage systems to experience intoxication [29]. These substances contain hydrogen sulfide, methane, and ammonia, all potent neurotoxins capable of causing headache, disorientation, respiratory paralysis, and death. Peer curiosity and social imitation are primary motivators, while the absence of awareness about the lethality of such gases exacerbates the risk. The abuse of these unconventional substances exposes a critical gap in toxicological literacy and public health education, especially among rural and low-income youths.

Socioeconomic and health effects associated with substance abuse

Substance use is linked to violent behavior, resulting in physical, emotional, or sexual abuse [30]. Drug and substance addicts are more likely to perpetrate violence against their spouses or children, leading to broken families [31]. Substance use also has devastating health consequences, including increased risk of life-threatening diseases such as cancer, sexually transmitted disease, heart disease and mental health conditions (bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders, antisocial personality disorder illnesses [32]. In Nigeria, a significant proportion of these health issues have been linked to substance use. Moreover, substance use is a leading cause of mental illnesses [33]. Alarmingly, a substantial percentage of young people who develop mental health issues have been found to be users of overdose of marijuana, inhalants, or other illicit substances [34–36]. A recent study among Nigerian students showed that 24.5% have used drugs for recreational purposes and 17.5% are current users [37]. Substance use poses a significant threat to socioeconomic stability and public health globally, with far-reaching consequences for individuals, families, and communities. Another study on Nigerian youths revealed that 47.8% engage in substance use to feel good/high. Peer pressure and socioeconomic status significantly influenced substance use [38]. Youths attributed their abuse to parental genes (59.1%), culture and ethnicity (78.4%), and environment (76.2%). In Kaduna, Nigeria, this abuse has reached alarming levels, with a prevalence rate of 21% among secondary school students [39], rendering it a major public health concern. Furthermore, a study among selected southwestern universities in Nigeria reported a substance use prevalence rate of 45.7% [40]. Additionally, significant correlations have been reported between family dynamics, geographical location, and substance use [41]. Research suggests that the relationship between socioeconomic status (SES) and substance use in young adulthood is complex. Data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics in the United States indicates that lower childhood SES is associated with smoking, while higher SES is linked to alcohol and marijuana use, revealing that young adults from higher SES backgrounds are more likely to engage in substance use [42]. Furthermore, the proliferation of harmful practices, including cultism and gang violence, has contributed to a culture of fear, intimidation, and despair [43, 44], resulting to an increase in mental health issues among Nigerian youths with attendant socioeconomic cascades like poverty, unemployment, and social inequality [45, 46]. The lack of access to mental health services, coupled with the stigma surrounding mental illness, has exacerbated this crisis [47]. The Basic Registry and Information System in Nigeria (BRISIN) has called for urgent sensitization against the use of pit latrine as many youths now sniff latrines as substitute for codeine significant socioeconomic and health challenges was the effects among abusers and the use of sanitary pads and diapers was reported an abused substance [48], similar case was reported in Zimbabwe, as it was discovered by the youths to be a favorable way of getting high because of accessibility and affordability [23]. Police in Indonesia have arrested several teenagers who reportedly used the chemicals in menstrual pads to get high [24]. The use of unconventional substance among youth is a growing incidence necessitating the need for substance use advocacy and government intervention.

The study illustrates a shift from traditional psychoactive substances like cannabis, opioids, and alcohol to unconventional and harmful variants, including fermented urine, lizard dung, gutter water, pit-latrine gas, and boiled sanitary pads. This evolution reflects the desperate adaptation to economic hardship, limited access to conventional drugs, and psychosocial instability. Socioeconomic deprivation emerged as a central driver of this behavior. Many youths are driven to unconventional substances due not only to the affordability but also due to peer influence, unemployment, and psychological distress. The normalization of these practices on social media platforms further compounds the problem, where young people are often exposed to misleading portrayals of these substances as cheap highs.

Implication of the study

The findings of this study have significant implications on the Nigerian society. The health effects are serious and diverse, spanning neuro-impairment, respiratory distress, gastrointestinal infections, and psychiatric disorders to fatal toxicities. The inhalation of hydrogen sulfide and ammonia from latrine gas or fermented urine poses acute risks of asphyxiation and neurotoxicity, while the ingestion of sanitary pad extracts may cause hormonal disruption and organ failure. From a sociocultural perspective, substance use has transformed from a hidden activity to a socially situated coping behavior, reflective of systemic governance, youth engagement, and mental health support failures. The study emphasizes that drug abuse is no longer a problem concerning illicit substances alone but an expression of disregard for society and a manifestation of mental and emotional desperation among the youth of Nigeria. This phenomenon constitutes a toxicological and public health challenge due to the unforeseen chemical composition that such improvised substances would be made of. Without an appropriate system for toxicovigilance in place, it is quite hard to monitor such emerging threats, thus placing a heavier burden on healthcare.

Recommendation

Improve public awareness and education

Youth sensitization on the dangers of both conventional and emerging substances through national campaigns is needed to curb the growing menace. This can be achieved through social media advertisement, outreaches and educational programs in rural and urban areas. Collaboration with media outlets, religious organizations, non-governmental organization and government agencies are necessary to broader reach and impact.

Develop a comprehensive national drug surveillance framework

Establishment of a Toxicovigilance and Substance Monitoring System under the NDLEA and Federal Ministry of Health to monitor new substances and detect usage patterns in real time is recommended, this would ensure tracking and urgent intervention.

Expand mental health and rehabilitation services

Improve access to affordable, youth-friendly mental health care, counselling, and rehabilitation programmes, particularly in rural and underserved communities to facilitate recovery from addiction.

Policy reforms and enforcement

Government agencies should strengthen existing drug laws in respect of unconventional and improvised substances by its implementation and punishment of offenders.

Youth empowerment and socioeconomic interventions

Addressing the root causes of substance abuse is vital in eliminating the challenge and reducing social vices. This can be achieved through investment in education, job opportunities, and poverty alleviation programs. Empowerment of youth through skill acquisition and entrepreneurship can prevent idleness and a shift them from drug-seeking behaviours.

Conclusion

Drug use is among the social menaces affecting the youthful population in Nigeria, the effects include criminal activities, depression, school dropout, and suicidal attempts among others. The present study demonstrates the emergence of new and unconventional substances and cheap highs among Nigerian youths. This widespread abuse has public health and socio-economic consequences which demand urgent attention that encompasses education and awareness campaigns, the establishment of a policy framework for substance users, and restrictions that involve all strata of the society.
