Regulatory roles of GA and sugar metabolism genes with metabolomic profiling during seed dormancy release in Gleditsia sinensis
Authors: Fuhua Liu, Yang Zhao, Xiurong Wang, Qingying Zeng, Xingfu Zhang, Biao Wang, Lang He, Xueyan Jian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGleditsia sinensis is a significant economic and ecological tree species in Guizhou Province, offering high economic value and substantial development opportunities. Reproduction of G. sinensis primarily involves seed propagation, requiring a yearly supply of high-quality seeds for production. Nonetheless, seeds are characterised by a hard and dormant composition, and the precise molecular mechanism behind dormancy release remains unclear. This study conducted physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses on G. sinensis seeds at different stages of low-temperature stratification to elucidate the molecular regulatory mechanisms involved in seed dormancy release.ResultsThe content of soluble protein gradually decreases during the stratification and germination process, while the content of soluble sugar shows an upward trend. The sucrose content gradually decreases during the process of dormancy release in stratification and germination. The seeds of G. sinensis are jointly regulated by nutrient and energy metabolism, osmotic regulation, and the antioxidant enzyme system during the stratification and germination process. Through the analysis of gibberellin (GA) metabolism and sugar synthesis metabolic pathways, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to GA synthesis such as GA3, kaurenoic acid oxidases (KAO), gibberellin 20 oxidase (GA20ox), and gibberellin 2 oxidase (GA2ox), as well as DEGs related to carbohydrate metabolism such as Amylose (AMY), phosphofructokinase (PFK), and sucrose synthase (SUS), were screened. Correlation analysis identified 8 key DEGs regulating the GA synthesis pathway, including KAO and GA2ox, along with 23 DEGs associated with starch and sucrose metabolism, such as UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase 2 (UGP2).ConclusionsThe mechanism of dormancy release in G. sinensis seeds involves the synthesis and metabolic regulation of GA and sucrose. Background

G. sinensis is one of the key tree species for development in Guizhou Province, possessing high economic value and vast development potential. It is necessary to increase the scale and investment in seedling cultivation to promote the development of the G. sinensis industry [1]. G. sinensis propagation primarily relies on seedlings, with a low germination rate. It belongs to hard-seeded species, with a dense and hardness seed coat, a highly keratinized epidermis, difficulty in water absorption, and the embryonic root finds it challenging to penetrate the seed coat [2]. Seeds that have been removed from hardness still have difficulty in germination [3]. During the germination process using conventional techniques, there are problems such as excessive water absorption, easy rupture of the embryonic root, bursting of the seed coat, outflow of endosperm, and bacterial infection. Stratified germination can effectively soften the seed coat, slow down the water absorption rate, and promote physiological after-ripening of seeds. Whether seed dormancy is lifted is directly related to seed germination and seedling production. Elucidating the mechanism of dormancy release of G. sinensis seeds is crucial for seedling cultivation.

Temperature is the main environmental factor that controls the release of seed dormancy. Low-temperature stratified germination is widely used to break the dormancy of various seeds. Khan proposed the “three-factor” theory of plant hormone regulation, believing that Gibberellin (GA) promotes seed dormancy release and germination, while Cytokinin (CTK) degrades Abscisic acid (ABA) [4], and ABA inhibits seed dormancy release [5]. CTK, Ethylene (ETH), and Brassinosteroids positively regulate wheat seed dormancy release and play a role in regulating ABA synthesis, catabolism, and signal transduction [6]. In Paris polyphylla seeds, genes associated with the metabolism and signaling pathways of ABA, GA, and Auxin (IAA), exhibit differential expression during the dormancy release process induced by seed stratification [7]. GA promotes seed germination by increasing the growth potential and cell elongation of the embryonic root, and weakens the endosperm and surrounding tissues of the embryonic root to overcome the mechanical binding force of the seed epidermis [8]. Although several plant hormones have been shown to promote the release of seed dormancy, the release of seed dormancy in some plants does not follow the same rules. Energy metabolism is crucial for the release of seed dormancy. Nutrients such as starch, protein, and lipids are consumed at high rates during seed germination and seedling establishment [9]. There are significant differences in the genes involved in glycerolipid metabolism, fatty acid degradation, starch and sucrose metabolism, and pentose phosphate pathway, glycolysis pathway, pyruvate metabolism, and tricarboxylic acid cycle in the dormancy release process [10], multiple genes involved in regulating germination of various seeds have been identified. When the sugar supply is insufficient, fatty acids such as linoleic acid are rapidly broken down to provide energy [9]. Hormones and energy metabolism can regulate the release of seed dormancy, and the study of hormones and energy metabolism can promote the storage and development of seed germplasm resources.

High-throughput transcriptomics provides gene expression profiles for biological growth and development. Metabolomics can reveal the relationship between metabolite function and metabolic network regulation, and reveal key pathways and regulatory networks during seed stratification and germination. GA biosynthesis gene expression is upregulated during dormancy-breaking in Pennisetum glaucum seeds [11]. Coordinated regulation of seed dormancy release in Pyrus calleryana involves Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with CTK and ETH metabolism/signaling, starch/sucrose metabolism, and ERF/MYB transcription factors (TFs) [5]. Metabolomic analysis of pecan seeds revealed that amino acids and sugars play an important role in regulating the release of seed dormancy [12]. Dormancy release and germination of seeds are closely related to anthocyanin and flavonoid synthesis and are also influenced by lipid and sucrose metabolism [13]. The continuous development of transcriptomics and metabolomics technologies is providing technical support and a theoretical basis for the mechanisms and regulatory mechanisms of seed dormancy and germination.

Seed dormancy is controlled by a number of regulatory factors, including hormone levels, energy metabolism, nutrients, and environmental factors. Despite major breakthroughs in dormancy release regulatory networks in Arabidopsis thaliana and cereal seeds [5]. Current research on breaking dormancy in G. sinensis seeds has primarily focused on physical and chemical treatments, such as sulfuric acid scarification and hot water immersion. However, the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying seed dormancy release remain poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, we investigated the effects of low-temperature stratification and sand storage treatments on dormancy release in G. sinensis seeds. By analyzing dynamic changes in gene expression associated with metabolic pathways and hormone biosynthesis, as well as corresponding shifts in metabolic profiles, this study provides critical insights into the mechanisms of dormancy release. Our findings establish a theoretical foundation for further research on G. sinensis seed germination and contribute to the sustainable utilization of its germplasm resources.

Materials and methods

Test material preparation

We selected three excellent G. sinensis trees in Huaxi District, Guiyang City, and collected high-quality seeds from half-sibling families (106.66 E, 26.42 N). The trees are vigorous, have large fruit sets and plump, healthy seeds. The harvested seeds are placed indoors for natural drying and then stored in a 4 °C refrigerator. These specimens were identified by Professor Yang Zhao of the Forestry College of Guizhou University as G. sinensis. A number of the collected seed samples have been deposited in the herbarium of the Forestry College of Guizhou University. The certificate specimen number of the seed specimen is Gs202312. The collection of plant material and experiments was conducted under relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation, and in the absence of competing conflicts of interest.

High-quality seeds with intact morphology were selected for sulfuric acid treatment. The seeds were immersed in 98% concentrated sulfuric acid for 40–50 min, with thorough agitation at 2 min intervals to facilitate testa softening and partial exfoliation. When the seed coat turns from brown to red or bright red, and more than 80% of the seed coats have fine cracks, remove the seeds and rinse them with distilled water until the pH of the distilled water approaches 7. Select seeds with cracks and soak them in a constant temperature water bath at 37 °C for 12 h. After soaking, pick the seeds that have swollen due to water absorption and place them in water. Remove the bad seeds floating on the surface, and select the superior seeds to soak in a 0.5% potassium permanganate solution for 10 min for disinfection treatment [14]. The fine sand substrate was first sun-dried outdoors for complete dehydration, followed by thorough washing with deionized water to remove particulate impurities and organic residues. The cleaned sand was then loosely packed into autoclave-resistant bags to prevent compaction during sterilization. Terminal sterilization was performed using a high-pressure steam autoclave (Model HVA-110, HIRAYAMA, Japan) at 121 °C for 120 min to achieve complete microbial inactivation. The sterilized sand was subsequently cooled to room temperature in a laminar flow cabinet before use in stratification experiments. Uniformly sized seeds after water absorption and expansion stage were randomly selected and sown in germination trays containing sterilized sand, maintaining a 1:5 (v/v) seed-to-sand ratio. The seeds were buried approximately 2 cm below the sand surface to ensure consistent stratification conditions. The germination tray is placed in a 4 °C refrigerator for low-temperature stratification to promote germination, with the humidity set at 75%. Observe the morphological changes of seeds and radicles daily, water them appropriately, and spray a 0.1% carbendazim solution daily for disinfection. Referring to the criteria established by previous researchers for the stratification and germination stages, the stratification time of G. sinensis seeds and the morphological changes during the seed germination process were used as the basis for dividing the germination stages of G. sinensis seeds [15–17]. During the process of sand storage and low-temperature stratification, seeds from each stage were collected to determine the hundred-seed weight. Thirty seeds from each stage were selected, and their transverse and longitudinal diameters were measured using a vernier caliper. The morphological changes in radicle development were observed and photographed under a stereomicroscope (Leica KL300, Germany).

Determination of seed water absorption rate, germination rate, and physiological indicators

To verify the impact of low-temperature stratification on the germination rate and water absorption rate of G. sinensis seeds, seeds without any treatment were selected as the control group (CK). Two experimental groups were established: one consisted of seeds that had been treated with sulfuric acid and warm water to induce water absorption and swelling, which were directly placed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator for germination testing (T1 treatment group); the other consisted of seeds that had also been treated with sulfuric acid and warm water to induce water absorption and swelling, but were subjected to low-temperature stratification for 8 d before being removed for germination testing (T2 treatment group). For each treatment, 100 seeds were placed in a petri dish with three replicates and cultivated in a constant temperature and humidity incubator at 25 °C, with 8 h of light per day and 60% humidity. The control group and the T1 treatment group were directly placed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator for 11 d. The T2 treatment group was removed after 8 d of low-temperature stratification for germination and compared with the control group and the T1 treatment group, which had been placed in the incubator for 8 d, in terms of water absorption rate. Subsequently, the T2 treatment group was placed in the constant temperature and humidity incubator for 3 d. The germination rates of the three treatment groups were compared on the 11th d. Ensure timely hydration daily, with germination considered when the radicle reaches ≥ 0.5 cm. Weigh and record using a 1/10,000 electronic balance, and calculate the water absorption rate. For each treatment, 50 seeds were selected to determine the water absorption rate, with three replicates set up.

Physiological parameter determination: Soluble sugar content was measured using the anthrone colorimetric method [18], soluble protein content was determined by the biuret method [19], and malondialdehyde (MDA) content was assessed via the thiobarbituric acid condensation method [20]. Free proline content was measured using the acidic ninhydrin method. Each treatment was replicated four times. Sucrose, fructose, and glucose content were determined using the hexokinase method [21].

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

According to the external morphological changes of the seed embryo, G. sinensis seeds in five stratified germination stages (A ~ E) were selected (Fig. S2). The embryonic root and plumule tissues of seeds were aseptically dissected, immediately rinsed with deionized water, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80℃. Three independent biological replicates were processed for each sample group. Total RNA was extracted using RNA prep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, China), and detected by agarose gel electrophoresis (1%), NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, USA). Library construction was performed exclusively with RNA specimens meeting rigorous QC thresholds: minimal 1 µg total input, concentration ≥ 35 ng/µL, and absorbance ratios (A260/280 ≥ 1.8, A260/230 ≥ 1.0) confirming nucleic acid purity. Magnetic beads with Oligo (dT) were employed in the process of AT base pairing with poly (A). The mRNA was then isolated and added to the fragmentation solution, which facilitates random fragmentation. The isolation of small fragments of approximately 300 bp was achieved through magnetic bead screening. Add 6 bp random primers and use the mRNA as a template to reverse transcribe a strand of cDNA. Subsequently, buffer, dNTPs, RNaseH, and DNA polymerase I were added to synthesize double-stranded cDNA and perform end repair, followed by adding an ‘A’ base to the 3’ end. The products were purified and fragments sorted, and the sorted products were used for polymerase chain reaction amplification and purification. When the sequencing library has been qualified, the Illumina HiSeq platform is employed for on-machine sequencing.

De novo assembly and annotation, differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

The raw reads were quality-controlled with fastp to remove adapter sequences and filter out low-quality reads [22]. Subsequently, the clean reads were subjected to Trinity software for de novo transcriptome assembly. Trinity successfully identified and reconstructed potential transcripts from the clean reads, which were then utilized for downstream analysis. For the selection of non-redundant sequences, CD-HIT software was employed to choose the longest coding sequences as representative unigenes [23]. Expression levels were estimated by integrating RSEM [24], and Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values were used to quantify the expression abundance of the corresponding single genes. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq R package (version 1.10.1) for inter-group comparisons. DEGs were screened according to |log2 Fold Change|>2 and p-value < 0.05. To obtain more comprehensive gene functional annotation information, the method of comparison by BLASTx alignment, seven databases (Table S1) were employed for gene functional annotation [25]. A series of Cluster analyses was performed using GEBE DEVONO& OmicShare cloud platform tool (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/), and DEGs were divided into 20 clusters, 8 of which were significantly enriched (p ≤ 0.01). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was used for functional annotation of DEGs.

weighted gene co‑expression network analysis of DEGs

The WGCNA R 4.3.1 software package [26] should be employed to ascertain the outcomes of weighted correlation network analysis, with the removal of genes exhibiting TPM expression values below 1 in the sample. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 [27]. The cor function was employed to compute the correlation between gene modules and differential metabolites (DEMs), with the p-value being determined by the corPvalueStudent function. The construction of heatmaps and the determination of correlations between gene modules and DEMs will be undertaken to identify key genes [28].

Untargeted metabolomics measurement and analysis

The samples were accurately weighed (50 mg each), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then 400 µL of an extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1) was added. The samples were then frozen and ground. Low-temperature ultrasonic extraction was performed for 30 min, after which the samples were placed at −20 °C for 30 min. Finally, the supernatant was removed and the samples were analyzed on the machine. Metabolomic profiling was performed using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation was achieved following the manufacturer’s protocol for column specifications, mobile phase composition, and gradient elution. Raw data were processed with Progenesis QI software, with metabolite identification based on MS/MS spectral matching scores. DEMs were selected using the thresholds of variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, p-adjusted < 0.05, and fold change (FC) > 1.5.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

EIF5A was selected as the reference gene [29]. Ten DEGs involved in the regulation of GA and sugar metabolism synthesis and metabolic pathways were selected for analysis. Primers were designed using Primer 5.0 (Table 1). qRT-PCR was performed using the T100 Thermal Cycler PCR instrument (BIO-RAD, USA). The qRT-PCR reaction parameters were as follows: 95 ℃ for 3 min; 95 ℃ for 5 s, 60 ℃ for 15 s, 40 cycles. The reaction system consisted of 1.5 µl each of forward and reverse primers, 2.5 µl of template cDNA, 12.5 µl of 2×SYBR Premix ExTaq, and 8.5 µl of ddH2O (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) [30]. The relative gene expression levels were calculated based on the threshold cycle number using the 2−∆∆Ct method [31]. Three technical replicates and three biological replicates were performed.

Results and analyses

Comparison of the seed water absorption rate and germination rate

The untreated G. sinensis seeds exhibited remarkably low germination and water absorption rates (Fig. S1), with a positive correlation observed between these two physiological parameters. The seeds subjected to sulfuric acid and warm water treatment (T1) demonstrated significant increases in both germination rate (47.3%) and water absorption capacity compared to untreated controls, although the overall germination percentage remained relatively low. Comparative analysis revealed that seeds subjected to low-temperature stratification treatment (T2) exhibited significantly enhanced germination performance, demonstrating an 87.32% increase in germination rate and a 17.57% improvement in water absorption capacity relative to the sulfuric acid and warm water-treated group (T1). During the low-temperature stratification and germination process, it was observed that this treatment allows the seeds to absorb water slowly and sufficiently, making the radicles less prone to breakage and the seed coats less likely to burst. Notably, the sand medium provides critical physical protection that minimizes bacterial contamination risk, even in cases of endosperm leakage. Usually, the seed coat is ruptured by the endosperm absorbing excessive water, and the outflowing endosperm, due to its high nutrient content and other conditions, is highly susceptible to bacterial proliferation, leading to seed mold and deterioration. More importantly, the sustained moisture environment created by low-temperature stratification promotes physiological after-ripening processes, thereby regulating hydration kinetics and successfully breaking seed dormancy. These combined effects demonstrate that low-temperature stratification serves as an effective pretreatment for enhancing germination performance in G. sinensis seeds through controlled water uptake and biochemical maturation.

Classification of germination stages and phenotypic changes of G. sinensis seeds in stratified germination

Through systematic morphological analysis of G. sinensis seeds at different stratification time points, we classified the germination process into five distinct developmental stages (A-E) based on embryonic characteristics (Fig. 2). Stage A represents untreated control seeds (Fig. 2A&B), while Stage B comprises seeds immediately after sulfuric acid/warm water treatment showing initial imbibition (0 d stratification, Fig. 2C&D). Stage C seeds (4 d stratification) exhibit embryonic root swelling without breaking through the seed coat. (Fig. 2E&F), progressing to Stage D (8 d stratification) with visible embryonic root protrusion (0.2 ~ 0.5 cm length, Fig. 2G&H), and finally reaching Stage E (12 d stratification) characterized by advanced embryonic root growth (> 2 cm length, Fig. 2I). Untreated G. sinensis seeds exhibited a relatively small size, but their volume increased significantly upon water absorption. During low-temperature stratification, seed coat permeability progressively improved, leading to continuous expansion in seed diameter and weight. Concurrently, the embryo underwent differentiation and growth, culminating in radicle protrusion (germination). The germination rate (pearl rate) increased steadily throughout stratification, with a particularly pronounced rise during stage D, indicating active dormancy release at this phase (Table S2).

Changes in sucrose, glucose, and fructose content during the dormancy release process of G. sinensis seeds

The sucrose content gradually decreased during the process of stratification and germination-induced dormancy release (Fig. 2a) , reaching its lowest point in period D at 2.32 mg·g−1. Compared to the control (A) (45.71 mg·g−1), periods B, C, D, and E showed decreases of 38.24%, 70.37%, 94.92%, and 87.63%, respectively, with significant differences (p < 0.05). The fructose content exhibited a trend of initial decrease followed by an increase, with a 67.76% decrease in period B compared to period A. However, there were no significant changes in periods B, C, and D (Fig. 2c).

Functional annotation of unigene and DEGs analysis

The Illumina HiSeq platform was used to sequence 15 libraries, and a total of 103.61G of data was obtained. The error rate of each sample was below 0.02%. The average Clean Reads of Q20 and Q30 accounted for 95.9% and 93.3% of the total Clean Reads, and the GC content accounted for 95.9% and 93.3% of the total Clean Reads, the GC content accounted for 44.7%, indicating a good sequencing performance (Table S3). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the samples subjected to different treatments. The results demonstrated that the biologically repeated samples exhibited superior aggregation (Fig. S2J), suggesting that each group of samples possesses specificity and excellent reproducibility, thereby indicating the reliability of the data. The correlation coefficient within the group was found to be high (Fig. S2K).

Compared with period A, 5216 unigenes were up-regulated and 3873 unigenes were down-regulated in period B (Fig. S3). Compared with period B, 8684 unigenes were up-regulated and 7207 unigenes were down-regulated in period C. The number of genes involved in dormancy release is the largest in the early stages of stratification germination (period B ~ C), while the number of genes involved in dormancy release is the least in the later stage of stratification germination (period C ~ D).

When the soft threshold (β) was set to 6, the resulting network distribution was scale-free (Fig. S12B). The pattern correlation heatmap illustrates the presence of modules exhibiting high correlation (Fig. S12C). Correlation analysis between co-expression network modules and metabolite expression levels revealed that in the GA synthesis and metabolism pathway, gibberellin A51 (GA51) exhibited the highest correlation with the blue module (Fig. S13). Furthermore, the correlation between genes and modules was found to be significant, with a positive correlation between the gene and GA51 (r = 0.818, p < 1.94e-4). In the starch and sucrose metabolic pathways, the metabolite dextran exhibits the highest correlation with the turquoise module. Furthermore, the correlation between genes and modules is significantly negatively correlated with genes and dextran (r=−0.871, p < 2.37e-5). The correlation between isomaltose and the blue module is the highest, with a significant positive correlation between genes and modules (r = 0.736, p < 1.76e-3). Sucrose exhibits a poor correlation with each module and a high correlation with the black module.

The correlation between genes and modules was combined with the correlation between gene expression and metabolite expression in the five periods. Genes with expression levels less than 5 were removed. Finally, the key metabolite GA51 and two DEGs (TRINITY_DN14149_c0_g1) in the GA synthesis pathway were identified. TRINITY_DN691_c1_g1 demonstrated a significant positive correlation (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5a). They belong to the genes encoding KAO and GA2ox. In the starch and sucrose metabolism pathways, the metabolite D-glucopyranose is significantly negatively correlated with 14 DEGs (Fig. 5b), including 2 encoding UGP2 (TRINITY_DN2136_c0_g1, TRINITY_DN20934_c0_g1), 2 encoding 6-phosphate/phosphate translocator protein (GPT) (TRINITY_DN46536_c1_g1, TRINITY_DN7996_c0_g1), and 3 encoding glucose-1-phosphate adenylyltransferase (GLGC) (TRINITY_DN7100_c0_g1, TRINITY_DN7938_c0_g2, TRINITY_DN1824_c0_g2).

Discussion

Regulation of nutrient metabolism and osmotic adjustment substances during dormancy release

Low-temperature stratification is beneficial for breaking the dormancy and promoting the germination of G. sinensis seeds. As the stratification period extends, the water absorption capacity of the seed coat continuously increases, leading to an expansion in seed volume, and ultimately, the radicle breaks through the seed coat. During the stratification process, the seed’s transverse diameter, longitudinal diameter, weight, and the rate of radicle emergence all show significant increases. The germination and dormancy of seeds are closely related to the dynamic balance of internal energy within the seeds. During the process of transitioning from a dormant state to a germination state, the stored substances in seeds are converted from macromolecular forms into absorbable small molecular substances. Studying the dynamic changes in storage substance content helps to reveal the intrinsic physiological mechanisms during seed dormancy [32]. Soluble proteins, as osmotic regulators involved in the vital activities of seeds, exhibit a gradual decrease in content during the process of stratification-induced dormancy release. This reduction may be attributed to the enhanced metabolic activities that consume substantial energy, leading to the depletion of the initial raw materials for protein synthesis. Consequently, soluble proteins are continuously broken down into smaller molecules such as free amino acids to provide energy. Similar findings have been reported in studies on Paeonia ostia ‘Feng Dan‘ [33] and Paeonia decomposita [34]. Soluble sugars are energy substances that can be directly absorbed by seeds for respiratory consumption and utilization, and they can be formed by converting starch, fat, and soluble proteins in the seeds. Soluble sugars can directly influence the development process of seeds [35]. Soluble sugars include glucose, maltose, and sucrose. During the process of dormancy release in Acanthopanax senticosus seeds, a gradual increase in soluble sugar content was also observed [36]. In our research, the extension of low-temperature stratification time led to an upward trend in the soluble sugar content of G. sinensis seed embryos. Various metabolic activities within the seeds gradually intensified, and the demand for carbohydrates continuously increased. Glucose is the most important sugar in plant metabolism. The intermediate products of glucose breakdown, such as amino acids, nucleotides, and fats, serve as crucial sources of material and energy for cellular metabolism. During the germination process of guar bean seeds, the sucrose content decreases appropriately, while the fructose and glucose contents gradually increase. The total soluble sugar content shows no significant change. The observed sucrose content exhibited a consistent trend with our findings, whereas fructose and glucose contents displayed inverse variation patterns compared to the present results [37]. Notably, fructose and glucose contents showed rapid accumulation during embryonic root elongation (period E), likely resulting from enhanced biochemical reactions and metabolic activity that catalyzed polysaccharide (e.g., starch) degradation into monosaccharides to provide energy and nutrients for embryonic growth. Concurrently, soluble protein content decreased during seed dormancy release, suggesting protein degradation into small molecular nitrogen and carbon sources to support embryo development. Proteins may indirectly participate in energy metabolism through the tricarboxylic acid cycle, leading to their degradation in content. The MDA content in G. sinensis seeds showed no significant change during the period from seed water absorption and expansion to the seed pearl stage. However, it significantly increased during the embryonic root elongation stage (period E), which may be attributed to certain stress factors or a significant rise in physiological and metabolic activities within the seeds during this period, leading to the production of a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS). A significant increase in MDA content was also observed after the dormancy release of Sternbergia lutea bulbs [38]. The proline metabolic pathway plays a crucial regulatory role in the dormancy release of Polygonatum cyrtonema seeds [39]. Proline significantly decreases during the radicle emergence (period D) and the embryonic root elongation (period E), possibly because the seeds have developed a certain level of stress resistance by this stage, leading to a reduced demand for proline, which may be converted into other substances. After seed dormancy is released, the cells are well-hydrated, osmotic stress is alleviated, and the osmotic protective function of proline is no longer necessary, leading to a decrease in its synthesis or an increase in its degradation. ROS signaling may stimulate ProDH expression, promoting proline oxidation.

Metabolism and regulation of endogenous hormones during G. sinensis seed dormancy release

The advent of high-throughput sequencing technology has led to the revelation of an increasing number of plant genome sequences, which are employed in a diverse range of biological studies, including genome sequencing, transcriptome sequencing, and epigenome sequencing. The KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the release of seed dormancy is primarily associated with ribosome, organic acid metabolism, sugar metabolism, hormone synthesis and transduction, and others. The synthesis and metabolism of various plant hormones are involved in regulating seed dormancy release and germination. ABA promotes seed dormancy, and GA promotes seed germination. The IAA regulates seed dormancy and germination by regulating the synthesis and transport of ABA and GA [40]. Seed dormancy is not only determined by the absolute content of plant hormones, but also related to the balance and proportional relationships among these hormones. Excessive ABA content may be the reason for the difficulty in germination of viable seeds [41]. GA is a positive regulator of seed germination and plays a promoting role in overcoming mechanical obstacles at the top of the embryonic root and softening the seed coat. KAO is a key enzyme regulating GA synthesis, and three DEGs encoding KAO were identified as up-regulated during the seed dormancy release process. IAA positively regulates the biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways of ABA and negatively regulates GA, inducing elongation and differentiation of embryonic cells to promote seed germination. In this study, the expression level of GID1 was relatively low during the early stages of stratification and germination, as well as during the seed coat rupture phase. However, it was significantly up-regulated during the embryonic root elongation period, indicating that GA plays a crucial role in promoting the release of dormancy and the growth of the embryo in G. sinensis seeds. GA2ox is primarily upregulated in seed dormancy release, and it has also been found in other plants such as tomatoes, where seed germination and development are associated with GA2ox [42, 43].

Energy metabolism and regulation of stored substances during the dormancy release process of G. sinensis seeds

The process of seeds returning from dormancy to active development necessitates a certain quantity of energy, and sugars and lipids represent the principal carbohydrates and energy storage substances in plants. In this study, the DEGs expression of two Amylose (AMY) and two E3.2.1.2 was significantly up-regulated during the pearl and embryonic root elongation (period D & E). Phosphoglucomutase (PGM) and GPT showed up-regulated expression, providing energy for seed germination. The enzymes invertase (INV), SPS, GPI, and GLGC play crucial roles in the biosynthesis and metabolism of sucrose [44]. The DEGs regulating INV show significantly up-regulated expression during both the pearl and embryonic root elongation stages. In the process of sugar fermentation, hexokinase (HK), pyruvate kinase (PK), and PFK were the key rate-limiting enzymes, determining the direction and rate of metabolic pathways [15]. PFK can catalyze the irreversible phosphorylation of fructose 6-phosphate to form fructose 1,6-bisphosphate [45]. In this study, the DEGs of five PFK were up-regulated during the pearl and embryonic root elongation (period D & E). During the process of seed dormancy release in G. sinensis, the DEGs of SUS and SDH were mostly up-regulated, providing carbohydrate substances for seed dormancy release.

The role of TFs in the release of dormancy in G. sinensis seeds

Transcriptome analysis revealed elevated expression levels of TFs from the MYB, AP2/ERF, C2C2, bHLH, and NAC families during late stratification and embryonic root elongation stages (period D&E), with most TFs exhibiting overall up-regulated patterns. bHLH family TFs may contribute to seed dormancy release through the ICE-CBF pathway-mediated scavenging of excess ROS [46]. The up-regulated of bHLH TFs coincided with significantly increased MDA content during stage E, suggesting enhanced metabolic activity and elevated ROS production. This temporal correlation indicates that bHLH-mediated antioxidant responses may protect against oxidative damage during the embryonic root elongation stage. Previous studies have demonstrated that low temperature stress during seed germination induces the accumulation of ROS and ABA, which contribute to maintaining seed dormancy [47]. However, both ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) function as oxidative and nitrosative signaling molecules that interact with phytohormones such as ABA and GA to regulate dormancy release and germination [48]. Importantly, moderate ROS accumulation has been shown to promote seed dormancy alleviation [49]. Our study observed a significant increase in MDA content during the embryonic root elongation stage (period E), coupled with significant up-regulated of ROS-related metabolic pathways in GO enrichment analysis, including ROS metabolic process (GO:0072593) and oxylipin biosynthetic process (GO:0031408). These results collectively suggest that controlled ROS generation plays a beneficial role in facilitating dormancy release in G. sinensis seeds. MYB TFs are the core regulators of ABA signaling, with MYB96 and MYB101 acting as positive regulators of ABA response during seed dormancy [5]. The up-regulated of MYB family TFs during late stratification, coupled with increased GA biosynthesis, suggests their potential regulatory role in maintaining the balance between ABA and GA signaling pathways. The AP2/ERF family TFs exhibited significant up-regulated during late stratification in G. sinensis, consistent with previous reports demonstrating their role in promoting seed germination through integration of ETH and GA signaling pathways [6]. As ETH is known to antagonize ABA and synergize with GA to weaken seed coat integrity, and considering that ERF subfamily members have been shown to directly activate GA biosynthesis genes (e.g., GA3ox) in callery pear [5]. The co-upregulation of AP2/ERF family TFs and GA metabolism genes (e.g., KAO, GA20ox) during the D and E periods suggests that they may enhance GA accumulation through similar mechanisms, thereby promoting radicle growth. The C2C2 family TFs regulate both ABA biosynthesis and signaling pathways, potentially facilitating seed dormancy release through ABA reduction. Furthermore, C2C2 family TFs have been reported to regulate cold-responsive genes, which may contribute to cellular homeostasis maintenance and cold adaptation during low-temperature stratification in G. sinensis seeds.

Correlation analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome reveals the molecular mechanism underlying the release of seed dormancy

In the correlation analysis, 8 key DEGs that regulate the GA synthesis pathway and 23 key DEGs that regulate the starch and sucrose metabolism pathways were identified. GA2ox is a key enzyme that catalyzes the degradation of active GA through biological activity [50]. GA2ox inactivates biologically active GA1 and GA4, thereby exerting a negative regulatory effect on GA synthesis [51]. KAO plays a pivotal role in the central step of the GA biosynthetic pathway [52], participating in the conversion of kaurenoic acid into GA12 and ultimately promoting GA synthesis. The activity of UGP2 can influence the supply of dextran, catalyze the conversion of D-glucose-1-phosphate, generate UDP-glucose, and ultimately facilitate the synthesis of amylose [53]. In this study, two DEGs that regulate UGP2 were identified. GPT catalyzes the conversion between glucose-6-phosphate and phosphate. Glucose-6-phosphate represents the pivotal substance in the dextran synthesis pathway, known as glycogenesis. Glucose-6-phosphate is converted into phosphoric acid by the action of GPT, thereby regulating the metabolism of dextran. SUS is an enzyme that promotes sucrose synthesis. It can catalyze the reaction between sucrose, glucose, and fructose, and its reaction is reversible. SUS is closely related to the sexual development of biological cold resistance [54] and may play a role in the low-temperature stratification germination process of G. sinensis. In the starch synthesis pathway, the enzyme plays a crucial role in the conversion of glucose molecules into sucrose molecules. In the sucrose metabolism pathway, SUS is involved in the decomposition reaction of sucrose molecules, resulting in the conversion of sucrose into glucose. GLGC can facilitate the reaction of dextran to synthesize starch, which may influence the rate of synthesis of starch synthesis. The enzyme catalyses the glycosyl transfer reaction of inorganic phosphate and glucose-1-phosphate to form α-D-glucose-1,4-phosphate. This process ultimately results in the formation of linear α-starch. In the sucrose metabolism pathway, GLGC plays a role in regulating the metabolic balance between sucrose and starch. The study identified three DEGs that regulate GLGC. The study of Sechium edule revealed that the genes regulating GLGC can promote starch and sucrose metabolism and promote sucrose synthesis [55].

The present study did not investigate the alterations in the hormone content of G. sinensis seeds at different stages of stratified germination. Furthermore, no attempt was made to correlate the changes in hormone content with transcriptomics. Subsequent research may wish to focus on changes in hormone content.

Conclusions

During the stratification and germination process of G. sinensis seeds, the soluble protein content gradually decreases, while the soluble sugar content shows an increasing trend; the sucrose content gradually decreases, while glucose and fructose show no significant changes during the dormancy release process. The seeds of G. sinensis are jointly regulated by nutrient and energy metabolism, osmotic regulation, and the antioxidant enzyme system during the stratification and germination process. Through the analysis of GA metabolism and sugar synthesis metabolic pathways, DEGs related to GA3, KAO, GA20ox, GA2ox, and other GA synthesis, as well as DEGs associated with AMY, PFK, SUS, and other carbohydrate metabolism, were screened. Correlation analysis identified 8 key DEGs regulating the GA synthesis pathway, including KAO and GA2ox, along with 23 DEGs associated with starch and sucrose metabolism, such as UGP2. The metabolism and regulation of GA and carbohydrate substances are key factors in promoting the release of dormancy and germination of G. sinensis. This study investigates the changes in physiological indicators, key differential genes, and metabolites during the stratification-induced dormancy release process of G. sinensis seeds, providing a theoretical foundation for the molecular regulatory mechanisms involved in seed dormancy release and germination.
