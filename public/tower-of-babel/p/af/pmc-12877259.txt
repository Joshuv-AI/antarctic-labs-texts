Joint effects of physical frailty and traditional cardiovascular risk factor control on cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes
Authors: Jie Li, Jiang Li, Yuefeng Yu, Ying Sun, Bowei Yu, Wenqi Shen, Lingli Cai, Ningjian Wang, Bin Wang, Yingli Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPhysical frailty has been found to increase the risk of multiple adverse outcomes including cardiovascular disease (CVD) in diabetic patients, but whether this could be modified by traditional risk factor control remains unknown. We aimed to explore the joint and interaction effects of frailty and traditional risk factor control on the risk of CVD.DesignA population-based cohort study.Setting and participantsWe included 15,753 participants with type 2 diabetes at baseline from UK Biobank.MeasurementsPhysical frailty was assessed by Fried criteria’s frailty phenotype. The degree of risk factor control was determined by the numbers of the following factors controlled within the target range, including glycated hemoglobin, blood pressure, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, smoking, and kidney condition. Incident CVD included coronary heart disease, stroke, or heart failure. Cox proportional hazard models were used to assess the individual and joint effects of frailty and risk factor control on the risk of CVD.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 13.5 years, 1129 incident CVD events were observed. Compared with non-frailty, both prefrailty and frailty were significantly associated with increased risk of CVD (HR 1.22, 95% CI [1.13, 1.31] for pre-frailty and 1.70 [1.53, 1.90] for frailty). For the joint effects, participants with frailty and a low degree of risk factor control (control of 0−1 risk factors) had the highest risk of CVD (2.92 [2.04, 4.17]) compared to those with non-frailty and optimal risk factor control (control of 4−5 risk factors). Moreover, a significant additive interaction between frailty and risk factor control was observed, with around 3.8% of CVD risk attributed to the interactive effects.ConclusionsBoth prefrailty and frailty were associated with a higher risk of CVD in participants with type 2 diabetes. Moreover, physical frailty could interact with the degree of risk factor control in an additive manner to increase the CVD risk.

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the fastest growing chronic non-communicable diseases, with its global prevalence expected to reach 12.2% (783.2 million) by 2045 [1]. Patients with diabetes are at a 2−3 times higher risk of incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) than those without diabetes [2,3], Meanwhile, CVD is the leading cause of death and disability among diabetes patients and poses a huge economic and healthcare burden [4]. Thus, it is critical to identify modifiable CVD risk factors among patients with diabetes to tailor effective prevention strategies.

Frailty is a public health problem that increases with age and frequently coexists with diabetes in middle-aged and older individuals [5]. It increases the risk of multiple adverse outcomes, including mortality, hospitalization, disability, cognitive impairment, as well as microvascular and macrovascular complications in diabetes patients [6,7]. However, longitudinal evidence is limited and inconclusive on the association between frailty and incident CVD. Two prospective cohort studies have shown that frailty defined by Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illness, and body weight Loss (FRAIL scale) and frailty index (FI) was associated with an increased risk of CVD in diabetes [8,9]. However, a retrospective study failed to determine the association between frailty defined by Johns Hopkins (weighted comorbidity score identified from electronic medical records) and macrovascular complication [10]. Frailty phenotype, proposed by Fried et al., is the most frequently used epidemiological measure and more clinically practical than other criteria, which consists of five items including weight loss, exhaustion, low grip strength, slow walking pace, and low physical activity [6,11,12]. However, to our knowledge, the evidence for the longitudinal association of frailty phenotype with CVD among patients with diabetes is limited.

Previous evidence supported a range of interventions that target traditional risk factors including smoking, hyperglycemia, high blood pressure (BP), high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and a poor kidney condition might reduce the risk of CVD complications in patients with type 2 diabetes [13,14]. A recent secondary post hoc analysis of the ADVANCE clinical trial found that frailty attenuated the benefit of intensive glucose-lowering and BP-lowering treatments among patients with type 2 diabetes [15]. Hence, the association between traditional risk factor control and the risk of CVD might be modified by the frailty status. However, the possible joint or interactive effects of frailty and traditional risk factor control on the risk of CVD remain largely unknown. Understanding these features could help to tailor interventions for CVD prevention in patients with diabetes.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the longitudinal association of physical frailty using the frailty phenotype with the risk of CVD in patients with type 2 diabetes and comprehensively outlined the joint and interaction effects of physical frailty and traditional risk factor control on the risk of CVD within a large UK Biobank cohort.

Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large population-based cohort study, which recruited more than 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years from the UK between 2006 and 2010 [16]. Participants were invited to complete touch-screen questionnaires and face-to-face interviews, have standard physical measurements, and provide biological samples at baseline. The UK Biobank study was approved by the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (REC reference 11/NW/0382) and all participants provided written informed consent.

In the present study, a total of 29,824 participants with diabetes at baseline were included using UK Biobank-defined first occurrences (category ID 2404) or glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level ≥ 48.0 mmol/mol (6.5%) [17]. The sources of first occurrence data were hospital inpatient records, death registers, primary care records, and self-reported medical conditions at the assessment centers. Participants diagnosed with type 1 diabetes or diagnosed with diabetes before 40 years old were excluded to capture primarily patients with type 2 diabetes (n = 4406) (Fig. S1). We further excluded participants with prevalent coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, or heart failure (HF) (n = 5372) and those who had no data for frailty or traditional risk factor control assessment (n = 4293). Finally, we included 15,753 individuals in the main analysis.

Physical frailty assessment

Physical frailty was evaluated by frailty phenotype, a widely used physical frailty measurement based on five individual components (weight loss, exhaustion, low grip strength, slow walking pace, and low physical activity), and the definitions of the specific items have been adapted for use in UK Biobank [18]. Among the five criteria, low grip strength was based on objective measurement, and the other four items were assessed using a self-reported questionnaire (see details in Table S1). Participants were classified into the frail (met three or more items), the prefrail (met one or two items), or the non-frail (met none of the items), as consistent with previous literature [18,19].

Ascertainment of outcomes

The primary outcomes in this study were incident CVD and its three major components: CHD, stroke, and HF. Diagnostic information of outcomes was obtained from primary care, hospital inpatient records, and death register records according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes: I20-I25 for CHD, I60-I64 for stroke, and I50 for HF.

Definition of risk factor control

Five traditional risk factors including HbA1c, BP, LDL cholesterol, smoking, and kidney condition were used to evaluate the risk of CVD in diabetes management, as suggested in previous studies and guidelines [14,[20], [21], [22]]. The following thresholds were used to specifically define the optimal status of the five risk factors control: HbA1c level < 53 mmol/l (7.0%), systolic BP and diastolic BP < 140/90 mmHg, LDL < 100 mg/dL, no current smoking, albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) < 10 mg/g and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m2 [14,23,24]. The ACR was calculated by dividing the albumin concentration by creatinine concentration in urine and eGFR was calculated by serum creatinine and age according to sex [25,26]. BP was measured twice for each individual and the mean of two readings was used. Smoking status was collected through touchscreen questionnaires at baseline. Other blood markers including HbA1c, LDL, albumin, and creatinine were measured at a central laboratory and detailed information has been described elsewhere [27]. The degree of traditional risk factor control was categorized as low (control of 0−1 risk factors), medium (control of 2−3 risk factors), and high (control of 4−5 risk factors) [22].

Covariates

Baseline information on age, sex, race, education, employment status, Townsend deprivation index, cancer history, family history of heart disease and stroke, moderate alcohol, healthy diet, and medication use for diabetes, cholesterol, and hypertension was collected through questionnaires or verbal interviews. The Townsend deprivation index was calculated based on areas where the participant’s postcode was located, and higher scores indicate higher levels of socioeconomic deprivation [28]. Moderate alcohol consumption was defined as alcohol intake > 0 g and ≤ 14 g/day for women, and alcohol intake > 0 g and ≤ 28 g/day for men [29]. A healthy diet was defined as Modified Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) style score ≥ 5, a common proxy for evaluating cardiovascular health [30]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters, which were measured by trained nurses. Diabetes duration was calculated as the years between the age at diagnosis of diabetes and age at baseline assessment for diagnosed patients and assigned as 0 years for undiagnosed patients. Missing covariates were replaced by the median value for continuous variables and a missing indicator category for categorical variables.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Cox proportion hazard models were performed to evaluate the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for the associations between physical frailty status and CVDs. The follow-up time was calculated from the baseline date to the date of outcome diagnosis, death, or the censoring date (30 March 2023), whichever occurred first. The models were adjusted for age, sex, race, education, employment status, Townsend index, cancer history, family history of heart disease or stroke (in the corresponding models), BMI, moderate alcohol, healthy diet, diabetes duration, the use of diabetes medication, cholesterol-lowering medication, antihypertensive medication and the control of traditional risk factors. The Schoenfeld residual method was adopted to test the proportional hazard assumption and no significant violation was found. In addition, we used the restricted cubic spline model to examine whether the risk of CVDs linearly correlated with the increasing number of frailty phenotype components. We also assessed the relative importance of frailty and other traditional risk factors in predicting CVD by calculating the R2 values of the Cox model [31].

To examine the joint association of physical frailty and the degree of risk factor control on CVD risk, we treated participants with non-frailty and high degrees of risk factor control as the reference group to conduct a joint analysis on the association between frailty and risk factor control with 3*3 groups. In addition, we performed both multiplicative and additive interactions between frailty and risk factor control, considering them as continuous variables. The multiplicative interaction was tested by including a multiplicative interaction term in the adjusted Cox models. The additive interaction was assessed by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction among participants with both exposures (AP), and the synergy index (SI) [32,33]. The confidence interval of the three additive interaction measures was calculated through bootstrap percentile method [32].

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of our results. First, we used the Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model to account for the competing risk of death. Second, missing values for covariates were imputed by multiple imputations with chained equations to account for the influence of missing covariates on results. Third, we excluded participants who were diagnosed with CVDs within the first 2 years of follow-up to minimize the potential reverse causality. Fourth, we used the FI, another commonly used frailty measure, to evaluate the consistency of the results. The FI included 49 self-reported items, covering cardiometabolic, immunological, cranial, musculoskeletal, well-being, infirmity, cancer, pain, respiratory and sensory traits [34]. We calculated FI score as the number of items present divided by 49, and classified participants into the non-frail (FI score ≤ 0.1), the prefrail (0.1 < FI score <0.21), and the frail (FI score ≥ 0.21) [35].

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2. (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

During a median follow-up of 13.5 years (188,715 person-years), 1129 incident CVD events were observed, including 858 CHD events, 233 stroke events, and 346 HF events. Participants with prefrailty and frailty had a significantly higher risk of developing CVD (Table 2). Specifically, compared with non-frail participants, the HRs for total CVD were 1.22 (95% CI: 1.13, 1.31) and 1.70 (1.53, 1.90) for those with prefrailty and frailty, respectively (P for trend <0.001). Similarly, when comparing prefrail participants with those with non-frail, the HRs were 1.18 (1.09, 1.29) for CHD, 1.20 (1.02, 1.42) for stroke, and 1.28 (1.12, 1.46) for HF. For frail participants, the HRs were 1.61 (1.41, 1.82) for CHD, 1.63 (1.28, 2.08) for stroke, and 1.79 (1.49, 2.14) for HF. The association between frailty status and CVD risk did not appear to be significantly modified by covariates (Table S2). Discussion

In this large cohort study, we found that both prefrailty and frailty were significantly associated with an increased risk of incident CVD and its three major subtypes (CHD, stroke, and HF). More importantly, the highest risk of CVD was observed in participants with frailty and a low risk factor control and frailty interacts with the degree of risk factor control in an additive manner in the development of CVD among type 2 diabetes patients.

To our knowledge, this is the first large population-based cohort study investigating the longitudinal association between frailty phenotype and the incidence of CVD in patients with type 2 diabetes. Two prospective cohort studies have found that frailty evaluated by FRAIL scale and FI was associated with an increased risk of CVD [8,9]. One study from a diabetes cohort (n = 560,795) in Taiwan with a mean 3.1 years of follow-up [8], where frailty status was defined by FRAIL scale, found that patients with frailty had an increased risk (HR 1.13 [95% CI: 1.01, 1.25]) of CVD (including myocardial infarction, unstable angina, HF, new-inset stroke, and submission to revascularization procedures). Another study including two prospective cohorts from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) (n = 3746) and English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) (n = 1518) indicated that frailty defined by FI increased risks of CVD (CHARLS, HR 2.72 [95% CI: 1.97, 3.77]; ELSA, 2.41 [1.43, 4.06]), where CVD included angina, heart attack, HF, other heart problems and stroke [9]. However, a retrospective study of new oral antidiabetic medication users (n = 54,505) from US with a mean 2.4 years of follow-up, found that frailty defined by Johns Hopkins adjusted clinical groups was not associated (HR 0.99 [95% CI: 0.86, 1.13]) with macrovascular complication [10].

The discrepancies in these findings might be attributed to the difference in study design, frailty measures criteria, duration of follow-up, and confounders adjustments. In the present study, we adopted the frailty phenotype developed by Fried et al. to assess physical frailty [11], which is relatively easy to apply in clinical practice and has become one of the most frequently used frailty measurements in patients with diabetes [6,36]. We found that both prefrailty and frailty were significantly associated with an increased risk of CVD, after adjusting covariates including demographic variables, cancer history, family history, lifestyles, the use of medication, diabetes duration, and traditional risk factor control. These associations remained robust when further concentrating on CVD subtypes including CHD, stroke, and HF, which had not been reported in previous studies. Moreover, we compared the relative importance of frailty with traditional risk factors in predicting CVD and found that the relative strength of frailty was only lower than that of SBP and eGFR.

Several possible biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between frailty and CVD in individuals with diabetes. One is that frailty is closely associated with sarcopenia among patients with diabetes and the latter could lead to insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [37], which might promote the development of CVD. Additionally, it’s reported that frailty increases the risk of hypoglycemia [38], which is an important risk factor for CVD [7,39].

More importantly, we evaluated the joint effects of frailty and traditional risk factor control on the risk of CVD in patients with diabetes and observed that the CVD risk was highest in patients with a combination of frailty and a low degree of risk factor control. Furthermore, the additive interaction between frailty and risk factor control was identified in our study. Approximately 3.8% of CVD risk can be attributed to the additive effects of frailty and low degree of risk factor control, meaning that the combined effects of frailty and low degree of risk factor control were much greater than simply adding the two effects together. Given the prevalence of frailty in diabetes is up to 9%–21% (10.8% in our study) [6,40], such a percentage increase is considerable regarding the number of diabetes patients with CVD complications and therefore might have far-reaching public health implications. The public health consequence of a low degree of risk factor control would be greater in diabetes patients with frailty.

Our study highlighted the importance of simultaneously addressing frailty and traditional cardiovascular risk factors to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease in diabetic patients. The significant additive interaction indicated that integrated care strategies focusing on both aspects might yield better outcomes than targeting either factor alone. This approach aligned with the growing recognition of the need for multidisciplinary care models involving geriatricians, cardiologists, endocrinologists, and primary care providers in middle-aged and older patients with diabetes [5]. Future research should explore specific interventions for effectively managing frailty and cardiovascular risk factors to improve the long-term prognosis of this vulnerable population. Conducting randomized controlled trials to test these integrated management strategies will provide valuable evidence for optimizing care and reducing the cardiovascular disease burden among patients with diabetes.

Given frailty is common in diabetes and is a reversible syndrome, frailty assessment, identification, and management should be part of routine diabetes care. It is imperative to implement early and tailored lifestyle, nutritional, and pharmacological interventions to prevent or delay the development of CVD complications in diabetes patients presenting with prefrailty or frailty, particularly in those patients combined with a low degree of risk factor control.

The major strengths of the current study included population-based study design, long-term follow-up, and rich information on covariates in diabetes patients. However, several limitations could not be ignored. First, except for grip strength, four components of frailty phenotype were self-reported and reporting bias might be introduced. However, previous evidence indicated that characteristics of frailty are similar regardless of whether self-reported or test-based measures are used [41]. Second, the diagnosis of CVD was comprehensively identified using primary care, hospital inpatient records, and death register records, though the misclassification bias from the undiagnosed CVD might still exist and lead to an underestimation of the magnitudes of the true association. Third, as the relatively limited number of events for each CVD subtype, we could not further evaluate the joint association of frailty and the degree of risk factor control and their interaction in each CVD subtype. Fourth, our CVD definition included both major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and minor adverse cardiovascular events (e.g., angina). Given the potential heterogeneity [42], future research could differentiate between these two types to better understand the distinct impacts of frailty and risk factor control on MACEs and minor events in diabetes. Fifth, as with any observational study, the residual confounding could not be completely ruled out (including sedentary time), although we adjusted a wealth of potential confounders. Finally, our findings might not be directly generalized to other ethnicities, as the participants in the current study were primarily White Europeans.

Conclusions

In this large cohort study of middle-aged participants with type 2 diabetes, both prefrailty and frailty were significantly associated with increased risks of overall CVD and subtypes, including CHD, stroke, and HF. Notably, the highest risk of CVD was observed in those with physical frailty and low risk factor control, and an additive interaction between physical frailty and the degree of risk factor control. These results highlight the importance of integrating routine clinical assessment and surveillance of physical frailty into routine care for diabetes patients to reduce the risk of CVDs, especially for those with a low degree of risk factor control.
