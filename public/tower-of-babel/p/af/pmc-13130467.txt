Identification and validation of Kif15 as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in colon cancer
Authors: Huiming Wu, Dingwen Xue, Xiaoyu Li, Min Deng, Renkai Guo, Chenfei Jin, Yipeng Cui, Fei Luo, Liang Xue, Huiyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to systematically identify and validate KIF15 as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in colon cancer (CC) using integrated bioinformatics analyses. We further explored its role in the tumor immune microenvironment and its potential value in immunotherapy, and validated its expression and clinical significance through immunohistochemical analysis.MethodsGene expression profiles of CC were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed, and key genes were identified using the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape. A nomogram model was established based on multivariate Cox regression, and its predictive accuracy and stability were evaluated using calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). To investigate the role of KIF15 in the tumor microenvironment, single-cell RNA sequencing data from the TISCH database were analyzed to determine its expression distribution across immune cell subsets. Background

CC is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide and one of the most prevalent gastrointestinal cancers, with more than 1.2 million new cases reported annually [1, 2]. It is also the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality, accounting for approximately 694,000 deaths each year [3]. As a highly heterogeneous tumor, the initiation and progression of CC are closely associated with aging and are characterized by a series of genetic and epigenetic alterations, including copy number variations, gene mutations, chromosomal rearrangements, and aberrant DNA methylation [4, 5]. Although patients with early-stage CC generally have a favorable prognosis, nearly 20% present with local or distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis [6]. In recent years, the widespread implementation of effective screening strategies, particularly colonoscopy, has contributed to a decline in both incidence and mortality rates in the United States, accompanied by a significant improvement in 5-year survival [7]. These findings underscore the importance of early detection and risk stratification, highlighting the urgent need to identify reliable biomarkers for early screening and prognostic evaluation in CC. Meanwhile, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for CC. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting PD-1 and PD-L1, have demonstrated substantial clinical benefit in patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) CC. However, their efficacy remains limited in microsatellite-stable (MSS) or mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR) tumors [6, 7]. Therefore, the identification of novel immunotherapeutic targets and predictive biomarkers is urgently needed to improve treatment outcomes in these patients. With rapid advances in bioinformatics and molecular biology, high-throughput data analysis has become a powerful approach for discovering potential biomarkers. The kinesin superfamily proteins (KIFs) are microtubule-associated motor proteins widely distributed in eukaryotic cells. To date, 45 KIF members have been identified in humans and are classified into 14 subfamilies based on structural characteristics [8]. Accumulating evidence indicates that KIFs play essential roles in cell cycle regulation, mitosis, and chromosome segregation, and their dysregulation may contribute to malignant transformation [9]. KIF15 (also known as kinesin-12, KNSL7, or HKLP2) is a member of the KIF family and functions as a plus-end-directed mitotic motor protein. It was initially recognized for its role in spindle assembly and mitotic regulation [10, 11]. KIF15 maintains bipolar spindle formation by regulating microtubule sliding and partially overlaps functionally with Eg5 (kinesin-5). Overexpression of KIF15 has been reported in multiple solid tumors and is implicated in tumor progression, immune-related pathways, and therapeutic resistance [12]. Previous studies have revealed a close association between KIF15 and the tumor immune microenvironment in malignancies such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma; however, its role in CC remains largely unexplored [13]. Ma et al. reported that KIF15 promotes radioresistance in tumor cells by activating the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, suggesting its potential prognostic significance [14]. Given its involvement in tumor progression and immune regulation in other gastrointestinal malignancies, a systematic investigation of KIF15 expression patterns, clinical relevance, and immunological implications in CC is warranted. In this study, we comprehensively analyzed the expression profile, prognostic value, and immune-related associations of KIF15 in CC based on TCGA data and integrative bioinformatics approaches. Our findings aim to provide novel insights into early diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and precision therapy in CC.

Data collection

GEO dataset acquisition

Relevant CC datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database up to August 1, 2024. The following keywords were used: “Colorectal cancer,” “Colon cancer,” “Rectal cancer,” “CC,” “Colon carcinoma,” and “Colorectal tumor.” Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) were applied to optimize the search strategy. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) human CC tissues and corresponding adjacent or normal control tissues; (2) sample size ≥ 50; and (3) availability of complete gene expression data required for analysis. The exclusion criteria were: (1) non-human samples; (2) sample size < 50; (3) incomplete or unavailable data; and (4) duplicate datasets.

TCGA dataset acquisition

Gene expression and corresponding clinical data for CC were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The filtering criteria were as follows: Project = TCGA-COAD; Disease Type = adenomas and adenocarcinomas; Data Type = Gene Expression Quantification.

The expression profiles and clinical information were integrated into a standardized expression matrix for subsequent analyses. All data preprocessing and analyses were performed in the RStudio environment using R software (version 4.0.3).

Clinical sample collection

Clinicopathological data were retrospectively collected from patients diagnosed with CC who underwent treatment at the Department of Colorectal and Anal Surgery, Shanxi Bethune Hospital, between January 2016 and November 2019. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients who underwent radical resection with R0 margins; (2) no preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy; (3) pathological confirmation of CC; (4) signed informed consent from patients or their legal guardians. The exclusion criteria were: (1) non-CC pathological diagnosis; (2) presence of other malignancies before surgery; (3) participation in other clinical studies; (4) inability or unwillingness to participate. Baseline clinical characteristics were collected, including age, sex, pathological TNM stage, tumor location, tumor size, and lymph node metastasis status. The proportions of PD-1– and PD-L1–positive cells in pathological tissues were also recorded for immune correlation analysis. Survival information was obtained through telephone follow-up, with a final follow-up date of November 2024. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were obtained from the Department of Pathology at Shanxi Bethune Hospital, and tissue microarrays containing tumor and adjacent normal tissues were constructed for subsequent analyses. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Bethune Hospital.

Research methods

Identification of key modules

GEO datasets and corresponding platform annotation files were processed using the GEOquery package (v2.65.2) in R. Raw expression data were log2-transformed when necessary, and unnormalized datasets were normalized using the normalize.quantiles function from the preprocessCore package. Probe IDs were converted to gene symbols according to platform annotations. Probes mapping to multiple genes were excluded, and the average expression value was calculated for genes matched by multiple probes. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package (v3.40.2). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those meeting the criteria of adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥ 2. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to control for multiple testing. Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 package to visualize upregulated and downregulated genes, and heatmaps were constructed using the pheatmap package to illustrate expression patterns across samples. Identified DEGs were uploaded to the STRING database to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network with a confidence score threshold of 0.7. The network was visualized and further analyzed in Cytoscape (v3.8.2). Key modules were identified using the MCODE plugin (v2.0.0) with the following parameters: Degree Cutoff = 2, Node Score Cutoff = 0.05, K-Core = 5, and Max Depth = 100.

Screening and expression analysis of key genes

Based on clinical data from the TCGA-COAD cohort, Cox regression analysis was performed to identify prognostic genes within the key modules that were significantly associated with overall survival (OS). Standardized pan-cancer expression data (TCGA-TARGET-GTEx, PANCAN; n = 19,131) were downloaded from UCSC Xena. Expression data of candidate prognostic genes were extracted from Solid Tissue Normal, Normal Tissue, Primary Tumor, and blood-derived tumor samples. Expression values were transformed as log2(x + 0.001). Tumor types with fewer than three samples were excluded, resulting in 34 cancer types included for analysis.

The expression distribution of candidate genes was systematically evaluated across cancers. Immune regulatory gene expression data were extracted, and Pearson correlation analysis was performed to identify core genes significantly associated with immune regulation.

After identifying the key gene, its expression pattern and clinical relevance in CC were analyzed using the TCGA-COAD cohort. Differences between tumor and normal tissues were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by ROC curve analysis and calculation of the area under the curve (AUC). Associations between gene expression and clinicopathological parameters (age, gender, stage, T, N, and M classification) were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Prognostic analysis of the key gene

Patients in the TCGA-COAD cohort were divided into high- and low-expression groups according to the median expression level of the key gene. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were used to compare differences in OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free survival (DFS), and progression-free survival (PFS).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, including age, gender, stage, T, N, and M classification, were performed to determine whether the key gene served as an independent prognostic factor. A nomogram was constructed based on multivariate Cox regression results, and a risk score was calculated. Predictive accuracy was evaluated using calibration curves and time-dependent ROC curves, with AUC values calculated to assess discriminative ability. DCA was performed to evaluate clinical net benefit.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Six CC single-cell RNA sequencing datasets (GSE108989, GSE139555, GSE146771, EMTAB8107, GSE166555, and GSE179784) were obtained from the TISCH database, along with corresponding h5 raw data files and cell annotation information.

Data preprocessing and integration were performed in R using the MAESTRO and Seurat packages. Quality control was first conducted to remove low-quality cells and lowly expressed genes. The expression matrix was normalized, and highly variable genes were identified. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied for dimensionality reduction, followed by t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualization. Cell clustering was performed based on a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph.

Cell subtypes were annotated according to TISCH-provided annotations. The expression levels of the key gene across different cell types and immune cell subsets were compared to evaluate its cell-specific expression pattern within the tumor microenvironment of CC.

Functional enrichment and mutation analysis

TCGA-COAD samples were ranked according to KIF15 expression levels. The top 25% and bottom 25% of samples were defined as high- and low-expression groups, respectively. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R, with adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 2 as thresholds.

Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, were conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v4.5.2). Statistical significance was defined as pvalueCutoff < 0.05 and qvalueCutoff < 0.05. Results were visualized using clusterProfiler.

Immune correlation analysis

To systematically evaluate immune cell infiltration patterns under different KIF15 expression levels, immune scoring analysis was performed using the immunedeconv package in R based on TCGA-COAD data.

To further investigate the relationship between KIF15 expression and immune checkpoint molecules, differential expression and correlation analyses were conducted for key immune regulatory genes, including ITPRIPL1, SIGLEC15, TIGIT, CD274 (PD-L1), HAVCR2, PDCD1 (PD-1), CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1LG2.

Identification and analysis of downstream hub genes

DEGs identified between high- and low-KIF15 expression groups were imported into Cytoscape. The cytoHubba plugin was applied using six commonly used algorithms (MCC, MNC, Degree, Closeness, Radiality, and EcCentricity) to identify the top 15 candidate downstream hub genes. Overlapping hub genes were determined using an online Venn analysis tool.

GeneMANIA was used to explore genes with similar expression profiles and to analyze associated biological pathways. The miRWalk database was used to predict miRNAs potentially regulating the identified hub genes. Finally, expression levels of four hub genes in CC were further validated using TCGA data.

IHC analysis

Tissue microarray Sect. (5 μm) were baked at 55 °C for 30 min, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10–15 min. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer under high-pressure heating.

Sections were incubated with a primary antibody against KIF15 (1:50 dilution) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. Staining was visualized using DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

IHC results were evaluated using the H-score method (range 0–300), calculated as staining intensity (0–3) multiplied by the percentage of positive cells. The median H-score was used as the cut-off value to classify patients into low- and high-expression groups.

Associations between KIF15 expression and clinicopathological characteristics were analyzed. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess prognostic significance. LASSO regression and multivariate Cox regression were conducted using the survival, survminer, glmnet, timeROC, and rms packages in R to identify independent prognostic factors. ROC and time-dependent ROC curves were generated, and AUC values were calculated to evaluate model performance. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between KIF15 and PD-1/PD-L1 expression. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of key modules

After systematic retrieval and screening, three publicly available datasets (GSE24550, GSE21815, and GSE44076) were ultimately included in the analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) before batch effect correction demonstrated clear separation among samples from different datasets (Fig. 1A), indicating significant batch effects. After batch correction, samples from different datasets were well mixed in PCA space (Fig. 1B), suggesting satisfactory integration and comparability for subsequent joint analysis. Based on the integrated expression matrix, differential expression analysis between CC tissues and normal tissues identified a total of 611 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 206 upregulated and 405 downregulated genes. The overall distribution of DEGs is presented in the volcano plot (Fig. 1C). A heatmap generated using the pheatmap package further illustrated distinct expression patterns between tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 1D). The identified DEGs were subsequently imported into the STRING database to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. After removing disconnected nodes, a preliminary PPI network was obtained (Fig. 1E). Cytoscape was then used for network visualization and module analysis. A core module comprising 13 key genes, including KIF15, KIF20A, RRM2, and TOP2A, was identified using the MCODE algorithm (Fig. 1F).

Identification of key modules

Screening and expression analysis of the key gene

Based on the TCGA-COAD dataset, RNA-seq (level 3) expression data and corresponding clinical information from 346 CC tumor samples and 30 normal colon tissues were included in the analysis. Univariate Cox regression was performed to identify prognostic genes within the candidate module. The results indicated that KIF15, ANLN, TRIP13, UBE2C, TOP2A, BUB1, TPX2, CEP55, and ECT2 were significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. 2A). Subsequent pan-cancer analysis based on the TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset demonstrated that KIF15 was broadly upregulated across multiple tumor types (Fig. 2B). Moreover, KIF15 expression showed significant correlations with immune regulatory genes in various cancers (Fig. 2C). Given its consistent overexpression and immune relevance, KIF15 was selected as the core gene for further investigation. A systematic evaluation of KIF15 expression revealed that its mRNA level was significantly higher in CC tissues compared with normal colon tissues (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2D). ROC curve analysis showed that KIF15 achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.874 (Fig. 2E), indicating strong discriminatory performance between tumor and normal tissues. Further analysis of clinicopathological characteristics in the TCGA-COAD cohort demonstrated that KIF15 expression was not significantly associated with age, sex, T stage, N stage, M stage, or overall TNM stage (Fig. 2F–K). Prognostic analysis of the key gene

Based on the TCGA-COAD cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic value of KIF15 (Fig. 3A–D). The results demonstrated that patients with high KIF15 expression had significantly shorter overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with those in the low-expression group (all P < 0.05). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in disease-free survival (DFS) between the two groups (P = 0.916). It should be noted that DFS data in the TCGA-COAD cohort were only available for a subset of patients; therefore, the smaller sample size may reduce the statistical stability of the DFS analysis. Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted using TCGA clinical data to assess the associations of KIF15 expression and clinicopathological features with patient prognosis. The analysis revealed that KIF15 expression, age, T stage, and TNM stage were all significantly associated with OS (all P < 0.05). These variables were further incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression analysis, which confirmed that KIF15 expression, age, T stage, and TNM stage were independent predictors of OS (Fig. 3E; all P < 0.05). Based on the multivariate Cox model, a nomogram was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 3F). The model exhibited a concordance index (C-index) of 0.784, indicating good predictive performance. Calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted and observed survival outcomes (Fig. 3G). Time-dependent ROC analysis further demonstrated that the model had satisfactory discriminatory ability, with AUCs of 0.788, 0.777, and 0.712 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3H). Finally, DCA indicated that the nomogram provided substantial net clinical benefit for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction (Fig. 3I–K).

Prognostic analysis of the key gene

Single-cell analysis

Using six CC scRNA-seq datasets obtained from the TISCH database, along with their corresponding h5 raw data and cell annotation information, we systematically analyzed the expression distribution of KIF15 within the tumor microenvironment (TME). The results showed that in five datasets (GSE179784, GSE166555, GSE146771, GSE139555, and GSE108989), KIF15 was predominantly highly expressed in Tprolif cell populations (Fig. 4A–C, E–F). In contrast, in the EMTAB8107 dataset, KIF15 exhibited relatively high expression in B cells, epithelial cells, malignant tumor cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and myofibroblasts (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that KIF15 may play an important role in the interactions between the immune microenvironment and tumor cells in CC, potentially influencing tumor initiation and progression.

Single-cell analysis

Functional enrichment and mutation analysis of the key gene

By comparing DEGs between high and low KIF15 expression groups, a total of 692 significantly differentially expressed genes were identified (Fig. 5A). KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed that these DEGs were primarily associated with cell cycle–related functional modules, including mitosis, chromosome segregation, and microtubule organization (Fig. 5B). Further GSEA demonstrated that KIF15 high-expression samples were significantly enriched in multiple pathways closely related to cell cycle regulation and chromosomal dynamics, including chromosome condensation and decondensation, activation of mitotic checkpoints, and sister chromatid segregation, suggesting a critical role of KIF15 in mitotic regulation. In addition, enrichment of the “RHO GTPases Activate Formins” pathway indicates that KIF15 may influence cytoskeletal remodeling, regulating cell migration and spindle dynamics during mitosis. Regarding DNA damage repair, significant enrichment in “DNA Repair Pathways Full Network” and “Retinoblastoma Gene in Cancer” pathways further suggests that high KIF15 expression may impact genomic stability, cell cycle progression, and tumor development through RB pathway regulation (Figs. 5C–D). Moreover, mutation profile analysis revealed that key genes, including TP53, CSMD1, MUC4, LRRK2, and IGF2R, exhibited significantly different mutation frequencies between high and low KIF15 expression groups (Fig. 5E

Immune-related analysis

We further analyzed the immune cell infiltration characteristics of KIF15 in CC tissues using TIMER-based scoring. TCGA cohort samples were divided into high- and low-expression groups according to KIF15 expression. The results showed that higher KIF15 expression was associated with significantly increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, neutrophils, B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and macrophages, suggesting that KIF15 may play a key regulatory role in the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating immune cell infiltration (Figs. 6A–B). To further explore the potential relationship between KIF15 expression and immune checkpoint molecules, we compared the expression of several key immune regulatory genes between the high- and low-expression groups, including ITPRIPL1, SIGLEC15, TIGIT, CD274 (PD-L1), HAVCR2, PDCD1 (PD-1), IGSF8, CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1LG2. The analysis revealed that CD274, HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, and ITPRIPL1 were significantly upregulated in the KIF15 high-expression group, whereas SIGLEC15 and IGSF8 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6C). Correlation analysis further indicated statistically significant positive correlations between KIF15 and multiple immune checkpoint genes, with the strongest correlation observed for ITPRIPL1 (correlation coefficient = 0.51), suggesting a potential synergistic role in immune regulatory pathways (Fig. 6D).

Immune-related analysis

Identification and analysis of downstream hub genes

We applied the cytoHubba plugin to score genes within the PPI network using six common algorithms and selected the top 15 genes. By integrating the results via an online Venn analysis tool, we identified four overlapping hub genes (TTK, CDK1, CHEK1, and KIF2C) (Fig. 7A). To further explore their functional roles, GeneMANIA was used to construct a co-expression network and annotate the main biological processes associated with these hub genes (Fig. 7B). The PPI connections were primarily composed of physical interactions (77.64%), co-expression (8.01%), predicted interactions (5.37%), co-localization (3.63%), genetic interactions (2.87%), and pathway associations (1.88%). The analysis revealed that TTK, CDK1, CHEK1, and KIF2C interact significantly with multiple cell cycle-related genes, including WEE1, CKS2, CCNA2, CDC25C, and CCNB1, with most interactions mediated via physical contact, suggesting that these hub genes may play crucial roles in regulating cell division and tumorigenesis. Using the TCGA database, we further analyzed the expression levels of the four hub genes in CC tissues compared to normal colon tissues (Fig. 7C). All four genes were significantly upregulated in CC tissues, indicating their potential as cancer driver genes. To investigate upstream non-coding regulatory mechanisms, we predicted miRNAs targeting these hub genes using the miRWalk database, identifying five key miRNAs (Fig. 7D), providing a theoretical basis for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Immunohistochemistry analysis results

A total of 94 patients were included in this study based on the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were obtained from the Department of Pathology, Shanxi Bethune Hospital, and tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing both tumor and adjacent normal tissues were constructed. IHC staining was then performed. KIF15 expression was evaluated using the H-score method, and the median H-score was used as the cut-off value: samples below the median were classified as the low-expression group, and those equal to or above the median as the high-expression group (clinical details in Table 2). Representative staining of KIF15 in tumor and adjacent normal tissues is shown in (Fig. 8A and B). IHC results demonstrated that KIF15 is highly expressed in CC tissues (Fig. 8C). Diagnostic ROC curve analysis indicated that KIF15 has good potential for CC detection, with an AUC of 0.886 (Fig. 8D). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that high KIF15 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8E). Time-dependent ROC analysis further showed that KIF15 predicts 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival with AUCs of 0.726, 0.773, and 0.748, respectively (Fig. 8F), confirming its potential value in CC prognostic assessment. Pearson correlation analysis indicated that KIF15 expression is positively correlated with immune checkpoint molecules PD-1 and PD-L1 (P < 0.001), with correlation coefficients of 0.299 and 0.364, respectively (Fig. 8G and H ). To identify key factors affecting patient prognosis, LASSO regression was performed using lambda.1se as the cutoff (Fig. 8I and J). Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that KIF15 is an independent prognostic factor for CC patients (P = 0.001) (Fig. 8K).

Discussion

Currently, the incidence of CC is increasing, with persistently high mortality. CC is characterized by difficulties in early diagnosis, challenges in treating advanced disease, and a high propensity for metastasis; approximately 20% of patients have metastases at diagnosis, and nearly half of early-stage CC patients eventually develop metastatic disease during their lifetime [15]. Resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs is a major factor affecting long-term prognosis. Despite substantial efforts to improve survival rates, the clinical benefits have been limited. With the advent of next-generation sequencing and advances in molecular oncology, it has become increasingly recognized that numerous genetic and molecular markers can serve as auxiliary diagnostic tools, prognostic indicators, and therapeutic targets, offering important implications for patient management [16].

Kinesin superfamily proteins (KIFs) are microtubule (MT)-dependent motor proteins with ATPase activity that are involved in intracellular transport and mitotic processes [17]. In eukaryotic cells, KIFs play critical roles in spindle formation, chromosome alignment, and cytokinesis, and their dysregulation has been closely associated with tumor initiation, progression, and poor prognosis. For example, KIF11 drives glioblastoma invasion, proliferation, and self-renewal [18]; KIF14 promotes gastric cancer progression and metastasis, correlating with poor patient prognosis [19]; and KIF4A serves as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in lung cancer [20]. KIF15, a member of the KIF family, participates in mitosis and neuronal development, and is overexpressed in multiple malignancies, often correlating with adverse outcomes [21–22]. For instance, Ding et al. [23] demonstrated via IHC that KIF15 is highly expressed in gastric cancer, promoting tumor cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. Xenograft experiments further showed that downregulation of KIF15 suppressed tumor growth and induced apoptosis. While KIF15’s oncogenic roles in other cancers are well documented, studies in CC remain limited. Ma et al. [14] reported that KIF15 promotes radiotherapy resistance in tumor cells via activation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, suggesting its potential prognostic value. Given its association with tumor progression and immune regulation in other digestive system malignancies, a systematic evaluation of KIF15 expression, clinical significance, and potential role in immunotherapy in CC is warranted.

In this study, analysis of DEGs from the GEO dataset GSE44076 identified 1,504 DEGs, including 675 upregulated and 829 downregulated genes. Subsequent PPI network analysis using Cytoscape highlighted KIF15 as a key gene for further investigation. We found that KIF15 is highly expressed in CC tissues (P < 0.05). Stratified analysis revealed that KIF15 expression was not significantly associated with patient age, sex, or tumor T, N, or M stage (P > 0.05). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with low KIF15 expression had significantly better overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with high-expression patients (P < 0.05), suggesting that KIF15 is closely linked to poor prognosis in CC. In the TCGA-COAD cohort, OS was statistically significant, whereas disease-free survival (DFS) was not, likely due to the limited availability of DFS data in only a subset of patients, reducing statistical power. In contrast, OS data were more complete and reliable, providing robust results. Similar discrepancies between OS and DFS due to incomplete DFS data have been reported in prior TCGA-based studies [24]. Multivariate Cox regression confirmed KIF15 as an independent prognostic factor. Diagnostic ROC curve analysis showed an AUC of 0.874, indicating good predictive value for CC diagnosis. The KIF15-based survival prediction model demonstrated good predictive performance, with calibration curves and DCA confirming consistency and clinical net benefit.

Furthermore, IHC validation using patient tissue samples confirmed high KIF15 expression in CC (P < 0.0001). Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated that high KIF15 expression correlated with poor prognosis, and LASSO and multivariate Cox regression confirmed KIF15 as an independent prognostic factor (P = 0.001). These findings underscore KIF15’s potential utility in early diagnosis and prognostic assessment of CC.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that high KIF15 expression was associated with pathways related to cell cycle regulation and chromosome dynamics, including chromosome condensation and decondensation, mitotic checkpoint activation, and sister chromatid segregation, highlighting its role in mitosis. These findings align with previous studies. Under normal physiological conditions, KIF15 localizes to the cytoplasm during interphase and does not interact with microtubules (MT). Upon nuclear envelope breakdown, KIF15 localizes to kinetochore fibers (K-fibers) and antagonizes Eg5-mediated centrosome separation, ensuring spindle bipolarity [25]. As a plus-end-directed motor, KIF15 moves along MTs and localizes to chromosomes via Ki67 [26]. Silencing Ki67 revealed that KIF15 does not affect chromosome alignment but enhances spindle pole separation, forming longer bipolar spindles, indicating its role in regulating spindle mechanics rather than generating pole-separating forces [27]. The enrichment of the “RHO GTPases Activate Formins” pathway suggests that KIF15 may regulate cytoskeletal remodeling, cell migration, and mitotic spindle dynamics. DNA repair-related enrichment, including “DNA Repair Pathways Full Network” and “Retinoblastoma Gene in Cancer,” indicates that KIF15 may influence genomic stability, cell cycle progression, and tumor development via RB pathway regulation.

Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1 and PD-L1, has gained prominence [28–30]. Although ICIs are effective in microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) and mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) CC, their efficacy is limited in microsatellite-stable (MSS) and proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) CC [31–33], underscoring the need for novel immunotherapeutic targets. Mi et al. [13] reported that KIF15 expression correlates with B cells, CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, and is positively associated with tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) in multiple cancers. In this study, single-cell analysis revealed high KIF15 expression in Tprolif cells and B cells, suggesting its role in tumor-immune interactions within the CC tumor microenvironment (TME). TIMER analysis showed that higher KIF15 expression correlated with increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, neutrophils, B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and macrophages. Evaluation of immune checkpoint gene expression revealed that high KIF15 expression samples had significantly elevated CD274, HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, and ITPRIPL1, whereas SIGLEC15 and IGSF8 were downregulated. Correlation analysis indicated significant positive associations between KIF15 and several immune checkpoints, most notably ITPRIPL1 (r = 0.51), suggesting potential synergistic roles in immune regulation. IHC validation also demonstrated positive correlations of KIF15 with PD-1 and PD-L1 (P < 0.001), supporting its potential as a CC immunotherapy target.

Overall, our study demonstrates that KIF15 is overexpressed in CC and significantly associated with poor prognosis. KIF15 expression influences immune cell infiltration and is closely correlated with key immune checkpoint molecules, highlighting its potential role in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Therefore, KIF15 may serve as a promising biomarker for CC diagnosis, prognosis, and personalized therapy, as well as a potential target for immunotherapy. Limitations include the relatively small IHC validation cohort and the lack of in vivo and in vitro functional experiments, which warrant further investigation to elucidate the mechanistic role of KIF15 in CC progression and its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

In summary, KIF15 plays a significant role in CC and shows promising potential as a biomarker. Further basic research and clinical validation could establish KIF15 as an important tool for early diagnosis, prognostic evaluation, and even precision therapy in CC.
