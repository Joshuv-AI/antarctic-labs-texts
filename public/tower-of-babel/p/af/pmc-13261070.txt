Visualizing cellular G-quadruplexes with a bioinspired chemiluminescent probe
Authors: Pengfei Wang, Xiaoyu Li, Zhichun Wang, Ziyue Guan, Jean-Louis Mergny, Jing Yang, Mingpan Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluorescence imaging of G-quadruplexes (G4s) is fundamentally limited by autofluorescence and phototoxicity from external excitation. Chemiluminescence, which generates light internally via chemical reaction, offers a promising solution. Here, we report the first chemiluminescent probe, PCL, for selective G4 visualization in vitro and in cells. PCL conjugates the high-affinity G4 ligand PhenDC3 with a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-activatable imidazopyrazinium chemilumophore. Crucially, we demonstrate that PCL’s excitation-free mechanism enables high-contrast, low-background imaging of G4s in living cells. This work establishes a novel chemiluminescent paradigm for visualizing G4s with minimal perturbation.

Introduction

Optical imaging has become a cornerstone of in vivo biomedical studies, creating unprecedented opportunities to interrogate biomolecules within their native cellular environments. Among its various modalities, fluorescence imaging is one of the most widely used technologies, due to its high spatial and temporal resolution, and multiplexing capability [1–3] However, a significant limitation of fluorescent probes is their dependence on external excitation light, which can induce high background noise from autofluorescence, phototoxicity, and photobleaching. In contrast, chemiluminescence imaging presents a powerful alternative that circumvents these issues [4–6]. As chemiluminescent probes generate light internally through a chemical reaction, they eliminate the need for external illumination altogether. This intrinsic property results in an exceptionally low background for imaging biomolecules, and avoids photobleaching and light-induced cell damage [7–9].

G-quadruplexes (G4s) are a noncanonical nucleic acid secondary structure formed by guanine-rich DNA or RNA sequences [10, 11]. They play critical biological roles in cells, including telomere maintenance, regulation of DNA replication, transcription, translation, and phase separation, but may cause genomic instability [12–19]. Growing evidence suggests that G4 structures are implicated in cancer development, with particular significance for those formed at telomeric ends and promoter regions of oncogenes [20–27]. Consequently, the ability to visualize G4s within living cells has become crucial. Over the past decade, a wealth of fluorescent small-molecular probes [28–30,32,33] and antibodies [34–36] have been developed for this purpose [37–39]. However, their reliance on external excitation light introduces a fundamental limitation: the high intrinsic fluorescence quantum yield of G4s themselves generates significant autofluorescence background under UV or blue-light excitation, severely compromising signal fidelity and contrast [40–42]. This intrinsic interference highlights a pivotal, yet overlooked, opportunity for chemiluminescence. As a modality that generates light internally through a chemical reaction, chemiluminescence inherently eliminates the need for excitation light, thereby circumventing the autofluorescence problem at its source and promising imaging with near-zero background. Despite the ubiquity of fluorescence imaging and the clear need for a superior contrast mechanism, the development of a functional chemiluminescent probe for cellular G4s remains an unmet challenge and a significant gap in the chemical biology toolkit.

Coelenterazine, a key luciferase substrate found in most marine bioluminescent organisms, exhibits high sensitivity to reactive oxygen species (ROS) [43–45]. Drawing on this property, we designed and synthesized an imidazopyrazinone-based chemiluminescent probe PCL, which consists of a G4-binding ligand PhenDC3 (P) and a luminophore moiety CLA (Fig. 1). PhenDC3 is a high-affinity and selective G4 stabilizer [46]. The planar diphenanthroline core stacks onto or between G-tetrads via π–π interactions, and the flanking quinoline groups enhance both binding affinity and specificity [47, 48]. In contrast, PhenDC3 exhibits minimal binding affinity toward double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) or other structures and may be used to reveal G4 formation [46, 49–52]. CLA is derived from coelenterazine by retaining its key chemiluminescent core structure [43]. It is conjugated to PN, which is a derivative of P modified with an amino side chain via an amide bond to form the chemiluminescent PCL compound, thanks to a flexible linker (Fig. 1). We speculate that PCL enables the imaging of intracellular G4s by binding to them and emitting light upon activation by exogenous or endogenous ROS (Fig. 1).

Design of the G4-specific chemiluminescent probe PCL, which integrates the G4-binding ligand PhenDC3 (P and PN) with the bio-inspired luminophore CLA.

Material and methods

Oligonucleotides

All oligonucleotides (sequences given in Supplementary Table S1) used in this study were purified by HPLC and purchased from Sangon Biotech (China). All oligonucleotides were annealed in the corresponding buffer containing 70 mM KCl by heating to 95°C for 5 min. The solutions were then slowly cooled to room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C.

UV-vis, fluorescence, and luminescence spectroscopic studies

All experiments were conducted in potassium phosphate (PBS) buffer. For UV-vis absorption spectra, samples were analyzed in 1 cm path length quartz cuvettes using an Agilent Cary 3500 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent, USA). Fluorescence and chemiluminescence assays were performed using 96-well black opaque microplates on a SpectraMax iD3 (Molecular Devices, USA). To acquire chemiluminescence spectra, 0.1% H₂O₂ solution was added prior to measurement, followed by immediate transfer to the microplate reader for data acquisition.

Relative quantum yield measurement

The quantum yield (Φ) of samples was determined by the relative method using coelenterazine as the reference standard [43]. Both sample and coelenterazine were separately dissolved in DMSO, followed by the addition of 0.1% H₂O₂. ROS selectivity of PCL and CLA

The ROS selectivity of the PCL and CLA was primarily assessed using a Tanon Chemi Dog Ultra chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China). The compound was dissolved in PBS buffer containing 5% DMSO, whereas other reagents were prepared in PBS buffer. Chemiluminescent images were acquired immediately after mixing the compound with distinct ROS species, using a fixed exposure time of 60 s. All assays were performed in a 96-well black opaque microplate with a final volume of 200 μL per well, and each sample was tested in triplicate. The following list describes the detailed experimental conditions for determining the relative chemiluminescence intensity (RCI) of the compound in the presence of various ROS (H₂O₂, O₂⁻, ClO⁻, TBHP, ¹O₂, •OH) and antioxidants (SOD and catalase) [43]. All RCIs are reported as values normalized to the blank chemiluminescence observed in the absence of any ROS. H₂O₂: Hydrogen peroxide (1 mM, 10 μL); O₂⁻: 6-Hydroxypurine (50 mM, 10 μL) and xanthine oxidase (50 U/mL, 20 μL); ClO⁻: Sodium hypochlorite (1 mM, 10 μL); TBHP: tert-Butyl hydroperoxide (1 mM, 10 μL); ¹O₂: Hydrogen peroxide (0.3 M, 35 μL) and sodium hypochlorite (0.1 mM, 10 μL); •OH: Hydrogen peroxide (0.3 M, 35 μL) and ferrous sulfate (0.1 mM, 10 μL); O₂·⁻ + SOD: 6-Hydroxypurine (50 mM, 10 μL), xanthine oxidase (50 U/mL, 20 μL), and SOD (400 U/mL, 200 μL); H₂O₂ + catalase: Hydrogen peroxide (1 mM, 10 μL) and catalase (500 U/mL, 200 μL); •OH + 21HT: Hydrogen peroxide (0.3 M, 35 μL), ferrous sulfate (0.1 mM, 10 μL) and 21HT (100 μM, 10 μL).

Chemical stability measurements

Chemical stability of PCL and CLA under varied pHs was determined by the HPLC (Shimadzu LC-2050C, Japan). The compounds (100 μM) were added onto the Eppendorf tubes with 0.02 M buffers, which are constructed by sodium acetate for pH 4.0, 5.0, and 5.5, potassium phosphate for pH 6.0, 6.5,7.0 and 7.4, and Tris-HCl for pH 8.0, 8.5, and 9.0. All experiments were incubated at 37 ± 0.2°C. Samples of 100 μL were taken at time points of 0, 20, 60, 120, and 240 min, respectively, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. HPLC was performed with the following flow and gradient. Conditions:(a) flow: 1.00 mL/min; (b) gradient elution: A: H2O + 0.1% formic acid, B:CH3OH; (c) column oven temperature: 50 °C; (d) autosampler injection volume:10 μL (e) method: 0–1 min 20% B, 8 min 50% B, 12 min 95% B, 14 min 20% B, 15 min 20% B; (f) detector: 254 nm under UV; (g) HPLC column: Welchmat Ultimate XB-C18 column (4.6 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm). HPLC chromatograms were further plotted in Origin 2021 for visualization. The compound purity was quantified by the peak area normalization method using Shimadzu LC LabSolutions, and statistical plots were visualized using the GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

Mechanistic study of chemiluminescence by HPLC-MS

The PCL or CLA (1 mM) was added to 20 mM pH 7.0 PBS buffer, followed by the addition of 0.1% H₂O₂ and 0.01 M NaOH, and incubated at 25°C. Samples were automatically injected by an HPLC autosampler at the incubation times of 0, 5, 20, 35, 65, and 95 min for analysis according to the method described above. The major fraction was further analyzed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). HPLC chromatograms were further plotted in Origin 2021 for visualization.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of DNAs and RNAs

CD spectra were acquired using a Chirascan spectrometer (Applied Photophysics, UK) at 25°C with a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette. They were recorded from 220 to 320 nm at a scan rate of 50 nm/min, a bandwidth of 1 nm, and a wavelength interval of 1 nm. Sample solutions contained 5 μM oligonucleotides and 1.5 equivalents of compounds in 20 mM pH 7.0 PBS buffer with 70 mM KCl, in a final volume of 1 mL. Buffer baselines were subtracted from all spectra.

UV-melting of G4s

UV-melting experiments were performed on an Agilent Cary 3500 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Agilent, USA) equipped with a Peltier temperature controller. All samples in 20 mM pH 7.0 PBS buffer and 70 mM KCl containing 5 μM DNA/RNA G4 and 3.0 equiv. ligand were heated from 20°C to 95°C at 0.2°C/min. Absorbance at 295 nm (G4 signature) was recorded with a data interval of 0.5°C. Melting curves were generated using Origin 2021. Tm values were calculated by nonlinear regression fitting to a two-state Boltzmann sigmoidal function with baseline correction.

G4 fluorescent intercalator displacement assay (G4-FID)

G4-FID experiments were performed using a SpectraMax iD5 (Molecular Devices, USA) with a 96-well black opaque microplate at 25°C. The microplate is filled with the 200 μL 10 mM pH 7.0 Tris-HCl buffer supplemented by 100 mM KCl, 10 μL of a solution of pre-folded oligonucleotides (5 μM), a fluorescent probe thiazole orange (TO) (10 μM), and a 5 μM ligand stock solution, which was diluted in different volumes (final concentrations are 0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 μM). After 5 min of orbital shaking at 500 rpm, the fluorescence spectrum was measured (excitation: 480 nm, emission area: 510–530 nm). The percentage of displacement is calculated from the fluorescence intensity, using: percentage of displacement = 100 − [(F/F0) × 100]. F0 and F are the fluorescence intensity of TO before and after the addition of the corresponding ligands. The percentage of displacement traces was plotted, and DC50 values, which are defined as the concentration required to decrease the fluorescence intensity by 50%, were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. All titrations were performed in triplicate [50].

Binding affinities between the ligands and DNAs or RNAs are measured by the microscale thermophoresis (MST)

Oligonucleotides labeled with Cy5 at the 5′ end were used. MST experiments were performed using a Monolith NT.115 (Nano Temper, Germany), and all samples were loaded into standard MST-grade glass capillaries. The MST buffer consisted of a mixture of 20 mM pH 7.0 PBS with 70 mM KCl and 0.05% Tween 20, and 30 nM G4s. For chemiluminescent probe PCL or G4 ligand P/PN, the maximum concentration was fixed at different concentrations of 5–50 μM and subsequently 1/2 diluted for 16 steps. MST traces were plotted, and Kd values were generated by the NanoTemper analysis software. Graphs were further plotted in GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 for visualization. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Imaging DNAs and RNAs in non-denaturing polyacrylamide gels

A 10 μM solution of FAM-labeled and unlabelled G4s was added to a 10 mM pH 7.0 Tris-HCl buffer with 70 mM KCl. Subsequently, 20 μM of the PCL was added and incubated for 30 min. The total loading volume for each sample was 10 μL. Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was performed under light-protected conditions using 20% polyacrylamide gels in 1 × TBE buffer (pH 7.0, 10 mM KCl), at 4°C and 8 V/cm. The images were performed on a Tanon chemi dog ultra (Tonon, China), capturing dual-channel images through:(i) 490–530 nm bandpass filter (FAM fluorescence, FL mode), (ii) Chemiluminescence imaging (CL) mode. Consequently, after completing FAM and chemiluminescence imaging, all oligonucleotides were stained with GelBlue (Sangon Biotech, China) (diluted 1:10 000 in 1 × TBE) for 15 min. Visualization was performed under blue-light transillumination (470–490 nm excitation). Merged images were generated using TanonImage v2.1.3.

Cell culture and CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay

HEK-293T (embryonic kidney), U-2 OS (osteosarcoma), A549 (lung adenocarcinoma), and HeLa (cervical carcinoma) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, KeyGEN BioTECH, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, KeyGEN BioTECH, China). All cells were cultured in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37°C. Growth inhibition was determined by the CCK-8 colorimetric assay. HEK-293T, U-2 OS, A549, Hela cells were cultured in high glucose DEME (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). All cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL and incubated for 24 h. Then, PN and PCL at different concentrations (3–200 μM) were added into relevant cells for 48 h, respectively. Then, 20 μL of CCK-8 solution (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) was added to the wells, and the cells were incubated for another 40 min. The optical density (OD) was detected at 450 nm by an automated microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). The IC50 values were analyzed using Graphpad Prism 8.0.1. All the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell imaging of endogenous and exogenous G4s

The U-2 OS cells and HEK-293T cells in the logarithmic growth phase were separately seeded into 6-well cell culture plates containing DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. For live-cell imaging, cells were incubated with Hoechst 33 342 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum for 20 min. The staining solution was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with 1 ml of fresh DMEM medium containing PCL at a final concentration of 30 μM, followed by further incubation for 20 min. After staining, the cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS. For fixed-cell imaging, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with 1 × PBS. Thereafter, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min and washed three times with 1 × PBS. The cells were then incubated at 37°C in 5% CO₂ for 3 h with one of the following: 100 U·mL⁻¹ RNase-free DNase I (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), 10 mg·mL⁻¹ DNase I and protease-free RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), or PBS as a control. Subsequently, the cells were stained with 30 μM PCL in PBS at 37°C for 30 min, followed by DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) staining for 15 min. After staining, the cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS. Chemiluminescence imaging of the cells was recorded on an IX53 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a peltier-cooled CCD camera (OLYMPUS, Japan). For chemiluminescence imaging, freshly prepared 500 μM hydroxyl radical buffer (H2O2/ FeSO4 = 1:1, PBS) was added, the excitation light source was turned off, and images were recorded with an open filter for an acquisition time of 40 s. For fluorescence imaging, the excitation light source was turned on, and images were recorded. The excitation and emission wavelengths were 405/420–460 nm for DAPI and 640/660–730 nm for Cy5-21HT, respectively.

Then the pre-folded Cy5-labeled 21HT sequence (1 μM) was transfected into the U-2 OS and HEK-293T cells using Lipo6000™ (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and incubated for 4 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂/95% air. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min, these cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min and washed three times with 1 × PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated at 37°C under 5% CO₂ for 3 h with one of the following treatments: 100 U·mL⁻¹ RNase-free DNase I, 10 mg·mL⁻¹ DNase I and protease-free RNase A, or PBS as a control. The cells were then stained with 30 μM PCL in PBS at 37°C for 30 min, followed by three washes with 1 × PBS. Finally, the cells were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min and washed three times with 1 × PBS. The imaging methods were performed as described above.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of PCL probe

The synthesis and characterization of PN, CLA, and PCL are described in detail in the Supplementary Materials. Their chemical structure and purity were confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectrometry, HRMS spectrometry and HPLC analysis (Supplementary section 2).

Spectroscopic studies of PCL interactions with G4s

We first assessed the physico-chemical properties of PCL, including its spectra, chemiluminescence activation, ROS selectivity, and chemical stability. The chemiluminescence emission maximum remained constant at 520 nm, regardless of the presence of the G4-forming sequence 21HT (sequence given in Supplementary Table S1) (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Fig. S1). This invariance suggests that the light-generating chemiexcited state, produced by the chemical reaction, is structurally well-defined and minimally perturbed by the binding microenvironment. In contrast, the fluorescence emission of PCL exhibited a pronounced 20 nm blue shift upon binding to 21HT (Fig. 2A, B). This shift is characteristic of a rigidochromic effect, where constrained rotation and reduced solvent relaxation upon intercalation into the G-tetrad stack raise the energy of the emissive excited state. Furthermore, the fluorescence of PCL was red-shifted by 32 nm relative to its precursor PN, indicating extended conjugation within the probe (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2). These distinct spectral behaviors underscore a key feature of our design: while fluorescence reports sensitively on the local binding environment, the chemiluminescence output provides a stable, environment-independent optical readout, which is advantageous for reliable and quantitative imaging.

Chemiluminescent images were acquired immediately after mixing PCL with distinct ROS, using a fixed exposure time of 60 s.

Chemiluminescence responses of PCL against ROS

PCL and CLA exhibited distinct ROS sensitivity profiles, with hydroxyl radicals inducing strong chemiluminescence enhancements (77.06 and 63.37-fold increases in intensity, respectively; Fig. 2C, D; Supplementary Table S2). The relative chemiluminescence quantum yields (Φ) of PCL and CLA relative to the coelenterazine standard were determined to be 1.96 and 3.06, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). Under H₂O₂ activation, the chemiluminescence of PCL reached its peak within approximately 5 min, and the reaction was complete within 17 min (Supplementary Fig. S4). In contrast, CLA exhibited a weaker chemiluminescence over a 30-min period. The observed effect likely stems from the structural conversion of the carboxyl group to a long-chain amide following the conjugation of CLA and PN. This structural modification enhances electron-donating ability, accelerates the formation and decomposition of the key chemiluminescent intermediate dioxetanone (Supplementary Fig. S7A), and consequently results in higher ROS sensitivity for PCL compared to CLA (Supplementary Table S2) [55]. The enhanced ROS sensitivity and altered reaction kinetics of PCL compared to its parent CLA highlight that conjugation with the PhenDC3 moiety does not merely serve as a passive “warhead” but actively modulates the chemiluminescent properties. This tunability is a key asset for designing next-generation probes with tailored activation profiles. As shown in Supplementary Figs S5 and S6, the pH stability profile of PCL was similar to that of CLA [43]. They remained stable under acidic conditions for at least 4 h and exhibited only minimal degradation at physiological pH (ca. 7.4). Their degradation rate increased progressively with higher pH values. Then we used high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) to monitor the reaction process of PCL and investigate its chemiluminescence mechanism. To accelerate the reaction, PCL was treated with 0.1% H₂O₂ at pH 8.0 to facilitate monitoring. As shown in Fig. S7A, upon addition of the activator H₂O₂, PCL promptly generated the degradation intermediate 11a, which was confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Supplementary Fig. S7B). It subsequently underwent alkaline hydrolysis to remove the acetyl group, yielding the final degradation product 11b (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Overall, after conjugation with CLA, PCL retained its chemiluminescence capability while exhibiting an enhanced responsiveness.

Stabilization of G4s by PCL

Next, we evaluated the thermal stabilization of G4s and the binding selectivity of PCL towards G4s (sequences given in Supplementary Table S1). CD spectra revealed that the folding topologies of G4 DNAs or RNAs are not significantly altered after the addition of compounds P, PN or PCL (Figs 1, 3A and Supplementary Fig. S8). UV-melting showed that P, PN, or PCL could enhance the thermal stability of all G4s (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S9) and the corresponding changes in melting temperature (ΔTm) are summarized in Table S3. The G4-fluorescent intercalator displacement (G4-FID) assay was then used to evaluate the binding selectivity of these ligands [50]. DNA affinity was evaluated by the DC50 value, defined as the ligand concentration that reduced probe fluorescence by 50%. Similar to the ligand P [50], PCL exhibited a high selectivity for G4s over duplexes (Fig. 3C, D for PCL, Fig. S10 for P and PN). The DC50 values and selectivity ratios, calculated as DC50 (duplex) / DC50 (G4), are given in Table S4. We further employed microscale thermophoresis (MST) to quantify binding (Fig. 3E, F and Supplementary Fig. S11). Again, PCL exhibited a high selectivity for G4s over duplex DNA and RNA, as demonstrated by the comparison of the dissociation constant (Kd) (Supplementary Table S5). Furthermore, the conjugation of CLA leads to a decrease in the selectivity of PCL over duplex compared to P. This may be attributed to the position of the luminophore modification and the length of the linker, which affect the degree of freedom of the probe to intercalate into the G4s, resulting in a reduced binding affinity for G4s. Meanwhile, the weak basicity of the side chain increases its affinity for duplex DNA, further compromising the selectivity (Supplementary Table S5). Although conjugation with CLA reduced PCL’s binding affinity compared to P and PN (Fig. 1), it still exhibited significant selectivity for G4s (Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables S4, S5).

Visualization of G4s in gel by PCL

The ability of PCL to visualize G4s was assessed in vitro via native gel electrophoresis, using FAM-labeled 21HT G4 alongside control ssDNA and dsDNA (sequences in Supplementary Table S1). A chemiluminescence signal was detected exclusively in the lane of 21HT with PCL (Fig. 1, Fig. 4A FL channel). It is notable that the inherent fluorescence of the FAM-labeled oligonucleotides was quenched by the iodide ions of PCL (Fig. 1, FL channel), confirmed by GelBlue staining of all oligonucleotides (Fig. 4A). The chemiluminescence intensity of PCL in the gel increased with higher concentrations of 21HT (Supplementary Fig. S12). Furthermore, PCL exhibited greater staining efficacy for parallel G4s (c-Kit2, c-MYC, TERRA, and NRAS; sequences in Supplementary Table S1) than for hybrid (24TTG) or antiparallel (22CTA) ones (Supplementary Fig. S12), a preference that correlated well with the binding profiles from MST experiments (Supplementary Table S5). The above results demonstrate that chemiluminescence, as an independent detection modality, can be successfully applied to nucleic acid gel imaging, and its signal remains unaffected by the quenching effects commonly encountered in traditional fluorescence labeling systems.

Visualization of intracellular G4s imaging by PCL

The capability of PCL for intracellular G4 imaging was then evaluated. The normal cell line HEK-293T (human embryonic kidney), cancer cell lines such as U-2 OS (osteosarcoma), A549 (lung adenocarcinoma), and HeLa (cervical carcinoma), were selected to evaluate the cytotoxicity of PCL using the CCK-8 assay. Consistent with the P [52], PCL exhibited limited effects on the viability of all tested cells at concentrations up to 50 µM (Supplementary Fig. S13). We next evaluated PCL’s performance in live cells, selecting HEK-293T (normal) and U-2 OS (cancer) cell lines. Crucially, upon stimulation with a low concentration of a ROS activator, a distinct chemiluminescence signal was observed in both cell types (Supplementary Fig. S14). Consistent with reports of elevated G4 levels in cancer, the chemiluminescence signal was significantly more intense in U-2 OS cancer cells compared to HEK-293 cells [34].

To determine whether the cellular chemiluminescence of PCL originated from DNA G4s, RNA G4s, or both, we performed nuclease digestion assays (Fig. 4B, C). DNase treatment selectively reduced the nuclear signal, while RNase treatment abolished the cytoplasmic and nucleolar signals. This clear compartment-specificity indicates that PCL visualizes nuclear DNA G4s and cytoplasmic/nucleolar RNA G4s. The specificity of this interaction was confirmed by a combined DNase/RNase treatment, which eliminated all chemiluminescence. This pattern of selective G4 imaging was consistently observed in HEK-293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S15). To further validate that PCL reliably targets G-quadruplex structures, we introduced exogenous Cy5-labeled 21HT G4s into cells and repeated the nuclease digestion assay. The chemiluminescence signal from PCL showed strong colocalization with the Cy5 fluorescence (Fig. 4D). Importantly, this colocalization was observed with higher imaging contrast in the chemiluminescence channel, enabling precise correlation (Fig. 4E–G and Supplementary Fig. S16). This validates PCL’s specificity and underscores a key strength: chemiluminescence facilitates high-confidence colocalization studies by providing a near-zero-background signal that can be cleanly overlaid with complementary fluorescent markers without spectral cross-talk from excitation. Furthermore, the nuclease sensitivity of the PCL signal for these exogenous G4s mirrored the results from endogenous targets, providing direct confirmation that PCL specifically images both native DNA and RNA G4s within their distinct cellular compartments.

In summary, we have successfully developed PCL, the first chemiluminescence probe for G4s, and demonstrated its capability for background-suppressed, high-contrast imaging in living cells. Our work experimentally established that this excitation-free modality offers a distinct advantage: it produces target-specific signals with minimal concomitant background. This enables clean detection and unambiguous colocalization, as validated in our cellular assays. Looking forward, the full potential of this strategy can be unlocked by addressing current limitations [56]: developing chemilumophores with near-infrared emission and higher quantum yields for deep-tissue imaging [54, 57, 58], and engineering advanced delivery systems like polymeric nanoprobes or signal amplification schemes like CRET for sensitive [5], long-term tracking of G4 dynamics.
