Exploring the planned behavior of breast self-examination and its predictors among women in Northern Iran
Authors: Hajar Adib-Rad, Halimeh Cheraghalizadeh, Hajar Pasha, Mohamad Chehrazi, Fatemeh Nasiri-Amiri, Shabnam Omidvar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women worldwide. This study aimed to explore the planned behavior of breast self-examination (BSE) and its predictors among women in Northern Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 240 women in Babol, Iran, from 2020 to 2022. Data were collected using BSE barriers, knowledge and practice of BSE, and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) questionnaires. Data were analyzed using a linear regression model and Pearson’s correlation coefficient.ResultsThe mean TPB construct score was 53.2 ± 3.9. The majority of women did not perform the BSE correctly (91.2%), and over half of the women lacked knowledge about BC and timely detection methods (54.6%). There was a significant positive correlation between attitude and “Do not know how to do it” (r = 0.160, P = 0.01), and a negative correlation with the marriage age (r = −0.210, P = 0.001), while a significant negative correlation was found between behavioral intention and ‘‘fear of biopsy’’ as a barrier (r = −0.130, P = 0.05). Additionally, there was a significant positive correlation between the perceived behavioral control and ‘‘Fear of feeling pain during examination’’ as a barrier (r = 0.130, P = 0.04). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the lack of training by health care providers (β = 0.16, P = 0.02) and being afraid of finding a lump in the breast (β = 0.13, P = 0.04) were positive predictors of TPB. Conversely, marriage age (β = −0.13, P = 0.049) and attitude toward BSE (β = −0.19, P = 0.005) were negative predictors of TPB construct. Additionally, “fear of feeling pain during examination” positively predicted perceived behavioral control (β = 0.13, P = 0.05). Notably, the results showed BSE practice was able to positively predict behavior (β = 0.94, P = 0.0001). Knowledge was positively associated with “afraid of finding a lump in the breast” (β = 0.13, P = 0.04).ConclusionsLow levels of knowledge about BC, BSE, symptoms, and risk factors point to the need for targeted planned behavior-based interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24280-0.Keywords: Breast cancer, Breast Self-Examination, Theory of planned behavior, Knowledge

Background

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common type of malignancy among women, accounting for approximately 29.8% of cancer-related deaths [1]. Its invasive nature makes it a life- threatening condition [2]. In low-and middle-income countries, morbidity and mortality rates are higher [3]. Epidemiological data indicate a global increase in BC prevalence due to delayed screening among women [4]. This disease has the highest prevalence among Iranian women, with 25%, with diagnosis occuring a decade earlier (40–49 years) compared to developed countries(50–59 years), highlighting the urgent need for prevention and early diagnosis [2, 5].

BC in women is preventable through knowledge of breast self-examination (BSE) [6]. It can be cured if diagnosed quickly through clinical diagnosis and BSE. Investigations revealed that the BC screening can decrease the possibility of mortality and improve the survival rate [7]. One-third of cancers are preventable if detected at initial stages, while another third of cancers may be curable [8]. Timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment of BC are important strategies for improving the prognosis of the disease [9]. Today, low BSE adherence and delayed diagnosis of BC remain global challenges [7], limiting treatment options in 80% of cases and unfortunately contributing to a 50% mortality rate among BC patients [2]. Delays in symptom recognition and treatment worsen survival rate [10], prognosis, and overall coutcomes [1].

Recommended screening methods include BSE, Clinical breast examination (CBE), and mammography. BSE is an easy, confidential, safe, cost-free, and noninvasive method that requires no special equipment. When done correctly and regularly, it enables women to discover breast abnormalities such as masses or cysts at home [8, 11].

Despite its potential in early detection, knowledge and beliefs about health and disease can influence health behaviors such as screening [12]. Unfortunately, behavioral acceptance of BSE remains low among Asian women, probably due to ignorance of its benefits, insufficient awareness, and mainly the psychosocial-demographic factors. In this regard, it is important to be aware of barriers to undergoing BC screening [13]. A literature review reveals a lack of knowledge and fear as important barriers to hiding the willingness to screening engaement [14]. In Iran, 70% of BC-related deaths result from poor awareness and delayed medical consulation [15]. Effective BSE needs knowledge of BC signs and symptoms, risk factors, and proper technique- factors that influence women ’s ability to perform it correctly and regularly [16]. ​ Today, health education programs are grounded in behavioral theories, such as the theory of planned behavior (TPB), are critical for improving screening practices [4]. The TPB points to the role of cognitions and beliefs in healthy or risk-taking behaviors [7]. The TPB is a widely used framework for understanding and predicting health behaviors. This principle is particularly relevant in the case of breast cancer prevention. The early detection by BSE is critical but often neglected by women [17]. The TPB, developed by Ajzen [18], is a well-established framework for predicting health behavior, including cancer prevention [4]. In this theory, intention is the main determinant of behavior, which is itself influenced by three constructs: attitude towards behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. The contribution of each of these factors in predicting intention and behavior is different according to the type of behavior and the studied population [18].

Since knowledge and beliefs about health and disease can influence health behaviors like screening, and given the rising BC incidence and the gap between knowledge, attitude, and BSE practice, targeted policies are needed to address planned behavioral predictors of BSE adherence. Therefore, this study aims to explore the planned behavior of BSE and its predictors among women in Northern Iran.

Methods

Study design

This descriptive-analytical study was conducted among 240 women attending comprehensive healthcare centers in Babol, northern Iran, between 2020 and 2022. Participants met the following inclusion criteria: Iranian nationally, aged 30–55 years, education level of ≥ 5 grade, registered in Iran’s integrated electronic health record system (SIB platform), non- pregnant, non- breastfeeding, Babol residents, no personal/family history of benign/malignant breast masses, poor knowledge and practice on BSE, no prior formal health education on BSE, no employed in healthcare, and willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria included: withdrawal of consent, relocation during the study, and incomplete questionnaire responses. All participants received explanations regarding study objectives, procedures, completing the questionnaires, voluntary participation, data confidentiality, and their right to withdraw without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This study analyzes baseline assessment data from a previously published randomized controlled trial [19].

Sample size

This study was conducted in urban and rural comprehensive health centers of Babol, Iran, applying a multi-phase sampling approach. The first stage was done as a cluster: in this way, the Babol was divided into four geographic districts (North, South, East, West). In the second stage, one active comprehensive health center was randomly selected from each district (2 urban, 2 rural). From the SIB electronic health record, 466 women were initially invited via electronic notice; 226 were excluded due to unmet eligibility criteria. Overall, 240 eligible women (60 per center) with poor knowledge and practice of BSE were enrolled. The sample size was calculated based on an estimated correlation coefficient (r = 0.2), 80% power, 95% confidence level, and 10% dropout rate using the following formula [20, 21].

Data collection

To collect data, questionnaires assessing demographic and fertility characteristics, BSE barriers, BSE knowledge and practice, and TPB were used. Self-report questionnaires were administered with researcher’s presence. If women encountered difficulties understanding any questions, the researcher provided necessary explanations to resolve ambiguities and ensure comprehension. Additionally, researcher completed observational checklist for BSE practice.

Measures

Demographic-fertility characteristics: This questionnaire included the following variables: age, education level, husband’s education level, job, husband’s job, marital status, residence, income adequacy, menarche age, marriage age, marriage duration, number of pregnancies, number of children, BSE education sources, and BSE barriers.

Knowledge questionnaire: The knowledge of BC was assessed using a 40-item questionnaire evaluating awareness of signs and symptoms, related risk factors, and early detection methods of BC. Each answer was scored as follows: two points for correct answers, one point for “don’t know”, and zero points for incorrect answers. Total scores were categorized into three levels: good knowledge (54–80), average knowledge (27–53), and poor knowledge (0–26).

BSE practice checklist: This observational checklist evaluated participants’ BSE practice and included 14 items. Each item was scored one point for correct performance and zero point for incorrect performance. The levels of participants’ performance were categorized into three groups based on the total scores: good (10–14), average (5–9), and poor (0–4).

To assess knowledge and practice, a researcher-designed questionnaire was used. The content validity index (CVI) values were 0.84 for knowledge items and 0.87 for practice items, demonstrating good content validity. The content validity ratio (CVR) for knowledge and practice items was 0.77 and 0.79, respectively, indicating good content validity. For determining reliability, the calculated correlation coefficient was 0.85 for the knowledge and 0.84 for the practice questions.

TPB questionnaire: To assess the planned behavior of BSE, the validated questionnaire developed by Matlabi et al. [22], grounded in Ajzen’s TPB [18], was used. This questionnaire comprises 31 items in five constructs: attitude (8 items; score range: 1–40), subjective norm (7 items; score range: 1–35), perceived behavioral control (8 items; score range: 1–40), behavioral intention (4 items; score range: 1–20), and behavior (4 items; score range: 1–8). For the questions on attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention, a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree, agree, have no opinion, disagree, and strongly disagree) was used with a minimum score of one and a maximum score of five. Behavior questions were rated on a five-point Likert scale (always, most of the time, sometimes, seldom, and never) with scores ranging from 1 to 5. The total score ranged from 1 to 143. Higher scores reflect better condition within each construct. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the TPB constructs indicated excellent internal consistency: attitude (α = 0.99), subjective norm (α = 0.99), perceived behavioral control (α = 0.97), and intention (α = 0.99) [22].

​ Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, percentages) summarized demographics, fertility characteristics, BSE barriers, BSE knowledge/practice, and TPB constructs. Linear regression (Enter method) modeled TPB constructs as dependent variables against independent variables. Assumptions of linear regression were verfied, and the model was a reliable model for predicting data. Pearson’s correlation coefficient and multiple linear regressions identified associations (p-value < 0.05 significant). The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23 software was used to analyze the data.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 240 women completed the study. The mean age of the participants was 42.3 ± 6.9 years, and the mean age at menarche was 12.4 ± 0.75 years. The mean duration of marriage was 21.9 ± 8.2 years, and the mean age at marriage was 20.3 ± 5.2 years. Most women were married 98.8%, had two or fewer children (80%), and stated three or fewer pregnancies (85.8%). The majority (81.3%) were in reproductive age. Over half of the participants (56.7%) and their husbands (61.7%) had less than a high school diploma. Most women were housewives (91.7%), while the majority of their husbands were self-employed (83.8%). The majority of women were relatively satisfied with their income level (62.9%). Nearly half of the women resided in urban areas (49.6%). The most common contraceptive method was coitus interruptus (34.6%) (Table 1).

Sources of BC and BSE information

Most sources of BC and BSE information were healthcare providers (33.9%), followed by television (33%), the internet (12.8%), Family/friends (11%), Phone (6.5%), and radio (2.8%). Over half of the participants stated that they did not know about BC and its timely diagnosis (54.6%). Only 3.8% of women had ever performed BSE.

BSE barriers

The results showed that key barriers to performing BSE included: Being afraid of finding a lump in my breast (91.7%), not knowing how to do BSE (90.4%), lack of training by healthcare providers (89.6%), fear of biopsy (87.5%), lack of enough time for examination (86.7%), forgetfulness or negligence (85.4%), and fear of feeling pain during examination (10.4%). The most frequently cited barrier to performing BSE was fear of touching the mass in the breast (91.7%), while the least common was fear of feeling pain during the examination (10.4%).

BSE knowledge and practice

The mean total score of women’s knowledge about BSE was 18.8 ± 4.3 (range: 6–28). The mean score of knowledge dimensions included: BC signs and symptoms: 4.9 ± 2.4, BC risk factors: 5.2 ± 2.8, BC diagnosis methods: 8.1 ± 3.3. The highest and lowest mean knowledge scores were for BC diagnosis methods and BC risk factors, respectively. The mean practice score of BSE was 1.2 ± 1.3 (range: 0–4), only 8.8% of participants performed BSE correctly. A detailed checklist of BSE practice is shown in Supplementary Material file 1.

As shown in Table 2, only 3.8% of women reported performing BSE once. Most women stated they had not performed BSE yet but planned to do so in the next month (77%).

TPB construct

The mean score of the TPB construct was 53.2 ± 3.9 (range: 44–78). The mean scores of its dimensions included: attitude 16.5 ± 1.5 (range: 8–19), subjective norm 12.9 ± 0.9 (range: 8–20), perceived behavioral control 13.9 ± 3.4 (range: 8–38), behavioral intention 5.9 ± 0.7 (range: 4–8), and behavior 4.1 ± 0.3 (range: 4–9). Among the TPB dimensions, the highest mean score was for attitude, while the lowest was observed for behavior.

The highest correlation among TPB dimensions was with perceived behavioral control (r = 0.890, P = 0.0001), followed by subjective norm (r = 0.390, P = 0.0001). There was a significant positive correlation between the mean score of knowledge and its dimensions: BC signs and symptoms (r = 0.290, P = 0.0001), BC risk factors (r = 0.390, P = 0.0001), and BC diagnosis methods (r = 0.670, P = 0.0001).

Analysis of the relationship between BSE barriers and the knowledge showed a significant positive correlation between total knowledge (r = 0.130, P = 0.04) and the BC risk factors dimension (r = 0.150, P = 0.02) with the afraid of finding a lump in my breast.

The results of the study regarding the relationship between the BSE barriers and TPB dimension showed that there was a significant positive correlation between attitude and ‘‘Do not know how to do it’’ (r = 0.160, P = 0.01), and a negative correlation with the marriage age (r = −0.210, P = 0.001). There was a significant positive correlation between the perceived behavioral control dimension and ‘‘Fear of feeling pain during examination’’ as a barrier (r = 0.130, P = 0.04). There was a significant negative correlation between the behavioral intention dimensions and ‘‘fear of biopsy’’ as a barrier (r = −0/130, P = 0.05). The Correlation coefficient matrix between knowledge, practice, TPB construct, and BSE barriers is shown in Supplementary Material file 2.

The results of the study regarding interrelationships among TPB dimensions showed a positive correlation between subjective norm and perceived behavioral control (r = 0.180, P = 0.004). The findings of the research regarding interrelationships among BSE barriers revealed a significant negative correlation between forgetfulness/negligence and lack of enough time for examination (r = −0.130, P = 0.05). Additionally, there was a significant positive correlation between the ‘‘Do not know how to do it’’ and lack of training by health providers (r = 0.40, P = 0.0001), forgetfulness/negligence (r = 0.230, P = 0.0001), as well as a significant negative correlation with the marriage age (r = −0.194, P = 0.004). Women who cited ‘‘Do not know how to do it’’ as a barrier were more likely to attribute it to lack of training by health providers, while those with higher marriage age were less likely to cite ‘‘Do not know how to do it’’ as a barrier (Supplementary Material file 2).

After adjusting for other variables, the results of multiple regression analysis showed that BSE barriers- lack of training by health care providers (β = 0.16, P = 0.02), and afraid of finding a lump in the breast (β = 0.13, P = 0.04)-acted as positive predictors of the TPB construct. Conversly, marriage age was a negative predictor of TPB construct (β=−0.13, P = 0.049). On the other hand, women with stronger TPB construct stated the lack of training by health care providers and being afraid of finding a lump in the breast as key inhibitory factors for BSE. Additionally, higher marriage age was associated with weaker TPB constructs. Collectively, these barriers accounted for 7.3% of the variance in TPB among women after adjusting for covariates (Table 3).

Discussion

This study explored the planned behavior of BSE and its predictors among women in Northern Iran. The results of this study showed that over half of the women did not know about BC and its timely diagnosis methods. The highest knowledge scores pertained to BC diagnosis methods, while the lowest score relatet to BC signs and symptoms. These results are consistent with a prior research indicating insufficient BC knowledge among Iranian women [23]. In another study, less than a quarter of women (24.5%) demonstrated sufficient knowledge of BC symptoms and risk factors [24]. Additionally, the results of Reza Begi Davarani et al. (2017) study showed that the highest and lowest awareness of BC in women was related to diagnosis methods and risk factors, respectively [21]. Given that knowledge is the strongest predictor of BSE behavior, the low knowledge observed in the present study emphasizes the need for BSE screening educational programs. Variations in the level of awareness may stem from differences in the study population, timing, or regional contexts.

The results of the present study indicate that the practice of BSE among women was very low, which was aligned with the results of Matlabi et al. [22] and Naghibi et al. [25]. Only 24% of women stated performing BSE [26], with 12% regular BSE [21] and 14.6% practicing irregular BSE [27]. These findings underscore the uregent need for targeted educational programs to improve sustained behavioral change in different healthcare settings.

Other findings of the current research showed that healthcare providers and television emerged as the most sources of BC information, highlighting the potential of clinic-based counseling and media campaigns to improved awareness and behaviors [27]. Similar studies showed that face-to-face counseling was effective in enhancing BSE awareness and performance [28, 29]. Sama et al. (2017) showed that the majority of participants (88.1%) obtained the awareness of BC via television and radio [24]. Television was the most important source of BSE information for 19.6% of individuals [30]. There was a relationship between the prevention of BC and sources of information, especially radio and television [6]. Media platforms, particularly audiovisual tools, play an important role in education by engaging the senses of the learners and leveraging auditory and visual memory retention [31].

Insights from the present study suggest the important barriers to BSE in women. Key barriers to BSE included “Afraid of finding a lump in breast” (most common) and then “Do not know how to do it”, while “fear of feeling pain during examination” was the least cited. These results align with Rezabeigi Davarani et al. [21] and Charkazi et al. [23]. In another study, it was shown that forgetfulness, insufficient knowledge of BSE techniques, and time constraints accounted for 25% of the important barriers [32]. The reason for not performing BSE in more than half of women was a lack of skill or knowledge [21]. A significant deterrent was lack of procedural knowledge (54.9%), lack of self-confidence (24.5%) [30], and lack of skills [21], Fear of mastectomy (40%) and diagnosis (38%) [26]. Addressing these barriers requires targeted training that combine skill-building with psychological support to alleviate fears.

The research data showed that attitude emerged as the highest-scoring TPB dimension, while behavior dimension scored the lowest, suggesting disconnect between intention and action. Studies have shown that planned behavior can enhance BSE among women. Notably, positive changes in BSE behavior are more strongly related to the attitude construct than with other theoretical dimensions of TPB, emphasizing the critical role of attitude in creating behavioral changes related to BSE [22].

The results of the study regarding the relationship between BSE barriers and TPB constructs showed that women with a more favorable attitude were more likely to believe a lack of BSE knowledge as a barrier factor to its practice. Study in the literature revealed that a positive attitude is significantly related to improved BSE practice [33]. Additionally, in another study, researchers found that educational interventions using planned behavior improved attitudes and empowered women to adopt preventive behaviors for the early diagnosis and treatment of BC [21]. Considering the existence gap between attitude and BSE practice, the researchers suggest the need for sensitization campaigns and targeted educational programs to bridge this gap and improve regular BSE practice [29].

​ ​ According to the present study, there was a significant negative correlation between the behavioral intention dimension and fear of biopsy. Individuals with higher behavioral intention believed fear of biopsy was a less deterrent to performing BSE. This was in line with Chin et al. (2019), who identified perceived barriers as deterrents to screening. Perceived barriers to doing BSE can be considered as negative factors of screening behaviour. Also, a significant relationship was revealed between the perceived benefits and screening intention. Woman who knows the importance of early BC detection and the benefits of BSE are more likely to pursue breast examination confidently, without fear [4].

​ The research data showed that women with stronger TPB constructs stated a lack of training by healthcare providers and being afraid of finding a lump in the breast as barriers to BSE. Also, marriage age was a negative predictor of TPB. Shallo et al. (2019) revealed that the concept of cancer itself often causes fear or worry [34], which can be considerd as the third most important barrier to BC screening [35]. While fear or worry of BC detection can prompt women to follow early diagnosis [14], it can also paradoxically deter others from seeking early cancer diagnosis [34]. Similarly, Tan et al. (2023) reported that approximately 23% of women avoid BSE screening, leading to feelings of anxiety about the BSE procedure [7], which highlights the important requirement for BC training to address these behavioral and emotional complexities. In this context, interventions should consider the advantages of the perceived construct to enhancing self-efficacy, especially among women with more age of marriage, to help them overcome BSE fears by perceived benefits and empowerment.

The findings of the present study showed that there was a significant negative correlation between attitude toward BSE and age of marriage. Women with higher marriage age had a poor attitude toward BSE practice. Similarly, Tan et al. (2023) reported that women under 50 years of age are more prone to believe that screening can inhibit breast cancer, possibly due to generational differences in health beliefs [7].

The research findings showed that fear of feeling pain during examination could positively predict perceived behavioral control. Individuals with higher perceived behavioral control were more likely to perceive fear of feeling pain during the BSE as a deterrent. These findings align with those of Shalmaii et al.(2016), who found that the construct of perceived behavioral control has the strongest effect on predicting intention and preventive behavior, and modifying this construct may prevent inappropriate behaviors to some extent [36]. Also, there is a significant relationship between knowledge and women’s intention to perform BSE. Therefore, increasing the awareness of BSE, as a painless method in education [35], can increase the intention to perform BSE [4].

The findings of this study revealed that the behavior construct could positively predict the performance of BSE. Women with stronger behavioral constructs reported engaging in BSE more frequently. In the study by Rezabeigi Davarani et al. (2016), similar results were observed as in our study, where the mean scores for knowledge, attitude, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention were significantly higher among women with a history of BSE [21]. In this context, Parker et al.(2020) believe that the intention to perform a behavior is strongly related to its actual performance [37].

​ After adjustment of other variables, the collected data showed that women with more knowledge of BSE reported a higher prevalence of being afraid of finding a lump in the breast as an inhibiting factor for BSE practice. Additionally, these women had a lower marriage age. A review of the literature in this context revealed that knowledge, motivation, and skills predict BSE behavior. BSE training programs that emphasize skills, especially among older individuals, can motivate participation [38] and alleviate any concern about touching the lump in the breast during BSE. In Dadzi et al. (2019) study, it was observed that more than 50% of the respondents lacked knowledge of proper BSE techniques. Additionally, as the age increased, awareness decreased, and BSE practice became less frequent [39]. Therefore, healthcare providers must implement educational programs related to BC and screening methods for women, so that they can improve the state of awareness, practice, and relieve concern.

Regarding the inter-relationships’ among TPB constructs, the findings showed a positive correlation between subjective norms and perceived behavior control. Women with stronger subjective norms had higher perceived behavioral control. This is consistent with Dewi et al. findings that the variance of attitude and subjective norms could predict BSE performance behavior [40]. In general, behavioral intentions can be predicted from the attitude towards the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. These factors, combined with perceptions of behavioral control, account for significant variability in actual behavior [18].

Since one of the goals and policies of the Ministry of Health is to promote women’s health and increase BC screening behaviors, the results of this study, which showed the significant predictors of the TPB construct and its dimensions in BSE, suggesting its utility for designing targeted screening interventions. Such interventions could optimize the provision of screening services, ensure continuity, and increase engagement behaviors in various domains of women’s health. However, integrating additional psychosocial factors (e.g., cultural norms, healthcare access) may improve predictive power in future studies.

The findings of this study have to be seen in light of several limitations that could be addressed in future research. The first, the reliance on self-reported data for knowledge, practice of BSE and TPB constructs, introduces potential biases related to the confidence in the participant’s answer. To solve this, we tried questionnaire clarity and made the researcher available to answer participants’ questions during completion. The second is the generalizability of these results is limited by the inclusion of women aged 30–55 years only. Nonetheless, these results must be interpreted with caution. Future research should evaluate the applicability of the TPB model in different age groups, while expanding Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)-based interventions to include broader age groups and testing hybrid behavioral models to enhance effectiveness. Third, although the planned behavior provides a valuable framework for understanding health behaviors, BSE practices may be influenced by complex factors beyond the model’s scope. Therefore, future research should consider additional variables or alternative models to offer a more comprehensive understanding of the predictors.

Conclusions

This study highlights critical gaps in BC knowledge and BSE practice among Iranian women, driven by attitudinal and procedural barriers; to improve early detection, we recommend implementing educational programs that combine skill-building with fear-reduction strategies, leveraging healthcare provider networks and media platforms (TV and digital) for BC awareness campaigns. Additionally, behavioral interventions and public health strategies targeting planned behavior constructs are recommended for Iranian women to increasing awareness, reduce delay in BC identification, and encourage preventive screening. This study’s results offer valuable insights for designing predictors of planned behaviour aimed at improving BSE adherence, ultimately contributing to reducing BC.
