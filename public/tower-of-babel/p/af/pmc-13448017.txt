Identification of a protein S–binding site in the C2 domain of factor VIII light chain
Authors: Shoko Furukawa, Masahiro Takeyama, Keiji Nogami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe demonstrated that protein S (PS) directly impairs the intrinsic tenase complex, independent of activated protein C (APC), by competitively inhibiting the binding of factor (F)IXa to the A2 domain (residues 488-490) and light chain (LCh) of FVIIIa.ObjectivesThis study investigated the PS-binding sites within the FVIII–LCh subunit.MethodsBinding interactions between PS and the FVIII–LCh were characterized using ELISA, a synthetic C2-domain peptide (residues 2228-2240), and surface plasmon resonance. The interacting residue was examined using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide hydrochloride–mediated crosslinking and N-terminal sequencing. The functional role of the identified site (K2239) was evaluated using recombinant FVIII mutants and FXa generation assays assessing sensitivity to PS-mediated and APC/PS-mediated inhibition.ResultsPS bound to the FVIII-C2 domain and competitively inhibited FIXa binding to the LCh. Using a synthetic peptide (2228-2240) and crosslinking, lysine 2239 (K2239) was identified as a residue contributing to PS interaction. A recombinant FVIII-K2239A mutant showed reduced binding affinity to PS (Kd, 6.7 vs 3.5 nM) and impaired PS-mediated inhibition of FXa generation. This effect was additive with mutations in the previously identified A2-domain site (S488A/R489A/R490A). Importantly, while the A2 site contributed to APC/PS-dependent inactivation, the contribution of K2239 to this process appeared limited, suggesting a more prominent role in the APC-independent pathway.ConclusionFactor VIII residues 488 to 490 and 2239 participate in APC-independent interaction with PS, while residues 488 to 490 also contribute to the APC/PS-dependent inactivation of FVIIIa.

Introduction

Factor (F)VIII functions as a cofactor of FIXa in the intrinsic tenase complex, which catalyzes the phospholipid surface–dependent conversion of FX to FXa [1]. Factor VIII is synthesized as a 2332-amino acid, single-chain, multidomain molecule (A1-A2-B-A3-C1-C2) with a molecular mass of ∼300 kDa [2,3]. It is processed into a series of metal ion-dependent and independent heterodimers via cleavage at the B-A3 junction, generating a heavy chain (HCh) consisting of the A1 and A2 domains along with heterogeneous fragments from the partially proteolyzed B domain, linked to a light chain (LCh) composed of the A3, C1, and C2 domains [[2], [3], [4]]. Factor VIII circulates in the form of a complex with von Willebrand factor (VWF), which stabilizes its cofactor activity [5].

Factor VIII is activated by limited proteolytic cleavage by thrombin at Arg372, Arg740, and Arg1689, converting the dimer into its active trimeric form [6]. Proteolysis at Arg372 and Arg1689 is essential for generating the cofactor activity of FVIIIa [7]. Cleavage at the first site (Arg372) exposes a functional FIXa-binding site within the A2 domain that is cryptic in the unactivated form [8]. Cleavage at the second site (Arg1689) dissociates FVIIIa from VWF [9], contributing to its overall specific cofactor activity [10]. The active form, FVIIIa, is rapidly inactivated by serine proteases, mainly activated protein C (APC) [6], by proteolytic inactivation following cleavage at Arg336 in the A1 domain and Arg562 in the A2 domain [6].

Protein S (PS), a vitamin K–dependent anticoagulant factor, functions as a cofactor for APC [11], which catalyzes the proteolytic inactivation of FVa and FVIIIa in the prothrombinase and tenase complexes, respectively, on phospholipid membranes [12]. The physiological anticoagulant role of PS is evident from clinical observations that its deficiency is associated with a high risk of thrombosis [13,14]. In addition to its APC-dependent role, PS also exerts APC-independent anticoagulant effects, including inhibition of prothrombin activation, extrinsic tenase activity, and intrinsic tenase activity [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]].

We demonstrated that PS directly impaired the intrinsic tenase complex by competitively inhibiting the binding of the FVIII(a) HCh and LCh subunits to FIXa [20]. We also found that the 484 to 509 region within the A2 domain, which contains a FIXa-binding site [21], contributes to a PS-binding site, specifically through the sequential basic residues at positions S488/R489/R490 [22]. We also identified residues 2228 to 2240 in the C2 domain as a FIXa-interacting region [23,24].

The C2 domain interacts with multiple ligands, including VWF, phospholipids, thrombin, and FXa [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]], and contains major antibody epitopes [[30], [31], [32], [33]], highlighting its functional importance. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that PS-binding sites may also exist within the C2 domain. In this study, we aimed to identify the PS-binding sites within the C2 domain and to evaluate their potential role in anticoagulant regulation.

Methods

Reagents

Recombinant (r)human FVIII (Kogenate FS) was a generous gift from Bayer Corp. Human thrombin, PS, APC, FIXa, FX, anti-PS mAb (Haematologic Technologies Inc), recombinant hirudin (Calbiochem), FXa substrate S-2222 (Diapharma Inc), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC; Sigma), and horseradish peroxidase–labeled streptavidin (Chemicon) were purchased from the indicated vendors. The active site of FIXa was modified with Glu-Gly-Arg-chloromethylketone (EGR-ck, Calbiochem) to yield EGR-FIXa [23]. A synthetic peptide corresponding to FVIII-C2 domain residues 2228 to 2240 and its biotinylated version were prepared by Sigma-Aldrich Japan, with a purity of >95%. Phospholipid vesicles (phosphatidylserine/phosphatidylcholine/phosphatidylethanolamine, 10:60:30) were prepared using N-octylglucoside [34].

Factor VIII subunits

The intact LCh subunit was isolated from intact FVIII [35]. A cDNA encoding the human FVIII-C2 domain sequence was constructed, transformed into Pichia pastoris cells, and expressed using a yeast secretion system [23,36]. The C2 protein was purified via ammonium sulfate fractionation and carboxymethyl Sepharose chromatography [24,27]. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis of the purified protein, followed by staining with GelCode Blue Stain Reagent (Pierce), confirmed >95% purity. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay [37].

Mutagenesis, expression, and purification of mutated rFVIII

Mutated rFVIII was prepared as BDD-FVIII (lacking Q744–S1637; wild-type [WT] FVIII), FVIII mutated E2228A, D2233A, K2236A, K2239A (polar sites within residues 2228 to 2240), S488A/R489A/R490A in the A2 domain, and the combined S488A/R489A/R490A/K2239A mutant were stably expressed in baby hamster kidney cells and subsequently purified [38]. Resultant FVIII forms were typically >90% pure as assessed by SDS-PAGE, with albumin being the primary contaminant. Factor VIII concentrations were measured using an ELISA [33], and FVIII activity was determined via a 1-stage coagulation assay. Samples were quick frozen and stored at −80 °C. Throughout this study, amino acid residues were numbered according to the legacy FVIII amino acid numbering system, which starts from the beginning of the mature protein sequence.

PS-binding ELISA

This assay was performed using modified methods as previously described [20,22]. Microplate wells were coated with LCh subunit (150 nM), EGR-FIXa (150 nM), C2 domain (200 nM), or PS (200 nM) in 20 mM Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 7.4, overnight at 4 °C. The wells were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.01% Tween 20 and blocked with phosphate-buffered saline containing 5% human serum albumin for 2 hours at 37 °C. Subsequently, varying amounts of PS, LCh, or biotinylated synthetic peptide in HEPES-buffered saline (HBS; 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 0.1 M NaCl, 0.01% Tween 20) supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2 and 1% human serum albumin were added and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. Binding of the PS or biotinylated peptide was quantified by adding biotinylated anti-PS monoclonal antibody and/or horseradish peroxidase–conjugated streptavidin, followed by the O-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride substrate. Reactions were quenched with H2SO4, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm using a microplate reader. Nonspecific binding, measured in the absence of PS or biotinylated peptide, accounted for <3% of the total signal. Specific binding was calculated by subtracting the nonspecific binding values.

Zero-length crosslinking with EDC

Crosslinking reactions with 150 nM PS, various concentrations of EDC, and a biotinylated (and/or unlabeled) synthetic peptide (residues 2228-2240) were performed in HBS buffer containing 1 mM CaCl2 at 23 °C for the indicated times. The reactions were terminated by the addition of SDS electrophoresis sample buffer and boiling for 3 minutes.

Electrophoresis and western blotting

SDS-PAGE was performed on 8% gels at 150 V for 1 hour. For western blotting, proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane at 50 V for 2 hours in a transfer buffer containing 10 mM CAPS (pH 11) and 10% (v/v) methanol. The membrane was then probed with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated streptavidin. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (PerkinElmer Life Science), and densitometric scans were quantified with the Fusion Solo S (Vilber Lourmat).

Amino acid sequencing of the crosslinked product

PS (1 μM) was incubated with unlabeled peptide 2228 to 2240 (1 mM) for 30 minutes at 22 °C. EDC (5 mM) was then added to the reaction mixture, and the incubation continued for 1 hour at 22 °C. After adding SDS sample buffer and boiling, the mixture was subjected to electrophoresis on 8% gels. Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane at 50 V for 2 hours in blotting buffer supplemented with 0.5 M dithiothreitol. The blots were stained for 10 minutes with 0.2% Coomassie brilliant blue in 40% methanol and 10% acetic acid. Individual bands were excised and sequenced (Hokkaido System Science Co, Ltd).

Surface-plasmon resonance–based assay

The interaction kinetics of PS with WT and mutant FVIII were determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) using a BIAcore T200 system (Cytiva). PS was covalently coupled to a CM5 sensor chip at a density of ∼2.32 ng/mm2. Ligand association was monitored for 2 minutes in HBS buffer containing 1 mM CaCl2 at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. Dissociation was subsequently recorded for 2 minutes by flowing pure buffer over the chip. The signal was corrected for nonspecific binding using a reference surface. All reactions were conducted at 37 °C. Association (kass) and dissociation (kdiss) rate constants were determined via nonlinear regression analysis. The equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated from the ratio kdiss/kass.

Data analyses

All experiments were performed in at least triplicate, and the results are shown as mean ± SD. Nonlinear least-squares regression analyses were performed using Kaleidagraph (Synergy Software). The interactions between LCh, the C2 domain, or the peptide and PS in the ELISA were analyzed with a single-site binding model using the following equation:(1)Absorbance=Amax·[S]Kd+[S]where [S] is the concentration of PS or synthetic peptide in the solid-phase binding assay, Kd is the dissociation constant, and Amax is the maximum absorbance signal at saturation.

Data from studies evaluating the PS-mediated inhibition of the interaction between active site–blocked FIXa and the LCh subunit were fitted to the following equation using nonlinear least-squares regression:(2)%binding=Bmax·[LCh]Kd·[1+[L]Ki]+[LCh]+Cwhere L represents the concentration of PS or EGR-FIXa, Bmax is the maximum binding, Kd is the dissociation constant for the interaction of FVIII–LCh with either EGR–FIXa or PS, Ki is the apparent inhibition constant for L, and C is a constant for the binding of FVIII–LCh and EGR–FIXa that is unaffected by L.

Results

Direct binding of PS and the FVIII–LCh subunit by ELISAs

We previously demonstrated that PS directly impairs the intrinsic tenase complex, independent of APC, by competitively inhibiting the interaction between FVIIIa and FIXa through residues 488 to 490 in the A2 domain within the HCh, as well as through the LCh subunit of FVIIIa [20,22]. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the PS-binding sites on the LCh subunit involved in these anticoagulant reactions. We first examined the binding of the FVIII LCh to PS using a solid-phase ELISA, as previously described [20]. PS was added to the immobilized LCh subunit, and its binding was detected using an anti-PS mAb, as described in the Methods section. The interaction between PS and the LCh subunit produced saturable binding curves (Figure 1A), and the data showed a good fit to a single-site binding model. As this method is not a true equilibrium binding assay, the resulting Kd values represent an apparent Kd (Kdapp) for the interactions. Nevertheless, the Kdapp value obtained for this interaction (93.8 ± 16.0 nM) was comparable with that determined by an SPR-based assay (43.8 nM) [20]. Furthermore, to assess the influence of PS on the interaction of the LCh subunit with FIXa (EGR–FIXa), we examined the inhibitory effects of PS in a fluid-phase assay (Figure 1B). PS inhibited the interaction between EGR–FIXa and the LCh subunit in a dose-dependent manner. Inhibition reached ∼60% at the highest concentration (100 nM), yielding an apparent Ki value of 4.6 ± 1.2 nM. Direct binding of the 2228 to 2240 peptide to PS

To determine whether residues 2228 to 2240 of the C2 domain, which are part of a FIXa-binding site [23,24], also contain a PS-binding site, we quantified the direct binding of the C2 domain and a synthetic peptide (residues 2228-2240) to PS via ELISA. PS bound directly to the immobilized C2 domain in a dose-dependent manner, yielding a saturable binding curve with an apparent dissociation constant (Kdapp) of 263 ± 20.3 nM (Figure 2A). To further localize this binding region, a biotin-labeled peptide corresponding to residues 2228 to 2240 was tested. This peptide also interacted directly with immobilized PS, exhibiting a dose-dependent, saturable binding curve with a Kdapp value of 103.6 ± 29.4 μM (Figure 2B). In contrast, a scrambled-sequence biotinylated control peptide showed negligible binding (data not shown). Zero-length crosslinking of peptide 2228 to 2240 and PS

The involvement of residues 2228 to 2240 in the interaction with PS was further investigated using an EDC-mediated crosslinking approach. EDC reacts with the carboxyl group of a carboxylic acid residue to form an unstable O-acylisourea adduct, which in turn reacts with the amino group of a nearby ε-Lys or the N-terminal α-amino group, or with the hydroxyl group of a Ser or Thr to form an isoamide or ester bond, respectively [40]. A constant concentration of PS (150 nM) was incubated with the biotinylated 2228 to 2240 peptide and EDC. The results (Figure 3A) showed the EDC dose-dependent formation of an ∼75-kDa product, indicating a covalent crosslink between the 2228 to 2240 peptide and PS. The adduct’s mass (∼75 kDa) was consistent with a 1:1 stoichiometry between the peptide (∼2 kDa) and PS (∼75 kDa). However, at higher EDC concentrations (>5 mM), a loss of band density was observed. The reason for this was unclear, but it may be due to the formation of covalent aggregates that did not resolve on the gel or transfer properly. Additionally, varying amounts of the biotinylated 2228 to 2240 peptide were incubated with PS (150 nM) and EDC (2 mM) under identical conditions. Crosslinked adducts were again observed in a peptide dose-dependent manner (Figure 3B). The specificity of the crosslinking interaction between peptide 2228 to 2240 and PS was examined in a competition assay using an unlabeled version of the peptide to block formation of the biotinylated peptide-PS compounds. Various amounts of unlabeled peptide were incubated with fixed concentrations of PS (150 nM) and biotinylated peptide 2228 to 2240 (150 μM), and the mixtures were then reacted with EDC (10 mM). Titration with the unlabeled peptide resulted in a loss of the biotinylated product (Figure 3C), a response quantified by band densitometry (Figure 3D). At 400 μM, the unlabeled peptide completely inhibited the formation of the EDC crosslinked product. The IC50 value for this inhibition was ∼79.4 μM, similar to the above-determined Kdapp. These data are consistent with the specific interaction between the 2228 to 2240 sequence and PS.

N-terminal sequence analysis of the peptide–PS crosslinked adduct

N-terminal sequence analysis of the peptide–PS adduct was performed to identify the specific residues within the 2228 to 2240 region involved in binding to PS. The adduct contained 2 N-termini, with each sequencing cycle cleaving 1 residue from each chain. Figure 4 shows the sequencing data (in picomoles of recovered residue) for the peptide (Figure 4A) and PS (Figure 4B). The data shown is the average of 2 analyses performed on different material preparations. However, residues could not be resolved beyond 13 cycles, possibly due to similar residue yields, the limited amount of available material, and/or high background levels from the simultaneous analysis of 2 N-termini. The similar residue yields from both N-termini supported a 1:1 binding stoichiometry between the peptide and PS. Notably, K2239 in the peptide, expected at sequencing cycle 12, was not detected in either of the crosslinked preparations. In contrast, analysis of the native peptide (data not shown) confirmed the detection of K2239 at cycle 12 with yields consistent with other identified residues. Furthermore, all expected residues for the 13 cycles of PS were detectable. Residues involved in a covalent crosslink are not detected by this method, which creates a gap in the sequence [41]. Therefore, we tentatively conclude that K2239 in the peptide forms a crosslink with an as-yet-unidentified carboxylic acid residue in PS, although this interpretation is limited by the available data. Elucidation of a PS-binding site in the C2 domain

To investigate a PS-binding site in the C2 domain, a panel of 4 rFVIII single-point mutants (E2228A, D2233A, K2236A, and K2239A) was prepared using BHK cells, targeting polar sites within residues 2228 to 2240. Additionally, 2 rFVIII mutants containing the PS-binding site in A2 [22] (S488A/R489A/R490A and S488A/R489A/R490A/K2239A) were prepared to evaluate the contribution of PS-binding sites in the A2 and C2 domains. Specific activities were measured using 1-stage coagulation and FXa generation assays. The 4 individual rFVIII mutants with mutations in residues 2228 to 2240 exhibited specific activity ranging from half of or similar to that of WT-FVIII. To determine whether 4 polar sites within residues 2228 to 2240 of the C2 domain were responsible for the PS binding, we evaluated the binding between FVIII mutants and PS using an SPR-based assay (Table 2). The data were fitted using a 1:1 binding model with mass transfer. The binding affinity of WT-FVIII for PS was slightly higher than those previously reported for full-length FVIII (3.5 nM vs 14.3 or 9.6 nM) [20,22]. Discussion

PS acts as a cofactor for APC in the inactivation of FVIIIa [12] and acts as an APC-independent anticoagulant by suppressing the intrinsic activity of the FXase complex [19]. Regarding this latter mechanism, we previously reported that PS impairs FXase assembly by directly competing with the interaction between FVIIIa and FIXa [20,22]. The presence of PS with FVIIIa resulted in a significantly lower affinity (∼340 pM) for FIXa during FXase assembly [20]. This inhibitory effect was evident at physiological concentrations of free PS (∼150 nM), supporting a role for PS in the in vivo regulation of the FXase complex [14]. Foundational studies have located several sites on FVIIIa responsible for FIXa binding within the A2, A3, and C2 domains [8,21,23,42,43]. We have identified that a sequential basic cluster of residues at positions S488/R489/R490 in the A2 domain contributes to a PS-binding site [22]. Furthermore, since we previously identified residues 2228 to 2240 in the C2 domain as a binding site for FIXa [23], we hypothesized that a PS-binding site may also exist in the C2 domain of FVIIIa. In the present study, using multiple approaches, we identified residue K2239 as contributing to a PS-binding site within the C2 domain.

Direct-binding ELISAs revealed that the presence of PS inhibited the binding of EGR–FIXa to the FVIII–LCh subunit, providing evidence that the A3 and C2 domains may contain PS-binding sites on the LCh. Furthermore, PS bound directly to the immobilized C2 domain. We previously reported that while PS binding to the A3 domain was observed, binding to the C2 domain was not detected in an SPR-based assay; our current ELISA-based finding differs from that previous report [20]. This discrepancy may be due to the weaker binding affinity for the C2 domain (263 ± 20.3 nM) (Figure 2A) compared with the A2 and A3 domains (∼10 nM [20]) and/or differences in binding assay conditions, although the exact reasons remain unclear. However, the binding reaction showed a saturable curve, and a synthetic peptide (residues 2228 to 2240) also bound directly to PS, supporting the presence of a direct interaction. These findings were obtained under phospholipid-free conditions and therefore reflect direct interactions; the interaction profile may differ in the presence of phospholipid membranes. Previous studies suggested that the inhibitory effect of PS on the FVIIIa–FIXa complex was not markedly influenced by phospholipid concentration within the experimental range [20].

The formation of covalent bonds between the 2228 to 2240 peptide and PS using the zero-length crosslinker EDC was consistent with our proposed interactions between C2 and PS. The crosslinking is base sensitive, indicating an ester linkage between a carboxylic acid and a hydroxyl group [40]. The 2228 to 2240 sequence in C2 contains 2 basic and 2 acidic residues (Figure 4A). Through sequence analysis, we identified the previously discussed residue, K2239, as contributing to the crosslinking. Based on these data, we propose that K2239 may provide a key amino group in C2 for EDC-mediated crosslinking to carboxylic acids in PS. However, the presence of a base-resistant isoamide bond, in addition to the ester linkage, cannot be completely ruled out. Given the extremely short spacer arm of EDC and the steric constraints at the protein–protein interface, direct crosslinking is difficult to evaluate with EDC; therefore, we did not attempt direct crosslinking of the intact C2–PS complex.

PS negatively regulates intrinsic FXase activity in an APC-independent manner during the coagulation process [19,20]. Regarding this mechanism’s contribution, an FXa generation assay using an FVIII mutant with Ala substitutions in residues 2228 to 2240 revealed that the K2239A mutant impaired the direct inhibition of FXase by PS. This finding, consistent with EDC crosslinking experiments, suggested that this residue contributes to a PS binding site in the C2 domain. While investigating the FIXa binding sites in this domain, we identified K2239 as a residues contributing to PS binding. The effects of K2239 and the 488 to 490 region in the A2 domain appeared to contribute additively to PS-mediated inhibition. However, the observation that the K2239A mutant retains intrinsic activity comparable with WT-FVIII suggests that, unlike the PS-binding site in the A2 domain, this residue is not a major determinant of FIXa binding. This also suggests that the observed competition with FIXa in ELISAs cannot be attributed solely to this residue, and it remains unclear whether this residue alone governs the overall binding of PS to the C2 domain. These findings suggest that PS–mediated regulation of FVIIIa involves coordinated interactions with multiple domains, in which binding affinity does not necessarily directly correlate with functional impact. In contrast to the 488 to 490 region in the A2 domain [22], the proximity of the PS- and FIXa-binding regions within residues 2228 to 2240 of the C2 domain may allow these interactions to influence each other. These findings indicate that the C2-domain interaction is not merely structural but functionally contributes to APC-independent downregulation of intrinsic Xase activity under conditions relevant to free plasma PS.

PS primarily acts in the negative regulation of FVIIIa and acts as a cofactor for APC in the inactivation of FVa [6,12]. As a cofactor, PS is thought to increase APC activity ∼2-fold [44] by inducing a conformational change in the enzyme. This supports the idea that the association of FVIIIa with PS and APC is involved in its APC/PS-catalyzed inactivation. To investigate the function of this mechanism in FVIII, particularly in the region spanning residues 488 to 490 and K2239, we performed an FXa generation assay using APC/PS-induced FVIIIa inactivation. The results showed that K2239 in the C2 domain alone does not significantly contribute to the inhibition of APC/PS-catalyzed FVIIIa. In contrast, the 488 to 490 region in the A2 domain was essential for the PS-binding sites to function as an APC cofactor [22]. These findings suggest that the role of PS-binding regions may differ between APC-dependent and APC-independent negative regulation and that PS–mediated regulation of FVIIIa may involve coordinated interactions with multiple domains in which binding affinity does not necessarily directly correlate with inhibition of intrinsic Xase activity. Accordingly, as this study did not directly examine the potential contributions of other domains, a comprehensive interpretation of the role of PS in FVIII function requires further investigation, including the potential contribution of the FVIII A3 domain.

It is noteworthy that amino acid sequence comparisons of human, bovine, porcine, and canine FVIII reveal that residues 2228 to 2240 within the C2 domain are highly conserved across these species. Specifically, K2239 is conserved in humans, swine, and canines, while bovines have an arginine (R) at this position. This finding suggested that the specific interaction mechanism between PS and the C2 domain might be species dependent, with other species potentially exhibiting compensatory alterations in the PS-binding site sequence.

Although PS is well established as a cofactor for APC in the regulation of coagulation, our results suggest that it also contributes to the modulation of intrinsic Xase activity through direct interaction with FVIII, representing an additional layer of regulation within the coagulation system. These findings may also have implications for the development of FVIII therapeutics. Specifically, it may be possible to design FVIII variants that are less susceptible to direct inhibition by PS, while still remaining responsive to anticoagulant regulation through the PC pathway under conditions of enhanced coagulation. To enable such approaches, further studies are required to clarify the interactions between PS and FVIII.
