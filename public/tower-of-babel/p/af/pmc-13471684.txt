Gunshot wound to the trifurcation in an infant: Vein-graft repair with 27-year follow-up
Authors: Avery Bryan Daily, Fredrick Willius Knight
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNoniatrogenic penetrating vascular injuries in the pediatric population are uncommon. Lower leg gunshot wounds with arterial injury in infants are exceedingly rare, and optimal management remains unclear. We present the case of an 11-month-old infant who sustained a gunshot wound to the trifurcation successfully repaired with a saphenous vein interposition graft. This case provides a long-term insight into vein-graft durability.Keywords: Pediatric vascular injury, Vascular trauma, Vein-graft repair, Lower-extremity vascular repair, Arterial injury

Noniatrogenic arterial injuries of the lower extremity in pediatric patients are usually treated with open surgical repair. Infrapopliteal injuries are rare, and management strategies are not well defined. We report the case of an 11-month-old infant who sustained a gunshot wound to the trifurcation injuring all three tibial vessels. He underwent successful revascularization with a saphenous vein interposition graft. The 27-year graft outcome is notable. The patient provided written consent for this report.

Case description

An 11-month-old man was accidentally shot in his right lower leg with a 0.38 caliber bullet. He was hemodynamically stable without active bleeding. His physical examination revealed an entrance wound on the anterolateral upper third of his lower leg and an exit wound on the posteromedial aspect of the middle third (Fig 1). His foot was cool with absent pedal pulses and decreased capillary refill. No Doppler flow was audible. He had a foot drop. A plain X ray showed a fracture of the proximal fibula.

An illustration showing the bullet entering the lateral leg, passing through the trifurcation, and exiting medially.

He underwent emergency surgery. An intraoperative arteriogram was performed through a cut down on the right common femoral artery. This demonstrated occlusion of the proximal aspect of all three tibial vessels with reconstitution of the anterior and posterior tibial arteries at midcalf level. Exploration of the right popliteal artery and its branches was performed through a medial incision just below the knee. The peroneal artery was found to be transected and was clipped. The anterior and posterior tibial arteries were intact but contused and without Doppler flow. The tibial nerve appeared contused but was intact. Upon opening the proximal posterior tibial artery, it was noted to be thrombosed with intimal damage for 2 cm. The vessel distal to this only admitted a 1 mm dilator. A segment of the proximal greater saphenous vein from the ipsilateral leg measuring 3 mm in diameter was interposed between the proximal tibioperoneal trunk and the proximal posterior tibial artery. The anastomoses were oriented end-to-end and sewn with interrupted 8-0 Prolene using 2.5× loupes (Fig 2). Topical papaverine was used for spasm. A completion arteriogram confirmed patency of the vein graft with spasm proximal and distal to both anastomoses. Despite a warm ischemia time of approximately 5 hours, fasciotomies were performed out of an abundance of caution. The patient was placed on low-molecular-weight dextran for 24 hours. The fibular fracture was not addressed at the index operation. His postoperative course was uncomplicated, and he maintained a warm foot with a palpable posterior tibial pulse. He was not discharged on antiplatelet medication.

Reversed saphenous vein graft interposed between the tibioperoneal trunk and the posterior tibial artery.

The patient underwent a tendo-Achilles lengthening with tendon transfer for his foot drop when he was 4 years old and later played high school football. Graft surveillance by duplex scan was performed annually for 20 years and showed patency of the vein graft with triphasic waveforms and normal ankle-brachial indices (ABIs). He had a computed tomography angiogram 9 years after his repair and a magnetic resonance angiogram 13 years later (Fig 3), which showed patency of the vein graft without stenosis or aneurysmal changes.

Magnetic resonance angiogram at 13 years showing a patent vein graft without stenosis or dilatation.

He was lost to follow-up but was contacted for this report. The patient, now 28 years of age, has remained asymptomatic with full function of his right leg and no limb-length discrepancy. Although he had a palpable posterior tibial pulse, his resting ABI was 0.6. An ultrasound examination suggested graft thrombosis with a large serpentine collateral. A magnetic resonance angiogram (Fig 4) confirmed graft occlusion with a serpentine collateral from the anterior tibial artery to the peroneal artery and reconstitution of the distal anterior and posterior tibial arteries. Given the duplex change in waveforms (triphasic to monophasic) and ABIs (1.0-0.6) between ages 20 and 28 years, the graft likely thrombosed during this period.

Magnetic resonance angiogram 28 years after injury showing occlusion of the vein graft with a serpentine collateral from the anterior tibial artery to the peroneal artery and reconstitution of the distal anterior tibial artery and posterior tibial artery.

Discussion

The treatment approach for arterial injuries in the pediatric population depends on several factors. These include the child's age, the mechanism of injury, the level and extent of the vascular injury, hemodynamic status/active bleeding, the degree of ischemia, the availability of suitable autologous conduit, coexisting injuries, and the likelihood that the extremity will be functional.

In older children, most lower-extremity vascular injuries are noniatrogenic from either firearms or blunt trauma.1, 2, 3, 4 Management generally parallels adult guidelines,3,5, 6, 7 with aggressive open revascularization for acute limb ischemia being the standard approach.1,2,4,6,8,9 Not all penetrating vascular injuries require surgery.10 Minor intimal injuries with nonocclusive thrombus may be observed if perfusion is adequate.10 Injuries to a single tibial runoff vessel may not require intervention.8 Observation of minor injuries is appropriate if the foot is perfused.10 Revascularization is indicated for active bleeding or threatened limbs (≥IIa Rutherford ischemia).10

In neonates, infants, and young children, the vast majority of arterial injuries are iatrogenic.11 These are usually secondary to catheter studies performed through the femoral or brachial artery.11 These vessels are prone to injury and thrombosis due to their small size and propensity to undergo intense vasospasm. Open repair is technically challenging with mixed outcomes.6,12, 13, 14, 15 The trend is to treat most of these patients for the first 4 hours with anticoagulation.11,16 These young children rapidly develop collateral circulation, and their limb ischemia will improve in the vast majority.6 If limb ischemia progresses, alternative therapies such as thrombolysis or open revascularization are entertained.11

The patient in this case report was atypical because he was an infant with a noniatrogenic arterial injury. It is difficult to say if he was Rutherford class IIa or IIb.10 His foot and lower leg were not mottled but he had no Doppler flow. He had a foot drop, but this was due to a peroneal nerve injury. It was impossible to determine if he had rest pain or sensory loss. He warranted further diagnostic imaging, and a femoral arteriogram was performed in the operating room. The conduct of the operation by the vascular surgeon was based on his experiences with adults at a level I trauma center in the 1980s. Several factors contributed to a favorable outcome: (1) prompt transport to the emergency department; (2) an isolated injury to the trifurcation vessels causing thrombosis without significant bleeding; (3) the bullet striking the fibula but sparing the tibia; (4) damage to the peroneal nerve but not the tibial nerve; (5) an adequately sized saphenous vein; and (6) a short length vein graft.

Vein-graft repairs of lower-extremity arterial injuries in preadolescent children are durable.17 However, trifurcation gunshot injury in an infant requiring tibial reconstruction is exceptionally rare. Long-term behavior of small-caliber vein grafts in growing children remains poorly characterized.18

Conclusions

This case highlights a rare penetrating arterial injury in an infant successfully treated with a vein graft. Extended follow-up provides valuable insight into long-term graft durability, demonstrating that vein grafting remains a viable option for limb salvage in the selected pediatric patients.
