Rapid-onset cerebral fat embolism syndrome leading to brain death: A case report
Authors: Jordyn D Williams, Jonathan D Gates, Alfred J Croteau
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFat Embolism Syndrome (FES) is a rare clinical phenomenon attributed to fat droplet embolization and subsequent multisystem organ failure, typically following traumatic orthopedic injury. It classically presents with hypoxemia, transient neurologic deficits, and skin changes that appear a day or more after the initial insult. Its exact mechanism remains unclear, although the mechanical obstruction of capillaries or production of toxic intermediaries following fat hydrolysis and extravasation of marrow have been described. Here, we present a new case of cerebral FES with symptom onset within 12 h and brain death within 48 h.PatientA 22-year-old male with multiple orthopedic fractures developed Fat Embolism Syndrome with neurological and respiratory symptoms within 12 h of presentation.ResultsThe patient developed intractable cerebral edema and hypernatremia leading to brain death within 48 h.ConclusionDiffuse fat emboli can lead to rapid development of fatal cerebral edema even in the absence of anatomical shunts. MRI is the superior modality to detect FES and should be pursued early in patients with risk factors or clinical signs of neurologic involvement.Keywords: Fat embolism syndrome, Trauma, Orthopedics, Brain death, Anatomical shunts

Introduction

Since the first reported case of FES in 1873, experts have developed competing theories to explain the phenomenon. In the mechanical theory, fat globules are released during traumatic injury (e.g., long bone fracture), enter venous circulation, then travel through pulmonary circulation and enter arterial circulation (via arteriovenous shunts or microembolization) to occlude end-organ capillaries [1]. In the biochemical theory, supported by animal studies, disseminated fat causes a delayed but profound inflammatory cascade resulting in tissue damage [2]. This theory may explain the characteristic onset of FES 24–72 h after injury as well as how FES may emerge in the absence of recent trauma. Treatment largely remains supportive, although early fixation of bone trauma has been shown to improve outcomes, likely by preventing further delivery of fat into the circulation [3]. The role of anti-inflammatory therapies like glucocorticoids or free-fatty acid scavengers like albumin is controversial, as different studies have produced different results [4].

Here we report a case of FES with rapid neurological decline without an arteriovenous shunt. Despite anti-inflammatory therapy, the patient progressed to clinical brain death within 48 h.

Case presentation

Day 0

A 22-year-old male presented after being ejected from his motorcycle during a vehicular collision. On scene, EMS placed a tourniquet for an open right tibial-fibular fracture with profuse bleeding. His initial GCS was 15 and there was no reported loss of consciousness. In the trauma bay, he was in mild distress but hemodynamically stable with normal mentation. Imaging disclosed a right grade 3 open tibia/fibular fracture and a left closed midshaft femur fracture with medial displacement, foreshortening, and medial angulation of the distal fracture segment (Fig. 1). At the bedside, orthopedic surgery placed a traction pin into the left proximal tibia with traction hanging and placed his right lower extremity in a long-leg splint with realignment. A CTA showed no vascular injuries or trauma to the head, spine, chest, abdomen, or pelvis. He was optimized medically with plan for orthopedic fixation the next day.

Plain radiographs showing (left) a left closed midshaft femur fracture and (right) a right grade 3 open tibia/fibular fracture.

Day 1

At around 5 am, fewer than 12 h after presentation, he became increasingly somnolent, had an episode of vomiting, and became hypoxic to <63 %. He then showed mental status changes, including stuttering and dysarthria, right hand “pill rolling” motions, upper extremity areflexia, and orientation only to self. Narcan was administered without change and a stroke code was activated (NIH score: 18). He was intubated for airway protection after the onset of agonal breathing and decorticate posturing.

A repeat head CT was unchanged from admission, but MRI showed diffuse signal abnormalities concerning for embolic phenomena, as well as edematous changes of cortical gray matter and the cerebellar folia (Fig. 2). A CTA chest showed multifocal, centrilobular groundglass opacities in the lung apices as well as bilateral pleural effusions. An EEG demonstrated moderate-severe encephalopathy.

An axial DWI (Diffusion-Weighted Imaging) MR showing multiple foci of restricted diffusion scattered throughout the brain parenchyma.

There was increasing concern for fat embolism syndrome, and methylprednisolone 40 mg was started every 8 h to modulate biochemical inflammation. To regulate cerebral edema, hypertonic saline was administered, and the serum sodium goal was changed to 150 mEq/L.

Day 2

At 1 am, his exam worsened to include fixed, dilated pupils without a corneal reflex, concerning for brainstem involvement. A single dose of 50 g of 25 % mannitol was administered. A CTA head did not show any vascular occlusion, stenosis, or aneurysm, but a repeat CT showed multifocal infarcts with loss of sulcation from worsening cerebral edema. There was no evidence of brainstem function on repeat exam, and no clear cerebral activity was noted on his overnight EEG. Due to extensive brain swelling, he was not a candidate for neurosurgical intervention. To reduce inflammation and intracranial pressure, he was continued on methylprednisolone, and placed on q6 25g IV albumin therapy for FFA scavenging.

Day 3

He soon showed evidence of brainstem herniation, with loss of the gray-white matter junction and obliteration of the basal cisterns (Fig. 3). He had concomitant lactic acidosis and anemia, and his serum sodium continued to climb, consistent with central diabetes insipidus. Vasopressin was started to promote renal water reabsorption. However, his autonomic instability continued to worsen, with refractory hypertension and intermittent hyperthermia. Worsening multifocal lung opacities supported a diagnosis of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). To assess for contributory anatomical shunts, an echocardiogram was performed, which showed a normal ejection fraction and no atrial septal defects and no patent foramen ovale.

A non-contrast head CT showing diffuse loss of gray-white matter differentiation and effacement of the basilar cisterns.

He underwent formal brain death testing a few days later. Off sedation, he showed no reaction to supraorbital or mandibular pressure; no movement to noxious stimuli; and no corneal, cough, or gag reflex. A nuclear medicine study showed no cerebral blood flow and time of death was declared.

Discussion

The typical course of FES is milder than the case described here. Patients are nearly always hypoxic [5], but significant respiratory dysfunction is less common, with only half of patients requiring mechanical ventilation [6]. Neurological changes are frequent but typically minor, presenting as intermittent confusion and altered loss of consciousness, rather than EEG-confirmed encephalopathy, refractory cerebral edema, and brain death [7]. Mortality is typically below 10 %, especially in younger patients without comorbidities. A recent nationwide retrospective study published in Clinical Epidemiology found an all-cause mortality rate of 8.3 % for patients under 40 years old [8]. Rates of brain death in fat embolism syndrome are not common enough to have been formally calculated but have been reported in few case studies [9,10].

Degree and type of injury

This patient had fractures to all three of the most frequently injured bones in FES (the femur, tibia, and fibula, respectively) [11]. He had a left midshaft femur fracture as well as multiple fractures of the right tibia and fibula. Both historical and recent data suggests increasing risk of FES as the number of fracture sites increases [8].

Symptoms and associated imaging studies

This patient was hypoxic within 12 h, with altered mentation shortly afterward. This is uncharacteristic of FES, with symptom onset typically between 24 and 72 h. An MRI scan was the first imaging modality to visualize changes to the brain; CT scans were ‘normal’ at approximately the same time an MRI showed pathologic findings. Numerous other studies and case reports have reported similar results. Eventually, CT scans did show pathologic findings, but this took longer to detect, further demonstrating the importance of early MRI in patients with suspected FES [12,13].

Anatomic abnormalities

This patient developed devastating cerebral edema secondary to fat emboli without evidence of an anatomical shunt. The mechanical theory would predict earlier, and more severe disease, would occur in patients with atrial septal defects or a patent foramen ovale. Fat from bone marrow could enter venous circulation, reach the right heart, and pass through into systemic circulation to occlude brain vasculature. PFOs have been well established as risk factors in acute ischemic stroke [14], but more data is needed to evaluate the link between right to left shunts and cerebral fat embolism. A 1999 study showed that patients with right to left shunts had higher levels of emboli burden in the brain (microembolic signals detected on transcranial doppler) and were more likely to develop neurological symptoms [15]. Other studies have shown no statistical significance between PFOs and the development of FES [16].

Conclusion

Cerebral fat embolism syndrome is a rare but potentially fatal consequence of orthopedic fractures. The symptom onset is classically within 24–72 h but may present earlier in patients with severe impending disease. Early utilization of MRI can help identify cerebral involvement if there are clinical signs of neurologic dysfunction. The contribution of anatomical shunts to the risk of developing cerebral fat embolism syndrome remains an area of needed study.
