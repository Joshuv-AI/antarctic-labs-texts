Incidence and clinical manifestations of sudden death in young adults: a two-decade, nationwide, retrospective, cross-sectional study
Authors: Yair Maayan, Koral Yuhter, Hadasa Sarshevsky, Itai Reches, Or Sandman, Alona Sarig, Lucy Balagour Greenstein, Meir Schechter, Yair Hanina, Yonatan Felzenbuch, Nataly Zilberman Sharon, Aya Bardugo, Sharon Ohayon Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimSudden death in healthy young adults is a rare but devastating event, and its causes and clinical features are not well understood. We describe the clinical manifestations and causes of sudden death in a nationwide-representative cohort of young adults.MethodsAll natural non-traumatic deaths that occurred in the Israel Defense Forces between 2000 and 2024 were systematically investigated by a multidisciplinary review committee. Sudden death was defined as death occurring within 24 h of symptom onset. Demographic data, cardiovascular risk factors, prodromal symptoms, and circumstances of death were collected. Cases were stratified by age of death: 18–25 (younger cohort) and >25 years (older cohort).ResultsDuring 4,928,078.2 person-years, there were 64 individuals (57 men and 7 women) who experienced sudden death; 31 and 33 were under or above 25 years old, respectively. The calculated incidence was 1.23 events per 100,000 person-years. Cardiac causes were identified in 67.7% of deaths in the younger and 87.9% in the older cohort. Non-cardiac etiologies, including metabolic, respiratory, and intracerebral hemorrhage, were observed more frequently in the young (9.7%, 6.5%, and 9.7%) compared with older individuals (3%, 3%, and 0%, respectively). Cardiovascular risk factors were more frequent in older individuals, with all of the following being more prevalent smoking (33.3% versus 6.5%, P = 0.010), overweight/obesity (30.3% versus 12.9% P = 0.083), hypertension (33.3% versus 6.5% P = 0.007), dyslipidemia (51.5% versus 6.5% P < 0.0001), and diabetes (12.1% versus 3.2% P = 0.355). Prodromal symptoms occurring within 24 h before death were similar between the two age groups, with 51.6% in the younger cohort and 45.5% in the older cohort exhibiting any symptoms.ConclusionAbout half of the patients who had sudden death experienced prodromal symptoms within 24 h, and some of them had cardiovascular risk factors. This highlights the need for broader risk assessment and preventive strategies that address both cardiac and non-cardiac mechanisms.

Introduction

Sudden death is defined as a natural, non-traumatic death occurring within 24 h of symptom onset in a person not previously considered at acute risk.1 Sudden death was estimated at 29–39 cases per 100,000 person-years in the general adult population compared with 1–2.8 cases per 100,000 person-years in those younger than 35 years.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 The rarity of sudden death in young adults has limited systematic investigation; however, the unexpected loss of young, and often healthy individuals, highlights the critical need to identify those at risk.

Sudden death in older adults has often been linked to cardiometabolic comorbidities,2, 8, 9 however, among younger adults some reports suggest that only half of the cases are attributable to cardiometabolic causes, and the etiologies of sudden death in this age group remain under-researched.10, 11 Prodromal symptoms precede sudden cardiac arrest or death in an estimated 18–50% of cases, yet data on these manifestations and surrounding circumstances are limited and rarely studied systematically.12, 13, 14 These knowledge gaps hamper efforts to identify at-risk individuals and to guide preventive measures strategies. To address these gaps, we analyzed 24 years of systematically investigated sudden death cases that occurred in young men and women serving in the Israel Defense Forces (IDF), representing the young population of Israel.

Methods

Setting

This is a retrospective observational study based on the retrospective digital and manual medical records of the IDF Medical Corps from January 2000 until December 2024. All Israeli citizens are legally required to enlist for active duty at age 18–21 for an average duration of two to three years, with some continuing into long-term military service. Before conscription, all Israeli adolescents undergo standardized medical evaluations to determine eligibility for military service. Individuals with serious chronic illnesses (e.g uncontrolled epilepsy or inflammatory bowel diseases) are exempted, with approximately 0.9% of adolescents disqualified annually for medical reasons and an additional ∼11% not enlisted for non-medical reasons.15

Per institutional regulations, every natural death, excluding trauma, suicide, drowning, or accidents, must undergo a formal inquiry by a multidisciplinary team headed by a relevant medical specialist. The committee, which includes several specialists who collectively determine the cause of death, is charged with determining the cause of death and identifying any systemic or individual failures. The process includes a systematic examination of complete electronic health records, in addition to interviews with eyewitnesses and family members, consultations with the decedent’s family physician to assess pre-existing medical conditions and risk factors, and discussions with pre-hospital emergency responders and in-hospital medical teams to evaluate the medical management provided. Post-mortem investigations, such as imaging and autopsy, are carried out only with the family's approval. In the absence of an autopsy, causes of death are determined only if unequivocal clinical evidence exists. Otherwise, cases are classified as sudden unexplained death (SUD). Each case is summarized in a comprehensive report, which is presented to the Surgeon General for the purpose of drawing conclusions and systemic learning.16 The findings of the formal inquiry served as our primary source for determining the cause of death, identifying risk factors, and documenting prodromal symptoms.

Study design and participants

For the present study, we manually reviewed all committee reports of cases that occurred between 2000 and 2024. Included in the study were all natural deaths investigated by the medical Committee in which symptoms or signs of illness occurred less than 24 h before death (defined as Sudden death). Sudden deaths were further categorized by etiology into cardiac ischemic, cardiac non-ischemic, sudden unexplained death (which is presumed to be cardiac in origin,2, 6, 15, 17, 18), metabolic, respiratory, intracranial hemorrhage, and aortic dissection. Prodromal symptoms within the 24 h prior death were classified as chest pain, dyspnea, gastrointestinal symptoms, general symptoms (e.g., weakness or fatigue) or unknown (in cases where prodromal symptoms were not documented).

Statistical methods

Because military service in Israel is mandatory for young adults but voluntary beyond the initial term, we categorized deaths as 18–25 years (younger cohort) and >25 years (older cohort). Means and standard deviations (SD) were reported for continuous variables, and numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Incidence was calculated per 100,000 person-years, including all serving personnel. The Institutional Review Board of the Israel Defense Forces Medical Corps (No. 2256-2021) approved this study.

Results

Between January 2000 and December 2024, during a total follow-up of 4,928,078.2 person-years, 192 cases of natural non-traumatic death occurred and were investigated. Of those, 64 cases were classified as sudden death cases and included in the study (57 men and 7 women). The calculated incidence of sudden death in our cohort was 1.23 events per 100,000 person-years, without evidence of change in incidence over the years (Fig. 1). Most of the deceased (87.5%) were born in Israel, and the majority (70.3%) served in non-combat roles. The younger cohort (18–25 years) included 31 individuals, 7 of whom were women. The older cohort (≥25 years) included 33 individuals, all of whom were men. Cases attributed to cardiac etiologies accounted for 67.7% of cases in the younger cohort, compared to 87.9% in the older cohort, Metabolic (heat stroke, hypoglycemia), respiratory (asthma, pulmonary embolism), and intracerebral hemorrhage causes of death were observed more frequently in the younger cohort (9.7%, 6.5%, 9.7%, respectively) than in the older age group (3%, 3%, 0%, respectively). The distribution of specific cardiac causes varied, with ischemic heart disease confirmed in 30.3% of deaths in the older cohort, compared to none in the younger cohort. Non-ischemic cardiac causes were identified in 3% of older individuals versus 13% among younger individuals. Prodromal symptoms occurring within 24 h before death were similar between the two age groups, with 51.6% in the younger cohort and 45.5% in the older cohort exhibiting any symptoms. Chest pain was reported in 16.1% of cases in younger individuals and 18.2% in older individuals; dyspnea in 9.7% versus 3.0%; and gastrointestinal symptoms in 19.4% versus 15.2%, respectively. General symptoms (e.g., dizziness or weakness) were reported in 22.6% of individuals in the younger cohort and in none of the older cohort. Overall, similar rates of chest compression and defibrillation were performed.

Discussion

This study characterizes sudden death cases in young healthy adults over 18 years. Individuals who died before the age of 25 years had fewer cardiovascular risk factors, and they showed a different distribution of causes of death, with more causes attributed to non-cardiac causes compared to the older ones. About half of the patients experienced prodromal symptoms within 24 h across both age groups, underscoring the need for broader risk assessment and preventive strategies.

Most studies on sudden death in young adults examine only sudden cardiac death (SCD), thereby underestimating non-cardiac etiologies.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 The few studies that did assess all-cause sudden death reported wide variation, with 25–87% of cases attributed to cardiac etiologies, reflecting differences in age cutoffs (ranging from 20 to 40 years), case definitions, and study populations.8, 9, 11, 16, 23, 24 In our cohort, 67.7% of the deaths up to age 25 were attributed to cardiac etiologies, most classified as sudden unexplained death (SUD), consistent with prior reports.2, 8, 17, 18, 22

Previous limited studies have reported that warning signs precede sudden death in approximately 18–50% of cases; however, most of these investigations focused on older populations (with a mean age at death over 50 years) and provided little to no comparison with younger individuals.13, 14, 20, 25 Our findings highlight a similarly high prevalence of prodromal symptoms, suggesting that early warning signs may be more common than previously recognized, even among younger populations. Recognizing and integrating these prodromal symptoms with individual risk factor profiles could substantially enhance early identification of those at risk for sudden death, offering an opportunity for timely intervention and prevention even in young adults, in whom such events are often unexpected.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite systematic data collection over 25 years, the number of sudden death cases was small (n = 64), which limits the statistical power for analyses and modeling. Second, while individuals under 25 years generally represent the broader Israeli population, those over 25 years are mostly career military personnel, with lower external validity. Third, autopsy data were available in only 28 cases, and post mortem CT was performed in 11 additional cases, the lack of autopsy data in some of the cases can potentially affect the accuracy of cause-of-death classification. Fourth, the study excluded cases in which symptoms first appeared more than 24 h before death, which may have led to the exclusion of some relevant cases and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The strength of this study lies in its comprehensive and active investigation of each sudden death case by a dedicated military review committee.

Conclusion

In this cohort of healthy young adults, we delineated the clinical phenotype and etiologic spectrum of sudden death, showing that prodromal symptoms within 24 h are common, present in approximately half of cases, and that a meaningful subset exhibited conventional cardiovascular risk factors. This highlights the need for broader risk assessment and preventive strategies that address both cardiac and non-cardiac mechanisms. Continued research is warranted to explore these patterns and their potential implications further and guide effective screening and intervention efforts.
