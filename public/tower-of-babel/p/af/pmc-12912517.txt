The Morbidity Profile and Working conditions of Handloom Weavers in Tamil Nadu - A Descriptive Study
Authors: P Seenivasan, S Doss Prasanna, R Tamilarasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context:Handloom weaving is one among the most culturally significant, age-old industries in India. But it is largely unorganized, exposing workers to various occupational health hazards and suboptimal working conditions. Recognizing and addressing their health issues is very crucial.Subjects and Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted among 330 handloom weavers aged >18 years and of both sexes selected by cluster sampling method from July 2023 to September 2023 in Kanchipuram district in Tamil Nadu. After obtaining ethical approval, the data were collected by face-to-face interview method using a questionnaire designed using previous literatures, Ergonomic Evaluation Checklist, and the Nordic standard musculoskeletal questionnaire and the working environment details were recorded by observation. Data were analyzed using SPSS software (IBM) and expressed as percentages or mean with standard deviation.Results:Many were at the age group of 41–60 years with mean (SD) of 45.85 (±10.37) and majority were males (n = 231, 70%). Their average total duration of work was 13.52 years. The working h per day was 8.65 h. The mean overall Ergonomic Index was 29.29%. Workstation design and adaptability (23.67%), working posture (6.30%), and general conditions (32.98%) were all noticeably poor indicated sub-optimal ergonomic conditions, despite the Hand Tool Design Index was satisfactory (63.28%). The common health problems reported were musculoskeletal disorders (n = 261,79.10%), eye problems (n = 167, 50.60%), respiratory problems (n = 49,14.80%), and gastrointestinal problems (n = 46, 13.90%).Conclusions:This study revealed a considerable health problems and risk among handloom weavers, highlighting the need for interventions to improve health monitoring, and working environment.

INTRODUCTION

India, with its rich cultural heritage, is home to a variety of traditional skills and crafts. Handloom weaving is one among the most culturally significant and age-old industries that provides the valuable fabric to the rural and semi-urban communities in India. The art and expertise of handloom weaving have been passed on for generations and have survived this modern world of mechanization. The Tamil Nadu state is renowned for its rich tradition of handloom weaving, with specialization in unique textile production with clusters like Kancheepuram, Chennimalai, and Madurai.[1] Despite its importance, handloom weaving is largely unorganized and labor-intensive, exposing the workers to various occupational health hazards and suboptimal working conditions.[2]

Handloom weavers often experience a range of health problems due to their nature of work, which includes prolonged sitting, repetitive hand movements, exposure to cotton dust, and inadequate ventilation in weaving units. Studies have shown a high prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders, ocular problems, and dermatological conditions among handloom weavers due to poor ergonomic design and long working h.[345] Furthermore, studies in various parts of India have also reported respiratory issues such as chronic bronchitis and byssinosis due to exposure to cotton dust in inadequately ventilated workspaces.[67]

In recent years, handloom weaving has declined as the handloom weavers more often shift to power looms or to other occupations. But these power looms do not have the diversity, creativity, and quality as handloom fabrics. Nonetheless, this shift has been due to the economic insecurity, diminished returns, financial instability, market fluctuations, and poor socio-economic status. This has been compounded by various health problems that grapple the handloom weavers especially poor nutrition, delayed healthcare access, and a high prevalence of anemia and other chronic conditions.[891011] Most handloom weavers work in small-scale or home-based units often have low wages, job insecurity, and lack of social security benefits resulting in significant psychological stress.[12] Depression, anxiety, and substance abuse are also commonly reported among handloom weavers, particularly among those facing occupational injuries or economic distress.[13] The lack of formal health coverage and limited access to occupational health services further aggravates their situation.[14]

According to the Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics, the handloom products export from India was valued at $343.69 million in the financial year 2019, with the US, UK, Italy, and Germany, being pertinent importers. This handloom sector contributes nearly 15% of the cloth production in the country and contributes to the export earnings of the country. Also, according to the ministry of textiles, India alone contributes 95% of the world’s hand-woven fabric.[15] Despite the significant contributions of handloom weavers to India’s economy and cultural heritage, the health issues associated with this sector did not receive much attention and few studies have been done on general occupational hazards rather than providing a comprehensive assessment of morbidity patterns specific to handloom workers.[16] Recognizing and addressing the health issues is crucial for ensuring the well-being of the handloom weavers and has a major role in the continuity and sustainability of this age-old craft. Hence, this study aims to determine the extent of various health problems faced by the handloom weavers and its associated factors including their working environment.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This is a cross-sectional study conducted between July 2023 to September 2023 in Kanchipuram district which is a well-known hub for the handloom weavers in Tamil Nadu. Before the conduct of the study, permission and approval was obtained from the Director of Public Health, District Health Officer and Local authorities, Authorities of the handloom weavers co-operative society. Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee.

According to the handloom weavers report maintained by the co-operative society at Kanchipuram, around 60329 weavers are in the study area of which (39214) 65% are Handloom weavers. This study included all the traditional handloom weavers aged more than 18 years and of both sexes who work in both individual households and cooperatives. The handloom weavers with more than 1 year of experience and who gave consent to be the study participants were included to determine the 12-month prevalence of various health problems including the most common Musculo skeletal disorders among the weavers. The sample size was calculated based on the findings of a population based cross sectional study done in Burkina Faso in 2020 by Kabore, P. A. et al., where the 12-month prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders was 85.4%.[17] Considering the Confidence level of 95%, absolute precision of 5%, design effect of 1.5 and 10% non-response rate with excess sampling to account for non-response, the sample size derived is 330.

The total sample size of 330 weavers for this study were included by multi-stage cluster sampling method. Out of the 5 blocks in Kanchipuram District, Kanchipuram Block with 7142 handloom weavers was selected by Lot method. Kanchipuram block consists of 43 village panchayats with each village being considered as a cluster. A cluster size of 10 was determined to include more clusters for the study as selecting additional members in the same cluster will not provide any significant new information. Out of the 43 villages in Kanchipuram block, 33 clusters were chosen by simple random sampling. The participants in each cluster were selected by Random Walk method. The survey in each cluster was started from the first household and moving in the right-hand direction and as per inclusion criteria, included the handloom weavers who were available at the time of visit and gave consent to participate until the sample size is reached in that cluster.

The questionnaire for data collection was designed using previous literatures, Ergonomic Evaluation Checklist, and the Nordic standard musculoskeletal questionnaire.[1819] The questionnaire included information on demographic characteristics, health status related questions including musculoskeletal symptoms, and occupational environment. The data was collected from respondents by face-to-face interview method and the working environment details were recorded by the investigator through observation using an Ergonomic Evaluation Checklist. Each item in the checklist was assessed as either Present/Provided (Yes) or Absent/Not provided (No). A score of 1 was given for Yes and 0 for No.

Normality of the data was elicited by Shapiro wilk test. Univariate analysis was used to describe the categorical data using percentages, and continuous data using mean with standard deviation. For comparing variables chi square test, independent sample t test, one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used. In the bivariate analysis to determine the presence of association between variables, an odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval and a P value of less than 0.05 was determined to decide on the presence of a significant association among variables.

The mean height of 330 participants was 159.53 (±9.30 cm) and the mean height in meters was 1.59 (±0.09 m). These findings suggest a relatively low prevalence of smoking behavior but a notable proportion of alcohol users.

Musculoskeletal problems

A high prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders (n = 261,79.1%) was found among workers, with the most affected regions being the upper back (90.3%), ankle and feet (57.3%), shoulder (33.3%), and elbow (70.0%). The most common joints involved were the shoulder and ankle joints. This highlights the ergonomic strain associated with weaving activities though these variables do not have any statistically significant association (P > 0.05) with socio demographic factors and working environment.

Eye problems

Half of the study participants (n = 167, 50.6%) reported to have at least one eye-related problems. The most common complaints were dimness of vision (38.8%), followed by eye redness (9.4%), eye irritation (6.7%), eye pain (6.4%), and eye watering (6.1%). The high prevalence of vision-related issues may be linked to prolonged exposure to minutiae work and poor lighting conditions but it had no statistically significant association (P > 0.05).

Respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urinary issues

Respiratory problems were noted in some of the (n = 49, 14.8%) workers, with chest tightness (7.9%), breathlessness (6.4%), and chronic cough (4.2%) being common symptoms. Gastrointestinal complaints (n = 46, 13.9%) included abdominal pain (7.6%), belching (7.3%), and constipation (2.4%). Urinary issues were reported by 8.8% of participants, with frequent urination (8.2%) and burning or painful urination (2.4%) being the reported complaints.

Skin and ear problems

Skin-related issues were noted in 8.2% of the population, with skin ulcers (0.6%), rashes (5.5%), and skin irritation (4.2%). Ear problems were reported by 7.3%, with hearing loss (6.1%) and ear pain (1.1%) being the main complaints.

Chronic illness

The most common chronic illnesses seen in Handloom Weavers were Diabetes Mellitus (n = 67, 20.3%) and Hypertension (n = 40, 12.1%). Other conditions included bronchial asthma (1.8%), thyroid disorders (1.5%), stroke (0.6%), and tuberculosis (0.6%).

Ergonomic assessment

Table 4 shows the ergonomic parameter index of the handloom units. The ergonomic index value of more than 50% was taken as having satisfactory working condition. Among the recorded parameters, the hand tool design index was alone satisfactory (63.28 ± 15.95), whereas the Workstation design and adaptability index (23.67 ± 12.11) were relatively low and denotes the unsatisfactory workstation. The Working posture index was unacceptably low (6.30 ± 8.98), indicating the poor ergonomic conditions and General Condition Index (32.98 ± 12.79) was also unsatisfactory. The overall mean Ergonomic Index was 29.29 ± 6.78, suggesting suboptimal working conditions that likely contributed to the high prevalence of musculoskeletal and visual complaints in the study participants though they do not have significant statistical association (P > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

The present study done in handloom weavers provides a comprehensive understanding of the socio-demographic profile, occupational characteristics, health problems, and ergonomic conditions of handloom weavers. The findings highlight different occupational health concerns that may require corrective measures to improve the working conditions of this vulnerable workgroup.

Socio-demographic and personal characteristics

Many handloom weavers in the study belonged to the age group of 41–60 years, indicating an aging work population. This result is in line with earlier research done by Patil and Chavan, in 2019, which found that traditional weaving is steadily declining among younger generations because of poor economic returns and physically exhausting work conditions.[20] The predominance of male workers (70%) is consistent with findings from similar occupational study done Ghosh et al.,[21] where they have mentioned that handloom weaving is traditionally male-dominated, though female participation is not insignificant. The higher proportion of married individuals engaged in handloom industry (90.30%) is indicative of the prevailing sociocultural standards.

Work characteristics and occupational exposure

Long-term exposure to occupational hazards was reflected in the mean work duration of 13.52 years and the daily work h of 8.65 h. According to a study done by Sen et al.[22] in 2021, weavers worked long h, which led to cumulative fatigue and health problems. It is also concerning that all employees in this current study, lack personal protective equipment (PPE), leaving them vulnerable to airborne fibers, repetitive strain injuries, and dangerous dyes that have been linked to respiratory and dermatological issues which was also reported in a study by Sharma and Gupta in 2020.[23] The predominance of weaving (88.2%) as the primary activity is in line with conventional practices, with very few workers engaged in accessory processes like warping and dyeing.

Health problems among handloom weavers

The most common health issue (79.12%) was musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs), which primarily affected the elbow, ankle, and shoulder joints. These findings are similar to those of Das et al. (2020),[24] who found that prolonged static load, awkward postures, and repetitive body movement were the main causes of MSDs in handloom weavers. Eye problems, including diminished vision (38.8%) and eye redness (9.4%), were also common, likely due to poor illumination and constant visual strain, as reported in previous research by Mandal et al.,[25] done in 2017. Similar to findings by Prasad and Kumar (2016),[26] who found chronic bronchitis and asthma to be common conditions among textile workers, the prevalence of respiratory issues (14.8%) highlights the inhalation risks created by textile fibers.

Chronic illnesses such as diabetes mellitus (20.3%) and hypertension (12.1%) suggest a dual burden of occupational and lifestyle-related diseases. Although the prevalence of these conditions is consistent with national epidemiological trends, weavers’ poor dietary habits, sedentary postures, and work-related stress may exacerbate them as stated in a study by Reddy et al.[27] Gastrointestinal issues (13.9%) could be linked to irregular meal timings and occupational stress, while hearing loss (6.1%) may be attributed to long-term loom noise exposure.

Ergonomic conditions and workplace adaptability

The study’s mean Ergonomic Index of 29.29% indicated sub-optimal ergonomic conditions. Workstation design and adaptability (23.67%), working posture (6.30%), and general conditions (32.98%) were all noticeably poor, despite the Hand Tool Design Index being comparatively satisfactory (63.28%). These results are consistent with those of Chatterjee et al.[28] in 2018, who highlighted that musculoskeletal stress and decreased productivity are caused by poorly designed workstations. Chronic pain and exhaustion are caused by poor ergonomic conditions, which iterates the need for workplace modifications.

Workplace improvements, such as improved lighting, ergonomic workstations, and the requirement to wear personal protective equipment (PPE), could largely reduce the occupational hazards. Regular health screenings and ergonomic training programs should be implemented to prevent MSDs and chronic illnesses. Furthermore, encouraging and facilitating the older weavers to pursue alternate sources of income could reduce the long-term effects of occupational health issues.

CONCLUSION

To conclude, this study underlined the substantial health burden among handloom weavers, highlighting the need for strengthening the interventions to improve their health check-up, and PPE usage among them. This study has also enlisted the various occupational risks faced by handloom weavers and highlighted the need for immediate ergonomic and health interventions like regular health check-ups, health education on health issues, importance of PPE usage, posture correction and safety practices for injury prevention. To ensure sustainable livelihoods in the handloom industry, further research should explore the impacts of various cost-effective ergonomic intervention strategies that can boost weavers’ health outcomes and in turn their productivity.
