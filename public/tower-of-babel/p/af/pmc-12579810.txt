Enhancing dental trauma management: insights into physical education graduates’ knowledge and performance
Authors: Fahimeh Pakravan, Ali Yousefian Najafabadi, Zohreh Meshkati, Mehdi Nasr Isfahani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInjuries sustained during physical activities are a common concern among athletes, with dental trauma representing a significant yet often under-addressed component. Timely and appropriate intervention is critical to successful outcomes, making the awareness and performance of first-line responders—particularly physical education graduates—an essential focus. This study evaluates their knowledge and practices regarding emergency management of dental trauma.Materials and methodsThis cross-sectional descriptive study assessed 206 physical education graduates in Isfahan between 2024 and 2025. Data were collected using a researcher-designed questionnaire comprising 12 targeted items, validated with a content validity ratio (CVR > 0.51) and confirmed reliability (ICC = 0.884). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (Version 26), including descriptive measures (mean, standard deviation, frequency) and inferential tests (independent t-tests, ANOVA).ResultsParticipants demonstrated moderate proficiency in dental trauma awareness and self-reported practical knowledge, with an overall mean score of 9.74 ± 4.80 (scale: 0–20). Awareness and performance scores were closely aligned (9.08 ± 4.99 and 9.07 ± 5.39, respectively). Significant predictors of higher competency included academic achievement (P = 0.023), direct exposure to dental trauma (P = 0.001), engagement in high-contact sports such as martial arts (P = 0.016), and formal training in trauma management (P = 0.012). Conversely, gender, general athletic history, and school-level sports involvement were not statistically associated with performance outcomes.ConclusionMost PE graduates demonstrated limited preparedness for managing dental trauma. Academic progression, trauma exposure, and targeted training were associated with better awareness and applied knowledge. These findings support the integration of oral emergency response modules into sports education curricula and certification programs—promoting health literacy and alignment with WHO health promotion objectives.Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06806-5.Keywords: Dental trauma, Emergency management, Physical education, Awareness, Sports injuries

Introduction

Injuries related to physical activities are an inevitable aspect of an athlete’s career. Historically, dental injuries and their irreversible consequences were prevalent, especially among athletes in high-impact sports. However, the adoption of protective measures like helmets and mouthguards in professional sports has significantly reduced dental trauma incidence. Unfortunately, these interventions remain less common among amateur athletes, exposing them to increased risk [1–4].

Adolescence and young adulthood mark a phase of growing physical capacity and social sensitivity. During this time, individuals often participate in sports like martial arts and ball games, where facial impacts are more frequent. Facial aesthetics—including anterior dentition—play a pivotal role in self-confidence and peer acceptance. Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) affecting anterior teeth can disrupt not only oral functionality but also social behavior, such as smiling and communication, potentially leading to social withdrawal and diminished self-esteem [5, 6].

Despite the availability of clinical strategies for managing dental trauma, less attention has been paid to first-response capabilities among non-dental professionals, particularly physical education (PE) graduates. These individuals frequently supervise sports activities and often act as informal first responders. However, their knowledge and self-reported applied practices regarding dental trauma may be limited due to the lack of formal education in oral health emergencies. Understanding these gaps is essential for developing targeted interventions, revising educational curricula, and informing public health strategies within sports-related environments [6–10].

The success of dental trauma management relies heavily on timely response. Studies emphasize that prognosis depends on critical factors such as the duration of extra-alveolar time, storage conditions, and root surface handling [11]. Given that PE instructors and fellow athletes are often present at the time of injury, their awareness and preparedness can substantially influence clinical outcomes [12–15].

Physical education instructors and sports graduates, despite their frequent role as de facto first responders, typically receive minimal training in dental emergency protocols. Research demonstrates considerable gaps in knowledge and confidence among coaches, amateur trainers, and athletes when managing dental trauma [16–19]. Additionally, surveys reveal that more than half of elementary school students’ mothers were unaware of dental injuries sustained by their children, underscoring the broader need for community education and the role of school-based professionals in raising awareness [8]– [9, 20].

One critical component of effective dental trauma management is the selection of appropriate storage media when immediate replantation of avulsed teeth is not possible. The ideal solution in such cases is Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), commercially known as “Save-A-Tooth,” which maintains optimal pH and osmolality, supports cellular metabolism, and exhibits antibacterial properties [13–15, 21, 22]. Another widely available alternative is milk, especially pasteurized and packaged varieties, which offer physiological pH, nutrient density, and sterility [23]. While saliva and tap water are readily accessible, their enzymatic activity and low osmolality compromise PDL cell survival [22]. Egg albumin, due to its protein content and sterility, also presents a promising medium [13, 21]. Other options like coconut water, VIASPAN, propolis, aloe vera gel, and contact lens solution have been evaluated but are typically unavailable in settings like sports fields and schools [24, 25].

Given these limitations in practical access and awareness, physical education instructors must be well-informed about storage options and emergency protocols. As individuals often entrusted with athletic supervision and safety, their actions can play a crucial role in preserving dental health and minimizing long-term consequences.

This study investigates the self-reported awareness and applied knowledge of dental trauma management among physical education graduates in Isfahan. It aims to explore the influence of demographic factors, prior trauma exposure, and educational background on emergency preparedness. The findings are intended to inform future training standards, curriculum design, and policy-level decisions for enhancing response capabilities in athletic settings.

Methods

Conceptual framework

This study was anchored in the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) model, with emphasis on the knowledge and applied knowledge domains due to feasibility constraints. Although attitudes—such as perceived importance, confidence, and willingness to intervene—were not directly measured, they represent a valuable avenue for future research ([26, 27]). It is recommended that subsequent studies include attitude-oriented items to better capture the full scope of the KAP framework.

Study design and participants

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in Isfahan between 2024 and 2025 to evaluate dental trauma-related knowledge and performance among physical education graduates. Ethical approval was secured from the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.DHMT.REC.1403.055). Participants held bachelor’s, master’s, or doctorate degrees in physical education. Inclusion criteria consisted of informed consent and completion of the questionnaire, while exclusion criteria included failure to complete the instrument (defined as more than two unanswered questions) or voluntary withdrawal.

Sampling strategy and sample size

Participants were selected via convenience sampling from diverse settings, including sports clubs, referee training workshops, postgraduate programs, and in-service training courses for school instructors. This method ensured access to varied demographic and experiential backgrounds but limited generalizability. Sample size calculation was based on a 95% confidence level, 80% test power, an error level of 0.1, and a previously reported awareness rate of 6.6%, yielding a required sample of 175 participants. Ultimately, 206 eligible individuals were recruited through non-random selection.

Data collection instrument

Data collection was carried out using a structured, researcher-designed questionnaire distributed in educational and athletic environments. Participants completed the survey in writing, with the researcher present to address any queries. The questionnaire contained 12 items—Questions 1 to 8 assessed knowledge, while Questions 9 to 12 evaluated performance. Correct answers were awarded one point, incorrect answers received zero, and “don’t know” responses were unscored. Scores for each domain and the overall score were standardized on a scale of 20. After completing the questionnaire, participants received an educational pamphlet containing correct answers and guidance on dental trauma management.

Instrument validation and reliability

The questionnaire was developed through literature review and expert consultation. Content validity was evaluated by a panel of 15 dental specialists who assessed clarity, relevance, simplicity, and importance. Items with at least 80% approval were retained, resulting in a final instrument with a Content Validity Ratio (CVR) exceeding 0.51. Reliability was measured using internal consistency and test–retest methods. Fifty physical education graduates completed the questionnaire initially, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.743. The test was repeated 2 to 4 weeks later with 30 of these participants, producing an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.884, indicating strong reliability.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS Version 26. Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation, frequency, and reliability indices such as CVR, ICC, and Cronbach’s alpha. Inferential statistics—including independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA—were applied to compare quantitative variables across demographic groups. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used.

Ethical considerations

All procedures complied with ethical standards, as approved by the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.DHMT.REC.1403.055). Participants provided written informed consent, ensuring voluntary involvement. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, and no personal identifiers were disclosed. All individuals were briefed on the study’s objectives prior to participation.

Results

Overall findings and effect sizes

Statistical analysis revealed significant differences across several subgroups based on p-values. To assess the practical relevance of these findings, effect sizes were calculated. Notably, the largest effects were observed for academic attainment and dental trauma experience. The eta-squared value for education level was η² = 0.042, while Cohen’s d between participants with no trauma experience and those with three or more incidents was approximately 0.85, indicating a substantial difference in self-reported applied knowledge (Appendix 1).

To provide a more detailed visualization, Supplementary Fig. 2 stratifies dental trauma management scores by participants’ education level, prior trauma exposure, and sport history. The awareness dimension yielded a mean score of 9.06 ± 4.92. Three individuals (1.5%) selected “don’t know” for all seven awareness questions, and 12 participants (5.8%) answered all questions incorrectly. The majority (106 participants, 51.5%) scored between 1 and 10, followed by 66 participants (32%) scoring between 11 and 15, and 19 individuals (9.2%) scoring above 15. Three individuals (1.5%) achieved a perfect awareness score of 20.

In the performance dimension, the average score was 9.35 ± 5.39. Among respondents, 23 participants (11.2%) indicated a complete lack of knowledge by selecting “don’t know” for all five performance-related questions, while 16 (7.8%) answered all incorrectly. A total of 79 participants (38.3%) scored between 1 and 10, 47 (22.8%) scored between 11 and 15, and 41 participants (19.9%) scored above 15. Seven participants (3.4%) achieved the maximum score of 20.

Appendix 2 and 3 detail item-level responses. The most frequently selected “don’t know” options in the awareness section were for Questions 3 and 5 (39.3% and 44.7%, respectively), and for Questions 8 and 12 (40.3% and 43.7%) in the performance section. The highest proportion of correct answers in the awareness domain was recorded for Questions 2 and 4 (52.4% and 63.1%), while performance Questions 10 and 11 showed the highest accuracy (65.5% and 48.5%).

Relationship between scores and participant characteristics

Mean overall scores, as well as awareness and performance scores, were analyzed against participant demographics. The mean total score was 9.74 ± 4.80. Graduates with master’s and doctoral degrees scored significantly higher than those with bachelor’s degrees, suggesting that higher academic attainment contributes to improved knowledge and performance.

Martial arts participants reported higher average scores (10.57 ± 4.76 overall; 12.15 ± 6.02 performance) than those in ball sports or other disciplines, although no significant differences were observed in the awareness domain. Graduates who had managed dental trauma incidents “two times” (10.35 ± 5.37) or “three times or more” (11.83 ± 4.11) scored significantly higher in both dimensions, whereas those with no prior experience obtained the lowest scores.

Training history also played a role; participants with formal training in dental trauma scored higher overall (10.10 ± 4.99) and in performance (10.81 ± 5.21), although the difference was not significant in awareness. Furthermore, graduates knowledgeable about tooth replantation scored significantly higher overall (11.34 ± 4.89) than those without such knowledge.

No statistically significant differences in scores were observed based on gender, years of sports experience, or school sports club involvement. These findings collectively underscore the influence of education, training, and direct experience on preparedness in dental trauma management.

Discussion

Methodological considerations

While this study aimed to assess both awareness and practical competencies among physical education graduates, it is important to clarify that performance scores reflect self-reported applied knowledge, not real-time emergency behavior. The questionnaire captured perceived preparedness in dental trauma scenarios but did not verify hands-on proficiency. High scores may signal confidence rather than actual competence (Appendix 4). To enhance ecological validity, future studies should employ scenario-based assessments or simulation exercises.

Moreover, multiple subgroup comparisons were conducted across demographic and experiential variables. Though this allowed for richer analysis, it increased the risk of Type I error. Because the study was exploratory, no statistical corrections (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) were applied. Therefore, significant findings should be interpreted with caution and validated through confirmatory research with corrected thresholds.

Knowledge, exposure, and athletic context

Contrary to expectations, the study found no significant link between years of sports activity and knowledge or performance scores. This outcome aligns with prior findings from Mohandas and Chandan, and Rouhani et al. [8, 28], who reported no correlation between sports activity history and dental trauma knowledge. Conversely, Sepet et al. [29] observed higher knowledge among participants with work experience exceeding five years, particularly involving mouthguard use. Vliet et al. [30] also noted better trauma knowledge among Dutch hockey coaches with more than a decade of experience.

Study by Moeini et al. [31] further supports the relationship between experience and knowledge in contact sports like rugby, kabaddi, and hockey. These findings suggest that high-contact sports increase trauma exposure and foster improved performance, whereas this connection fades in less physically intense activities.

Participants involved in martial arts scored significantly higher than those engaged in ball sports or other disciplines. This is consistent with the findings of Vliet et al. [30, 32] who found that field hockey coaches were better informed than water polo coaches. However, Elareibi et al. [33] reported minimal differences in awareness among coaches from taekwondo, water polo, and basketball, indicating that sport type may not always predict trauma preparedness.

The study also found that repeated dental trauma exposure significantly boosted awareness and performance scores. Participants with three or more incidents demonstrated markedly higher scores than those with one or none. While Trabelsi et al. [9] documented trauma exposure rates ranging from 9 to 44% across physical education teachers, they did not establish a consistent link with performance. However, Panahi et al. [34] reported a direct correlation between trauma exposure and awareness regarding avulsion events, supporting the idea that experience enhances understanding—often through peer dialogue and independent learning.

School physical education instructors

Although no significant difference emerged between school sports instructors and other graduates, the knowledge and performance of school physical education teachers is critical due to the vulnerability of adolescents. Ages 8 to 15 are marked by high participation in contact sports and increased sensitivity toward appearance and identity formation.

Salehi et al. [16] reported that 44% of dental trauma cases occur in children aged 8–10. Esmaeil Pour et al. [35] emphasized that less experienced athletes are more prone to dental injuries. Rouhani et al. [8] observed that athletes had lower awareness than coaches, particularly within this young age group. These results highlight the need for increased trauma management capacity among school-based instructors, as they serve as the primary responders in youth athletic environments.

Impact of training and educational formats

Graduates who received prior training in dental trauma management demonstrated significantly better performance, and those with knowledge of tooth replantation scored higher in both dimensions. This emphasizes the role of targeted education—whether delivered via pamphlets, seminars, case presentations, or videos.

While Vliet et al. [30] found that instructors preferred written materials, they also emphasized that interactive formats such as peer discussions and case presentations resulted in stronger retention. Al-Asfour et al. [36] echoed this by highlighting the efficacy of Q&A sessions, visual tools, and dynamic engagement during seminars, suggesting that combining visual and interactive techniques is critical for lasting improvement.

Advancing health literacy and global frameworks

In addition to knowledge and performance, emerging frameworks in dental public health advocate for understanding oral health literacy as a multidimensional construct encompassing empowerment, access, and contextual application. Tools such as the Health Literacy in Dentistry scale (HeLD-14) and the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-5) offer validated mechanisms to explore how individuals interpret and respond to oral health information [37].

The HeLD-14, validated in diverse populations—including English-speaking adults [37, 38]—assesses domains like communication, support, and access, demonstrating high internal consistency (α = 0.92). Its concurrent validity with OHIP-5 [39–41] enhances its utility in evaluating oral health–related quality of life. Integrating such tools into future studies could offer richer insight into readiness and decision-making capacity in emergency dental trauma situations.

Aligned with the World Health Organization’s definition of health promotion [42], this study’s findings underscore the importance of health literacy as a foundation for empowering individuals. Equipping physical education graduates with the ability to evaluate, apply, and disseminate trauma management information can foster a culture of preventive care, peer education, and informed response—especially in high-visibility youth sport contexts.

Implications for curriculum and certification

The observed knowledge gaps suggest a need for curriculum reform. Integrating dental trauma management into coaching syllabi, certification standards, and undergraduate programs may enhance preparedness. National guidelines and scenario-based training modules could strengthen real-world emergency response capacity among physical education professionals.

Limitations

The questionnaire assessed awareness and performance but omitted validated oral health literacy instruments (e.g., HeLD-14, OHIP-5), limiting evaluation of decision-making and empowerment. Despite simplified language, some participants may have faced comprehension barriers. Self-reported data, incomplete follow-ups due to athletic obligations, and a two-week gap between stages may have introduced variability and recall bias. Prior training exposure varied, and the study’s regional scope may limit generalizability.

Conclusion

This study identifies critical gaps in self-reported applied knowledge of dental trauma management among physical education graduates. Stronger awareness and applied knowledge levels were associated with advanced education, contact sport involvement, prior trauma exposure, and targeted training, while other factors—such as gender and general sports engagement—showed minimal influence. The disconnect between theoretical familiarity and practical readiness emphasizes the need for observational and simulation-based research. Interactive educational methods—such as case discussions and media-driven instruction—demonstrate promising potential for improving emergency response capabilities.

By embedding evidence-informed dental trauma protocols into professional training and certification programs, educational institutions can foster confidence and competence in real-world scenarios. This strategy not only bolsters individual and community-level resilience, but also supports the WHO’s global health agenda promoting equitable oral health literacy and cost-effective prevention across sport-based communities.
