Associations of urinary iodine concentration (UIC) with Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) among non-diabetic population: analysis of the NHANES 2017–2020
Authors: Zhaoguang Chen, Jianfeng Yu, Yanbin Wang, Donglei Zhang, Jianyu Hao, Xinjuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease(MASLD) and diabetes are manifestations of metabolic syndrome. Urinary iodine concentration (UIC) is closely related to diabetes and glucose metabolism, but studies on the relationship between UIC and MASLD in non-diabetic individuals are limited. Our purpose was to explore the associations between UIC and MASLD as well as liver fibrosis in the non-diabetic population.MethodsWe analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted from 2017 to 2020. Participants were categorized into three groups based on UIC (µg/L): low (< 100), normal(100–299), and high(≥ 300). Vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) was employed to measure the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM), which serve as indicators of MASLD and liver fibrosis, respectively. Multiple logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between UIC and MASLD as well as liver fibrosis.ResultsThe study included 1281 non-diabetic participants aged ≥ 20 years. We found that high UIC was associated with a lower MASLD risk (OR = 0.57, 95%CI: 0.37–0.86, P = 0.008) compared to normal UIC. Stratified and sensitivity analyses confirmed these findings. The relationship between log2-UIC and MASLD was non-linear (P = 0.023), with a cutoff value of 84.62 µg/L. MASLD risk decreased rapidly when UIC exceeded this value. No significant association between UIC and liver fibrosis was observed.ConclusionsIn non-diabetic individuals, high UIC is associated with a lower MASLD prevalence, suggesting potential dietary interventions for MASLD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04310-2.Keywords: Urinary iodine concentration, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Non-diabetic individuals, National health and nutrition examination survey, Vibration-controlled transient elastography

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), characterized by hepatic steatosis, affects approximately 38% of the global population [1]. MASLD is closely associated with metabolic dysfunction and is widely recognized as the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome [2]. MASLD can progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, severely endangering human health. Early recognition and systematic management of modifiable risk factors are essential for the primary prevention and attenuation of MASLD.

Iodine is an essential micronutrient that regulates growth, development, and metabolism by synthesizing thyroid hormones [3]. The primary sources of iodine include seafood, eggs, and dairy products. Iodine deficiency can lead to hypothyroidism. Emerging evidence demonstrates a robust link between thyroid dysfunction and both metabolic syndrome and MASLD [4]. Studies have shown that hypothyroidism is linked to weight gain, cardiovascular risk factors, and dyslipidaemia, and significantly elevates both the incidence and mortality of cardiovascular disease [5, 6].Moreover, overt hypothyroidism, subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH), and even Low-normal thyroid function (LNTF) have all been implicated in MASLD [7, 8].Consequently, the potential link between iodine and MASLD warrants investigation.

Studies have demonstrated that iodine is primarily excreted by the kidneys [9], and urinary iodine concentration (UIC) serves as a commonly used indicator for assessing the iodine nutritional status of populations [10]. Previous studies have confirmed a close relationship between UIC and metabolic syndrome, as well as diabetes and mpaired glucose regulation [11–13]. A recent study indicated that, after adjusting for covariates, there is no significant association between UIC and NAFLD in the general population [14]. However, studies investigating the associations between UIC and MASLD in non-diabetic populations are limited.

In the current study, we examined the associations of UIC with the prevalence of MASLD and liver fibrosis in a non-diabetic population from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted from January 2017 to March 2020.

Methods

Study design and participants

The NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey that recruits participants through stratified, multi-stage, cluster probability sampling to represent the non-institutionalized civilian population of the United States. Participants in the survey underwent a series of in-depth interviews, followed by comprehensive physical examinations and a battery of laboratory tests at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). The NHANES protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study adhered to the reporting guidelines established by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) initiative.

Our research utilized data obtained from NHANES conducted from 2017 to 2020, during which eligible participants underwent hepatic vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) assessments. We selected individuals who were 20 years old or older and completed the MEC (n = 8544). Then, we first excluded individuals who did not have valid VCTE data(n = 1149) or UIC data(n = 4992). Second, we excluded individuals diagnosed diabetes (n = 510). We further excluded participants with positive hepatitis B surface antigen or anti-hepatitis C virus serology, as well as those reporting significant alcohol consumption [15](> 30 g/day for men and > 20 g/day for women)(n = 369). Finally, we excluded participants with any missing covariate data (n = 243). Ultimately, our study included 1281 individuals, and the study flowchart is depicted in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of subject inclusion. Data from the NHANES 2017-March 2020 (pre-pandemic) cycle was used

Participants were considered to have diabetes if any of the following conditions were met: (1) a self-reported diagnosis of diabetes, (2) use of anti-diabetic drugs, or (3) a Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) [16].

Definition of MASLD and measurement of liver stiffness

Experienced technicians performed hepatic VCTE examinations using the FibroScan 502 V2 Touch (Echosens, Paris, France) equipped with medium (M) or extra-large (XL) probes to assess hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter, CAP) and fibrosis (liver stiffness measurement, LSM). Consistent with prior studies, MASLD is defined as hepatic steatosis (CAP of ≥ 263 dB/m) + ≥ 1 metabolic risk factor, in the absence of any other possible causes of chronic liver disease and excessive alcohol consumption [17]. The metabolic risk factors were defined as follows: (a) BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (23 kg/m2 for Asia) or waist circumference ≥ 94/80 cm for men/women; (b) fasting serum glucose of ≥ 100 mg/dl or glycated hemoglobin ≥ 5.7% or diabetes or treatment for diabetes; (c) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or specific anti-hypertensive drug treatment; (d) plasma triglycerides (TG) ≥ 150 mg/dl or lipid-lowering treatment; and (e) plasma high-density lipoprotein (HDL) < 40 mg/dl for men and < 50 mg/dl for women or lipid-lowering treatment [18].The cutoff values for LSM were 8.2 kPa for significant fibrosis (F ≥ F2), 9.7 kPa for advanced fibrosis (F ≥ F3), and 13.6 kPa for cirrhosis (F = F4) [19]. Additionally, we defined liver fibrosis using a threshold of LSM ≥ 8 kPa [20] and analyzed LSM as a continuous variable (after log transformation) to conduct a multidimensional analysis of the association between UIC and urinary iodine.

Measurement of urinary iodine concentration

Studies have shown that nearly all iodine ingested by the human body is excreted through urine [21]. Urine samples were collected at the MEC, and UIC was measured using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) [22]. Urinary iodine (µg/L) was categorized into three groups based on previous studies: low UIC: <100 µg/L; normal UIC: 100–299 µg/L; high UIC: ≥300 µg/L [23].

Covariates

We collected self-reported data on age, gender, ethnicity, alcohol consumption in the past 12 months, ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), marital status, leisure-time physical activity (PA), education levels, smoking history, total energy intake and hypertension history. Detailed descriptions of the laboratory methods used to measure HbA1c, total cholesterol (TCH), TG, HDL, and urinary creatinine can be found elsewhere [22]. The diagnostic criteria for hepatitis B and C infections aligned with those reported in prior investigations [22]. Age was classified into three groups: 20–40, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years. Race was self-reported as Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, or other races. Education was divided into < High school, High school and Some college or above. Marital status is categorized as Married, Widowed/Divorced, and Never married. BMI is categorized as < 30.0 or ≥ 30.0. Smoking status was classified, in accordance with established literature, as current, past, or never smoker [15]. PIR was stratified into three groups: <1.0,1.0–3.0,>3.0 [24]. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or a self-reported history of hypertension diagnosed by a physician. We categorized leisure-time PA into four groups based on previous literature [25]. Data on total energy intake were estimated from each food component by NHANES analysts based on two days of 24 h recall interviews [26].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (with lower and upper quartiles) depending on the data distribution, whereas categorical variables were displayed as counts and percentages. Continuous data were compared using t-tests, while categorical data were analyzed using the χ² test. Since the percentage of missing data was minimal (ranging from 0 to 5% for any variable), no imputation method was employed. Logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95%CIs for the association between UIC and MASLD, as well as different degrees of liver fibrosis. We also employed multivariate linear regression to analyze the association between UIC and log-transformed LSM (as a continuous variable).The crude model did not adjust for any covariates. Model 1 adjusted for age (categorical variable), gender, race, urinary creatinine (continuous variable), BMI (categorical variable) and waist circumference. Model 2, based on Model 1, additionally adjusted for PIR (categorical variable), education levels, marital status, TG, TC, and HDL. Model 3 further adjusted for leisure-time PA (categorical variable), smoking status, total energy intake and hypertension based on Model 2.

We further stratified the analyses by age, gender, education levels, smoking status, leisure-time PA, BMI, marital status and hypertension. Age was categorized as 20–40, 40–60 or ≥ 60 years, gender as male or female, education levels as < High school, High school or Some college or more, smoking status as current, past or never, leisure-time PA as < 150 or ≥ 150 (min/wk) [27], BMI as < 30.0 or ≥ 30.0, hypertension as yes or no. The significance of the interactions was estimated using the p values for the production terms between UIC and the stratified factors.

The UIC underwent a log2 transformation to conform to a normal distribution. Subsequently, we employed restricted cubic splines(RCS) with three knots located at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles to investigate the nonlinear association between UIC and MASLD. We used a two-piecewise logistic regression model with smoothing to analyze the association threshold between UIC and MASLD after adjusting for the covariates in Model 3. The likelihood-ratio test and bootstrap resampling method were utilized to determine inflection points.

All analyses were performed using the statistical software packages R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software version 1.3. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of the participants according to the presence of MASLD. Among the 1281 participants, the prevalence of MASLD was 45.3% (a total of 580 participants with MASLD). The mean age of the participants was 48 years (48.0 ± 17.0), and 596 (46.5%) were male. Participants with MASLD were older, had a higher proportion of married or living with a partner, a higher BMI, larger waist circumference, and has less leisure-time physical activity and a lower level of education. They were also more likely to be male and of mexican american descent. Additionally, they exhibited higher levels of TC, TG, HbA1c, along with lower levels of HDL. However, no significant differences were observed in UIC, urinary creatinine, and the PIR. Participants with MASLD also had a higher prevalence of hypertension (37.6% vs. 25%, P < 0.001) and demonstrated higher levels of LSM and CAP.

Nonlinear relationships

We conducted additional analyses to examine the correlation between the risk of MASLD and UIC levels (log-transformed) using multivariate analysis. Restricted cubic splines (Fig. 2) indicated that the association between UIC and MASLD was nonlinear (P for non-linearity = 0.023). Additionally, as shown in Table 4, a threshold effect can be observed, with an inflection point at the log2-UIC value of 6.35 (UIC = 84.62 µg/L). When the UIC value is below the cutoff, the risk of MASLD remains almost unchanged or even increases, and when the UIC value surpasses the cutoff, the risk decreases rapidly (OR:0.733, 95%CI: 0.589 ~ 0.913, P = 0.0055).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the associations between UIC and MASLD in a non-diabetic population using the NHANES database. Our results demonstrate that individuals with higher UIC levels exhibit a significantly lower risk of developing MASLD.Sstratified analyses demonstrated that the relationship between UIC and MASLD remained robust. This finding contributes to a better understanding of the risk factors for MASLD.

Considering the potential underlying association between UIC and diabetes, we selected a non-diabetic population for our study. Previous research has examined the relationship between iodine status, diabetes, and glucose metabolism, but the results have been inconsistent. A study from South Korea indicates that higher UIC is associated with lower fasting blood glucose levels and blood pressure, independent of thyroid function and other confounding factors [28]. Another study by Chen Jingmin and colleagues found that higher UIC is associated with a lower risk of prediabetes [12]. A cross-sectional study based on NHANES suggests that participants with very high UIC (> 400 µg/L) have a significantly reduced risk of developing diabetes compared to those with normal UIC(100–299 µg/L) [13]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study in China indicates that participants with a UIC of 300 to 799 µg/L Have a lower risk of metabolic syndrome, hypertension, prediabetes, and central obesity compared to those with a UIC of 100 to 299 µg/L [29]. Collectively, the findings from the aforementioned studies indicate that higher UIC is associated with lower blood glucose levels and a reduced incidence of diabetes.In contrast, Liu et al. suggested that excessive iodine intake from drinking water may elevate the risk of hypertension and diabetes [30]. Similarly, a French study identified high iodine intake among women as a potential risk factor for diabetes [31]. These two studies indicate that excessive iodine intake may be associated with an increased risk of diabetes.These inconsistent findings may stem from disparities in ethnicity, diverse lifestyle factors among study participants, and variations in study designs.

Iodine exposure may influence the development and progression of MASLD by modulating thyroid function. Iodine is an essential component of thyroid hormones, and adequate dietary iodine intake is crucial for maintaining normal thyroid function [32]. Thyroid hormones regulate lipid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation in the liver through thyroid hormone receptors (THR) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptors [33]. A substantial body of research indicates that thyroid hormone imbalances are associated with the development of MASLD. Hypothyroidism has been shown to be an independent risk factor for MASLD [34, 35], while hyperthyroidism is associated with a reduced incidence of MASLD and decreased liver fat content [36, 37]. Recently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the thyroid hormone receptor beta (THR-β) agonist resmetirom for treating metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [38, 39]. Additionally, a phase IIb multicenter study involving men with type 2 diabetes mellitus and hepatic steatosis who were given levothyroxine showed a significant decrease in intrahepatic lipid content [40]. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that iodine may regulate the effects of thyroid hormone-mediated lipid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation.This may offer novel insights for the prevention and treatment of MASLD.

Moreover, we observed a nonlinear association between UIC and MASLD. Our study provides evidence for a potential protective relationship between UIC and MASLD. However, both iodine deficiency and excessive iodine intake can lead to thyroid dysfunction [41, 42]. Iodine-induced thyroid dysfunction is more common in individuals with underlying thyroid diseases, partly due to impaired thyroid hormone regulation in these patients [43]. Given the dual role of iodine, further studies are needed to confirm the optimal iodine status for this finding.

We found that UIC was closely associated with MASLD (diagnosed based on CAP), but not with liver fibrosis (diagnosed based on LSM). This discrepancy may be attributed to the relatively low levels of liver fibrosis among the participants, as well as the low proportion of significant fibrosis (As shown in Supplementary Table 4).

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to its cross-sectional design, we cannot establish a causal relationship between UIC and MASLD. Secondly, the possibility of correlations being influenced by unmeasured confounding factors cannot be ruled out, despite our efforts to account for numerous confounders. Thirdly, some covariates in our study relied on self-reporting, introducing the potential for interpretation bias or recall issues. Finally, our study used a single-point urine measurement to assess the iodine status of the population, which may be influenced by recent fluid intake [44]. However, we adjusted for urine creatinine levels to control for the effects of fluid intake as much as possible [45].

Conclusions

In summary, our study is the first to demonstrate that high UIC is associated with a lower incidence of MASLD in a non-diabetic population. These findings highlight the importance of optimal iodine intake. Additionally, there is a need to enhance the monitoring of iodine intake in the population. Our study findings may inform future dietary recommendations, pending longitudinal validation.
