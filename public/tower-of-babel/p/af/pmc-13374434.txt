Short-term oral antibiotic discontinuation and its predictors among outpatients of public hospitals in Eastern Ethiopia: a prospective follow-up study
Authors: Tilahun Tadesse Demeke, Tigist Gashaw, Tewodros Assefa, Ammas Siraj Mohammed, Abera Jambo, Dumessa Edessa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of discontinuation of short-term oral antibiotic therapy and to identify its predictors among patients attending outpatient department pharmacy units of public hospitals in Harar, Eastern Ethiopia.DesignFacility-based prospective follow-up study.SettingAll public hospitals in Harar, Eastern Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 422 patients who received short-term oral antibiotics from Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HFCSH) and Jugal General Hospital (JGH) from 20 January to 20 April 2024 were recruited using a systematic random sampling technique. Of the participants enrolled during the initial recruitment, only 400 completed the follow-up interview and were therefore included in the final analysis. The mean age of the respondents was 35.89±13.21 years and 280 (70.0%) were female.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was discontinuation of short-term oral antibiotic therapy, measured based on self-reported leftover antibiotics. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with discontinuation. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of short-term oral antibiotic discontinuation was 35.3% (95% CI 31% to 40%). Factors independently associated with antibiotic discontinuation included larger family size (AOR=1.18; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.36), a negative attitude towards antibiotics (AOR=2.09; 95% CI 1.19 to 3.66), dissatisfaction with medication-related information (AOR=7.20; 95% CI 2.84 to 18.24), prescriptions for dental indications (AOR=7.50; 95% CI 2.21 to 25.50), treatment duration of ≥7 days (AOR=2.88; 95% CI 1.08 to 7.68) and interruption of therapy during the treatment course (AOR=4.06; 95% CI 2.14 to 7.33).ConclusionsShort-term oral antibiotic discontinuation remains a substantial challenge among outpatients, highlighting important gaps in adherence and continuity of care. Patient perceptions, the adequacy of medication counselling and treatment-related characteristics appear to play a critical role in shaping adherence behaviour. Targeted interventions to improve patient education, communication and follow-up support are essential to promote appropriate antibiotic use and help reduce the risk of antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Antibiotics, a class of antimicrobials, are essential for treating bacterial infections and reducing mortality.13 However, their effectiveness is threatened by antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which arises from improper use, including both underuse, such as treatment discontinuation and overuse.4 5 Premature discontinuation can promote resistance in individuals,6 contribute to household storing and self-medication, thereby increasing community-level AMR.7 Poor adherence also leads to adverse patient outcomes and increased healthcare burdens.3

Globally, AMR caused approximately 4.71 million deaths in 2021,8 disproportionately impacting low-income countries, particularly Sub-Saharan Africa, which reported 255 000 AMR-related deaths in 2019.9 Due to the need for complete pathogen eradication, adherence to prescribed antibiotic treatment regimens is critical.10 Despite its clinical importance in reducing AMR, premature discontinuation of antibiotic therapy remains a major global challenge, contributing to poor clinical outcomes and the emergence of AMR.6 10

Globally, non-adherence to short-term antibiotic therapy is highly prevalent. Studies reported discontinuation rates ranging from 30% to 50%, with considerable variation across populations and healthcare settings.1113 Factors such as inadequate information regarding prescribed antibiotics, longer treatment duration, a higher number of doses per regimen and the need to take medications more than once daily were reported to be significantly associated with antibiotic non-adherence. Additionally, symptom improvement and forgetfulness were identified as the most commonly reported reasons for poor adherence to antibiotic therapy.1214

In low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia, the magnitude of discontinuation is likely higher due to healthcare system limitations, inadequate patient education and widespread misconceptions about antibiotic use.1416 Although exact pooled discontinuation rates vary, available African studies show moderate-to-high discontinuation rates.17 18 In Ethiopia, improper antibiotic use and resistance are alarming issues,19 where more than one-third of patients receiving antibiotics interrupt their treatment.1922 Studies conducted in the study area have revealed 33.6% of antibiotic treatment discontinuation22 and a high (59.7%) prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria.23 The continued misuse of existing antibiotics undermines the potential benefits of newly developed ones.24

Although several previous studies have assessed antibiotic discontinuation rates and their predictors, most relied on participants’ past experiences over extended recall periods, which may have introduced recall bias. Additionally, previously conducted Ethiopian studies were community-based, and none attempted to examine the magnitude of the problem through prospective follow-up.162022 To our knowledge, this is the first prospective follow-up study in Ethiopia assessing short-term oral antibiotic discontinuation among outpatients. Furthermore, factors associated with antibiotic discontinuation remain understudied in Eastern Ethiopia. Therefore, to address these evidence gaps in the existing literature, the present study was a facility-based study that prospectively followed patients until completion of their therapy to determine the magnitude of short-term oral antibiotic discontinuation and its predictors among outpatients attending public hospitals in Harar, Eastern Ethiopia.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A facility-based prospective follow-up study was conducted from 20 January to 20 April 2024, among patients who received short-term (≤10 days) oral antibiotics from outpatient department pharmacy units of Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HFCSH) and Jugal General Hospital (JGH) in Harar city, Eastern Ethiopia. Harar is the capital city of Harari Regional State, and it is 532 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of the country. Harari Regional State is one of the fourteen regional states in Ethiopia, and it has a total estimated population of approximately 270 000. The region has two public hospitals (HFCSH and JGH). HFCSH is a comprehensive hospital that has a catchment population of more than 5 million and serves as a university teaching hospital. JGH is a general hospital that provides inpatient, outpatient and emergency services.

Participants and eligibility criteria

All patients who received short-term oral antibiotics for ≤10 days from outpatient pharmacy units of HFCSH and JGH during the data collection period were included, and those who had difficulty hearing or speaking the local languages, as well as those who were unable to provide a means of reporting medication consumption for follow-up phone interview, were excluded from the study.

Sampling technique and sample size

The minimum sample size required for this study was determined using the single population proportion formula for cross-sectional studies, assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. Previous Ethiopian studies estimated non-adherence rates using population-level data and were not conducted at the facility level. In contrast, the Nigerian study23 was facility-based and conducted in outpatient clinics, closely matching the setting of our study. Therefore, a prevalence of discontinuation (51%) reported in a study from Nigeria was used to estimate the required number of participants. Based on these assumptions, the sample size was calculated as follows:

n= Zα22P(1−P)d2 = (1.96)2(0.51)(0.49)(0.05)2 = 384≈ 384

Adding 10% or 38 participants for the non-respondent rate23 yields a final sample size of 422.

All public hospitals in Harar were included in the study. HFCSH accounted for 67.2% of the estimated total patient flow during the data collection period, whereas JGH accounted for 32.8%. Considering the proportion of client flow between HFCSH and JGH (2:1), a total of 284 and 138 patients were selected from HFCSH and JGH, respectively, using a systematic random sampling technique (figure 1). The sampling interval for each hospital was calculated separately using the formula K=N/n, and every Kth patient was included after selection of the first participant using the lottery method.

Data collection instruments, clinical data and quality control

One trained pharmacist per hospital collected data using interviewer-administered questionnaires adopted from previous studies,20 23 25 26 and further developed by the research team (online supplemental table 3). Data collectors were trained on the objective of the study, informed consent, data collection tools, data collection and handling methods and also to collect data while keeping the identity of the study participants anonymous. The principal investigator and a supervisor strictly monitored the entire data collection process. The questionnaire was originally prepared in English, translated into the local languages and subsequently back-translated into English to ensure consistency and accuracy. In addition, a pretest was conducted on 5% of participants from a population different from the actual study population to assess the clarity and applicability of the questionnaire. The questionnaire gathered data on socio-demographics, drug treatment, disease variables, knowledge and attitude towards antibiotics, satisfaction with healthcare provider consultation and reasons for antibiotic discontinuation. Eight knowledge and seven attitude questions (online supplemental table 1) were used to assess respondents’ knowledge and attitudes. Those who scored above the mean score for knowledge or attitude items were considered to have good knowledge and a positive attitude towards antibiotic use.20 23 Satisfaction with healthcare service (online supplemental table 2), was rated on a 5-point scale and later dichotomised to satisfied/dissatisfied.25

Data collection occurred in two phases. Initially, outpatients receiving new short-term oral antibiotics at two hospital pharmacies were interviewed face-to-face at the exit to collect socio-demographic and contact information, knowledge/attitude, treatment details and satisfaction with the healthcare service. Antibiotic prescription details, including name of the antibiotic, strength, frequency of administration, duration of therapy (DOT) and number of pills actually dispensed, were also collected during this initial interview (online supplemental table 3). This prescription data were used to estimate the expected treatment completion date. Participants were not informed about future assessment of completion to avoid bias. But they were informed about future phone calls for additional information and further clarification. The second phase of data collection involved post-treatment phone interviews using open-ended questions (online supplemental table 4), to assess the start time, any new medications and duration of antibiotic use, amount consumed and leftover quantity, comparing this to the estimated completion date. Participants who were unreachable on the first call were called three additional times on different days. To identify the source of leftover antibiotics, participants were asked to distinguish between if they forgot to take their antibiotics during therapy or if they completely stopped taking the ongoing treatment. Those who reported interruption during therapy as a source of leftover pills received additional calls for further clarification of completion. Following careful review of the telephone follow-up responses, participant responses were categorised into the most appropriate classification regarding consumption of the prescribed antibiotics based on the proportion of medication consumed and the total DOT completed by the patient (online supplemental table 5). Discontinuation was defined as missing more than one dose without resuming.27 28 Furthermore, participants who stopped treatment prematurely were asked to disclose their specific reasons for discontinuation, and their responses were categorised into a precoded list of possible options (online supplemental table 6) reported in previous studies of a similar type.26 Data quality was ensured through interviewer checks after each interview, daily supervisor reviews and checks for completeness and consistency during data analysis.

Outcome variables

The dependent variable of this study was short-term oral antibiotic course discontinuation.

Independent variables

The independent variables are divided into five categories, including sociodemographic variables, patient-related variables, clinical and drug-related variables, healthcare professional and healthcare system-related variables.

Data processing and analysis

Following data collection, the responses were coded and entered into Epi-Info V.17 data entry software before being exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences V.26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data for the total sample as counts (percentage) for categorical data and as mean±SD for continuous data. Bivariate and multivariable analyses were conducted to determine factors associated with antibiotic course discontinuation, using binary logistic regression. Variables with p<0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the final model of multivariable analysis. The model of the goodness of fit was tested by the Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic, and it was found to be insignificant (p>0.05). Correlation between independent variables was analysed by a multicollinearity test using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and no variables were observed with a VIF of >5. The OR with the 95% CI was calculated to measure the strength of the association between the outcome and independent variables. Variables with a p<0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Result

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

Of the 422 participants approached during the data collection period, 400 successfully completed the telephone follow-up interview and provided information regarding their treatment completion status, resulting in a response rate of 94.8%. Consequently, the final analysis was based on a complete-case analysis of these 400 participants. The mean age of the participants was 35.89±13.21 years. Females accounted for nearly two-thirds, 280 (70.0%) of the respondents, while 268 (67.0%) were residents of urban areas. Among the respondents, 217 (54.3%) were married and the mean family size was 4.06±2.03 members. Regarding educational status, 120 (30.0%) participants had completed secondary education, whereas 115 (28.8%) were employed. In terms of satisfaction with healthcare information services, 328 (82.0%) participants reported satisfaction with the medical consultation provided and 324 (81.0%) were satisfied with the pharmacy counselling service they received. Furthermore, 269 (67.3%) participants demonstrated good knowledge and 265 (66.3%) showed a positive attitude towards antibiotic use (table 1).

Prescribed antibiotics and their indication

A total of 482 antibiotics from 10 different types were prescribed. Most of the participants, 322 (80.5%), received only one type of antibiotic at a time and only 78 (19.5%) of them received two different types of antibiotics. The maximum DOT was 10 days and the minimum was 3 days. Almost all antibiotics were dispensed in tablet or capsule dosage forms. Liquid (syrup) dosage form was encountered in only two cases. Regarding the types of antibiotics received, amoxicillin was the most commonly received antibiotic in 175 (43.8%) of participants, followed by ciprofloxacin 134 (33.5%) and metronidazole 71 (17.8%), respectively. Tinidazole was the least prescribed antibiotic with only 3 (0.75%) encounters (table 3).

Prevalence of discontinuation of short-term oral antibiotics

Of the 400 participants in this study who reported their medication consumption via phone, despite variations in how long it took them to begin, the majority, 380 (95%), started their first dose on the intended date, and 9 participants (2.30%) never initiated their treatment at all. A significant portion of participants, 141 (35.3%; 95% CI 31% to 40%), reported discontinuing their treatment regimen and having leftover pills at home, whereas 100 participants (25% of the total 400) specifically mentioned interrupting their therapy at some point (table 4).

Factors associated with discontinuation

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, family size, indication, DOT, attitude towards the use of antibiotics, satisfaction with drug information and interruption during the course of therapy were factors significantly associated with short-term oral antibiotic discontinuation.

Patients prescribed antibiotics for 7 to 10 days were 2.88 times more likely to stop treatment early compared with those on regimens less than 7 days (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.88; 95% CI 1.08 to 7.68). Participants with a negative attitude towards antibiotic use were two times more likely to discontinue their medication compared with those with a positive attitude (AOR=2.09; 95% CI 1.19 to 3.66). Respondents who reported dissatisfaction with drug information were seven times more likely to discontinue treatment (AOR=7.00; 95% CI 2.84 to 18.24). Participants who received antibiotics for OPI or dental infection were 7.5 times more likely to stop their regimen prematurely compared with those treated for RTI (AOR=7.50; 95% CI 2.21 to 25.50). Patients who experienced interruptions during their course of therapy had a fourfold higher likelihood of discontinuing their regimen compared with those with no interruptions (AOR=4.06; 95% CI 2.14 to 7.73). The odds of discontinuation also increased by 1.18 times for every one-unit increase in family size (AOR=1.18; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.36) (table 6).

Discussion

This study, conducted in public hospital outpatient pharmacies in Harar, Eastern Ethiopia, assessed the prevalence and determinants of early discontinuation of short-term oral antibiotic therapy. Discontinuation was determined based on patients’ self-reported presence of leftover pills during follow-up telephone interviews conducted after the expected completion date of therapy. The findings showed that more than one-third of participants did not complete their prescribed antibiotic courses. Multiple patient, treatment and condition-related factors independently predicted premature discontinuation, including family size, negative attitudes towards antibiotic use, dissatisfaction with drug-related information, indication for antibiotic therapy, duration of treatment and treatment interruption during therapy.

A key contribution of this study to the international literature is its comprehensive assessment of interrelated behavioural, clinical and health-system factors influencing antibiotic discontinuation in a real-world outpatient setting. In particular, it highlights the combined influence of patient perceptions, regimen complexity and treatment context on adherence behaviour. Unlike many previous studies that focused on specific diseases or limited predictors, this study provides broader evidence across multiple clinical indications and age groups, thereby offering a more complete understanding of antibiotic use behaviour in the outpatient setting.

The overall prevalence of discontinuation (35.3%) was higher than reports from Malawi 20%,29 India 17.4%3 and Northeast Ethiopia 27.5%.30 This discrepancy may be explained by variations in study populations and methodologies. Unlike several earlier studies restricted to paediatric populations,3 29 the present study included participants from all age groups. This is corroborated by a study in France, which showed that medication non-adherence varies with age.28 Furthermore, while the Malawian study focused on a single condition (fast-breathing pneumonia),29 the present study included a wide range of infectious conditions. Antibiotic adherence is known to vary depending on clinical indication.31 Lastly, the study in northeast Ethiopia was conducted at the community level and evaluated past antibiotic experiences of the subjects,30 whereas this facility-based study assessed current antibiotic use, improving data reliability. Conversely, our study’s discontinuation rate was comparatively lower than those documented in Nigeria, 51%,23 and Gondar, Ethiopia, 67.9%,21 these differences could be due to variations in study design and setting. Although both the current and previous studies used self-report to measure the rate of discontinuation, the previous studies often inquired about antibiotic experiences over longer recall periods—past year or past 6 months, which inherently increases the potential for bias.21 23 The Nigerian study was also confined to the adult population. This is in agreement with other studies that showed the impact of age group on medication compliance.12 28

One of the most important and novel findings of this study is the strong association between treatment interruption during therapy and premature discontinuation of antibiotics. Participants who experienced interruption were approximately four times more likely to discontinue treatment entirely. Although limited direct evidence exists in this context, plausible explanations include reduced perceived effectiveness of antibiotics following missed doses, delayed symptom improvement and decreased patient confidence in therapy. These behavioural responses may subsequently lead to intentional discontinuation.29 Notably, treatment interruption was more frequent among participants on higher dosing frequencies and longer treatment durations. Almost all participants who experienced interruption (99%) were on treatment for at least 7 days, and a substantial proportion (79%) were prescribed antibiotics three to four times daily. This suggests that regimen complexity plays a critical role in adherence behaviour. The finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that higher dosing frequency and longer treatment duration are associated with increased discontinuation rates.3 12 32 These results highlight the importance of simplifying antibiotic regimens whenever clinically feasible.

Another notable finding was the significant association between increasing family size and antibiotic discontinuation. Each unit increase in family size was associated with a 1.18-fold increase in the odds of discontinuation. While limited direct evidence exists, a negative association between cohabitation and adherence has been reported in Italy.33 Possible explanations include increased socioeconomic burden, food insecurity, reduced healthcare access and competing household responsibilities, all of which may adversely affect medication adherence.27 34 Furthermore, larger family sizes may increase the likelihood of interpersonal conflict, household instability and divergence of opinion, which can contribute to conflicting health information, forgetfulness and low numeracy, further increasing the risk of non-adherence.35 36

Negative attitude towards the use of antibiotics and dissatisfaction with drug-related information provided by the pharmacists were also associated with twofold and seven times increased odds of discontinuation, respectively. This finding was in line with studies conducted in Nigeria and Ethiopia.20 23 Clear and patient-centred medication counselling improves understanding, attitude, confidence and adherence, highlighting the important role of pharmacists and the need to strengthen counselling services.262835 Longer treatment duration and dental-related indications were additional significant predictors of antibiotic discontinuation. Patients prescribed antibiotics for seven to ten days were substantially more likely to discontinue therapy compared with those receiving shorter treatment courses. Likewise, participants treated for dental conditions demonstrated markedly higher odds of discontinuation. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Europe.33 37 38 One possible explanation is the common use of combination antibiotic therapy for dental infections, particularly regimens involving amoxicillin and metronidazole, which may increase regimen complexity and medication burden. Such complexity may contribute to confusion, forgetfulness, intolerance and poor understanding of treatment instructions. In addition to this, prolonged treatment duration increases the possibility of discontinuation due to early symptom improvement, travel, fasting practices or lifestyle-related interruptions.

The findings of this study have important implications for clinical practice, antimicrobial stewardship and public health policy. Strengthening pharmacist-led counselling services may improve patient understanding of antibiotic therapy and enhance satisfaction with medication-related information. Simplification of antibiotic regimens, where clinically feasible, may reduce treatment interruption and improve adherence. In addition, patients receiving longer treatment courses, more complex regimens or living in larger households may benefit from targeted adherence counselling and closer follow-up. Integrating adherence monitoring into routine outpatient pharmacy services may further improve treatment outcomes and support global efforts to combat AMR.

This study included participants from multiple public hospitals and covered a broad spectrum of infectious conditions across all age groups, thereby enhancing the external validity and generalisability of the findings to outpatient populations receiving short-term oral antibiotics in similar healthcare settings. Nevertheless, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to inpatient settings, private healthcare institutions or healthcare systems with substantially different infrastructures and patient characteristics.

This study has several limitations. Reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall bias and response inaccuracies. Additionally, the study did not assess the effect of medication cost or affordability on antibiotic discontinuation. Adherence to the timing and schedule of antibiotic administration, which may contribute to antibiotic resistance, was also not evaluated. Further research is warranted to address these limitations.
