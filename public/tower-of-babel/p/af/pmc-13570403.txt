Effects of Various Immunoglobulin Products on Serum (1‐3)‐β‐D‐glucan Levels
Authors: Hidekatsu Yae, Mitsutaka Nishimoto, Hiroshi Okamura, Masatomo Kuno, Yosuke Makuuchi, Hideo Miyagawa, Yasuhiro Nakashima, Mika Nakamae, Masayuki Hino, Hirohisa Nakamae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground(1‐3)‐β‐D‐glucan (β‐D‐glucan), a component of fungal cell walls and an important diagnostic biomarker of invasive fungal infections, is affected by various factors, particularly in allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo‐HCT) recipients. Patients receiving allo‐HCT also receive immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT); however, little is known about the effects of different types of immunoglobulin products on serum β‐D‐glucans. Here, we examined the levels of serum β‐D‐glucan following IgRT, as well as the correlation between changes in serum β‐D‐glucan and IgG levels.MethodsSerum β‐D‐glucan levels in patients who underwent allo‐HCT and IgRT at our institution between 2021 and 2024 were analyzed retrospectively. Patients who had their serum β‐D‐glucan measured within 7 days before and after IgRT were included, and changes in serum β‐D‐glucan levels were evaluated across product types. Finally, the magnitude of these changes was assessed using effect sizes.ResultsA total of 125 administrations of three types of immunoglobulin product (Privegen [n = 63], Venoglobulin [n = 38], and Venilon‐I [n = 24]) were examined. The median dose of immunoglobulin was 538.9 (57.4–700.9) mg/kg. We noted a significant increase in serum β‐D‐glucan levels after administration of Privigen, with a large effect size (Cohen's dz = 0.92; 95% confidence interval 0.48–1.35, p < 0.01). No meaningful changes were detected with Venoglobulin or Venilon‐I. Elevated serum β‐D‐glucan levels returned to baseline within approximately 3 weeks of Privigen administration. Multivariable analysis indicated that Privigen was an independent factor associated with higher serum β‐D‐glucan levels.ConclusionDifferent immunoglobulin products have different effects on serum β‐D‐glucan levels after IgRT.

Introduction

(1‐3)‐β‐D‐glucan (β‐D‐glucan) is a polysaccharide component of fungal cell walls, and is a useful diagnostic biomarker for invasive fungal infections [1], although elevated levels of serum β‐D‐glucan can be associated with conditions other than fungal infections. For example, hemodialysis [2, 3] and hemolysis [2] affect serum β‐D‐glucan levels, as well as severe mucositis associated with intestinal graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo‐HCT), although evidence for the latter remains inconclusive [2, 4]. In addition, administration of blood‐derived products such as albumin or immunoglobulin can affect serum β‐D‐glucan levels [2, 3, 5, 6]. Indeed, an increase in serum β‐D‐glucan levels after immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT) has been reported. However, data on the relationship between serum β‐D‐glucan levels in allo‐HCT recipients and the type of immunoglobulin product used are limited. Here, we conducted a single‐center retrospective study to investigate the effects of different immunoglobulin products on serum β‐D‐glucan levels, as well as the correlation between changes in serum β‐D‐glucan and IgG levels.

Patients and Methods

Study Designs and Patient Selection

This retrospective study was conducted at Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital, Osaka, Japan. Patients who underwent IgRT for hypogammaglobulinemia following allo‐HCT between May 2021 and May 2024 were included, provided that serum β‐D‐glucan levels were measured within 7 days before and after immunoglobulin administration. Patients with clinically diagnosed fungal infections at the time of immunoglobulin administration or measurement of serum β‐D‐glucan were excluded. Based on a prior literature reporting that serum β‐D‐glucan levels generally return to baseline within approximately 3 weeks after immunoglobulin administration [5], cases with repeat administration within 21 days were excluded to avoid potential carryover effects.

The study protocol was approved by the appropriate institutional review boards and ethics committees of Osaka Metropolitan University Graduate School of Medicine and was conducted in accordance with the principles set out in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided broad informed consent.

Types of Immunoglobulin Product

The immunoglobulin products tested included a pH 4‐treated acidic preparation, Privigen (CSL Behring, King of Prussia, PA, USA); a polyethylene glycol‐treated preparation, Venoglobulin (Japan Blood Products Organization, Tokyo, Japan); and a dried sulfonated preparation, Venilon‐I (KM Biologics CO., Ltd., Kumamoto, Japan). All products were administered intravenously. The formulation type was selected at the discretion of the attending physician, without standardized institutional criteria.

Measurement of Serum β‐D‐Glucan and IgG

In principle, serum β‐D‐glucan and IgG levels in allo‐HCT recipients are measured weekly to monitor fungal infections and hypogammaglobulinemia, respectively. Serum β‐D‐glucan levels were measured using the Fungitec G Test MK “Nissui” (Nissui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), a kinetic colorimetric assay based on a chromogenic substrate method. The cutoff value was 20 pg/mL, and results below the detection limit of 5 pg/mL were recorded as 5 pg/mL. Serum IgG levels were measured using a turbidimetric immunoassay (Auto Kit IgG. N; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). The measurable range is 0.05–7.5 g/L. Serum β‐D‐glucan and IgG levels measured at 3 days (± 3 days) before, and 4, 11, and 18 days (± 3 days) after immunoglobulin administration were collected retrospectively from medical records.

Definitions

The conditioning regimens were classified as myeloablative or reduced intensity according to a previous consensus report [7]. The refined disease risk index [8], and the diagnosis and severity of GVHD [9, 10], were assessed according to standard criteria. The relationship between immunoglobulin administration and various factors, including the use of blood products and the presence of hemodialysis, was analyzed. The use of blood products and hemodialysis was defined as blood product transfusion and hemodialysis, respectively, occurring within 7 days before or after immunoglobulin administration.

Statistical Analysis

Changes in serum β‐D‐glucan levels before and at 4 days (± 3 days) after immunoglobulin administration were analyzed for each product using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Temporal trends in serum β‐D‐glucan levels were analyzed using the Friedman test at baseline, and at 4, 11, and 18 days (± 3 days) after immunoglobulin administration. To account for the independence of serum β‐D‐glucan measurements, only the initial administration in each patient was analyzed in comparison of serum β‐D‐glucan levels before and after immunoglobulin administration. The effect size of the impact of immunoglobulin administration on serum β‐D‐glucan levels for each product was calculated using Cohen's dz, defined as the mean of the paired differences divided by the standard deviation of the paired differences [11, 12]. To account for repeated administrations in the same patients, linear mixed‐effects models with patient ID as a random intercept were used to analyze factors affecting the change in serum β‐D‐glucan levels (Δβ‐D‐glucan) during the 7‐day period before and after administration of each product. Variables included the type of immunoglobulin product, use of blood products, hemodialysis, occurrence of GVHD, time since allo‐HCT, and the graft source. The correlation between changes in serum IgG levels (ΔIgG) and Δβ‐D‐glucan after immunoglobulin administration was analyzed using linear regression analysis. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses and the generation of graphs were performed using GraphPad Prism (ver. 8.2.0, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, US) and EZR (ver. 1.37, Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) [13].

Results

Patient Characteristics and Immunoglobulin Administration

A total of 74 allo‐HCT recipients, accounting for a total of 146 IgRT, were eligible for inclusion in the study. Of these, 7 were excluded due to active fungal infections during the study period, and 14 were excluded due to repeat administration within 21 days of a previous immunoglobulin infusion. Therefore, data from 125 administrations of 67 patients were included in the analysis. Immunoglobulin was administered once to 34 patients, twice to 17 patients, and three or more times to 13 patients. Changes in Serum β‐D‐glucan Levels Associated With each Product

Δβ‐D‐glucans after the first administration of immunoglobulin product in each patient (31 for Privigen, 20 for Venoglobulin, and 16 for Venilon‐I) were measured and compared with baseline levels (Figure 1). We noted a significant increase in Δβ‐D‐glucan (11.8 pg/mL, 95% confidence interval [CI] 8.8–17.4) only after administration of Privigen (Figure 1a), along with a strong effect size (Cohen's dz = 0.92; 95% CI 0.48–1.35, p < 0.01). The median Δβ‐D‐glucan for Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I were 0.0 pg/mL (95% CI −4.3 to 2.05) and 0.15 pg/mL (95% CI−0.65 to 2.85), respectively. In the analysis of 125 administrations using a linear mixed‐effects model, administration of Privigen was also significantly associated with an increase in Δβ‐D‐glucan (p < 0.01) and no significant changes were observed with Venoglobulin (p = 0.64) and Venilon‐I (p = 0.83) (Figure S1). When we compared serum β‐D‐glucan levels for up to 21 days post‐administration, the highest levels in the Privigen group were observed within 1 week post‐administration, followed by a gradual decrease to levels almost equivalent to baseline by Week 3 (Figure 1b). By contrast, we noted no significant changes within 3 weeks of administration of the other two products. Among the 18 cases showing increases in Δβ‐D‐glucan of > 20 pg/mL (all receiving Privigen), 2 had fever and received preemptive enhanced antifungal therapy. No definite fungal infection was identified in either case.

Correlation between ΔIgG and Δβ‐D‐Glucan

There was a statistically significant positive correlation between the magnitude of ΔIgG and that of Δβ‐D‐glucan following administration of Privigen (r = 0.40, p < 0.01; Figure 2). For the other two products, serum β‐D‐glucan levels remained largely unchanged, irrespective of the degree of change in serum IgG levels.

Discussion

The data presented herein demonstrate product‐dependent differences in serum β‐D‐glucan levels following IgRT. Privigen alone was associated with elevated serum β‐D‐glucan levels post‐administration, whereas Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I had little or no effect. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to demonstrate the differential effects of immunoglobulin products on serum β‐D‐glucan levels in recipients of allo‐HCT.

Previous reports describe increases in serum β‐D‐glucan levels after IgRT. A study of 241 patients with hematologic malignancies identified IgRT as a statistically significant factor for false‐positive β‐D‐glucan (eight cases among 22 patients receiving IgRT, including Privigen); however, how many of the patients received a particular product used was not reported [3]. Other studies also report increases in serum β‐D‐glucan in almost all patients after IgRT; however, these studies did not address differences between products specifically [5, 6]. Herein, we found that serum β‐D‐glucan levels fluctuated only slightly after administration of Venoglobulin or Venilon‐I, and that Privigen was the only product that had a significant effect.

β‐D‐glucan is a limulus amebocyte lysate‐reactive material, the serum levels of which increase after immunoglobulin administration, possibly due to its inclusion in immunoglobulin products [14]. β‐D‐glucan is released from the cellulose membranes within the deep filters used to classify blood during the manufacture of immunoglobulin products [15]. Studies focusing on the amount of β‐D‐glucan in immunoglobulin products show that Privigen contains high levels, and although the comparison was based on a small sample size, differences among lots were negligible [5, 6]. The amount of β‐D‐glucan in a product increases serum β‐D‐glucan directly post‐administration. We found that Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I, which contain low levels of β‐D‐glucan, had a minimal effect on serum β‐D‐glucan levels. In recent years, methods such as deep filter flash filtration, which removes β‐D‐glucan efficiently, have been developed [16], and measures are being implemented to minimize the impact of immunoglobulin products on serum β‐D‐glucan levels. Wider use of these immunoglobulin products means that concerns regarding serum β‐D‐glucan following immunoglobulin administration may be mitigated. Increased serum β‐D‐glucan levels can be particularly problematic for immunocompromised patients eligible for IgRT. This is because it can be difficult to interpret elevations in serum β‐D‐glucan levels in the absence of specific symptoms or clinical evidence of fungal infection, leading to frequent, and in many cases unnecessary, empiric antifungal therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study, and only three types of products could be analyzed. Given the relatively small sample sizes, especially in the Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I groups, the study may have been underpowered to detect modest changes in β‐D‐glucan levels. Therefore, a lack of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution. Second, it was not possible to examine β‐D‐glucan levels in the products. The results would have been more reliable if the β‐D‐glucan content of the products had been confirmed. Third, antifungal prophylaxis, which can substantially affect the β‐D‐glucan levels, is a potential confounding factor when evaluating increases in β‐D‐glucan after immunoglobulin administration. However, in the present study, all but two patients received antifungal prophylaxis prior to immunoglobulin administration, and no patients initiated or discontinued antifungal prophylaxis during the evaluation period. Therefore, the effect of antifungal prophylaxis was unlikely to have materially influenced the comparison of β‐D‐glucan levels before and after immunoglobulin administration.

In conclusion, this study reveals significant differences in the effects of an immunoglobulin product on serum β‐D‐glucan levels after IgRT. This is possibly due to the presence of β‐D‐glucan in the products themselves. Privigen, in particular, was associated with elevated serum β‐D‐glucan levels after administration, whereas Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I had minimal impact. As Privigen is widely used internationally, these findings are likely to be applicable beyond Japan. In contrast, Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I are primarily used in Japan, and extrapolation of these results to other immunoglobulin products should be performed with caution. Nevertheless, because β‐D‐glucan contamination is influenced by manufacturing processes, similar variability may also exist among other immunoglobulin preparations. Clinically, physicians should recognize that serum β‐D‐glucan responses to an immunoglobulin product may differ depending on the product type. While formulations such as Privigen are associated with elevated serum β‐D‐glucan levels, which may lead to false‐positive results in tests for invasive fungal infections, others (e.g., Venoglobulin and Venilon‐I) have little impact. Awareness of these differences is essential for appropriate interpretation. Although the present study did not directly assess diagnostic performance, future studies should investigate whether time‐ or product‐specific interpretive adjustments could improve the accuracy of fungal infection diagnostics.
