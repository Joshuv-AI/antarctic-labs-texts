Using machine learning models to predict the risk of mild cognitive impairment for COPD patients: A multi-cohort study
Authors: Xiang Tang, Shuyuan Li, Hongjiang Liu, Yong Ge, Deju Zhou, Teng Sun, Cheng Zhang, Shoujie Feng, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe evaluation of cognitive function in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is critical. This study aimed to construct a risk prediction model capable of predicting the rsik of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in COPD patients using machine learning methods.MethodsThis study enrolled 2142 COPD patients from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), analyzing 16 sociodemographic, health, and psychological indicators. The predictors screened by LASSO regression were integrated with five machine learning models—Logistic Regression, Neural Network, XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest —to identify the optimal model for predicting MCI risk in COPD patients. External validation was conducted using the Health and Retirement Study dataset (HRS). Model performance underwent comprehensive evaluation across three critical dimensions: discrimination capacity (AUC-ROC), calibration accuracy (Brier score and calibration curves), and clinical applicability (decision curve analysis). Additionally, the SHAP method was employed to elucidate the feature contributions to the final model.ResultsThe analysis included 2142 COPD patients, of whom 420 were diagnosed with MCI. 11 predictive variables were selected to construct five machine learning algorithms. Among these, the LightGBM model demonstrated superior performance across all evaluation dimensions - including discrimination capacity, calibration accuracy, and clinical utility - achieving AUROC values ranging from 0.722 to 0.782.The eight most significant features for predicting MCI in COPD patients were sex, residence, instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) score, educational level, social activity engagement, marital status, Internet use and depression.ConclusionThe machine learning model accurately predicts MCI risk in COPD patients, enabling timely clinical interventions and improved care through early identification of high-risk individuals.Keywords: CHARLS, HRS, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Mild cognitive impairment, Machine learning, Shapley additive explanation

Introduction

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a chronic and progressive airway and lung disease, recognized as one of the top ten leading causes of death globally [1]. Studies have shown that chronic hypoxia and inflammatory responses caused by COPD may damage nerve cells and blood vessels in the brain, promoting the onset and progression of cognitive dysfunction [2,3]. Previous research indicates that the prevalence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in COPD patients is significantly higher than that in the general population [4]. Once MCI develops—manifesting as memory decline, attention deficits, reduced executive function, and other symptoms—patients face greater challenges in self-management, such as adherence to medication, proper use of inhalers, and participation in rehabilitation exercises [5,6]. These self-management issues further compromise treatment efficacy in COPD patients and exacerbate MCI progression. MCI, a transitional stage between normal cognitive aging and dementia, increases the risk of progressive cognitive decline [7]. Longitudinal studies have revealed that nearly half of the individuals meeting MCI criteria develop dementia within three years [8]. COPD complicated by MCI severely impacts patient health and is associated with higher mortality and disability rates [9]. Therefore, developing models to predict MCI risk and identify key predictors in COPD patients is critical.

MCI refers to a modest cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, or social cognition) that is not due to delirium or another mental disorder and does not interfere significantly with independence in daily activities [10].

However, predictive models for MCI in COPD patients remain limited [11]. This study developed a prediction model for MCI in COPD patients using standardized datasets from China (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, CHARLS) and the United States (Health and Retirement Study, HRS). Employing multiple advanced machine learning algorithms, we constructed a robust and interpretable prediction model that demonstrates strong generalization capability across diverse populations.

Methods

Data sources

China health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a large-scale interdisciplinary survey project hosted by the National Development Institute of Peking University and carried out by the China Social Science Survey Center of Peking University [12].It is an ongoing community-based cohort study among a nationally representative sample of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, since 2011 and were followed up on every 2 years, with the latest wave of 2020 year. CHARLS employs a multistage PPS (Probability Proportional to Size) random sampling approach with implicit stratification to ensure the sample's strong representativeness.The CHARLS datasets can be downloaded at the CHARLS home page at http://charls. pku.edu.cn/en. A longitudinal analysis was conducted using data from CHARLS Wave 4 (2018) as the baseline and Wave 5 (2020) as the outcome measure for predictive modeling.

Health and Retirement Study (HRS)

The Health and Retirement Study (HRS) is a nationally representative longitudinal survey of middle-aged and older adults in the United States, providing data on health, economic, and demographic characteristics [13]. The sampling design employs a stratified multistage area probability-based framework, with primary sampling units defined geographically. Oversampling was implemented for African Americans, Hispanic Americans, and residents of the state of Florida to improve the representation of these subgroups. To maintain cross-sectional representativeness, a new cohort of participants is added every six years. Surveys are conducted biennially using computer-assisted personal interviews. For external validation, this study utilized data from HRS Wave 14 (2018) as the baseline and Wave 15 (2020) as the outcome.

Cohort selection

For both cohorts, the inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥45 years for CHARLS (wave 4) or ≥50 years for HRS (wave 14) at baseline; (2) a self-reported, physician-confirmed diagnosis of COPD at baseline; and (3) no pre-existing MCI at baseline. The exclusion criteria were: (1) loss to follow-up in the final wave (CHARLS wave 5 and HRS wave 15); and (2) having ≥25 % missing values in the cognitive module or other variables of interest. Outcome variables

The cognitive function was evaluated based on the methodology from the American Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [14].Here, participants underwent face-to-face assessments across four cognitive domains: orientation, memory, calculation, and drawing. Orientation and calculation were evaluated using the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS), with target items including the year, month, date, day of the week, and current season. Each correct response in the orientation domain earned 1 point, yielding a maximum score of 5. For the calculation component, participants were asked to serially subtract 7 from 100 five times consecutively, with each correct subtraction awarded 1 point (total 5 points). Immediate word recall was assessed by reading 10 randomly selected words aloud to the participants and tallying the number of words they could recall immediately (1 point per word). Delayed word recall was evaluated after the participants completed a depression scale survey, calculation test, and drawing task. The total memory score combined immediate and delayed recall scores, with a maximum of 20 points (1 point per word across both tasks). For the drawing assessment, the participants were shown a picture and asked to reproduce it accurately. Each correct drawing earned 1 point. The overall cognitive score was calculated as the sum of orientation (5 points), calculation (5 points), memory (20 points), and drawing (1 point), resulting in a maximum total score of 31 points [15]. The specific assessment method of the items is provided in Table S1. This structured approach ensured a comprehensive and standardized evaluation of cognitive function.

In this study, we defined MCI according to the aging-associated cognitive decline (AACD) established by the International Psychogeriatric Association (IPA), and participants were categorized as having MCI if their total score fell more than one standard deviation (SD) below age-appropriate norms [16]. In particular, all participants aged ≥45 years were classified into groups of every 5 years, and the participants in each age group who met the AACD criteria were classified as having MCIs.

Candidate variables

We conducted a preliminary assessment of predictive factors related to MCI based on their clinical significance and scientific knowledge and established predictive factors in previous research.The specific selected variables were as follows:We selected 16 factors that may be related to MCI, including socio-demographic factors (age, sex, marital status, residence,education level, and living alone); healthy factors (smoking, drinking, social activities, living alone,Lawton-IADL scores,fall down and Internet use); and mental factors (depression and life satisfaction).More details about the variables are presented in Table S2.

Data preprocessing

The cognitive module variables and other related variables showed a certain degree of missing data, and the proportion of missing values was less than 25 %.Previous studies have demonstrated that when multiple imputation methods are applied to datasets with missing values below 25 %, the accuracy of statistical parameter estimation remains satisfactory [17]. In this study, an analysis of missing data patterns revealed that the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm was used to assess the missing portion of cognitive data. Consistent with the Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test results (P < 0.001), the MCAR null hypothesis was rejected, indicating that the missing cognitive data in the CHARLS dataset were likely Missing at Random (MAR) or Missing Not at Random (MNAR), but not entirely random. To further investigate, a chi-square test was conducted to generate a new dichotomous variable based on the presence or absence of missing values in each variable. This variable was then tested against other covariates, revealing significant differences (P < 0.001) with variables such as sex, region, marital status, and education. Additionally, the analysis confirmed that the missing cognitive data in CHARLS depended on observed variables, aligning with the MAR assumption. Consequently, multiple imputation methods were deemed appropriate for handling missing values. In this study, variables including gender, region, marital status, and education level were incorporated as explanatory variables to predict the cognitive ability. The random forest imputation method was employed to generate complete cognitive data for the 2018 cohort. Missing values in other COPD-related variables and HRS validation dataset were processed using the same protocol.

To standardize the variables for equitable model evaluation and mitigate overfitting, Z-score normalization was applied, enhancing the model's prediction accuracy, generalizability, and stability, and facilitating cross-variable comparisons. The processed dataset was partitioned into a 70 % training set and a 30 % testing set for subsequent model development and validation.In this study cohort, the proportion of MCI patients (19.6 %) was significantly lower than that of cognitively normal individuals, indicating a notable class imbalance issue. To mitigate the impact of this problem on the performance of machine learning models, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to process the training dataset during model training [18]. SMOTE generates new synthetic samples by interpolating within the feature space of minority class instances, thereby increasing the number of minority class samples. This study implemented SMOTE using the R-4.3.2 smotefamily package (version 1.4.0), increasing the proportion of MCI-positive cases to 40 %. It is important to note that the oversampling process was applied exclusively to the training set, while the final model performance evaluation was conducted on a completely independent test set that had not undergone any sampling processing, ensuring the objectivity and generalizability of the evaluation results. After SMOTE processing, the number of samples in the training set increased from 1499 to 2077.

Model construction and evaluation

All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were executed using R-4.3.2 software (Windows environment). Continuous variable distributions were evaluated via Kolmogorov–Smirnov testing. Parametric data are presented as mean ± SD with Student's t-test applications; non-parametric equivalents as median (IQR) with Mann–Whitney U testing. Categorical variables appear as frequency (%) analyzed by χ2/Fisher's exact tests. Statistical significance threshold was α = 0.05.

To avoid overfitting and multicollinearity issues, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method was employed to screen 16 candidate predictors for the MCI model. We applied 10-fold cross-validation to determine the appropriate tuning parameter (λ) in the LASSO regression analysis to screen out the optimal predictive variables for the MCI model. After variable screening, 11 variables were included in the final MCI prediction model (sex, residence, IADL score, education level, social activities engagement, marital status, Internet use, depression, life satisfaction, hypertension, living alone) (Fig. 2). Additionally, five ML algorithms were deployed to forecast MCI risk in COPD cohorts: Logistic Regression (LR), Neural Network (NN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest (RF). Hyperparameter optimization during model training was performed using the grid search method.

In the testing stage, comprehensive performance was assessed through three pivotal metrics: discrimination capacity, calibration accuracy, and clinical utility. We used the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) to measure the discrimination of the model; calibration was evaluated using Brier scores supplemented by calibration plots; to determine the clinical benefit, we employed decision curve analysis (DCA), which can calculate the net benefit at different probability thresholds. Performance indicators, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score, were derived from the confusion matrix analysis.

To elucidate the contribution of each feature to the final model, we used the Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) method to interpret the best-performing black-box model. SHAP values provide a unified and interpretable measure of feature importance by capturing the interactions between features. Positive SHAP values indicated a positive contribution to the prediction outcomes, whereas negative values suggested inhibitory effects. The absolute magnitude of the SHAP values reflects the relative influence of each feature on the model predictions.

Results

Model evaluation and comparison

Five machine learning models—Random Forest, LightGBM, Neural Network, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression—were utilized to predict the occurrence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in patients with COPD. Model performance was assessed using three key metrics: discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. For discrimination, evaluated via ROC curves, LightGBM and Random Forest achieved the highest AUC values in the training set (0.782 and 0.775, respectively).The top performers were LightGBM and Logistic Regression, which achieved AUCs of 0.722 and 0.721 on the test set and 0.731 and 0.717 on the validation set, respectively (Fig. 3A–C). Calibration, measured by Brier scores and calibration curves, showed good agreement between predicted and actual outcomes across all models (Brier scores <0.25). Notably, LightGBM and XGBoost achieved the lowest Brier scores on the testing set (0.131 and 0.132, respectively) and also led on the validation set (0.130 and 0.135, respectively) (Fig. 3D and E). However, the calibration curves revealed subtle deviations between predicted and observed probabilities in certain ranges, particularly for the Random Forest and Neural Network models. Decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed significant net clinical benefits in both testing and validation sets, with LightGBM and XGBoost showing higher net benefits in both testing and validation sets (Fig. 3F and G). The DCA for the LightGBM model showed a significant net clinical benefit over the threshold probability ranges of approximately 5 %–45 % in the test set and 5 %–60 % in the validation set. This wide range suggests that the LightGBM model is robust and clinically useful for risk stratification under various clinical decision-making preferences. Comprehensive analysis indicated potential overfitting in the Random Forest and Neural Network model, while LightGBM demonstrated the most stable and robust predictive performance across all datasets. Feature importance

The mean SHAP value bar plot ranked features by their importance in predicting MCI occurrence among COPD patients, as shown in Fig. 4B. For the LightGBM model, the top eight predictors of MCI were sex, residence type, IADL score, marital status, social activity frequency, education level, depression and life satisfaction. Fig. 4A presents the analysis results of a SHAP bee swarm plot based on an LightGBM model predicting the risk of Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) in patients with COPD. The data indicate significantly elevated MCI risk in COPD patients with the following characteristics: female gender, rural household registration, higher instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) scores (indicating poorer functional ability), lower educational level, living alone, presence of depressive symptoms, lower life satisfaction and having hypertension. Conversely, protective factors associated with relatively lower MCI risk include: married status, participation in social activities and Internet use.

We further analyzed the impact of eight key variables on MCI prediction using Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) (Fig. 5). First, the gender-specific PDP revealed a markedly elevated MCI risk among female patients, highlighting their vulnerability as a high-risk population for cognitive impairment. The PDP analysis for residential area demonstrated that rural-dwelling COPD patients face a significantly higher risk of MCI, likely attributable to disparities in public health services. The PDP for IADL scores indicated a positive correlation between higher IADL scores (reflecting poorer functional capacity) and increased MCI risk in COPD patients, suggesting that declining instrumental activities of daily living significantly heighten susceptibility to MCI. Educational level displayed a dose-dependent protective association, with each additional year of education reducing MCI risk. The social activity PDP showed an inverse relationship between social engagement frequency and predicted MCI probability, reinforcing the protective role of active social participation. The PDP for marriage indicates that being married is a protective factor for cognitive function. The PDP for internet use suggests that it may help reduce the risk of MCI. In contrast, the PDP for depression reveals that depression is a risk factor for MCI and may significantly increase its incidence.

Discussion

The co-occurrence of COPD and Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) severely compromises patients' quality of life. To enable timely risk assessment and prompt intervention for COPD patients at risk of MCI, we leveraged the large-scale CHARLS database and multiple machine learning models to predict and identify factors influencing MCI onset in COPD patients. HRS database was utilized for external validation. We comprehensively evaluated model performance across three critical dimensions: discrimination (AUC-ROC), calibration (Brier score, calibration curves), and clinical utility (decision curve analysis). To elucidate how individual features contribute to predictions in the top-performing model, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) methodology to interpret this black-box algorithm. Our results demonstrate that the LightGBM model achieved the most stable and robust predictive performance, with superior discrimination, optimal calibration, and enhanced clinical net benefit across both test and validation sets compared to other algorithms.

Through LASSO regression analysis, we identified 11 pivotal predictor variables categorized into three domains: socio-demographic factors (sex, residence, education level and marital status); healthy factors (social activities, living alone,Lawton-IADL scores and Internet use); and mental factors (depression and life satisfaction).The associations between MCI and these 11 variables have been extensively validated across prior epidemiological and clinical studies.

Regarding sociodemographic factors, cognitive function in older women is more susceptible to cognitive impairment compared to older men. The underlying mechanisms may be closely related to female-specific biological risk factors. For instance, women carrying the APOE ε4 allele face a significantly higher risk of developing cognitive impairment compared to male carriers, and they exhibit faster hippocampal atrophy during disease progression [19]. Furthermore, historically lower educational attainment among women and their increased vulnerability to psychological factors such as social isolation and depression in later life may further contribute to this disparity [20]. Older adults residing in rural areas exhibited a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment than their urban counterparts. The core reason underlying the disparity in cognitive impairment risk between urban and rural older adults lies in the unequal distribution of resources. Rural areas lag behind urban areas in economic foundation, social security coverage, healthcare resources, and the promotion of healthy lifestyles. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of exposure to childhood hunger and lower educational attainment among rural older adults further exacerbate the risk of cognitive decline by diminishing cognitive reserve [21]. Regarding educational level, multiple studies have demonstrated that education serves as a critical protective factor for cognitive function. Older adults with higher educational level exhibit enhanced neuroplasticity and develop more optimized neural networks, which enhances their cognitive reserve and enables them to better compensate for age-related cognitive decline [22,23]. Additionally, Sneha Mantri et al. highlighted that educational level is not only associated with cognitive function but also positively correlated with the quality and duration of education [24]. Therefore, it is essential to promote universal access to education and the equitable distribution of educational resources to enhance overall educational attainment. Regarding marital status, being married serves as a protective factor for cognitive function, likely due to spousal emotional support, reduced risk of depression and anxiety, guidance toward healthier lifestyles, and access to stable financial resources [25]. Xiangyuan Huang et al. found that older adults who were continuously married or widowed but remarried exhibited better cognitive abilities compared to those who remained unmarried after widowhood [26], which aligns with our conclusions. Older adults living alone face a heightened risk of cognitive dysfunction, with the risk escalating as the cumulative duration of solitary living increases [27,28]. The underlying mechanism may lie in the fact that living alone limits opportunities for complex social interactions, leading to insufficient daily cognitive stimulation and consequently hindering the building and maintenance of cognitive reserve, which helps counteract brain pathology.

Regarding healthy factors, studies across multiple countries have consistently demonstrated that instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) performance serves as an effective predictor of cognitive dysfunction in older adults. From a neuropathological perspective, early neurodegenerative changes in the brain (such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposition and tau tangles) first lead to impairments in higher cognitive functions, including memory, executive function, and processing speed. As an "integrator" of higher cognitive functions, IADL have extremely high requirements for cognitive reserve—particularly in sensitive domains like financial management and technology use. A decline in IADL scores directly reflects the occurrence of underlying neuropsychological damage. Moreover, IADL impairments are more severe in patients with multiple-domain mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and amnestic MCI [29,30]. Participating social activities positively in later life has been shown to reduce the incidence of cognitive impairment. Isobel E. M. Evans et al. revealed that maintaining a socially active lifestyle in old age improves cognitive function, with cognitive reserve longitudinally mediating this association [31]. This vulnerability may be attributed to the lack of daily companionship and diminished social support in solitary living environments. Compared to older adults who did not use the Internet, those who used the Internet exhibit a significantly lower risk of developing mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Recent research suggests that Internet use may help delay cognitive decline, potentially by preserving the structural integrity of the globus pallidus—a deep brain region [32]. As an important structure within the basal ganglia, the globus pallidus contributes to the regulation of motor control, executive functioning, and cognitive processing [33]. The novel cognitive stimuli and sustained social engagement associated with Internet use may help maintain gray matter volume in this region or slow its atrophy, thereby supporting overall cognitive health. Chronic hypertension can induce maladaptive and progressive degenerative changes in the cerebral vascular system, leading to a variety of pathological alterations. These include thickening and hardening of blood vessel walls, decreased ability to regulate cerebral blood flow, microhemorrhages and microinfarcts, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and cerebral atrophy. These pathological changes—particularly cerebral atrophy, microinfarcts, and microhemorrhages—directly damage neurons and disrupt neural connections, thereby impairing normal brain function. Ultimately, under the combined effects of mechanisms such as oxidative stress, infarction, inflammation, and dysfunction of the blood-brain barrier, the brain undergoes sustained damage to both its structure and function, resulting in the development of cognitive impairment [34,35].

Regarding mental factors, older adults experiencing depression are at a heightened risk of developing cognitive impairment. Studies have shown that the risk of depression-related mild cognitive impairment (MCI) stems from the synergistic effects of shared neuroanatomical substrates (e.g., reduced volume of the hippocampus and insula), common neurobiological pathways (including hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, neuroinflammation, and decreased neurotrophic factors), and behavioral factors such as social isolation [36]. These mechanisms suggest that persistent cognitive deficits during the course of depression may constitute a "cognitive scar," which exists independently of emotional symptoms and serves as a prodromal manifestation of MCI. This underscores the importance of early assessment and intervention of cognitive function in the treatment of depression.Additionally, older adults with lower life satisfaction are more prone to depression and may exhibit unhealthy lifestyle habits, such as physical inactivity and insufficient sleep, further contributing to cognitive dysfunction [37].These findings emphasize the importance of addressing psychological well-being and promoting mental health strategies to mitigate cognitive decline in the aging population.

This study developed a machine learning prediction model for cognitive impairment in COPD patients based on nationally representative sampling cohorts, demonstrating the following advantages over previous studies [11]:First, our model was developed using a national-level cohort (CHARLS) and externally validated in an independent international cohort (HRS). This approach better captures the characteristics of real-world COPD patients and improves the model's generalizability. Second, the research systematically constructed and compared five distinct machine learning algorithms (e.g., LightGBM, XGBoost) and employed the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method to interpret the results of this "black-box" model, thereby achieving an effective balance between high predictive accuracy and high clinical interpretability. In addition, during variable selection, we prioritized clinically accessible indicators over complex or costly tests. This design significantly improves clinical applicability, particularly in resource-constrained environments.

Nevertheless, certain limitations warrant consideration. First, the educational level of our sample was relatively low, with 89.8 % of participants having completed junior high school education or below. This may limit the generalizability of our model to populations with higher education levels, as cognitive reserve and lifestyle factors associated with higher education could influence MCI risk. To address this, future studies could include more diverse educational backgrounds or apply stratification techniques to adjust for education effects. Second, the calibration curves revealed subtle deviations between predicted and observed probabilities for certain models (e.g., Random Forest and Neural Network). These deviations, potentially indicative of mild overfitting, highlight an area for model refinement. Future work could employ techniques such as Platt scaling, isotonic regression, or enhanced regularization to improve calibration accuracy and the model's clinical reliability. Finally, although the sample size in this study was derived from a large-scale, nationally representative cohort, it could still be considered relatively limited for training complex machine learning models. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further validate the stability of our model.

Conclusion

In summary, this study employed CHARLS and HRS datasets to develop five ML algorithms for predicting MCI in COPD patients and identifying its influencing factors. The LightGBM algorithm outperformed others in predictive accuracy and stability. By integrating easily accessible clinical and demographic variables, the model facilitates preclinical risk stratification. It enables healthcare authorities and clinicians to proactively identify and monitor high-risk COPD patients vulnerable to MCI, facilitating timely interventions to prevent or mitigate cognitive decline.
