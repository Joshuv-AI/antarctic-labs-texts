Inhalable epitope vaccine 1022-SpaA V3 establishes nasal mucosal fortress against zoonotic Streptococcus suis
Authors: Hong Chen, Yu Zheng, Jikun Mei, Peng Zhang, Yu Li, Fengyang Li, Liancheng Lei, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus suis serotype 2 (SS2), a zoonotic pathogen, can breach the respiratory barriers and cause systemic infections in both humans and swine. Current vaccines against SS2 are mainly formulated as an injectable product and primarily induce serum IgG antibodies. However, there is an urgent need for inhalable vaccines that induce the mucosal immune response. Leveraging immunoinformatics, we designed a multi-epitope vaccine targeting conserved epitopes from two virulence factors of SS2, SSU05-1022 (IgA1 protease) and SpaA (pilus-associated protein). Four cytotoxic T-lymphocyte (CTL), seven helper T-lymphocyte (HTL), and six B-cell epitopes were selected and fused using AAY/GPGPG/EAAAK linkers, with the L7/L12 ribosomal protein incorporated as an immunostimulatory adjuvant. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed strong binding affinity between the vaccine (1022-SpaA V3 ) and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4),and further cellular experiments confirmed that TLR4 mediated the pro-inflammatory cytokine expression induced by the candidate vaccine in macrophages. The recombinant vaccine was subsequently successfully expressed in vitro with high solubility. Intranasal delivery of 60 µg vaccine robustly elevated respiratory mucosal sIgA and IgG in serum, enhanced the proportion of B and CD4⁺T cells in nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT), lungs and spleens, and activated the CD80⁺ dendritic cell/macrophage in NALT and lungs. Challenge experiments in mice indicated that the inhalable vaccine provided significant, albeit strain-dependent, protection against selected SS2 clinical isolates, including the virulent strain SC19, 05ZYH33, CVCC606, and JZLQ022. Thus, these data suggest that the 1022-SpaA V3 vaccine, optimized through epitope prioritization, adjuvant synergy, and mucosal delivery, represents a promising candidate for combating SS2 infections via dual mucosal-systemic immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05751-x.Keywords: Streptococcus suis serotype 2, Multi-epitope vaccine, Mucosal immunity, Immunoinformatics

Introduction

Streptococcus suis (S. suis) is a significant zoonotic pathogen that initially establishes itself on the mucosal surfaces of the upper respiratory and digestive tracts in pigs. By adhering to and colonizing these mucosal barriers, it disrupts their integrity and subsequently initiates systemic infections, leading to conditions such as septicemia, meningitis, and endocarditis [1, 2]. Furthermore, Streptococcus suis is capable of infecting humans, making it a pathogen of dual concern for both the global swine industry and public health. Its impact includes substantial economic losses and potential health risks. To date, 29 classical serotypes of Streptococcus suis have been identified, categorized according to variations in their capsular polysaccharides (CPS) [3]. Of these, Streptococcus suis serotype 2 strains demonstrate the highest pathogenicity and possess a transmission advantage. Epidemiological evidence, particularly from Asia-Pacific regions such as China and Thailand, has consistently identified SS2 as the leading cause of invasive meningitis in clinical settings [4]. Its public health significance has escalated to become a key focus of global infectious disease control and research [5].

Conventional vaccines, while capable of inducing systemic immune responses, fail to effectively activate mucosal sIgA production and exhibit limited protective efficacy due to Streptococcus suis serotype diversity and virulence factor variability [6, 7]. A key risk of whole-cell vaccines is that their antigenic composition is unfocused, which may fail to elicit robust immunity against critical, conserved epitopes necessary for broad protection [8, 9]. Furthermore, Streptococcus suis downregulates its CPS during mucosal attachment to facilitate colonization. Therefore, the primary protection from conventional vaccines mediated by anti-CPS opsonophagocytosis becomes ineffective in preventing initial colonization [10].

The pathogenic process involves the specific binding of surface adhesins to host cell receptors and the destruction of host cells by secreted proteins [11, 12]. Specifically, the virulence factor SSU05-1022 exhibits IgA1 protease activity, which cleaves host IgA1 to compromise mucosal immunity and facilitate bacterial colonization [13, 14]. As an immunogen, vaccination with this protein induces antibodies enhancing host resistance against pathogens such as Neisseria [15], Streptococcus pneumoniae [16], and Streptococcus pyogenes [17]. However, its large molecular weight and suboptimal immunogenicity limit its standalone efficacy. Similarly, the isopeptide-forming pilus-associated protein SpaA is a critical outer membrane virulence factor that enhances bacterial survival in the bloodstream [18]. SpaA is widely conserved across strains, including Streptococcus mutans [19], Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae [20], Paenibacillus alvei [21], and Streptococcus sobrinus [22], and plays an essential role in adhesion and aggregation for colonization [23]. Despite their individual potential, a synergistic multi-epitope formulation integrating these targets is urgently needed to overcome the limitations of current standalone and whole-cell vaccines.

Effective mucosal immunity offers distinct advantages for intervening in the early infection process of Streptococcus suis. Studies have confirmed that an intranasal vaccine designed against multiple virulence factors can elicit both effective mucosal and systemic immune responses, clearing colonizing bacteria of different serotypes from the nasopharynx to provide protection against systemic infection [24]. Based on this principle, the design of the inhalable vaccine in this study aims to mimic the natural route of infection to directly activate resident immune cells in the upper respiratory tract. Here, we employed an immunoinformatics-based approach to design the multi-epitope subunit vaccine 1022-SpaA V3, incorporating conserved epitopes from SSU05-1022 and SpaA proteins. High-immunogenicity T- and B-cell epitopes were screened, and the L7/L12 ribosomal protein was integrated to enhance structural stability. Structural simulations and molecular docking predicted its robust interaction with immune receptors TLR4. Combined with murine experiments, the vaccine demonstrated efficacy in eliciting mucosal antibody responses, reducing bacterial loads in organs, and conferring significant protection against SS2 infection.

Materials and methods

Selection of target proteins

The complete protein sequences of Streptococcus suis SSU05-1022 (Proteome ID: HEM4464508.1) and SpaA (Proteome ID: WP_044475486.1) required for this study were retrieved from the UniProt database and were provided in FASTA format.

Identification and selection of Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) and Helper T lymphocytes (HTL) epitopes

Identification of CTL epitopes was performed using the IEDB MHC-I analysis server, where peptide length was dictated by the specificities of the selected MHC class I alleles [25]. Candidate peptide selection was conducted according to three established parameters: antigenicity scores exceeding 0.5, positive immunogenicity values, and confirmed safety characteristics demonstrating neither allergenic nor toxic properties. Following this screening protocol, 14-amino acid-length peptides meeting all criteria were identified as optimal candidates.

HTL epitopes were predicted using the NN-align 2.3 algorithm available through the IEDB analysis platform (http://tools.iedb.org/mhcii/). For this prediction, the mouse H-2-I allele was specified as the locus. The identification and ranking of HTL epitopes were performed according to their IC50 values, whereby epitopes exhibiting the lowest IC50 values were prioritized due to the inverse correlation between IC50 and binding affinity for MHC class II molecules. Final selection of optimal epitopes for the MHC class II allele reference set was based on criteria including IC50 values below 100 nM and peptide length not exceeding 15 amino acids [26]. Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) plays a pivotal role in host defense against bacterial pathogens by modulating immune cell activity and enhancing antimicrobial responses [27, 28]. Based on this function, we employed the IFNepitope platform (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/ifnepitope/predict.php) incorporating machine learning algorithms to screen candidate epitopes. This was followed by selection based on comprehensive criteria including antigenic response capacity, immunogenic potential, and safety profiles, ultimately identifying helper T lymphocyte epitopes with demonstrated IFN-γ inducing potential.

Identification and selection of linear B-cell epitopes

The selection of candidate B-cell epitopes was accomplished by integrating prediction results from two bioinformatics tools. The ABCpred analysis platform (http://www.imtech.res.in/raghava/abcpred) provided a confidence score range of 0.5–0.95, while the BepiPred-2.0 system (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/BepiPred-2.0/) yielded an output range of 0.5–1.0 [29, 30]. Eligible epitopes were required to meet the following criteria: amino acid sequence length between 10 and 20 residues with concurrent identification by both prediction tools. The final selection criteria mandated an affinity threshold exceeding 0.9, coupled with strong antigenic properties and the absence of allergenic potential and toxicity.

Evaluation of the antigenic, allergic, and toxic properties of the chosen epitopes

Antigenicity evaluation of the predicted epitopes was performed using the VaxiJen v2.0 web server (http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html). A threshold value of 0.5 was established for this assessment, wherein epitopes exceeding this threshold were classified as antigenic-positive and advanced to subsequent construction stages, while those below this cutoff were discarded [31]. For T-cell epitope analysis, the IEDB immunoinformatics suite was employed to systematically evaluate the immune activation potential of both CTL and HTL epitopes. This involved specialized prediction modules for MHC-I (http://tools.iedb.org/immunogenicity/) and MHC-II (http://tools.iedb.org/CD4episcore) binding affinity. During the selection process, higher-scoring epitopes were prioritized as their elevated prediction values demonstrated positive correlation with the capacity to elicit protective immune responses.

Additionally, within the development of epitope vaccines, assessing allergenic potential is essential to reduce risks of hypersensitivity and ensure safety in clinical applications [32]. The potential allergenicity of candidate vaccine sequences was evaluated using AllerTOP v.2.1 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/allertop_test/accounts/signup/). This prediction tool employs auto cross-covariance transformation methodology combined with machine learning algorithms to accurately identify allergenic properties in protein sequences. The method characterizes key amino acid properties through five E-descriptors, encompassing molecular size, hydrophobicity, relative abundance, and structural propensities for α-helices and β-strands. Classification was performed using a k-nearest neighbor algorithm (k = 1), trained on a balanced dataset comprising 2427 known allergens and an equal number of non-allergens from diverse species. Potential epitopes were evaluated for harmful toxic effects via the ToxinPred web server (http://crdd.osdd.net/raghava/toxinpred/), and only non-toxic candidates were retained for further development.

Development of a multi-epitope subunit vaccine

A multi-epitope vaccine was constructed using experimentally validated epitopes demonstrating confirmed immunogenicity, antigenicity, and safety profiles [33]. The molecular assembly employed a rational linkage design to connect functional modules: GPGPG spacers connected B-cell and helper T-lymphocyte epitopes, AAY linkers joined cytotoxic T-lymphocyte epitopes, while EAAAK connectors anchored adjuvants at both N- and C-termini. To enhance the immunostimulatory efficacy, four distinct adjuvants with synergistic effects were incorporated into the final vaccine design. The selected adjuvants were Beta-defensin-3 [34], HABA protein [35], L7/L12 ribosomal protein [36], and Heat-labile enterotoxin IIA B chain [37]. The vaccines were named 1022-SpaA V1, 1022-SpaA V2, 1022-SpaA V3, and 1022-SpaA V4, respectively.

Physicochemical characterization of the vaccine candidate

Computational assessment of vaccine physicochemical properties constitutes an essential initial phase in the development process. Critical parameters were systematically characterized using the Expasy ProtParam platform, including amino acid composition, relative molecular mass, theoretical isoelectric point, instability coefficient, aliphatic side chain index, and hydrophobicity balance value. The Protein-Sol web tool (https://protein-sol.manchester.ac.uk/) was employed to predict solubility characteristics, utilizing an algorithm that compares target sequences against standardized reference datasets to generate solubility scores. A predictive value exceeding 0.45 indicates that the protein’s solubility in Escherichia coli expression systems surpasses the average level of its native soluble proteome. These physicochemical parameters exert decisive impacts on vaccine functionality, as they directly determine whether immunization can effectively trigger adaptive immune responses through coordinated activation of B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes [38].

Forecasting of secondary and tertiary structures

The PSIPRED prediction system (http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/) was employed to assess the secondary structure of the vaccine sequence, thereby delineating its structural domain organization and spatial conformation. This tool effectively predicts topological and helical arrangements in transmembrane regions, identifying structural elements such as alpha-helices, beta-strands, and coils. Its algorithm utilizes a two-stage neural network-based approach for secondary structure prediction [39]. Engineered proteins are constructed by fusing sequences derived from various native proteins or peptides to confer innovative biological activities and potentiate immune responses synergistically. Such chimeric designs necessitate rigorous assessment of their conformational stability, as they lack evolutionarily optimized native folding. Particular emphasis is placed on mapping disordered segments, which critically govern both structural integrity and functional performance. Disorder propensity was predicted using DISOPRED3, implemented in PSIPRED (http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/).

Validation and refinement of the tertiary structure

By integrating the global conformation assessment from ProSA-web (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php) with the local geometry analysis of PROCHECK in SAVES v6.0 (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu), the structural reliability and stereochemical properties of the three-dimensional vaccine model were thus comprehensively validated. ProSA calculates a Z-score by comparing the model against experimentally determined protein structures from the PDB derived from X-ray crystallography and NMR [40]. The Ramachandran plot module within PROCHECK was employed to systematically examine the dihedral angles of amino acids in protein secondary structural elements, including α-helices, β-sheets, and random coils. The stereochemical reliability of the molecular model was confirmed through precise measurement of the Phi (φ) and Psi (ψ) angles for each residue [41].

Mapping of spatially clustered B-cell epitopes

Conformational B-cell epitopes for the designed multi-epitope vaccine were predicted using the ElliPro tool (http://tools.iedb.org/ellipro/). This method defines epitopes based on the protein’s three-dimensional structure by modeling each as an ellipsoid. Residues with a protrusion index (PI) score of 0.9 or higher were considered part of a conformational epitope, as they fall within an ellipsoid encompassing 90% of the protein’s structure. The ElliPro tool was selected for this analysis due to its recognized performance in structure-based epitope prediction, which has been demonstrated in independent evaluations to achieve an AUC value of 0.732 in key protein-specific assessments [42].

Docking analysis of selected vaccine construct

Acting as key sentinels, Toll-like receptors (TLRs) recognize bacterial pathogen-associated molecular patterns and are fundamental to orchestrating the innate immune response against microbial invasion [43]. Ligand binding to TLR2 or TLR4 triggers the rapid release of pro-inflammatory cytokines—such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6—and chemokines, coordinating early inflammatory reactions to eliminate pathogens. Additionally, TLR signaling facilitates the transition from innate to adaptive immunity by promoting antigen-specific responses. Molecular docking studies between the vaccine candidate and TLRs were performed using the HDOCK web server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/). Based on the crystal structures of TLR2 (PDB ID: 2Z7X) and TLR4 (PDB ID: 2Z64), conformational analysis combined with binding free energy calculations was employed to identify antigenic epitope conformations with optimal binding efficacy [44].

Dynamic simulation of the vaccine construct bound to TLR2 and TLR4

All molecular dynamics simulations were carried out for 100 nanoseconds using Gromacs 2023 [45]. The protein was described with the CHARMM36 force field, while the GAFF2 force field parameters were employed for the ligand. The simulation system was constructed by placing the protein-ligand complex in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions, followed by solvation using the TIP3P water model. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with the Verlet cutoff scheme, and a cutoff distance of 1.0 nm was applied for both van der Waals and Coulomb interactions. The system underwent initial equilibration in the NVT ensemble followed by the NPT ensemble (100 ps each with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps). Production data were subsequently collected under constant conditions of 300 K temperature and 1 bar pressure.

Receptor Blocking and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from mice and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/well. After overnight culture to allow full adherence and optimal cell condition, the cells were pre-treated with anti-TLR2 monoclonal antibody or anti-TLR4 monoclonal antibody to block the corresponding receptors. An isotype-matched control antibody group and an untreated cell group were included as controls. Following incubation for the indicated time, the cells were stimulated with 1022-SpaA V3 protein at a final concentration of 300 ng/mL. After stimulation, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent or a commercial RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined prior to reverse transcription for cDNA synthesis. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green or TaqMan chemistry with cDNA as the template. The mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were analyzed, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the control group.

Immune simulation method

To evaluate the immunostimulatory capacity of the designed vaccine, we employed the C-ImmSim platform (https://kraken.iac.rm.cnr.it/C-IMMSIM/) for in silico simulation analysis [46]. Immune response prediction relies on the simulated activation of key mammalian anatomical structures—lymph nodes, thymus, and bone marrow. In accordance with established protocols, the vaccine was administered in three doses spaced four weeks apart. Using default parameters with injections at steps 1, 84, and 168, the simulation was configured with the following: a volume of 50, 1050 total steps, an LPS-free formulation, and a random seed value of 12,345.

Codon optimization and in silico cloning

Codon optimization and reverse translation were performed using the Java Codon Adaptation Tool JCat (https://www.jcat.de) to enhance the expression of the target protein in Escherichia coli K12 [47]. The vaccine protein sequence was optimized using JCat to determine parameters like GC content and CAI. The optimized sequence was subsequently flanked with BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites at the 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively. Finally, the modified sequence was inserted into the pET28a(+) vector using SnapGene software.

Bacterial strains and culture

Four pathogenic S. suis SS2 strains (SC19, 05ZYH33, CVCC606, JZLQ022) stored in our laboratory were cultured at 37 °C/5% CO₂ in Todd Hewitt broth (Hopebio, Qingdao, China). Recombinant vector-containing Escherichia coli (E. coli BL21) was grown in kanamycin-supplemented LB medium at 37 °C.

Mice immunization and challenge assays

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female ICR mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China). The mice were acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation and housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water.

A total of 110 ICR mice (6–8 weeks old) were acclimatized for 2 days and subsequently randomly allocated into five experimental groups (n = 22 per group): MOCK group (unvaccinated and non-challenged control), PBS group (15 µl), 30 µg vaccine-immunized group, 60 µg vaccine-immunized group, and 80 µg vaccine-immunized group. The 1022-SpaA V3 vaccine protein was dissolved in 15 µL of PBS at specified concentrations for inhalation administration. All groups received two intranasal immunizations of either PBS or 1022-SpaA V3 vaccine via the respiratory route at 14-day intervals. Prior to each immunization, the mice were placed under spontaneous ventilation with a mixture of 2% isoflurane and 98% air until deep anesthesia was achieved. Blood samples were collected through tail vein bleeding from six randomly selected mice per group at 14 and 28 days post-primary immunization for serum preparation. Fourteen days after both the primary and booster immunizations, three mice from each group were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) along with nasal irrigation samples (NIS) were collected. The remaining mice underwent SS2 strain SC19 challenge infection, with the exception of the MOCK group which served as non-infected controls.

A total of 60 ICR mice (6–8 weeks old) were acclimatized for 2 days and randomly divided into six groups (n = 10 per group), including MOCK group (unvaccinated and non-challenged control), PBS group (primary and booster immunization via intranasal route, 15 µl), i.h.+i.h. group (primary and booster immunization via subcutaneous injection), i.n.+i.h. group (primary immunization via intranasal route and booster via subcutaneous injection), i.n.+i.n. group (primary and booster immunization via intranasal route), and i.h.+i.n. group (primary immunization via subcutaneous injection and booster via intranasal route). All groups received two immunizations at 14-day intervals, each with a 60 µg dose of vaccine. For respiratory delivery, the 1022-SpaA V3 vaccine protein was dissolved in 15 µL of PBS. For subcutaneous immunization, the same dose of protein was dissolved in 200 µL of PBS and administered via multiple injections across the dorsal region. And mice in the intranasal immunization group were subjected to spontaneous ventilation with a mixture of 2% isoflurane and 98% air until deep anesthesia was achieved prior to immunization. Fourteen days post-booster immunization, mice were subjected to SS2 strain SC19 challenge infection, except for the MOCK group, which remained unchallenged.

BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, n = 10/group) were randomly assigned to three groups: PBS control, commercial vaccine (100 µL, im), and 1022-SpaA V3 (60 µg, in). A prime-boost immunization regimen was administered at a 14-day interval. Fourteen days post-boost, all mice were challenged with the SC19 strain, and mortality was monitored daily for 7 days.

Quantification of IgG and IgA antibodies using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

An indirect ELISA was performed to quantify IgG and sIgA antibodies in immunized mice. Plates were coated with 1 µg/well of recombinant 1022-SpaA V3 antigen and blocked with 5% BSA. Serum (dilute at a ratio of 1:10), BALF (undiluted) and NIS (undiluted) were used as primary antibody sources. HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG or IgA secondary antibodies (1:5,000) were applied, followed by TMB substrate. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry analysis of B cells, T cells, macrophages (MA) and dendritic cells (DC) in mice

On the 28th day following the initial intranasal vaccination, blood samples were obtained from ICR mice via tail vein puncture and transferred to anticoagulant tubes. Red blood cell lysis buffer was applied to remove erythrocytes, and the remaining cell pellet was collected by centrifugation before being resuspended in staining buffer supplemented with a viability stain. Subsequently, mice were euthanized. Nasal cavity tissue was isolated by removing the lower jaw, skin, and muscle tissues to expose the upper palate; individual cells were liberated via enzymatic digestion with a cocktail of Type I collagenase (0.15%), Type IV collagenase (0.15%), and hyaluronidase (0.15%) at 37 °C for 0.5 h. The harvested cell suspension was pelleted by centrifugation and subsequently reconstituted in staining buffer for subsequent staining procedures [48]. Single-cell suspensions were generated from lung and spleen tissues through mechanical dissociation. Erythrocytes were depleted by lysis buffer treatment, and the remaining cell population was processed by centrifugation followed by resuspension in staining buffer. Aliquots of single-cell suspensions prepared from NALT, lung, spleen, and blood were then stained for downstream analysis [49]. Following washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), data acquisition was performed using a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and subsequent data analysis was conducted.

Colony-Forming Unit (CFU) assay

Following humane euthanasia, the lungs, spleen, liver, and brain were aseptically collected and placed in sterile tubes containing PBS and stainless steel beads. Tissue samples were disrupted with a mechanical homogenizer, and 100 µL of each resulting homogenate was sequentially diluted in PBS and seeded onto brain heart infusion (BHI) agar plates. After 24 h aerobic incubation at a constant temperature of 37 °C, the bacterial load was determined through colony enumeration, with results expressed as colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of tissue. Protective efficacy was expressed in units of protection, defined as the reduction in bacterial load calculated from the difference between the mean ± standard deviation of log₁₀ CFU per spleen in PBS control groups and that of the immunized groups.

Hematoxylin and Eosin(H&E) staining

Following routine paraffin embedding, 5-µm sections of lung, spleen, liver, and brain tissues were prepared by microtome. Sections underwent standard H&E staining: hematoxylin, differentiation in 5% acetic acid, eosin counterstaining, dehydration through graded ethanol, xylene clearing, fume hood drying, and mounting with Canada balsam.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (San Diego, CA, USA), with quantitative results reported as means ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons among three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was utilized. Comparisons of multiple groups over different time points were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001, with non-significance (ns) assigned to results where P ≥ 0.05.

Results

Identification of CTL epitopes

Streptococcus suis adopts a facultative intracellular lifestyle, enabling evasion of antibody- and complement-mediated clearance [49]. CTLs eliminate infected host cells and eradicate intracellular bacteria through MHC-I-restricted recognition of pathogen-derived epitopes, thereby compensating for limitations of humoral immunity [6]. The 14-mer CTL epitopes for SSU05-1022 and SpaA were predicted by the IEDB server. A systematic screening of CTL epitopes targeting the SLA supertype family was conducted, yielding dominant epitopes covering five alleles: SLA-1*0401, SLA-1*0202, SLA-1*0201, SLA-2*1002, and SLA-3*0101. Based on Rank < 0.05, one representative epitope from each SLA supertype was selected. These prioritized CTL epitopes subsequently underwent a multidimensional functional evaluation, including analyses of immunogenicity, antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity, with the results summarized in Table 2.

Identification of HTL epitopes and IFN-γ epitopes

We performed 15-mer HTL epitope screening against the H2-I supertype family, covering nine principal alleles: H2-IEk, H2-Iek, H2-Ias, H2-IAq, H2-IAu, H2-Iaq, H2-Iau, H2-IAk, and H2-IAs. The established prediction system provides systematic coverage of murine MHC class II molecular binding sites. Through computational screening against murine H2-I alleles, 15-amino acid HTL epitopes demonstrating superior binding capacity to MHC class II molecules were selected. Candidate epitopes were subsequently evaluated through a multi-parameter optimization process incorporating immunogenic potential, antigenic specificity, absence of allergenic properties, and lack of toxicity to determine their suitability for incorporation into the vaccine platform (Table 2). IFN-γ secreted by activated HTLs and NK cells establishes a crucial defense mechanism against intracellular microbial infections by coordinating both innate and adaptive immune responses. Consequently, epitopes exhibiting strong predicted IFN-γ induction profiles were prioritized. These immunogenic sequences were further analyzed using a machine learning-based prediction system to confirm their cytokine-inducing capabilities (Table 2).

Identification of linear B-cell epitopes

Candidate B-cell epitopes were prioritized for the vaccine following a comparative analysis of predictions from both ABCpred and BepiPred 2.0 servers. The epitopes displayed prediction scores between 0.52 and 0.93 and 0.5–1.0, respectively. Among these, only ten 18-mer sequences were consistently identified by the two tools. Final selection for incorporation into the vaccine construct was based on a high binding threshold (>0.9), together with desirable antigenic properties and confirmed non-allergenic and non-toxic profiles (Table 2).

Construction of a multi-epitope subunit vaccine

In the final assembly of the vaccine candidate, the highest-ranking epitopes were incorporated into the construct. This included 4 CTL epitopes, 7 HTL epitopes, and 6 B-cell epitopes, which were conjugated using specific peptide linkers. The CTL epitopes were joined via AAY linkers, whereas GPGPG spacers were used to interconnect the HTL and B-cell epitopes. To augment the overall immunogenic efficacy of the vaccine candidate, four adjuvants were incorporated into its rational design through a literature-based selection strategy. These adjuvants include Beta-defensin-3, Hemin-Binding Protein A (HABA) protein, L7/L12 ribosomal protein, and Heat-labile enterotoxin IIA B chain. Beta-defensin-3 is an antimicrobial peptide that not only exhibits direct antimicrobial activity but also enhances innate immune responses by promoting dendritic cell activation and antigen presentation. HABA protein is involved in iron acquisition and may influence bacterial virulence and trigger immune system activation, thereby enhancing the immune response. The L7/L12 ribosomal antigen, a constituent of the ribosomal complex, is recognized for its capacity to elicit dual antibody-mediated and cell-mediated immunity, thereby positioning it as a candidate of interest in prophylactic formulation research. Heat-labile enterotoxin IIA B chain is a subunit of a bacterial toxin known for its strong immunostimulatory properties, particularly in enhancing mucosal immunity. Each vaccine variant was named accordingly: 1022-SpaA V1 (with Beta-defensin-3), 1022-SpaA V2 (with HABA protein), 1022-SpaA V3 (with L7/L12 ribosomal protein), and 1022-SpaA V4 (with Heat-labile enterotoxin IIA B chain). The vaccine construct was conjugated with an adjuvant at both its C- and N-termini via an EAAAK linker. A schematic diagram of the final multi-epitope vaccine is presented in Fig. 1A.

Characterization and Evaluation of the Multi-Epitope-Based Vaccine Candidate

Furthermore, these four variants were designed to evaluate the impact of different adjuvants on vaccine efficacy, immune response and allergenicity, and toxicity (Additional Table 1). Evaluation showed the final vaccine candidate to be devoid of allergenic properties, suggesting that the vaccine constructs, 1022-SpaA V1, V2, V3, and V4, are likely to be safe for use in animal models. The VaxiJen server predicted their antigenicity scores to be greater than 0.7, classifying them as potential strong antigens. Vaccines with high immunogenicity can induce robust neutralizing antibody responses and long-lasting immune memory, thereby providing more effective protection. The IEDB server was utilized to predict the immunogenicity of the vaccine constructs. The results indicated that the immunogenicity scores for all four vaccine constructs were greater than 0, suggesting that they possess strong immunogenic properties. Additionally, ToxinPred server analysis identified 1022-SpaA V4 as toxic, whereas the other three vaccine constructs (1022-SpaA V1, V2, and V3) were classified as non-toxic (Additional Table 1). Thus, the 1022-SpaA V4 was excluded in the following analysis.

ProtParam analysis revealed distinct physicochemical properties among the three vaccine constructs. The structural compositions consisted of 418 (V1), 738 (V2), and 588 (V3) amino acid residues, respectively. In vivo half-life predictions demonstrated differential stability across expression systems: 30 h in mammalian reticulocytes, 20 h in yeast, and > 10 h in E. coli. Moreover, the predicted instability index (II) values were 32.8, 35.27, and 24.69 for 1022-SpaA V1, V2, and V3, respectively. Notably, 1022-SpaA V3 exhibited a lower instability index, indicating greater structural stability under physiological conditions, reduced susceptibility to degradation, and prolonged functional activity. These properties suggest that 1022-SpaA V3 is a promising candidate for vaccine antigens or other biomedical applications. Furthermore, the molecular weight of 1022-SpaA V3 was calculated to be 59.2 kDa, with a theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of 4.58. Additionally, the GRAVY score of − 0.149 indicates that the protein is hydrophilic and exhibits good solubility (Additional Table 2).

Prediction of secondary structure

Solubility prediction via the Protein-Sol server yielded a score of 0.78 for the 1022-SpaA V3 variant (Fig. 1B), suggesting a favorable solubility profile. Secondary structure analysis was performed using the PSIPRED server to characterize the structural composition of the protein. The distribution of amino acids among different conformations was found to be 44.56% alpha-helices, 12.76% beta-turns, and 42.68% random coils (Fig. 1C, D). Notably, the high proportions of alpha helices and random coils suggest that the vaccine structure maintains both stability and flexibility. This structural balance facilitates the effective presentation of antigenic epitopes and enhances immune response activation. Thus, the designed protein represents a promising multi-epitope vaccine candidate with potential for high immunogenic efficacy.

Tertiary structure refinement and validation

The three-dimensional model of vaccine construct 1022-SpaA V3 was visualized using PyMOL (Additional Fig. 1A). Evaluation of the Ramachandran plot indicated that 85.9% of residues fall within the most favorable regions, 12.0% occupy additionally allowed regions, and 0.9% are present in disallowed regions (Additional Fig. 1B). The structure obtained a z-score of −4.66 (Additional Fig. 1C), consistent with a high-quality structural model. These findings validate the structural robustness of the vaccine design, justifying its selection for in-depth functional and immunological analyses.

Discontinuous B-cell epitopes of 1022-SpaA V3

Bioinformatic epitope scanning of the candidate vaccine’s structure-activity relationship regions using ELIPro revealed three discontinuous B-cell epitope clusters within its core structural domain, collectively spanning 316 amino acid residues. B-cell epitopes meeting a threshold between 0.702 and 0.726 were chosen for further analysis (Additional Fig. 2. A–C, Additional Table 3). Specifically, 86 epitope residues were predicted from amino acid residues 335, 338–346, 348–350, 352, 359–369, 381–382, 384–400, and 402–443. Additionally, 112 epitope residues were predicted from 63, 68–80, 83, 94, 97–129, 508, and 512–588. Finally, 118 epitope residues were predicted from 1 to 4, 33, 163–180, 203–218, 226–242, 244–264, 268, and 272–305 within the vaccine sequence (Supplementary Table S3).

Interaction analysis and functional verification of the vaccine construct with Toll-like receptors

Molecular docking simulations were carried out using the HDOCK platform to evaluate the binding of vaccine peptide constructs to TLR2 and TLR4 receptors. The three-dimensional structures of the resulting peptide-TLR complexes were visualized with PyMOL software. Critical residues participating in hydrogen bond formation between the peptides and TLRs are illustrated in Fig. 2A and B. Additional binding affinity estimates derived from HawkDock simulations yielded free energy values of −18.69 kcal/mol for TLR2 and − 16.33 kcal/mol for TLR4. These computational results collectively demonstrate substantial binding capability between the vaccine construct and TLR receptors, supporting its potential to trigger effective immunological activation.

Conformational stability throughout the molecular dynamics simulations was assessed using RMSD. The TLR2 complex exhibited continuous structural divergence, reaching an exceptionally high RMSD of ~ 40 Å without achieving equilibrium, indicative of a highly unstable receptor-ligand interaction. Conversely, the TLR4 complex reached a stable thermodynamic plateau after 80 ns (Fig. 2C). The RMSF values of the TLR2 and TLR4 protein complexes were relatively high, suggesting that these complexes have greater flexibility (Fig. 2D). Simulations indicated modest variations in both the Rg and SASA for the TLR4 complex, suggesting that ligand binding triggered a structural rearrangement of the target protein (Additional Fig. 3A, B). Hydrogen bonds play a critical role in ligand-protein binding. In the TLR4-ligand complex, the number of hydrogen bonds ranged from 0 to 30, with dynamic analysis revealing that the stable complex maintained an average of approximately 15 hydrogen bonds. The TLR2 complex showed hydrogen bonds ranging from 0 to 22, with about 6 hydrogen bonds in most cases, indicating that the TLR4 complex formed the highest number of hydrogen bonds (Additional Fig. 3C, D).

To identify the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) for responsible for sensing 1022-SpaA V3, TLR2 and TLR4 were blocked using corresponding monoclonal antibodies. Quantitative analysis showed that the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly increased in the 1022-SpaA V3 group relative to the PBS control. Neutralization of TLR4, rather than TLR2 dramatically reduced the expression of these three cytokines (Fig. 2E-G). Thus, these findings indicate that TLR4, instead of TLR2, mediates the 1022-SpaA V3-triggered pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in macrophages.

Immune simulations

The immune response induced by the injection of 1022-SpaA V3 into the host was simulated using C-IMMSIM. As the number of injections increased, the simulated antibody response showed a progressive enhancement. The predicted elevation in IgM antibody levels suggests that the initial immune response might be primarily mediated by IgM. Furthermore, the increased levels of IgG + IgM, IgG1 + IgG2, and IgM correlate with a rise in B cell density (Additional Fig. 4A, B). In parallel, as the antigen concentration decreased, there was a notable increase in memory B cells, which reflects an immune tolerance and a more robust immune response (Additional Fig. 4C). A triple immunization protocol elicited robust immune memory, along with a marked expansion of T helper and cytotoxic T lymphocyte populations. This suggests that repeated stimulation progressively strengthened the specific immune response and enhanced long-term defense against pathogens (Additional Fig. 4D–F). Additionally, 1022-SpaA V3 effectively triggered the activation of MA and DC populations. The subsequent cellular expansion and maturation processes contributed to enhanced initiation and regulatory control of host immune responses (Additional Fig. 4G, H). Notable elevation of IFN-γ, IL-2, and TGF-β concentrations was also predicted to occur during immunological activity. The prompt initiation of host defense mechanisms suggests these molecules serve pivotal modulatory functions in the immune cascade (Additional Fig. 4I).

Rational optimization of immunization dose and administration strategy for multi-epitope vaccine 1022-SpaA V3

Quantitative detection of antigen-specific immunoglobulins via indirect ELISA was performed to systematically analyze the humoral immune response characteristics and protective efficacy following immunization with varying inhaled doses of the multi-epitope vaccine 1022-SpaA V3 against SS2 challenge. In vaccinated mice, serum-specific antibody levels increased progressively over time, peaking at Day 28; whereas no significant antibody response was detected in PBS controls throughout the study period (Fig. 4A). To evaluate the dose-dependent protective efficacy SS2 challenge, mice were intraperitoneally administered 2.3 × 10⁹ CFU of SS2 strain SC19 per mouse at 14 days after booster immunization. All PBS-treated mice succumbed to infection within 3 days post-challenge. In contrast, survival rates in the 30 µg, 60 µg and 80 µg vaccine groups reached 60%, 100%, and 80%, respectively, demonstrating robust immunization-mediated protection (Fig. 4B). Longitudinal monitoring of body weight and core temperature revealed distinct recovery trajectories: PBS controls exhibited a 15.67% mean weight loss by Day 2 post-infection, whereas vaccinated mice showed transient weight loss within the initial 48 h. Notably, mice receiving 60–80 µg vaccines stabilized their body weight by Day 3, with gradual recovery thereafter (Fig. 4C). Core temperature remained stable across all groups during the infection window (Fig. 4D). Thus, the 60 µg dose emerged as the optimal formulation, achieving complete protection against lethal SS2 challenge while balancing immunogenic potency and biosafety.

To evaluate the impact of immunization routes on the protective efficacy of the vaccine against SS2, mice were subjected to prime-boost immunization via combined inhalation and subcutaneous administration or individual routes. Antigen-specific antibody titers, quantified by indirect ELISA, revealed no significant differences among groups at 14 days post-booster immunization, with all regimens eliciting comparably high antibody levels (Fig. 4E). The bacterial challenge was performed intraperitoneally using 2.3 × 10⁹ CFU of the virulent SS2 strain SC19, with the procedure conducted 14 days after the booster vaccine dose. All control mice succumbed to infection within 48 h. Strikingly, the intranasal prime-boost regimen conferred complete protection, whereas alternative routes showed markedly reduced survival rates or delay the mice death (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, intranasal immunization uniquely maintained physiological homeostasis in challenged mice, as evidenced by attenuated weight loss and reduced body temperature fluctuations compared to other groups (Fig. 4G, H). These findings underscore that immunization route critically dictates the quality of vaccine-induced protective immunity, with intranasal delivery optimizing survival outcomes during pathogen challenge. Following SC19 infection, subjects immunized with 1022-SpaA V3 demonstrated a final survival rate of 80% at 7 days post-infection, outperforming the commercialized vaccine group (Fig. 4I). Notably, the 1022-SpaA V3 group experienced no further mortality after day 2, indicating that it confers more robust immune protection than the commercial vaccine.

Thus, this study demonstrates that the multi-epitope vaccine 1022-SpaA V3 confers robust protection against systemic infection by SS2 strain SC19, as evidenced by significantly reduced bacterial loads in mouse blood, lungs, spleen, liver, and brain, alongside attenuated systemic histopathological damage. In addition, we evaluated its immunogenicity against diverse SS2 strains, including the epidemic strain 05ZYH33 (2.5 × 109 CFU/mouse) and clinical isolates CVCC606 (2.2 × 109 CFU/mouse) and JZLQ022 (5.5 × 108 CFU/mouse). Discussion

Streptococcus suis serotype 2 represents a major zoonotic pathogen that severely compromises both swine production and public health security [50]. Nevertheless, existing conventional vaccines remain limited in their capacity to provide broad-spectrum, long-lasting protection, especially effective mucosal immune responses [24]. This study adopted a reverse vaccinology strategy to construct a multi-epitope vaccine covering HLA supertypes by integrating CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes from SS2 antigens SpaA and SSU05-1022. The L7/L12 ribosomal protein was incorporated as a molecular adjuvant to enhance DC maturation and amplify innate immune responses through high-density hydrogen bond networks formed between the vaccine construct and TLR-like receptors. Breaking from conventional injection routes, intranasal delivery was employed to achieve “local-systemic” immune synergy. This approach significantly elevated sIgA levels in NIS and BALF, establishing a first-line mucosal defense. By overcoming the mucosal delivery barriers of traditional subunit vaccines, this work pioneers a novel paradigm for mucosal immunization design.

To avoid immune tolerance induced by direct binding to non-professional APCs, the 14-mer extended CTL epitopes were rationally designed to mandate internalization and natural intracellular processing by professional APCs [51, 52]. Additionally, although preliminary in vivo evaluations were conducted in a murine surrogate model, the epitopes were strictly predicted using SLA alleles to ensure the ultimate translational viability for pigs, the natural host of Streptococcus suis.

In structural design, the physicochemical properties and structural stability of a vaccine critically determine its immunogenicity [53]. The molecular weight of 1022-SpaA V3, below the conventional 100 kDa threshold for subunit vaccines, facilitates penetration through mucosal epithelial barriers. A GRAVY index of −0.149 indicates strong hydrophilicity, corroborated by a Protein-Sol solubility score of 0.78, which collectively explain the observed high-efficiency recombinant protein expression and suggest prolonged functional activity under physiological conditions. This finding aligns with prior studies demonstrating that vaccine protein stability positively correlates with in vivo half-life and immunogenicity. Furthermore, the antigenicity score and immunogenicity predictions of 1022-SpaA V3 underscore its potential as a potent antigen, likely mediating innate-adaptive immune synergy via TLR2/TLR4 signaling pathway activation. The significantly lower instability index of the V3 variant compared to V1 and V2 may stem from its unique secondary structure: a high proportion of random coils confers conformational flexibility, while beta-turns enhance structural rigidity through intramolecular hydrogen bond networks. This dynamic equilibrium optimizes epitope exposure. Additionally, the rationality of its tertiary structure and high-affinity binding to TLR receptors further support its potential as a functional immunogen. However, in vivo folding efficiency requires verification via circular dichroism or cryo-electron microscopy to address limitations of in silico predictive models. While in silico tools have inherent limitations—including training dataset biases and epitope mapping uncertainties—we mitigated these risks through multi-parameter filtering, cross-validation, and molecular dynamics, and several key results from computational predictions were experimentally validated. Structural claims are therefore presented as supportive evidence, and the continued adoption of these tools in recent studies confirms their sustained reliability [54].

Structural alignment between the RaptorX and AlphaFold predictions demonstrated a consistent macroscopic topology and a highly conserved rigid core with a local RMSD of 1.03 Å. Minor structural variations within the inherently flexible ligand-binding cleft yielded a global RMSD of 2.54 Å. Subsequent molecular dynamics simulations helped reconcile these initial static differences by driving the receptor-ligand complex toward a stable thermodynamic equilibrium, providing substantial support for the reliability of the interaction models. The combined docking and dynamic analyses offered valuable insights into the potential receptor-binding mechanisms of the vaccine construct. The calculated low binding free energy and the extensive hydrogen bond network observed in the TLR4 complex point toward a mechanism for the direct activation of innate immunity via this specific pattern recognition receptor [55, 56]. Dynamic profiling indicated that the TLR4 complex achieved equilibrium within 80 ns and exhibited higher relative stability compared to the TLR2 complex. These computational structural profiles align closely with the observations from the in vitro receptor blockade assays. Neutralization of TLR4 substantially attenuated the 1022-SpaA V3-induced transcription of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. The convergence of theoretical binding dynamics and cellular functional data strongly supports the premise that TLR4, rather than TLR2, serves as a primary receptor mediating the immunogenicity of the vaccine construct in macrophages.

Intranasal immunization with 1022-SpaA V3 elicited potent respiratory mucosal immunity through prime-boost immunization, inducing significant sIgA elevation in BALF and NIS. This response displayed dose-responsive kinetics, saturating at 80 µg; however, 60 µg lower doses proved optimal for this dosing regimen. This immunization concurrently activated tissue-specific cellular immunity marked by B and CD4⁺T cell expansion in NALT and lungs alongside CD80⁺ dendritic cell and macrophage upregulation, which established localized antigen presentation. Furthermore, the coordinated engagement of lung-resident T cells and APCs created a self-sustaining immune network that enhanced mucosal memory retention without systemic activation, thereby intercepting pathogens at respiratory entry sites and enhancing host defense through targeted mucosal delivery [57].

In terms of protective efficacy, the vaccine demonstrated marked superiority. A 60 µg dose of 1022-SpaA V3 conferred complete protection against SS2 epidemic strains 05ZYH33 and CVCC606, while providing partial protection against the clinical isolate JZLQ022, revealing strain-dependent efficacy. This disparity may stem from key amino acid mutations in the SpaA and SSU05-1022 epitope regions of JZLQ022, which likely reduce antibody cross-reactivity. Similar observations in Streptococcus pneumoniae’s vaccine underscore epitope conservation as a central challenge in broad-spectrum vaccine design [58]. Future studies should integrate pan-strain conserved epitopes or employ dynamic epitope optimization strategies to enhance vaccine coverage breadth.

Traditional intramuscular vaccines often cause injection-site tissue damage, leading to degraded meat quality and economic losses during processing. In contrast, the needle-free intranasal delivery of 1022-SpaA V3 avoids muscle damage, thereby preserving carcass integrity and mitigating financial losses. Additionally, this non-invasive approach minimizes vaccination stress, enhancing overall animal welfare.

Compared to traditional inactivated vaccines, this candidate exhibits superior safety profiles. ToxinPred analysis confirmed the non-toxic nature of the V3 variant, with no observed febrile responses or histopathological damage in animal models. While a respiratory challenge would ideally mirror the intranasal vaccination route, our attempts to establish lethal infection via intranasal or intratracheal instillation were confounded by the extreme viscosity of concentrated bacterial inoculum, which prevented accurate and reproducible dosing. As far as we know, there is currently no SS2 infection model established through nasal drops or tracheal instillation to mimic natural infection in mice. The intraperitoneal route was therefore adopted as it reliably induces systemic bacteremia and meningitis—the ultimate causes of mortality—allowing a rigorous assessment of vaccine-induced systemic protection. However, it should be noted that the intraperitoneal challenge route used in this study does not fully replicate the natural aerosol transmission dynamics of SS2. Future work will establish an intranasal challenge model to evaluate the vaccine’s specific protective efficacy against bacterial meningitis. Additionally, the duration of vaccine-induced immune memory requires further validation through long-term experiments. While this study achieved promising results in murine models, the immunoprotective efficacy and safety in large animal models (e.g., swine) remain unassessed. Although immune simulations suggest memory B/T cell proliferation, long-term protection necessitates extended tracking. Furthermore, the adjuvant effect of the L7/L12 ribosomal protein may involve activation of TLR4 or NOD-like receptor pathways, requiring clarification via gene knockout models to pinpoint its mechanistic targets.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully developed the SS2 multi-epitope vaccine, 1022-SpaA V3, demonstrating significant potential for clinical application. Its innovation lies in three key aspects: intelligent computational immunoinformatics-based epitope screening, structural stability optimization, and a targeted mucosal delivery strategy. Experimental data confirm that the vaccine effectively activates a dual-axis mucosal-systemic immune response. This work provides a novel strategic approach for combating SS2 infection.
