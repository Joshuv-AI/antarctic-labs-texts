TAS-102 plus bevacizumab versus TAS-102 alone for metastatic colorectal cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Yuan, Simin Lou, Qingyang Liu, Jiaojiao Wei, Xun Sun, Leitao Sun, Lulin Yu, Guanjun Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious meta-analyses have shown TAS-102’s potential in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). Thus, we conducted a meta-analysis to investigate the efficacy and safety of TAS-102 combined with bevacizumab versus TAS-102 monotherapy in the treatment of mCRC.MethodsA thorough search in four databases was conducted from inception to August, 2024. Overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) were incorporated to explore the efficacy. Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis were then carried out to determine the sources of heterogeneity. Funnel plots and Egger’s test were employed to assess publication bias.ResultsA total of 9 studies with 1,509 participants were included after screening. Compared with monotherapy, TAS-102 plus bevacizumab demonstrated encouraging outcomes both in OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.52, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.39–0.70, p < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 0.49, 95% CI = 0.39–0.62, p < 0.001). And ORR (relative ratio [RR] = 3.28, 95% CI = 1.67–6.32, p < 0.001) and DCR (RR = 1.58, 95% CI = 1.26–1.97, p < 0.001) both favored the combination group. In terms of hematological toxicity, combination group had an increased incidence of neutropenia (all grade RR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.00-1.32; grade ≥ 3 RR = 1.38, 95% CI = 1.21–1.58) and thrombocytopenia (all grade RR = 1.44, 95% CI = 1.06–1.94).ConclusionsTAS-102 plus bevacizumab showed superior efficacy and acceptable safety over monotherapy, particularly in RAS-mutant patients, prior bevacizumab use, multiple metastases, and poorer performance status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04073-w.Keywords: TAS-102, Bevacizumab, Colorectal cancer, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent digestive tract malignant tumors. It has been reported that nearly 20% CRC patients tend to have metastases at diagnosis and approximately 30% patients undergoing resection may suffer from recurrence and metastases [1, 2]. For a long time, conventional chemotherapy along with targeted therapy, has constituted the standard treatment for metastatic CRC (mCRC), either as the first line or the second line interventions [3, 4]. However, limited antitumor efficacy and acquired resistance increasingly compromise survival outcomes, necessitating novel therapeutic approaches.

Trifluridine-tipiracil (FTD-TPI), also referred to as TAS-102, is an orally administered cytotoxic anti-tumor medication composed of FTD and TPI in a molar ratio of 1:0.5. The combination of FTD and TPI attenuates first-pass degradation in hepatic and intestinal tissues, leading to substantially improved oral bioavailability. The RECOURSE and TERRA study have demonstrated the promising efficacy of TAS-102 monotherapy in previously treated mCRC patients [5, 6]. Despite several studies suggesting an acceptable safety profile for TAS-102, it is still noteworthy that escalating FTD concentrations may lead to an increasing incidence of side effects, imposing a constraint on achieving the highest antitumor activity. Also, resistance to fluoropyrimidines remains a considerable barrier to effective treatment in refractory mCRC patients. Given the limited efficacy on later-line treatment, improved treatment strategy, like combination regimen, arose at the right time. Bevacizumab is expected to be one of the various combination options, with mouse xenograft models confirming its synergistic effect [7]. C-TASK FORCE and DANISH which focused on the efficacy and safety of TAS-102 in combination with bevacizumab, also reported superior survival benefits over TAS-102 monotherapy, coupled with acceptable adverse events (AEs) [8, 9].

Though previous studies had reported superior efficacy of the combination regimen, it still remained a challenge for medical oncologists to make selection of the most effective later-line regimen among numerous novel systematic treatments. Which specific population could benefit more from the strategy, and whether this combination could make a step forward in the struggle against drug resistance developed from prior bevacizumab usage? Thus, we conducted this meta-analysis aimed to gain a more comprehensive and in-depth knowledge of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab in treating mCRC patients.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10, 11]. The review protocol was prospectively registered at PROSPERO (CRD42023439142, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42023439142).

Search strategy

Two qualified investigators independently searched all eligible articles in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Wanfang databases from inception to August 4, 2024, to explore the clinical benefits and safety of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab versus TAS-102 monotherapy. Search strategies were fully detailed in Supplementary Table 1. For a more comprehensive search, reference lists of included researches were also searched for additional articles. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Population: patients with histopathologically confirmed mCRC; (2) Intervention: patients treated with TAS-102 plus bevacizumab; (3) Control: patients treated with TAS-102 monotherapy; (4) Outcome: primary outcomes included overall survival (OS), defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause; and progress-free survival (PFS) defined as time from treatment start to disease progression or death due to any cause. Secondary outcomes incorporated objective response rate (ORR), defined as proportion of patients with a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) to study treatment; and disease control rate (DCR), defined as proportion of patients with a CR, PR or stable disease (SD); (5) Study design: both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies (e.g. cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional) were included.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) single-arm studies; (2) studies in which patients received other chemotherapy or targeted drug therapy; (3) studies without full-text or available data (hazard ratio [HR], relative ratio [RR], odds ratio [OR], and corresponding 95% confidence intervals [CIs]); (4) review, case report, meta-analysis or laboratory-only research.

Data extraction

Two investigators (JY and SML) independently extracted complete and valid data from the full text of the included articles as follows: (1) study baseline characteristics: first author, year of publication, follow-up, trial design, line of treatment, phase, regimen and group; (2) study population: total number, number in each group, median age and age range; (3) study outcomes: HR with 95% CI for OS and PFS, RR with 95% CI for ORR and DCR, the incidence of all grade and grade ≥ 3 AEs. We also retrieved supplementary materials from each clinical study to ensure that no data had been missed.

Quality assessment

Two researchers (JY and SML) used the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool to independently assess the risk of bias in RCTs and classify them as high, low, or unclear risk of bias. As for non-randomized studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was adopted. Additionally, the credibility of each outcome was categorized into four levels (e.g., high, moderate, low, or very low), using the Grade of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tool [12], which evaluated the quality of outcomes based on factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency (I2 >50%), indirectness (inclusion criteria), imprecision (95% CI crossing null) and publication bias (Egger’s P < 0.1). GRADE assessment was conducted using the GRADEprofiler v3.6. Any discrepancies arising during the quality assessment process were rigorously resolved through discussion and consensus among three authors (JY, SML and LLY).

Statistical analysis

The primary objective of this meta-analysis was to compare OS and PFS between TAS-102 plus bevacizumab and TAS-102 monotherapy. Effect estimates for these were presented as a combination of HRs and 95% CIs. For binary outcomes, including ORR and DCR, RRs with corresponding 95% CIs were adopted. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We used Review Manager 5.3 and STATA 12 to summarize the data and generate the forest plots. In addition, the I2 test was performed to assess inter-study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was considered significant if I² >50% or P < 0.10, in which case a random-effects model was employed. Zero-event adjustment was implemented via Mantel-Haenszel random-effects models in STATA 12.0. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis was conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and assess its potential impact. Publication bias and sensitivity analysis were also performed to assess the robustness of the results.

Results

Search results and characteristics of included studies

A total of 908 records were identified through database searches. After removing 199 duplicates, 709 unique records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 623 ineligible studies. Among the remaining articles, 77 were excluded for one of the following reasons: no full text, single arm, review/report/other study designs, and no available results. Finally, 9 eligible studies were included. The detailed flow of the PRISMA flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

The PRISMA flow chart showing the selection of the articles for review

Totally, 1,509 mCRC patients were enrolled in our study, with 743 patients in combination group and 766 patients in monotherapy group. Eligible patients with mCRC had previous treated with a fluoropyrimidine, irinotecan, oxaliplatin, an anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibody, an anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody (for patients with rat sarcoma virus [RAS] wild-type). 855 (56.7%) patients were male and 654 (43.3%) were female. The age of participants ranged from 20 to 90 years across the included studies. The median follow-up time ranged from 6.8 to 32.2 months. Among nine eligible articles, two were RCTs and the remaining seven were observational studies. All studies reported identical dosing regimen: TAS-102 was administered orally, twice daily, at a starting dose of 35 mg/m2 of body-surface area, on days 1 through 5 and on days 8 through 12 every 28 days; and bevacizumab at a dose of 5 mg/kg of body weight, was administered intravenously on days 1 and 15. Five studies documented therapy lines for mCRC patients [9, 13–16], among which Nie [15] and Shibutani [16] explicitly reported that all patients had received at least 2 previous chemotherapeutic regimens. Although the remaining 4 studies failed to report the therapy-line in the baseline characteristics, all investigations highlighted prior chemotherapy exposure in the inclusion criteria [17–20]. Except for Shibutani’s study [16] which failed to report Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version specification, all 8 studies assessed efficacy (e.g., OS, PFS, ORR, DCR) using RECIST version 1.1. For safety assessment, Prager [13] and Yoshida [20] applied National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0, whereas the remaining studies adopted version 4.0. The baseline characteristics of the included studies were shown in Table 1. Further information regarding detailed therapy line, dose-reduction/withdrawal regimens, inclusion/exclusion criteria and efficacy/safety assessment methodologies were detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Efficacy assessment

Overall survival

Six articles were incorporated in OS analysis, with 2 RCTs and 4 observational studies. And the HR result from random effect model demonstrated a superior advantage in combination group (HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.70, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 65.4%, p= 0.013).

Forest plots of efficacy for mCRC patients treated with TAS-102 plus bevacizumab therapy versus monotherapy. Outcomes on (A) overall survival, (B) progress-free survival, (C) objective response rate and (D) disease control rate

Progression‐free survival

Totally, five studies reported a PFS outcome. And survival benefit in PFS remained in the combination group (HR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.62, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). In terms of heterogeneity, moderate heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 57.2%, p= 0.053).

Objective response rate

Regarding ORR, a significant superiority was observed for the combination group (RR: 3.25, 95% CI: 1.67 to 6.32) in Fig. 2C. And no heterogeneity was observed (I2= 0.0%, p = 0.821). Among eight studies, 37 patients in combination group and 10 in monotherapy group achieved CR or PR, resulting in a 5% and 1% ORR for each group respectively (Table 2).

Disease control rate

As for DCR, pooled HR also favored the combination group (RR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.26 to 1.97) (Fig. 2D). Subgroup analysis on overall survival

Overall, both observational-design group (HR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.27 to 0.76, p = 0.003) and RCT-design group (HR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.49 to 0.74, p < 0.001) confirmed the superior efficacy of combination group with prolonged OS. When stratified by age (cut-off=65 years), both elder patients (HR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.49 to 0.80, p < 0.001) and younger patients (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.57 to 0.88, p = 0.002) could benefit more from combination strategy. When stratified by gender, survival benefits were observed in both male (HR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.55 to 0.82, p< 0.001) and female group (HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.49 to 0.77, p < 0.001). In terms of Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status (PS), survival benefits were observed only in patients with ECOG PS=0 (HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.47 to 1.37, p = 0.427). As for tumor site, both left side (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.56 to 0.79, p < 0.001) and right side (HR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.41 to 0.75, p < 0.001) indicated therapeutic superiority of combination in OS. When it comes to RAS status, results showed that both mutant type group (HR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.53 to 0.78, p < 0.001) and wild type group (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.51 to 0.95, p = 0.025) could gain additional benefits from combination. Regarding number of metastatic sites, combination group favored patients with multiple metastases (≥3) in OS (HR: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.76, p < 0.001) outcome whereas for those with 1 or 2 metastases, no statistical significance was observed. Both the resection group (HR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.55–0.84.55.84, p < 0.001) and the non-resection group (HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.38 to 0.70, p < 0.001) demonstrated better survival outcome in combination treatment. While combination regimen prolonged OS in patients previously treated with bevacizumab (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.57 to 0.86, p = 0.001), no statistical significance was found in patients without previously bevacizumab history (HR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.18 to 1.19, p = 0.111).

Subgroup analysis on progress-free survival

In PFS subgroup, both RCT design and ROS design validated the better survival benefits of combination arm (RCT: HR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.37 to 0.53, p < 0.001; ROS: HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.77, p = 0.001).When stratified by age (cut-off = 65 years), both elder patients (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.42 to 0.66, p < 0.001) and younger patients (HR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.57, p < 0.001) could benefit more from combination strategy. When stratified by gender, survival benefits were observed in both male (HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.42 to 0.66, p< 0.001) and female group (HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.43 to 0.62, p < 0.001). In terms of ECOG PS, survival benefits were observed only in patients with ECOG PS=0 (HR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.53, p < 0.001). As for tumor site, both left side (HR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.40 to 0.66, p < 0.001) and right side (HR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.27 to 0.56, p < 0.001) indicated therapeutic superiority of combination in PFS. When it comes to RAS status, results showed that only patients with mutant type (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.44 to 0.61, p < 0.001) may have prolonged PFS. Regarding the number of metastatic sites, statistical significance could be observed in all groups. Both the resection group (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.78, p = 0.001) and the non-resection group (HR: 0.37, 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.55, p < 0.001) demonstrated better survival outcome in combination treatment. Positive results were observed in both groups, regardless of usage history of bevacizumab.

Safety assessment

The safety profile of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab was demonstrated in Table 4 and Supplementary Tables 3–4. Our analysis indicated that compared with monotherapy, combination group increased the incidence of neutropenia (all grade RR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.00 to 1.32; grade ≥ 3 RR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.21 to 1.58) and thrombocytopenia (all grade RR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.94). Notably, results showed a declined tendency in anemia when the combination strategy was applied (grade ≥ 3 RR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.50 to 0.96). Beyond hematological toxicity, elevated incidence of non-hematological toxicities such as proteinuria (all grade RR: 3.19, 95% CI: 1.68 to 6.06; grade ≥ 3 RR: 3.65, 95% CI: 1.07 to 12.38) and hypertension (all grade RR: 2.91, 95% CI: 1.74 to 4.86; grade ≥ 3 RR:5.29, 95% CI: 1.89 to 14.77) was also observed. Beyond that, no elevated risk of gastrointestinal toxicity was found.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Funnel plot, Begg’s test and Egger’s test were conducted to assess publication bias from each result in this study. In terms of funnel plot, slight asymmetric distribution was found in OS, PFS and DCR (Supplementary Figure 5). Then, Begg’s test and Egger’s test were further adopted. And no publication bias was observed in this study (Supplementary Figures 6–7).

To estimate the influence of single study on overall results of meta-analysis, we conducted sensitivity analysis as presented in Supplementary Figure 8. Based on the analysis, the pooled outcome was not notably influenced by single-study removal, which confirmed the rationality and reliability of our meta-analysis.

Quality assessment

Cochrane Collaboration’s tool was adopted to assess the quality of two RCTs included in this study. And both of them were rated as moderate (Supplementary Figure 9). As for the remaining seven observational studies, NOS was applied with five studies ranked as moderate and two studies ranked as high quality (Supplementary Table 5). According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence was moderate for PFS and ORR, but low for DCR, owing to inherent limitations in the study designs. OS was graded as very low certainty due to substantial heterogeneity (Supplementary Tables 6–7).

Discussion

Principle findings and mechanisms

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we involved 9 articles and 1,509 patients, comparing the efficacy and safety of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab versus TAS-102 monotherapy in mCRC patients. The results revealed that combination regimen exhibited clinical superiority over the monotherapy with respect to OS, and PFS in refractory mCRC patients. Despite survival benefits, encouraging outcomes of ORR and DCR were found in combination arm, reflecting pharmacokinetic benefits and biological activity of the combination therapy.

Mechanisms underlying the synergistic effect had been verified in previous studies. As reported, bevacizumab normalized tumor vasculature and improved intra-tumor blood supply, drug delivery and uptake, resulting in an accumulation of FTD and antitumor activity [21]. Thus, the addition of bevacizumab in TAS-102 dramatically enhanced the concentration of TAS-102 in tumor tissue, stimulating FTD to exhibit an optimal antitumor activity when maintaining a safe serum FTD concentration to control the incidence of AEs.

Interpretation of subgroup analysis

According to our meta-analysis, moderate heterogeneity was observed both in OS and PFS. Thus, further subgroup analyses correlated to patient’s characteristics, tumor features and previous treatment were conducted to bring more clinical benefit and maximize the survival benefits through stratifying patients. Surprisingly, several encouraging findings were observed in this study, which may act as potential predictors of prognosis.

First of all, whether the combination of TAS-102 and bevacizumab could maintain its superior efficacy in mCRC patients with different RAS status had always been a controversy. Notably, in our current study, statistical significance was observed in both OS and PFS outcome for patients with RAS-mutant type, highlighting superior efficacy of combination in treating this specific group. Routine clinical management also supported our finding: while EGFR antibodies was added to chemotherapy in patients with left-sided RAS wild-type, antiangiogenic therapy, like bevacizumab, was selected as an adjunctive strategy in patients with RAS-mutated. It is also noteworthy for the result was controversial with C-TASK FORCE, a single arm RCT, which suggested limited efficacy of the combination in RAS-mutant tumors. One of the possible explanations for this discrepancy might lie in the small sample size of this RCT. Additionally, RAS was not a single mutant entity, and its biological and clinical effects exhibited significant heterogeneity. Recent evidence confirms that distinct mutations in KRAS (exons 2–4) and NRAS (exons 2–3) differentially activate downstream signaling pathways, thereby modulating antiangiogenic therapy sensitivity [22]. For example, compared to the G12D mutation, the G13D mutation retained some of the GAP mediated GTP hydrolysis activity, thereby reducing the intensity of cell proliferation signals and potentially affecting drug response. Therefore, the limited efficacy of combination therapy observed in the C-TASK FORCE study in the RAS mutation subgroup could be related to the mutation spectrum of the enrolled patients (such as a higher proportion of highly active mutations such as G12V). Future studies should integrate next-generation sequencing to delineate mutation-specific profiles (codon variants, co-mutation patterns) with functional validation of therapeutic implications, enabling biomarker-driven precision therapy optimization.

Secondly, while previous studies maintained that the persistent suppression of VEGF incurred acquired resistance of antiangiogenic inhibitor [23], the clinical implications of prior bevacizumab exposure on subsequent salvage-line regimens remain incompletely characterized. All nine included studies permitted prior bevacizumab exposure (Supplementary Table 2), necessitating subgroup analyses by treatment history. In our study, patients with previous bevacizumab administration demonstrated prolonged OS and PFS with combination strategy, indicating sustained clinical efficacy of bevacizumab continuation in salvage-line mCRC. However, only Kotani [14] reported the interval since prior bevacizumab exposure—a critical parameter for evaluating the possibility of bevacizumab rechallenge—precluding further interpretation of prior exposure effects. Given the exploratory nature and potential confounding factors inherent to subgroup analyses (e.g., limited sample size, unadjusted baseline variables, and unmeasured confounders), these observations warranted validation in prospective studies designed specifically to test the hypothesis. Consequently, though prolonged OS and PFS were observed with combination therapy in patients previously treated with bevacizumab, the interpretation of these findings remained limited by inadequate documentation of prior bevacizumab exposure across studies. Key details—such as interval between prior bevacizumab use and the start of the trial, therapy strategy (e.g., dosage, combination or monotherapy), and reasons for discontinuation—were not explicitly reported. This lack of detailed reporting introduced substantial uncertainty into the assessment of prior antiangiogenic therapy’s impact on treatment outcomes, highlighting the need for better documentation and standardization in defining prior bevacizumab exposure in future studies.

Furthermore, when stratified by the number of metastatic sites, prolonged OS and PFS were observed in patients with ≥3 metastatic sites, whereas for patients with 1 or 2 metastatic sites, no consistent result was found. This suggested that patients with more extensive disease burden could benefit more in the combination therapy. The underlying mechanism could be related to the synergistic effect of combination treatment, which contributed to higher concentration of tumor toxicity in blood and thus demonstrated a more pronounced suppression of tumor growth in patients with multiple metastases. However, given the small sample size and retrospective nature of the included studies, the generalized application of this result was limited.

Additionally, in current study, survival benefits appeared to be primarily observed in patients with ECOG PS=0, with no significant improvement seen in those with ECOG PS≥1. Consistent with our findings, a retrospective study conducted by Zeng and colleagues [24], also observed a significant association between higher ECOG PS and poor PFS (HR=2.447, 95% CI=1.077–3.151.077.151). These results suggest that ECOG PS may serve as an independent prognostic factor in mCRC patients, and could help in risk stratification and treatment selection. Interestingly, Lago’s study observed a longer interval from ECOG PS worsening in the combination group, implying a potential delay in functional decline—a clinically relevant insight that may reflect preserved physiological reserve and possible benefits for subsequent treatment lines [25]. However, although subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the impact of ECOG PS, the predominantly retrospective nature of the included studies limited the ability to fully adjust for confounding variables. Therefore, caution was warranted when interpreting survival outcomes, as patients with better performance status or fewer comorbidities could be more likely to receive combination regimens, potentially leading to overestimation of therapeutic efficacy.

Beyond that, survival benefits of the combination were observed regardless of age, gender, primary tumor site or resection.

Toxicity assessment

Toxicity has always been a major concern when comes to a new combination strategy. Fortunately, in current study, TAS-102 plus bevacizumab was associated with manageable toxicities in clinical practice setting compared with TAS-102 monotherapy.

In terms of hematological toxicity, a higher incidence rate of neutropenia and thrombocytopenia was observed in the combination arm. Researches indicated that adding VEFG inhibitors to chemotherapy could increase the risk of neutropenia through significantly delaying leukocyte recovery by acting on specific bone marrow hematopoietic progenitor cells [26]. Moreover, prolonged exposure to TAS-102 and bevacizumab for the purpose of maximizing therapeutic efficacy elevated the concentration of phosphorylated FTD in bone marrow cells, thus increasing hematological toxicities. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggested that neutropenia paradoxically predicts improved survival outcomes and might serve as a predictor of the efficacy of TAS-102 (with or without bevacizumab) [27–30]. This requires clinicians to pay more attention to the frequency as well as duration of drug administration [31]. G-CSF intervention, drug reduction or supportive treatment should be implemented timely to avoid AE-related drug withdrawal which may dramatically affect therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, while a positive outcome was observed in the incidence of severe anemia, this should be interpreted with caution due to limited sample size. Also, dose modifications were more common in the combination group, which may influence the assessment of severe adverse effects.

As for non-hematological toxicity, there was a numerically higher incidence of proteinuria and hypertension in the combination group. This may stem from the accumulation of angiogenesis inhibitor-related toxicity as a certain proportion of mCRC patients have received bevacizumab in previous treatment line.However, these bevacizumab-induced side effects can be also well managed with extensive experience of bevacizumab application in clinical setting.

Sources of heterogeneity

Several factors contributed to observed heterogeneity in the included studies. Firstly, significant variations in baseline characteristics in patients likely contribute, including differential eligibility regarding prior surgical resection (e.g., unresectable patients in Prager [13] and Pfeiffer [9] versus mixed populations in other studies), treatment line (e.g., ≥3-line in Nie [15] and Shibutani [16], ≤2-line in Prager [13], and non-first-line population in other studies), and performance status thresholds (ECOG PS 0–2 in Chida [17] and Kotani [14], 0–1 in Fujii [18] and Prager [13], and mixed populations in other studies), directly influencing patient prognosis and treatment tolerance.

Second, the frequency of disease and safety assessment was important in clinical settings. More frequent assessment facilitated earlier detection of AEs, especially at their controllable stage, enabling timely intervention to prevent the incidence of severe AEs, and thus reducing dose reductions or treatment interruptions. Among 9 including studies, Shibutani [16] performed evaluations every 8–10 weeks, whereas most studies assessed AEs once every 28 days (one treatment cycle). Notably, Nose [19] and Pfeiffer [9] implemented biweekly assessment for nadir hematology. The frequency of assessment substantially influenced clinical outcomes.

Third, strategies for dose reduction diverged substantially. Pfeiffer [9] adopted a stepwise TAS-102 dose reduction of 5 mg/m², whereas Fujii [18] and Nose [19] reduced by 10 mg/m² in response to severe AEs. Furthermore, the criteria for AE grading differed, with Prager [13] and Yoshida [20] applying National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) v5.0 and other studies using NCI-CTCAE v4.0. This potentially affected the reported incidence and severity grading of specific events.

Finally, inherent variability in clinician experience regarding AE management, particularly decisions leading to dose reduction or discontinuation, introduces an additional and inevitable source of heterogeneity.

Current stance and future perspectives

This study demonstrated therapeutic superiority in combination strategy, and provided robust evidence supporting clinical utility of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab in mCRC. Crucially, our comprehensive subgroup analysis addressed a significant gap in previous literature by identifying key sources of heterogeneity and delineating specific patient populations who most likely to derive survival benefit from combination therapy. Factors such as RAS-mutant status, prior exposure to bevacizumab, number of metastases, and ECOG PS emerged as potential predictive and prognostic markers. While further validation in larger cohorts was warranted due to current sample size limitations, these findings offer clinicians tangible guidance for personalized treatment selection, enabling a potential approach to salvage therapies in this heavily pre-treated population.

Cost-effectiveness analysis should also be considered in clinical decision-making. In China, the addition of bevacizumab to TAS-102 raised the monthly cost from ≈US3,400 to ≈US5,900, yet the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of ≈US9,100 per life-year gained remains below the willingness-to-pay threshold (3× gross domestic product [GDP] per capita) [32]. Strategic use in RAS-mutant patients with high metastatic burden could reduce pharmacy costs by 35% while retaining 80% of the population-level survival benefit [33]. As treatment paradigms evolving, molecular stratification could refine the combination’s role—particularly in microsatellite-stable (MSS) disease after immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) or in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative tumors where anti-HER2 regimens take precedence.

Although optimistic outcomes were observed in present study, superiority of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab was far from satisfaction. The dilemma every mCRC patients were now confronted with was the consequently undermining therapeutic efficacy in later-line treatment. Based on this, the introduction of other novel combination strategies (e.g., regorafenib or panitumumab), could offer promising alternatives. Beyond the optimal drug selection for specific populations, consensus has not yet been reached among existing evidence on the order sequence of drug administration for third-line and subsequent lines of treatment. Optimizing the sequence of drug administration in subsequent treatments to maximize potential benefits remains an urgent issue.

In addition to the aforementioned mechanisms of vascular normalization and enhanced drug delivery, the latest research [34] suggested that the expression levels of nucleotide metabolism related enzymes such as thymidine phosphorylase (TP), dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (ENT1), and CC (CDA) in tumor tissues may independently predict the clinical benefits of TAS-102. Specifically, low expression of TP and high expression of DPD are both associated with reduced FTD phosphorylation and decreased deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) embedding efficiency; Upregulation of ENT1 is believed to promote FTD entry into tumor cells, thereby enhancing cytotoxic effects. In addition, dynamic monitoring of KRAS mutation abundance changes using circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) may also serve as a non-invasive indicator for early efficacy evaluation. If these molecular features can be integrated into randomized controlled designs in the future, it is expected to achieve precise stratification based on the "drug metabolism genome" three-dimensional scale, further expand the therapeutic window of TAS-102 combined with bevacizumab, and reduce ineffective exposure.

Strengths and weaknesses

To our understanding, three prior meta-analyses had compared therapeutic regimens identical to our study (Supplementary Table 8) [35–37]. Aligning with these earlier reports, our analysis confirmed the superior efficacy of the combination therapy over monotherapy. Our study extended the existing evidence in three key aspects: (1) our study incorporated the most comprehensive trials (n=9 versus prior range n=3 to 7) and largest population (n=1,509 versus prior range n=276 to 1,182); (2) our study conducted extensive subgroup analyses (e.g., RAS-mutant type, prior usage of bevacizumab, number of metastases and ECOG PS) to elucidate heterogeneity sources; (3) our study performed a more comprehensive AE analysis (e.g., all grade AEs and grade≥3 AEs, hematological toxicity and non-hematological toxicity). Notably, our work corroborated the conclusions of the relatively recent and comprehensive meta-analysis by Moraes [35] regarding the efficacy and safety of the combination regimen. However, our updated findings substantiated and strengthened the robustness of these conclusions through a larger sample size and in-depth exploratory subgroup analyses that revealed potential treatment-benefiting populations.

The followed limitations merit consideration when interpreting the results. First, the lack of sufficient RCTs introduced inherent risks of bias and unmeasured confounding, potentially compromising the validity of findings. This methodological constraint substantially limited the generalizability and robustness of our findings. Second, difference in therapy line was observed (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2), which precluded further stratified analyses by therapy line and obscured potential differential treatment efficacy in certain population. Third, the impact of combination therapy on quality of life (QoL) was not evaluated due to limited data, representing a significant evidence gap for palliative care decision-making. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis was performed to reduce potential bias, however, some key covariates (e.g., baseline carcinoembryonic antigen [CEA] levels, metastatic burden, interval from prior bevacizumab exposure) could not be reassessed due to limited data. Additionally, heterogeneity in the assessment of AE profiles must be considered, including discrepancies in AE grading criteria (NCI-CTCAE v4.0 versus v5.0), frequency of AE assessment (28 days versus 8–10 weeks), and non-uniform dose reduction or discontinuation strategies (e.g., TAS-102 reduced in 5 mg/m² versus 10 mg/m² increments), which could introduce inherent bias. Moreover, although statistical methods were applied to address potential biases, several sources of bias remained unavoidable. Notably, patients with a more favorable ECOG PS status were likely to receive TAS-102 plus bevacizumab treatment, potentially leading to selection bias, while the reliance solely on published studies could have resulted in publication bias, and an overestimation of treatment efficacy. To mitigate these limitations, further large-scale prospective cohorts with standardized therapy-line documentation were desperately needed to provide a verified answer and lay a solid foundation for clinical settings. Additionally, studies incorporating comprehensive biomarker profiling were essential to elucidate baseline patient characteristics.

Conclusion

In general, superior efficacy of TAS-102 plus bevacizumab over TAS-102 monotherapy have been demonstrated, along with a favorable safety profile. The main concern lies in the higher incidence of neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Patients with RAS-mutant type, prior usage of bevacizumab, a greater number of metastases and a lower ECOG PS may benefit from the combination regimen.
