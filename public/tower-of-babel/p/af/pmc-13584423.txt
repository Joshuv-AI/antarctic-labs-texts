The readiness–anxiety paradox in artificial intelligence adoption among nursing students: a multidimensional regression analysis
Authors: Zeynep Kızılcık Özkan, Semra Eyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study examined the relationship between medical artificial intelligence readiness and AI-related anxiety among nursing students following surgical nursing education.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted with 252 nursing students from two universities who had completed surgical diseases nursing courses. Data were collected between February and July 2024 using the Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale and the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale. Group comparisons, correlation analyses, and hierarchical regression analysis models were performed.ResultsOur findings indicate a notable “awareness–apprehension paradox”: higher ability (technical readiness) was associated with lower learning anxiety (B = − 0.25, p < .01), whereas higher ethics (ethical readiness) was associated with greater concerns regarding job replacement (B = 0.41, p < .001) and anxiety. Female students had significantly higher anxiety scores, and regular AI use was associated with greater readiness. AI utilization and sociotechnical perceptions together accounted for variance in readiness outcomes. Higher levels of medical AI readiness were not uniformly associated with lower anxiety; instead, increased readiness coexisted with elevated concerns in specific anxiety dimensions, indicating a complex association between technological preparedness and psychological adaptation.ConclusionThe results suggest that developing technical competence alone may not be sufficient to align with confident technology adoption. They further indicate that, in the absence of corresponding organizational safeguards, ethical awareness may function more as a “cognitive demand” than as a resource. The study adds to the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) framework by illustrating the dual nature of AI readiness. Nursing education programs may therefore benefit from integrating ethical reflection, critical technological awareness, and psychological preparedness alongside AI skill development to support sustainable implementation of AI-supported clinical practice.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Nursing education, Surgical nursing, Technology readiness, Anxiety, Healthcare innovation, Healthcare education

Background

The expanding role of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare services [1, 16] has been reflected in the surgical process, and it has been reported that AI contributes to the development and improvement of existing surgical techniques [39]. It has been shown to benefit patients in many ways, such as providing individualised treatment, which is particularly important in the surgical process, reducing medical errors that can occur during surgery, supporting patient self-management and improving health outcomes [21, 52]. It is predicted that the amount of health-related savings in the field of health is 150 billion dollars, underlining the integration of Artificial intelligence in the healthcare sector [29]. The development of modern artificial intelligence in healthcare has a significant impact on nurses [12]. They serve as both educators and users of AI-based technologies, playing a crucial role in integrating these new technologies [29, 37]. Nevertheless, there exists a necessity for the establishment of guidelines that are pertinent to the role of the clinical nurse [12], and it is evident that there is a necessity to comprehend nurses' attitudes towards the matter [37]. Increased exposure to AI technologies is generally assumed to enhance professional readiness and facilitate technology adoption. However, emerging evidence suggests that technological preparedness does not necessarily eliminate psychological concerns related to AI use. It has been reported that nurses have expressed concerns about the potential impact of AI applications on their roles and the quality of care provided. These concerns include the risk of job loss, the lack of human input in decision-making processes, the inability of AI to provide holistic and humanistic care, uncertainty about liability in the event of negative outcomes, and ethical issues [21]. A recent meta-analysis examining the attitudes and knowledge of medical, dental and nursing students regarding AI highlights the importance of considering students' views in order to provide more data and gain insight into how AI can be effectively integrated into the educational process [5]. A study of 2,167 students pursuing various health professions in Canada revealed that the majority held a favourable viewpoint regarding the growing role of AI in their discipline. Additionally, the students expressed a desire for fundamental AI knowledge to be incorporated into their curriculum [49]. However, the fact that technology can cause anxiety in students' lives cannot be denied [19]. The thought that artificial intelligence may get out of control brings AI anxiety to high levels [1]. The main concerns are the obstacles that may be experienced in communicating with artificial intelligence, technical problems in collecting accurate medical data, data privacy of patients, and the fact that technology companies developing AI programs prioritize the profitability of their business rather than human life [39].

This developing tension underscores a significant yet inadequately examined concern in nursing education: the question of whether heightened preparedness for medical AI alleviates anxiety or, conversely, exacerbates it. The knowledge derived from this process should assist in the creation of an effective curriculum.

Prior research has predominantly analyzed AI readiness or AI-related anxiety in isolation, frequently concentrating on general healthcare or multidisciplinary student groups. Comprehending this relationship is particularly significant, as anxiety towards AI may impact students' readiness to adopt clinical technologies, even when they have sufficient technical competence. Without addressing both psychological adaptation and educational preparedness, efforts to integrate AI into nursing curricula may remain insufficient [12]. There is a scarcity of studies that have concurrently explored the interaction of these constructs within the realm of surgical nursing education, where the integration of technology is anticipated to progress at the fastest rate. Therefore, one could assert that nursing students, who will eventually transition into the surgical nurses of the future, need more comprehensive information about AI and its related products, which are anticipated to be increasingly employed in surgical settings and operations.

In this study, the terms “awareness–apprehension paradox” and “readiness paradox” are used to describe related but distinct aspects of students’ responses to artificial intelligence in nursing education. The awareness–apprehension paradox refers to a situation in which increased awareness of AI’s potential benefits is accompanied by heightened concern, uncertainty, or anxiety about its ethical, educational, professional, and practical implications. This interpretation is consistent with research showing that AI anxiety involves concerns about learning AI, job replacement, sociotechnical blindness, and AI configuration [55]. The readiness paradox, in contrast, refers more specifically to the coexistence of perceived preparedness or willingness to engage with AI and simultaneous anxiety or hesitation toward its use. In healthcare education, AI readiness is commonly understood as students’ perceived cognitive, ability-related, visionary, and ethical preparedness to use AI in medical contexts [25]. However, emerging evidence suggests that readiness and anxiety may coexist rather than operate as simple opposites. For example, Güven et al. [20] found a significant negative relationship between medical AI readiness and AI anxiety among medical students, indicating that higher readiness may reduce anxiety but does not necessarily eliminate it. Therefore, in the present manuscript, the awareness–apprehension paradox is used as the broader concept describing the coexistence of knowledge or awareness and concern, whereas the readiness paradox is used as its more specific expression in relation to students’ preparedness for AI adoption. These terms are used to clarify how nursing students may simultaneously recognize the value of AI and experience anxiety about its educational and professional consequences.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between medical artificial intelligence readiness and AI-related anxiety among nursing students after surgical nursing education. Moreover, demographic characteristics and previous AI usage were examined to gain a better understanding of the factors that influence this readiness-anxiety dynamic. By identifying how preparedness and anxiety coexist, this study seeks to inform curriculum-level strategies that support sustainable and confident AI adoption in future surgical nursing practice. Methods

Study design

The present study employs a descriptive and cross-sectional research design. This study examined AI anxiety of nursing students and their level of preparation for medical AI. The STROBE guidelines were followed during the study.

Study setting and sampling

Data for the cross-sectional and descriptive study were collected between February and July 2024. Nursing students were selected from two universities' faculties of health science nursing departments for this study, employing the convenience sampling method (N = 336). In accordance with this population, the sample was determined using power analysis during the research planning phase. The minimum number of people to be included in the sample was calculated as 154 by power analysis using the G. Power 3.1.9.4 program with an effect size of 0.3, a confidence level of 95% and a power ratio of 80%. In light of the potential for missing or incomplete data, a total of 260 structured questionnaires that met the specified inclusion criteria were distributed. Nevertheless, 8 of them were excluded from the analysis due to missing or incomplete data, thus resulting in a total of 252 participants included in the study. A total of 185 women (73.4%) and 67 men (26.6%) participated in the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following criteria are used to select nursing students: (1) Enrolment in the nursing programmes of the university; (2) ≥ 18 years of age; (3) Successful completion of the surgical diseases nursing course in the fall or spring semester of the 2023–2024 academic year; (4) The Turkish reading and understanding ability. The exclusion criteria for nursing students were as follows: (1) Having not undertaken the surgical diseases nursing course at the time of the study, or having failed the course due to absenteeism; (2) Enrolling in other departments of the university other than nursing.

Data collection

The researchers informed the students who met the inclusion criteria in both oral and written form about the nature of the research and obtained informed consent from the students in the classroom setting. The students were required to respond to the questions posed in the information Form, the Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale and the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale. The average time required for data collection was 15 to 20 min. The data were collected between February 2024 and July 2024.

Data source

The information form

The form, developed by the researchers in accordance with the existing literature on the subject, inquires about the age, gender, and use of AI applications [16]. In this study, "the use of artificial intelligence" refers to students' self-reported use of AI-based digital tools for academic, informational, and learning purposes. Instead of focusing on the direct clinical application of AI in patient care, the use of AI is conceptualized as the general educational use of AI tools (such as Siri, Google Bard, Chat GPT, Google Assistant, etc.) for purposes like information searching, homework assignments, creating study materials, and reviewing health or clinical examples.

The Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale (AIAS)

The scale, whose Turkish version was tested for validity and reliability by Akkaya et al. [1], was originally developed by Wang and Wang [55]. It is a 5-point Likert scale where 'Strongly disagree' is scored 1 and 'Strongly agree' is scored 5. The original version has 21 items grouped into four factors, but the Turkish version includes 16 items organized into four sub-dimensions. The cut-off point for the scale is 48 points. The sub-dimensions are learning, job replacement, sociotechnical blindness, and artificial intelligence configuration. The overall reliability of the scale is 0. 96. In this study, the internal reliability was found to be 0. 929, as shown in Table 2.

The Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale (MAIRS)

The 5-point Likert-type scale is used to measure how ready people are for using artificial intelligence in medicine. The score ranges from 22 to 110, with each item giving 1 point for 'strongly disagree' and 5 points for 'strongly agree' [25]. A higher score means a higher level of readiness. The scale has 22 questions and is divided into four parts: cognition, ability, vision, and ethics. In the original study, the reliability of the whole scale was 0. 877, and for the four factors, it was 0. 830, 0. 770, 0. 723, and 0. 632 respectively [51]. In this study, the reliability for the full scale was found to be 0. 957, and for the four factors, 0. 899, 0. 934, 0. 838, and 0. 862 respectively (see Table 2).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (Armonk, NY, USA). To determine whether the data adhered to a normal distribution, we employed the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and examined the skewness and kurtosis values. Data was deemed to be normally distributed if the values resided within the range of + 1.5 to-1.5 on the total scale [46]. For the AI anxiety scale, the skewness-kurtosis coefficients were (−0.367; −0.185), while for the Medical AI Readiness Scale, they were (−0.575; 1.088). We conducted t-tests and ANOVAs to investigate the relationship between scale scores and socio-demographic factors. To clarify the model building procedure, hierarchical regression analysis was conducted based on theoretical relevance and previous empirical findings. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were performed to examine the predictive relationships between demographic characteristics, AI utilization, medical AI readiness dimensions, and AI-related anxiety subdimensions. Variables were entered into the regression models in three sequential steps to evaluate their incremental explanatory contribution. In Step 1, demographic variables (age and gender) were entered as control variables. In Step 2, AI utilization status was added to evaluate the role of prior exposure to AI. In Step 3, the four MAIRS dimensions were included to examine their independent predictive associations with AI-related anxiety dimensions. Standardized beta coefficients, R2 values, and ΔR2 changes were reported for each model. Prior to interpreting the regression results, key assumptions were examined, including linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, normal distribution of residuals, and multicollinearity. Variance inflation factor and tolerance values were inspected to confirm that multicollinearity was not a concern. The significance level was set at α = 0. 05, and results with p < 0. 05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Principles of Medical Research. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee. The institutional permission was obtained from the Faculty of Health Sciences of both universities. Prior to the study, permission to use the scales was obtained from the authors who developed the scales and conducted the validity and reliability tests. Informed consent was obtained from the participants who were informed that the data would be used for scientific purposes and would remain confidential.

The role of demographics and AI experience

In Step 1 (demographics), age was not significantly associated with any of the outcome variables. Gender, however, was significantly related to learning anxiety (β = 0.18, p < 0.05) and job replacement concerns (β = 0.21, p < 0.01), indicating gender-based differences in affective and occupational responses to AI.

In Step 2, utilisation of AI showed significant negative associations with learning anxiety (β = − 0.22, p < 0.01), sociotechnical blindness (β = − 0.19, p < 0.05), and concerns about AI configuration (β = − 0.24, p < 0.01). These results indicate that greater exposure to AI is associated with lower anxiety and fewer sociotechnical misconceptions, which may reflect desensitisation or competence-building among individuals who engage more frequently with AI.

Impact of MAIRS dimensions (Step 3)

Integrating the MAIRS dimensions in Step 3 was associated with a substantial increase in the explanatory power of the models, with ΔR2 values ranging between 0.19 and 0.26 (all p < 0.001). A key finding concerned the role of ethics (ethical readiness), which emerged as the most reliable and significant correlate of all the measured outcomes. Specifically, it showed positive associations with learning anxiety (β = 0.34, p < 0.001), concerns about job replacement (β = 0.41, p < 0.001), and sociotechnical blindness (β = 0.27, p < 0.01). These results indicate that a more sophisticated ethical consciousness is associated with greater awareness of the risks and secondary effects related to AI.

The data also indicated positive associations between “cognition (cognitive readiness)” and both “job replacement” concerns (β = 0.26, p < 0.01) and “sociotechnical blindness” (β = 0.31, p < 0.01), suggesting that deeper cognitive engagement with AI frameworks co-occurs with a heightened perception of broader structural and organisational consequences. Conversely, “ability (technical readiness)” was negatively associated with stress rooted in uncertainty, showing inverse relationships with “learning anxiety” (β = − 0.25, p < 0.01) and concerns about “AI configuration” (β = − 0.29, p < 0.01). Additionally, a slight negative association was observed between “vision (clinical readiness)” and anxieties related to “configuration” (β = − 0.18, p < 0.05).

Ultimately, the final models accounted for a significant proportion of the variance across the dependent variables (R2 ranging from 0.29 to 0.38), with the model concerning “job replacement” showing the highest explained variance (R2 = 0.38). Taken together, these results underscore the nuanced, multifaceted nature of AI readiness: while ethical and cognitive attributes are associated with a more critical awareness, technical proficiency is most consistently linked to lower levels of anxiety-related responses.

Discussion

Investigating the relationships among nursing students following surgical nursing education, this research explores the association between readiness for medical artificial intelligence (AI) and AI-related anxiety, highlighting a complex interplay between technological competence and psychological responses.

Principal findings

The integration of AI into surgical procedures is becoming increasingly common, with expectations that it will become a widespread element in future surgical practice. Understanding the attitudes of surgical nurses—key healthcare professionals in perioperative care—and nursing students preparing for these roles toward the incorporation of AI into clinical settings is essential. Accordingly, this study aimed to examine levels of anxiety and preparedness among nursing students pursuing careers in surgical nursing in relation to AI.

The findings indicated that students reported moderate levels of AI-related anxiety alongside above-average readiness for medical AI. While several studies have observed high anxiety and relatively low readiness among healthcare students [12, 20, 59], other research aligns with the present results, reporting moderate anxiety levels [15, 30, 47], and moderate to slightly above-average readiness for AI integration [22, 51]. Previous studies have also found that nursing students [59], midwifery students [22] and operating room nurses [15] frequently report elevated scores in the “sociotechnical blindness” dimension of AI anxiety. Similarly, the highest readiness scores are often observed in the “Ability” sub-dimension of medical AI readiness [2, 8, 12, 34, 48, 57]. Consistent with prior research, current data show a pattern where students express confidence in their ability to use and communicate about AI applications with patients while simultaneously experiencing heightened sociotechnical anxieties. Elevated scores in “sociotechnical blindness” dimension —previously associated with concerns about potential impacts on professional and social relationships—support this observation [59]. This pattern suggests that even technically proficient students may remain cautious about how AI integration could influence interpersonal dynamics within clinical environments.

Demographic influences

The study identified a significant negative association between student age and the “Cognition” and “Ethics” dimensions of medical AI readiness. This finding indicates that older students tend to report lower scores in adapting to legal and ethical aspects of AI use in clinical contexts [51]. Similar patterns have been observed in other research, where individuals under 22 years of age demonstrated higher ethics (ethical readiness) scores than older students [56]. However, findings across studies are inconsistent. For example, some research found no significant association between age and ethics (ethical readiness) among nursing students [57], and age was not significantly related to AI readiness among dental students [22]. Several studies have not specifically examined the role of age in AI readiness [12, 17, 34, 48, 51], highlighting the need for further investigation to clarify this relationship. It is possible that differences in technological exposure and familiarity with digital systems may partially explain variations in AI readiness across age groups, as suggested by prior work [23].

Gender differences have also been observed in relation to AI-related anxiety. Female students reported significantly higher anxiety levels than male students, particularly in the sub-dimensions of "Sociotechnical blindness" and "AI configuration". These differences may be associated with perceptions that AI could negatively affect social relationships [59], or with assumptions about the opacity or unpredictability of AI systems [23]. Similar gender differences have been reported across various professional fields, including teaching [50], tourism [10], and healthcare [9, 36] where women often report higher levels of sociotechnical anxiety regarding AI systems.

Some studies also indicate that male students demonstrate higher awareness of AI-related issues than female students [24].

The study demonstrated that students' levels of readiness for medical artificial intelligence were not statistically significantly influenced by gender. Gencer and Gencer [17] found that male students demonstrated a higher level of readiness than female students, specifically in the "Cognition" sub-dimension. Similar findings have been reported in studies involving dental students [8, 22] and resident physicians [44], where readiness levels did not significantly differ between male and female participants.

Our findings come from Turkish nursing programs and may not generalize to other cultural or educational settings. Given the context of the study, current conclusions should also be taken into account in the context of Turkish culture and education. Recent studies suggest that gender differences in AI anxiety may vary across countries and educational contexts. For example, [40] found that women reported higher AI anxiety and lower perceived AI knowledge and AI use in an Italian sample, whereas Asio and Sardina [6] reported that gender did not significantly moderate the relationship between AI self-competency and AI anxiety among higher education students in the Philippines. Similarly, Sivenas [42] found small and non-significant gender differences in AI anxiety among Greek secondary school students. In Türkiye, students’ familiarity with technology and AI-related knowledge may be particularly important, as Kaya et al. [26] showed that computer use and knowledge about AI positively predicted attitudes toward AI, while AI learning anxiety negatively predicted such attitudes. Cultural factors may also play a role. Turkey has a centralized education system where curriculum, textbooks and assessment frameworks are largely determined at national level [27], and exam-oriented education culture can shape the perception of students and educators of the benefits, risks and legitimacy of AI tools in learning environments. Türkiye has been characterized by relatively higher power distance and collectivist tendencies [7], and high power-distance educational cultures may position teachers as authoritative figures, potentially shaping students’ autonomy, trust, and willingness to engage critically with AI-supported learning [33]. This situation has also been reflected in nursing and health education fields in Türkiye. Therefore, Turkish students’ technological familiarization and cultural attitudes toward authority may influence both AI readiness and AI anxiety, and these factors should be considered when applying the results to other educational settings.

According to the analysis results, the significant difference observed in favor of University A in the total MAIRS score and ‘Ability,' and ‘Ethics’ sub-dimensions scores is supported by current findings in the literature. The literature has shown that the lack of institutional policies and guidance principles directly affects students' ethical concerns regarding AI, and this constitutes a separate psychometric factor [4]. In this context, the high averages at University A can be considered a reflection of the academic climate provided by the institution and its access to AI tools. Furthermore, the institutional differentiation observed in the AIAS sub-dimensions mean scores of the “Job Replacement” and "Sociotechnical blindness” is consistent with the study conducted by [18], which shows that AI perception is shaped not only at the individual level but also by the institution's academic discipline-focused approaches and level of technological interaction; and that institutional differences become more pronounced, especially in non-technical fields [18]. Findings favoring University A suggest that students at this university exhibit a more flexible cognitive attitude towards change, or that institutional academic experiences reduce the level of "Sociotechnical blindness”. The literature reports that the use of AI and the educational environment can increase academic self-efficacy, but do not always affect general anxiety or basic cognitive processes to the same extent. As emphasized in the literature, institutional investments transform skills and adoption dimensions more, while ethical perceptions are selectively shaped by institutional policies; and cognitive awareness is selectively shaped by department-based experiences [61].

Utilisation of AI and anxiety: a technostress perspective

The findings further indicate that students who actively used AI technologies were more likely to report significantly higher readiness levels compared to those who did not. This pattern is consistent with technostress adaptation models, which propose that increased familiarity with technology is associated with lower uncertainty and perceived complexity, and with reduced anxiety related to technological use [13, 32]. In high‑technology clinical environments such as operating rooms, repeated exposure to digital systems may be associated with greater perceived control and lower uncertainty. From the perspective of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), direct interaction with AI technologies tends to be linked with higher perceived ease of use and lower affective resistance toward technological systems [13].

Moreover, AI use was associated with lower “sociotechnical blindness”, suggesting that experimential engagement with AI systems may be linked to a better understanding of how these technologies intersect with clinical workflows, decision‑making processes, and patient monitoring systems. These competencies are particularly relevant within surgical safety cultures. Similar associations have been reported in previous studies. For example, students who had taken AI‑related courses reported higher readiness levels than those who had not participated in such courses [48], and AI knowledge has been identified as an important correlate of AI anxiety among midwifery and nursing students [12]. Likewise, Hammoudi Halat et al. [22] reported significant relationships between AI knowledge, perceptions of AI, and readiness levels among students [22]. Consistent with these findings, students who reported using AI in the present study demonstrated significantly higher scores in the “Cognition”, “Ability”, and “Vision” dimensions of the MAIRS scale. Students who do not utilize AI may therefore be less likely to feel confident in explaining AI applications, using them appropriately, or differentiating between potential opportunities and risks associated with AI technologies. Previous studies have also shown that students’ motivation, self‑efficacy, and satisfaction with technological systems are associated with their readiness, anxiety, and attitudes toward AI applications, particularly in e‑learning contexts [3].

Furthermore, Demir Kaymak et al. [12] reported that AI knowledge was a significant correlate of AI anxiety among midwifery and nursing students. This pattern may be consistent with two distinct hypotheses. Firstly, individuals with limited exposure to artificial intelligence may be more likely to experience anxiety regarding this field of technology. Secondly, individuals with in‑depth knowledge and experience of AI may also be more likely to report anxiety, potentially due to heightened perceptions of the threats and risks associated with the technology [23]. Qualitative studies have likewise indicated that students often hold both positive and negative perceptions regarding AI technologies [58].

Future research should also consider long-term design to explore how AI readiness and AI-related anxiety develop over time. Preparation and anxiety should not be considered as fixed features because they may change by repeated exposure to artificial intelligence tools, institutional guidance, artificial intelligence literacy training and a greater understanding of the ethical and educational uses of artificial intelligence. Recent research has shown that there is a significant negative relationship between the medical readiness of AI and the fear of AI among students, indicating that those who are most prepared may be less worried about using AI [20]. Furthermore, it was found that anxiety related to AI affects educators' intention to adopt generative AI tools, indicating that users' concerns can play an important role in the integration of AI into educational practice [54]. Long-term research will help to clarify whether initial anxiety is reduced with the gain of experience and competence of users, or whether new concerns arise when artificial intelligence is deeply incorporated into learning, education, and evaluation. Such research also allows to study the time-dependent relationship between AI preparedness, anxiety, AI knowledge, self-efficacy, perceived usefulness and actual AI use.

The paradox of ethical awareness as a cognitive demand

A remarkable finding of the study is the strong positive association between “ethics (ethical readiness)” and “learning anxiety”, “job replacement” concerns, and “sociotechnical blindness” (Table 5). This finding may be better understood by distinguishing “ethics (ethical readiness)” from “abilitiy (technical readiness)”. Technical readiness generally refers to the knowledge, skills, and confidence required to understand and use AI technologies in clinical settings, whereas ethics (ethical readiness) reflects awareness of broader issues such as privacy, algorithmic bias, accountability, transparency, and patient safety. While ability (technical readiness) primarily functions as a competency-related resource, ethics (ethical readiness) involves critical reflection on the potential risks and unintended consequences of AI implementation [25]. Therefore, ethics (ethical readiness) may not always reduce anxiety; rather, it may heighten students’ sensitivity to the ethical, professional, and organizational demands associated with AI use.

This paradoxical role of ethics (ethical readiness) can be explained through cognitive load theory and moral distress literature. Cognitive load theory suggests that learning becomes more demanding when individuals are required to process complex and interacting sources of information [43]. In AI-supported nursing education, students are expected not only to understand how AI tool’s function but also to evaluate their implications for privacy, bias, accountability, transparency, and patient safety. This combination of technical learning and ethical evaluation may increase cognitive and emotional burden, thereby contributing to AI-related anxiety. Moral distress literature provides a complementary explanation: moral distress occurs when individuals recognize the ethically appropriate course of action but feel constrained by institutional, organizational, or contextual barriers [31]. Nursing students who understand the ethical risks of AI but perceive insufficient guidance, limited authority, or unclear governance structures may therefore experience greater anxiety. This interpretation is consistent with recent healthcare AI literature emphasizing ethical concerns related to privacy, transparency, responsibility, equity, and patient safety [35, 45].

From the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) perspective, ethics (ethical readiness) may therefore operate as a dual process. It may function as a resource by supporting responsible, reflective, and patient-centered AI use; however, it may also become a demand when students perceive heightened accountability and continuous risk assessment without adequate institutional support, ethical guidance, or governance structures. In this sense, the positive association between ethics (ethical readiness) and AI-related concerns should not be interpreted simply as resistance to technology or lack of competence. Rather, it may reflect students’ professional vigilance and awareness of the conditions required for safe and trustworthy AI implementation.

The present findings can also be situated within broader theories of technology adoption. According to the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), students’ willingness to engage with AI depends partly on whether they perceive AI tools as useful and easy to use in academic and clinical learning contexts [11] Similarly, the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology emphasizes performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions as key determinants of technology use [53], while “Diffusion of Innovations” theory suggests that adoption is shaped by perceived relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability [38]. Applied to nursing education, students may be more likely to adopt AI when they perceive it as beneficial for learning and clinical decision-making, compatible with professional nursing values, easy to understand, safely trialable, and visibly useful in educational practice.

The Revised Artificial Intelligence Device Use Acceptance (RAIDUA) model further extends this interpretation by emphasizing that AI adoption is influenced not only by perceived usefulness and technical competence but also by privacy concerns, ethical AI leadership, trust-building strategies, and socially responsible AI governance [41]. From this perspective, students with higher ethics (ethical readiness) may be more aware of data privacy, algorithmic bias, accountability, and the potential consequences of AI-supported decision-making. Such awareness may promote informed and responsible AI acceptance, but it may also intensify concerns about job displacement, professional accountability, and sociotechnical risks when safeguards are perceived as inadequate. Thus, integrating JD-R with TAM, UTAUT, Diffusion of Innovations, and RAIDUA strengthens the conceptual contribution of the study by showing that the readiness–anxiety paradox reflects the simultaneous influence of perceived usefulness, implementation conditions, ethical awareness, and concerns about trustworthy AI deployment. In high-stakes areas such as nursing and healthcare, AI-related anxiety may therefore represent not opposition to progress, but a form of professional vigilance and patient advocacy rooted in students’ commitment to competence, safety, and ethical care.

Ability (Technical Readiness) as a psychological buffer

Conversely, ability (technical readiness) was negatively associated with both “learning anxiety” (β = − 0.25) and concerns about “AI configuration” (β = − 0.29). Grounded in Self-Efficacy Theory, these findings suggest that higher levels of technical proficiency tend to coincide with perceiving AI less as an “opaque threat” and more as a “manageable instrument” [14]. Unlike ethics (ethical readiness), which was positively associated with heightened risk sensitivity, technical competence was positively associated with perceived control and with lower anxiety levels, potentially through increased user confidence. This pattern indicates that AI pedagogical frameworks may benefit from a dual-track strategy: technical modules could emphasize confidence-building, whereas ethical training might be aligned with proactive risk-management to reduce the likelihood of “ethics-induced burnout” [28, 60]. Within perioperative environments where robotic systems and digital workflows are becoming the clinical standard, nurses reporting higher technical literacy also tended to report viewing AI as a form of “clinical augmentation” rather than a threat to professional stability. This nuance may help account for why anxieties regarding job displacement in this study were more closely associated with ethical and cognitive domains than with technical proficiency alone.

Conclusion

The process of AI integration among surgical nursing students appears to be characterized by a dynamic tension between technical skill, practical exposure, and ethical consciousness. While higher technical proficiency is associated with lower learning-related stress, heightened ethical and cognition (cognitive readiness) tends to co-occur with greater professional vigilance—a pattern referred to here as the “readiness paradox.” Despite above-average readiness scores, students reported moderate levels of AI-related anxiety. In this context, superior preparedness was found to be linked with increased concern rather than greater comfort, particularly regarding sociotechnical impacts and occupational displacement. The findings further suggest that active, experiential engagement with AI tools is associated with reduced sociotechnical blindness and a more grounded sense of readiness. Gender and age also emerged as variables associated with these patterns; female students reported more pronounced concerns about configuration and sociotechnical risks, whereas older students reported particular challenges in adapting to the ethical and cognitive shifts required by AI. Overall, developing a safe and effective AI-supported practice is likely to require a multidimensional pedagogical approach. Curricula may benefit from going beyond technical training to incorporate ethical deliberation and hands-on participation in ways that support professional identity. Longitudinal research will be important to track how these readiness–anxiety dynamics evolve over time and to provide a stronger empirical basis for sustainable clinical adoption.

Strengths and limitations

This cross-sectional study of second-year students in one country contributes to the limited literature on AI readiness in high-technology, high-accountability surgical nursing contexts. The hierarchical modelling approach indicates that readiness dimensions account for additional variance beyond demographics and exposure. Although these findings provide insights into the role and potential of artificial intelligence in the context studied, the generalization of artificial intelligence must be inter preted cautiously due to differences incultural and educational contexts. Therefore, the results may not fully reflect educational environments with different cultural norms, institutional structures or technological levels. Future research should include samples of various cultural and educational contexts and examine how different factors affect attitudes, uses, and outcomes related to artificial intelligence in education.

However, the design precludes causal inference and limits generalizability, underscoring the need for longitudinal, observational, and cross-cultural comparative studies on AI readiness and anxiety in nursing education. Given the cross‑sectional nature of the current study, causal inferences about these relationships cannot be drawn. Therefore, further research using more detailed qualitative approaches, longitudinal designs, and content analyses is needed to clarify the underlying factors associated with AI‑related anxiety and readiness and to assess the generalizability of these findings across contexts.

Self-report bias

All measurements were based on self-reported responses, which may introduce response or social desirability bias. However, validated instruments widely used in healthcare education research were employed to enhance measurement reliability. Future research incorporating behavioural or performance-based indicators of AI competence may provide complementary insights.
