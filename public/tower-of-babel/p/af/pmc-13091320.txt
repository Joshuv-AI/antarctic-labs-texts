Vineyard floor mulching and removal modulates Marselan aroma formation in association with microclimate-dependent metabolic and transcriptomic regulation
Authors: Meng-Bo Tian, Ming-Yu Li, Ning Shi, Hao-Cheng Lu, Xiao-Tong Gao, Yu Wang, Shu-De Li, Chang-Qing Duan, Keji Yu, Jun Wang, Fei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vineyard geotextile mulching is widely used to control weeds and conserve soil moisture in water-limited vineyards, but its effects on berry aroma formation and underlying transcriptomic regulation remain unclear. Here, whole-field mulching was applied from anthesis and removed at two key phenological stages across three vintages (2015–2017). Soil properties, grape volatile profiles, and transcriptomic responses were analyzed. Mulching promoted downward nutrient movement and upregulated genes involved in photosynthesis, carbon metabolism, terpenoid and norisoprenoid biosynthesis, enhancing volatile accumulation particularly in 2015 under higher radiation and lower rainfall conditions. Mulching removal around veraison resulted in higher volatile accumulation than either full-season mulching or no mulching. Co-expression network analysis identified modules enriched in light signaling, transport, and transcription, with bHLH075, bZIP05, WRKY21, and YABBY5 as potential regulators. In summary, this study elucidates the dynamic aroma–environment interactions and molecular responses in grape berries, providing insights for optimizing vineyard management to enhance grape and wine aroma quality in semi-arid regions.

Introduction

Grape berry aroma is a major contributor to wine quality and regional typicity, representing an emergent outcome of tightly coordinated metabolic networks shaped by berry development and environmental factors (Previtali et al., 2021). Grape volatile compounds arise mainly from multiple metabolic pathways; including fatty acid–derived C6 and C9 compounds via the lipoxygenase pathway; isoprenoids synthesized through the plastidial methylerythritol phosphate pathway; and norisoprenoids generated from carotenoid degradation (Jackson, 2008). Regarding the biosynthesis and accumulation of grape aroma; climatic conditions play a pivotal role in shaping grape aroma metabolism; with light availability; temperature regimes; and water status acting as primary drivers of aroma-related pathways (Crespo et al., 2018). The biosynthetic activity of aroma compounds is directly influenced by these environmental factors and is also indirectly constrained by upstream carbon allocation; precursor availability; and cellular energy status (Parker et al., 2018); which are themselves strongly regulated by environmental conditions. Moreover; the effects of a given environmental factor are not constant but may vary across developmental stages. For example; high solar radiation in arid regions may promote carbon assimilation during early berry development; whereas excessive radiation during summer may accelerate the degradation of aroma-related metabolites (Friedel et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). Consequently, grape berry aroma exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal plasticity and high sensitivity to environmental fluctuations.

Beyond the grapevine climate, vineyard floor microclimate constitutes an important yet often overlooked interface through which these environmental signals are transmitted to the vine. As an eco-friendly practice, vineyard floor mulching has been widely adopted to reduce herbicide use and preserve the soil surface (Tarara, 2000). Depending on the material; mulching can be classified into organic and inorganic types. Organic mulches (cover crops) primarily influence soil structure; moisture retention; and microbial activity through gradual decomposition (Wang, Zhang, et al., 2021; Xi et al., 2011). In contrast; inorganic mulches; including plastic films or geotextiles; are more commonly used in semi-arid vineyards since they exert more immediate microclimate effects by modifying surface reflectance; soil temperature; and water evaporation (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025). Such changes to grapevines have the potential to indirectly affect the availability of metabolic precursors and the regulation of aroma biosynthesis in developing berries (Wang et al., 2020). However, the influence of inorganic mulching is not static, as its physiological and metabolic impacts on grapevine may vary with developmental stage. Consequently, adjusting the timing of mulching removal represents a potentially powerful yet underexplored strategy.

Despite growing interest in the effects of vineyard floor mulching on grape berry performance, most studies have focused on single developmental stages, often at harvest, thereby overlooking the dynamic nature of aroma formation across the entire course of berry development (Alises et al., 2025; Mairata et al., 2025). Such approaches provide limited insight into when and how key aroma-related metabolic shifts are established. Also, the molecular mechanisms underlying mulching-induced modulation of berry aroma are poorly understood. How vineyard floor mulching influences the expression of genes involved in aroma biosynthesis, upstream carbon metabolism, precursor supply, and transcriptional regulatory networks has yet to be elucidated, and robust evidence integrating multi-stage and multi-vintage transcriptomic data is largely lacking. Together, these gaps highlight the need for integrative studies combining temporal metabolite profiling with transcriptional analyses across multiple developmental stages and growing seasons.

The north foothills of the Mt. Tianshan in Xinjiang, China, provides a distinctive viticultural context for investigating aroma regulation under vineyard floor management. This region is characterized by a semi-arid climate with high solar radiation, low disease pressure, and limited precipitation, offering both favorable conditions for quality grapevine production and a pronounced capacity for artificial microclimatic regulation. Marselan (Vitis vinifera L., a hybrid of Cabernet Sauvignon × Grenache) represents one of the most rapidly expanding red winegrape cultivars in China and has emerged as a key variety in recent years, which underscores its growing importance to the Chinese wine industry. In this study, a three-year field experiment was conducted in a semi-arid vineyard of the north foothills of Mt. Tianshan to investigate how vineyard floor mulching and removal timing influence Marselan berry aroma formation and underlying transcriptional regulations. Black geotextile mulching was applied before anthesis and removed at two different stages, and grape berries were sampled at five key phenological stages to capture the temporal dynamics of grape aroma. By integrating volatile profiling with transcriptomic analyses, this study aimed to elucidate the metabolic and molecular mechanisms through which vineyard floor mulching modulates aroma biosynthesis across berry development. We hypothesized that mulching reshapes carbon allocation and precursor availability, thereby reprogramming aroma-related metabolic pathways and gene expression networks. The findings of this study are expected to provide mechanistic insights into aroma–environment interactions and offer a scientific basis for optimizing vineyard floor management strategies to improve grape and wine aroma quality in semi-arid regions.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and plant material

The study was conducted over three consecutive growing seasons (2015, 2016, and 2017) in a commercial vineyard situated at the northern foothills of Tianshan Mountains, in Manas County, Xinjiang, China (44°13′58′′N, 86°14′21′′E, altitude 517 m). The experimental site is located in a semi-arid region. Own-rooted Marselan grapevines (Vitis vinifera L.) were planted in 2007 with a spacing of 3.0 m × 1.0 m. The vineyard rows were oriented north-south, with a slight inclination of 44° north by east. In 2015, the vines were trained using a fan training system with two trunks, which two permanent trunks are trained from the base of the vine and spread laterally along the trellis, forming a fan-shaped canopy. In latter two years, the training system was changed to a modified vertical shoot positioning system, which is characterized by a single trunk with shoots trained vertically upward. Each row meter of Marselan vines retained 15–18 nodes, with an average yield of approximately 12 t/ha. Nutrient and pest management practices followed industry standards. Furrow irrigation was employed, providing 0.23 m3 of water per vine every two weeks during the growing season, resulting in a total seasonal irrigation volume of around 2.3 m3 per vine.

Experimental design and sampling

The black geotextile mulch was installed on the floor (covering the entire vineyard ground) before anthesis as we previously reported (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025). Treatments included the control (CK) and the three different mulching strategies: (1) mulch was removed before veraison (M-BV), (2) mulch was removed post veraison (M-PV), (3) mulch was removed after harvest (M). Four rows with similar vigor were selected, and buffer rows were applied between treatments. Three independent blocks in the middle of each row were selected to avoid edge effect, and 30 vines in each block were set as one biological replicate with buffer blocks were set between each block.

Four hundred berries without any visual injuries per replicate were sampled at five key phenological stages: (1) E-L31 (berries pea-size (7 mm diam.)), (2) E-L 33 (berries still hard and green), (3) E-L 35 (berries begin to color and enlarge), (4) E-L 37 (berries not quite ripe), (5) E-L 38 (berries harvest-ripe). Berries were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Detection of soil properties

The soil sampling location was positioned midway between two adjacent rows, maintaining a distance of 1.5 m from the trunk at 2017 harvest. Samples were collected at five depths below the ground: 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm, 60–80 cm, and 80–100 cm. Each treatment consisted of nine sampling locations. Basic soil information including pH, particle size, cation exchange capacity (CEC), electrical conductivity (EC), and organic matter content was detected according to Qi et al. (2019). Soil nutrient elements were determined using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (Agilent 5110 ICP-OES); as described in our previous study (Lu et al., 2023a).

Extraction of volatiles in grape berries

For each replicate, about 60 g of frozen berries were de-seeded under liquid nitrogen. Then the de-seeded samples were ground into powder with an addition of polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (1 g) and D-gluconic acid lactone (0.5 g). The frozen powder was transferred into 50 mL falcons and melted at 4 °C for 8 h. The clear grape juice was obtained through centrifuging at 8000g for 15 min.

For free-form volatile compounds, 5 mL of grape juice was used directly, bound-form volatiles were extracted using Cleanert® PEP-SPE (150 mg/6 mL, Bonna-Agela Technologies, China) followed by acid hydrolysis, as previously described (Tian et al., 2023). A total of 5 mL of the solution was added to a 20 mL vial containing a magnetic stirrer, 10 μL of internal standard (4-methyl-2-pentanol), and 1 g NaCl.

Determination of volatile compounds in grapes

Headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) was employed for the extraction and determination of volatile compounds in berries. The SPME fiber (2 cm DVB/CAR/PDMS, 50/30 μm, Supelco, USA) was then exposed to the headspace for 30 min using a 5:1 splitless injection mode. An Agilent 6890 GC coupled to an Agilent 5973 MS, fitted with an HP-INNOWAX capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm, J&W Scientific, USA) were used as previously described (Lu et al., 2024).

The identification of volatile compounds was conducted using the Automated MassSpectral Deconvolution and Identification System (AMDIS) with the reference standards in the NIST 14 MS database. The quantification of volatile compounds was according to the corresponding external standards. The concentrations of those volatile compounds without corresponding standards were estimated with equations of standards having the same functional group and/or similar numbers of carbon atoms. The concentrations of volatile compounds were expressed as μg/kg of fresh berry weight in grapes.

Berry RNA isolation, sequencing, and data analysis

Samples of 50 Marselan berries from E-L 31, E-L 35, and E-L 38 were randomly selected from each replicate for RNA extraction, and total RNA was isolated from the frozen de-seeded berries using a SpectrumTM Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 54 RNA-seq libraries (2 treatments (CK and M) × 3 replicates × 3 developmental stages × 3 vintages) were constructed on an Illumina HiseqTM 2000 platform to yield 150-bp pair-end reads, whereas other treatments were not included in RNA-seq. The clean reads were aligned with the genome of the PN40024.T2T (V5) using STAR aligner with default parameters. Gene expression levels were normalized by transcripts per million (TPM) according to the gene length in the genome. The transcriptome data in this manuscript has been uploaded to NGDC (National Genome Science Data Center) with the accession number CRA015742.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of data was performed by SPSS 23.0 with three replications of each treatment, means of metabolome were separated by Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05) when the ANOVA test was significant, and the transcriptome was tested by student's test (p < 0.05). Figures were performed by GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Cluster heatmaps were performed by the ‘pheatmap’ package in R (version 4.3.2). Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the SIMCA (version 14.1, Umetrics, Sweden).

Results

Identify the environmental variations under mulching treatment

The mesoclimate variation among vintages, as well as mulching effects on major environmental factors such as incident light and reflective light have been described in our previous study (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025). Briefly, in 2016, rainfall was concentrated in the early developmental stage, and mean temperatures in June, August, and September were over 1 °C higher than in the other two years. The other years were closer to the long-term average. Mulching significantly reduced soil-reflected solar radiation (SR) and photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), which contributed approximately 13%–60% of the environmental SR and PAR. It also suppressed reflectance above 400 nm without affecting near-UV wavelengths.

Mulching modified soil properties (Supplementary Table 1), whereas its effect on different soil depths varied. For the topsoil (0–20 cm), mulching significantly reduced total K, available N, and several available nutrients such as Ca, Mg, Zn, and B. However, some opposite trends were found in soil from 20 to 60 cm, where mulching increased the available N and effective Ca. The effects of mulch covering on soil pH, EC, CEC, and mechanical composition were limited. PCA was employed to identify the soil differences between CK and M in five depths (Supplementary Fig. 1). PCA distinguishes the surface soil (0–40 cm) from the deeper soil. The upper layer of soil was abundant with higher clay particles, various nutrients, and organic matter. The particles of deeper layer soils were larger, pH, EC, and effective Ca were higher. Then we conducted two PCA based on the upper and deeper layers separately (Fig. 1). The results revealed that mulching increased total P, effective B, and effective P in the surface soil, whereas other nutrient levels were generally higher in the CK soil. The enrichment of these three nutrients under mulching extended into deeper soil. Transcriptomic response to environmental changes

Based on the transcriptomic data comparing mulching and control treatments, we applied relatively relaxed differential expression thresholds (FDR < 0.1, |log₂FC| > 0.5), considering the mild nature of the mulching treatment. Totally 1519 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, with the harvest of vintage 2015 having the most DEGs (Fig. 2a). KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs under mulching treatment were significantly enriched in photosynthesis and carbon metabolism (Fig. 2b), suggesting that mulching exerts a notable influence on photosynthetic machinery. Focusing on the DEGs related to photosynthesis, nine genes in photosystem I and ten genes in photosystem II showed a consistent trend that were down-regulated at the early developmental stage but significantly induced at harvest (Fig. 2c). The light responsive transcription factors VviHYH, VviPIF4, and photoreceptor VviCRY1 also presented a similar trend. Green leaf volatiles (GLVs), mainly C6/C9 aldehydes and alcohols, accumulated rapidly around veraison, peaked, and subsequently declined toward harvest (Fig. 3a). With respect to mulching effects, significant differences in total C6/C9 compounds between mulching treatments and CK were observed from veraison in 2015 and 2017. At harvest in all three vintages, berries from mulching treatments contained higher levels of green leaf volatiles than those from CK. To further discriminate the treatments, a PLS-DA model across the three vintages was established (Fig. 3b). The results indicated that M-PV (negative X-axis) generally showed higher levels of green leaf volatiles than other treatments. Dynamics of terpenoids under mulching

Terpenoids showed a generally declining trend during the growing season (Fig. 4a). Limited difference between M and CK was found at harvest in three consecutive years, however, M-BV and M-PV improved the terpenoids in the first vintage. The PLS-DA model at harvest suggests CK and M berries had a similar terpenoid profile (Fig. 4b), since they were located in the positive part of PC1. The loading plot indicates CK berries had more citronellol, while M-BV berries were abundant with oxygenated terpenoids. Regarding the difference in terpenoid profile during ripening, mulching enhanced the accumulation of terpenoids at all three stages in 2015 (Fig. 4c), but this effect narrowed in the latter two vintages. Particularly, terpenoids in mulching treatment were consistently lower than CK at E-L 31 in 2016 and 2017, indicating a lower concentration of terpenoid precursors after mulching treatment. Dynamics of norisoprenoids under mulching

Mulching enhanced the accumulation of norisoprenoids and presented the difference compared with CK from E-L 33 to E-L 35, as well as M-BV, which showed higher norisoprenoids than CK in most stages in three consecutive years. However, only the harvest of the first vintage showed significant differences among the different mulching treatments, the differences at harvest of the other two years were limited. In the PLS-DA model based on the concentration at harvest, M-BV and M were separated from M-PV and CK by the X-axis, although M and CK were located closely in the score plot. Aroma regulatory network analysis

A weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) based on 1519 DEGs was conducted to identify the co-expressed genes in the current study, also, the correlation between modules and volatiles was analyzed (Fig. 6a). Module 1 (ME1) is the module with the most DEGs, and showed a positive correlation with terpenoid accumulation, while negatively correlated with norisoprenoids. ME2 presented the opposite trend from ME1. ME3 and ME4 positively correlated with norisoprenoids and green leaf volatiles. The eigengene-based connectivity (kME) of each gene was calculated, and the top 50 genes with the highest kME values from each of the four modules were selected for constructing the network between grape volatiles and berry transcriptome. Seventy genes with absolute weight > 0.7 in the network were identified as hub genes (Fig. 6b), aiming to reveal transcriptomic responses and regulatory roles in volatile synthesis under mulching conditions. Grape norisoprenoids showed a high correlation with the transcripts in the current study, especially β-damascenone and TPB, followed by linalool oxide and rose oxide, suggesting that these compounds may be the most sensitive to transcriptional responses or best reflect systemic changes under mulching treatment. Focusing on the 70 hub genes (Supplementary Table 2), they were generally classified into metabolic pathway, signaling, transporter, transcription factors, etc. Particularly, 10 hub genes related to photosystem were identified, suggesting their key role in regulating norisoprenoids and terpenoids under mulching. The metabolic pathway mainly involved in isoprenoid biosynthesis (Vitvi05_01chr19g11340, Vitvi05_01chr05g05720), lipoxygenase pathway (Vitvi05_01chr05g07150), glycosidase/glycoside transferase (Vitvi05_01chr07g21170, Vitvi05_01chr03g05650), etc. The enrichment of signaling and transporter genes highlights their potential roles in transducing environmental cues under mulching into transcriptional changes that influence volatile biosynthesis. As two out of four transcription factors (TFs), bZIP05 and bHLH075 showed a positive correlation with damascenone and TPB, and the other two TFs (WRKY21, YABBY5) negatively correlated with norisoprenoids. The GLVs and the upstream carotenoid-derived metabolite (geranylacetone) showed a weak correlation in the network. The promoter regions (2000 bp upstream) of four hub TFs are enriched with cis-elements responsive to light, multiple phytohormones, slow temperature, anaerobic conditions, circadian cues, meristem activity, and MYB binding sites, indicating a characteristic multi-signal integrative regulatory profile. Discussion

Mulching modifies soil composition besides affecting grapevine microclimate

As an eco-friendly practice in vineyard, black geotextile mulching is ideal to suppress weed growth, protect soil from disturbance, alter the grapevine microclimate and thus affect grapevine growth and berry composition at harvest (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025; Wang, Gao, et al., 2021). While numerous studies have reported soil modifications induced by organic mulching in vineyard systems (Guerra & Steenwerth, 2012; Mairata et al., 2025), our study demonstrates that inorganic mulching also exerts significant effects on soil composition. Although the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood, according to our previous results that mulching reduces soil erosion and evaporation, which maintains soil moisture and moderates soil temperature (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025). These patterns suggest a possible redistribution of nutrients under mulching; which may be associated with changes in soil moisture and temperature. Our findings show that mulching promotes the accumulation of specific nutrients; such as phosphorus and boron; while decreasing others; indicating a complex redistribution of nutrients rather than a uniform improvement in soil fertility. Unlike the control (CK); where nutrients are more concentrated in the surface soil; mulching appears to facilitate the downward movement of nutrients. This pattern may be beneficial in arid viticultural regions; where grapevine roots tend to develop deeper in response to drought stress (Fort et al., 2017).

DEGs between mulching and control reveal the dynamics of light and heat during growing season

To gain insight into the molecular response of grape berries to mulching, we analyzed the transcriptomic changes under this treatment. Mulching induced only a limited number of DEGs even under mild thresholds, and the number of DEGs was even lower in 2016 and 2017 (Fig. 2a). This suggests a mild transcriptomic response in Marselan grape berries under mulching. Grape berries possess photosynthetic capacity, particularly at early developmental stages when chloroplasts are abundant in the skin (Teixeira et al., 2022). In this study; genes related to light-harvesting complexes and both photosystems were consistently downregulated by mulching treatment at E-L 31; likely due to the reduced light environment caused by diminished reflectance from the vineyard floor. Notably; photosystem II (PSII) was more strongly affected than photosystem I (PSI); as indicated by larger absolute fold changes and the significant repression of three PSII genes in 2015. This may be attributed not only to PSII's greater sensitivity to reduced light intensity; but also to alterations in light quality under mulching. Since PSI and PSII exhibit distinct responses to different spectral regions; with PSII being more dependent on red light; the spectral shift caused by mulching could further enhance the differential impact on the two photosystems (Oguchi et al., 2021). As a heat stress transcription factor; VviHSFA2 mainly regulates many HSP20 directly (Liu et al., 2023). At E-L 31; when both heat shock and photosystem genes showed their highest expression level; most heat shock genes were upregulated whereas photosystem genes were downregulated. This supports our previous results at transcriptomic level that black geotextile mulching heats the cluster zone and reduces the reflective light from the vineyard floor (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025).

Mulching effects on grape-derived volatiles via transcriptomic regulation

Grape-derived volatiles are a key contributor to wine quality and originate from diverse metabolic pathways. GLVs are generated from the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway and are commonly linked to herbaceous or green aromas. A previous study reported that inter-row mulching significantly enhanced GLVs accumulation in Cabernet Sauvignon berries (Wang et al., 2020). However; in the current study; the GLV differences between treatments at harvest were limited. A recent study suggests the microclimate effect on GLVs between Cabernet Sauvignon and Marselan could be the opposite regarding the C6/C9 compounds (Yao et al., 2024). This provides evidence of genetic differences between two cultivars and provides insights into the microclimatic regulation on GLVs. An interesting result shown by PLS-DA is that mulching removal around veraison enhances the accumulation of GLVs; especially removal post veraison. This indicates that mulching effect on grape composition is highly dependent on the stages; since the flavonoids showed the same phenomenon in our previous data set (Tian, Gao, et al., 2025).

Although Marselan is considered a neutral cultivar, the low odor thresholds of terpenoids may still contribute to the wine aroma. The terpenoids identified in the current study are all monoterpenes and their derivatives and showed a generally decreasing trend during ripening, which is consistent with previous studies (Zhang et al., 2016). In the early stages of berry development; mulching berries exhibited lower terpenoid concentrations; particularly in 2017 (Fig. 4a). This may be attributed to insufficient light exposure during the early period on the one hand; as light is considered to induce terpenoid biosynthesis (Lu et al., 2024); which is also consistent with the downregulation of photosynthesis-related genes observed at the green stage (Fig. 2c). On the other hand; mulching removal treatments (M-BV and M-PV) showed higher oxygenated terpenoids at harvest; which suggests an enhanced exposure to light and oxidative conditions. A potential reason is that mulching reduces light availability during the early stages of grape development; placing the berries in a relatively low-light stress environment. With mulching removal at/post veraison; the berries were exposed to intense light and elevated temperatures; likely activating antioxidant-related metabolic pathways in berry and resulting in the accumulation of oxidized terpenoids. This kind of compensatory phenomenon following the release of stress has also been reported in previous studies (Friedel et al., 2016; Tian, Lu, et al., 2025). Although terpenoids are mainly synthesized in the early stages of berry development, the transcriptional responses to mulching highlight their environmental sensitivity and enhance our understanding of terpenoid regulation. Under mulching conditions, mevalonic acid (MVA) pathway was more affected compared to methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway, since there were more DEGs in MVA pathway (Fig. 4d). This may be explained by their subcellular localization since the cytosolic MVA pathway is dependent on cytosolic acetyl-CoA pools and integrates environmental signals such as carbon fluxes and stress-related hormones, making it more sensitive to external perturbations (Hemmerlin, 2013). Moreover, gene expression pattern in terpenoid pathway was highly consistent with photosystem genes. This provides evidence that terpenoid biosynthesis at the transcript level is induced by light and suggests that the discrepancy between terpenoid metabolites and transcripts may be due to other factors, such as arid climate.

Volatile norisoprenoids are important aroma-active compounds derived from the oxidative cleavage of carotenoids. The evolution of norisoprenoids in the current study is consistent with the cleavage of carotenoids reported previously (Zhuge et al., 2023). The cleavage of carotenoids is also affected by the microclimate around cluster zone (Asproudi et al., 2020); which might contribute to the early enhancement of norisoprenoid accumulation under mulching conditions. M-BV showed higher acyclic norisoprenoids at harvest according to the PLS-DA model while M-PV was located on the opposite side of the score plot; this indicates that the light exposure and oxidative stress caused by mulching removal during veraison is vital for the accumulation of acyclic norisoprenoids like β-damascenone and β-ionone. Although significant differences in carotenoid pathway–related DEGs were observed at harvest; their expression levels were relatively low. In contrast; during the early stages of berry development; PSY and ZEP showed no significant differences with a slightly lower expression observed in the mulching treatment. This indicates that the variation in norisoprenoids may not result from differences in carotenoid substrate availability under mulching conditions. Interestingly; two acyclic norisoprenoids; MHO (6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one) and geranylacetone showed a different pattern compared to cyclic norisoprenoids among mulching and CK (Fig. 5c). This; on the one hand; could be the differentially expressed CCDs. Three CCDs in grapevine involved in norisoprenoids biosynthesis have been identified; VviCCD1; VviCCD4a; and VviCCD4b (Meng et al., 2019). In the current study; VviCCD1 (Vitvi05_01chr13g26400) showed a different pattern compared to VviCCD4a (Vitvi05_01chr02g19450) and VviCCD4b (Vitvi05_01chr02g19460). This might contribute to the difference between cyclic and acyclic norisoprenoids; since it was reported that different CCDs accept different (apo)-carotenoid substrates (Huang et al., 2009; Lashbrooke et al., 2013).

Gene regulation network

In WGCNA, two predominant modules (ME1 and ME2) showed correlation with terpenoids and norisoprenoids, indicating the isoprenoid metabolism was more affected by mulching compared to green leaf volatiles (Fig. 6). Terpenoids and norisoprenoids are tightly linked to developmental progression, plastid differentiation, and carotenoid turnover. Also, norisoprenoids are reported to be driven by the soil nutrition (Lu et al., 2023b); which is observed in the current study. In contrast; GLVs originate from the LOX pathway and are characterized as rapid-response metabolites to transient stresses (Matsui & Koeduka, 2016). It is plausible that the key enzymes in the GLV pathway may be maintained at certain basal levels, allowing their activities to be quickly triggered without a strong requirement for de novo transcription. These may result in weak correlations between GLVs and modules, also the weak connection in the network. Apart from isoprenoid-related genes, the metabolic genes in network also contained genes involved in chlorophyll and flavonoid metabolism. This could be the fact that these metabolic pathways share common upstream signals like light and temperature caused by mulching. The categories of 70 hub DEGs reveal that the volatile differences between mulching and control are from integrated system-level regulation rather than the action of a single metabolic pathway (Supplementary Table 2).

As an important transcription factor in mediating plant–environment interactions, several members of the bHLH family have been reported to regulate flavonoid and terpene biosynthesis (Sun et al., 2025). Overexpression of VvbHLH9 significantly activates VvDXS1 and VvTPS31 in grape leaves; thereby promoting the accumulation of terpene compounds. A similar result was also found in the current network that bHLH075 and DXS1 were both correlated with norisoprenoids. The basic leucine zipper (bZIP) and WRKY transcription factor families are widely recognized for their central roles in phytohormone signaling and the regulation of biotic and abiotic stress responses (Liu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022), while YABBY family members are mainly associated with organ development and polarity establishment and have only recently been linked to hormone-related regulation and stress responses (Zhang et al., 2020). In the current study, bHLH75, bZIP5, WRKY21, and YABBY5 showed strong associations with aroma-related traits. The promoter analysis suggests that these transcription factors share common elements like light, phytohormones, and low temperature, suggesting that they may act as integrative nodes sensing black mulching–induced microenvironmental changes and modulating downstream aroma-related metabolic pathways. Functional validation of the DNA-binding and transcriptional activation abilities of these transcription factors will be an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of how vineyard floor mulching and its removal regulate grape berry aroma formation through coordinated environmental, metabolic, and transcriptomic processes in a semi-arid viticultural region. By integrating multi-season field experiments with dynamic volatile profiling and transcriptome analyses, we demonstrate that inorganic geotextile mulching exerts profound but development-dependent effects on grape berry aroma accumulation. Mulching altered soil nutrient distribution and vineyard microclimate, thereby reshaping photosynthetic capacity and precursor availability during berry development. These changes were accompanied by coordinated transcriptional reprogramming, particularly in pathways related to photosynthesis, carbon allocation, terpenoid biosynthesis, and carotenoid degradation, ultimately influencing the accumulation of key aroma compounds such as C6/C9 compounds, terpenoids, and norisoprenoids. Importantly, our results reveal that mulching removal before/post veraison enhanced volatile accumulation more effectively than continuous mulching or no mulching, highlighting the critical role of developmental timing in mediating aroma–environment interactions. Co-expression network analysis further identified mulching-responsive gene modules and candidate transcription factors, including bHLH, bZIP, WRKY, and YABBY family members, suggesting that aroma modulation is governed by complex regulatory networks integrating environmental cues with metabolic control. Collectively, this work advances mechanistic understanding of how vineyard floor management shapes grape berry aroma and provides a scientific basis for optimizing mulching strategies to improve grape and wine aroma quality in semi-arid regions.
