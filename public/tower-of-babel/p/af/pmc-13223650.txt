Psycho-social-contextual landscape of return to sport after hamstring strain injuries: a scoping review
Authors: Salvatore Di Liberto, Kenny Guex, Mario Bizzini, Diarmuid Strokes, Catherine Blake, James Matthews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHamstring strain injuries (HSI) are a leading cause of time loss and reinjury in elite sport, yet return-to-sport (RTS) decisions remain largely based on physical criteria. This scoping review aimed to map the psychological, social and contextual factors associated with RTS after HSI and to examine how psychological readiness has been conceptualised in RTS after HSI.MethodsFollowing Joanna Briggs Institute methodology, seven databases (MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, SPORTDiscus, Scopus and ProQuest) were systematically searched for studies published until September 2025 in five languages. Eligible studies included original research identifying or describing psychological, social or contextual factors influencing RTS after HSI. Methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool and the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine. Data were synthesised thematically.Results10 studies (n=663 participants, mostly in elite football) met the inclusion criteria. Three overarching domains were identified. Psychological factors were most frequently reported, including anxiety, fear of reinjury or failure and self-perceived readiness, as well as motivation and self-efficacy. Social factors encompassed therapeutic alliance, communication within the rehabilitation team and hierarchical dynamics within professional environments. Contextual factors referred to situational and organisational influences such as season timing, resource availability and operational practices. Psychological readiness was inconsistently defined and typically assessed informally rather than through validated instruments.ConclusionsPsychological, social and contextual factors are under-screened in RTS after HSI. Incorporating structured psychosocial screening and shared decision-making alongside physical criteria may improve RTS safety and sustainability. Athlete-centred research is warranted to refine the assessment of psychological readiness and inform holistic, context-sensitive RTS frameworks.

Introduction

Return-to-sport (RTS) criteria are commonly used in sports rehabilitation to guide practitioners in making informed decisions about an athlete’s readiness to resume activity. Current RTS protocols for adult hamstring strain injuries (HSI) primarily emphasise biomechanical indicators, including pain levels, muscular strength, flexibility and functional performance.1 Despite these criteria, HSI continue to be associated with prolonged time out of sport, high re-injury rates and significant financial costs to athletes and sports organisations.26 This ongoing challenge has garnered increasing attention in the sports medicine community, particularly as recent studies highlight HSI as the most prevalent injury in both professional women’s and men’s soccer leagues.7 8

A recent consensus statement on RTS management recommended that a holistic approach, which considers physical, psychological, social and contextual factors, is used to optimise recovery from sports-related injuries.6 Encouragingly, the importance of psychological factors in RTS decisions for HSI management has also been highlighted in a recent Delphi study.5

In athletes recovering from ACL injuries, psychological factors such as cognitive appraisals (eg, perception of reinjury risk), motivational elements (such as adherence to the rehabilitation programme) and emotional responses (eg, fear) play a crucial role in shaping the recovery quality and RTS timing.9 10 These aspects have been extensively studied, with supportive evidence linking high anxiety levels or low self-efficacy to a delayed or incomplete RTS from ACL injury.1113 Psychological readiness is increasingly recognised as a critical turning point in RTS trajectory with literature14 highlighting how it can directly influence the effectiveness and sustainability of sport resumption, ultimately determining the success of the RTS process.

Alongside psychological factors, social support from family, teammates, coaches and rehabilitation professionals, together with effective communication and a strong therapeutic alliance between athlete and clinician, are associated with improved rehabilitation outcomes.1517 Contextual factors, including competitive level and access to resources, can influence injury perception, recovery expectations and satisfaction with rehabilitation16; external pressures and cultural beliefs may also shape RTS decisions.18 While there is considerable literature on musculoskeletal injuries and biopsychosocial factors, there is limited evidence regarding the extent of research addressing psychological, social and contextual factors in RTS following HSI. Furthermore, a holistic approach specific to HSI has yet to be established, even though this type of injury remains highly prevalent in sports.7 8 Consequently, this scoping review aims to establish the first step in this process by examining the current evidence regarding psychological, social and contextual factors influencing RTS following HSI.

The primary objective of this review is to identify recurring themes and conceptual gaps within the existing literature on the psycho-social-contextual dimensions of return to RTS, intending to contribute to a more comprehensive and multidimensional understanding of the RTS process in the context of HSI. By advancing this understanding, the findings may ultimately support the development of more holistic and athlete-centred rehabilitation strategies that could potentially help reduce the risk of re-injury. Secondary objectives include mapping how the construct of psychological readiness for RTS is defined and operationalised concerning HSI.

Methods

Study design and protocol registration

This review was conducted following the guidelines outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis.19 The study protocol was registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) repository in March 2024 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8RK7E).

Study team

To ensure a robust and clinically relevant review, the study team comprised experts in evidence synthesis, quantitative and qualitative research methodology, sports and exercise psychology, clinical therapeutic relationships, rehabilitation of sports-related injuries and database research methodology.

Data sources and search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across seven major electronic databases: MEDLINE; Embase; PsycINFO; CINAHL; SPORTDiscus; Scopus and ProQuest (Theses and Dissertations). These databases were selected to ensure broad coverage of relevant research in sports medicine, psychology and rehabilitation. The search included all records indexed in the selected databases up to September 2025. To minimise language bias, studies published in English, French, German, Spanish and Italian were included. The inclusion of multiple languages was essential to capture diverse research perspectives, particularly from non-English-speaking countries where sports science research is expanding. The search strategy (see online supplemental appendix 1) was designed and refined with the support of an experienced librarian (DS) to systematically identify studies addressing three core concepts: Athletes with HSI; psychosocial management; RTS processes. Keywords and constructs (eg, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, Boolean operators) were developed a priori, based on a preliminary search, conceptual papers and relevant reviews.1015 2023 Examples of search terms included ‘hamstring strain injury’, ‘psychological readiness’, ’social support’ and ‘return-to-sports criteria’. Database-specific MeSH and free-text terms were employed to maximise search relevance.

Eligibility criteria

All study designs were considered, including randomised controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies and qualitative studies. Grey literature was also eligible for inclusion. These broad inclusion criteria were necessary given the limited availability of primary research on psychological, social and contextual factors in RTS after HSI.

Population and exclusion criteria

Studies involving adults (≥18 years) were included. Studies on children and adolescents were excluded due to differences in biomechanical and developmental factors affecting RTS.24 25 Studies examining conditions other than HSI or involving surgical management were excluded, as post-surgical recovery follows distinct trajectories influenced by surgical techniques and rehabilitation protocols.26

Study screening and selection

Two independent reviewers (SDL and JM) screened all titles and abstracts using predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. Full-text articles were retrieved for studies where eligibility was unclear. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (KG) available to mediate unresolved conflicts. The Covidence platform was used for screening and data extraction, while EndNote V.21 was employed for reference management.

Study selection criteria

Studies were eligible if they examined, described or assessed psychological, social and/or contextual factors during the RTS phase after an HSI affecting sports participation.

Data extraction

Data were extracted on study characteristics (author, year, design, country), sample characteristics (sample size, age, sex, sport type) and psychological, social and contextual outcomes, and reported RTS readiness measures. The lead author performed the initial data extraction using a customised form, with subsequent review by JM and KG to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Data synthesis

Psychological, social and contextual factors were identified across studies, the operational definition of these (see online supplemental appendix 2) was developed through discussions within the research team and by referring to conceptual studies and relevant literature on sports injuries.1015 2023 A content analysis was conducted to identify recurring patterns and synthesise the emerging evidence. Throughout the process, the research team held regular meetings to discuss and validate emerging themes, ensuring a shared and robust interpretation of the collected evidence.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers using the 2018 Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).27 The MMAT evaluates five key domains, making it suitable for assessing studies across various methodologies while ensuring reliability and validity.28 29 For descriptive purposes, studies were assigned a score ranging from 0 to 5 based on the number of criteria met, with higher scores indicating stronger methodological quality. To assess the strength of evidence, studies were rated using a modified version of the 2009 Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) model. This framework categorises studies from Level 1, the highest level of evidence (eg, RCTs), to Level 5, the lowest level of evidence (eg, opinion rather than data). Any discrepancies in MMAT scoring or OCEBM classification were resolved by consensus, with input from the senior author (JM) when necessary.

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

The authorship group acknowledges a sex imbalance among the contributing researchers. The team was formed within the framework of SD’s PhD research and assembled based on members’ expertise, availability and willingness to contribute, involving collaborators from two different countries (Switzerland and Ireland). The study population included both male and female athletes and stakeholders from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, reflecting a commitment to inclusivity in participant representation.

Results

Study selection process

A comprehensive framework of the study selection process is presented as supplementary material (online supplemental appendix 3). From 4543 studies, 3573 underwent the title/abstract screening process, 62 were assessed for eligibility and 10 were included for the final analysis.

Study characteristics

40% (n=4) of the included studies were descriptive quantitative following MMAT classification (table 1).3033 Among these, three were cross-sectional studies: one employed a Likert-scale questionnaire,30 and two used frequency analysis methods,32 33 while one was a Delphi study that also incorporated Likert-scale analysis in its consensus process.31 30% of the studies were qualitative in design, all of which were Delphi studies using content analysis.5 34 35 The remaining 30% consisted of one letter to the editor36 and two quantitative studies (one prospective observational study37 and one RCT).38 The studies included data from 663 participants from various countries, comprising 546 health professionals and 117 athletes. Most studies (60%) were conducted in Europe, 20% were multicentric and the remainder were carried out in Oceania and South America. Football was the most frequently represented sport, appearing in 60% of the studies, while Australian Rules Football was represented in 1 study of 10. The remaining studies focused on clubs involving multiple sports.

Psychological, social and contextual factors at the RTS stage

A synthesis of the reviewed studies revealed that psychological, social and contextual factors were variably represented throughout the literature (figure 1). The psychological domain emerged most prominently, with nine studies addressing this aspect (table 2). The contextual domain appeared in seven studies, while the social domain was the least explored, with five studies providing relevant data. The data were organised into three main categories—psychological, social and contextual—within which specific themes and associated factors were identified and defined. These findings will be presented and critically discussed in the following sections, according to this three-domain structure.

Categorisation and definitions of psychological, social and contextual factors

RTS, return-to-sport.

Psychological domain

Emotional states

Emotional states are among the psychological aspects reported to be linked to the RTS process. Anxiety emerged as the most frequently cited factor, reported in three of the included studies.5 31 32 Two studies emphasised the importance of systematically screening athlete anxiety during the RTS process,5 31 whereas a third study highlighted the challenges of addressing this factor within clinical decision-making.32 In addition, various forms of fear were also highlighted as central elements. Fear of movement, fear of re-injury and fear of failure are reported in the literature as screening criteria,5 31 38 reflecting concerns related to athletes’ physical and psychological vulnerability. Lastly, two studies identified apprehension as a factor that should be attentively screened during the RTS process.5 31 Importantly, apprehension should be distinguished from fear of movement, as it refers to a more general sense of unease or anticipation of harm related to sport as compared with fear of specific sport-related situations, such as sprinting or changing direction.

Cognitive processing

Cognitive processing was identified as a relevant domain in the RTS process discussed in a total of six studies. The first factor, athlete-appraisal readiness, was reported in four studies.5 30 32 33 Nevertheless, two of these studies highlighted challenges in applying this criterion in clinical settings. In Balci and Ulkar,30 psychological readiness was recognised by interviewed specialists as a central element in RTS assessment; yet, its consistent integration into daily practice was found to be lacking. Similarly, Valente et al33 reported that, among physiotherapists working in Brazilian professional football, psychological readiness was assessed in 82% of cases via informal conversations, while only 8% used structured and periodic screening methods. The second cognitive dimension, discussed in two studies,35 37 concerns the subjective meaning athletes assign to their injury experiences and the perceived consequences for their sporting careers. Notably, self-predicted recovery time was identified as a key factor associated with achieving an optimal RTS stage.37 Furthermore, in the context of English professional football, stakeholders reportedly prioritised players’ subjective experiences during high-intensity actions, such as sprinting, as important indicators of cognitive readiness and the significance of the injury.35

Volitional states

The volitional states were addressed in four studies.5 30 32 34 Self-efficacy, defined as the athlete’s perceived ability to meet the physical and psychological demands of RTS, was reported in two studies.5 34 Paton et al5 identified player confidence as a key element for RTS, highlighting that negative emotions were associated with poorer performance and increased pain. Similarly, van der Horst et al34 emphasised the role of a positive mental attitude, which was perceived to reduce the reinjury risk and enhance performance during RTS. Motivation-related factors were reported in two studies.30 32 Athlete-driven pressure to progress emerged as a major challenge during RTS,32 while pressure to return quickly was also identified as influencing the decision-making process.30

Social domain

Interpersonal interactions

Several studies explored the influence of interpersonal interactions at RTS stage, with particular attention to therapeutic alliances, shared decision-making, communication patterns and fear of litigation.5 30 31 34 Within professional sports environments, stakeholders emphasised the importance of positioning the athlete as an active participant in the RTS decision-making process, highlighting their involvement in discussions and recovery-related choices.5 31 34 This athlete-centred approach was considered essential for fostering trust and adherence to rehabilitation.31 34 In this context, shared decision-making was described across studies as a commonly applied approach involving coordinated input from multiple stakeholders (eg, athlete, sports physician, physiotherapist, strength and conditioning coach and head coach).5 31 34 This collaborative process was framed as a strategy to support multidisciplinary assessment and foster alignment throughout the RTS process. Moreover, effective communication between them was also described as pivotal for promoting transparency, maintaining consistency in decision-making and achieving a shared understanding of rehabilitation objectives.5 Finally, one study reported that fear of litigation may influence clinical and managerial decision-making, particularly when RTS could result in reinjury, long-term impairment or career-threatening consequences; however, this factor was perceived as less influential than other environmental pressures (eg, pressure from the coach, pressure from the athlete).30

Internal and external environmental influences

The working environment emerged as a recurring element across the studies analysed, with two distinct subdomains identified from a social perspective: the internal and external environments surrounding the athlete.3032 Within the internal environment, hierarchical pressures were consistently reported to have a decisive influence on RTS decisions.30 32 In elite football, pressures from club management and internal staff were described as the most impactful factors affecting the decision-making process.32 This view is further supported by survey data indicating that pressure from the head coach significantly affects the clinical judgements of sports physicians and physiotherapists.30 In contrast, other studies explored the external environment,31 32 where the recommendation was to limit the influence of external pressures and prioritise internal team dynamics.31 Notably, Dunlop et al32 reported that pressures from the media, sponsors and agents were perceived as having a relatively minor influence on the RTS process, based on findings from their global survey.32

Contextual domain

Sport environment

Five studies analysed the influence of sports culture on RTS process following HSI. The time of the season consistently emerged as a key factor in the decision-making process.31 32 36 Critical phases, such as playoffs, finals or decisive matches, were found to influence the timing of RTS, often placing pressure on both athletes and support staff. Additionally, the level of sport played was identified as a contextual challenge31; nonetheless, evidence from a prospective study indicated that the level of competition did not significantly predict RTS outcomes after HSI.37 Other contextual variables investigated across the literature include team depth, playing position, individual playing style, transfer market periods and competition schedule, all of which may interact with RTS decisions in elite sports environments.3032

Healthcare management and resources

In the context of healthcare management, two studies explored these factors from different perspectives.30 32 Dunlop et al32 highlighted time constraints as a major challenge, noting that limited time availability can hinder the ability of professionals to provide optimal care throughout the rehabilitation process.32 On the other hand, another study emphasised that inadequate technical equipment impacts the RTS decision-making process by restricting comprehensive screening and assessment capabilities.30

Environmental and procedural enablers

Three studies addressed the role of pain management and performance analysis in the RTS process.5 31 35 One study reported that several national and international experts in muscle injury management recommended a cautious approach regarding the use of analgesics prior to medical clearance for RTS.31 In addition, two studies emphasised the importance of tracking the number of unrestricted training sessions completed before RTS, with both reporting a high level of expert consensus on this aspect.5 35

Quality assessment

The MMAT ratings of the analysed studies are summarised in table 1. All qualitative studies were assessed as being of good quality, each receiving a score of 5.5 34 35 Among the quantitative studies included in the final analysis, one was rated as high quality,38 while a non-randomised study was assessed as moderate quality with a score of 3 out of 5.37 The remaining quantitative descriptive studies ranged from poor30 33 to moderate quality.31 32 According to the OCEBM model, the highest quality of evidence was observed in the quantitative trials, classified as Levels 1B38 and 2B,37 although most studies (77%) were rated at Level 4.53035

Discussion

This synthesis identifies a wide range of psychological, social and contextual factors that mediate RTS in athletes recovering from HSI. Across the ten included studies, football emerged as the most represented sport.3136 Female participants were notably under-represented, with only 32 women among the 663 participants included5 30; this imbalance may partly reflect the persistent sex disparity observed within sports medicine and research environments.3941

The majority of studies were conducted within elite or professional sporting contexts, with only one study focusing on athletes at a recreational level.37 Furthermore, a substantial proportion of participants across the included studies were clinicians rather than athletes themselves. As a result, many of the findings identified in this review primarily reflect clinician perspectives53036 on the RTS process, rather than athletes’ lived experiences.37 38 This imbalance is important when interpreting the current evidence, as clinicians and athletes may perceive recovery, risk and readiness to RTS differently. The limited representation of athletes in the RTS HSI literature restricts the depth of understanding of how psychological, social and contextual factors are experienced and negotiated by athletes during the RTS process.

Among the three domains, the psychological dimension is the most extensively explored, whereas social and contextual factors appear to be comparatively under-investigated and often underestimated in clinical practice. Across the RTS process, elements such as shared decision-making,5 31 34 effective communication5 and the central role of the athlete emerge as particularly influential.5 31 34 Additionally, several studies reflect a growing interest in athletes’ emotional states5 31 32 and cognitive processes,5 30 32 33 35 37 recognising their relevance to recovery outcomes. However, the conceptualisation of psychological readiness varied considerably across studies,303234 35 and its practical application30 33 did not reflect the increasing emphasis placed on this construct in current clinical recommendations.5

Psychological considerations for RTS from HSI

Three key themes were identified within the psychological domain: emotional states, cognitive processing and volitional states (figure 1). Aligned with previous ACL literature,4244 fear is a common experience among athletes with HSI5 31 38; however, this review suggests that it may manifest in a variety of forms, such as fear of re-injury,31 fear of movement5 38 and fear of failure.31 These distinctions may have relevant clinical implications, as different types of fear may influence RTS trajectories. For instance, fear of re-injury may relate more closely to athletes’ perceptions of reinjury risk, potentially requiring greater emphasis on reassurance, education and graded RTS exposure. Fear of movement may contribute to protective movement behaviours or avoidance during sprinting, highlighting the potential value of progressive loading strategies and movement-specific confidence building. In contrast, fear of failure may be more closely linked to performance expectations and external pressures, suggesting the importance of communication within the wider support team and access to psychological support could play a pivotal role, as suggested previously.5 31 34 Additionally, anxiety and apprehension emerged as the most frequently referenced criteria used to support decision-making from a psychological perspective,5 31 32 underscoring their perceived clinical relevance. The review also highlighted that there is increasing emphasis on the athlete’s self-appraisal, particularly in terms of their perceived readiness to RTS and the significance of their injury.5 30 32 33 35 37 These forms of athlete self-assessments appear to be adopted by professionals as they provide reassurance and guidance in the clinical decision-making process. Self-efficacy5 34 and motivation30 32 were also highlighted, with motivation having both positive and negative consequences. On the one hand, more adaptive forms of motivation can facilitate engagement and adherence, while on the other hand, maladaptive forms of motivation may place pressure on clinicians and lead to premature or overly optimistic RTS decisions.30 32 As such, this factor requires careful consideration, with some studies in wider literature highlighting the role of autonomous versus controlled motivation for sustained engagement in sport-related behaviour.45 Given the critical importance of RTS in an athlete population, screening for anxiety and motivation should be prioritised, with particular attention to fostering constructive, positive communication.5 34

Social consideration for RTS from HSI

Two central themes were identified within the social domain: the interpersonal interactions and the environmental influences (figure 1). As supported by previous research,1517 the social context surrounding the athlete during injury recovery can significantly impact the rehabilitation trajectory. Particularly in HSI, shared decision-making within the team emerged as a salient aspect, widely endorsed by the practitioners interviewed.5 31 34 This collaborative approach in professional soccer typically involves the athlete, sports physician, physiotherapist, strength coach and head coach, promoting a multidimensional assessment of readiness. Moreover, the therapeutic alliance5 31 34 and quality of communication5 between the athlete and the healthcare team were identified as areas requiring further development, as shortcomings in these aspects may compromise a safe RTS. Concerning the surrounding environment, two critical influences were highlighted: hierarchical pressures within the club or institution,30 32 and external pressures from agents, sponsors or the media.31 32 These top–down dynamics may affect the objectivity of RTS decisions. Given the high recurrence rates of HSI,46 47 the use of a shared decision-making model is consistently recommended across studies.5 31 34 This approach, when supported by effective communication,5 may help to mediate both internal and external pressures, promoting athlete well-being. Despite its recognition as a best practice,6 evidence suggests that implementation of shared decision-making remains inconsistent within the sport medicine settings.48

Contextual consideration for RTS from HSI

Contextual factors influencing the RTS stage following HSI are rarely reported in the literature; however, some themes related to the sports environment, healthcare management and performance have been identified (figure 1). Among these, particular emphasis is placed on the timing within the competitive season,31 32 36 with researchers highlighting the need to consider periods of overload that may compromise recovery and risky RTS.33 Consistent with broader findings in sports medicine, which underline the influence of cultural norms such as the ‘no pain, no gain’ mentality and risk-taking behaviour,22 the use of painkillers has emerged as a concern warranting clinical attention.31 Other studies draw attention to time constraints and the lack of adequate technical facilities,30 32 both of which are viewed as barriers to optimal rehabilitation and RTS decision-making. Additionally, the completion of unrestricted training sessions has been proposed as a performance-based indicator of readiness for RTS.5 35

Psychological readiness conceptualisation

Across the studies, no clear consensus was reached regarding the definition of psychological readiness in the RTS process following HSI. While some studies attempted to conceptualise this construct,303234 35 none provided a precise or operational definition. Notably, several researchers acknowledged the critical importance of evaluating psychological readiness5 30 33; however, this assessment was often carried out informally, relying on unstructured conversations among team members rather than systematic tools or validated measures.33 Furthermore, the inconsistency in how and when this criterion is applied in clinical practice further complicates its applicability and interpretability. In comparison with the London Delphi consensus,5 in which 86% of specialists recommended the systematic assessment of athletes’ psychological readiness, these findings support and reinforce the need to address this gap in the upcoming phases of the research. Collectively, these findings underscore a pivotal gap in the current literature and clinical practice,30 revealing the need for more objective and standardised approaches to assess psychological preparedness during the RTS process in HSI.

Clinical implications

Based on the findings of this scoping review, preliminary clinical implications can be outlined, although they remain tentative due to the limited and varied nature of the available evidence. Athletes’ self-appraisal of readiness appears to play a potentially important role in the RTS process following HSI, with emotional responses such as fear, anxiety and apprehension frequently reported too. Although no standardised assessment approach currently exists, clinicians may consider exploring these dimensions through brief psychological screening and structured discussions during rehabilitation. When appropriate, validated tools adapted from other injury contexts (eg, the ACL-RTS after injury scale,49 the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia,50 51 the injury-psychological readiness to RTS scale52 53) could help identify psychological stressors that may delay or complicate RTS decisions. The literature also highlights the possible benefits of adopting an athlete-centred approach, involving the athlete in decision-making while balancing this with objective clinical indicators. This approach may promote shared decision-making and improve adherence to rehabilitation strategies. Multidisciplinary collaboration is occasionally mentioned as advantageous, though the practical details of such involvement remain insufficiently defined. Nevertheless, clinicians might explore structured interprofessional interactions, particularly in complex or high-stakes cases. A few studies suggest that clinicians should remain vigilant to excessive athlete motivation, which, if unchecked, could result in premature RTS decisions. Monitoring this risk may involve assessing psychological overcommitment or unrealistic expectations and introducing additional objective milestones when necessary. The development of a strong therapeutic relationship and open communication are recognised as strategies to help clinicians detect and manage internal and external pressures—such as those from club staff, agents or media—that could influence decision-making. These interpersonal aspects may also foster greater trust and transparency throughout the RTS process. Contextual factors, including the timing of RTS within the competitive calendar, should also be considered, particularly during high-stakes periods that may prompt rushed returns. Awareness of these temporal pressures could assist clinicians in anticipating organisational or environmental influences on return-to-play decisions. Lastly, although only referenced in a subset of studies, pain management strategies—especially the cautious use of analgesics—and completing at least one unrestricted training session were suggested as potential clinical indicators to support safe and confident RTS decisions. These factors, while procedural, may serve as useful checkpoints when combined with broader psychological and physical assessments. Although mainly informative, these implications may offer valuable guidance for stakeholders involved in return-to-sport decision-making following HSI and might form the basis for future research aimed at refining clinical practice in this area.

Limitations

The overall quality of the available evidence was limited, with most included studies rated as low to moderate in quality and primarily based on Delphi methods or survey designs, which inherently restrict the depth and granularity of the findings. The current evidence was also thematically narrow, with a strong focus on football and football-related professionals,3136 limiting the applicability of the findings to other sports disciplines. In addition, the perspective of athletes themselves was under-represented, reducing the comprehensiveness of the current understanding. Furthermore, the small number of eligible studies and their predominant concentration in European contexts constrain the generalisability of the results to other geographical regions or interdisciplinary frameworks.

Future directions

Future research would benefit from incorporating more robust observational designs capable of capturing the dynamic interactions within both team and individual sports settings. There is also a clear need for studies with stronger methodological rigour to empirically test themes that have thus far emerged mainly through expert consensus. Expanding research to include a broader range of sports disciplines and more diverse sporting contexts may improve the applicability of findings across different environments. Moreover, greater inclusion of female participants is warranted, given the limited representation of women within the current evidence base. Nonetheless, this review provides a useful starting point and identifies several priority areas for future investigation.

Conclusions

This scoping review identified a range of psychological, social and contextual factors that may influence the RTS process following HSI, as well as considerable inconsistency in the definition and assessment of psychological readiness. Although interpretation is limited by the heterogeneity of study designs and populations, the findings are consistent with those observed in similar injury contexts, such as ACL injuries. The review underscores the need to integrate non-physical factors into RTS decision-making and highlights a notable lack of evidence capturing the athlete’s subjective experience. Future prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the influence of these factors on re-injury risk and to inform the development of safer, more athlete-centred RTS frameworks.
