Current Variation in the Postoperative Management of Patients With Cerebral Palsy Undergoing Lower Extremity Surgery: A Survey of Surgeon Practices
Authors: Arianna Trionfo, Christina Herrero, Jason J Howard, M Wade Shrader
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren with cerebral palsy (CP) often develop lower limb deformities requiring surgical management. However, optimal postoperative management strategies—including weight-bearing progression, immobilization, and rehabilitation protocols—remain unclear. The purpose of this study was to evaluate current postoperative practices following lower extremity surgery in youth with CP.MethodsA 42-question electronic survey was sent to 114 practicing orthopaedic surgeons in the American Academy for Cerebral Palsy and Developmental Medicine. Six questions regarding seven surgical procedures (pelvic osteotomy, proximal femoral osteotomy, tibial osteotomy, isolated soft-tissue procedures, foot osteotomies, and foot fusions) were presented. Surgeons were asked about weight bearing, immobilization, initiation of physical therapy, standardized protocols, evaluation for inpatient rehabilitation, and educational sessions. Consensus was defined as >75% agreement for a given response (based on Delphi methodology).ResultsSixty-five surgeons from North America responded (57% response rate), with predominantly neuromuscular practices. Consensus was reached on only four of 42 questions (9.5%). Regarding weight bearing, 87.7% of surgeons allowed immediate weight bearing after isolated soft-tissue procedures. For immobilization, cast use reached consensus only for distal lower extremity surgeries including tibial osteotomy (95%), foot osteotomy (98%), and foot fusion (100%). Concerning rehabilitation and planning, no consensus was reached for any item.ConclusionsThere was substantial heterogeneity in postoperative practices for children with CP undergoing lower extremity surgery. Other than immediate weight bearing after soft-tissue procedures and casting after distal bony procedures, no clear consensus emerged for weight-bearing progression, immobilization method, or rehabilitation planning. This variability may influence functional outcomes and patient satisfaction. Introduction

Patients with cerebral palsy (CP) commonly develop progressive lower extremity deformities during growth, including musculotendinous contractures, joint instability, and bony malalignment. These impairments can hinder gross motor development, impair ambulation and sitting balance, cause pain, and significantly reduce quality of life [1,2]. Lower extremity surgery is commonly performed in this population to improve gait mechanics, alleviate pain, and enhance overall function [3]. Such procedures—including soft-tissue releases, osteotomies, and joint stabilization—require carefully coordinated postoperative management strategies to optimize outcomes [4].

Despite the evidence supporting orthopaedic surgery for lower extremity deformity correction in CP [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]], there is a lack of consensus regarding optimal postoperative strategies. Specifically, prior literature reports substantial heterogeneity in weight-bearing restrictions, immobilization methods, and rehabilitation guidelines [3]. This variability contributes to inconsistent recovery timelines and functional outcomes and confounds the ability to compare data between studies.

In adult orthopaedic surgical disciplines, such as sports medicine, joint replacement, and trauma, substantial research has focused on refining postoperative care protocols to enhance recovery, improve function, and reduce complications [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. These efforts have included early mobilization strategies, structured weight-bearing progression, and tailored rehabilitation protocols [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. By contrast, similar efforts for children with CP remain limited in spite of the unique postoperative challenges these patients face. Patients with CP are particularly susceptible to disuse atrophy, muscle weakness, and osteopenia, all of which may further compromise function and delay recovery. Therefore, children with CP stand to gain even more from postoperative care improvements and standardization using protocols specifically adapted to their clinical needs and functional goals.

While practice variation can be inferred from prior publications, no study to date has systematically evaluated the differences in postoperative management strategies for children with CP undergoing lower extremity surgery. The aim of this study was to characterize the current spectrum of postoperative practices and highlight areas requiring the development of evidence-based guidelines. Understanding areas of consensus and divergence, particularly regarding weight-bearing status, immobilization practices, and rehabilitation approaches, will inform future protocol development and help stimulate prospective research in this area.

Materials and methods

After institutional review board approval, a 42-question survey was designed and distributed electronically to 114 active orthopaedic surgeon members of the American Academy of Cerebral Palsy and Developmental Medicine. Survey questions were originally written by the senior author and refined by two established pediatric orthopaedic surgeons with predominantly neuromuscular practices. The survey was designed to query individual practices regarding weight-bearing restrictions, immobilization, and formal rehabilitation protocols following lower extremity surgery in youth with CP. The surgeons were asked about their postoperative management for the following surgical procedures: pelvic osteotomies, proximal femoral osteotomies, tibial osteotomy, isolated soft-tissue procedures, foot osteotomies, and foot fusions. Since this is the first survey of its kind, the questionnaire was not validated in a prior setting. Data collection occurred over a 3-month period via research electronic data capture (REDCap), a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies [14,15]. Using the Delphi consensus guidelines, an agreement greater than 75% was considered as consensus [16].

Results

A total of 65 responses were obtained (57% response rate). All completed a pediatric orthopaedic surgery fellowship and graduated between 1976 and 2021. Of respondents, 91.9% report practicing in the United States and 8.1% in Canada. Within the United States, the geographic distribution was Northeast (12, 19.4%), Southeast (10, 16.1%), Southwest (19, 30.6%), Northwest (6, 9.7%), and Central (10, 16.1%). Most surgeons reported that neuromuscular conditions comprised a large portion of their practice (41, 63% had ≥50% neuromuscular cases), and 52 (80%) worked in academic settings.

Using a 75% agreement threshold, consensus was reached on only four of 42 survey questions (9.5%). Immediate weight bearing was permitted after isolated soft-tissue-only releases in 87.7% of surgeons. For bony procedures, time to weight bearing varied widely, with a majority of surgeons allowing full weight bearing between 4 and 6 weeks for most procedures. No bony procedure achieved greater than 75% consensus on a single weight-bearing timeline (Fig. 2).

With respect to immobilization, three responses reached consensus regarding type of postoperative support, with cast immobilization used by nearly all respondents after tibial osteotomy (94.7%), foot osteotomy (98.2%), and foot fusion (100%). For all other bony procedures, the methods and time frame of immobilization were variable, with none reaching consensus (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This survey highlights notable variation in postoperative management among pediatric orthopaedic surgeons in North America treating youth with CP undergoing lower extremity surgery. The lack of consensus on most questions suggests that clinical decision-making is often guided by individual surgeon training, institutional norms, and experience rather than robust, evidence-based guidelines.

There was considerable variability in the timing of weight bearing following bony procedures. While most respondents reported initiation of weight bearing at 2 to 4 weeks postoperatively, some waited as long as 6 to 8 weeks. This likely reflects concerns over mechanical stability and potential complications such as implant failure, nonunion, or delayed healing. These precautionary measures may be intended to protect the surgical construct and minimize adverse events. In contrast, most surgeons reported allowing immediate weight bearing following soft-tissue-only procedures suggesting greater confidence in the healing process for these interventions.

Weight-bearing status is a critical factor influencing recovery, pain control, functional restoration, and complication rates. Only one prior study has directly examined postoperative weight bearing in children with CP. In a retrospective review of 25 children, Schaefer et al. found that children who underwent femoral osteotomy and followed early weight-bearing protocols stood 26 days sooner and returned to baseline walking nearly 4 months earlier than those who were restricted from weight bearing [17]. The authors concluded that “early mobilization after proximal femoral osteotomies in children with CP is safe, with reduced recovery time.”

In contrast, early mobilization is well established in other adult orthopaedic subspecialties. Studies in elderly fracture care and foot and ankle surgery have demonstrated that early weight bearing can promote recovery and reduce complications without significantly increasing the risk of hardware failure [[10], [11], [12], [13],18]. The variability revealed in our survey likely reflects a lack of high-quality, CP-specific evidence to guide these decisions. This underscores the need for further studies comparing outcomes and complications in early versus delayed weight-bearing strategies following lower extremity surgery in this population.

Importantly, weight bearing in patients with CP is inherently different from that in typically developing children. Ambulatory children with CP take significantly fewer steps per day than their peers [19], further highlighting the importance of carefully tailored postoperative activity planning. Risks associated with prolonged immobilization include muscular atrophy, decreased endurance, contracture formation, impaired neuromuscular coordination, and overall deconditioning, all of which can delay functional gains and prolong rehabilitation [[20], [21], [22]]. Additionally, extended periods of unloading may worsen bone density loss in a population already predisposed to osteopenia [23]. Although standard postoperative protocols often recommend 3 to 8 weeks of non-weight-bearing status in children with CP [17,20], this approach may inadvertently extend recovery timelines by exacerbating muscle weakness and reducing activity levels. While some studies suggest that children can return to preoperative gait function within 7 to 10 months, others have noted longer rehabilitation periods [24].

With respect to postoperative immobilization practices, our analysis revealed substantial variations in across procedures. Except for bony surgeries at or distal to the tibia, where there was consensus on the use of postoperative casting, responses varied widely. This may be due to biomechanical considerations, with distal procedures commonly considered to bear more stress than more proximal procedures. In some cases, internal fixation methods (eg, plates, screws, or intramedullary rods) may offer sufficient stability to obviate the need for casting after proximal osteotomies.

While there continues to be variation in immobilization after hip reconstruction [25], recent literature suggests that postoperative casting may not be necessary. Vasconcellos et al. [26] found no difference in radiographic outcomes or complication rates between children immobilized in spica casts versus those in abduction pillows after combined pelvic/femoral osteotomies [26]. They concluded that an abduction pillow was a “safe and effective alternative” to spica casting, allowing for easier care and mobility post surgery. For these cases, internal fixation likely provides adequate stability for early mobilization without increasing the risk of complications [26,27]. Early motion protocols in these cases have also been associated with benefits such as reduced caregiver burden, improved hygiene, and shorter hospital stays without negatively affecting surgical outcomes [26,27].

Prolonged casting in patients with CP carries unique risks. Sensory impairments and cognitive limitations can prevent these patients from recognizing or communicating discomfort, leading to complications such as pressure injuries and skin breakdown from ill-fitting or overly tight casts [[28], [29], [30]]. Additionally, cognitive challenges may impede a patient’s ability to report cast-related problems, resulting in delayed intervention. Therefore, while immobilization may be necessary for surgical healing, it must be balanced against the risk of iatrogenic harm. Future investigations should evaluate outcomes after fixed-length cast immobilization versus early mobilization strategies. Until then, surgeons should consider individual patient risks (skin integrity and cognitive function) when planning postoperative immobilization.

While postoperative physical therapy is a cornerstone of recovery for most institutions, only 50% to 70% of surveyed surgeons reported having standardized rehabilitation protocols for children with CP after lower extremity surgery. Accordingly, in the current study, initiation of postoperative physical therapy varied from immediate to 8 weeks. Rehabilitation guidelines have been established in other orthopaedic disciplines, such as arthroplasty, trauma, and sports medicine, where structured protocols have been shown to improve pain control, expedite return to function, and reduce complications [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Enhanced recovery after surgery pathways in scoliosis, for instance, has demonstrated significant benefits in minimizing hospital stays and promoting early mobilization [31].

Patients with CP are particularly susceptible to disuse atrophy following lower extremity surgery. A systematic review by van Bommel et al. [32] reported significant muscle weakness at 6 to 12 months post multilevel surgery, emphasizing the need for aggressive strength training [32]. This review also noted wide variation in therapy content, dosage, and duration, which the authors attributed to the heterogeneity of the CP population. Similarly, Lennon et al. found that increased therapy dosing was associated with improved functional recovery in a cohort of 69 patients with CP [33].

Only about half of surgeons reported evaluating patients with CP for possible inpatient rehabilitation following surgery. This likely reflects real-world barriers such as lack of access to specialized pediatric rehabilitation facilities or the presence of postoperative weight-bearing restrictions that prevent meaningful participation in intensive therapy. Inpatient rehabilitation can offer significant benefits for appropriate candidates, particularly those with complex needs or limited caregiver support, making it important to consider and address these systemic and clinical limitations during surgical planning.

Despite the critical role of caregiver involvement in postoperative success, only 49% to 58% of surgeons reported providing formal preoperative education on rehabilitation planning. This represents a potential missed opportunity as setting expectations early and providing caregivers with the information needed to prepare for the postoperative course are essential components of effective care. Wilson et al. in 2014 recommended standardized physical therapy assessments, including gait analysis, functional evaluations, and quality-of-life measures, before and after single-event multilevel surgeries [34]. Educating families on postoperative treatment frequency and therapy goals facilitates alignment between care teams and families, supporting a smoother recovery process.

As with surgical planning, shared decision-making and preoperative education help patients and caregivers understand the anticipated recovery timeline, mobility restrictions, and the level of support their child will require, allowing them to plan accordingly and reduce stress. Proactively involving caregivers not only facilitates smoother transitions from hospital to home but also promotes adherence to rehabilitation protocols, ultimately enhancing outcomes for children with CP.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As with all survey-based research, there is potential for nonresponse bias. However, given the relatively small field of neuromuscular orthopaedics, it is likely that the respondents are the representative of the surgeons most actively involved in treating this population. Additionally, the multiple-choice format may have limited respondents to selecting their most commonly used approach, potentially omitting alternative strategies they also use. While the survey attempted to cover a broad range of procedures, responses to individual surgeries may not fully reflect the complexity of cases where procedures are combined, as is often the case in lower extremity CP surgery. In addition, this study does not account for the heterogeneity of patients’ gross motor function, comorbidities, or surgical technique, all of which may influence decision-making and limit the generalizability of findings.

Institutional workflows, available resources, and external factors, such as insurance coverage, socioeconomic challenges, and access to specialized rehabilitation services, also influence postoperative care and were not directly addressed in this survey. In addition, this survey did not address specifics of postoperative therapies (frequency, intensity, and duration), which would be of particular interest to a multidisciplinary team. Despite these limitations, this work provides the first broad snapshot of North American practice patterns in CP postoperative management. A critical future priority will be the development of evidence-based postoperative care guidelines, ideally informed by prospective, multi-institutional trials assessing both functional and patient-centered outcomes.

To conclude, this study identified significant variability in postoperative management strategies for children with CP undergoing lower extremity surgery. The differences observed in weight-bearing protocols, immobilization practices, and rehabilitation approaches reflect both the complexity of care and the lack of standardized, evidence-based guidelines. These findings highlight the urgent need for multicenter, prospective studies to evaluate postoperative strategies and their impact on outcomes. Such efforts will be critical for developing tailored, data-driven protocols that optimize recovery and long-term function in this vulnerable population.
