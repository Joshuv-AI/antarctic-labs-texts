Unveiling CTGF and APOA1: key hub genes and therapeutic targets in dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Ran Wang, Jinmeng Chen, Jian Zhu, Xianlin Zhang, Xiaomei Li, Yiyao Jiang, Pingping Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), a life-threatening cardiovascular disease, affects more than 0.04% of the general population. However, the key genes associated with DCM are still unknown.MethodsThree publicly available datasets (GSE120895, GSE53081, and GSE42955) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA), gene ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on these DEGs. A protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network was then constructed to select the hub genes.Results862 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using an integrated bioinformatics approach. WGCNA indicated that DEGs in the blue and turquoise modules exhibited the strongest associations with DCM risk (blue module: r = 0.29, P = 2.5e-4; turquoise module: r = 0.25, P = 1.6e-3). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the hub genes revealed that the RTK-RAS pathway score was associated with DCM risk. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis revealed that the expression levels of CTGF and APOA1 may serve as sensitive and specific predictors of DCM risk (AUC of CTGF = 0.83, P = 0.002; AUC of APOA1 = 0.867, P = 0.001). Furthermore, the expression of CTGF and APOA1 was confirmed by Western blot.ConclusionsOur results revealed that CTGF and APOA1 are correlated with DCM risk and represent potential biomarkers worthy of further investigation. The identification of these hub genes and the implicated RTK-RAS pathway provides a hypothesis and a direction for future functional research on DCM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04260-0.

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) has an incidence of 5–7 cases per 100,000 people per year and a prevalence of more than 1 in 2500 in the general population [1]. It is a common and serious heart disease characterised by stretching and thinning of the heart muscle, dilation of one or both ventricles and reduced heart function [2]. As defined by the European Society of Cardiology, DCM can be divided into two main types: genetic and acquired [3]. The genetic aetiology of DCM is complex. A variety of gene mutations and abnormal expression patterns can lead to the onset and progression of DCM. Previous studies have shown that mutations in genes encoding sarcomeric, fibronectin, cytoskeletal, desmosomal and ion channel proteins play key roles in the development of DCM [4]. However, the mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of DCM are still unclear. Thus, studies are needed to identify the exact genes that cause DCM to provide effective diagnostic and therapeutic methods for DCM.

Using ChIP-seq and transcriptomics, we previously studied the role of lysine-specific demethylase 5 A (KDM5A) in the proliferation of cardiac fibroblasts cultured from DCM samples. However, the results do not accurately or comprehensively explain the cause of DCM [5]. To address this gap, bioinformatics methods are needed to identify the key genes and regulatory mechanisms that affect DCM. Hub genes encode regulators that are essential for biological processes. Other genes in the network act in synergy with the hub genes to regulate disease processes. Thus, the identification of the hub gene that controls the progression of DCM will be an important target for precise treatment. Recently, the use of bioinformatics analysis to search for hub genes that cause cardiovascular diseases, such as myocardial ischaemia, aortic valve calcification, and atherosclerosis, has become widespread [6–8]. However, the hub genes associated with DCM remain to be studied. In this study, we analysed three human expression profiling datasets downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and identified the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between DCM tissues and normal tissues. We also detected significant modules and hub genes via various bioinformatics approaches. These findings may facilitate further investigations into the underlying molecular and gene regulatory mechanisms of DCM.

Materials and methods

Access to public data

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) is an open functional genomics database of high-throughput data, including microarray, gene expression and chip data. Three expression profiling datasets [GSE120895, GSE53081 and GSE42955] were obtained from the GEO. The GSE120895 dataset includes information on forty-seven DCM tissue samples and eight heart tissue samples from individuals with a normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The GSE53081 dataset contains information on eight DCM tissue samples and eight heart tissue samples from individuals without heart disease. GSE42955 consists of information on twelve DCM tissue samples and five healthy heart samples.

DEG identification

Statistically significant DEGs were identified via the limma package with a threshold of P < 0.05 and a log2 (fold change) ≥ 0.5 or log2 (fold change) ≤ −0.5. P values were adjusted via the Benjamini‒Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The sample sizes for the DCM and control groups in each dataset were as follows: GSE120895 (47 DCM samples, 8 controls), GSE53081 (8 DCM samples, 8 controls), and GSE42955 (12 DCM samples, 5 controls).

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA can be used to explore coexpressed gene modules and analyse associations between gene networks. In addition, it can be used to find the relationships between modules and traits and to further screen hub nodes in a network. The detailed procedure has been described previously [9].

Functional annotation of DEGs via DAVID and metascape analyses

DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp; version 6.8) was used for functional annotation of the DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO is generally used for enrichment analysis of genomic data. GO analysis revealed the main enriched cellular component (CC), biological process (BP) and molecular function (MF) terms. KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp) is a comprehensive database of genomic, chemical and systemic functional information. DAVID was used for the GO and KEGG analyses.

Metascape (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) is a powerful tool that can be used to annotate large numbers of genes or proteins according to their functions. It integrates several major functional databases, such as GO, KEGG and UniProt, to analyse not only human data but also data from many other species and to analyse not only a single dataset but also multiple gene sets simultaneously. Metascape was used for this study.

PPI network construction, module analysis, and hub gene selection and analysis

This procedure has been described in a previous study [10]. The PPI network was constructed via the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) online database (http://string-db.org; version 12.0). The minimum required interaction score was medium confidence > 0.4.

Cytoscape (version 3.7.2), an open-source visualisation software tool, can be used to visualise the PPI network and can be used to analyse the network in more detail. All the data and information were exported and plotted via Cytoscape software. The DEGs are shown as nodes, and the interactions between DEGs are shown as edges between nodes.

The Molecular Complex Detection tool (MCODE), a plug-in of Cytoscape, was used to screen and identify the most significant module in the PPI network, and the criteria were as follows: degree of cut-off = 2, MCODE score > 5, k score = 2, node score cut-off = 0.2, and maximum depth = 100.

As a plug-in to Cytoscape, CytoHubba was then used to identify hub genes in the network, and the hub genes were assessed according to the degree, edge percolated component (EPC), maximum clique centrality (MCC) and maximum neighbourhood component (MNC) algorithms.

Finally, a Venn diagram was used to identify common hub DEGs between the MCODE and CytoHubba analyses.

Identification of chemicals associated with the hub genes, interactions among the hub genes, KEGG pathways and core mutations

The CTD database is a large public resource of literature-based predictions regarding associations between chemistry, genetic products, phenotypes, diseases and environmental exposures. We searched the database for hub genes to identify chemicals associated with DCM risk. Hub gene interactions were analysed via Pearson correlation analysis.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated on the basis of the expression data of the hub genes obtained from the GEO datasets (GSE120895, GSE53081, and GSE42955). The correlation analysis was performed via R software with the cor.test() function, and statistical significance was determined with a P value threshold of 0.05.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the hub genes was performed using specific tools (e.g., DAVID, KOBAS, or clusterProfiler in R). The KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was used as the reference source for pathway annotations. The enrichment results were visualised via the [R package ggplot2].

Hub genes with a common function show a relatively high degree of enrichment and are selected as an appropriate gene group cluster in a specific pathway. The core mutation analysis of hub genes was based on the UCSC database: UCSC (University of California, Santa Cruz) provides a powerful genomic information resource for visualising and analysing genomic data, including mutation data. The UCSC Genome Browser has a variety of features; for example, it allows viewing of different annotations of the genome, comparative genomics analysis and visualisation of mutation data.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis is a graphical tool used to evaluate the performance of binary classifiers by plotting the relationship between the true positive rate (TPR) and false-positive rate (FPR). The true positive rate (TPR) was plotted against the false-positive rate (FPR) to evaluate the performance of the classifier. The relationships between the hub genes and DCM risk were assessed via ROC analysis.

Tissue samples and ethics

From June 2019 to December 2024, we obtained 18 left ventricular myocardial tissue samples from DCM patients undergoing heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation. These samples were used for Western blot. All the samples were used to verify the results of the bioinformatic analysis. Ten left atrial appendage (LAA) samples were obtained during valve replacement surgery as control samples [11, 12]. The patients’ characteristics are summarised in Supplementary Table 1.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Bengbu Medical College (reference number: 2024[103]). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the Ethics Committee approved the procedures.

Western blot

Western blot was performed as described in our previous study [13]. Briefly, tissue was lysed with RIPA buffer (Sigma–Aldrich, USA), and the protein concentrations of the lysates were determined using BCA protein assay reagent (Pierce Biotechnology, USA). Total protein samples (40 µg) were separated electrophoretically, transferred to a PVDF membrane and probed via monoclonal primary antibodies against CTGF (rabbit anti-human, 1:1000, Servicebio, GB11078), APOA1 (rabbit anti-human, 1:1000, Proteintech, 14427-1-AP), ERK (rabbit anti-human, 1:1000, Servicebio, GB11560), RAF1 (rabbit anti-human, 1:1000, Servicebio, GB11780), MEK1 (mouse anti-human, 1:1000, Servicebio, GB 15601), KRAS (rabbit anti human, 1:1000, Servicebio, GB115607), PI3K (rabbit anti human, 1:1000, Servicebio, GB11525) and GAPDH (rabbit anti human, 1:5000, Servicebio, GB15004) were used. HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:3000, Servicebio, GB23303) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:3000, Servicebio, GB23301) secondary monoclonal antibodies were used. The bands were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Bio-Rad, USA) and analysed via Image-Pro Plus software.

Statistical analysis

The analyses in this study were performed via Perl, R (version 4.3.3), SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, USA). The R package limma was used for DEG screening. OmicShare Tools was used for data visualisation [14].

Results

Identification of DEGs and WGCNA

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and control samples, we analysed three publicly available datasets (GSE120895, GSE53081, and GSE42955) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The raw data were normalised via the limma package in R. To minimise batch effects, the Combat function from the sva package was applied before DEG analysis. DEGs were identified based on the following selection criteria: adjusted P value < 0.05 (Benjamini‒Hochberg correction), log2-fold change (FC) ≥ 0.5 or ≤ −0.5, and cross-validation across all three datasets to ensure robust identification. The volcano plots of the DEGs are shown in Fig. 1a. A total of 862 DEGs that were consistently dysregulated across the three datasets were identified (Fig. 1b). The complete list of DEGs is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

To explore the coexpression patterns among these DEGs and identify potential functional modules, we applied weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA). First, outlier samples were detected using hierarchical clustering, and those with clustering heights above the 95th percentile were removed. Genes with excessive missing values were excluded via the goodSamplesGenes() function. A scale-free network was then constructed by determining an appropriate soft-thresholding power (β). Based on the pickSoftThreshold() function, β = 12 was selected as the optimal value, where the scale-free topology fit index (R²) exceeded 0.85 (Fig. 1c).

Next, a signed adjacency matrix was generated and converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to quantify gene connectivity. Gene modules were detected using hierarchical clustering of the TOM dissimilarity matrix implemented in the flashClust package. The dynamic tree cut method was applied, with a minimum module size of 30 genes and a deepSplit value of 2 to allow more refined module separation. Each module was assigned a unique colour for visualisation (Fig. 1d).

To determine the clinical relevance of these modules, we calculated module eigengenes (MEs), which represent the first principal component of each module’s expression profile. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed between MEs and clinical traits (DCM vs. control). The ME values of five modules (yellow, green, blue, turquoise, and grey) were significantly correlated with DCM risk (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1e).

Among them, the ME values of the blue and turquoise modules exhibited the most significant associations with DCM risk (blue module: r = 0.29, P = 2.5e-4; turquoise module: r = 0.25, P = 1.6e-3). Given their significant association with the disease trait, we further examined the inter-module relationships. Further analysis revealed that these two modules also presented the closest interaction (module distance = 0.2) (Fig. 1f and g), suggesting potential functional connectivity. Additionally, to further validate the importance of these two modules, we assessed gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) values. GS is a measure of the correlation between individual gene expression and DCM status, whereas MM represents the correlation between a gene’s expression and its module eigengene. Genes in the blue (n = 149) and turquoise (n = 146) modules presented high GS and MM values, indicating their statistically significant associations with DCM risk.

These findings suggest that genes in the blue and turquoise modules are not only significantly correlated with DCM status but also highly coexpressed and functionally interconnected within the coexpression network. Therefore, these two modules were selected for downstream enrichment analysis to explore their potential biological roles in DCM pathogenesis.

Enrichment analysis of the DEGs

To further investigate the biological functions of the key gene modules associated with DCM, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on genes within the blue and turquoise modules. These two modules were selected because they showed the strongest correlations with DCM (blue module: r = 0.29, P = 2.5e-4; turquoise module: r = 0.25, P = 1.6e-3) (Fig. 1g) and exhibited the closest interaction on the basis of module clustering analysis (distance = 0.2) (Fig. 1f), suggesting their potential functional relevance to disease pathogenesis.

Biological process analysis revealed that the DEGs were enriched mainly in the terms “cellular response to fibroblast growth factor”, “positive regulation of the apoptotic process” and “apoptotic process” (Fig. 2a). Cellular component analysis revealed that the DEGs were associated mainly with the cytosol, cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 2b). In terms of molecular function, the DEGs were involved mainly in protein binding, DNA binding and calcium ion binding (Fig. 2c). KEGG analysis revealed that the DEGs were enriched in pathways related to cancer, lipids, atherosclerosis and the AGE-RAGE signalling pathway (Fig. 2d). Metascape enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs between the control and DCM groups were significantly enriched in the positive regulation of cell death and negative regulation of organelle organisation (Fig. 2e).

Analysis of the CTD, hub gene interactions, pathways, and genetic alterations

We searched the CTD database for the 7 hub genes, all of which were associated with DCM, as shown in Fig. 4a. In addition to DCM, Fig. 4a presents associations of the hub genes with four other diseases. These diseases were included for comparison to explore whether the identified gene‒chemical associations are specific to DCM or are commonly involved in other conditions. The selection of these diseases was based on their potential relevance to cardiovascular diseases and their presence in the CTD database, allowing us to assess overlapping or distinct gene‒chemical interactions across different disease contexts.

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted via gene expression data from GEO datasets (GSE120895, GSE53081, and GSE42955), and correlation coefficients were calculated for the hub genes across different samples. The results of the Pearson correlation analysis are shown in Fig. 4b. KRAS expression was positively correlated with EGR1 expression (R = 0.78, P < 0.001) and negatively associated with the relative level of APOA1 (R = −0.607, P = 0.012). EGR1 was positively associated with COL1A1 (R = 0.717, P = 0.0017). FOSs were positively associated with APOA1 (R = 0.659, P = 0.005).

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the RTK-RAS pathway, including the KRAS pathway, is potentially associated with DCM risk (Fig. 4c). Amplification of the hub genes may be the main cause of DCM according to genetic alteration analysis (Fig. 4d).

ROC curve, correlation and linear regression analyses

ROC curve analysis indicated that the identified hub genes had diagnostic potential in DCM samples on the basis of our enrichment of DEGs from three GSE datasets. The area under the curve was 0.63 for KRAS with P = 0.264, 0.598 for FOS with P = 0.382, 0.566 for EGR1 with P = 0.621, 0.83 for CTGF with P = 0.002, 0.585 for COL1A1 with P = 0.450, 0.569 for CCL2 with P = 0.540, and 0.867 for APOA1 with P = 0.001 (Fig. 5; Table 1). To further verify the diagnostic value of these hub genes, we also performed univariate linear regression analysis of the associations between the expression levels of the seven genes and DCM risk according to the gene coexpression profile summary for the three datasets. Table 1 shows that APOA1 expression was associated with DCM risk according to the univariate linear regression model (P = 0.004).

Western blot

On the basis of the above results, we verified the role of CTGF and APOA1 in the diagnosis of DCM. Western blot was used to verify the findings mentioned above, and the expression of CTGF, KRAS, ERK, MEK1, and RAF1 was significantly greater in DCM patients than in healthy controls (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6a, c, d, e, f). The original Western blot images without cropping are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The relative expression of APOA1 and PI3K was significantly lower in DCM patients than in healthy controls (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6b, g).

Discussion

In the present study, by bioinformatic analysis of the gene expression profiles of DCM and control samples from three datasets, we found that (1) the RTK-RAS pathway plays a key role in the pathogenesis of DCM and (2) the expression levels of the hub genes CTGF and APOA1 are significantly correlated with the progression of DCM. These genes may be precise potential therapeutic targets for DCM.

Role of the RTK-RAS pathway in DCM

In general, previous studies of DCM have enriched DEGs in pathways related to focal adhesion, ECM receptor interaction and cell adhesion, among others [15–17]. Our findings go beyond this. Interestingly, in our study, the enrichment of pathways reflecting the pathogenesis of DCM was more similar to that of cancer. In particular, we speculated and verified that the RTK/RAS pathway is associated with DCM risk.

RTKs are located on the cell surface and transduce extracellular signals to the nucleus via the RAS/mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase pathway. This pathway controls a wide range of cellular functions, including survival, proliferation and differentiation [18]. Previous studies have shown that the RTK/RAS/MAP kinase pathway is the most common target of these oncogenic mutations [19]. The functions of RTKs in myocardial regeneration, malformations of the heart and outflow tract, DCM, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and cardiac fibroblasts have been widely studied [20–23]. However, the role of the RTK/RAS pathway in cardiovascular disease is unclear. Although we validated the results of the bioinformatic analysis with DCM samples, the specific mechanisms have not been elucidated due to the lack of cellular and animal experiments. One possible mechanism is that RAS is responsible for the activation of downstream cascades. The effectors controlled by RAS, such as PI3K, ERK and MEK, have been implicated in various cardiac diseases [24–26]. Therefore, we will confirm the role of the RTK/RAS pathway in DCM in a future study.

Role of CTGF and APOA1 in DCM

CTGF is a member of the CCN family of multifunctional matricellular proteins. CTGF has been implicated in mediating cell adhesion, proliferation and skeletal development, which are common features of cardiac remodelling. In addition, as a profibrotic cytokine, CTGF upregulation is associated with the deleterious process of cardiac fibrosis, which is a hallmark of DCM [27–29]. In the protein kinase C ε (PKCε) mouse model of DCM, Koshman YE and colleagues suggested that increased CTGF contributes to the pathological remodelling of DCM. Compared with control mice, PKCε mice presented an approximately 50% increase in collagen content. CTGF-neutralising antibodies and CTGF inhibition can slow the progression of LV remodelling and improve the ECM remodelling gene signature [30]. Zhao JQ and colleagues predicted that eleven genes, including CTGF and COL1A1, may play a role in the development of DCM by comparing DEGs between DCM samples and healthy control samples [31]. In our study, CTGF expression was significantly associated with DCM risk according to bioinformatic analysis and validation tests. Currently, the diagnostic criteria for DCM mainly include echocardiography, myocardial magnetic resonance imaging and patient clinical features. Although the evidence for genetic DCM has been curated, the exact biomarkers for the diagnosis of DCM are still unclear. Jordan E and colleagues reported that 19 highly expressed genes were recommended for the genetic evaluation of DCM [32]. However, the emergence of innovative sequencing technologies and bioinformatic analysis methods has made early prediction and precise diagnosis of DCM possible. We speculate that genetic test panels, including CTGF testing of blood or myocardial samples from DCM patients, will improve the accuracy of DCM diagnosis by combining existing diagnostic criteria.

APOA1 is a major component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and, in addition to its role in reverse cholesterol transport, has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [33]. APOA1 deficiency results in the failure of the formation of normal HDL particles, the inability to transport cholesterol effectively from tissues back to the liver and, therefore, a predisposition for coronary heart disease [34]. Previous studies on the role of APOA1 in cardiovascular disease have focused on diabetic cardiomyopathies and coronary heart disease [35–37]. Recently, the cardioprotective functions of APOA1 have been demonstrated. Amin R and colleagues reported that APOA1 overexpression can reduce myocardial interstitial fibrosis and myocardial apoptosis and improve cardiac function [38]. Li S and colleagues investigated the prognostic value of the ratio of APOB to APOA1 in patients with heart failure. They reported that an APOB/APOA1 ratio ≥ 0.69 was an independent predictor of increased mortality risk in HF patients [39]. Persistent inflammation is one of the pathogenetic features of DCM [40]. In our study, we found that APOA1 levels decreased in patients with DCM. On the basis of these results of previous studies and our findings, we speculate that low APOA1 expression is associated with worsening cardiac function and an inflammatory state in DCM patients. Therefore, APOA1 may serve as a diagnostic or prognostic marker for DCM, especially for assessing inflammatory and metabolic states.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, some datasets on DCM in the GEO might be ignored since the descriptions of the datasets were unclear. With the emergence of new integrated analysis methods, hub genes are expected to be discovered through more comprehensive DCM datasets. Second, we focused on the role of hub genes in DCM and verified their expression in DCM patient samples. Although multiple bioinformatic analysis methods were used to identify the hub genes associated with DCM through rigorous screening, cellular and animal studies are needed to demonstrate the relationships between hub genes and DCM. Third, while our study integrated multiple GEO datasets to minimize bias and identified hub genes through rigorous bioinformatic screening, we acknowledge a critical limitation regarding the Western blot validation cohort. The control samples were obtained from the LAA during valve replacement surgery, whereas the DCM samples were from the left ventricular myocardium. Although using surgical LAA tissue as a control is a common practice given the extreme difficulty in obtaining healthy left ventricular myocardium, differences in anatomical structure and cellular composition between these tissues may introduce potential bias in comparing protein expression levels. Furthermore, demographic characteristics such as age and sex between the DCM and control groups were not matched, and due to the limited sample size, we were unable to perform multivariate adjustment for these confounders. Therefore, the observed protein expression differences should be interpreted with caution, and our primary conclusions rely more heavily on the bioinformatic analysis of matched left ventricular tissues from public datasets. Future studies with perfectly matched control tissues or experimental models are needed to confirm these findings. Finally, the ROC analysis represents a preliminary, internal validation, and the reported AUC values require confirmation in an independent, prospective cohort to assess true diagnostic utility and guard against overfitting. Accordingly, CTGF and APOA1 are considered to be candidate biomarkers whose diagnostic value needs further rigorous external validation. Furthermore, the role of these biomarkers relative to or in combination with standard diagnostic tools remains to be determined and is an important direction for future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we performed an integrative analysis of three GEO datasets and revealed that CTGF and APOA1 levels are correlated with DCM risk and represent potential biomarkers worthy of further investigation. The identification of these hub genes and the implicated RTK/RAS pathway provides a hypothesis and a direction for future functional research on DCM.
