Size-optimized ceria nanoparticles attenuate intervertebral disc degeneration by rescuing nucleus pulposus cell senescence via reducing oxidative stress and stimulating PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Xinxing Wang, Zhiwei Liu, Ling Zou, Yanbin Shi, Yong Chen, Anwei Zhang, Xiaoling Liu, Yonghui Wu, Mei Gao, Zhongliang Deng, Kai Shen, Junli Liu, Daigui Cao, Xiaochao Yang, Shengli Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD), a condition characterized by nucleus pulposus (NP) cell senescence and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, is a major cause of degenerative spinal disorders. Current clinical management remains limited. In this study, we evaluate PEG600-coated ceria nanoparticles (CeNPs) of three sizes (1.2, 4, and 40 nm) for their potential to mitigate NP cell senescence and IVDD in rat model. Among them, the 4 nm-PEG600 CeNPs exhibited the highest therapeutic efficacy, which can be attributed to their low cytotoxicity and improved physiological stability within disc tissue. Mechanistically, CeNPs attenuated IVDD by countering ROS accumulation-induced NP senescence, as supported by assessments of ECM metabolism, senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) expression, cellular senescence and proliferation, and mitochondrial function. Importantly, beyond their direct ROS-scavenging ability, the 4 nm PEG600-CeNPs suppressed cellular senescence through inhibition of AKT Ser473 phosphorylation in the PI3K-AKT pathway. These findings establish size-optimized CeNPs as a promising dual-targeting (antioxidant and anti-senescence) strategy for IVDD treatment.

Introduction

Spinal degeneration with low back pain (LBP) is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder that affects almost all ages, contributing to frailty and disability in both developed and developing countries as the leading cause [1]. Notably, non-specific LBP accounts for approximately 90 % of all cases. Among the identifiable causes, intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) stands out as the most prevalent structural pathology detectable by radiological imaging [2]. IVDD is increasingly recognized as a pathology that can originate as early as adolescence and is presumed to be an initiating factor of LBP. Although its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, current clinical management relies primarily on conservative measures for symptomatic relief and surgical interventions for decompression. However, these approaches are largely palliative, offering only temporary functional improvement without addressing the underlying processes of tissue repair and regeneration [3,4]. Therefore, developing novel and effective strategies targeting pathological mechanisms is critical to achieve long-term functional maintenance for IVDD.

The intervertebral disc (IVDs) is composed of three primary components: a central, highly hydrated nucleus pulposus (NP), a surrounding annulus fibrosus (AF) with concentric lamellae, and cartilaginous endplates (CEP) that connect to the vertebral bodies. Each of these structures plays a crucial role in maintaining the disc's normal biomechanical function [5,6]. Growing evidence indicates that degeneration of the NP is an initial pathological event in IVDD. Prolonged abnormal biomechanical stress promotes the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which disrupts the hypoxic environment of the NP and induces cellular senescence. Senescent NP cells undergo irreversible cell cycle arrest and exhibit the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), characterized by the sustained secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and catabolic factors. The persistent presence of this inflammatory milieu ultimately drives extracellular matrix (ECM) catabolism, compromises the biomechanical integrity of the disc, and contributes to the clinical manifestations of disc herniation and nerve compression [[7], [8], [9]]. Specifically, superfluous ROS (O2•-, •OH, H2O2, etc) can induce oxidative stress damage, such as Ca2+ overload, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, DNA damage and inflammatory pathways activation, which further result in mitochondrial dysfunction and irreversible cellular senescence [10,11]. In addition, impaired mitochondria in senescence cells trigger more severe oxidative stress by producing excess ROS, thereby a vicious cycle is generated to increase NP cell senescence and accelerate IVDD development [12]. Therefore, rejuvenating NP cell senescence by restoring the redox homeostasis in NP tissue may be a promising strategy to restrict IVDD progression.

Although various antioxidant nanozymes, such as polydopamine and Prussian blue nanoparticles, have been demonstrated to effectively eliminate excess ROS in IVDs, their clinical translation remains challenging. Key obstacles include low bioavailability, unfavorable pharmacokinetics, non-renewable antioxidant capacity, and potential toxicity at high doses [[13], [14], [15]]. Metal-based nanomaterials have acquired extensive concern in recent years. Among them, ceria nanoparticles (CeNPs) enable transient and regenerative exchange between Ce3+ and Ce4+ oxidation states on account of oxygen vacancy in the crystal structure. This unique property confers potent antioxidant capabilities, enabling CeNPs to mimic superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities. Consequently, CeNPs show great potential for ROS scavenging, anti-inflammatory effects, and tissue repair, with applications explored in spinal cord injury, sepsis, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and acute lung injury [16]. Recently, Han et al. reported that CeNPs-loaded hydrogel could treat IDD by filling AF defects and inhibiting ROS and inflammatory reactions in AF area [17]. Nevertheless, annulus fibrosus (AF) repair addresses only one aspect of IVDD pathology. Inhibiting nucleus pulposus (NP) cell senescence and subsequent tissue degeneration remains the central therapeutic challenge. Given the unique physiological structure of IVDs, locally applied ceria nanomaterials must possess potent ROS-scavenging capability, low histo- and cytotoxicity, and favorable diffusivity within the hydrated NP microenvironment. Furthermore, an optimal drug retention time is crucial to avoid disease exacerbation from repeated injections.

Size control is an effective measure to regulate the pharmacokinetic parameters and ROS clearance activity for nanomaterials [18]. Smaller-sized CeNPs typically possess more specific surface and higher Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio, which endow nanoparticles with stronger simulated enzymatic activity [19]. Relatively, larger-sized nanoparticles diffuse slower in vivo, and have longer residence time in the tissue, which is beneficial for long-term treatment and target therapy in most cases. Herein, we constructed three sizes of CeNPs with hydrophilic modification to accommodate the relatively enclosed hydrated microenvironment in IVDs, and 4 nm CeNPs exhibited strong simulated enzymatic activity, low cytotoxicity, appropriate residence time and the most favorable therapeutic effect on IVDD. More importantly, we demonstrated that 4 nm CeNPs could mitigate the progression of IVDD by restraining NP cell senescence via reducing oxidative stress and stimulating PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Experimental section

Synthesis CeNPs of 1.2 nm

The CeNPs of 1.2 nm were synthesized by our previous methed [20]. Briefly, 434 mg Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, 774 mg TOPO and 564 mg OAc were dissolved in 2 mL ethanol at 80 °C. Then added 5 mL ODE in the mixture and heated to 180 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the products were washed by acetone and toluene for several times until the supernatant was colorless. The products were dispersed in 10 mL THF and stored at 4 °C.

Synthesis CeNPs of 4 nm

700 mg Ce(NO3)3·6H2O and 1 g TOPO were dissolved in 3 mL ethanol at 90 °C. After that, 5 mL ODE were added to the mixture and the ethanol was removed through vacuum for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, 100 μL OAm was slowly added under stirring condition and solution was heated to 190 °C for 15 min. The other procedures were as same as the CeNPs of 1.2 nm.

Synthesis CeNPs of 40 nm

217 mg Ce(NO3)3·6H2O and 290 mg TOPO were dissolved in 2 mL ethanol at 80 °C. Then added 5 mL ODE in the mixture and heated to 180 °C for 30 min. The other procedures were as same as the CeNPs of 1.2 nm.

Surface modification of CeNPs

The CeNPs were modified with PEG600 ligands using our previous method [20,21]. In brief, 554 mg PEG600, 105 mg NHS and 175 mg EDC were dissolved in 10 mL dichloromethane at room temperature for 8 h to activated carboxyl group. Then the solvent was removed by rotary evaporation. After that, 20 mL water solution containing 100 mg alendronate sodium and 250 mg Na2CO3 were added to the activated ligand and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. After that, the 1 mL synthesized CeNPs and 5 mL THF were mixed with 5 mL ligand solution stirred at 80 °C for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, the water layer containing modified CeNPs was separated and precipitated out by acetone. The surface modified CeNPs were dialyzed in ultrapure water for 24 h.

Characterization of CeNPs

The morphology and size of the CeNPs of 1.2 nm were determined by FEI Talos F200X transmission electron microscope (TEM) operated at 200 kV and the other size of CeNPs were determined by Hitachi HT7700 instrument operated at 80 kV. The size distribution was measured by Nano Measure 1.2 software. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on TA SDT650 instrument with heating rate at 20 °C min −1.

ROS scavenging capability

The ROS scavenging capability of the surface modified CeNPs was evaluated by our previous procedures [20,21]. In detail, a working solution containing 800 μL EDTA-NA2 (0.1 M), 300 μL NBT (2 mM), 200 μL riboflavin (0.6 mM) and 11.4 mL sodium phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.8) was prepared. The 50 μL diluting CeNPs was mixed with 100 μL working solution in 96-well plates and shaken for 60 s, then exposed to white light for 90 s. The ROS scavenging capability was determined by detecting the absorbance at 560 nm. The assay principle is based on the photolysis of riboflavin under light irradiation, which generates blue-colored radicals. The radical scavenging activity is quantified by measuring the decrease in absorbance at the characteristic wavelength, as the color fades upon radical elimination. The group without nanoparticles (only working solution) was designated as the positive control. The experiment was performed with n = 3 biological replicates, each comprising n = 3 technical replicates.

Transcriptome data collection and analysis

Bioinformatics analyses and visualization were conducted using R (v4.3.2). Gene expression profiles of normal and senescent human NP tissues and cells were obtained from two publicly available datasets in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession numbers: GSE167199 and GSE245147; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Differential expressed genes (DEGs) of each dataset were calculated using R package “limma” using a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.5, and visualized by volcano plot using R package “EnhancedVolcano”. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were carried out with the R package “clusterProfiler” and “org.Hs.eg.db” based on GO and KEGG database [22,23]. the Benjamini-Hochberg method was utilized to adjust P values in functional annotation and P.adjust <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Gene set “Oxidative stress” was extracted from GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org).

Local pharmacokinetic parameters of CeNPs

Male SD rats (250–300 g) were supplied by Army Medical University, and all animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (AMUWEC20228022), following institutional guidelines. Rats were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 4). For each group, the respective CeNPs (8 μg in 4 μL PBS) were locally injected into the disc between coccyx vertebrae 7 and 8 (Co7/8) using a 33-gauge Hamilton micro-syringe. IVDs along with adjacent vertebrae were collected at 1, 3 and 7 days post-administration. Samples were microwave-digested in 20 mL concentrated nitric acid using a CEM MARS6 system following the manufacturer's standard tissue digestion protocol. Elemental concentrations of cerium were determined by ICP-AES (PerkinElmer Avio 200). Outliers showing the greatest deviation from group averages were excluded to minimize experimental error.

Establishment and treatment of IVDD model

All surgical procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. Male SD rats weighed 250–300 g were randomly assigned to 5 groups (n = 4): (1) sham group (without needle puncture); (2) PBS group (with needle puncture and PBS injection); (3) 1.2 nm-PEG600 group (with puncture and 1.2 nm-PEG600 CeNPs injection); (4) 4 nm-PEG600 group (with puncture and 4 nm-PEG600 CeNPs injection); (5) 40 nm-PEG600 group (with puncture and 40 nm-PEG600 CeNPs injection). After the anesthesia, a 21-gauge needle was inserted into the center of the Co7/8 IVDs and maintained in position for 10 s. Then the test substance (1 μg in 4 μL PBS) was simultaneously injected into the disc using a 33-gauge Hamilton micro-syringe.

Imaging analysis

MRI was performed at 4 weeks or 8 weeks post-surgery. T2-weighted imaging and mapping were performed using a 7.0T MRI scanner (BioSpec 70/20 USR, Bruker, USA). Disc degeneration was evaluated according to the Disc Height Index (DHI) and modified Pfirrmann grading system (Table S1) [24], which incorporated assessments of disc structure and signal intensity. The DHI was calculated using the standardized formula: DHI = 2 × (A1 + A2 + A3)/(B1 + B2 + B3 + B4 + B5 + B6), where Ax represents triplicate disc height measurements at the anterior, middle, and posterior regions, Bx denotes six corresponding vertebral body height measurements (superior/inferior endplates at three matched positions). All evaluations were conducted under blinded conditions to eliminate observer bias.

Histological analysis

IVDs samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, decalcified in 10 % EDTA (Solarbio, China) for 4 weeks, and paraffin-embedded. Serial 5-μm sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Safranin-O/Fast green to evaluate morphology and cellularity of IVDs. The scoring criteria were derived from published high-impact studies (Table S2) [25].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For antigen retrieval, tissue sections were incubated in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 65 °C overnight. Following permeabilization with 0.1 % Triton X-100 and blocking with 5 % normal serum, slides were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S3) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, samples were labeled with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Rabbit streptavidin-biotin detection system kit (ZSGB-Bio, China) was performed to detect the signal according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cell isolation and culture

SD rats (4-week-old male) were euthanized for tissue harvest. For NP cell isolation, the gel-like NP tissues were carefully separated from IVDs under sterile conditions, and digested in 0.25 % type II collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C for 4 h with gentle agitation. The isolated cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F12 (DMEM/F12, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Invitrogen, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells in passage 3 (P3) were employed for subsequent in vitro modeling and experimental assays.

Senescence analysis

To establish an oxidative stress-induced senescence model, NP cells at 70–80 % confluence were treated with varying concentrations of Tert-Butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μM; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 2 h. After PBS washing, cells were maintained in fresh complete medium for 24 h. For senescence assessment, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (15 min) and stained using a Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) at 37 °C overnight. Four random fields per sample were imaged under an inverted microscope (IX71, Olympus, Japan), and SA-β-Gal-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.53, NIH, USA) with threshold-based analysis.

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU Imaging Kit (APExBIO, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were pulsed with 10 μM EdU for 2 h at 37 °C, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (15 min) and permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100 (20 min), nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (5 μg/mL, 20 min). Four random fields per sample were imaged using fluorescence microscopy. EdU-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Intracellular ROS assay

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Sigma-Aldrich). NP cells were stimulated with 30 μM tBHP for 2 h. The medium was then aspirated and replaced by fresh medium containing respective nanoparticles. After 24-h treatment, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 20 min and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (protected from light). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ, with values normalized to the intensity of Hoechst blue.

Mitochondrial assays

Following senescence induction and CeNPs treatment for NP cells, mitochondrial quality was assessed using 250 nM MitoTracker™ Green (Invitrogen, USA), mitochondrial ROS was detected using 5 μM MitoSOX Red (Beyotime, China). Cells were incubated with the fluorescent probe for 30 min at 37 °C in serum-free medium, and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (protected from light). Live-cell imaging was captured (n = 4), fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker and MitoSOX was quantified and normalized to the intensity of Hoechst using ImageJ.

Western blot analysis

Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and cell lysates were centrifuged at 12000 g for 15 min (4 °C) to remove cell debris. Protein concentrations were calculated using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Proteins were electrophoretically separated with 10 % or 12 % SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5 % fat-free milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S3) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies. Finally, protein bands were visualized using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the JC-1 assay kit (Beyotime, China). Following incubation with JC-1 probes (1 mg/L, 37 °C, 20 min), cells were washed with PBS and imaged immediately under a fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing

Primary NP cells were stimulated with tBHP for 2 h, and incubated with normal fresh medium or medium containing 100 μM 4 nm-PEG600 for 24 h (n = 3). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol. After assessing RNA quality and integrity, quantified RNAs were utilized for stranded RNA-seq library preparation Sequencing was performed with Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least 3 biologically independent experiments. Statistical comparisons were performed using a two-tailed student's t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (GraphPad Software, version 8.0, San Diego, CA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

NP cell senescence in IVDD correlates with oxidative stress

To characterize transcriptomic alterations in NP tissue and cells during IVDD process, we obtained datasets GSE167199 and GSE245147 from the GEO database. Normalization of the expression matrix produced linearly aligned boxplot distributions (Fig. S1A), indicating successful data standardization. Using a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.5, we identified 1476 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the GSE167199 dataset, and 3601 DEGs in the GSE245147 dataset. The distribution of these DEGs is visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 1A and B). To systematically characterize the biological processes and potential pathways associated with these DEGs, we conducted comprehensive enrichment analyses using both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. The GO analysis highlighted the involvement of multiple biological process (Fig. 1C), including IVDs matrix biosynthesis (ossification, extracellular matrix organization), oxidative stress response (response to oxygen levels, response to hypoxia), and cell cycle (nuclear division, negative regulation of cell cycle process). And the KEGG analysis further identified specific signaling pathways (Fig. 1D), with the majority demonstrating significant connections to oxidative stress and cellular senescence mechanisms. To elucidate the potential interplay between oxidative stress and cellular senescence in IVDD, we performed intersection analysis of these DEGs (p < 0.05) with oxidative stress-related genes curated from GeneCards database, and venn diagram revealed that oxidative stress-related genes shared 118 overlaps with GSE167199 DEGs, and 191 overlaps with GSE245147 DEGs (Fig. 1E), which were visualized with heatmaps (Fig. S1B). KEGG pathway analysis of the overlapping oxidative stress-associated DEGs revealed significant enrichment of cellular senescence-related pathways in both datasets (Fig. 1F), such as cellular senescence, cell cycle, p53 and MAPK signaling pathway. These transcriptomic findings suggest that oxidative stress and cellular senescence may constitute pivotal pathological mechanisms underlying NP degeneration. Construction and characterization of CeNPs with diverse sizes

Initially, we established a nanoparticle synthesis system utilizing Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, TOPO, and ODE as core components. By varying the concentrations of oleic acid (OAc) and oleylamine (OAm) within the system, and controlling the reaction temperature and duration, we synthesized ceria nanoparticles (CeNPs) of distinct sizes. Based on statistical analysis of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, these CeNPs were tentatively designated as 1.2 nm, 4 nm, and 40 nm CeNPs (Fig. 2A). XRD analysis revealed characteristic peaks at (111), (200), (220), and (311), confirming the fluorite cubic crystal structure of the CeNPs (Fig. 2B). Surface hydrophilicity modification was achieved through polyethylene glycol dicarboxylic acid 600 (PEG600) functionalization. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) showed discernible mass loss between 320 °C and 480 °C, indicating successful conjugation of PEG600 ligands to the CeNP surfaces (Fig. 2C). The ROS scavenging activity of the three CeNP sizes was assessed by measuring superoxide anion (O2·-) inhibition (Fig. 2D). Results demonstrated that the 1.2 nm CeNPs exhibited the highest ROS suppression efficiency. At 1 μg/mL, the inhibition rate of 1.2 nm was significantly higher than those of both the 4 nm and 40 nm CeNPs, while the latter two sizes showed comparable activity. Given the critical role of the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio in CeNPs for their ROS scavenging capacity [16], the surface valence distribution of cerium was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 2E). Results revealed Ce3+ surface concentrations of 73.7 %, 37.2 %, and 33.2 % for the 1.2 nm, 4 nm, and 40 nm CeNPs, respectively. Therapeutic effects of CeNPs on IVDD in vivo

Local accumulation represents one of the critical determinants governing the translational potential of inorganic nanomaterials for biomedical applications. It should be noted that pronounced tissue accumulation would elevate the risk of cumulative toxicity due to prolonged release kinetics of metal ions [27]. To evaluate in vivo biodegradation of CeNPs with diverse sizes, the CeNPs were injected into the disc between coccyx vertebrae 7 and 8 (Co7/8) using a 33-gauge Hamilton micro-syringe. Tissue samples were harvested at predetermined timepoints (1, 3, 7 days post-surgery), while no swelling or erythema was observed at the injection site throughout the observation period. The ICP analysis revealed a distinct size-dependent accumulation situation of CeNPs in IVDs tissue (Fig. 3A): the 1.2 nm-PEG600 exhibited rapid clearance kinetics with tissue concentrations declining to a lower level by day 1 post-injection and maintaining sustained reduction thereafter; the 4 nm-PEG600 demonstrated intermediate diffusion characteristics, showing gradual concentration decay over the 7-day observation period; the 40 nm-PEG600 displayed prolonged tissue retention with significant elimination only by day 7. The reliability of these results is strongly supported by our previous findings, which have consistently demonstrated that nanoparticles with larger dimensions exhibit compromised diffusion capacity and a pronounced tendency toward aggregation and localized retention at the administration site [20]. Further in vitro biocompatibility assessment via cell counting kit 8 (CCK8) assay revealed that 1.2 nm-PEG600 inhibited the growth of NP cells at concentrations ≥200 μg mL−1, 40 nm-PEG600 induced marked growth suppression at concentrations ≥100 μg mL−1, however, 4 nm-PEG600 demonstrated no significant cytotoxicity even at elevated concentrations (Fig. 3B). Visual inspection revealed that both 1.2 nm and 4 nm CeNPs formed clear solutions in PBS, whereas the 40 nm CeNPs yielded a turbid suspension with incomplete dissolution (Fig. S2A). Microscopic observation revealed prominent sedimentation of 40 nm CeNPs in cell culture media compared to the 1.2 nm and 4 nm variants (Fig. S2B), which may represent a significant factor contributing to their reduced cellular proliferation activity. Needle puncture-induced IVDs injury has been extensively documented to trigger oxidative stress damage, ultimately leading to cellular senescence-associated disc degeneration [28]. Given the demonstrated ROS-scavenging capacity of CeNPs, we further investigated their therapeutic potential to suppress senescence and mitigate IVDD in a rat caudal puncture model. Interestingly, CeNPs of three distinct size exhibited markedly divergent therapeutic efficacy in mitigating IVDD, highlighting a critical size-dependent therapeutic paradigm. The therapeutic variations of CeNPs were initially revealed through radiographic imaging analysis. T2-weighted (T2W) MRI provides superior assessment of IVD degeneration morphology, including disc height reduction and decreased hydration signal. The Pfirrmann grading system - a globally recognized classification based on these signal intensity and structural features - remains the gold standard for evaluating degenerative disc changes [22]. The T2W MRI analysis revealed significantly elevated hydration signal intensity and DHI in both sham-operated and therapeutically effective groups compared to degenerative controls (Fig. 3C). Among the 3 treatment groups, the 4 nm-PEG600 group maintained the highest hydration intensity (Fig. 3D), DHI (Fig. 3E) and lowest Pfirrmann scores (Fig. 3F) throughout the study period, demonstrating sustained efficacy in retarding disc degeneration. In contrast, the 1.2 nm-PEG600 group showed progressive signal intensity reduction, structure collapse and Pfirrmann score elevation, reaching PBS control levels by endpoint. The 40 nm-PEG600 group exhibited intermediate protection on NP signal; however, its therapeutic advantage diminished significantly at 8 weeks.

The CeNPs attenuate extracellular matrix degradation and senescence-associated proinflammatory phenotypes

In histological staining of tissue sections, Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining revealed pronounced structural disorganization of NP tissues in PBS-treated discs, characterized by loss of proteoglycan-rich matrix architecture and compromised NP-AF boundary demarcation. The 4 nm CeNPs demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy, significantly preserving tissue integrity throughout the treatment duration with only minimal histological evidence of tissue damage. In contrast, the 40 nm CeNPs, with moderate therapeutic effects, showed reduced NP volume and substantial AF damage at 4 weeks, followed by marked degenerative changes and ill-defined NP architecture by 8 weeks. While the 1.2 nm CeNPs partially preserved subchondral bone morphology and limited AF tissue integrity compared to PBS controls, it failed to mitigate NP tissue loss (Fig. 4A). Safranin O/Fast Green (SO/FG) staining demonstrated superior proteoglycan retention and ECM preservation in the 4 nm-PEG600 group, particularly within NP tissues, compared to other treatment cohorts (Fig. 4B). Statistically, IVDD treated with 4 nm-PEG600 demonstrated significantly reduced histological degeneration scores compared to other treatment groups, accompanied by marked improvements in matrix staining, morphology of NP and AF (Fig. 4C). Aggrecan (ACAN) and type II collagen (COL2) are two essential structural components of the ECM, predominantly found in cartilage and NP tissue. Functionally, ACAN exhibits exceptional hydrophilicity that enables the NP to withstand compressive forces and maintain tissue elasticity. Meanwhile, COL2 forms a fibrillar network that interacts electrostatically with ACAN and other proteoglycans, creating a biomechanically stable ECM architecture [29]. The immunohistochemical analysis revealed significant reductions in both ACAN and COL2 expression, particularly within the NP region, in PBS-treated model groups, indicating that modeling led to decreased matrix synthesis. Notably, 4 nm CeNPs demonstrated remarkable protective effects on ACAN (Fig. 4D and E) and COL2 (Fig. 4F and G) content within IVDs. The CeNPs alleviated NP cell senescence and reduced ROS-related mitochondrial dysfunction in vitro

Previous experiments indicated that 50 μg/mL was the highest concentration at which all three types of nanoparticles exhibited no significant cytotoxicity (Fig. 3B). Therefore, this concentration was utilized to investigate their inhibitory effects on NP cell senescence. Tert-Butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP), a stable organic peroxide, has been demonstrated to induce intracellular oxidative stress and cellular senescence more reproducibly than H2O2 in experimental models [31]. NP cells were treated with gradient concentrations of tBHP (0–50 μM), and the optimal concentration (30 μM) was determined via CCK8 assay (Fig. S3A). Subsequently, DCFH-DA and SA-β-Gal staining validated that the oxidative stress-induced senescence of NP cells was successfully constructed (Fig. S3B and C). Following tBHP-induced oxidative stress, treatment with CeNPs significantly attenuated intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 6A and B) and SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 6C and D). In contrast to observations in animal models, both 1.2 nm and 4 nm CeNPs demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy, whereas the 40 nm CeNPs exhibited comparatively reduced effectiveness. The EdU-Cy3 azide assay enables specific fluorescence labeling of proliferating cells by detecting DNA synthesis during S-phase, while senescent cells-characterized by G1-phase arrest-fail to incorporate EdU and thus remain unlabeled. This differential labeling provides a robust method to distinguish proliferative capacity from senescence states [32]. Following tBHP treatment, NP cells exhibited significantly reduced EdU incorporation rates, indicating proliferation arrest. MitoTracker, a membrane potential-independent mitochondria-specific fluorescent probe, is widely employed to assess mitochondrial mass and spatial distribution dynamics in live cells [34]. Following tBHP treatment, MitoTracker staining of NP cells revealed fragmented mitochondrial networks and reduced fluorescence indensity, indicating compromised mitochondrial structural integrity. The 1.2 nm and 4 nm CeNPs significantly mitigated tBHP-induced damage, whereas the 40 nm particles exhibited limited efficacy (Fig. 7A and B). These findings validated the capacity of 1.2 nm and 4 nm CeNPs in disrupting the ROS-mitochondrial dysfunction cascade and tBHP-induced cell senescence, and the therapeutic efficacy of 1.2 nm and 4 nm CeNPs showed no significant differences at established safe concentrations. The relatively poor therapeutic efficacy of 40 nm CeNPs may be attributed to their larger size and inferior dispersity, leading to an extracellular aggregation tendency and reduced cellular uptake.

Given that 4 nm CeNPs revealed the optimal therapeutic efficacy in vivo, we further investigated their dose-dependent effects on cellular senescence in vitro. The 4 nm CeNPs demonstrated concentration-dependent benefits in scavenging intracellular ROS (Fig. S4A and B), attenuating senescence markers (Fig. S4C and D), restoring proliferation rates (Fig. S4E and F), maintaining anabolic/catabolic homeostasis (Fig. S4G and H) and preserving mitochondrial function (Figs. S5A, B, C, D, E, F). These findings further validate the biological security of 4 nm CeNPs. In contrast, 1.2 nm CeNPs exhibited significant inhibition of cell viability at concentrations >100 μg/mL, providing a plausible explanation for their inferior performance in vivo. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that CeNPs represent a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for augmenting intracellular antioxidant defenses, scavenging mitochondrial ROS and preserving mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby restoring redox equilibrium and protecting against senescence-associated bioenergetic collapse.

Underlying mechanism of CeNPs on suppressing cell senescence

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CeNPs-mediated suppression of NP cell senescence, total RNA was isolated from senescent NP cell group and 4 nm-PEG600 intervention group (n = 3). RNA sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear segregation of samples into two distinct clusters (Fig. 8A). The volcano plot exhibited 726 down-regulated genes and 98 up-regulated genes in CeNPs intervention group (Fig. S6A). KEGG enrichment and GSEA analysis revealed significant modulation of senescence-associated signaling networks, including ferroptosis, p53 signaling pathway, mitophagy, cellular senescence, cell cycle and PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 8B and C). Notably, both current and previously acquired human NP cell transcriptomes (GSE245147) revealed the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway as a consistently top-ranked KEGG pathway (Fig. 1F). We therefore propose that the PI3K-AKT pathway may serve as a crucial mechanism of NP cell senescence in IVDD, and a primary therapeutic target through which CeNPs exert anti-senescent effects. Nevertheless, the precise role of PI3K-AKT signaling in cellular senescence regulation remains contested. On one hand, pharmacological inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway restores the normal quiescent phenotype of myofibroblasts by suppressing cellular senescence and the SASP, while promoting apoptosis and autophagy [36]. From the perspective of treatment, Melatonin demonstrates protective effects against apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction in NP-MSCs via PI3K/AKT pathway activation [37]. Ginsenoside Rg1 activates the NRF2 antioxidant pathway through AKT-dependent enhancement of P62-KEAP1 binding, thereby inhibiting cellular senescence [38]. The PPI protein interaction analysis revealed the interconnections among the PI3K-AKT associated genes (Fig. S6B). Heatmap visualization revealed pronounced downregulation of senescent gene Cdkn1a (P21Cip1) and upregulation of matrix gene Col2a1 in CeNPs-treated group (Fig. S6C). However, the expression changes of PI3K and AKT were not observed at the RNA level. We subsequently quantified the protein levels of PI3K and AKT, and found that neither tBHP exposure nor CeNPs treatment induced significant alterations in PI3K expression. The AKT protein level was significantly downregulated under tBHP exposure, and further reduced with 1.2 nm or 4 nm CeNPs treatment. Given that the activation of AKT signaling is critically dependent on phosphorylation at Ser473, we specifically quantified the phospho-AKT (Ser473) level, which was greatly inhibited by tBHP, but rescued by 4 nm CeNPs. In contrast, the 1.2 nm and 40 nm CeNPs showed minimal effect on AKT activation (Fig. 8D and E). This suggested that the oxidative stress induced by tBHP likely suppressed PI3K-AKT activation primarily by inhibiting AKT expression and phosphorylation at Ser473, consistent with established findings [39]. While unable to promote AKT expression, the 4 nm CeNPs could counteract the effect of oxidative stress by enhancing AKT phosphorylation. To verify the necessity of AKT phosphorylation in CeNPs-regulated cellular senescence, we employed its specific activator SC79 and inhibitor Perifosine [40,41]. We found that SC79, in combination with 4 nm CeNPs treatment, further enhanced AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 and reduced the expression of senescence-associated proteins P16INK4a and P21Cip1. Conversely, Perifosine inhibited AKT phosphorylation while increasing the expression of these senescence-associated proteins (Fig. 8F and G). On the other hand, the changes in senescence markers P16INK4a and P21Cip1 did not consistently correlate with the level of AKT phosphorylation in the 1.2 nm treatment group. Specifically, these markers did not uniformly decrease with enhanced AKT phosphorylation, nor did they increase when AKT phosphorylation was suppressed (Fig. 8F and G). This indicates that the PI3K-AKT pathway might not be the primary mechanism through which 1.2 nm treatment inhibits senescence. Conclusion

In this study, three distinct sizes of CeNPs, designated 1.2 nm-PEG600, 4 nm-PEG600 and 40 nm-PEG600, were synthesized and evaluated for their potential to attenuate IVDD. Both short-term (4 weeks) and long-term (8 weeks) assessments demonstrated that CeNPs effectively preserved IVD structure and function during degeneration. Among these, 4 nm-PEG600 exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy, attributed to its minimal cytotoxicity and optimal stability within the IVD physiological environment relative to the other sizes. This enhanced biocompatibility and stability facilitated prolonged retention and more homogeneous diffusion of the nanoparticles throughout IVD tissue at the same time. Although the 1.2 nm CeNPs exhibited notable anti-senescence effects at the cellular level, their poor in vivo therapeutic performance may be attributed to cytotoxicity at high concentration and inadequate tissue retention. Mechanistic investigation revealed that CeNPs attenuate IVDD primarily by modulating ROS accumulation triggered NP cell senescence, which is verified by detecting matrix synthesis metabolism, SASPs, senescence staining, proliferative activity and mitochondrial status. Specifically, beyond direct scavenging of microenvironmental ROS, AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 within the PI3K-AKT pathway was identified as an essential event through which 4 nm-PEG600 inhibits NP cell senescence. Collectively, these findings demonstrate inorganic nanoparticles as a promising therapeutic strategy for IVDD, leveraging combined antioxidant and anti-senescence effects.
