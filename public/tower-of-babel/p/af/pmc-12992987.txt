Selenotrisulfide delivery restores redox balance in myocardial infarction via a thiol-exchange reaction in situ
Authors: Shifen Li, Beiduo Wang, Weiwei Zheng, Xinman Hu, Liyin Shen, Jun Deng, Yang Zhu, Wenxing Liu, Changyou Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enzyme activity of redox-related selenoproteins is impaired post-tissue injury or inflammation, exacerbating oxidative stress and apoptosis. In this study, we present a strategy using selenotrisulfide (STS) to enhance the selenium content and restore selenoprotein-like activity in vivo via thiol-exchange reactions. The exact mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) change was observed at cysteine residue sites by liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC-MS), demonstrating the feasibility of the thiol-exchange reaction and the modification of selenium with proteins. Compared to the traditional selenium sources such as sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), L-selenomethionine (SeMet) and L-selenocysteine ((Sec)2), STS exhibited superior antioxidative and therapeutic efficacy by augmenting selenium levels and oxidoreductase-like activities in vitro and in vivo. Proteomic analysis revealed that STS could better improve myocardial contraction and regulate glucolipid metabolism to enhance energy supply and cardiac repair. Furthermore, the core-shell nanofibrous ZPB@STS patch significantly contributed to lower inflammatory response, less cell death and collagen deposition, and stronger cardiac contraction through the cooperative interaction of selenium-regulation from STS and mechanical support from the elastomeric polyurethane fibrous patch.Keywords: Selenium, Thiol-exchange, Myocardial infarction, Glucolipid metabolism, Nanofibrous cardiac patch

Introduction

Selenoproteins are essential for maintaining normal life activities in the body for antioxidation, immune regulation, DNA repair, spermatogenesis, and cardiovascular health [1,2]. Low selenium levels in the disease microenvironment affect the function of these proteins and are highly susceptible to oxidative stress and immune dysregulation. Common selenium supplements include inorganic selenium such as sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), organic selenium such as selenomethionine (SeMet), and selenocysteine (Sec), etc. [3,4] The inorganic selenium supplements are highly bioavailable, but exhibit a narrow window from therapeutic effect to toxic effect. The traditional organic selenium supplements are hypotoxic, yet their efficacy is unsatisfactory [5]. Orally administered selenium will experience the trans-selenation pathway, and is directly converted to Sec to synthesize selenoproteins under the catalyzation of selenocysteine lyase (SCLY) [6]. However, the traditional selenium supplementation strategies are fundamentally limited in myocardial infarction due to poor tissue targeting, lack of local selenium bioavailability, and inability to directly restore impaired selenoprotein activity [7].

Selenotrisulfide with the structure of -S-Se-S- is an essential intermediate in the selenite metabolic pathway, which can participate in thiol exchange reactions in vivo [8]. This bimolecular nucleophilic substitution reaction (SN2) represents the mechanism underlying the interactions of many enzymes in biological processes [9]. A nucleophilic thiolate (R–S−) of the L-cysteine residue (Cys) preferably attacks the more electrophilic selenium atom of STS over its sulfur atoms to conduct a thiol exchange reaction (R-SSeS-R + R′-SH→R-SSeS-R′+R-SH) [10]. A stable and isolatable selenotrisulfide has been used to investigate the metabolic fate of selenite [11]. Because the free thiols of proteins are thought to be the important binding sites for selenium transportation, in this study, we propose a new regulator for selenium-related physiological process based on the “thiol-exchange” reaction to restore selenium levels in vivo.

Myocardial infarction (MI) is one of the typical representatives of oxidative stress-related diseases, and its pathological features are related to selenoproteins’ activity in infarction. GPx1, the most well-known in cardiac tissues, can be cardioprotective against myocardial injury in mice and exhibits the regulation of oxidative stress. GPx3 and GPx4 are also highly expressed in cardiac tissues, and the latter is closely connected to ferroptosis. TrxRs (TrxR1-3) are more beneficial in transferring electrons from nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) to a variety of substrates, thus maintaining cellular redox balance and preventing oxidative damage caused by ROS. Furthermore, the selenoproteins have been involved in cardiac development by ER stress regulation, protein synthesis and cell growth. Thus, all these selenoproteins are essential for heart repair [5]. The shared functional part of all these selenoproteins is the active site of selenocysteine (Sec), thereby making Se widely recognized as a promising element in antioxidative stress therapy. Selenium supplements can activate the PI3K/AKT pathway that is associated with redox, and targets the G2/M-phase inhibitory system to ameliorate oxidative stress-induced cell cycle arrest in cardiomyocytes [12,13]. Selenium takes a protective function to reduce the infarct size and incidence of post-ischemic ventricular arrhythmias [14].

In this study, we report the thiol-exchange by a selenium molecule (Pen-S-Se-S-Pen, STS) to regulate the selenium physiological behavior, including the selenium content in infarcted tissues and enzyme-like activity of selenoproteins, thereby effectively scavenging ROS and promoting the recovery of cardiac function (Scheme 1). Both STS and its analogs (selenotrisulfides) contain the unique structure of -S-Se-S-, which can participate in the selenium transformation process by “thiol-exchange” reaction in vivo [7]. Thus, we hypothesize that STS can also react with proteins at the cysteine residues (-SH) under mild conditions (37 °C) by the thiol-exchange reaction, thereby transmitting new selenium active sites in proteins. After injury, this approach is expected to facilitate a rapid recovery in selenoprotein-like activity. Compared to the traditional selenium supplements, this quick and productive reaction will lead to a more promising selenium supplementation. The beneficial utility of STS as a novel selenium supplement is fully established through the characterization of chemical and protein reactions in vitro, selenoproteins-like activities in vitro and in vivo, and proteomic analysis. For sustained release and applications in vivo, a core-shell ZPB@STS patch is fabricated to effectively improve STS residency and achieve better therapeutic effects in long-term treatment of MI.

A selenium-containing and core-shell nanofibrous myocardial patch (ZPB@STS) is designed to enhance selenium content and modulate selenoprotein-like activity in the heart of rat post-myocardial infarction, enhancing the anti-oxidative function of these proteins and decreasing the inflammation to better repair the heart structure and function. Due to the key characteristic of the polymeric 3-(methylbis(2-hydroxyethyl)amino)propane-1-sulfonate (SB-diol) in naming, and that ‘Z’ usually is the initial letter symbol for zwitterionic polymers, the novel polyurethane was designated as “ZPB” in this article.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of STS (Pen-S-Se-S-Pen)

Because of the two methyl groups at the β carbon atom, a chemically stable selenium trisulfide (Pen-S-Se-S-Pen, STS) was successfully synthesized using 4 equivalents of penicillamine and 1 equivalent of seleninic acid [15]. STS has higher stability surpassing its analogous structures (e.g., GSSeSG) that exhibit extreme instability under physiological conditions. On the other hand, the binding of selenium to proteins through the STS appears to be more stable compared to the other analogs [9]. In the 1H NMR spectrum of STS (Fig. 1a), the characteristic peak of δ = 4.06 ppm is assigned to the -CH near amidogen, and the methyl groups appeared at δ = 1.5 ppm and δ = 1.41 ppm. There were four carbon atoms with different chemical environments in STS as verified by 13C NMR spectrum (Fig. 1b), and only the carbon atoms of -CH and -CH3 were observed in DEPT-135 NMR spectra (Fig. 1c). The elemental analysis (%) of STS (C10H20N2O4S2Se) calculated: C: 31.91, H: 5.32, N:7.45, Se: 21.28; found: C: 31.14, H: 5.6, N: 7.5, Se: 20.68. In addition, the molecular weight of STS was determined to be 376.01 by Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI TOF–MS, positive ion mode) based on m/z = 377.01 ([M + H]+) (Fig. 1d). It showed a needle-like polycrystalline structure (Fig. 1e and Fig. S1). All these results confirm the successful synthesis of stable STS.

Thiol exchange reaction of STS

The thiol exchange reaction of STS was verified by its reaction with cysteine (Cys) and bovine serum albumin (BSA), which represent the model small molecules and proteins, respectively. Once STS was mixed with Cys, 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 1f–h) revealed that the characteristic peak of -CH of Cys moved from δ = 4.21 ppm (peak 3) to δ = 4.33 ppm (peak 3′), and the -CH2 of Cys underwent a larger chemical shift from δ = 3.0 ppm (peak 4) to δ = 3.5 ppm (peak 4′) because of closer connection to more electronegative selenium atom after reaction. Accordingly, the new characteristic peak of STS appeared at δ = 3.69 ppm (-CH, peak 1′). The integral area of the peaks remained almost unchanged after 4 min, suggesting a fast and stable reaction, which was also verified by the 77Se NMR spectra (Fig. 1i), showing a new single peak at δ = 649 ppm in the obtained product.

The exchange reaction between STS and proteins with free sulfhydryl groups can occur through the same mechanism. The increased selenium content in the harvested heart tissue lysates was detected along with the increase of STS concentration, which reached a plateau at 50 μM (Fig. 2a). Meanwhile, the systemic thiol amount decreased (Fig. 2b), verifying the potential of the exchanging reaction. It is well known that sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), L-selenomethionine (SeMet) and L-selenocysteine ((Sec)2) are common and traditional selenium supplements, which are normally applied for dietary selenium supplementation and prevention of oxidation-induced damage. Compared with these selenium supplements (Fig. 2c and d), only the STS group showed pronounced elevation of selenium content. Again, the addition of IAA resulted in a sharp decrease. In the Na2SeO3 group, the thiol amount remained unchanged compared with the Control group, precluding its possible reaction with the sulfhydryl groups. SeMet and (Sec)2 were likely taking the thiol-exchange reaction with rather low efficiency. Taken together, the mechanism of STS with a higher selenium supplement efficiency should lie in the thiol-exchange reaction, which extremely surpasses those of traditional selenium supplements.

To further demonstrate the exchange reaction sites between STS and proteins, BSA was used as a model protein to be denatured by dissolving in urea solution and further reduced with dithiothreitol to expose enough sulfhydryl groups (Fig. 2e). After reacting with STS for 30 min at 37 °C, the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of enzymatic-cleaved peptides of BSA and STS reaction products was analyzed by Liquid Chromatograph Mass Spectrometer (LC-MS). A definite increment of 226.95 was observed at the 288th and 312nd Cys sites (Fig. 2f, Fig. S2 and Tables S1–2), which matches the theoretical value of the STS exchange reaction. Surprisingly, the thiol-exchanged BSA exhibited GPx-like activity. However, when the sulfhydryl groups were blocked with iodoacetamide (IAA) in advance, the obtained BSA lose the enzyme-like activity. It is well-known that the selenium active site is the core component through which most selenoproteins exert their antioxidant effects and participate in cellular life processes. In MI lesions, the core catalytic sites (Se) of some oxidoreductases are destroyed due to the excessive ROS, down-regulating the enzyme activities and anti-oxidative effect, and thereby worsening oxidative stress [16]. Therefore, STS could serve as a new-generation of selenium supplements, which not only enhances selenium content more effectively but also enables the exchanged proteins to exhibit GPX-like activity.

Anti-oxidation of STS in vitro

One of the features of selenium supplements is their prominent anti-oxidation performance [17,18], as demonstrated in Fig. 3a–c, where most of the free radicals, including ABTS+, OH• and DPPH, were eliminated. The scavenging capacity of Na2SeO3 and (Sec)2 to DPPH was compromised because of its poor dissolution in alcohol, which is the only good solvent for DPPH.

The anti-oxidation performance of these selenium supplements was also assessed in cultured cells in vitro, where they take the functions through selenoenzymes, including GPxs and TrxRs, etc. [19] Typically, the primary physiological role of GPxs is to maintain a suitably low intracellular level of hydrogen peroxide, minimizing potential free radical damage. The TrxRs family is a necessary part of maintaining homeostasis of the thiol system. Besides, selenium supplementation also can improve the activities of downstream superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), which are the most important first-line free radical scavengers in organisms, maintaining the body's metabolic homeostasis. The 4 μM STS was chosen for this study because no apparent cytotoxicity and optimal GPx-like enzyme activity were found when its concentration was lower than 6 μM (Fig. 3d and Fig. S3). All the cells co-cultured with STS and the other 3 types of selenium supplements showed normal cell proliferation without difference to the blank control (Fig. 3e), ensuring the reasonable assessment and comparison. Furthermore, H&E staining of liver, spleen, lung and kidney after being treated by different selenium supplements for 3 d post-MI showed no obvious damage (Fig. S4).

The activities of typical selenoenzymes were measured using the cell lysates following the co-culture of cells with selenium supplements under stimulation of 500 μM H2O2 for 2 h (Fig. 3f–i). In accordance with the equal-selenium concentrations, the molar concentration of (Sec)2 was half of that of other selenium supplements in this study. Both the Na2SeO3 and STS significantly improved the GPx-like activity, whereas the (Sec)2 and SeMet not even at higher concentrations (Fig. S5). The TrxR-like activity was only slightly enhanced by STS. Compared to the traditional selenium supplements, the impact of STS on the activity of SOD and CAT was quite pronounced [20]. Consistent with these observations, the STS group also possessed higher levels of associated selenoprotein genes (Fig. 3j and Fig. S6, Table S3). The four types of genes of the GPx family were distinctly up-regulated by STS, whereas those of GPx1 and GPx3 were also up-regulated by Na2SeO3 and SeMet, respectively. All groups contributed positively to the GPx4 gene expression. Among the transcript levels of SelW, SelT and SelK genes, only the STS had a notable up-regulation. STS also had an apparent impact on the enzyme activity of TrxR1. Taken together, STS could exert an influence on the regulation of most of the intracellular selenoprotein levels, with stronger efficiency than other selenium supplements. Correspondingly, the lowest intracellular ROS level was found in the STS group (Fig. 3k,l and Fig. S7) under 500 μM H2O2 stimulation, suggesting its substantial potential of antioxidation through the improvement of selenoenzyme activity in situ. No significant differences were observed when being cultured with a lower H2O2 concentration (200 μM) and longer duration. This phenomenon might be ascribed to the different oxidative environments in vitro and in vivo. The higher concentration of H2O2 was needed to establish rapidly a well-defined myocardial cell oxidative injury model in vitro [21], whilst subsequent in vivo experimental results further validated the efficacy of STS as shown below. Furthermore, the co-culture with Na2SeO3, (Sec)2 and STS significantly reduced the gene expression level of intracellular Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and the organic selenium treatment (SeMet, (Sec)2 and STS) also decreased that of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) to some extent (Fig. S8).

Repair effect of STS in vivo

The selenium content of the damaged myocardium post-MI becomes lower, leading to a decline in the activities of the associated selenoproteins, and thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and pathologic process [5]. Intravenous administration of STS with a concentration of 120, 240, 360, and 480 μg/kg normal saline significantly improved the selenium contents of local myocardium and serum (Fig. 4a and b and Fig. S9), where 360 μg/kg normal saline exhibited the highest selenium concentrations.

Therefore, the STS and traditional selenium supplements (360 μg/kg normal saline) were applied by intravenous injection for a 3-d treatment. The blood test of experimental animals in each group showed the normal levels of hemoglobin, red blood cells and platelets (Fig. 4c–e and Fig. S10). Compared to the Sham group, the values of leukocyte, neutrophil, monocyte and eosinophil were significantly increased and lymphocyte decreased in the MI group. After treatment with the selenium supplements, all the inflammatory cell levels were recovered to varying degrees, and alleviated mostly in STS. Nonetheless, more leukocytes and neutrophils existed in the Na2SeO3 group, suggesting a possible heavier immune response. Meanwhile, albumin (ALB) and urea levels were reduced after MI in all groups (Fig. S11). Indicators related to liver functions such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and total bilirubin (TBIL) were within the normal range and similar to those of the Sham group. The levels of creatinine (CRE) correlated with kidney function also remained normal in all groups. Thus, in this study, the injection of these selenium supplements at the used concentration would not cause extra damage to the liver and kidneys. By contrast, markedly increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase (CK) post-MI, which are the common auxiliary diagnostic indicators of acute myocardial infarction, were recovered to the normal levels by STS administration (Fig. 4f and g).

Furthermore, different selenium supplements were intravenously administered on 0 and 2 d post-MI, revealing that the selenium levels in both tissue and serum were improved significantly in the STS group compared with those in the other treatment groups (Fig. 4h and i). As expected, the activities of redox-related selenoenzymes such as GPx-like and TrxR-like, and antioxidant enzyme (SOD) were enhanced significantly under STS treatment (Fig. 4j–l).

During the inflammation stage, the alleviative oxidation also contributes to the infiltration and polarization of reparative macrophages [22,23]. In the MI group, a larger number of inflammatory M1 macrophages and a smaller number of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages were observed (Fig. 4m–o). After the treatments with Na2SeO3, SeMet and (Sec)2, the M1 and M2 macrophages were down and up regulated, respectively. The STS treatment most significantly decreased M1 but increased M2 macrophages synchronously. Moreover, the expression of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), a typical pro-inflammatory cytokine that generates an inflammatory ecosystem and promotes the development of malignant diseases [24], was most significantly inhibited by STS treatment too (Fig. 4m–p). As a representative anti-inflammatory cytokine, the level of interleukin-10 (IL-10) was substantially enhanced in the STS group (Fig. 4m–q). Consequently, the STS treatment maximally protected the myocardial cells against apoptosis (Fig. 4m–r).

To study the more specific features of proteins at a large scale by different treatments, the protein expression levels and post-translational modifications were characterized to disclose more holistic and comprehensive understanding of the disease mechanism and cell metabolism. The differential proteins of MI vs Sham were analyzed by KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) (Fig. S12) to sort into six parts (Metabolism, Organismal Systems, Environmental Information Processing, Genetic Information Processing, Human Diseases, and Cellular Processes) from a commonality perspective. It is known that carbohydrate and lipid metabolism might be perturbed post-MI [25,26], characterized by profound impairment in glucose utilization and mitochondrial energy metabolism, which directly contributes to contractile dysfunction, adverse remodeling, and heart failure progression [27]. Among the selenium-containing compounds tested, SeMet, (Sec)2, and STS, but not Na2SeO3, exerted comparable regulatory effects on lipid metabolism (Figs. S13–16). To investigate the underlying transcriptional programs, WGCNA was employed to construct gene co-expression networks to screen for modules with higher relevance to different groups (Fig. 5a–c). This identified several distinct modules, among which the blue module showed the strongest association with the STS group. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that this module was significantly enriched for proteins involved in core metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, carbon metabolism, propanoate metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 5d and Fig. S17,18). These glucolipid metabolism processes have been reported to play important roles in regulating myocardial energy metabolism [28]. Consistent with this metabolic rewiring, the STS group also modulated the citric acid (TCA) cycle, a central hub in mitochondrial energy production [29], indicating a coordinated regulation of core bioenergetic pathways (Fig. 5e). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed a significant upregulation of lipid metabolism pathways in the STS group compared to the MI group (Fig. 5f–h and Figure S19). Concurrently, the NOD-like receptor (NLR) signaling pathway was remarkably suppressed, suggesting a potential mechanism for attenuating inflammasome formation and protecting against inflammatory cell death (Fig. 5i and) [30]. Inflammasomes are known as the typical NLRs-formed multi-protein complexes consisting of an NLR, the adaptor protein ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD) and the effector molecule pro-caspase-1 [31]. Fig. S20 reveals significantly decreased levels of NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3), ASC and cleaved Caspase-1 in cardiac infarction after being treated with STS. Functional assessment demonstrated that the STS treatment conferred superior recovery of cardiac contractile function post-MI compared to Na2SeO3, SeMet, and (Sec)2 treatments (Fig. 5j and k). Analysis of commonly expressed proteins indicated the elevated levels of contraction-associated proteins in the STS group. This included cardiac-specific actin (Actc1), which forms the core of the thin filament and the intermediate filament proteins Desmin and Vimentin that are critical for structural integrity [[32], [33], [34]]. Furthermore, the expression of Myh6, encoding the α-myosin heavy chain essential for contractile force generation and cardiac pump function, was also increased [35]. In deed, the cytoskeleton staining of cardiomyocytes demonstrated that STS could effectively enhance the contractility of cells (Fig. S21). High performance liquid chromatography inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy (HPLC-ICP-MS) assay excluded the possibility for STS to be involved in the classical selenium metabolic pathway within 6 h (Fig. S22) [36].

Therefore, compared with Na2SeO3, SeMet, and (Sec)2 groups, the STS treatment can better improve the cardiac muscle contraction function and regulate the glucolipid metabolism processes to enhance energy supplementation and cardiac repair.

Enhanced repair effect of ZPB@STS in vivo

So far the dietary selenium supplementation has been the most common way to supply selenium. However, the local absorption and selenoprotein conversion rates remain low. The selenium supplementation in situ with the help of biomaterials would be a more precise and straightforward way to achieve a better curative effect. Meanwhile, the zwitterions such as sulfobetaine (SB) have the advantages of hydrophilicity and antifouling, and thereby can decrease the foreign-body response [37]. To further improve the residence of the STS in the infarcted area and achieve a better therapeutic outcome, it was loaded into electrospun nanofibers of a novel polyurethane [38,39], which was polymerized from poly(propylene fumarate) (PPF), 2,2-bis(hydroxymethyl)propionic acid (DMPA) and 3-(methylbis(2-hydroxyethyl)amino)propane-1-sulfonate (SB-diol) with 1,6-hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI), followed by chain extension with Lys-OMe (Fig. S23). The polymer (ZPB) structure was characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 6a), where the chemical shifts at δ = 6.73 ppm (peak a) and δ = 5.21 ppm (peak b) are assigned to the double bonds and -CH connecting to the methyl in PPF segment, respectively. The methylene next to the ester group in the PPF segment appeared at δ = 4.25 ppm (peak c). The characteristic peaks of HDI and DMPA were found at δ = 2.94 ppm (peak d) and δ = 1.05 ppm (peak i), respectively. The peaks at δ = 4.35 ppm, 3.65 ppm, 2.05 ppm and 3.10 ppm (e-h) represent the existence of the SB-diol. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) displayed that it had a number molecular weight of 22.8 kDa and a polydispersity index of 1.33. Owing to the sulfonate function of zwitterionic SB-diol, the zeta potential of ZPB film shifted from positive to negative when the environment pH was transferred from acidic to alkaline (Fig. S24).

The core-shell structure of nanofibers is one of the most effective means to load and maintain small-molecule drugs [40]. The pure ZPB and core-shell ZPB@STS nanofibrous patches were manufactured by electrospinning, whose even fiber structure was observed on SEM images (Fig. 6b). The core-shell structure of ZPB@STS patches was verified by TEM and cross-section of SEM imaging and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Fig. 6c). The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy elemental mapping revealed the existence of selenium in the ZPB@STS only (Fig. S25 and Table S4). Compared to the more uniform ones in the pure ZPB patch, the fibers in the ZPB@STS became more widely distributed (Fig. 6d) [41]. Besides, the ZPB patch had an excellent water response due to the addition of hydrophilic components of zwitterion and DMPA, whose area could be folded down to one-seventh of its previous size (Fig. 6e). When the patch was folded in water and freeze-dried quickly, the transformed shape could be maintained for 3 d without change. When submerged in water again, the ZPB patch would be wetted quickly, and became soft enough to support shape change (Video 1). The final recovery shape was almost identical to the original. The water-response application of ZPB patches was also demonstrated on a rat heart surface (Video 2). The patch could well fit the curvature of the heart because the side in contact with the heart would be wetted quickly and contracted. Moreover, this sensitive water-responsive behavior would offer a promising basis for its subsequent use in minimally invasive implantation.

Proper mechanical support applied by cardiac patches was another advantage of our nanofibrous film (Fig. 6f and Fig. S26). The tensile strength and Young's modulus of ZPB patches were about 0.85 MPa and 14.72 MPa, and those of the ZPB@STS patches were enhanced to 3.15 MPa and 61.57 MPa, respectively. The breaking elongation of the ZPB@STS patch was reduced to 112% from the previous 237%, which was still large enough to allow elastic transformation during heart beating. Meanwhile, the ZPB and ZPB@STS patches remained largely undegraded within 14 d in PBS or H2O2 solution (Fig. S27). Subcutaneous implantation demonstrated a larger number of inflammatory cells in the ZPB group than in the ZPB@STS group (Fig. S28). Therefore, the ZPB@STS film exhibited favorable biocompatibility and degradation stability as an external patch.

In the initial state after MI, plenty of immune cells are recruited and to produce proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines and ROS. This period usually lasts for 3 d, during which monocytes and macrophages completely infiltrate into the infarct area [42]. To further examine the encapsulation effect of the core-shell structure, the ZPB@STS patch was immersed in PBS to monitor the release of STS (Fig. 6g and Fig. S29). 40% of STS could be released in 24 h from the patch, and all the STS would be released in 72 h. This period correlates very well with the development of initial inflammation in vivo, conveying that the ZPB@STS patch may have a better therapeutic effect in the initial stage.

Both the ZPB and ZPB@STS patches had no cytotoxicity (Fig. 6h). After the cells were cultured with the ZPB@STS patch, the enzyme activity of GPx-like was higher than that of the STS (Fig. 6i). Additionally, the impact of STS and ZPB@STS patches on the infarcted tissue was compared in the rat MI model after transplantation for 3 d. The STS was administered only 1 time at 0 d, and the patch containing the same dosage of STS was fixed on the lesion after MI. While no significant effect in the STS group, the ZPB@STS patch group could noticeably improve the selenium levels in localized infarct areas. Meanwhile, the selenium content in serum and the enzyme activity in local tissue in the ZPB@STS patch group were significantly improved compared with the MI and STS groups too (Fig. 6j–l).

In the 28-d treatment, the STS was intravenously administered on days 0, 3 and 7, while the ZPB@STS patch containing the same dosage for one injection was fixed on day 0 (Fig. 7a). The echocardiographic characterization (Figure S30 and Fig. 7b–j) demonstrated the poor contraction in the left ventricular anterior wall and severely damaged heart functions in the MI group on both the 7th and 28th day. In a short-term treatment (7 d), the restoration effect of the STS group to the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) was apparent, which was weakened in the long term (28 d). Along with the development of MI, regression of inflammation and tissue remodeling are activated in 10 d, followed by fibrosis, scarring, and wall thinning of left ventricular [43]. Therefore, as time prolonged, the association of the ZPB patches to the left ventricular dilatation became more significant, which are revealed by the left ventricular end-diastolic (LVEDV), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), interventricular septum (IVS) and left ventricular internal diameter (LVID). With a synergistic effect of both the STS and elastomeric polyurethane, the best therapeutic effect in MI was achieved by the ZPB@STS patch. The least fibrous tissue and empty cavity therewith were achieved under ZPB@STS treatment as shown by the Masson's trichrome staining and H&E staining (Fig. 7k). The synergistic action of anti-oxidation and robust mechanical support do contribute to the decreased collagen area and prevent the LV wall thinning (Fig. 7l and m). The cardiac troponin (cTnT) and α-actinin staining demonstrated that treatment in the STS group and especially the ZPB@STS group led to enhanced myocardial repair and cardiac contractility (Fig. 7n and Fig. S31). In Fig. S32, there was no obvious damage to each organ and no inflammatory cell infiltration, revealing that the nanofibrous film were safe for animal application in vivo.

Therefore, the design of the ZPB@STS patch is a more efficient and convenient way to achieve the selenium supplementation quickly in situ, and thereby facilitates the MI repair for the long term than the direct injection.

In this study, a selenium supplement (STS) functioning via the thiol-exchange mechanism was proposed for the first time to improve the selenium content and enzyme-like activity of oxidoreductases during cardiac repair, which offers higher bioavailability of selenium than traditional selenium supplements such as Na2SeO3, SeMet and (Sec)2. The structural similarity (selenotrisulfides containing -S-Se-S-) of STS is an imperative intermediate in the selenium transformation process, and the exchange reaction is integrally involved in the transport of selenium in vivo [11]. Although STS has been -reported as a molecular entity, its application as a selenium supplement in myocardial infarction is unprecedented, particularly in light of its positive therapeutic effects and potential regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism.

In the complex physiological environment having thiols and amino groups, the S–Se–S bonds preferentially undergo thiol exchange rather than amine substitution because selenium is a soft, highly polarizable electrophile that reacts rapidly with soft thiolate nucleophiles via a low-energy exchange mechanism, whereas amines are harder, less nucleophilic under physiological conditions, and do not form thermodynamically favored Se–N or S–N bonds [44]. Despite thiols are also abundant in biological systems overall, their effective concentrations and accessibility in plasma are relatively low [45]. STS would be more inclined to undergo a series of thiol exchange reactions rather than dissipate through non-specific reactions, thereby transferring selenium to human serum albumin (HSA) in plasma [11], which can then be transplanted to hepatocytes [46]. Selenotrisulfides binding to hemoglobin (Hb) through the thiol groups with high affinity validates the exchange possibility of STS and the free sulfhydryl groups [8]. It has also been reported that several proteins with free cysteine thiols such as myoglobin (Mb) are reactive with STS through a thiol-exchange reaction in cardiomyocytes [47]. Therefore, it is supposed that there might be two pathways for STS to restore the oxidoreductase-like activities. One is that some oxidoreductases with thiol groups directly capture and react with STS to obtain selenium; the other is that some selenium-transporting intermediates with thiol groups take the selenium first, and transfer selenium to oxidoreductases successively. Although specific mechanisms still need to be studied further, the STS showed comparable or even exceeding therapeutic efficacy than inorganic selenium (Na2SeO3), and did not cause further inflammation, showing a favorable bio-feasibility and safety profile.

Selenoproteins depend on selenium as an active site to reduce free radical levels in vivo, which are known to play an important role in antioxidant defence, immune cell activation and proliferation, and inflammation regulation [[48], [49], [50]]. Compared to the traditional selenium supplements, the unique reaction feature of STS endows it with a higher efficiency of selenium supplementation. Specifically, STS could upregulate anti-oxidative enzyme-like activities of GPx, TrxR, SOD and CAT, and selenoproteins’ gene expression, suggesting that it has the potential to act as a new generation organic selenium supplement. The active selenocysteine (Sec) residues in GPx and TrxR naturally undergo repeated oxidation–reduction, and short-lived Se–S intermediates are intrinsic to their physiological function [51,52]. The active sites formed after STS and thiol exchange also are likely to participate in this reaction. Furthermore, proteomic analysis revealed that STS significantly suppressed inflammasome activation and the expression of cleaved Caspase-1. Studies indicate that selenium nanoparticles inhibit the secretion of inflammatory cytokines via the NF-κB pathway during cerebral and nephrolithiasis formation, thereby suppressing NLRP3 expression and the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome [53,54]. In lipopolysaccharide-induced hepatic morphological damage, the upregulation of selenoprotein (GSH-Px) activity coincides with the inhibition of the NOD1 signal pathway [55]. Consequently, STS may regulate cellular oxidative balance by inhibiting inflammasome activity and modulating downstream inflammatory factor release via upregulating the activity of selenoproteins. Additionally, the STS could promote the cardiac muscle contraction function and regulate the glucolipid metabolism process to enhance energy supplementation. Inflammation is energy-intensive, and selenium chemistry could be involved in the ROS-energy metabolism interaction network to act on the process of sugar metabolism in liver [56], although the relationship between selenium chemistry and glycolipid metabolism, energy metabolism in the heart, still requires further research. Meanwhile, with the assistance of biomaterials [57], the sustained release of STS could be realized, resulting in a better therapeutic effect. In summary, STS supplementation in situ is more efficacious in boosting localized selenium levels and thereby better MI therapy compared to intravenous injection. Given the traditional selenium supplementation strategies are fundamentally limited in myocardial infarction due to poor tissue targeting, lack of local selenium bioavailability, and inability to directly restore the impaired selenoprotein activity, our in situ selenium supplementation strategy is expected be applicable to a wide type of similar diseases.

Conclusion

To overcome the reduced selenium content in myocardial tissue post-MI, a selenium supplement (STS) was successfully synthesized, which enhanced the selenoenzyme-like activity and alleviated oxidative stress significantly. The STS underwent thiol exchange with free sulfhydryl groups in proteins, leading to the formation of Se catalytic sites to decrease the ROS level and prevent oxidation. This reaction was firmly verified by the reaction with BSA and Cys as the protein and small model molecules, respectively. Cell and animal experiments demonstrate that STS displayed more significant improvement in the selenium content, redox-related proteins’ enzyme-like activity (GPx, TrxR, SOD and CAT) and gene expressions (GPx families, TrxR families, SelW, SelT, SelK and Sepx1) than the traditional selenium supplements, and thereby maximally decreased the inflammation response and apoptosis in the local lesion. Bioinformatic analysis reveals that the cardiac muscle contraction and glucolipid metabolism process were enhanced to accelerate cardiac repair. Furthermore, the ZPB@STS nanofibrous patch with a core-shell structure notably contributed to the lower fibrosis ratio, thicker left ventricular wall and improved heart functions under the synergistic effect of STS and mechanical support of elastomeric polyurethane fibrous patch. Collectively, the surpassing efficiency of the novel STS for selenium supplement (STS) is attractive for other selenium-deficient applications and disease therapy as well.
