The effect of social alienation on stigma among stroke inpatients in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yu Wu, Jian Liu, Lovel Fornah, Jun Zhao, Shicai Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStigma adversely affects the rehabilitation among stroke inpatients, which decreases the motivation and functional improvement. According to prior studies, there was a correlation between social alienation and stigma. This study aimed to investigate the factors influencing stigma among stroke inpatients, providing evidence for future targeted stigma interventions.MethodsThe study was a cross-sectional survey conducted in China from July to November 2023 using a convenience sampling method. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee. A total of 220 stroke inpatients completed a demographic questionnaire, the generalized social alienation scale, and the stroke stigma scale. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the impact of the diverse factors on stigma.ResultsThe results showed that the score of stigma among stroke inpatients was 47.36 ± 4.28, indicating a moderate level of stigma. Social alienation was positively correlated with stigma(r = 0.366, P < 0.001), indicating stigma increased with the increase of social alienation. Age(B=-2.105, P < 0.001), level of education[Junior high school(B = 1.545, P = 0.040), senior high school or technical secondary school(B = 3.480, P < 0.001), undergraduate or junior college(B = 3.716, P < 0.001), and graduate(B = 5.483, P < 0.001)], and social alienation(B = 0.158, P < 0.001) were all significantly associated with stigma in stroke inpatients. Among all educational levels, graduate(B = 5.483, P < 0.001) showed the most robust correlation with stigma. The results indicated that stroke inpatients who were younger and had higher levels of education had higher levels of stigma.ConclusionsFindings suggest that social alienation is a significant contributor to perceived stigma among stroke inpatients, particularly among younger and more educated individuals. Tailored psychosocial interventions that address feelings of social alienation may help reduce stigma in these groups.

Introduction

According to global epidemiological data, approximately 16.9 million people suffer a stroke each year, an annual global incidence of 258 per 100,000 population [1]. Stroke is the second-leading cause of death globally, following ischemic heart disease [2]. It is a prevalent neurological condition in China [3] that includes ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes [4]. According to research, more than half of stroke survivors commonly experience impairments such as cognitive dysfunction, speech and swallowing difficulties, motor deficits, and emotional instability [5]. Given the high burden of post-stroke disabilities, understanding associated psychosocial challenges, including stigma, is crucial for comprehensive rehabilitation.

Most stroke survivors experience stigma, which involves dealing with negative perceptions, societal stereotypes, and internalized self-doubt related to their illness [6] [7]. Stroke inpatients, especially during the acute and sub-acute phases, often struggle to accept their condition due to the visibility of physical deficits and their dependence on caregivers. In contrast to community-dwelling stroke survivors who gradually adapt to their impairments [8], hospitalized patients may face more intense psychological stress and heightened stigma due to their ongoing dependence and limited recovery [9]. Previous studies in China found that younger age, caregiver dependence, and low income were associated with higher stigma levels among stroke survivors [10–12]. Their results indicated that younger, caregivers-assisted, and low-income stroke survivors tended to have a higher level of stigma. In addition, Lin Zhang et al. [13]identified a relationship between social alienation and stigma. However, their study primarily explored self-esteem as a mediator and moderator in adults with disabilities, without examining additional contributors to stigma. Various factors influence patients’ social behavior, leading to psychological and behavioral manifestations of automatic alienation and isolation, known as social alienation [14]. Social alienation drives a wedge between patients and their loved ones, making it challenging for them to feel part of their families or connect with society. What’s more, it not only strengthens patients’ sense of isolation but also elevates the probability that their illness-related stigma will become more severe [15].

This study adopted the social cognitive processing model to explore the influence of social alienation on stigma. The social cognitive processing model, proposed by Lepore in 2001 [16], emphasizes that social constraints affect the social-psychological adjustment of patients by prolonging cognitive processing. According to the social cognitive processing model, cognitive processing is thwarted or delayed when an individual has social constraints, maintaining disease-related distress and hindering adjustment [17]. Social constraints reflect the perception that one cannot share disease-related concerns with others. Considering that social constraints are an important factor contributing to social alienation [18], the social cognition processing model has been adjusted. We conceptualized social alienation as a proxy for social constraints, in line with prior adaptations of the social cognitive processing model in chronic illness populations. This study used social alienation as a proxy for social constraints and stigma as an indicator of social-psychological adjustment following cognitive processing. Although stigma among community stroke survivors has been studied, little is known about how hospitalization-related factors and social alienation contribute to stigma among stroke inpatients. This study aimed to explore the impact of social alienation and demographic factors on stigma among stroke inpatients in China, addressing a key gap in the literature and informing targeted interventions.

Methods

Study design and sample

This cross-sectional study employed a convenience sampling method [19]. The study was conducted at a hospital for rehabilitation in Beijing, China, between July and November 2023.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first clinical diagnosis of stroke[confirmed by head computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)]; (2) onset of 1 to 6 months; and (3) ability and willingness to complete questionnaires. Exclusion criteria included (1) inability to complete questionnaires due to severe dysphasia or aphasia; and (2) cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders.

A power analysis was conducted using G*Power software to determine the required sample size. According to the results of previous study [20], the effect size f 2 was selected as 0.2. The power was 0.95, alpha was 0.05, and a total number of predictors was 8. The following variables were included as predictors in the regression model: gender, body mass index, age, level of education, marital status, primary caregiver, average monthly family income, and total social alienation. Consequently, a sample size of 122 participants was deemed appropriate. This study recruited 220 stroke inpatients, among whom 198 were included.

Data collection

To ensure the accuracy, data collection was conducted face-to-face with stroke inpatients in the rehabilitation ward. Researchers received standardized training to ensure consistency in data collection. Before data collection, patients were informed about the study’s purpose and provided written informed consent.

Measures

Demographic information

Demographic information was selected based on previous studies demonstrating their influence on stigma in stroke patients and included gender, body mass index, age, level of education, marital status, primary caregiver, and average monthly family income.

Social alienation

Social alienation was measured using the Generalized Social Alienation Scale, developed by Jessor R & Jessor S and translated by Shuang Wu et al. [21]. The Generalized Social Alienation Scale was divided into four dimensions: social isolation (5 items), powerlessness (4 items), self-alienation (3 items), and meaninglessness (3 items). Each item was answered on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with a higher score indicating a higher level of social alienation. The total score range of the scale was from 15 to 60. The Generalized Social Alienation Scale demonstrated good reliability among stroke inpatients in China, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.769.

Stigma

The Stroke Stigma Scale, developed by Minfang Zhu et al. [22], was used to assess stigma. The Stroke Stigma Scale contained four dimensions: self-perception (5 items), somatic impairment (4 items), discrimination experience (4 items), and social interaction (3 items). It consisted of 16 items with scoring using a Likert 5-point scale, each rated from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with a higher score indicating more severe stigma. The total score range of the scale was from 16 to 80. The Stroke Stigma Scale demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.916.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 21.0 was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics for variables regarding demographics were performed for the stroke inpatients. The mean and standard deviation for continuous data were calculated, whereas frequencies and percentages were utilized for categorical variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, the t-test (for two-group comparisons) or ANOVA (for multiple-group comparisons) was used. If normality assumptions were not met, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov‌ test was conducted to test normality on the continuous data. The Pearson correlation analysis was employed to investigate the relationship between stigma and social alienation. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the impact of the diverse factors on stigma. The results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. To account for multiple comparisons across five subgroup analyses(level of education), Bonferroni correction was applied (0.05/5 = 0.01).

Results

Before performing multiple linear regression, the data were evaluated for linearity, normality, independence, homogeneity of variance, and multicollinearity to determine suitability for linear regression analysis. The results of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that the continuous variables of age, social isolation, and stigma were normally distributed(P > 0.05). The results showed that the continuous independent variable and the dependent variable were linear, the residuals followed normal distribution, the observed values were independent (Durbin-Watson was 1.859), the residuals had homogeneity of variance, and there was no multicollinearity between the independent variables (Tolerance ranged from 0.480 to 0.907, VIF ranged from 1.103 to 2.084). The data in this study were suitable for multiple linear regression.

Discussion

We conducted a cross-sectional study to examine the impact of demographic factors and social alienation on perceived stigma among stroke inpatients. This study explored the impact of social alienation on stigma among stroke inpatients in China, where cultural factors such as self-esteem and societal expectations may influence stigma perceptions. The most important finding was that stigma was associated with age and level of education. The level of stigma was moderate among stroke inpatients. Significant correlations were found between stigma and social alienation. Consistent with previous study [23], inpatients experiencing stroke sequelae and higher social alienation reported greater stigma.

The results of this study showed that the average score of stigma among these 198 patients was 47.36 ± 4.28. This moderate level of stigma suggests that stroke inpatients may benefit from early interventions during hospitalization to prevent long-term psychosocial consequences. Our findings align with those of Fred Stephen Sarfo et al. [24], who observed moderate stigma levels among stroke survivors in West Africa. The self-perception score was the highest in this study. In clinical health education, we should focus on introducing related knowledge of stroke so that inpatients have a correct cognition of their condition, which may reduce their level of stigma [25]. In addition, the discrimination experience score in this study was the lowest, aligning with the findings of Minfang Zhu et al. [22]. These similarities reinforce the validity and generalizability of our findings across different cultural contexts. This observation may be related to the infrequent discrimination among doctors, nurses, and patients in hospital settings. Further research is needed to explore the varying experiences of discrimination among stroke patients in diverse settings.

This study found that social alienation was positively associated with stigma among stroke inpatients(r = 0.366, P < 0.001), which could be explained by Pearson correlation analysis of stigma and social alienation. Social isolation of social alienation was significantly positively correlated with stigma(r = 0.327, P < 0.001). During the novel coronavirus pandemic, infection control policies limited social interaction in hospitals [26]. This isolation may have amplified patients’ feelings of disconnection from their social networks, contributing to perceived alienation and stigma. In addition, discrimination experience of stigma was significantly positively correlated with social alienation(r = 0.373, P < 0.001). Patients had to endure the effects of stroke sequelae in this situation, making patients vulnerable to discrimination, which also increased the stigma of stroke inpatients [27].

The results of multiple linear regression analysis showed that age, level of education, and social alienation had effects on the stigma of stroke inpatients. We found that age has a moderate effect on stigma(B=>−2.105, P < 0.001). This study’s results were consistent with Amanda M Hughes et al. [28], indicating that younger stroke inpatients had more severe stigma than older patients. This may be related to the fact that younger patients generally had a strong body and did not anticipate the cause of stroke, so younger patients may feel stigmatized more easily than older patients. Medical professionals should pay attention to the emotions of young stroke inpatients and give them timely psychological guidance, which can reduce their stigma. However, in terms of the level of education, this study found that level of education has a large effect on stigma(P < 0.05), contrary to the results of Zixiu Zheng et al. [29]. In contrast to our inpatient sample, Zixiu Zheng et al.‘s study [29] focused on young and middle-aged stroke survivors and had a different classification of educational level, which may explain the differences in educational influence on stigma. This study found that stigma was more severe in patients with a higher level of education compared to stroke inpatients in primary school or below. Higher education may raise expectations regarding personal autonomy, professional identity, and social functioning. The sudden loss of these roles due to stroke may therefore provoke an intensified experience of stigma in this group [30]. Currently, there is a lack of studies regarding the effect of social alienation on the stigma experienced by stroke inpatients. The results of this study showed that there was a correlation between social alienation and stigma(B = 0.158, P < 0.001), indicating that stroke inpatients who possessed high social alienation had more severe stigma than those with low social alienation, which was the same as Petter Persson et al. [23] ’s, indicating the reliability of the results of this study. Hospitalization and sequelae of stroke limited the daily activities of patients, which made patients decrease to participate in social activities, leading to social alienation [31]. When stroke inpatients temporarily suspend their work for rehabilitation in the hospital, it could lead to changes in their social roles [32]. Compared with their previous healthy lives, stroke inpatients may experience a profound sense of role disruption and heightened vulnerability to stigma.

Our study has certain limitations. First, we chose a rehabilitation centre in Beijing exclusively for our study, limiting the representativeness of the sample. In addition, the reliance on convenience sampling could limit the generality of the findings. Therefore, it is necessary to carry out large sample studies with multi-centre and stratified sampling in the future, which may enhance the representation and universality of research results. In addition, qualitative studies can be appropriately conducted to explore subjective experiences of stigma in different socio-cultural contexts. Second, the study was conducted using a self-reported cross-sectional design, limiting its ability to capture the dynamic trend in stigma and social alienation experienced by the particular population under investigation and potentially introducing reporting biases. Future recommendations use validated psychometric tools and the latest statistical software to explore how stigma and social alienation evolve over time in longitudinal studies, providing a theoretical basis for conducting randomized controlled trial intervention clinical research. Third, we did not adjust for stroke severity or length of hospitalization, which may have influenced stigma perception. Future studies should include these potential confounding variables to improve model accuracy.

Conclusions

This study highlights the need for stigma-reduction interventions tailored to stroke inpatients, especially those who are younger and highly educated. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the causal relationship between social alienation and stigma.
