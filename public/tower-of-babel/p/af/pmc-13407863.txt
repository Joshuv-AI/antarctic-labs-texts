Prediction of ocular length in hypotony condition: a measurement study using immersion ultrasound biometry and Dino-Lite® microscope in goat eyes
Authors: Umar Mardianto, Andi Arus Victor, Prasandhya Astagiri Yusuf, Vetnizah Juniantito, Aria Kekalih, Tri Rahayu, Alhadi Bustamam, Lukman Edwar, Dewi Liputadika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiometry examination before cataract surgery is crucial to determine intraocular lens (IOL) power. It is mainly influenced by the measurement of eyeball axial length (AXL) and anterior chamber depth (ACD). The hypotony condition shortens these two parameters. The study was conducted to find an appropriate formula that can be used to correct the measurement of AXL and ACD in hypotony conditions. This was a prospective pre-post-experimental study in goat eye. The study population was adult goat eyes that met the inclusion, exclusion, and dropout criteria according to the sample size calculation. The data characteristics of IOP, AXL, and ACD in ultrasound biometry measurements of immersion techniques and Dino-Lite® in both eyes were normally distributed (p > 0.05). There was a linear relationship between IOP with AXL (p = 0.003) and ACD (p = 0.002) in ultrasound biometry and also in Dino-Lite® AXL (p = 0.001) and ACD (p = 0.008). A linear regression formula was found to predict AXL and ACD size correction in goat eyes. The formula obtained in this study can predict the actual size of the adult goat eyeball. Introduction

Cataracts are the leading cause of blindness in the world.1 Cataract surgery with good visual outcomes depends on the biometric examination to determine the power of the intraocular lens to be implanted. The selection of the intraocular lens to be implanted is influenced by accurate anatomical measurements of the eye and calculation formulas to predict the power of the intraocular lens required by the patient.2.

Biometry is an attempt to measure the anatomical parameters of the eyeball, which is an important step toward achieving optimal visual acuity after cataract surgery. The ocular AXL is required to measure the power of the IOL. AXL plays a major role in the biometry formula, a 1 mm difference in AXL will result in a 2.5-3D post-operative refractive error.3,4 Inaccuracies in biometric measurements—specifically anterior chamber depth, axial length, and keratometry—can contribute to 42%, 36%, and 22% of intraocular lens power calculation errors, respectively.5(doi: 10.3390/life15010045). The hypotony of the eyeball was caused by decreased IOP. Some conditions that cause hypotony include uveitis, trauma, or retinal detachment. The ultrasound biometry technique will compress the eyeball so that in the hypotony eye AXL measurement will be shortened due to indentation on the ocular surface. Even though using immersion techniques, the prager’s pressure on the sclera will result in changes in AXL and ACD. 6

The research subjects used animal eyes whose anatomical dimensions and structures are similar to human eyes, namely adult goat eyes. 6–9 The AXL of the human eye ranged from 22.5 to 24.0 mm and in the goat eye, the average was 24.24 mm.10–12 The average ACD in human eyes is 3.24 mm, while in goat eyes it is 3.39 mm.13 Human eyes have normal IOP values of 13–21 mmHg, while the IOP of Toggenburgers goats is 23 mmHg. 7,14

Methods

This research design is a prospective pre-post-experimental study on goat eye. The study was conducted in the Wet Lab Room on the 6th floor of the RSCM Kirana Building using Axis II® ultrasound biometry, and Dino-Lite® microscope. The IOP measurement was using I-Care®. The population of this study was healthy adult goat eyes, obtained from legal slaughterhouse (commercial pens for consumption) with a minimum sample size of 30 goat eyeballs. Goat eyes included in this study were goat eyes that met the inclusion, exclusion, and dropout criteria listed in more detail in the appendix. Goat eyes that were included originated from adult goats with normal macroscopic ocular anatomy, confirmed by the presence of permanent medial mandibular incisors, and with intraocular pressure of more than 10 mmHg. Exclusion criteria included eyes with any anterior or posterior segment abnormalities, such as corneal ulcer, keratitis, keratoconjunctivitis, intraocular tumors, or staphyloma posticum, eyes with globe damage occurring during enucleation or transport, and eyes exhibiting globe dimensions exceeding 10% of the normal human axial length range. These carefully defined criteria ensured that only structurally intact and physiologically comparable eyes were included.

Enucleated goat eyes were reinflated to approach pre-enucleation pressure, then subjected to four cycles of 10% decompression from initial pressure to achieve hypotonic intraocular pressure (IOP < 10 mmHg). Linear regression analysis was performed using SPSS version 20 to obtain the predictive equation. This research was carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations. This research has been approved by The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Indonesia-Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital with research ethics number: KET-358/UN2. F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2023 to protect medical research subjects. The animal eye holder that was used in this research has been patented by the Ministry of Law and Human Rights of the Republic of Indonesia with patent number IDS000009134. All methods in this research were in full compliance with Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

This research consistently applied research ethics that encompass animal welfare, adhering to the 3R principles: Reduction, Replacement, and Refinement. Reduction was achieved by optimizing the use of animal tissues, while Replacement was implemented by utilizing goat eyes obtained from commercial slaughterhouses. Refinement was ensured by minimizing animal suffering, as the enucleation procedure was performed post-mortem, thereby eliminating any potential pain or distress to the animals. The goat eye samples used in this study were sourced from residual tissues obtained from commercial slaughterhouses, further reducing animal suffering.

Discussion

Goat eye examinations are conducted in an experimental animal eye holder that can change position according to the purpose and method of examination. Morphometric examination of the clinical anatomical parameters of the eyeball is performed using ultrasound biometry.1,15 The best technique for performing ultrasound biometry is immersion technique, as it has minimal pressure on the structures of the eyeball and no pressure on the corneal structures. Another morphometric measurement technique that also does not make direct contact with the structures of the eyeball is using Dino-Lite® microscopy.16–18 Previous literature showed that an ultrasound immersion technique is less accurate than the swept-source optical coherence biometry.19 However, the swept-source optical coherence biometry could not be performed in dense cataract. In such circumstances, ultrasound immersion technique remains the only practical and clinically applicable approach.

The accuracy of these biometry measurements is important as a 0.1 mm error in measuring the axial length of the eyeball will result in a refractive error of approximately 0.23 diopters (D).1,15 In addition, intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation formulas vary across axial lengths. The Hoffer Q formula demonstrates superior accuracy in short eyes,20 while the Barrett Universal II formula performs better in long eyes.21 According to the European Registry of Quality Outcomes in Cataract and Refractive Surgery, the percentage of patients with a prediction error within ± 0.5 D after cataract surgery is only 73.7%.22

From this study, the IOP of goat eyes (using the I-care device) before enucleation was 15.92 mmHg, which is similar to the normal IOP range of human eyes of 13–21 mmHg. This result is slightly different from the literature which states that the IOP of Toggenburgers goats is 23 mmHg (9–37 mmHg), but is still within the normal range for goat eye IOP. 7,14 This difference may be due to the different species of goats slaughtered at the slaughterhouse in the Palmerah area, West Jakarta, Indonesia; another possibility is that the difference in mean IOP is due to the use of different IOP measuring instruments. 23

The eyeball length (AXL) of goats obtained from this study was 24.95 mmHg (using immersion technique ultrasound biometry) and 24.10 mmHg (using Dino-Lite®). These results are similar to the findings of Ribeiro AP et al. which state that the average AXL length of goat eyes is 24.24 mm.12 This is longer than the AXL of the human eye which ranges from 22.5 to 24.0 mm.10–12 The depth of the frontal eye chamber (ACD) of goats obtained from this study was 4.50 mm (using immersion technique ultrasound biometry) and 5.11 mm (using Dino-Lite®) which is much deeper than the average ACD of goat eyes of 3.39 mm and ACD of human eyes of 3.24 mm.13

The average AXL and ACD size of the post-enucleated goat eyeball was also still longer than the range of AXL and ACD sizes of normal human eyes. Adjustments were made to each AXL and ACD size data so that the model equations obtained at the end of the study could be tested on human eyes. This adjustment was made based on the assumption that the normal eye axial length (AXL) is approximately 23 mm and the anterior chamber depth (ACD) is approximately 3 mm.

From the research conducted, efforts to reduce the IOP of experimental animals with an average IOP reduction of 10% for four times obtained a shortening of the AXL length and ACD depth to reach hypotony conditions in both the right and left eyes (IOP < 10 mmHg). The interesting thing in this study is that in the examination of AXL and IOP consistently lowered in 5x measurements, the AXL in the immersion technique ultrasound biometry measurement is consistently longer than the measurement with Dino-Lite®, while in the examination of IOP and ACD consistently lowered in 5x measurements, the ACD is longer in the measurement with Dino-Lite® than the immersion technique ultrasound biometry. This may be due to the shape and size of the goat cornea which is not round (like a trapezium) so there is pressure on the cornea by the preger cells when measurements are taken in all IOP conditions. In addition, there was also a linear relationship between IOP and the length of AXL and ACD in measurements using immersion ultrasound biometry techniques as well as in measurements with Dino-Lite® (p-value < 0.05) which indicates that changes in IOP affect changes in AXL and ACD in all goat eyes, both right and left eyes.

The primary limitation of this study is that the regression model was developed using goat ocular morphometry, which may not fully represent the characteristics of human eyes. Consequently, the predictive equation derived in this study cannot yet be directly applied to human hypotonic eyes. Further research and validation using the measurement of human eyes are required to refine and verify the model before clinical application can be considered. We are still researching how to make the equation work on human eyes.

Conclusion

The immersion and Dino-Lite® ultrasound biometry models of goat eyes were found to predict AXL and ACD under normal conditions from hypotony in goat eyes. Although the R-square of AXL in this study was better in the Dino-Lite® group, the model equation from the immersion ultrasound biometry group was still used for clinical purposes. Calculations using the SRK formula show that an error of 1 mm will result in a difference of 2.5 D, so using this formula can determine how much refractive surprise is obtained. This study still needs to be tested on humans to assess or optimize the model equation formula obtained from goat eyes.
