Transplacental transfer efficiency of environmental chemicals in association with gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Jordana Leader, Jessica Robinson, Brian Whitcomb, Damaskini Valvi, Chia-Yang Chen, Yap Seng Chong, Kok Hian Tan, Sharon Ng, James Chun Yip Chan, Johan G. Eriksson, Shiao-Yng Chan, Jonathan Huang, Youssef Oulhote
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Many environmental chemicals have been shown to cross the placental barrier, threatening the health of offspring. Additionally gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) may affect the transplacental transfer ratios (TTRs) of environmental chemicals, but the rate of transfer of a wide variety of environmental chemicals have not been widely studied.Objective:We examined the TTRs of per- and poly- fluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), phthalates, parabens, bisphenols, and benzophenones in pregnant women, and if GDM increases transplacental transfer of these chemicals.Methods:We examined 576 mother-newborn pairs from the Growing up in Singapore Towards healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) birth cohort enrolled 2009–2010. Fetal cord plasma to maternal plasma ratios from samples around delivery (TTRs) were estimated for 10 PFAS, 6 phthalates, bisphenol-S (BPS), 2 parabens, and 2 benzophenones. GDM was defined as elevated fasting plasma glucose or 2 h plasma glucose from an oral glucose tolerance test at mid-gestation. We estimated covariate-adjusted associations of GDM with TTRs using multivariable regressions.Results:Median TTRs ranged from 0.13 for benzophenone to 2.2 for mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate. Overall, TTRs were highest for phthalates and short-chain PFAS and lowest for benzophenone and long-chain PFAS. GDM was significantly associated with higher TTRs for perfluorooctanoic, perfluorononanoic, and perfluoroheptanoic acids, BPS, monoethyl phthalate, and oxybenzone. Mothers with GDM had 15% (95% confidence intervals [CI]: 5%, 25%) and 123% (95% CI: 32%, 214%) higher TTRs for oxybenzone and BPS respectively as compared to mothers without GDM.Conclusions:We found high TTRs for multiple short half-life chemicals and more recently introduced short-chain PFAS. Importantly, GDM was associated with increased transplacental efficiency of several classes of environmental chemicals, particularly chemicals with shorter half-lives.

Background

Since the 1950s, over 350,000 man-made chemicals have entered the market (Naidu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Many of these chemicals are endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs) and can bioaccumulate in the environment and harm human health. Exposure to persistent environmental chemicals is therefore a pervasive public health issue. For instance, perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) can impact a wide range of health outcomes including immune function, thyroid function, liver disease, and kidney function, and are also associated with reproductive and developmental outcomes (Blake & Fenton, 2020). Parabens, that are used as a preservative in several consumer products including cosmetics, personal care products, and medicinal products were originally considered relatively safe; however, recent studies have shown they can act as endocrine disrupters, along with observed obesogenic effects (Andersen et al., 2021; Reimann et al., 2021). This brings up concerns about future health outcomes for the fetus, by increasing the risk of adverse health outcomes such as low birth weight and cryptorchidism, and later chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, and cancers (Tang et al., 2020). Bisphenol-S (BPS) has been marketed as a replacement for bisphenol-A, one of the earlier chemicals discovered to be an endocrine disrupter and was widely used in consumer products (Pérez-Bermejo et al., 2021). While BPS works via different pathways, exposure can similarly be a risk factor for obesity and dysregulation of the reproductive system (Thoene et al., 2020). Phthalates are industrial chemicals used as plasticizers, stabilizing agents, and softeners to create soft and flexible plastic and therefore used in many products (Hlisníková et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2020). Phthalates have been shown to be most significantly associated with reproductive disorders in both males and females due to their endocrine-disrupting properties (Hlisníková et al., 2020). Finally, benzophenones are a group of chemicals widely used in numerous commercial products that are exposed to sunlight, such as personal care products, including sunscreens, body lotions, shampoos, nail polish, lipsticks, and hair sprays, and other synthetic products. Recent epidemiological investigations have identified associations between benzophenones and fecundity and other reproductive outcomes, as well as adverse birth outcomes (Yao et al., 2023).

Despite the potential developmental health effects associated with exposure to these chemicals, they are still widely used in consumer products and industrial processes, leading to ubiquitous exposure (Andersen et al., 2021; Fenton et al., 2021; Hlisníková et al., 2020; Pérez-Bermejo et al., 2021). A common route of exposure for offspring is through transplacental transfer (Varshavsky et al., 2020). The placenta is a crucial barrier to protect the fetus and also plays a role in the transfer of nutrients. However, the placenta has also been shown to allow for the transfer of chemicals, surpassing the barrier, resulting in newborns being exposed to a burden of chemicals from the mother. Specifically, parabens, PFAS, and phthalates have been shown to cross the placental barrier, entering the fetal bloodstream (Eryasa et al., 2019; Genuis et al., 2012; Kang et al., 2021).

Various conditions of pregnancy can affect the morphology of the placenta (Sirpurkar & Anjankar, 2015). One such condition is gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a common complication in pregnancy, affecting both the mother and fetus’ health. GDM is defined as the onset of high blood glucose concentrations during pregnancy, which may resolve after childbirth (Hewage et al., 2020). Worldwide, GDM affects 1 out of 6 births (Hewage et al., 2020). Our previous study in the Faroe Islands revealed that gestational diabetes may affect transplacental transfer of PFAS (Eryasa et al., 2019). In this new study, we aimed to replicate those findings in a different cohort in Singapore, and to extend the research to additional environmental chemicals to characterize the transplacental transfer rates (TTR) of a wide range of chemicals, and to investigate whether GDM is associated with greater transplacental transfer of PFAS, phthalates, parabens, and bisphenols, increasing disease risk for the offspring.

Methods

Study population

The study population for this analysis was a subset (N = 576) of participants from the on-going Growing up in Singapore Towards Healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) birth cohort study. The primary objective of the GUSTO study was to evaluate exposures and influences in early development to adverse metabolic functions and body composition later in life. The GUSTO study enrolled 1468 pregnant women at least 18 years of age between June 2009 and September 2010 attending their first trimester antenatal dating ultrasound scan clinic at Singapore’s two major public maternity units. Women receiving chemotherapy, psychotropic drugs or those with type I diabetes mellitus were excluded. Women and their children have been followed up periodically for over 12 years. Further details of this study have been detailed in a previous cohort profile (Soh et al., 2014). A subset of women in the GUSTO cohort with available fetal and maternal plasma samples quantified for EDCs were included in this study. Comparisons of mothers who were enrolled at baseline with mothers and newborns included in this analysis are presented in supplementary material; Table S4. Overall, there were no noticeable differences in the main characteristics, except for maternal BMI that was significantly higher in the initial cohort compared to the included mothers in this analysis. This study was approved by the National Health Care Group Domain Specific Review Board (reference D/09/021) and the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (reference 2009/280/D). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants upon recruitment.

Assessment of exposure to environmental chemicals

Maternal and cord blood was collected at delivery to measure environmental chemicals. Fetal blood samples were collected from the umbilical cord at birth immediately following delivery. Non-fasting blood samples were obtained from mothers at the hospital at the time of delivery. The blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and processed within 4 h. Samples were centrifuged at 1600g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma and stored at −80 °C. Samples were thawed prior to analysis using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). All analyses were conducted at the National Taiwan University Hospital laboratory.

All samples were analyzed for chemical concentrations and quantified in ng/mL. The precise analysis procedure of PFAS in this cohort is described in greater detail in a previous publication (Chen et al., 2024). PFAS compounds analyzed included: pentafluorobenzoic acid (PFBA), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA), perfluorobutanesulfonic acid (PFBS), Perfluorohexanesulphonic acid (PFHxS), and Perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS). The limit of detection for PFAS ranged from 0.007 ng/mL to 0.27 ng/mL. The limit of quantitation for PFAS ranged from 0.07 ng/mL to 3.33 ng/mL. The exact analysis procedure of phthalates in this cohort is described in greater detail in a previous publication (Hwa et al. 2022). Briefly, The LC-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method was employed using an Eksigent LC 100 system (Eksigent Technologies) coupled with a 6500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (Sciex). The recovery of phthalate metabolites was between 83.1 % and 105.4 % (acceptable range 80 %–120 %). The acceptable accuracy calculated by the calibration curve was between 80 % and 120 %. The phthalate metabolites analyzed included: Mono-methyl phthalate (MMP), Mono-ethyl phthalate (MEP), Mono-n-butyl phthalate (MBP), Mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP), Mono-n-octyl phthalate (MNOP), Mono-(3-carboxypropyl) phthalate (MCPP), Mono-(2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl) phthalate (MECPP), and Mono-carboxyisononyl phthalate (MCINP). The limit of detection for phthalates ranged from 0.006 ng/mL to 0.41 ng/mL. The limit of quantitation for phthalates ranged from 0.07 ng/mL to 0.41 ng/mL. The bisphenols and parabens analyzed included: bisphenol-S (BPS), methylparaben, and propylparaben. The limit of detection for bisphenols and parabens ranged from 0.007 ng/mL to 0.06 ng/mL. The limit of quantitation for bisphenols and parabens ranged from 0.07 ng/mL to 0.33 ng/mL. The benzophenones analyzed include benzophenone and oxybenzone. The limit of detection and quantitation is 0.75 ng/mL for benzophenone and 0.14 for oxybenzone.

Gestational diabetes mellitus measurement

Participants underwent a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (75 g-OGTT) between 26 and 28 weeks of gestation, when additional blood samples were collected to measure other biomarkers such as plasma folate concentrations. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was defined according to WHO guidelines using fasting plasma glucose (≥7.0 mmol/L) or 2 h plasma glucose levels (≥7.8 mmol/L).

Covariates

Interviewer-administered questionnaires administered at recruitment and 26–28 weeks of pregnancy were used to assess demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, maternal depression and anxiety, parity, obstetric, and medical history data. Maternal folate concentrations were measured in ng/ml in plasma at 26–28 weeks gestation (Bevital laboratories, Norway). Maternal education was categorized as primary school or less, secondary school, vocational school, polytechnical school, university or higher, maternal ethnicity was categorized as Chinese, Malay and Indian or other and household monthly income was categorized as $0-$999, $1000-$1999, $2000-$3999, and $4000-$5999 or greater than $6000. BMI was calculated using study staff measured height and self-reported pre-pregnancy weight, and child biological sex was obtained from medical records.

Statistical analyses

Of the 1468 enrolled women, 781 participants had available samples for chemical analyses; however, among the 781, only 579 had paired maternal and cord plasma analyzed. Chemicals were included in the TTR analysis if there were more than 50 mother-newborn pairs with maternal and cord detectable chemical concentrations. Numerous participants were below the limit of detection or quantification for certain PFAS, phthalates, and parabens measured. All values below detection limit (LOD) were inputted using regression on order statistics (Helsel, 2005). This is a more robust and less biased approach than the common approach of imputing values < LOD as the LOD/2 (Oulhote et al., 2016; Shumway et al., 2002). Fifth, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles, mean, and standard deviations were used to summarize the distribution of PFAS, phthalates, parabens, and benzophenones in both maternal plasma and fetal cord plasma. We also calculated chemical distributions by demographic groups, and they were compared using geometric mean (GM) and standard deviation. This was done to give a picture of the potential correlates of transplacental transfer, while also accounting for possible physiological changes to the placenta or differing risks of exposure due to these correlates. The differences in the GM of chemical concentrations by demographic variable were assessed via t-test or analysis of variance, as appropriate. Correlations between chemical concentrations in each sample type (maternal plasma and fetal cord) were assessed using spearman correlation.

Transplacental transfer ratios (TTRs) were calculated as ratios of fetal cord blood concentrations to maternal blood plasma concentration for ten PFAS, six phthalates, BPS, three parabens, and two benzophenone compounds. Perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA), Perfluorododecanoic acid (PFDoA), Mono-n-octyl phthalate (MNOP), Mono (3-carboxypropyl) phthalate (MCPP), Monobenzyl phthalate (MBzP), Bisphenol-A (BPA) and butylparaben had insufficient sample sizes of both quantifiable cord and maternal blood plasma concentrations were excluded from further analyses. In order to explore the associations between GDM and these TTRs, we conducted covariate-adjusted multivariable linear regressions. All TTRs were standardized and final models included the following covariates: measurement plate (to account for different batches of chemicals being analyzed), maternal education (primary school or less, secondary school, vocational school, polytechnical school, university or higher), maternal ethnicity (Chinese, Malay and Indian or other), maternal folate concentrations (ng/ml measured in blood at 26–28 weeks gestation), pre-pregnancy BMI (<18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, >30), parity, household income ($0-$999, $1000-$1999, $2000-$3999, $4000-$5999, >$6000), maternal smoking during pregnancy (ever/never), and child sex. The resulting estimates represent the standard deviation (SD) change in TTR associated with gestational diabetes. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 and R Studio software.

Results

Descriptive statistics

This analysis included 576 mother-newborn pairs who had both delivery maternal and cord blood samples available. Mothers had a mean age of 30 years at recruitment, with almost all mothers under the age of 40 (97.2 %) (Table 1). The majority of mothers were Chinese (48 %) and Malay (31 %), with more than half of mothers having a previous child (59 %). Over 77 % of mothers reported taking a supplement containing folic acid and 81 % of mothers had sufficient folate levels measured in plasma at 26 weeks of pregnancy (>13.6 nmol/L) (Table 1). The prevalence of GDM in this study population was 18 %. Asian populations are at higher risk of GDM.

Overall, butyl paraben, PFHxA, MNOP, MCPP, and MBzP were detected in <10 % (ranging from 2 % to 8 %) of samples in the cohort. BPA was detected in <70 % of maternal plasma, but <8 % of cord plasma. PFDoDA was detected in 61 % of maternal plasma samples but only 11 % of cord plasma samples resulting in 44 mother-newborn pairs with available samples with detectable PFDoDA. Consequently, PFHxA, PFDoDA, MNOP, MCPP, MBzP, BPA, and butylparaben were excluded from further analyses. All other PFAS, phthalates, parabens, and bisphenols had greater than 50 mother-newborn pairs with quantifiable concentrations and were included in the analyses of transplacental transfer ratios. Benzophenone and PFBS had the highest mean concentrations in both maternal plasma (256 ng/mL and 16 ng/mL, respectively) and fetal cord blood (55 ng/mL and 26 ng/mL, respectively) samples (Table 2). Within maternal samples, the highest correlations were observed between PFNA and PFDA (ρ = 0.75), and the lowest were observed between PFOS and MEP (ρ = −0.00025). Similarly, within cord samples, the highest correlations were observed between PFDA and PFUnDA (ρ = 0.72), and the lowest were observed between PFPeA and Methylparaben (ρ = 0.00015) (Fig. 1).

Transplacental transfer ratios (TTRs)

Median TTRs ranged from 0.13 for benzophenone to 2.2 for Mono-2-Ethylhexyl phthalate. Overall, the median TTR was 0.79 for PFAS concentrations, 1.87 for phthalate metabolite concentrations, and 0.71 for paraben concentrations. TTRs were highest for phthalates ranging from 1.21 (MMP) to 2.93 (MEHP) and short-chain PFAS, specifically PFBA (1.97), PFPeA (1.75), PFHpA (1.36) and PFBS (5.12). TTRs were lowest for benzophenone (2.60) and long-chain PFAS, specifically PFDA (0.85), PFUnDA (0.95) and PFOS (0.62) (Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 1).

Geometric mean TTRs of most PFAS, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnDA, and PFOS differed by mother’s ethnicity with mothers of Indian or other ethnicity having higher TTR of PFNA, PFDA, PFUnDA, and PFOS compared to Chinese or Malay mothers. Additionally, higher TTRs of MECPP were observed for mothers with Singaporean citizenship compared to mothers who were not Singaporean citizens, which may be reflective of a longer total lifetime living in Singapore. TTRs of BPS and PFOS differed by marital status, with married women having higher TTRs of PFOS and single women having higher TTRs of BPS. TTRs of PFHxS and MEHP differed by parity with higher parity being associated with higher TTRs. TTR of PFPeA were higher in women with lower household income compared to those with higher household income. Lastly, TTR of PFHxS were higher in ever or current smokers than non-smokers (Supplemental Table 2).

Associations of gestational diabetes and transplacental transfer ratios

In multivariable models adjusted for confounders, mothers with GDM had higher transplacental transfer rates of PFHpA (β = 0.30, 95 % CI: 0.01, 0.59), PFOA (β = 0.41, 95 % CI: 0.07, 0.75), PFNA (β = 0.18, 95 % CI: 0.03, 0.34), BPS (β = 2.42, 95 % CI: 0.63, 4.22), and oxybenzone (β = 0.15, 95 % CI: 0.05, 0.36) compared to mothers with no gestational diabetes (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table 3). For instance, women with GDM had a TTR for BPS 2.42 SD higher than women with without GDM. TTRs for phthalates or parabens did not differ in women with GDM compared to women without GDM.

Discussion

This study investigated the transplacental transfer efficiency of multiple key environmental chemical families, i.e. PFAS, phthalates, parabens, benzophenones, and BPS, using the ratios of concentrations of these chemicals measured in fetal cord plasma to maternal plasma. In this study of Singaporean pregnant mother-fetal pairs, we found higher TTRs for short-chain PFAS compared to long-chain. Additionally, we found higher TTRs for chemicals with shorter half-lives in the paraben family compared to parabens with longer half-lives. We also found that geometric mean transfer ratios for certain endocrine disrupting chemicals, particularly PFAS, did differ by mother’s demographic characteristics, specifically maternal ethnicity, citizenship, parity, marital and smoking status. While these correlates may be confounded by other factors, we sought to present them as research on this topic is scarce and may help identify predictors of TTRs, even if not causally. We further explored the association of GDM with TTRs and found that GDM affected the transfer of certain chemicals across the placenta. Specifically, women with GDM had higher TTRs of certain PFAS, oxybenzone, and BPS compared to women without GDM.

It is well documented that environmental chemicals can pass through the placenta and be transmitted to the developing fetus. More specifically, bisphenol-A and parabens have been shown to cross the placental barrier (Balakrishnan et al., 2010; Grandin et al., 2019; Li et al., 2023; Towers et al., 2015; Zbucka-Krętowska et al., 2019). This has been less studied for phthalates, however research is emerging (Tang et al., 2022). For PFAS, transplacental transfer is documented, with compelling evidence suggesting the impact of carbon chain length and the physico-chemical structure on transfer rates which supports the findings of this study (Eryasa et al., 2019; Gützkow et al., 2012; Pan et al., 2017). Within these classes of environmental chemicals, compounds with low molecular weight (below 1000 g/mol) or those that are lipophilic have been shown to more easily cross the placental membrane than metabolites with higher molecular weights (Mose et al., 2007).

While we have expressed TTR as the transfer of chemicals from mother to fetus, the TTR may also capture how efficiently the mother, and the fetus each metabolize the chemical. There is also the possibility that the fetus could additionally transfer back to the mother through the placenta as it has been shown that there is bidirectional transfer of cells across the placenta (Lo et al., 2000). If this is true for environmental chemicals, what is seen in the TTRs could actually be the net effect. Additionally, through repeated exposure to environmental chemicals, there is bioaccumulation of the chemicals in the fetus (Mitro et al., 2015). It is possible that TTR may capture the ratio of metabolic activity in the fetus over the ratio of metabolic activity in the mother, in addition to the ability of chemicals to be transferred across the placenta.

It is interesting to mention the high TTRs above 100 %. Chemicals and their metabolites can accumulate in the fetus and placenta, reaching concentrations higher than those found in the maternal blood. This phenomenon may be driven by active transport mechanisms that can move chemicals across the placental barrier more efficiently for some chemical, but also by higher affinity of some chemicals to fetal tissues than maternal tissues (Rager et al., 2020; Kotta-Loizou et al., 2024).

There is limited research regarding the associations between GDM and transplacental transfer rates of environmental chemicals. Our previous study conducted in the Faroese Island birth cohorts similarly found an increased transplacental transfer of PFAS compounds in mothers with GDM ranging from 13 % increased TTRs for PFOA (95 % CI: −3, 29 %) to 44 % increased TTRs PFUnDA (95 % CI: 15, 73 %) (Eryasa et al., 2019). Additionally, while not an environmental chemical, a previous study found that gestational diabetes interfered with the transplacental transfer of a metabolite of lidocaine (mono-ethylglycinexylidide) (Moises et al., 2015). The mechanism by which gestational diabetes may interfere with transplacental transfer of environmental chemicals is hypothesized to be due to functional and structural changes in the placenta under conditions of poor glycemic control (Eryasa et al., 2019). More specifically, changes in placental capillarization index in women with GDM may affect the rate of transplacental transfer (Eryasa et al., 2019; Mayhew, 2003; Moises et al., 2015). Subsequently, functional changes within the placenta due to gestational diabetes may generally change the transplacental transfer rate of environmental chemicals. As well, GDM is observed at a higher prevalence in Asian populations with the 2021 prevalence of GDM in the general Singaporean obstetric population being about 20 % (Atlas, 2015).

Our finding that GDM affected the transfer efficiency of certain chemicals across the placenta may have implications for future research in this field. GDM may increase fetal exposure to chemicals through increased placental transfer but also interact with these exposures to enhance adverse health effects in the mothers and children. Common mechanisms such as alteration in amino acid and lipid metabolism have been described to underlie the effects of both GDM and PFAS (Cetin et al., 2005; Guo et al., 2022; India-Aldana et al., 2023). Additionally, chemical exposures may also increase the risk for GDM (Ehrlich et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2022). Co-occurrence of GDM and higher chemical exposures is possible and might substantially enhance susceptibility. GDM may alter the mother’s ability to metabolize some of the chemicals as well as additionally impair the ability of the fetus to breakdown the chemicals compared to fetuses of mothers without GDM. Further research in this area could help advance knowledge in this field and inform interventions that reduce the impacts of chemicals for both mothers and their offsprings.

With any study, there are both strengths and limitations. A limitation of this study is that phthalates, BPS, oxybenzone and some parabens are preferably measured in urine due to their short half-lives, however in this study they were measured in blood (both maternal plasma and fetal cord blood). Moderate to strong correlations between urine and blood concentrations (r = between 0.45 and 0.90) have been reported for the secondary metabolites of DEHP and for the primary metabolite of DEP (Frederiksen et al., 2010; Hines et al., 2009; Högberg et al., 2008), with lower correlations (r = 0.37 and 0.39) for metabolites of DiBP and DiNP (Frederiksen et al., 2010). Similar correlations were found for parabens and their metabolites (Bräuner et al., 2022). While many of these environmental chemicals are often less detectable in blood than urine, it shows a relatively accurate representation of the chemicals circulating in the blood and therefore likely in the placenta. Additionally, using measurements on maternal and cord samples both acquired around the time of delivery would arguably constitute a better approximation of the transfer of chemicals from the mother to the fetus. There is however a possible risk of contamination of the blood samples if the medical equipment is plastic and contains EDCs, which could contribute to non-differential misclassification. However, if any exposure misclassification measurement occurred with the measurements being done in blood, it should be non-differential between the mother and fetus, thus not impacting the results shown in this study. Also, for phthalates, the metabolites were measured so these must be products of body metabolism and not contaminants from sample processing. Additionally, these samples were collected between 2009 and 2010, therefore we were not able to analyze newer replacement chemicals and explore their TTRs. Future research in a contemporary cohort would be beneficial to examine these newer replacement chemicals. Chemicals with shorter half-lives, such as phthalates, may be metabolized faster in the mother, therefore a single measurement at delivery may underestimate the total fetal exposure over the course of pregnancy from repeated maternal exposure. This could result in confounding as the denominator of the TTR is higher than the actual exposure, resulting in a lower TTR, biasing away from the null. The single assessment around the time of delivery will be more reflective for longer half-life chemicals. While we have information on GDM, and women with type I diabetes were excluded from the study, there is not information on type II which could additionally affect the results of this study, biasing results towards the null assuming type II diabetes would have similar effects on TTRs. Additionally, we do not have information on behaviors that could increase EDC exposure such as personal care product use or consumption of canned foods, which would be helpful to further understanding of EDC sources of exposure. Despite these limitations, this study represents both the largest study and one of the only studies investigating the trans-placental transfer of a larger variety of endocrine disrupting chemicals. Additionally, this study supports the previous.

Conclusion

We found higher transfer rates of certain chemicals in women with gestational diabetes mellitus as compared to women without gestational diabetes mellitus. This research highlights pregnancy as a sensitive window of environmental chemical exposure and the potential joint impacts of exposure to endocrine disrupting chemicals and GDM in enhancing fetal susceptibility to environmental diseases. These findings as well as future continued research could support the need for increased environmental counseling to women with GDM.
