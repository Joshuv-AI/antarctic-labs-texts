Rehabilitation nursing after lower limb fracture: Preventing deep vein thrombosis and enhancing quality of life
Authors: YanLing Ruan, FengTao Wang, XieQin Du, ShanShan Sun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to assess the preventive role of rehabilitation nursing in the formation of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the lower limbs after fracture and its effect on the patient's quality of life. Ninety patients with lower limb fractures were randomly divided into a study group (n = 45) and a control group (n = 45). Patients in the control group received routine care postoperatively, whereas patients in the study group received rehabilitation nursing intervention on the same postoperative day. A follow-up examination was conducted after 3 months. The occurrence rate of lower limb DVT (LDVT) complications, hemorheology, quality of life (SF-36) scores, coagulation profile, and nursing satisfaction were compared between the groups. After the intervention, the study group presented with lower extremity DVT and higher hemorheology indices than those of the control group (P < .05). The SF-36 scores of both groups increased, but the increase in SF-36 scores in the study group was more significant (P < .001). The satisfaction level of the patients in the study group with nursing services was higher than that of the control group (P = .004). Coagulation indicators can be effectively adjusted and the occurrence of postoperative complications can be reduced in patients undergoing spine fracture surgery such as DVT via standardized rehabilitation care, thereby improving patients’ quality of life and nursing conditions.

1. Introduction

Lower limb fractures are a prevalent clinical condition that significantly impacts patients’ daily functioning and occupational performance.[1] The deceleration of venous blood flow in these patients ultimately contributes to an elevated susceptibility to deep venous thrombosis (DVT).[2,3] The occurrence rate of lower limb fractures in China is approximately 3.6%, based on incomplete data, with the majority of DVT affecting the lower limbs and being rare in the upper limbs.[4] Currently, surgical treatment is commonly used for lower limb fractures. However, patients remain prone to various postoperative complications, with the most common and representative complication being lower limb DVT (LDVT).[5] Failure to prevent or treat LDVT promptly may cause thrombus detachment into the pulmonary blood vessels, leading to pulmonary embolism and endangering the patient life.[6,7] Therefore, taking effective intervention measures to prevent the formation of LDVT is of considerable practical significance for improving the patient quality of life and reducing the risk of pulmonary embolism after lower limb fractures. Rehabilitation nursing focuses on the patient and adopts targeted nursing measures to provide personalized services to patients. Massage helps to eliminate venous congestion, promote blood circulation, and adopt reasonable rehabilitation measures to effectively prevent LDVT in patients with lower limb fractures.[8,9] The impact of rehabilitation nursing on LDVT after lower limb fractures has been widely studied, but less so in the reported incidence of LDVT.[10,11] Therefore, this study aimed to explore the preventive effect of rehabilitation nursing on the formation of DVT after lower limb fractures and analyze its impact on patients’ quality of life.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study participants

From January 2021 to March 2023, we conducted an observational analysis of 90 patients with lower limb fractures admitted to our hospital. Patients were randomly divided into a study group (n = 45) and a control group (n = 45). The general information about the patients is shown in Table 1. The control group consisted of 45 patients (24 men and 21 women) with an average age of 49.5 ± 5.8 years. Among them, 12 patients had cervical fractures, 13 had thoracic spine fractures, and 20 had lumbar fractures. The study group consisted of 45 patients (25 men and 20 women) with an average age of 48.8 ± 4.5 years. Among the cohort, 13 patients had cervical fractures, 12 had thoracic spine fractures, and 20 had lumbar fractures. The patient data did not significantly differ between the groups (P > .05).

2.2. Selection criteria

The inclusion criteria were: patients aged 25 to 65 years; patients diagnosed with lower limb fractures radiographically, CT scans, and other imaging examinations; patients with only lower limb fractures and no other complex injuries; patients who met the surgical indications and underwent surgical treatment; patients who had not used anticoagulant drugs in the 2 months before the experiment; patients who were aware of the content of this nursing study, agreed to participate, and actively cooperated. Exclusion criteria: were patients with fractures in other locations; patients with a history of thrombosis; patients with blood or coagulation disorders; patients participating in different research projects simultaneously; patients resistant to cooperation with the nursing workers.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Liyuan Hospital of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology ethics committee.

2.3. Methods

The control group of patients received routine nursing interventions during the perioperative period, including preoperative patient disease education and surgical content education. Postoperatively, regular dressing changes were performed, infection prevention care was conducted, and patients were guided to engage in early rehabilitation training. The study group implemented evidence-based nursing interventions during the perioperative period with specific methods, including establishing nursing teams and implementing standardized nursing training.[4,12,13] Specialized training on standardized nursing was provided to the grassroots nursing staff of each nursing team, focusing on explaining the specific reasons for LDVT. Psychological factors: Various negative emotions such as anxiety, tension, and fear that patients experience preoperatively can cause increased blood viscosity in the circulatory system, causing increased blood clot formation. Prolonged bed resting period: Patients with lower limb fractures lose their mobility shortly after surgery. Although they can restrict lower limb movement during the subsequent recovery stage, they may still be unable to move normally because of pain and other reasons. This significantly reduces blood circulation in the lower limbs and easily triggers blood coagulation. Improper positioning: Some postoperative patients may change their resting position or maintain the same position for a long time because of pain, which can easily cause LDVT. Lifestyle habits: Patients who smoke or drink alcohol may experience adverse effects on their vascular endothelium and decreased venous blood flow velocity. Chronic diseases: If patients have additional conditions such as obesity, hypertension, or diabetes before surgery, their blood viscosity is higher than that of other populations, which significantly increases the probability of LDVT. Nursing staff need to be trained on relevant measures to assess patients’ clinical conditions to understand the risk of postoperative DVT.

Preoperative standardized nursing methods. Preoperative preparation nursing before the surgery starts: Nursing staff should assist patients in completing various examinations, and if necessary, a cart or wheelchair can be used to facilitate patient mobility. After the examinations, the skin temperature, pain perception, and motor function should be examined, and the clinical significance of these examinations and assessments should be explained in detail to increase patient cooperation. Preoperative psychological nursing: Comprehensive assessments should be conducted on patients’ psychological status before surgery, and the level of understanding of lower limb fracture surgery by patients and their family members should be confirmed. Anxiety, fear, and other psychological issues should be alleviated, and the general surgical process should be actively introduced. The operation procedures can be explained step by step using a bed model simulating the operating table, and the patient medical records handled by the surgeon should be introduced to significantly increase patient confidence in treatment. If necessary, testimonials from patients in the same or adjacent ward can be arranged to introduce postoperative recovery situations, allowing patients to understand the related issues of LDVT from the perspective of fellow patients and enhance their confidence in recovery.

Postoperative standardized nursing methods. Positioning nursing: If no abnormal conditions are present, postoperative patients can be transferred back to general wards for recovery. The affected limb should be suspended to control the elevation angle between 15° and 30°, and the hip joint should be maintained in a functional position to facilitate the return of venous blood and lymphatic fluid, alleviating early postoperative lower limb swelling and pain. The patient temperature and blood pressure should be measured at regular intervals. If the patient temperature remains consistently >38°C or if a rapid decrease in blood pressure occurs, the attending physician should be immediately notified for appropriate action. Prevention of complications in nursing: Air cushions should be placed between the affected limb and the bed surface to reduce the occurrence of pressure ulcers. Patients should be instructed to rinse their mouths before each meal and perform effective coughing after waking up in the morning, after naptime, and before bedtime, to effectively prevent respiratory tract infections and aspirational pneumonia. Urine bags should be regularly emptied, and the urinary catheter placement should be monitored to avoid compression, bending, or other issues. The urethral orifice should be cleaned with disposable iodine swabs daily, and the urine bag should be positioned lower than the wound to use the low-position drainage method better. Rehabilitation training: After surgery, patients should be encouraged to start rehabilitation training on the affected limb as early as possible. Passive exercises should be initiated on the second day after surgery, including flexion, abduction, and other movements for all joints except the fractured joint. Rotational movements can be performed for healthy, smaller joints. Five days after surgery, patients are encouraged to engage in assisted bedside exercises, using crutches, chairs, and other tools as necessary, with moderate exercise intensity. If pain occurs in the affected limb, the movements should be stopped immediately and resumed after the pain subsides. The exercises mentioned above should continue until the blood clots disappear. A reexamination should be scheduled after 3 months. Finally, relevant questionnaires should be filled out on-site and returned immediately.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data analyses used SPSS 21.0 software. Categorical data were represented as a number or percentage (n/%) and were analyzed using the χ2 test. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The paired t test was used for within-group comparisons, and the independent t test was used for comparisons between groups. P < .05 indicates significant differences.

4. Discussion

The 3 primary factors contributing to DVT are injury to the vein wall, sluggish blood flow, and hypercoagulability of the blood.[16] Dai et al suggested that venous stasis caused by long-term bed rest, blood hypercoagulability after surgery, and damage to the vessel wall can all cause DVT.[17] The incidence of postoperative DVT was 57% to 62%, 0.5% to 44%, even with routine prevention.[18] Sharpe et al reported that the incidence of lower extremity DVT in patients with spinal fractures is 20.3%, however, with appropriate anticoagulant therapy and high-quality nursing measures, this can be reduced to 2.2%.[19] In this study, the incidence of DVT in the control group was 17.78%, which was <20.3%, and the incidence of DVT in the study group was 2.2%, close to the reported 2.2%. These findings suggest that the nursing model significantly affects the occurrence of LDVT, highlighting the importance of adopting rational and effective postoperative care measures.

First, our results indicated a lower incidence of lower extremity DVT, infection, delayed healing, pain, and swelling in the study group. This suggested that rehabilitative care effectively prevents lower extremity DVT and relieves clinical symptoms. Li et al also reported that comprehensive rehabilitative care significantly helps prevent the disease in fracture surgery patients.[20] The rehabilitation process emphasizes both passive and active movement of the lower extremities. First, position guidance may alleviate postoperative stress reactions and increase vein patency.[21] Second, massage and functional training help improve lower limb blood circulation, flow, and rheology, thereby reducing the risk of DVT.[22] Massaging the lower limbs can also make patients feel comfortable and alleviate symptoms such as pain and swelling.[23]

Subsequently, the hemorheology in this study revealed that plasma viscosity, whole blood low/high shear viscosity, and EA all decreased in both groups, especially in the study group. Therefore, rehabilitative care would significantly improve the postoperative hemorheology of lower limb fracture patients, reducing hypercoagulability, and ultimately preventing the occurrence of LDVT. Evidence suggests that early postoperative massage and dual lower limb functional exercises can reduce hypercoagulability and decrease the risk of DVT occurrence.[24,25]

The SF-36 scores of both groups improved after the intervention, with the improvement in the study group being more significant. This indicated that rehabilitation care could improve the postoperative quality of life of patients with fractures more than conventional care. This result seems similar to that reported by Amarilla-Donoso et al, where effective nursing intervention after fracture surgery promoted patient recovery and improved patient quality of life.[26] Finally, we found that patient satisfaction in the study group was higher than that in the control group, indicating higher patient satisfaction with rehabilitation care services.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a small sample size, and potential heterogeneity might have affected the results. Therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution. Second, because of limited data and no follow-up, the preventive effect of rehabilitation nursing on postdischarge DVT remained unclear.

5. Conclusions

Perioperative standardized nursing interventions can effectively improve the quality of care and reduce the postoperative occurrence of complications such as DVT in patients with lower limb fractures, improving their quality of life and patient satisfaction. Therefore, its clinical application and usefulness would be worthy of consideration.
