Comparison of volatile compounds in Grifola frondosa under different cooking methods using HS-SPME-GC–MS and HS-GC-IMS
Authors: Zitong Liu, Jian Zhao, Ziheng Cheng, Xinfang Zhang, Meifan Pan, Beilei Pang, Ruiqi Zhang, Piaopiao Wei, Xuemei Cheng, Yudong Wang, Yongye Sun, Lirong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Grifola frondosa is a popular mushroom, and its flavor is highly dependent on the cooking method. In this study, the volatile compounds of Grifola frondosa under different cooking methods were identified. HS-SPME-GC–MS identified 36 volatile compounds, mainly aldehydes and alcohols, e.g. nonanal, decanal, 1-octen-3-ol and 1-octanol. HS-GC-IMS indicated that volatile compounds in Grifola frondosa were optimally retained under microwaving cooking for 10 min. Among them, the content of typical mushroom flavor compounds 1-octen-3-ol and 2-butanone (D) reached the highest when microwaving cooking for 10 min. The content of isoamyl formate with plum odor was the highest in the stir-frying cooking sample for 10 min. The content of acetic acid hexyl ester and 2-pentylfuran with fruity flavor reached the highest in the sample with steaming cooking for 10 min. The flavor of boiling cooking samples was the weakest. This study can provide a theoretical foundation for enhancing the flavor quality of Grifola frondosa.

Introduction

Grifola frondosa belongs to Basidiomycetes fungi. It is one of the edible and medicinal mushrooms, and is praised for its unique flavor and health benefits. Grifola frondosa is famous for its unique appearance and rich nutritional value. In China, Grifola frondosa is known as gray tree flower (He et al., 2017). It is not only edible, but also considered as a healthy food because it contains protein, carbohydrates, dietary fiber and minerals. Grifola frondosa has low fat content and relatively low calories. It also has antioxidant, anti-tumor, immunomodulatory and anti-diabetic effects (Wu et al., 2021). These functions are closely related to their bioactive components. Grifola frondosa has good taste and unique flavor, which is known as the first grade edible fungus (Mizuno & Zhuang, 1995). It is not only a leader in edible fungi, but also a medicinal fungus, and it is widely used as a cooking ingredient and dietary supplement in Asia (Wu et al., 2016).

Cooking is a traditional thermal processing process of food raw materials. It can improve the sensory characteristics of food. Mushrooms are usually eaten after cooking, rather than raw, which will change the sensory characteristics of edible fungi including color, flavor, texture, and taste (Soler-Rivas et al., 2009). Appropriate cooking methods and time will also make it more delicious. Cooking methods significantly impact the nutritional properties of mushrooms. Studies have found that boiling, microwaving, grilling, and frying affected the basic components of cultivated mushrooms (Irene, Mónica, Margarita & Cristina, 2017). Sun et al. (2019) discovered that four cooking methods (autoclaving, microwaving, sous-vide cooking, and stewing) can increase the polyphenols and flavor compounds in mushroom soup. Marta et al., (2021) showed that most cooking methods reduced the brightness, hardness, adhesiveness, and chewiness of wild delicious boletus edulis, with marinated mushrooms having the lowest flavor value. Previous studies have found that the nutritional components of edible fungi are affected by cooking methods (Luo et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023). All these experiments showed that different cooking methods can change the nutritional components and flavor of mushrooms. The flavor of mushrooms significantly influence the willingness of people to eat them. Changes in the flavor of mushrooms during cooking, such as increased meat flavor, can be attributed to changes in free amino acids and volatiles during cooking (Kido & Tanaka, 2023). In addition to their high nutritional value, mushrooms also possess unique flavors, with volatile compounds being significant factors influencing their flavor (Liang et al., 2022). Xie et al. (2024) found that 1-octen-3-one was the primary component in button mushrooms. Zhu et al., (2022) pointed out that C8 volatile compounds are important flavor substances in edible fungi, among which 1-octen-3-ol has the highest proportion.

According to the current data, there were few studies on the cooking methods of Grifola frondosa compared with other mushrooms. In view of the potential edible value and medicinal value of Grifola frondosa, as well as its unique flavor, it is necessary to further study the changes of sensory traits and flavor components of Grifola frondosa under different cooking methods. Four cooking methods of stir-frying, steaming, boiling and microwaving were used to process Grifola frondosa. The cooking time is set to 5 min, 10 min, 15 min and 20 min. After cooking, the sensory evaluation of color, texture, flavor and taste in Grifola frondosa were evaluated. The volatile compound changes in Grifola frondosa were analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) and headspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) technology. HS-SPME-GC–MS technology enables the qualitative detection of flavor compounds while combining the safety of HS-SPME with the high accuracy and separation capability of GC–MS, making it the most commonly used flavor analysis technique in edible fungi (Zhong et al., 2024). HS-GC-IMS is a novel gas separation technology that allows for visual analysis of volatile compounds. Compared to HS-SPME-GC–MS, HS-GC-IMS lacks a comprehensive database but offers advantages such as high analysis efficiency and low cost (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, the combined use of HS-SPME-GC–MS and HS-GC-IMS for detecting volatile compounds enables a more comprehensive acquisition of sample information. This study aims to identify rational cooking methods for Grifola frondosa to optimize its flavor, thereby providing references for its cooking practices.

Materials and methods

Materials

Grifola frondosa was harvested from Xi'an Baisite Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in 2024, and stored at 25 ± 2 °C. According to AOAC official method and slightly modified (Association of Official Analytical Chemists, 1990). The specific method is as follows: the Grifola frondosa was placed in the oven and dried at 102 °C for 4 h until a constant weight was achieved. The crude protein content was determined by Kjeldahl method accoding to AOAC official method 978.04. The crude fat content was determined by Soxhlet extraction method accoding to AOAC official method 966.15. The ash content was determined by roasting at 600 °C for 4 h accoding to AOAC official method 942.05. The carbohydrate content was calculated by the difference method, and the calculation formula was: carbohydrate content (%) = 100%-crude protein content (%)-crude fat content (%)-crude ash content (%). The main chemical components of the raw materials are: carbohydrate 70.4%, crude protein 18.6%, crude fat 4.5%, crude ash 6.5%. The final results of all components are expressed on a dry weight basis.

Cooking methods

Grifola frondosa was soaked in warm water at 50 °C for 30 min. After being completely softened, The Grifola frondosa samples were broken into small pieces and the excess water was drained. The treated Grifola frondosa samples were divided into five groups: uncooked, microwaving, stir-frying, steaming and boiling. The weight of each group of Grifola frondosa samples is 90 g. For steaming cooking (SC), water was added to a stainless steel steaming pot (samples:water = 1:10, w/v). After the water was boiled over high heat, the heat was reduced to the lowest level (approximately 120 W) and the samples were steamed for different durations (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, 20 min and 25 min). For microwaving cooking (MC), the prepared Grifola frondosa were placed in a microwave-safe bowl with ventilation holes, and then the bowl was transferred into the microwave oven. The power of 70 W was selected because it was the lowest stable power level that could achieve mild and uniform heating, and then the samples were treated with microwave for different durations (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, 20 min, 25 min). For boiling cooking (BC), water was added to a stainless steel pot (samples:water = 1:10, w/v). After the water was boiled over high heat, the Grifola frondosa was placed in the pot and boiled for different durations (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, 20 min and 25 min). In the stir-frying cooking (SFC), an induction cooker and a standard round-bottom iron wok were used for stir-frying. 10 g of soybean oil was placed in the iron wok (samples:oil = 9:1, w/w), followed by the addition of prepared Grifola frondosa, which was continuously stirred with a spatula. Grifola frondosa was stir-fried at different time periods (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, 20 min and 25 min). Throughout the process, the induction cooker was set to a constant minimum range (approximately 120 W) to maintain a stable frying temperature based on previous studies (Xu et al., 2026). After each cooking method, the samples of Grifola frondosa were evaluated by sensory evaluation. Then, the samples of Grifola frondosa cooked in different time periods of each cooking method were preserved.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation method followed the approach of previous studies (Oh et al., 2024), and was adjusted according to the actual needs and resources of this study. In short, a sensory evaluation group was formed with 20 trained members (10 women and 10 men, aged 20 to 30 years). The group members underwent a two-week training period. Initially, the group members evaluated the samples of Grifola frondosa treated by four cooking methods, and described them from four aspects: color, texture, flavor and taste. In order to reduce the fatigue of group members and focus on the sensory indicators that best distinguish the differences between samples, we selected these four most representative attributes for evaluation after group discussion. Consensus was reached through public discussion, and corresponding terms and definitions were established. Color: intensity of yellow color. Texture: The degree of smooth and wet mushroom surface. Aroma: Odors associated with cooked mushroom. Taste: The taste of chewing mushroom. Subsequently, by continuously observing, tasting, and discussing, the group members refined the set of descriptive attributes and reached agreement. The intensity of the four descriptions was rated on a 0 to 9 scale (with 0 indicating weak and 9 indicating strong). The samples were placed on plates and marked before being given to the panelists for evaluation at room temperature (25 °C). After each tasting, the group leader provided drinking water for each member to rinse out any residual mushrooms. The sensory evaluation study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao University. Participants signed informed consent forms after fully understanding the purpose of the study and their rights.

Determination of volatile compounds

HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis

The volatile compounds in Grifola frondosa were extracted by headspace solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME). The SPME method is modified slightly by Xu et al. (2025). The Grifola frondosa samples were placed in a 20 mL vial, and 2 ± 0.02 g was added to each vial. Then the 10 μg pre-configured standard solution (1,2-dichlorobenzene, 51.55 μg/mL) was added to each samples. The samples was extracted using a 50 μm/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Supelco Inc., Bellefonte, PA, USA). The samples bottle was heated at 60 °C for 30 min in a water bath on a heated magnetic stirrer. After extraction at 60 °C for 30 min, the fiber was immediately desorbed into the GC–MS inlet and maintained at 250 °C for 5 min. According to the scheme described by Xu, Wang, Tian, Liu, et al. (2023). Clarus690SQ8T (PerkinElmer, USA) was used to analyze volatile compounds on GC–MS equipment. The GC conditions were as follows: DB-5MS chromatographic column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), the temperature program was as follows: the initial column box was kept at 40 °C for 3 min, then increased to 100 °C at a rate of 6 °C/min, and then increased to 230 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min for 3 min. Helium was used as carrier gas (1.0 mL/min).

The mass spectrometry (MS) conditions were EL ion source 230 °C, MS grade rod 150 °C, scanning mass range of 10-550u. The volatile compounds were determined by comparing the retention index (RI) and MS data with the data of the real standard. The quantification of each compound was calculated by internal standard method.

HS-GC-IMS analysis

In terms of headspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS), an Agilent 490 gas chromatograph (provided by Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and an ion mobility spectrometer (FlavourSpec®, produced by Gesellschaft für Analytische Sensorsysteme mbH, Dortmund, Germany) were used to analyze the volatile components of the samples. The detection conditions were referred to our previous study (Xu, Wang, Tian, Liu, et al., 2023; Xu, Wang, Tian, Wang, et al., 2023). Grifola frondosa samples 2 ± 0.02 g were placed in a 20 mL volumetric flask. The samples bottle was placed in an incubator at 50 °C for 20 min. The samples bottle was placed in an incubator at 50 °C for 20 min, as this temperature and duration were sufficient to reach headspace equilibrium, ensuring adequate release and stabilization of volatile compounds in the gas phase for subsequent HS-GC-IMS analysis (Xu et al., 2025). Then 500 μL of the volatile samples was injected into a 50 °C samplesr, and the mode was not split. MXT-WAX column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 1 μm) was used for detection. The flow of nitrogen was programmed as follows: starting at 2 mL/min (holding for 5 min), increasing to 5 mL/min (holding for 5 min), then to 10 mL/min (holding for 5 min), followed by 20 mL/min (holding for 5 min), 50 mL/min (holding for 5 min), and finally reaching 100 mL/min (holding for 10 min).

The qualitative analysis of the volatile compounds was carried out according to several criteria: the retention index of the volatile compounds, the mass spectra of the compounds in the NIST library, and the drift time of the standards in the GC-IMS library.

Data analysis

The experiment was designed with biological replicates to account for natural variation in the raw material. Three replicates were used in all experiments, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and two-way analysis of variance were used for data analysis. SPSS statistical software (version 26.0) was used for data analysis. P-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Sensory evaluation of Grifola frondosa

As shown in Table 1, the sensory evaluation of Grifola frondosa was conducted based on four aspects: color, texture, aroma, and taste. The results showed that at the cooking duration of 10 min, the scores for these four aspects were significantly higher than those at other time points (P < 0.05), suggesting that this duration might represent the optimal cooking time. In terms of aroma, the scores of steamed and boiled samples after 10 min cooking were significantly lower than those of microwaved and stir-fried samples (P < 0.05). Regarding texture, the score of microwaving sample after 10 min cooking was significantly higher than those of the other cooking methods (P < 0.05). This indicated that different cooking methods had a significant impact on the sensory characteristics of Grifola frondosa, which was consistent with previous studies reporting substantial effects of cooking methods on the sensory attributes of mushrooms (Jiang et al., 2023). HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis of volatile compounds in Grifola frondosa under different cooking methods

A total of 9 aldehyde compounds, 5 alcohol compounds, 5 ester compounds, 3 amide compounds, 5 alkene compounds, 4 ketone compounds and 5 other compounds were identified by HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis (Table 2). The results indicate that aldehydes and alcohols are the main contributors to the flavor of Grifola frondosa. A variety of aldehyde compounds were detected, which exhibit relatively low odor thresholds and strong aroma activity. In contrast, although alcohols have relatively higher thresholds, they were found in significantly higher total concentrations, thereby compensating for their lower inherent potency. Overall, they dominate the volatile profile and are responsible for the characteristic flavor of Grifola frondosa. It was similar to the results of Splivallo et al. (2007), who observed aldehydes and alcohols as the main volatile flavor contributors in Lentinula edodes. In the aldehyde compounds, hexanal and heptanal can provide a grassy odor (Zorrilla & Evidente, 2022), while decanal and nonanal provide peel aroma. These aldehydes are common volatiles in mushrooms. As shown in Table 2, in the uncooked sample, the contents of hexanal and heptanal were the highest, at 39.46 μg/kg and 28.46 μg/kg respectively, significantly higher than those in samples obtained from various cooking methods (P < 0.05). In SC-10 min, the contents of hexanal and nonanal reached the highest values, at 31.24 μg/kg and 86.23 μg/kg respectively, and then significantly decreased with the extension of cooking time (P < 0.05). Additionally, in SC-10 min, the contents of decanal and benzeneacetaldehyde also increased significantly, to 15.44 μg/kg and 36.39 μg/kg respectively, and then the contents of decanal and nonanal decreased significantly (P < 0.05).

Alcohols mainly originated from amino acid metabolism and the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, exhibiting higher odor thresholds (Hu et al., 2024). Among the detected alcohols, 1-octen-3-ol, responsible for a strong mushroom and grassy odor, was a major contributor to mushroom aroma (Hiraide et al., 2004). In uncooked sample, the content of 1-octen-3-ol was the highest among the detected alcohols, at 1343.8 μg/kg, indicating it was the predominant alcohol in Grifola frondosa. However, after the four cooking treatments, the concentration of 1-octen-3-ol dropped significantly (P < 0.05). This finding was consistent with the research of Huang et al. (2022). For the same cooking duration, the content of 1-octen-3-ol after microwaving cooking was significantly higher than other three cooking methods (P < 0.05), suggesting microwaving cooking better preserves this compound. 1-octanol and phenethyl alcohol provide rose aroma. Their concentrations peaked in SC-10 min, at 191 μg/kg and 18.36 μg/kg respectively.

The flavor of mushrooms was also affected by alkanes, but their contribution is relatively weak (Li et al., 2019). In esters, ethyl acetate was undetectable in uncooked samples but emerged after cooking, reaching a peak concentration of 22.18 μg/kg in MC-5 min. This may be due to the oxidation reaction of aldehydes (Lu et al., 2021). In amides, Acetamide, 2-fluoro- was detected in uncooked samples at 13.84 μg/kg but was undetectable in the four cooking samples.

In summary, the results of HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis showed that the concentrations of various key volatile compounds (such as hexanal, nonanal, 1-octen-3-ol) reached or approached the peak under the corresponding cooking methods at the 10-min cooking time point, which provided a direct chemical basis for the conclusion that the sensory quality of 10-min cooking samples was better in sensory evaluation.

HS-GC-IMS analysis of volatile compounds in Grifola frondosa under different cooking methods

Based on the determination that Grifola frondosa exhibit optimal flavor performance after 10 min cooking through sensory evaluation and HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis, we selected uncooked samples and four other samples cooked for 10 min for HS-GC-IMS analysis. This method aimed to gain a deeper understanding of the changes in volatile compounds in Grifola frondosa under various cooking conditions. The data of volatile compounds under different cooking methods were first visualized in the form of 3D topographic maps (Fig. 1a). Among them, A: uncooked sample; B: microwaving sample; C: stir-frying sample; D: steaming sample; E: boiling sample. Fig. 1b showed a 2D top view and Fig. 1c a difference spectrum. In the 2D map, the background was blue, white indicated low substance concentration, and red indicates high concentration, with darker red representing higher concentration. In the different spectrum, the spectrum of uncooked sample A1 was used as a reference, and the spectra of Grifola frondosa sample processed by different cooking methods were subtracted from this reference. If two flavor compounds were the same before and after cooking, the point after subtraction is white. Red areas indicated increased substance concentration relative to the uncooked samples, and blue areas indicated decreased concentration. The results showed that the total amount and content of flavor compounds decreased after the four cooking methods. As shown in Fig. 1b, most signals fall within a retention time range of 100–600 s, and in the top view, the migration time between 1.0 and 1.8 showed significant variation. According to Fig. 1c, compared with uncooked sample, many blue spots were found in the range of 1.0–1.8 after four cooking methods, indicating that several main substances in Grifola frondosa were very sensitive to temperature and were easy to degrade or decompose, resulting in some signals disappeared or decreased after cooking. Based on the comparative analysis of HS-GC-IMS and HS-SPME-GC–MS results, the single method detected 36 and 27 volatile compounds, respectively, with a total of 57 volatile compounds identified by both methods combined. The compounds commonly detected by both techniques were benzaldehyde, benzeneacetaldehyde, heptanal, nonanal, 1-octen-3-ol, and acetic acid hexyl ester, suggesting that these substances may be present at relatively high concentrations in Grifola frondosa. Additionally, HS-GC-IMS detected three ether compounds, which were not identified by HS-SPME-GC–MS; however, HS-SPME-GC–MS detected three amides, five alkenes, and five alcohols, whereas HS-GC-IMS only detected two alcohols and failed to detect amides and alkenes. These findings indicate that HS-SPME-GC–MS demonstrates superior performance in detecting alcohols, alkenes, and nitrogen-containing compounds, which is consistent with the conclusions reported by Zhong et al. (2024). Compared with HS-SPME-GC–MS, HS-GC-IMS is capable of detecting both monomers and dimers of compounds. Therefore, the combined application of HS-GC-IMS and HS-SPME-GC–MS is highly necessary.

In HS-GC-IMS analysis, some volatile compounds were detected in the form of monomer (M) and dimer (D). The signal intensity ratio can indirectly reflect the concentration level of flavor substances and the strength of intermolecular interactions. As shown in Table 3, for heptanal and benzaldehyde, the intensity of M peak is generally higher than that of D peak, indicating that it mainly exists in the form of highly volatile free monomer in the headspace. This distribution characteristic means that the flavor substance tends to be released instantaneously and has strong olfactory explosive power. However, due to the relatively small dimer reserves, its aroma persistence is weak and easy to decay rapidly. In contrast, for 2-butanone and compounds, the intensity of D peak after cooking treatment was significantly greater than that of M peak. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that the cooking process promotes the release or formation of the compound, resulting in a significant increase in its concentration in the headspace, which makes it easier to form dimers through hydrogen bond association. According to the detection principle of ion mobility spectroscopy, larger dimer ions produce stronger signal response (Kanu & Hill Jr, 2008). Therefore, the strong D peak not only indicates a high concentration, but also means a dynamic flavor reservoir. This distribution pattern partially explains the more persistent and subtle characteristics of flavor in cooking samples.

Aldehydes plays a crucial role in mushrooms (Jiang et al., 2023) and have intense, overlapping flavor effects (Hou et al., 2021). According to the data listed in Table 3, compared with the uncooked sample, six aldehydes showed no significant difference in the MC-10 min sample, indicating that these six aldehydes were well retained after 10 min of microwave cooking. In SC-10 min, SFC-10 min and BC-10 min sample, except for n-Nonanal, the other aldehydes were significantly reduced (P < 0.05). Aldehydes are often produced through Strecker degradation and fatty acid oxidation (Lv et al., 2024). Microwaving cooking can better retain the six aldehydes, probably because microwave cooking rapidly heats up the interior of the sample and shortens the heat treatment time, thereby reducing the loss of aldehydes. In steaming and boiling cooking, water-soluble aldehydes may be lost with steam or water, resulting in a decrease in aldehydes. In stir-frying cooking, high temperature and oxygen promote the rapid oxidation of fatty acids, resulting in the decomposition or conversion of original aldehydes into other compounds. At the same time, the newly generated aldehydes may mask the original aldehydes, but the data show that most aldehydes are significantly reduced, indicating that the loss of aldehydes is greater than the generation.

Esters, common in fruits and known for their fruity aromas (Wang et al., 2021), were detected in multiple varieties in the Grifola frondosa samples. Their formation is closely related to lipid oxidation and alcohol esterification reactions (Zhao et al., 2024). According to the results in Table 3, the MC-10 min, SFC-10 min, and SC-10 min samples each retained three types of esters relatively well, whereas only one type of ester was well retained in the BC-10 min sample. In the MC-10 min sample, ethyl butyrate, which imparts a pineapple flavor, exhibited the highest peak area of 5149.11. The retention of esters in microwaving cooking may be attributed to the relatively low power, which limits steam flow, combined with good sealing. Additionally, during stir-frying cooking, the involvement of oils in lipid oxidation and alcohol esterification reactions promotes the formation of esters. In steaming cooking, the retention of esters may be because the detected esters are insoluble in water, thus avoiding a large loss of esters. Therefore, esters are also well retained in these two cooking methods. For example, the content of isoamyl formate in the SFC-10 min and SC-10 min samples was significantly higher than in other samples (P < 0.05), with values of 1886.09 and 1880.88, respectively. Regarding acetic acid hexyl ester, its content in the MC-10 min, SFC-10 min, and SC-10 min samples was significantly higher than BC-10 min sample, with the BC-10 min showing the lowest value of 270.56. This also reflects that microwaving, stir-frying and steaming cooking can better reduce the loss of the substance.

Ketones, which can form via amino acid thermolysis or polyunsaturated fatty acid oxidation (Du, Chen, et al., 2021), were only detected in three types in the Grifola frondosa samples. In MC-10 min, the concentration of 2,3-pentanedione rose to 32.47. As a product of the Maillard reaction, 2,3-pentanedione contributes caramel and nutty flavors. The content of 3-methyl-2-pentanone was 1406.87 in the uncooked samples and 1071.36 in MC-10 min, both significantly higher than in the other three cooking samples (P < 0.05). The lowest concentration of the substance in the BC-10 sample was 224.71, which further confirmed that boiling would lead to the loss of volatile substances. 3-methyl-2-pentanone in the sample is primarily a product of thermal oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, contributing fresh fruity and grassy aromas (Zhu et al., 2024).

Among the four cooking methods, the content of 1-octen-3-ol in MC-10 min was the highest, which was 8573.05, and was significantly higher than that of stir-frying, steaming and cooking samples (P < 0.05). It was due to the presence of unsaturated double bonds in 1-octen-3-ol, which maked it chemically unstable and prone to decomposition during cooking (Peng et al., 2022). This result was similar to those of Du, Sissons, et al. (2021) and Ashmore et al. (2014). Probably because microwaving cooking promoted the release of 1-octen-3-ol, and microwaving cooking was carried out under relatively sealed conditions, reducing the volatilization loss.

2-Pentylfuran is a furan compound with a nutty or grassy flavor (Liu et al., 2014), which is commonly found in heated foods and can be generated through Maillard reaction and lipid oxidation (Ouyang et al., 2024). In SC-10 min, the concentration of 2-pentylfuran in Grifola frondosa samples was 2046.92, significantly higher than in the other cooking samples (P < 0.05). It indicated that the hot and humid environment may be conducive to lipid oxidation to produce 2-pentylfuran. 1,4-Cineol, a monoterpene with a mild camphor odor and a cool, spicy note, showed the highest concentration in the boiling sample after 10 min cooking, also significantly higher than in the other samples (P < 0.05).

Additionally, this study indicated that boiling cooking can lead to a reduction in the volatile compounds of fresh Grifola frondosa. This is similar to previous studies (Tian et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2026). In BC-10 min, apart from 1,4-cineole and isovaleric acid methyl ester, the remaining volatile compounds showed a significant decrease. This might be due to the degradation of flavor components during the heating process, which also explained why the boiling sample had the weakest flavor performance. In contrast, the volatile compounds were better preserved in the uncooked sample and the sample subjected to MC-10 min. It suggested that MC-10 min could effectively retain the original flavor profile of Grifola frondosa. For steaming cooking and stir-frying cooking, although the volatile compounds were weaker than those of microwaving cooking sample, they could also contribute special flavors to Grifola frondosa. For example, acetic acid hexyl ester and isoamyl formate were the highest in the SC-10 min and SFC-10 min, respectively, which could contribute a light fruit flavor to Grifola frondosa.

PCA analysis of different cooking methods

Typically, a PCA model is chosen for reporting when the contribution rate reaches 60% (Chen et al., 2020). In the PCA plot (Fig. 3B), the PCA results show that PC1 and PC2 account for 84% (65% and 19%) of the total variance, indicating that HS-GC-IMS can explain most volatile compound information. Overall, the uncooked and microwaving samples had similar principal components, as do the stir-frying and steaming samples. In the nearest neighbor fingerprint analysis, the intensity of compounds in a specified region was used to rapidly compare samples and compute the Euclidean distance between each pair of samples (Zhang et al., 2022). This method enabled the analysis of volatile chemical similarity. Fig. 3b presented the nearest neighbor Euclidean distance plot between the control and the four cooking method samples. A shorter distance signifies higher similarity, whereas a longer distance implies greater variability. As shown in the figure, the boiling sample was the farthest from the uncooked sample, followed by the stir-frying and steaming samples, indicating significant variability in volatile compounds. In contrast, the microwaving sample was the closest to the uncooked samples, suggesting less variability in volatile compounds. This finding was consistent with the PCA results. Overall, the results demonstrated that the volatile compounds of Grifola frondosa samples subjected to different cooking methods underwent significant changes.

Conclusion

This study systematically compared the effects of four cooking methods on the volatile flavor compounds of Grifola frondosa, integrating sensory evaluation with HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis to identify 10 min as the optimal cooking duration. HS-GC-IMS further revealed that aldehydes and esters serve as the primary contributors to its flavor profile. Microwave cooking (MC-10 min) best preserved the original flavor compounds while significantly enhancing the content of typical mushroom-flavor substances, such as 1-octen-3-ol. Additionally, stir-frying and steaming exhibited distinct flavor characteristics: stir-frying enhanced plum-like aroma (associated with isoamyl formate), whereas steaming promoted fruity notes (e.g., acetic acid hexyl ester and 2-pentylfuran). Boiled samples displayed the weakest flavor intensity. These findings offer a scientific approach to optimizing the cooking and sensory quality of Grifola frondosa. In the future, we can further explore the synergistic improvement of the overall flavor acceptance of Grifola frondosa by compound cooking or seasoning. Additionally, the study primarily assessed volatile compounds and sensory attributes, without evaluating potential changes in nutritional components or bioactive properties during cooking, which could also influence the overall quality and health value of Grifola frondosa.
