Quality of life and surgical outcomes of endoscopic thyroidectomy versus open thyroidectomy in papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Miaofeng Wang, Guoping Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endoscopic thyroidectomy (ET) avoids larger neck incisions. It offers superior cosmetic outcomes and improves patient satisfaction. However, the effect of ET on quality of life (QoL) remains incompletely investigated. This study was therefore designed to compare QoL and key surgical outcomes between ET and conventional open thyroidectomy (OT) in patients diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma. This retrospective analysis enrolled 667 patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma who underwent thyroidectomy. To mitigate potential confounding factors, propensity score matching was applied, resulting in a final cohort of 44 ET patients and 66 OT patients for comparative analysis. The SF-36 and thyroid surgery-specific questionnaires were used to assess QoL, overall satisfaction, surgical and cosmetic outcomes at 15, 30, and 60 days postoperatively. Patients in the ET group generally exhibited higher SF-36 QoL scores than those in the OT group across several parameters. Thyroid surgery-specific questionnaires indicated lower swallowing discomfort in the ET group (P < .05). Significant differences were observed in operating time (P < .001), cosmetic outcomes (P < .001), hospital costs (P < .001), and overall satisfaction (P < .001). The ET group included a higher proportion of higher educated individuals (P < .05). No significant intergroup differences were observed in terms of postoperative drainage, visual analogue scale pain scores, length of hospital stay, or complication rates. ET offers comparable safety to OT, along with superior QoL and cosmetic outcomes, making it a viable alternative for both patients and surgeons.

1. Introduction

Thyroid nodules, defined as abnormal growths within the thyroid gland, exhibit considerable diversity in their size and composition. Their prevalence, predominantly identified via ultrasonography, ranges from 19% to 68% among different populations.[1] While most nodules are benign, approximately 5% to 10% are malignant.[2,3] Timely detection through regular ultrasound examinations is crucial for identifying potentially cancerous nodules and initiating prompt treatment. Various treatment modalities exist for thyroid nodules, with conventional open thyroidectomy being one of the primary options. While this procedure has proven safety and efficacy, it inevitably leads to the formation of a visible scar on the neck,[4,5] reducing cosmetic effects, postoperative satisfaction and QoL.[6] Among younger patient cohorts, especially those who are conscious about their appearance, the presence of an anterior-cervical neck scar may engender considerable apprehension. Such an outcome has the potential to impinge upon their self-regard and body image, thereby prompting a reluctance towards undergoing surgical intervention.

Endoscopic thyroidectomy (ET), relocating the incision site to less noticeable areas like the underarm or chest, is preferred by both surgeons and patients owing to its superior cosmetic outcomes and minimized visible scarring. Such an approach augments postoperative satisfaction and QoL, particularly amongst younger patients who harbor apprehensions regarding their physical appearance. Furthermore, extant research posits comparable safety and efficacy profiles between endoscopic and conventional open thyroidectomy approaches.

However, our understanding of patients’ QoL after ET is limited by short follow-up duration. Hence, through a comparative analysis of outcomes between endoscopic and conventional open thyroidectomy, we endeavored to ascertain whether ET confers enhanced postoperative convalescence and quality of life.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patients and study design

The study encompassed 667 patients diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) who underwent lobectomy and prophylactic central lymph node dissection at the Department of Thyroid and Breast Surgery, Shaoxing Central Hospital, between January 2015 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: tumor diameter <2 cm, no extrathyroidal invasion, and clinically negative lymph nodes. The exclusion criteria included a history of previous neck or breast surgery, prior thyroid radiofrequency ablation (RFA), and a history of radiation to the thorax or neck.

In addition, we utilized propensity score matching (PSM) to reduce selection bias, enhance comparability, and control for confounding factors. Figure 1 presents a flowchart of patient selection. After PSM analysis, 44 patients in the ET group and 66 patients in the OT group were matched and compared. Clinical data, including age, TSH levels, educational background, visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores, hospital stays, mean operation time, postoperative drainage, postoperative complications, and hospital costs, were collected. The study was designed as a retrospective cohort study, with patients categorized based on the surgical approach. All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 3 time points: 15 days, 30 days, and 60 days post-surgery.

2.2. Surgical procedure and QOL assessment

The surgical procedures of ET were described in detail in previous study.[7] SF-36 and thyroid surgery-specific questionnaires were used to assess QoL, surgical outcomes, cosmetic satisfaction. A scale from 0 to 10 was used to grade overall satisfaction and VAS pain scores. Neck movement, shoulder movement, voice, and swallowing were evaluated based on patients’ subjective assessments. The difficulty in performing these activities was graded as follows – no difficulty: 0, almost never (occasionally): 1, sometimes: 2, almost always: 3, always: 4. The SF-36 questionnaire assessed general QoL across 8 parameters: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. Higher scores indicated better outcomes for each patient. Overall satisfaction levels and cosmetic outcomes after surgery were also measured on a scale from 0 to 10.

The Institutional Review Committee of Shaoxing Central Hospital approved this study and waived the requirement for informed patient consent due to its retrospective nature. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

3. Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk) was used to analyze the data in this study. Continuous variables were calculated using the mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD) method. The chi-square test was used to assess differences between 2 groups in terms of age, mean operation time, VAS pain score, postoperative drainage, total hospital length of stay, postoperative complications, TSH levels, cosmetic outcomes, and overall satisfaction. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < .05. 4. Results

A total of 110 patients were enrolled in the study. All of the patients were female. As anticipated, mean operating time was longer in the ET group, which also had a greater percentage of higher education, as shown in Table 2. Furthermore, the hospitalization costs were significantly higher in the ET group. None of the 110 patients experienced permanent hypocalcemia or recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. Transient hypoparathyroidism was observed in 3 patients. The 2 groups did not show any statistically significant differences in terms of postoperative drainage, VAS pain score, hospital stays, postoperative complications, surgical outcomes, or the number of dissected lymph nodes.

5. Discussion

In recent decades, ET has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to open thyroidectomy (OT) for the management of thyroid tumors. Compared to OT, this method is favored for its reduced trauma, improved cosmetic outcomes, and enhanced quality of life (QoL). In clinical practice, the most commonly employed technique is ET via the areola approach (ETAA), which involves making small periareolar incisions to access and remove the thyroid tumor. This technique effectively eliminates visible scarring on the neck. Numerous studies have demonstrated that ETAA achieves outcomes comparable to those of OT in terms of resection extent and surgical safety for PTC.[8] However, due to the favorable prognosis of PTC,[9,10] limited research has focused on postsurgical QoL. Consequently, our current understanding of QoL in patients who undergo thyroid surgery using ET remains limited. Many studies have focused on immediate postoperative outcomes, such as surgical complications and recovery time, rather than on patients’ overall well-being. To address this gap, data were collected from a diverse group of patients who underwent either ET or OT for PTC. The SF-36 and thyroid surgery-specific questionnaires were administered at specific intervals during the postoperative period to assess various aspects of health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The findings of this study will offer valuable insights into which surgical approach may be more advantageous for improving QoL in patients with PTC. This information can assist surgeons in making informed decisions about treatment options for these individuals.

The study outcomes revealed no significant differences in tumor size, blood loss, postoperative drainage, VAS pain score, hospital stays, or the number of harvested lymph nodes. However, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the ET group had higher education levels, likely due to the increased concern among highly educated patients regarding neck scarring. The reported complication rate of thyroidectomy ranges from 2.5% to 8.1%.[11] Our study found no statistically significant differences in the rates of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypoparathyroidism, infection, or reoperation. Although ET can effectively treat various thyroid tumors, it is important to acknowledge the potential associated risks. Complications related to ET may include skin damage, hypercapnia, and the possibility of converting to an open procedure. The recent introduction of devices such as bipolar electrocoagulation forceps and intraoperative neuromonitoring has significantly enhanced the safety of ET.[12,13] Overall, the incidence of complications was comparable between the 2 groups, with no statistically significant differences observed. However, this approach is associated with certain drawbacks, including prolonged operation time and increased hospital costs. However, considering the benefits of ET, these drawbacks are generally deemed acceptable by most patients. The presence of a learning curve in ET is widely acknowledged.[7] With the accumulation of extensive experience and the refinement of surgical techniques, certain complications associated with ET are decreasing, along with a reduction in operation time.[14]

The SF-36, a widely used questionnaire for assessing QoL, has been extensively utilized in prior research to evaluate the overall well-being of individuals with thyroid cancer.[15] It provides valuable insights into various aspects of QoL, including physical functioning, mental health, and social interactions. Previous studies have reported a significant decline in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) following thyroidectomy compared to individuals who have not undergone the operation.[16,17] Surprisingly, at the final follow-up in the ET group, we observed that this procedure did not appear to negatively impact social life, mood, or work. In fact, some patients even forgot about the surgery experience. This is largely attributed to the relatively minimal trauma, barely visible scars, quicker recovery, and improved QoL associated with ET.

In this study, the QoLwas assessed at 3 time points: 15 days, 30 days, and 60 days post-operation. The results revealed significant differences between the 2 groups across various parameters evaluated by the SF-36 questionnaire. Specifically, at 15 days post-surgery, we observed notable disparities in general health, role-emotional, mental health, and social functioning. Another study indicates that the impact on QoL in patients with PTC is primarily related to the emotional and social effects of treatment,[18] which aligns with our findings and further substantiates our results. At 30 days after surgery, only social function exhibited a statistically significant difference between the groups. At 60 days post-surgery, there was no significant difference in SF-36 score between 2 groups. These findings suggest that the ET group may experience superior outcomes in the early postoperative period.

However, it is important to note that SF-36 may not fully capture the specific symptoms experienced by patients with PTC after surgery. For instance, scar contractures in the neck can restrict neck mobility and impede mastication, phonation, and breathing. Thyroid surgery-specific symptoms can vary significantly. To systematically assess the impact of surgical interventions on patients’ lives after thyroidectomy, we utilized thyroid surgery-specific questionnaire to document the severity and frequency of symptoms. This questionnaire mainly includes sensation of numbness, hoarseness, swallowing discomfort, shoulder movement impairment, reduction in physical activity, psychosocial impairment, cosmetic outcome, and overall satisfaction.

Although previous literature indicates that neck scars may adversely affect voice and swallowing function,[19] our study did not identify a significant difference in voice impairment between the groups at the 15 days post-operation. However, patients in the ET group experienced a significantly lower prevalence of swallowing discomfort, which can greatly improve overall QoL. Additionally, the favorable cosmetic outcomes in the ET group can profoundly influence patients’ social interactions and psychological well-being. This is particularly important as a noticeable neck scar can adversely affect QoL after surgery. An unsightly scar in a visible location may diminish self-esteem and confidence, making patients feel self-conscious about their appearance and potentially leading to social anxiety.[20] This emotional burden can impede patients’ full recovery and progress in life. By achieving superior cosmetic outcomes through ET, surgeons can alleviate these concerns. Enhanced cosmetic results enable patients to regain confidence in their appearance and participate in daily activities without anxiety about their scars. Therefore, it is crucial for surgeons to consider not only the thoroughness and functionality of the surgery but also the cosmetic outcomes, particularly in procedures involving prominent areas such as the neck.

In conclusion, ET offers significant advantages over open thyroidectomy, as corroborated by the findings of our study. Similar conclusions were drawn in our previous research.[21,22] Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, it was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size. Second, the 60-day follow-up duration might be too brief to capture long-term outcomes. Finally, the inclusion of only female patients precludes the generalization of the findings to the male population. Thus, further investigations involving multiple centers and prospective studies with longer follow-up periods are necessary to validate the safety and efficacy of ET for patients with PTC.
