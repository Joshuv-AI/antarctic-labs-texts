Investigating autistic hyperfocus and monotropism: Limited convergence of event-related potentials, laboratory tasks, and questionnaire responses
Authors: Patrick Dwyer, Andre Sillas, Clifford D. Saron, Susan M. Rivera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The autistic-developed monotropism account suggests autism is characterised by hyperfocus towards interests, although hyperfocus research has not explored associations among self-/caregiver-report and lab-based measures. Other findings suggest autistic attention has an enhanced capacity and/or is unusually prone to involuntary capture. This study used questionnaires and lab-based tasks to investigate autistic attention and probe its relations to inattention/distractibility, sensory experiences, and anxiety.Methods:18 autistic and 22 comparison adolescents completed self-report measures of hyper-focus, anxiety, and sensory experiences, and laboratory psychoacoustic tasks, visual working memory and cross-modal attention capture accuracy and reaction time tasks, and a hyperfocus paradigm relying on the N2pc and Pd event-related potentials. Participants’ caregivers completed proxy hyperfocus, anxiety, sensory experiences, and inattention questionnaires.Results:Autistic participants had elevated hyperfocus per self- and caregiver-report questionnaires, and exhibited less visual working memory capacity, potentially reflecting difficulty attending to multiple targets. However, groups did not differ in event-related potentials indexing hyperfocus, or behavioural cross-modal attention capture. Different types of attention measures were generally not related to one another. Participants and their caregivers overall viewed hyperfocus’ impact as modestly positive, with individual opinions differing. Self-reported hyperfocus was related to self-reported misophonia and sensory hyperreactivity.Conclusions:Questionnaires suggest hyperfocus is elevated in autism and has mixed, nuanced real-world impacts. However, some laboratory paradigms with explicit instructions in highly controlled, often non-naturalistic environments may struggle to capture real-world autistic attentional experiences; additional research is needed to align laboratory and questionnaire measures. This study also suggests autistic perceptual capacities are not globally enhanced.

Introduction

Attention in Autism

How autistic individuals attend to their environments can shape their experiences of the world and their development (Bradshaw et al., 2022; Vivanti et al., 2022). Some theories of autistic attention prioritise social attention; however, both social and non-social attention differences precede and predict emergence of autistic behaviours (Bedford et al., 2014; Canu et al., 2020; Sacrey et al., 2014; Zwaigenbaum et al., 2005). Moreover, non-social factors such as salience and perceptual load can influence social attention (Amso et al., 2014; Haskins et al., 2022). Thus, domain-general investigation of attention in autism appears promising.

However, the nature of domain-general attention in autism is debated. Evidence suggests autism is characterised by hyperfocus (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel, 2021; Dwyer, Williams et al., 2024); accordingly, the monotropism theory suggests autistic attention is directed narrowly and intensely towards targets of personal interest (Murray et al., 2005). Given the finite nature of attention, this means less attention is available for other tasks and targets (Murray et al., 2005). The theory is broadly consistent with accounts suggesting autism involves heightened attention to detail (e.g., Happé & Frith, 2006; Mottron et al., 2003), but suggests this simply reflects how an individual allocates their monotropic focus (Murray et al., 2005). Furthermore, the monotropism account predicts that autistic people will have difficulty shifting attention and cognitive set (Murray et al., 2005). This is consistent not only with lived experiences of autistic inertia, or slowness to start, stop, and shift tasks (Buckle et al., 2021), but also with findings of slow attention disengagement, or “sticky attention” (Landry & Bryson, 2004; Sacrey et al., 2014). Slow attention disengagement is postulated to shape opportunities for learning and development (Landry & Bryson, 2004; Sacrey et al., 2014); although fewer learning opportunities from unattended stimuli (e.g., social stimuli) could be paralleled by closer engagement with stimuli of interest (Sacrey et al., 2014). Thus, children may have strengths in the ability to assimilate information into existing knowledge and frameworks, e.g. by intense learning from narrow sources of interest, but might require support to capitalise on these strengths (Vivanti et al., 2022).

However, other accounts challenge the idea that autism is characterised by narrow hyperfocus on interests. One view suggests autistic people have an enhanced perceptual capacity to simultaneously process task-irrelevant stimuli, without impairing task performance (Remington et al., 2019). However, autistic people struggle at least as much as neurotypicals at simultaneously tracking multiple objects (Tullo et al., 2023). Moreover, distracting stimuli can capture autistic attention and disrupt task performance (Keehn et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2014; Poole et al., 2018; Venker et al., 2021; cf. Remington et al., 2012). Thus, autistic attention often appears endogenously-motivated, narrow, and hyper-focused, yet seemingly paradoxically, it can range over many stimuli and appears susceptible to exogenous capture.

This apparent paradox may be resolved if these diverse attentional patterns reflect underlying differences in attention regulation. Because attention has a limited capacity, hyper-focusing on some targets should reduce attention to others (Garau et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2005). Indeed, hyperfocus is observed not only in autism, but also in ADHD and schizophrenia (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel, 2021; Hupfeld et al., 2019; Luck, Hahn et al., 2019), where attention has traditionally been seen as reduced or impaired (Egeland, 2007). Thus, attention regulation differences, sometimes manifesting in hyperfocus and sometimes in inattention/distractibility (Luck, Leonard et al., 2019), may be a transdiagnostic phenomenon.

However, terms and constructs such as “hyperfocus” and “inattention” can be ambiguous. For example, hyperfocus is often regarded as similar or equivalent to “flow” (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel, 2021), intrinsically motivated states of positive intense absorption or concentration characterised by a number of specific features (see Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi, 2014; Rapaport et al., 2024 for more complete definitions), but measures of hyperfocus and flow can be inversely associated (Grotewiel et al., 2023). Rapaport et al. (2024) suggest hyperfocus involves some components overlapping with flow – intense focus, less awareness of task-irrelevant inputs, intrinsic reward, and a sense of control over a task – but flow that may involve a broader sense of control over oneself and one’s environment, as well as other features such as just-manageable challenge-skill balance. Meanwhile, an understanding of hyperfocus used in the schizophrenia literature simply characterises it as narrow but intense focus (Luck, Hahn et al., 2019; Luck, Leonard et al., 2019). Notably, under either of these definitions, flow could perhaps be characterised as a particular and highly positive form of hyperfocus. However, Hupfeld et al. (2019) measure hyperfocus based on several indicators, including getting suck on details – an indicator which seems distinct from flow.

Adding to this ambiguity regarding hyperfocus’ definition and meaning, one limitation of prior hyperfocus studies is that they generally use either self-report data, such as surveys and qualitative reflections (e.g., Garau et al., n.d.; Grotewiel et al., 2023; Hupfeld et al., 2019; Ozel-Kizil et al., 2016; Poole et al., 2023; Rapaport et al., n.d.), or laboratory tasks (e.g., Luck et al., 2014; Sawaki et al., 2017; Sklar, 2013), rather than both together. Thus, how self-reports about real-world hyperfocus experiences are related to specific attentional processes studied in more controlled settings is unclear. Clarifying these relationships could strengthen the conceptual foundations of hyperfocus and attention research.

One key question is whether hyperfocus is associated with task-based measures of inattention, distraction, or attention capture. Models of hyperfocus in schizophrenia suggest difficulties with attentional control can lead people with schizophrenia to focus narrowly on task-irrelevant targets (Luck, Leonard et al., 2019). As such, Distractor Target (DT) paradigms measure hyper-focusing on peripheral distractors that visually match what one is looking for (hyper-focusing on task goal), but in a task-irrelevant location. Thus, the task essentially incorporates both hyperfocus and attention capture within the same paradigm. Hyperfocus on peripheral distractors is measured through the lateralised N2pc and PD event-related potentials (ERPs), and those ERP components can be observed in the present study’s adolescent age range (Linton et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2016). The visual N2pc is a negative-going wave of differences between ERPs over contralateral and ipsilateral occipito-temporal sites between 150 and 300 ms post-stimulus, putatively reflecting covert attention towards a lateralised stimulus (Luck, 2011). In the same waveform of differences between contralateral and ipsilateral sites, the “distractor positivity” or PD apparently reflects suppression of attention (Gaspelin et al., 2023; Luck, 2011). Depending on the timing of suppression, the PD may occur before the N2pc (e.g., 100–275 ms) or afterwards (e.g., 275–500 ms) (Gaspelin et al., 2023). Thus, a robust N2pc could indicate capture of hyper-focused attention by distractors, while a subsequent PD could reflect suppression of attention towards them (Linton et al., 2021; Sawaki et al., 2017). Admittedly, Ashinoff and Abu-Akel (2021) and Rapaport et al. (2024) both understand hyperfocus to involve engagement for the sake of intrinsic reward and diminished awareness of irrelevant inputs, whereas the DT task is not designed to be intrinsically rewarding and indexes hyper-focusing on distractors. On the other hand, given that hyperfocus and inattention do seem to run together (Dwyer, Williams et al., 2024; Hupfeld et al., 2024), the DT task’s incorporation of both narrow focus and attention capture together taps both of these attentional aspects and thus makes conceptual sense as a composite index related to these attentional features consistent with hyperfocus. Thus, it is unclear whether DT task performance should be related to hyperfocus as operationalised in questionnaires.

Cross-modal distraction paradigms may also be relevant to autistic attention. On average, autistic people exhibit less multisensory integration than non-autistic people (Feldman et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2018). These group differences increase as autistic people are required to divide their attention in more challenging ways (Magnée et al., 2011), suggesting attention contributes to differences in cross-modal integration in autism (see also Bonneh et al., 2008). When autistic people are directed to focus on one modality, distraction from a competing modality can, on average, interfere with their performance more than in typical development (Keehn et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2014; Poole et al., 2018). Difficulties with cross-modal integration and distraction might relate to general autistic hyperfocus and susceptibility towards attention capture.

Present Study

In this study, autistic and non-autistic adolescents completed lab-based attention tasks. Participants and their caregivers also completed questionnaires measuring hyperfocus, sensory experiences, anxiety, and autistic and ADHD traits. The study also explored whether autistic people and their caregivers differ in perceptions of the frequency and valence of hyperfocus episodes.

Furthermore, in other studies, we have observed evidence suggesting that attention differences are related to autistic people’s sensory behaviours and experiences (Dwyer, Sillas, Prieto et al., 2024; Dwyer et al., 2025), which is not necessarily surprising given attention’s role in sensory experience (Posner & Rothbart, 1992). We also observed evidence of relationships with anxiety (Dwyer, Williams et al., 2024). We predicted that this study would also show relationships between attention differences and differences in sensory experiences and elevations in anxiety, but due to space limitations, this analysis is presented in Appendix B.

Methods

Participants

This study’s sample has been described more extensively elsewhere (Dwyer, Sillas, Saron et al., 2024). 18 autistic and 22 comparison participants, recruited through institutional research registries, social media, community groups, and personal contacts between July 2022 and August 2023, were included (Table 1). Our original goal was to recruit 20 participants in each group; based on effect sizes in relevant prior research (prepublication version of Linton et al., 2021), we calculated this would provide power of.82 to detect statistical differences in N2pc/PD responses. Based on prior work (Keehn et al., 2019) and depending on the correlation between repeated measures, power to detect group differences in cross-modal distraction would approach or reach.80. Collapsing across groups,.80 power to detect correlations between variables of r = .41 would – while exploratory – allow us to detect practically meaningful associations.

Intellectual disability was exclusionary. Participants in both groups had trichromatic colour vision and at least 20/40 (corrected) vision (Titmus T2S device). Hearing loss (monaural pure tone average ≥ 25 dB HL) was exclusionary; hearing thresholds were tested using an Otovation Amplitude T4 audiometer (automated Hughson-Westlake procedure).

Comparison participants were screened for autism using the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ; Berument et al., 1999) and had no suspected neurodivergence and no first-/second-degree autistic relatives. 17 autistic participants’ diagnoses were confirmed by clinician judgement in a visit including Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS)–2 administration (Lord et al., 2012); note that some participants and clinicians wore masks due to COVID-19 restrictions. Another received their diagnosis < 1 year previously, in an evaluation with an ADOS. 12 autistic participants had co-occurring ADHD.

Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, 2nd edition (WASI-II)

The WASI-II (Wechsler, 2011) yields indices of verbal comprehension (VCI), perceptual reasoning (PRI), and full-scale IQ. PRI subtests arguably most accurately estimate cognitive ability in autism (Nader et al., 2016) and appear more relevant to attentional task performance (Tullo et al., 2023), so PRI scores were used to covary for any potential group differences.

Hyperfocus Questionnaire

Participants and their caregivers completed an adapted Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire (Hupfeld et al., 2019). The questionnaire is based on a definition of hyperfocus as involving “heightened, intense focus,” more specifically operationalised based on timelessness, failure to attend to the world, ignoring personal needs, difficulty stopping/switching tasks, feelings of engrossment, and feeling stuck on details (Hupfeld et al., 2019). As adapted here,4 24 items queried frequency of hyperfocus experiences in 4 domains (6 per domain): in General (Dispositional Hyperfocus), in participants’ favourite School class, in their favourite Hobby, and in their most frequent Screen Time activity. Items were rated from “Never” (0) to “Daily” (5).

Additional Likert items asked about productivity and feelings of enjoyment as consequences of hyperfocus. Three items apparently centred on activities themselves (e.g., “I generate creative solutions or ideas when I am very engaged in an activity”), while two apparently focused on feelings and subjective experiences (e.g., “I enjoy how captivated I become when doing an activity that is engaging to me”). When crafting caregiver-report versions of the latter two items, versions asking about the caregiver’s feelings (e.g., “I enjoy how captivated my child becomes when doing an activity that is engaging to them”) and about the caregiver’s impression of the child’s feelings (e.g., “My child enjoys how captivated they become…”) were included.

Visual analogue scale items regarding the valence of Dispositional, School, Hobby, and Screen Time Hyperfocus were included, with the prompt, “I consider my experiences of focusing intensely on…to be”; the scale ranged from “Completely Negative” (0) to “Completely Positive” (100). Participants also answered open-ended questions about activities causing them to be very focused and an instance when they became very focused.

Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD-symptoms and Normal-behaviours (SWAN)

Caregivers completed this 18-item questionnaire, rating their child’s abilities to be attentive (9 items) and neither hyperactive nor impulsive (9 items) on a 7-point scale from −3 to + 3; higher scores reflect more ADHD traits (Swanson et al., 2012).

Attention tasks

Distractor Target (DT) task

Participants were instructed to press a button to circles of a target colour presented centrally, ignoring circles in two lateral locations (Fig. 1A); they pressed a different button when the central circle did not match the target colour. The central circle was randomly either grey (70 % of trials) or red, green, or blue (10 % each of trials). Lateral circles were either red, green, or blue, at equal (33 %) probability, but the two lateral circles could not be identically-coloured in any trial. The trials of interest were those where the central circle was grey, but a target-colour distractor was visible on the left or right (168 trials, 47 %).

Six blocks of 60 trials (360 total) were presented using Neurobehavioral Presentation on a Samsung 55 cm 1920 × 1080 pixel monitor. Participants were seated approximately ~65 cm from the monitor, resulting in a circle diameter of ~1.6° visual angle; the lateral circles were centred 2.5° from screen’s middle. The target colour (red, green, or blue) was randomly selected three times without replacement, so participants responded to each colour for two blocks each. To facilitate goal maintenance, a coloured letter (e. g., a red R) matching the current target colour served as a fixation point. Circles were displayed for 200 ms, with random (square distribution) ISIs of 1600 – 1800 ms.

A Tobii Pro Nano eye-tracker and the Presentation Extension for Tobii Pro (Martin, 2021) monitored fixations; stimulus presentation was paused if participants looked away from central fixation. For 5 participants (4 autistic), difficulties obtaining eye-tracking data (e.g., due to participant glasses) forced a halt to eye-tracking data collection, lest ISIs be distorted.

Visual Working Memory (VWM) task

In the Visual Working Memory task, participants viewed displays of four nearly-circular ellipses, of randomly-selected colours (Fig. 1B). Participants were seated approximately ~75–80 cm from the Samsung 1920 × 1080 pixel monitor, such that ellipses had a major axis of ~1.2° and a minor axis of ~1.1° visual angle. Ellipses were centred either ~3.6° (horizontally) or ~3.3° (vertically) from the screen centre. Arrays were presented for 200 ms, then replaced by a fixation cross for 1000 ms, before participants were presented with a near-identical array in which one ellipse randomly changed colour. Participants were asked to click the circle that changed colour.

Cross-modal distraction task

The cross-modal task was adapted from Keehn et al. (2019), with black-and-white Gaussian noise overlaid onto pictures of animals and musical instruments to decrease their salience relative to auditory stimuli, which could be congruent (50 % of trials) or incongruent (50 % of trials) with pictures (Fig. 2). For example, a cow moo sound would be incongruent with a picture of a piano. Sounds could appear before or after pictures: stimulus onset asynchronies (SOAs) ranged from −225 ms (sound first) to + 225 ms, in 75 ms increments. SOAs were calibrated with a Brain Products 240–1001 photosensor and the computer’s auditory output.

Participants were instructed to press a button with their finger to indicate whether images depicted animals or musical instruments; they were directed to ignore the sounds. Images were presented on the 1920 × 1080 pixel monitor. Sounds were presented through AKG 612 headphones at 68.5 dB SPL RMS intensity, calibrated via an artificial ear and sound meter (HBK 4153, HBK 2236). Auditory stimulus durations ranged from 407 to 781 ms, and visual stimuli were presented for 1500 ms, unless a button press ended the trial. The interval between trials ranged from 1500 to 2500 ms (square distribution); during this time, a fixation cross (Thaler et al., 2013) was visible. Stimuli were presented randomly in seven blocks of 48 trials (336 total).

EEG data processing and analysis

EEG acquisition and processing

In the DT task, EEG data were recorded from 125 channels and processed, using multiple software tools (Brunet et al., 2011; Delorme & Makeig, 2004; Lopez-Calderon & Luck, 2014; Oostenveld et al., 2011; Saggar et al., 2012), as described in Appendix C. Trials in which participants did not respond correctly were removed.

ERP analysis

Contralateral-minus-ipsilateral difference waves were taken from target-absent, target-colour distractor-present trials. Trials in which the target-colour-distractor was in the left hemifield were averaged and right-hemisphere voltages were subtracted from left-hemisphere voltages to produce a difference wave, and vice versa when the distractor appeared in the right hemifield; these two difference waves were then averaged to form a single overall contralateral-minus-ipsilateral difference wave. The topographic region of interest was selected based on inspecting average data in Cartool and EEGLAB; the eight interhemispheric channel pairs that exhibited the largest negative-going deflection in contralateral-minus-ipsilateral difference waves (Fig. 3A) were chosen for the N2pc analysis, and a corresponding window was defined ±60 ms around this deflection’s peak (129 – 249 ms). The PD topographic region was defined using nine channel pairs exhibiting the largest positive-going deflections in the difference waves peaking at 311 ms (Fig. 3B); the analysis window was defined as ±60 ms around this peak (251 – 371 ms). Mean N2pc and PD amplitudes were compared across diagnostic groups using ordinal cumulative link regression (Christensen, 2022), controlling for chronological age and WASI PRI due to slight trends for groups to differ on those measures.

Reaction time and accuracy data analysis

In Distractor Target (DT) task target-present trials, hit rate and median reaction times (RTs, for correct responses only) were compared across groups using ordinal cumulative link regression with likelihood-ratio-based analysis of deviance (Christensen, 2022; Hervé, 2022), accounting for chronological age and WASI PRI. In target-absent trials, median RTs were compared across groups and across trials with and without target-colour-distractors using cumulative link multilevel regression models with analysis of deviance (Christensen, 2022; Hervé, 2022); models included fixed effects of age and WASI PRI, as well as varying (random) intercepts by participant.

In the cross-modal distraction task, median RTs (to correct responses) and accuracy were compared across diagnostic groups, congruent and incongruent trials, and SOAs, accounting for age and WASI PRI, using cumulative link multilevel models (Christensen, 2022; Hervé, 2022).

In the Visual Working Memory (VWM) task, accuracy in selecting the colour-changing target, and median RTs for correct responses, were compared across groups using cumulative link regression, accounting for chronological age and WASI PRI.

Questionnaire data analysis

Hyperfocus Questionnaire scores for the Dispositional, Screen Time, Hobby, and School domains (6 items each), as well as regarding the valence of consequences in terms of activities (3 items), were compared across raters (self- and caregiver-report) and diagnostic groups using cumulative link multilevel regression models (Christensen, 2022; Hervé, 2022); models included fixed effects of group, reporter, their interaction, the covariates of age and WASI PRI, as well as varying (random) intercepts by participant. Convergence across raters was also evaluated using scaled, ranked regression (analogous to a Spearman’s correlation), with diagnostic group added as a factor to control for group differences.

Of particular interest were the two items about feelings and subjective experience, for which three respondent versions were collected: caregiver report of own feelings, caregiver proxy-report of child feelings, and child self-report of own feelings. To facilitate comparison across self- and caregiver-reports, these three report versions were considered a single repeated measures factor, and ranked data were compared across groups and report types using cumulative link multilevel regression.

Analysis of measure convergence

Associations among Dispositional Hyperfocus, N2pc and PD amplitudes from the DT task, cross-modal distraction (accuracy and RT differences between congruent and incongruent conditions), VWM accuracy, autistic traits (SCQ), and ADHD inattention traits (SWAN inattention) were examined using ranked regression, with diagnostic group as a covariate. These were, in effect, Spearman’s correlations controlling for group differences between autistic and non-autistic participants. Composite scores were created for some constructs, as described in Appendix B and by Dwyer, Sillas, Saron, et al. (2024). Exploratory analyses examining associations with anxiety and sensory reactivity are reported in Appendix B.

Results

Hyperfocus Questionnaire

As shown in Table 2 (and Supplementary Figure A.1), autistic participants exhibited significantly more of all forms of hyperfocus than comparison participants, pcor≤ .005. However, in all four hyperfocus domains, we observed interactions between diagnostic group and reporter (self vs. caregiver), pcor≤ .001. Autistic participants’ caregivers proxy-reported more Dispositional, Hobby, and Screen Time Hyperfocus than indicated by self-reports, pcor≤ .01, but comparison participants’ caregivers proxy-reported less School and Hobby Hyperfocus than indicated by self-reports, pcor≤ .03. Thus, group differences between autistic and comparison participants were heavily driven by caregiver-reports. Collapsing across groups, caregiver- and self-reports were only associated with one another for Hobby and Screen Time Hyperfocus.

As described in Table 2, the consequences of autistic participants’ hyperfocus were perceived less positively than comparison participants’ hyperfocus, p = .008, but there was also an interaction between group and reporter, p = .04 (Supplementary Figure A.2). Indeed, the group main effect was seemingly largely driven by comparison participants’ caregivers’ tendency to rate their children’s hyperfocus more positively than comparison participants self-reported it, corrected p = .07. Mean ratings in the autistic group were consistent with responses of “Slightly true of me”/“…my child” suggesting mixed but somewhat positive perceptions of hyperfocus.

When participants’ own feelings, caregivers’ feelings, and caregivers’ proxy-reports of participants’ feelings about hyperfocus experiences were compared (Table 3; Supplementary Figure A.3), a main effect of report type was observed, p = .002, along with an interaction of report type and group, p = .002. Post-hoc analyses suggested these effects were partly driven by autistic participants’ caregivers (falsely) expecting their children to have more positive feelings about hyperfocus than themselves, pcor= .02; however, autistic children’s and their caregivers’ feelings about autistic participants’ hyperfocus did not statistically differ, pcor= .96. Conversely, in the comparison group, caregiver proxy-reports of child feelings and caregiver-reports of own feelings about hyperfocus did not differ, pcor= .96, but comparison participants had less positive feelings about hyperfocus than their caregivers, pcor= .04. Overall, mean ratings appeared consistent with responses of “Slightly true…” to “True…,” suggesting mixed but somewhat positive feelings about hyperfocus.

Visual analogue scale ratings of the valence of hyperfocus in each domain are presented in Supplementary Table A.1 and Supplementary Figure A.4.

Visual Working Memory task

Autistic participants were less accurate than controls in selecting the colour-changing target, χ2 = 7.74, p = .005 (Fig. 4A), despite consideration of age, χ2 = 1.14, p = .29, and WASI PRI composite scores, χ2 = 10.49, p = .001, the latter of which was positively related to accuracy. Autistic participants’ median RTs for correct responses also trended towards being slower than controls’, χ2 = 3.36, p = .07 (Fig. 4B); age and WASI PRI scores were not related to RTs, p ≥ .66.

Cross-modal distraction task

There was a strongly trending effect of diagnostic group on accuracy, χ2 = 3.46, p = .06, with autistic participants appearing less accurate overall, regardless of whether pictures were congruent or incongruent with sounds (Fig. 5). Accuracy was greater when sounds were congruent with pictures, χ2 = 16.95, p < .0001, and was also influenced by SOA, χ2 = 16.64, p = .01, but post-hoc comparisons of SOAs in each congruency condition showed no clear patterns, all corrected p ≥ .06 (Supplementary Table A.2). There was a slight tendency for diagnostic group to interact with SOA, χ2 = 11.01, p = .09, but no interactions involving congruency approached significance, p ≥ .30, nor did age or nonverbal cognitive ability influence accuracy, p ≥ .58.

It proved difficult to fit cumulative link models to the reaction time data, so mixed ANCOVA on ranked (ordinal) reaction times was substituted (Kassambara, 2022). There was a main effect of diagnostic group on ranked median reaction times (RTs) from correct responses, F(1,36)= 10.97, p = .002, ηg2=.19, with autistic participants being slower than comparison participants (Fig. 6). Furthermore, responses to incongruent trials were slower than congruent trials, F(1,36)= 58.55, p < .0001, ηg2=.03. Moreover, stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA), Greenhouse-Geisser F(4.03,145.18)= 25.78, p < .0001, ηg2=.09, influenced RTs. The interaction between congruency and SOA closely approached statistical significance, F(4.15,149.57)= 2.37, p = .053, ηg2=.01, but no interactions involving diagnostic group approached significance, all p ≥ .36. Older age was linked to faster RTs, F(1,36)= 13.49, p = .0008, ηg2=.22. As described in Supplementary Table A.3, the effect of SOA was driven by faster RTs when sounds appeared before pictures, compared to when pictures appeared first – even when sounds were incongruent with pictures. This suggests sounds facilitated participants’ alertness and readiness to respond.

Distractor Target (DT) task

Behavioural data

Autistic participants responded less accurately on target-present DT trials than controls, χ2 = 6.81, p = .009 (Supplementary Figure A.5 A). Median RTs when participants responded correctly on target-present trials did not statistically differ between groups, χ2 = 1.85, p = .17, though they did appear to become faster with age, χ2 = 6.97, p = .008, and there was a visual trend for RT variability to be greater in the autistic group (Supplementary Figure A.5B).

Autistic participants were less accurate than controls in target-absent trials, χ2 = 7.71, p = .005, and participants in general responded less accurately when target-colour distractors were present, χ2 = 8.80, p = .003 (Supplementary Figure A.6 A). The interaction of diagnostic group and target-colour distractor presence did not influence accuracy, χ2 = 0.78, p = .38, though participants became increasingly likely to make correct rejections with age, χ2 = 4.39, p = .04.

There was no effect of group on target-absent trial RTs, χ2 = 0.00, p = .99, but RTs were slower when target-colour distractors were present, χ2 = 17.78, p < .0001 (Supplementary Figure A.6B). The interaction of diagnostic group and presence of target-colour distractors did not influence RTs, χ2 = 0.00, p = .97.

Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)

Unexpectedly, diagnostic groups did not appear to differ in mean N2pc amplitudes, χ2 = 0.68, p = .41, and there was only a slight trend towards group differences in PD amplitudes, χ2 = 2.37, p = .12 (Figs. 3, 7; Supplementary Figure A.7).

Associations Among Measures

Associations among attention measures are presented in Table 4 and those between attention and autistic and ADHD traits are in Table 5 (see also Supplementary Tables A.4–A.7). N2pc and PD amplitudes were associated, which is unsurprising given their spatiotemporal dependency, but no other attention metrics were associated with one another after correction. Autistic and ADHD traits were not significantly associated with attention measures after correction.

Furthermore, exploratory relationships between attention indices and anxiety/sensory reactivity are reported in Supplementary Table B.7. Controlling for multiple comparisons, self-reported hyperfocus was associated with self-reported misophonia and generalised sensory hyperreactivity. However, no associations between measures of different types (e.g., caregiver-report questionnaire scores and laboratory task performance) attained significance after correction.

Discussion

This study used multiple methods to explore domain-general attention differences in autistic and in non-autistic children and adolescents. Per questionnaires, autistic participants experienced greater hyperfocus in their daily lives, apparently broadly consistently with theories emphasizing the importance of monotropism in autistic experiences. However, unexpectedly, little evidence suggested questionnaire reports of hyperfocus translated into laboratory measures of hyperfocus and attention capture. Although autistic people were overall slower and trended towards being less accurate in a cross-modal distraction task, both groups’ accuracy and reaction times were similarly impacted by incongruent sounds, suggesting the auditory stimulus did not disproportionately capture attention in autistic people. Furthermore, autistic participants did not statistically differ from comparison participants in their N2pc and PD ERP responses from the Distractor-Target (DT) task, and although the DT task is described as a hyperfocus paradigm (Sawaki et al., 2017), there was no evidence suggesting that questionnaire reports of hyperfocus were associated with DT task ERPs, or with cross-modal task attention capture.

These results emphasise the complexity of domain-general attention differences in autism and the continued need for further clarification of the nature of and relationships among hyperfocus constructs. One key factor may be how much participants are intrinsically interested in stimuli; intrinsic interest is emphasised by the monotropism account and some definitions of hyperfocus, but not other definitions of hyperfocus. Unfortunately, manipulating the degree to which stimuli are individually engaging may risk introducing confounds, e.g., differences in low-level sensory properties. Using stimuli designed to be widely interesting may be a more promising route, but still could become problematic if participants differ in how much they find the stimuli absorbing. Another key factor that might relate to the poor observed convergence between measures may be the complexity of different contexts and the range of possible targets of attention in them; laboratory environments are often highly controlled, with few salient extraneous stimuli to capture attention, and tasks such as this study’s offer clear instructions (e.g., ignore this, focus on that). Participants’ ability to follow task instructions in a controlled setting may not be consonant with their ability to suppress distracting stimuli in more complex environments when stressed or under higher cognitive loads. Dual-task paradigms might help elucidate such differences (Kercood et al., 2014). Another approach would be to study not whether participants successfully follow directions to allocate their attention in particular ways, but how they deploy their attention naturalistically; passive tasks such as eye-tracking are widely used to study attention and cognition in autism, and are more accessible to children and individuals with intellectual disabilities (e.g., Falck-Ytter et al., 2013; Gaigg et al., 2020). Ecological momentary assessment (EMA), collecting information from participants as they move through their daily lives, has a long history of being used to explore everyday experiences of flow (Farnworth et al., 1996; Fullagar & Kelloway, 2009). This suggests EMA may have similar value in explorations of everyday neurodivergent attention states based on the individual interests of a person, but this does not resolve the problem of bridging between such experiences and the laboratory.

Further research from a variety of disciplines and approaches, including applied, experiential, and cognitive neuroscience studies, may provide greater clarity. As such research is pursued, the similarities and boundaries – which may be fuzzy, dynamic, and context-dependent – between concepts like hyperfocus, flow, monotropism, and distractibility should become clearer. Such conceptual and definitional clarity, and a shared lexicon across researchers in different fields, should improve understanding of how these attention constructs manifest in neurotypical and neurodivergent cognition.

Visual Working Memory (VWM) Task

Although autistic people did not exhibit heightened attention capture or hyperfocus on the DT and cross-modal tasks, autistic people’s VWM task responses were significantly less accurate, even after accounting for nonverbal cognitive ability. These results appear somewhat similar to those obtained by Tullo et al. (2023), who used a multiple object-tracking paradigm to measure attentional/perceptual capacity; in their study, autistic people had greater difficulty tracking multiple objects moving during a prescribed period. Similarly, in this study’s Visual Working Memory task, participants must covertly attend to and encode colours of multiple stimuli within a 200 ms period; as the array occupies a sizeable visual field area (~8°), this could be challenging for individuals with a monotropic-like, narrowed attentional focus. However, while the group differences observed by Tullo et al. (2023) did not survive correction for nonverbal cognitive ability, those in this study remained robust. One intriguing possibility is that autistic people might have had their attention captured by specific stimuli more often within the brief 200 ms window, perhaps consistent with evidence suggesting autistic people may, on average, require more time to process stimuli globally (Van der Hallen et al., 2015). However, in this study, simultaneous eye-tracking was only obtained for the DT task, so the possibility of attention capture cannot be explicitly investigated. Moreover, VWM task accuracy was not significantly associated with other attention measures. It is also possible that the group difference emerged not because of attention capture during the encoding phase, but a difference in maintenance of information over time. Working memory is closely intertwined with the direction of internal attention (Chun, 2011; Oberauer, 2019), suggesting the effect could still be attentional, but of a different nature.

Regardless, poor autistic VWM performance appears at odds with literature suggesting autistic people have a greater capacity to process more information simultaneously (e.g., Remington et al., 2019; Remington & Fairnie, 2017), and is more consistent with research suggesting autistic people struggle to follow multiple objects when instructed to do so (Tullo et al., 2023). This suggests findings of superior processing of multiple stimuli in autism may be related to contextual factors influencing attention.

Hyperfocus Questionnaire Responses

Interestingly, although autistic people reportedly engage in more hyperfocus across contexts than non-autistic people, these differences were apparently driven by caregiver-reports: autistic people self-reported significantly less Dispositional, Hobby, and Screen Time Hyperfocus than caregivers, whereas comparison participants self-reported more School and Hobby Hyperfocus than caregivers. These differences are particularly striking when one considers that the Hyperfocus Questionnaire used in this study offers clear response options, such as “Never (not in the past month)” and “Rarely (less than once per week)” (Hupfeld et al., 2019). Thus, differences between reporters are unlikely to reflect interpretations of the response scale. Instead, they may reflect participants and caregivers differing in knowledge of specific episodes of hyperfocus. As caregivers may be unaware of some of children’s hyperfocus episodes, comparison group self-reports might incorporate additional episodes omitted in caregiver-reports, particularly where caregivers are not present, such as school. Indeed, differences between comparison group self- and caregiver-reports appeared largest for schools. However, comparison participants may instead have exaggerated their hyperfocus in socially desirable domains, such as school. Social desirability was not measured in this study. It is also possible that autistic participants may have implicitly desired to minimise differences between themselves and others; however, as the present study contained no measures of neurodivergent identity or internalised stigma, this cannot be examined directly. Alternatively, perhaps due to interoception difficulties common in autism (Trevisan et al., 2021), autistic young people might experience more challenges identifying when they experience hyperfocus. Participants might also have differed in their thresholds for accepting that a hyperfocus episode matches the wording of an item; for example, if hyperfocus is just part of some autistic young people’s normal experience, some episodes or instances of it might not be salient enough for them to think to report those instances. If this is the case, EMA methods might help to provide answers more grounded in the moment.

Mean ratings of the valence and consequences of participants’ hyperfocus suggested that hyperfocus was perceived, on average, as modestly positive. This is consistent with research suggesting hyperfocus can have both positive and negative aspects, depending upon context (Dwyer, Williams et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2019), but strikingly, in this study, participants and their caregivers showed considerable inter-individual variability in ratings (depicted in Supplementary Figures A.2–A.4). Future studies might find it revealing to probe whether such variability is driven by differences in attentional phenotypes or by other factors, such as how much people’s social contexts accept and leverage their hyperfocus.

In addition, some of this study’s results regarding feelings about hyperfocus used a framework similar to Heasman and Gillespie (2018), who studied autistic people and their family members’ perspectives on various issues. Although in this study only caregivers attempted to report their children’s feelings about hyperfocus, not vice versa, this demonstrated that autistic participants’ caregivers often exaggerated their children’s positive feelings about hyperfocus, whereas autistic children’s actual mixed feelings about hyperfocus were not dissimilar to caregivers’. This finding is consistent with a double empathy problem lens (Milton, 2012) and the difficulties that others can have understanding autistic individuals’ perspectives.

Associations

We hypothesised that hyperfocus would be related not only to autistic traits, but also to ADHD inattention traits, as hyperfocus and inattention might be different manifestations of an underlying attention regulation difference. However, although questionnaire-reported hyperfocus was elevated in autistic people as a group, we observed no evidence that self- or caregiver-reported hyper-focus was related to continuous autistic or ADHD inattention traits. This might imply hyperfocus and inattention are separate and independent consequences of etiological factors related to the origin of neurodivergence. However, this study’s mixture of autistic people with and without co-occurring ADHD may have confused analyses; future, larger studies should examine whether relationships between inattention and hyperfocus are observable within separated groups of autistic people with and without co-occurring ADHD (or vice versa). Trending associations between autistic/ADHD traits and visual working memory accuracy, and between autistic traits and RT impacts of incongruent stimuli from the cross-modal task, did not attain significance after correction.

Interestingly, our supplementary analysis found that self-reported hyperfocus was related to self-reported experiences of sensory hyperreactivity and misophonia, which was consistent with our expectations. However, shared methods (such as measures being based on self-report) can be related to biases (Choi & Pak, 2005). Our evidence of attention-sensory relationships would be more robust if measures rooted in different modalities were associated, but none of these effects attained statistical significance after correction. Thus, our analysis appears somewhat inconclusive. Future, larger studies may be required to investigate sensory-attention relationships.

Limitations

Although this study has virtues such as its large battery of tasks and measures and its rigorous approach to task design and data processing, making it a richly detailed, “deep” dataset, it has other limitations. The most important limitation is its small sample size, which reflects collection of a large amount of data from each participant (the study included three in-person visits and additional online questionnaires), as well as delays related to the COVID-19 pandemic. While this study’s small sample was sufficient to detect some robust relationships among sensory indices (see Appendix B and Dwyer, Sillas, Saron, et al. (2024)), this may reflect the robustness of those relationships more than the study’s statistical power. In addition, the study sample is poorly representative in terms of gender and intellectual disability, which limits the generalizability of study conclusions.

Summary

This study aimed to richly characterise attention in autism using a combination of self-report, caregiver-report, and laboratory-based measures, with a particular focus on hyperfocus, a feature of autism emphasised in the monotropism account. Autistic people were indeed reported to engage in greater hyperfocus, particularly per caregiver-reports. However, this elevated hyperfocus in participants’ daily lives did not translate into greater evidence of hyperfocus on laboratory tasks, leaving the cognitive science underpinnings of real-world autistic hyperfocus unclear. Further research using dual-task or passive-viewing paradigms might encounter more success in bridging between laboratory-based and real-world hyperfocus.

This study also found autistic young people tended to report less hyperfocus than per parental proxy-report, the opposite of the pattern in the comparison group. In contrast, autistic people and their parents appeared broadly similar in their average perceptions of hyperfocus as being somewhat, but not exclusively, positive; however, autistic people’s parents falsely believed their children had more unambiguously positive views of hyperfocus, an evident misunderstanding consistent with the double empathy problem.

Finally, autistic people responded less accurately in a task requiring them to process and retain information about multiple stimuli in an array, suggesting that prior findings of elevated autistic perceptual capacity to process multiple stimuli at once are likely context-specific.
