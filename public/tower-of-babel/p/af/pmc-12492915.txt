Construction and verification of reliability and validity of the communication skill assessment scale in cancer palliative care for healthcare staff
Authors: Lanying Qiu, Xi Yang, Yingjun Ye, Guanmian Liang, Jianwen Hou, Shuai Han, Xiaoxue Wen, Xiaoying Shen, Yitong Guo, Haoyang Li, Dan Fan, Shuping Xie, Xiaowei Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe aimed to construct and test the reliability and validity of the communication skill assessment scale in cancer palliative care for healthcare staff.MethodsThe concept of communication skills was defined by the literature review. The palliative care communication skill assessment scale was developed through literature analysis, an open questionnaire survey, and expert correspondence. A total of 485 healthcare staff, including doctors and nurses, who worked in the medical oncology department of a Grade-A oncology hospital in Zhejiang Province were selected to screen the items of the scale and test their reliability and validity.ResultsThere were 39 items in the final version of the palliative care communication skill assessment scale for healthcare staff. Through exploratory factor analysis, six common factors were extracted, and the cumulative variance contribution rate was 80.47%. Item content validity index (CVI) ranged from 0.78 to 1.00, and the overall CVI of the scale was 0.91. The internal correlation test showed that the correlation coefficient of each dimension of the scale with a total score was 0.59–0.76 (p < 0.05), and the inter-dimension correlation coefficient was 0.46–0.51 (p < 0.05). The Kronbach coefficient of the aggregate table was 0.928. The retest reliability was 0.89. The broken half reliability was 0.84.ConclusionThe palliative care communication skill assessment scale we constructed has good reliability and validity, and it can be used to evaluate the communication skills of healthcare staff during palliative care.

Introduction

The WHO report shows that around 20.0 million new cancer cases were diagnosed worldwide in 2022, with approximately 9.7 million deaths due to cancer [1]. As a significant global public health issue, cancer not only poses a threat to human health but also exhibits a relentless upward trend in both incidence and mortality rates. China is confronted with a particularly grave situation in the prevention and treatment of cancer. In recent years, the incidence of malignant tumors in China has been on the rise annually. Statistical data from 2020 reveal that China had over 4.82 million new cases of malignant tumors and more than 2.57 million deaths [2].

The advancement of medicine has led to a significant increase in the survival rates of cancer patients and a substantial extension of their survival periods [3]. However, the quality of life and death remains a cause for concern. According to data from the UK’s Institute of Cancer Research [4], nearly half of the patients diagnosed with malignant tumors will Survive for more than 10 years. The long-term Survival with cancer has also led to widespread concern for the quality of life of patients and caregivers. Current research indicates that up to 30% of advanced cancer patients receive non-beneficial aggressive life support treatments such as resuscitation, intubation, surgery, and dialysis [5]. Nearly one-third of patients are still undergoing chemotherapy within the last six weeks of their lives [5]. These treatments, which merely prolong life, not only fail to reflect their intended value but also further exacerbate the physical and psychological burden on medical staff, patients, and their families [6].

The shift of the modern medical model from “disease-oriented” to “patient-oriented” emphasizes that medical personnel, in addition to using professional techniques to alleviate patients’ physical symptoms, must also pay attention to the humanistic care needs of “end-of-life” patients [7]. There is a growing focus on the “person” rather than just the “disease.” Consequently, the palliative care mode, which prioritizes symptom control and aims to improve the quality of life, has been integrated into the healthcare system [8]. The quality of palliative care is a decisive factor in whether patients can receive safe, comfortable, and dignified care in the final stages of life [9]. High-quality care should include the appropriate use of therapeutic communication skills, providing counseling and support for patients and their families, respecting patients’ wishes, and making treatment decisions that adhere to ethical and legal standards [10].

Given the critical role of communication skills in oncology palliative care, it is essential to enhance the competencies of healthcare staff in this field [11]. Commonly used communication assessment tools, such as the Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) [12], the Segue Communication Skills Assessment Scale proposed by Makoul in 2001 [13], serve important roles in evaluating general communication or empathic tendencies among healthcare providers. However, these instruments were not designed with the palliative care context in mind. For instance, the JSE focuses primarily on the provider’s self-perceived empathy and does not evaluate competencies like emotional regulation, delivering prognostic information, or grief counseling—skills that are crucial during end-of-life care. The Segue Framework, although structured around medical interview flow, lacks components assessing communication about advance care planning, spiritual beliefs, or death discussions. Similarly, Chinese-developed tools such as the Clinical Nurse Communication Competency Scale [14] and Nurse-Patient Communication Evaluation Scales [15] focus on general clinical environments and are rarely adapted to the emotional, ethical, and cultural complexity of oncology palliative scenarios. Our newly developed scale seeks to address these limitations by incorporating dimensions such as verbal/non-verbal expression, empathy, self-regulation, and behavior intention, tailored specifically to the emotional and existential challenges encountered in palliative oncology care. However, these tools do not specifically address the application of communication skills in palliative care for cancer patients, and their communication-related items are relatively generic, lacking systematic evaluation. Communication in palliative care presents distinct challenges not typically encountered in curative care. These include navigating emotionally charged conversations about death and prognosis, aligning treatment goals with patients’ and families’ values, supporting psychological distress, and mediating between differing expectations among stakeholders. Miscommunication in these contexts can result in delayed decision-making, patient and family dissatisfaction, and moral distress for healthcare providers. Despite the availability of general communication assessment tools, most lack specificity in domains critical to palliative settings, such as conveying unfavorable news, managing silence and emotion, and facilitating end-of-life discussions. Therefore, it is essential to develop a scale that captures the full spectrum of these competencies to guide training, assessment, and quality improvement initiatives in oncology palliative care. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to develop a scale for assessing the communication skills of healthcare staff in cancer palliative care for cancer patients and validate its reliability and validity.

Subjects and methods

Development of the initial draft of the scale

Literature review

A research team was composed of three oncology nursing management experts, two oncologists, two nursing graduate students, and three clinical nurses. A comprehensive literature search was conducted using both English and Chinese search terms, including “end-of-life care,” “palliative phase,” “dying patients,” “mortality,” “communication skills,” “scale,” “instrument,” “palliative treatment,” “communication requirements,” “physician-patient communication,” “oncology,” “palliative care,” “end-of-life care,” “palliative medicine,” “comfort care,” “doctor-patient communication,” “hospice care,” “attitude,” and “communication,” among others. The literature search covered the period from the establishment of the database up to June 2023, focusing on literature related to communication in palliative care for cancer patients.

Open-ended questionnaire

We acknowledged that differences in perspectives and thought patterns between research participants and researchers could lead to cognitive biases in understanding the issues at hand. To ensure that participants accurately comprehended the questions posed by the researchers and provided reliable feedback, an open-ended questionnaire method was designed. This approach not only focused on collecting participants’ direct responses but also utilized their detailed explanations to enrich the scale items. The responses to the open-ended questions were analyzed using thematic analysis. Two researchers independently conducted open coding of the transcripts to identify key concepts and expressions. Codes were then clustered into categories and refined into overarching themes through group discussion. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with the involvement of a third researcher if needed. These themes reflected the communicative needs and challenges faced in palliative care, and were systematically transformed into scale items. Care was taken to ensure that the wording of items preserved participants’ language while aligning with theoretical constructs from literature and expert consultations. The content of the questionnaire was designed according to purposive principles to ensure the specificity of the research and the validity of data collection [16].

In this study, we employed a maximum variation sampling method. Healthcare staff from the oncology department of a Grade-A oncology hospital in Zhejiang Province were purposefully selected for interviews, ensuring that they included individuals with diverse educational backgrounds, varying years of work experience, and different job positions. Inclusion criteria: (1) registered healthcare staff (physicians and nurses); (2) voluntary participation in the interviews under informed consent. Exclusion criteria included participants who exhibited severe emotional instability, defined as having observable signs of acute distress (e.g., uncontrollable crying, visible agitation, inability to concentrate) or self-reported psychological strain such as recent trauma or bereavement. These assessments were conducted during the pre-interview screening by trained oncology nurses or psychologists. This approach aimed to ensure ethical protection of participants who might be at risk of psychological discomfort during emotionally sensitive interviews. The open-ended questionnaire comprised the following questions: How do you understand the communication skills of healthcare staff with cancer patients and their families during the palliative care phase? What factors do you believe influence an individual’s communication skills? Can you provide some practical examples to illustrate these influences? Additionally, would you like to add any other points? The entire interview was expected to last approximately 30 min. Upon obtaining explicit consent from those healthcare staff, the conversations were recorded to ensure data integrity. The collected data was carefully organized and analyzed, and it was subsequently used to enrich and enhance the quality of the item bank for the scale (see Results 3.2).

Delphi method

Expert feedback in the Delphi rounds was used to evaluate each item’s importance, clarity, and relevance to palliative communication. In the first round, items with a mean importance score below 3.5 or a coefficient of variation (CV) above 0.25 were flagged for revision or deletion. Based on this, 9 items were removed, and 13 were modified to enhance clarity or specificity. For example, an original item stating, “I feel comfortable discussing serious matters with patients,” was revised to “I feel comfortable discussing terminal diagnoses and end-of-life options with patients” to reflect palliative-specific content. An item perceived as vague, “I support patients emotionally,” was removed due to lack of specificity and overlap with more targeted items on empathy and self-regulation. In the second round, items with improved consensus (CV < 0.20 and expert approval > 80%) were retained. All final items met pre-established thresholds, and disagreements were resolved through expert panel discussion and re-rating in round two (see Results 3.3 for details and examples of item modifications).

Pilot testing

A pilot study was conducted with 30 healthcare professionals from oncology and palliative care units to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and acceptability of the initial 42-item scale. Participants completed the scale and provided open-ended feedback on item wording and relevance. Based on the responses, 4 items were revised for clarity (e.g., replacing ambiguous phrasing such as “emotional needs” with more specific terms like “expressed distress”), and 2 items were removed due to redundancy and overlapping meaning with other items. No structural changes were made to the scale’s domains. The pilot confirmed that the revised items were understandable and appropriate for the target population, supporting the scale’s readiness for formal validation. See Results 3.4 for pilot findings.

Scale development

The development of the preliminary item pool was informed by findings from the open-ended questionnaire, expert interviews, literature review, and Delphi consultations. Items were designed to reflect key communication competencies in oncology palliative care, incorporating both patient-centered and clinician-facing perspectives.

After two Delphi rounds and pilot testing, the scale underwent systematic item refinement based on expert ratings and participant feedback. Redundant, unclear, or non-specific items were revised or deleted. Items were retained if they demonstrated clear relevance, appropriate language, and conceptual alignment with identified communication domains.

Following this refinement process, the final version of the scale included 30 items across six dimensions: (1) verbal communication, (2) non-verbal communication, (3) empathy, (4) emotional regulation, (5) active listening, and (6) behavioral intention. This finalized scale was subsequently subjected to psychometric testing, including item analysis, exploratory factor analysis, and reliability assessment.

Delphi expert consultation

From June 2023 to September 2023, 20 experts from seven cities, including Beijing, Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Shanghai, Hunan, Guangxi, and Guangdong, were invited to participate in the expert consultation. Inclusion criteria: (1) Clinical medical/nursing experts who had worked in oncology hospitals or oncology departments for at least 10 years, with extensive clinical experience; (2) Researchers or educators engaged in medical/nursing scientific research or teaching for at least 10 years in medical colleges; (3) Department heads or nursing directors who had been in charge of department management for at least 10 years, such as department chiefs, nursing directors, and head nurses; (4) who had at least a bachelor’s degree and hold an intermediate or higher professional title; (5) who had strong disciplinary representation in nursing education, research, management, and palliative care; and (6) who were willing to participate in the study and provided their opinions and suggestions.

In developing the expert consultation project, the structure of the consultation was divided into three parts. The first part provided an overview of the research background and conceptual framework. The second part consisted of a specific project assessment form that focused on evaluating communication competencies in oncology palliative care. This included project descriptions, dimension classifications, specific items, and corresponding expert ratings. Following each item, a section for suggested modifications was included, and at the end of each dimension, a “possible additions” section was provided to collect expert feedback. The third part comprised a background survey for the experts, which gathered general information, scoring criteria, and familiarity with the questionnaire content. The questionnaire was distributed and collected via email. After the first round of consultations, the research team summarized and discussed the expert feedback, made necessary revisions to the items, and prepared a subsequent consultation questionnaire. Two weeks later, a second round of expert consultation was conducted, during which the experts provided average ratings on the importance of the items. Items with an average importance rating greater than 3.50 and a top score exceeding 20% were retained. The consistency of expert opinions was validated using the coefficient of variation (CV < 0.25) and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (0.400–0.500, p < 0.05). Through repeated expert consultations and consistency testing, the team integrated expert opinions and optimized the selection of items. Finally, the feasibility of these items was tested through a pilot study involving 20 healthcare staff. Following the incorporation of expert feedback, the research team discussed and finalized the selected items. Additionally, a pre-test was conducted with the 20 healthcare staff.

Reliability and validity testing of the scale

Survey participants

This study employed a cluster sampling method to select oncologists and nursing personnel as survey participants from a Grade-A hospital in Zhejiang Province during October and November 2023. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with those used in the above section. Healthcare staff provided basic information and completed the communication skill assessment scale through an online questionnaire. The researchers explained the purpose of the survey, the instructions for completing the questionnaire, and the relevant information to the participants. Completed questionnaires were collected immediately upon submission to ensure data integrity.

Item analysis

This study utilized SPSS 26.0 for dual data entry and statistical analysis, aiming to assess the discriminative ability of scale items across different scoring groups and to verify item homogeneity, thereby ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the scale. The following item analysis methods were employed: Differential Trend Method: To ensure sensitivity, items with a standard deviation less than 0.8 were excluded [17]. Critical Ratio (CR) Value Analysis: The CR value was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of items, measuring the differentiation capability among participants. Items with a CR value less than 3 or those that were statistically non-significant (P > 0.05) were removed [18]. Correlation Coefficient Method: Items with a correlation coefficient to the total score of less than 0.3 were deleted [19]. Homogeneity Test: Items were removed if the correlation between the revised items and the total scale score was less than 0.4 or if deleting the item significantly increased Cronbach’s alpha coefficient [20]. Communality and Factor Loading Analysis: In the principal component analysis (PCA), a single factor was extracted. Items with a commonality of less than 0.2 or a factor loading of less than 0.45 were recommended for exclusion [21]. Any items that did not meet the standards set by the aforementioned analysis methods were removed to ensure the precision and consistency of the scale [22].

Validity testing

Content validity was assessed by calculating the Content Validity Index (CVI) for each item with the total scale. Structural validity was confirmed through exploratory factor analysis (EFA), utilizing principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal rotation with maximum variance. If the cumulative variance explained by the common factors exceeded 50%, and the number of extracted factors aligned with the predefined dimensions, the structure of the scale was deemed valid, provided that each item’s factor loading on the corresponding common factor was greater than 0.4, while loadings on other factors were relatively low. To evaluate internal consistency, correlation analyses were conducted between different dimensions and between each dimension and the total score. The criteria for scale consistency were as follows: correlations between dimensions and the total score should fall within the range of 0.3 to 0.8, while correlations among different dimensions should range from 0.1 to 0.6 [23].

Reliability testing

In this study, the internal consistency of the scale was evaluated by calculating Cronbach’s α coefficient for each subdimension as well as for the total scale, along with the split-half reliability. Test-retest reliability was validated using a 2-week interval to assess the external stability of the scale. This process involved randomly selecting 20 participants from 444 healthcare staff and conducting a second round of scale evaluation with them.

Results

Results of the literature review

In-depth research was conducted on relevant theories, and materials related to the study were collected. Simultaneously, literature from both domestic and international sources was reviewed in conjunction with China’s cultural context. Based on this foundation, theoretical dimensions of the scale were initially developed. These dimensions encompassed two main components: communication attitudes and communication competency assessment. Communication attitudes were divided into three aspects: cognition, emotion, and behavior. Communication competency was further categorized into eight dimensions: verbal communication, non-verbal communication, listening, expression of empathy, and emotional management, to measure the communication skills of healthcare staff.

Results of the open-ended questionnaire

The survey participants included 3 oncologists and 5 nursing staff from the oncology department of a grade-A hospital in Zhejiang Province. Their average age was 35 years. Among them, 5 held intermediate professional titles and 3 held senior titles. All of them were from the oncology radiotherapy and chemotherapy department, with 4 holding a bachelor’s degree, 2 holding a master’s degree, and 2 holding a doctoral degree. Eight items were revised based on feedback from the open-ended questionnaire. For example, 5 healthcare staff pointed out that certain phrases were inappropriately worded and lacked subject clarity. As a result, semantic adjustments were made, such as adding phrases like “I believe…”, “I hope…”, and “I am willing to…,” to provide more specific meanings and clarify the content. Additionally, 3 healthcare staff suggested that some items were ambiguous. For example, the item “comfortable environment” was found to overlap with items like “quiet environment” and “walking gently and carefully during ward rounds and procedures.” These were merged into a new item, “Able to provide a comfortable environment (privacy, warmth, quiet, relaxed).” Furthermore, a new item was added: “Able to guide patients in seeking assistance from relevant professionals.”

By synthesizing existing items from the literature related to healthcare communication skills in palliative care for cancer patients, along with the results from the open-ended questionnaire, an initial scale consisting of 57 items for evaluating communication competence in oncology palliative care was developed. All items used a 5-point Likert scale with positive scoring, where the total score for the scale is the sum of the individual item scores. A higher total score indicated a higher level of communication skills among healthcare staff.

Results of expert Delphi consultation

A total of 20 experts participated in the expert Delphi consultation phase. The average age of them was 47.65 ± 6.26 years, ranging from 32 to 57 years. Among them, 10 had a bachelor’s degree, 6 had a master’s degree, and 4 had a doctoral degree. Regarding professional titles, 17 had senior titles and 3 had intermediate titles. In terms of work experience, 17 had more than 20 years of experience, And 3 had more than 10 years. The experts’ specialties included end-of-life care nursing and nursing research (13 experts), scale development (3 experts), and psychology (4 experts). After two rounds of expert consultation, the opinions reached a complete consensus.

In the first round of consultation, a total of 20 questionnaires were distributed, all of which were successfully returned, achieving a 100% response rate. In the second round, 19 questionnaires were distributed, and all were returned, with both the response rate and the experts’ engagement rate of 100%. Following the first round of consultation, 1 expert was excluded due to an expert authority coefficient (Cr) < 0.7. After this exclusion, the average Cr of the remaining experts increased to 0.816. The consistency of expert opinions was evaluated using the Kendall concordance coefficient, with values of 0.138 and 0.114 in the first and second rounds, respectively. Meanwhile, the chi-square test results were 148.65 and 98.908, both significant (p < 0.001). These results demonstrate that the Delphi method successfully yielded a set of expert opinions with high authority and consistency.

In the first round of expert consultation, the item importance scores ranged from 4.04 to 5.32, and the coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 0.05 to 0.33. The 20 experts provided a total of 50 suggestions, covering content additions and deletions, language descriptions, and questionnaire structure. Seven experts held different views on the definition of dimensions. Considering the overlap in the definitions of communication attitudes and abilities, the experts recommended strengthening the conceptual definitions and classifying communication competencies based on communication skills and context. In response to their feedback, the classifications of some verbal and non-verbal communication items were adjusted. Three experts suggested replacing the term “behavior” with “behavioral intention,” a recommendation that was adopted. Following the item selection criteria and taking into account expert feedback as well as internal discussions among the research team, 20 items were deleted, including “Entering the nursing ward without waiting when necessary” and “Providing channels for social support to palliative care patients and their families.” Seven new items were added, such as “Willingness to create a positive atmosphere when discussing life and death issues with patients and their families” and “Healthcare staff expressing their understanding of the quality of life and quality of death.” Additionally, five items were combined, including “Friendly attitude, gentle tone” and “Moderate volume.”

In the second round of expert consultation, the importance scores for the items ranged from 4.04 to 5.31, with CV ranging from 0 to 0.30. Three experts provided a total of three suggestions for improvement. Based on the item selection criteria and after considering the expert feedback along with internal discussions within the research team, two new items were added: “The communicator’s ability to manage their own emotions” and “Empathy skills.” The final version of the scale was determined to consist of six dimensions and 48 items, and it was identified as the clinical testing version of the Communication Attitudes and Abilities Scale.

Results of the scale’s reliability and validity testing

General information of the research subjects

A total of 485 questionnaires were distributed to healthcare staff in the oncology department of a grade-A hospital, and 444 valid responses were returned (91.5%). Among the respondents, 148 were doctors (33.33%) and 296 were nurses (66.67%). There were 113(25.45%) males and 331(74.55%) females. Their ages ranged from 26 to 55 years. Regarding professional titles, 62(13.97%) had senior titles, 232(52.25%) had intermediate titles, and 150(33.78%) had junior titles. In terms of education, 257(57.88%) had a bachelor’s degree, 144(32.43%) had a graduate degree, and 43(9.68%) had a doctoral degree. A total of 444 participants were involved in item analysis, discrimination analysis, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA).

Results of item analysis

The collected data underwent initial item analysis. The total scores of the scale were ranked from low to high, with the lowest and highest 27% of scores selected as the low and high groups, respectively, based on the extreme group method in classical test theory, which maximizes group score differentiation while retaining statistical power [19]. The score differences between the two groups were compared. Using the discrete trend test method, one item was identified as meeting the criteria for removal. Additionally, the critical ratio (CR) method indicated that another item also met the removal criteria. For the homogeneity test, the correlation coefficient method identified two items that met the criteria for deletion. Cronbach’s α coefficient for the full scale before item deletion was 0.923. Items that showed a corrected item-total correlation below 0.30, or that increased the total scale’s Cronbach’s alpha upon deletion, were considered for removal to improve internal consistency. This process follows established recommendations in scale development literature [19, 24, 25]. Based on these four methods and six criteria, the study determined that if an item did not meet at least two of the standards, it should be removed. Consequently, six items—“treatment failure,” “approaching death,” “admission to palliative care wards,” “inpatient treatment,” “bereavement planning,” and “grief emotions”—failed to meet the criteria for item analysis and were consequently removed from the scale. Following rigorous screening, the final version of the communication competency scale was established with 42 items.

Results of validity analysis

The primary method used to optimize the scale items was exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Clear criteria were established during the analysis to enhance the structure of the scale: items with multiple factor loadings, factor loadings less than 0.4, and items associated with two or fewer common factors were removed. Additionally, items with ambiguous classifications were also excluded. Following three iterative rounds of comprehensive evaluation, the structure of the scale gradually stabilized, resulting in the removal of three items and refining the scale to a total of 39 items. The analysis results indicated that the actual scale structure was highly consistent with the initially hypothesized structure. Specifically, the correlation coefficients between each dimension and the total score ranged from 0.59 to 0.76 (p < 0.05), demonstrating a strong positive correlation between the dimensions and the total score, which supported the hypothesized structure. In the expert consultation evaluation, the CVI for each item ranged from 0.78 to 1.00, with an overall CVI of 0.91 for the scale. This high CVI value suggested a strong alignment between the scale items and the research concepts, indicating that the scale effectively covered all aspects of the research concepts.

Results of reliability analysis

In assessing the internal consistency of the scale and its dimensions, the primary reliability indicators used were Cronbach’s α coefficient and Spearman-Brown coefficient. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.928, indicating significant homogeneity among the indicators. For the individual dimensions, the reliability coefficients ranged from 0.817 to 0.862, further confirming the internal consistency of the dimensions and the scale’s reliability. In evaluating test-retest reliability, the results showed high consistency across different time points. Specifically, the reliability coefficient for the total scale at two different time points was 0.89, highlighting the stability of the measurement results. For the individual dimensions, the reliability coefficients ranged from 0.69 to 0.84, indicating that these dimensions maintained a consistent measurement over time. These reliability indices provide a quantitative basis for the scale’s temporal stability.

Discussion

This study defines the concept of communication skills in palliative care through a comprehensive literature review. This description represents an individual’s evaluative nature and relatively stable psychological attitude, manifested as specific behaviors toward particular entities. It is not a singular psychological phenomenon but rather a complex psychological structure composed of cognition, emotions, behavioral intentions, empathy, and contextual communication [26]. Based on the two core elements of attitude and ability, a preliminary version of a scale was developed using existing communication-related items. The content and structure of the scale’s dimensions are clearly defined. Additionally, through an open-ended questionnaire survey targeting healthcare staff, other items were incorporated and modified to better meet the self-assessment needs of healthcare staff, making it more applicable for self-evaluation from their perspective.

Beyond its psychometric strength, the scale has significant practical utility in clinical palliative care. It offers healthcare providers a structured tool to self-assess and reflect on their communication competencies, especially in emotionally charged interactions. Educators and supervisors can use the scale to identify training needs, design targeted workshops, and evaluate the effectiveness of communication interventions. Moreover, administrators may incorporate the tool into professional development or quality improvement initiatives aimed at enhancing patient- and family-centered communication. In team-based palliative care models, the scale may also foster interdisciplinary dialogue and skill alignment among physicians, nurses, and psychosocial providers. By linking communication behaviors with core palliative values—such as empathy, clarity, and shared decision-making—this tool supports more compassionate and effective care.

The scale’s value extends beyond measurement; it serves as a practical and ethical guide to more humane communication during one of the most vulnerable stages of patient care. By assessing domains such as empathy, emotional regulation, and non-verbal expression, the tool encourages clinicians to engage with patients and families in a way that prioritizes emotional presence, trust, and respect. For example, the inclusion of items related to navigating uncertainty, delivering bad news, and responding to distress aligns with the real-world demands of palliative care conversations. In doing so, the tool supports providers in holding difficult discussions around prognosis and care planning, while remaining attuned to patients’ psychological and spiritual needs. This process may improve patient and family satisfaction, reduce miscommunication, and enhance the quality of shared decision-making. Furthermore, by facilitating structured self-reflection, the tool can deepen clinicians’ self-awareness and empathy—key components of compassionate care that often erode under the emotional pressures of oncology work. Thus, the scale has the potential not only to assess skills, but to cultivate them.

The authority and representativeness of the scale items were ensured by 20 participating experts with advanced academic qualifications and backgrounds in education, management, and clinical fields. Following two rounds of expert consultations, strong support for the content validity of the scale was obtained from the experts. The CVI for individual items ranged from 0.78 to 1.00, with an overall CVI of 0.91, reflecting a high level of consensus among experts regarding the appropriateness of the items [27].

Structural validity was verified through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and internal consistency analysis. This analysis identified six key factors that collectively explained 80.47% of the variance, indicating a strong factor structure. The factor loadings for items on their respective common factors were significantly above the 0.4 thresholds, aligning with the expected dimensional structure and further validating the scale’s structural validity. Additionally, internal consistency analysis demonstrated that the correlations between the dimensions and the total scale score were higher than those among the individual dimensions, indicating the effectiveness of the scale’s structure.

Regarding the reliability of the scale, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients indicated significant internal consistency. The overall coefficient was 0.928, while the coefficients for each dimension ranged from 0.817 to 0.862, demonstrating good internal consistency among the various dimensions of the scale. The split-half reliability coefficients ranged from 0.721 to 0.860, further confirming the scale’s stable measurement consistency. These indicators collectively validate the reliability of the scale in terms of internal consistency [28]. In summary, the development process of the communication skills and attitudes scale for palliative care in oncology is scientifically rigorous, exhibiting good validity and reliability, thereby confirming its scientific merit.

Appropriate assessment of healthcare staff’s communication skills in palliative care is crucial for ensuring effective communication [29]. One approach involves developing targeted communication models based on the interactions between caregivers and patients. Zhan et al. constructed a prognostic communication strategy using a comprehensive methodology, emphasizing five key aspects, including pre-communication assessment, preparation, skills, hope enhancement, and capacity building [30]. While this research provides valuable insights into the content of prognostic communication, many studies originate from foreign contexts, which may introduce cultural differences, varying perspectives of healthcare staff, and practical situations that may not align with those in China [31].

The second approach to measuring communication skills in palliative care involves the use of a comprehensive scale with multiple indicators [32, 33]. Gao et al. have adapted and localized a problem prompt list for patients with advanced cancer [34]. This tool is designed to assess the communication efficiency between oncologists and patients, covering nine areas: cancer treatment decisions, treatment plans, palliative care, decision-making processes, daily activities, social and family support, advanced medical directives, and care needs. However, this assessment tool lacks specific content related to communication and has not been widely adopted in China.

The communication skill assessment scale for healthcare staff in cancer palliative care developed in this study is both scientifically sound and practical. It serves as a measurement instrument for the continuous and dynamic evaluation of healthcare staff’s communication skills. However, this study was limited by its selection of healthcare staff solely from specialized oncology hospitals, which restricted the sample sources. All participants were recruited from a single tertiary oncology hospital in Zhejiang Province. While this setting provided access to experienced professionals in oncology palliative care, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutional or regional contexts. When applying this tool to assess the communication skills and attitudes of oncology healthcare staff in the community or comprehensive hospitals, it is essential to consider differences in infrastructure, human resources, and other factors, which may necessitate appropriate adjustments to the tool’s content. Future research will be performed to expand the sample size and research scope to further validate the tool’s applicability for palliative care providers in various healthcare settings.

Conclusion

The palliative care communication skill assessment score developed in this study integrates domestic perspectives on life and death, as well as the characteristics of palliative care stages. In addition to evaluating dimensions of communication ability, the content emphasizes patients’ own language expression and performance abilities, as well as the capacity to adjust communication skills in specific clinical situations. This tool comprehensively reflects the communication skills and attitude levels of healthcare staff during the palliative care phase, aligning with the operational definitions of communication skills and attitudes. Furthermore, it is suitable for assessing the communication skills and attitudes of healthcare staff when interacting with hospitalized patients and their families during palliative care. Healthcare staff can utilize minimal resources to identify factors affecting communication skills and attitudes, as well as the underlying causes influencing their behaviors, thereby facilitating accurate assessment and improvement of palliative care quality. The design of the tool emphasizes objectivity and ease of understanding, with an average completion time of approximately seven minutes. It is applicable in clinical settings, reducing participant burden and enhancing data collection efficiency.
