Is Sepsis an Essential Fatty Acids Deficiency State?
Authors: Undurti N Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is associated with significant morbidity and mortality in critical care units, for which there is no effective therapy at present. The failure of corticosteroids and cytokine-targeted therapies suggests that an out-of-the-box approach is needed to understand their pathobiology and develop effective therapy. Essential fatty acids (EFAs) and their metabolites have both pro- and anti-inflammatory actions, regulate the production and actions of cytokines, and modulate immune response. Patients with sepsis have significantly lower concentrations of gamma-linolenic acid (GLA), dihomo-GLA (DGLA), arachidonic acid (AA) of the n-6 series, and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) of the n-3 series that are derived from EFAs in their plasma phospholipid fraction. Corticosteroids inhibit the metabolism of EFAs to their long-chain metabolites in addition to suppressing the formation of eicosanoids from DGLA, AA, EPA, and DHA. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) inhibit the activities of desaturases and thus decrease the formation of GLA and AA from LA and EPA and DHA from ALA. Thus, corticosteroids and cytokines interfere with EFA metabolism, which may explain their ineffectiveness in sepsis. It is suggested that sepsis is an EFAs deficiency state that leads to dysregulated inflammation and immune response and failure of resolution of inflammation and wound healing. It is proposed that restoring EFAs and their metabolism to normal can prevent and manage sepsis and other similar inflammatory disorders/diseases.How to cite this articleDas UN. Introduction

Despite considerable advances in the understanding of the pathobiology of sepsis, morbidity and mortality remain high. Sepsis can be triggered by local/systemic infection, injury, and major surgery. Sepsis is due to ill-understood complex interactions among invading organisms, tissue damage, and dysregulation in the inflammatory response and immune response.1 At present, sepsis is managed with antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and organ support therapy with little impact on its recovery process. Corticosteroids and anti-TNF-α and anti-IL-6 therapy are of little help in sepsis.2 These failures suggest that out-of-the-box thinking is needed to understand its pathophysiology and develop more appropriate therapies for sepsis.

In this context, the potential role of essential fatty acids (EFAs) and their metabolites in sepsis needs a close look. The interaction among vitamins, corticosteroids, and cytokines and their modulatory effect on EFAs metabolism may offer an explanation as to why the current therapeutic approaches are insufficient.

Metabolism of Essential Fatty Acids

Dietary EFAs:linoleic acid (c-LA, 18:2 n-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, 18:3 n-3) are essential nutrients needed for healthy skin and immune response. EFAs metabolites include several of their long-chain fatty acids, and pro- and anti-inflammatory molecules (Fig. 1). LA, ALA, and all their derived unsaturated fatty acids are considered polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Prostaglandin E1 (PGE1), derived from DGLA and prostacyclin (PGI2) and lipoxin A4 (LXA4) from arachidonic acid (AA), are platelet anti-aggregators; whereas prostaglandins (PGs), thromboxanes (TXs), and leukotrienes (LTs) derived from AA and EPA have pro-inflammatory actions. Resolvins (RSVs), protectins, and maresins formed from EPA, DHA suppress inflammation. The balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory eicosanoids determines the outcome of the inflammatory process (3 and Fig. 1).

Enzymes Δ6 and Δ5 desaturases determine the rate of metabolism of EFAs. Desaturases are aided by several co-factors, including B vitamins, folic acid, magnesium, and vitamin C. Hyperglycemia, insulin deficiency, hyperlipidemia, and ethanol inhibit the activity of desaturases (delta 6 > delta 5). Some viruses, IL-6, and TNF-α, and gut microbiota regulate the activity of desaturases.3 The cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX-2) and lipoxygenases (LOXs) enzymes convert AA to various eicosanoids. Enzyme phospholipase A2 (PLA2) that can be stimulated by several stimuli (Fig. 1) releases PUFAs (polyunsaturated fatty acids) from the cell membrane for their utilization to generate various eicosanoids.

Interaction between EFAs and Cytokines

Essential fatty acids and their metabolites influence the generation and action of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Despite its pro-inflammatory action, PGE2 suppresses the generation of IL-6 and TNF-α. Thus, feedback regulation exists among PUFAs, eicosanoids, and cytokines, and thus, the inflammatory process is regulated.3

Interleukin-6 and TNF-α enhance PLA2 activity but suppress that of desaturases that result in decreased formation of DGLA, AA, EPA, and DHA. This results in reduced generation of PGs, LTs, TXs, LXA4, resolvins, protectins, and maresins. This will have an adverse impact on inflammation and its resolution (3, Fig. 1). This set of interactions among EFAs and their metabolites, cytokines, desaturases, and PLA2 outlines the complex nature of the inflammatory process and difficulties in reestablishing homeostasis.

Corticosteroids and EFAs Metabolism

Corticosteroids are anti-inflammatory in nature by virtue of their ability to inhibit PLA2, desaturases, COX-2, and LOX enzymes and thus reduce the release and generation of DGLA/AA/EPA/DHA, PGs, LTs, TXs, LXA4, resolvin, protectins, and maresins.4 This results in suppression of inflammation. But their (corticosteroids) continued use results in the development of a deficiency of GLA/DGLA/AA/EPA/DHA, and this leads to a decrease in the formation of LXA4, resolvins, protectins, and maresins (due to their precursor deficiency and suppression of COX and LOX enzymes). As a result of this, resolution of inflammation and wound healing are delayed. AA and LXA4, resolvins, protectins, and maresins possess anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, anti-diabetes, and anti-hypertensive actions and hence, their reduced formation due to corticosteroid use may explain their various side effects.5–14 Furthermore, corticosteroid-induced decrease in various cytokines will also affect EFA metabolism. PGE2 is necessary to induce LXA4 formation that triggers inflammation resolution. These results suggest that an optimum degree of inflammation is necessary to produce the resolution of inflammation that shifts AA conversion from PGE2 to LXA4. In a recent study, we showed that recovery from radiation-induced inflammatory process and restoration of normal homeostasis are induced by administration of GLA, the precursor of AA.14 Radiation-induced inflammation is somewhat similar to sepsis-associated inflammatory process.

Antimicrobial Action of EFAs

Inhaled microbes stimulate alveolar macrophages to release LA, GLA, and AA that are microbicidal in nature.15 NK cells, cytotoxic tumor lymphocytes, cytokine-activated killer cells, dendritic cells, and leukocytes kill microbes even if they are deficient in perforin and granzyme by releasing AA and other fatty acids. Unsaturated fatty acids form an important constituent of cytolytic granules of CTL, NK, and γδT cells.16 that induce cytolysis of tumor cells. This suggests that PLA2/PLD-induced release of unsaturated fatty acids is involved in the cytolytic action of immunocytes.

M1 and M2 Macrophages and EFAs

Macrophages have a significant role in inflammation and its resolution. M1 macrophages (pro-inflammatory in nature) kill the invading microbes, whereas M2 (anti-inflammatory in nature) aid in the resolution of inflammation and restoring homeostasis (Fig. 2). PGE2 and LTs are secreted by M1 macrophages, whereas M2 form and release IL-4 and IL-13 and lipoxin A4 (LXA4), resolvins, protectins, and maresins (reviewed in 3). This implies that the availability of AA, EPA, and DHA needs to be available in adequate amounts for macrophage function. This suggests that GLA/DGLA/AA/EPA/DHA deficiency occurs in sepsis and other serious conditions that could be responsible for the inflammation seen in these conditions.

PGE2 and LXA4 Interact to Initiate Resolution of Inflammation

As already discussed above, PGE2 triggers LXA4 generation and suppresses LTB4 production by augmenting 5- and 15-lipoxygenase expressions. This results in suppression of inflammation and initiation of anti-inflammatory events (reviewed in 3, 15).

15-PGDH– (15-prostaglandin dehydrogenase, a prostaglandin degrading enzyme) deficient mice show enhanced concentration of PGE2 in various organs of the body, including bone marrow, colon, and liver tissues, and are endowed with augmented hematopoietic capacity, liver regeneration, and enhanced recovery of hematopoiesis after tissue damage.17 Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) secrete LXA4 that aids in recovery from acute lung injury.18 Enhanced plasma and tissue concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α are seen in metabolic syndrome and the elderly subjects, despite reduced plasma levels of AA/EPA/DHA (Table 1) seen in them.19 This suggests that a delicate balance exists between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, eicosanoids, and their precursors to keep inflammation in check and aid the tissue repair process, and maintain homeostasis.

Conclusions

Corticosteroids induce an EFAs deficiency state that can result in reduced formation of PGE2, LXA4, resolvins, protectins, and maresins that are needed to suppress inappropriate inflammatory processes but also to initiate tissue repair, regeneration, and wound healing. This explains the ineffectiveness of corticosteroids in sepsis. In addition, patients with sepsis have dysregulated EFAs metabolism (Table 2). In such a scenario, employing corticosteroids further worsens the dysregulated EFA metabolism. This explains why steroids are of no significant benefit in sepsis. Hence, restoring EFA metabolism to normal, such that pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines and eicosanoids are generated in a timely fashion, will ensure recovery from sepsis. Essential fatty acids (notably GLA, AA, EPA, and DHA) should be provided along with metabolic cofactors like various vitamins, magnesium, and insulin. In view of the pulsatile and day-to-day variation in the levels of cytokines and eicosanoids, it is recommended that all these factors need to be measured frequently (at least once in 24 hours) as suggested previously.20,21 This concept also explains why administration of vitamins, especially C, B1, B6, and/or B12 alone gave variable results. Experimental studies performed in animal models of sepsis showed that LXA4, protectins, and resolvins are beneficial.22–24 Development of stable synthetic analogs of LXA4, resolvins, protectins, and maresins for their potential use in sepsis and other inflammatory conditions is important.
