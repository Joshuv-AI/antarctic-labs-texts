Oral delivery of anti-mCD80 nanobody-engineered probiotic bacterial nanovesicles for macrophage-targeted Il17ra silencing in colitis
Authors: Manli Zhang, Yonggang Zhu, Feng Hu, Min Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease remains challenging to treat because effective intervention requires localized suppression of mucosal inflammation together with restoration of tissue homeostasis. Here, a hierarchically engineered oral nanotherapeutic is developed based on Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG-derived bacterial nanovesicles (BNVs) for inflammatory macrophage-targeted gene silencing in colitis. The vesicles are genetically engineered to display an anti-mCD80 nanobody, loaded with siIl17ra, and further encapsulated within calcium alginate microcapsules to improve gastrointestinal protection and enable gastrointestinal protection and intestinal-fluid-associated release in the lower gut. The resulting system preserves nanoscale vesicular morphology, exhibits favorable cytocompatibility, and shows enhanced uptake by inflammatory macrophages after nanobody decoration. Following internalization, siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs effectively suppress Il17ra expression and reprogram macrophages toward a pro-repair phenotype. Microcapsule incorporation further improves siRNA retention, restrains premature release under acidic conditions, and promotes sustained release under intestinally relevant pH conditions. After oral administration, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs display enhanced colorectal retention and markedly alleviate dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis, as evidenced by reduced disease activity, attenuated histopathological injury, enhanced epithelial regeneration, decreased inflammatory mediator expression, and reduced NF-κB/caspase-associated marker changes. This work establishes a microbiota-inspired oral nanomedicine platform for localized immunomodulation and mucosal repair in colitis. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), primarily comprising ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a chronic relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract characterized by epithelial barrier disruption, aberrant immune activation, and maladaptive host–microbiota crosstalk. Despite the rapid expansion of the therapeutic armamentarium from conventional immunosuppressants to biologics and small molecules, a substantial proportion of patients still fail to achieve durable remission or sustained mucosal recovery. Importantly, contemporary therapeutic paradigms have moved beyond symptomatic control toward objective disease modification, including endoscopic improvement, biomarker normalization, and mucosal healing. This evolving clinical framework highlights an unmet need for locally active and mechanistically precise therapeutic systems that can intervene within the intestinal inflammatory niche while minimizing systemic toxicities and treatment-associated burden [1–5] (Scheme 1).

Schematic diagram of the anti-inflammatory functions in IBD therapy with MC-siIL17RA/CD80-BNVs

Among the cellular determinants of intestinal immune homeostasis, macrophages are now recognized as central coordinators of both inflammatory amplification and tissue repair. Under physiological conditions, intestinal macrophages contribute to epithelial maintenance, microbial surveillance, and resolution of injury; however, in IBD, inflammatory monocyte-derived macrophages accumulate within the mucosa and sustain pathological cytokine circuits dominated by TNF-α, IL-1β, and NF-κB-associated signaling. Equally important, type 17 immunity has emerged as a major pathological axis in intestinal inflammation. The IL-23/IL-17 network not only reinforces myeloid-cell activation and chronic inflammatory tone, but also reshapes epithelial responses and mucosal remodeling in a context-dependent manner. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies capable of selectively reprogramming inflammatory macrophages while simultaneously attenuating IL-17-related signaling are conceptually attractive for restoring immune homeostasis and promoting mucosal repair [6–10].

In parallel with advances in mucosal immunology, extracellular vesicles (EVs) and bacterial membrane vesicles have attracted increasing attention as biologically active nanoscale entities for disease modulation and drug delivery [11]. Bacterial vesicles are not passive by-products of microbial growth; rather, they are organized interfacial structures that mediate cargo transfer, host communication, and ecological adaptation. Their functional significance in intestinal disease has become particularly evident with the demonstration that human IgA-coated bacterial vesicles are enriched in UC and can actively contribute to inflammatory pathogenesis. At the same time, the vesicular format offers a compelling materials advantage: lipid-bilayer nanostructures inherit bioactive interfacial features from their parent source while remaining amenable to engineering for targeting, cargo loading, and controlled delivery. Recent advances in EV immunogenicity, surface functionalization, and materials-assisted delivery have further accelerated the conceptual transition of EVs from endogenous messengers to programmable nanotherapeutic systems [12–20].

For IBD, oral administration remains especially appealing because it offers the possibility of direct access to diseased intestinal segments while reducing the systemic exposure associated with parenteral biologics. However, effective oral delivery of nucleic acids and vesicle-based therapeutics remains profoundly challenging. Free siRNA is highly vulnerable to degradation, native vesicles may suffer from poor gastrointestinal stability and limited colonic retention, and the intestinal mucus barrier further constrains transport to inflamed tissue. Recent materials-based solutions—including colon-directed probiotic delivery, self-assembled siRNA systems, macrophage-polarizing nanotherapeutics, adhesive particulate depots, and oral micro/nanomotors—have demonstrated that the spatiotemporal control of intestinal therapeutics can be substantially improved through rational carrier design. Nevertheless, most existing platforms address only selected barriers, and comparatively few integrate a biologically active vesicular chassis, immune-cell selectivity, nucleic acid therapeutics, and oral protection within a single hierarchical architecture [21–32].

These considerations suggest a particularly compelling design opportunity for IBD nanomedicine. A probiotic-derived vesicle platform could provide a biocompatible and microbiota-relevant nanoscale interface; surface engineering could endow the system with enhanced affinity toward pathogenic immune subsets; RNA cargo could deliver pathway-level therapeutic precision; and an outer protective matrix could preserve functionality during gastrointestinal transit while enabling regional release in the distal gut. Within this framework, inflammatory macrophages represent an especially relevant cellular target, given their central role in sustaining cytokine cascades and delaying epithelial restitution. Likewise, Il17ra constitutes a mechanistically meaningful intervention node because it lies at the interface of type 17 inflammatory signaling and myeloid-cell responsiveness. An orally administrable system that combines inflammatory macrophage selectivity with local Il17ra silencing would therefore address both delivery and mechanism in a coordinated manner.

Accordingly, in this work we developed a multistage oral nanotherapeutic strategy based on Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG-derived bacterial nanovesicles (BNVs). The vesicles were genetically engineered to display an anti-mCD80 nanobody for preferential interaction with inflammatory macrophages, subsequently loaded with siIl17ra to suppress IL-17RA-associated inflammatory signaling, and finally encapsulated within calcium alginate microcapsules to enhance gastrointestinal protection and pH-/intestinal-fluid-associated intestinal release. We hypothesized that this hierarchically engineered platform would integrate the interfacial bioactivity of probiotic-derived vesicles, the targeting precision of nanobody decoration, the mechanistic specificity of RNA interference, and the pharmacological robustness conferred by oral microencapsulation. Through this design, the present study aims to establish a microbiota-inspired nanomedicine framework for local immunomodulation, macrophage reprogramming, and mucosal repair in colitis.

Materials and methods

Isolation and physicochemical characterization of LGG-derived bacterial nanovesicles

Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) was expanded in de Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) medium and maintained under microaerophilic conditions at 37 °C until cultures reached the late exponential to early stationary phase. To harvest cell-free conditioned broth, intact bacteria were first depleted by centrifugation at 5,000 ×g for 20–30 min at 4 °C, followed by a second clarification step at 10,000 ×g for 20–30 min to remove residual debris and large particulate matter. The supernatant was subsequently passed through sterile 0.45 and 0.22 μm membrane filters to ensure complete removal of bacterial cells [11]. The clarified broth was concentrated with a 100 kDa ultrafiltration device and then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000–150,000 ×g for 2 h at 4 °C to sediment crude vesicles. After resuspension in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the pellet was washed once or twice under identical ultracentrifugation conditions. For density-based purification, the crude vesicle fraction was further resolved by sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation at 150,000–200,000 ×g for 2–3 h. Fractions enriched in nanosized vesicles were pooled, diluted with PBS, and pelleted again to remove residual sucrose. The final preparation was resuspended in sterile PBS, quantified by a bicinchoninic acid assay, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Particle morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy. Hydrodynamic diameter and PDI were measured by dynamic light scattering. Particle concentration was determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis after appropriate dilution in filtered PBS. Zeta potential was measured using a zeta potential analyzer. For short-term colloidal stability assessment, vesicle formulations were stored at 4 °C, and PDI values were monitored over 28 days. Each measurement was performed using three independently prepared vesicle batches. Unless otherwise stated, LGG-derived extracellular vesicles are referred to herein as bacterial nanovesicles (BNVs). (Materials/instruments used in this section: MRS broth and agar, Oxoid, UK; ultrafiltration devices, Sartorius, Germany; sterile membrane filters, Merck Millipore, Germany; ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter, USA; BCA kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; transmission electron microscope, JEOL, Japan; dynamic light scattering analyzer, Malvern Panalytical, UK.)

Genetic engineering of LGG for surface display of an anti-mCD80 nanobody

To promote preferential interaction between vesicles and inflammatory macrophages, LGG was engineered to express a mouse CD80-recognizing nanobody on the bacterial surface. The expression cassette was designed to contain an N-terminal secretion signal, the anti-mCD80 nanobody coding sequence, a flexible linker, a Gram-positive cell-envelope anchoring motif of the LPXTG class, and a C-terminal FLAG epitope to facilitate downstream verification. The synthesized construct was inserted into a lactobacillus-compatible expression vector and introduced into LGG by a suitable transformation procedure. Recombinant colonies were screened for stable maintenance of the transgene, and expression of the fusion protein was preliminarily verified at the bacterial level by anti-FLAG immunoblotting and, where appropriate, by fluorescence-based surface detection.

The engineered strain was propagated under the same culture conditions as the parental LGG strain, and vesicles were isolated from conditioned medium as described above. Vesicles derived from engineered bacteria were denoted CD80-BNVs. Successful incorporation of the nanobody fusion protein into the vesicular fraction was confirmed by anti-FLAG western blotting. (Gene synthesis and plasmid construction services, GenePharma, China.)

Surface accessibility ELISA for anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG fusion protein on intact BNVs

To evaluate the surface accessibility of the anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG fusion protein on engineered BNVs, an intact-particle anti-FLAG capture ELISA was performed. Briefly, high-binding 96-well ELISA plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with anti-FLAG antibody diluted in carbonate-bicarbonate coating buffer. After washing with PBST and blocking with 1–3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, intact BNV samples were added to the wells at equal particle numbers and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The groups included blank wells, parental BNVs, FLAG-BNVs, anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs, anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs pretreated with Proteinase K, anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs without detection antibody, and anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs incubated with an irrelevant HRP-conjugated antibody. For Proteinase K treatment, intact anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs were incubated with Proteinase K before ELISA detection, followed by enzyme inactivation and washing. After capture, wells were washed thoroughly to remove unbound vesicles. Captured BNVs were detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-nanobody antibody that recognizes the anti-mCD80 nanobody domain. After incubation and washing, TMB substrate was added, and the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

mCD80-Fc binding ELISA

To evaluate the specific binding of engineered anti-mCD80 BNVs to mCD80, a recombinant antigen-binding ELISA was performed. High-binding 96-well ELISA plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with recombinant mouse CD80-Fc diluted in carbonate-bicarbonate coating buffer. BSA-coated wells, Fc-only-coated wells, and recombinant mouse CD86-Fc-coated wells were included as specificity and background controls. After washing with PBST, plates were blocked with 1–3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Intact BNV samples were then added to the wells at equal particle numbers and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The tested groups included anti-mCD80 BNVs added to BSA-, Fc-only-, mCD86-Fc-, or mCD80-Fc-coated wells, as well as parental BNVs and FLAG-BNVs added to mCD80-Fc-coated wells. Additional controls included mCD80-Fc-coated wells incubated with anti-mCD80 BNVs without detection antibody or with an irrelevant HRP-conjugated antibody. After extensive washing to remove unbound vesicles, captured BNVs were detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-FLAG antibody. TMB substrate was added for color development, and the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Competitive blocking ELISA

To determine whether the binding of anti-mCD80 BNVs to mCD80 was CD80-dependent, a competitive blocking ELISA was performed. High-binding 96-well ELISA plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with recombinant mouse CD80-Fc diluted in carbonate-bicarbonate coating buffer. After washing with PBST, plates were blocked with 1–3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Intact anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs were pre-incubated with soluble mouse CD80-Fc, soluble mouse CD86-Fc, Fc-only protein, free anti-mCD80 nanobody, or irrelevant nanobody for 30–60 min at room temperature before being added to the mCD80-Fc-coated wells. Parental BNVs and FLAG-BNVs without anti-mCD80 nanobody were included as non-targeting vesicle controls. After incubation for 2 h at room temperature, wells were washed thoroughly to remove unbound vesicles. Bound BNVs were detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-FLAG antibody, followed by TMB substrate development. The reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Relative binding was calculated after blank subtraction and normalized to the no-blocker anti-mCD80 BNV group: relative binding (%) = (OD sample − OD blank)/(OD no blocker − OD blank) × 100.

Loading of Il17ra-targeting siRNA into engineered BNVs

Chemically synthesized Il17ra-targeting siRNA was handled under strictly RNase-free conditions. Purified BNVs or anti-mCD80-decorated BNVs were first normalized by total vesicle protein content. siRNA was then mixed with BNVs in RNase-free buffer at predefined BNV protein-to-siRNA input ratios, expressed as mass ratios (w/w).

For loading, BNVs and siRNA were incubated on ice in RNase-free buffer to facilitate vesicle-associated siRNA loading. Non-associated siRNA was removed by size-exclusion chromatography or ultrafiltration, and the resulting product was designated siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs when anti-mCD80-decorated BNVs were used as carriers. After loading and purification, vesicle preparations were re-characterized for morphology, particle size distribution, and siRNA content to confirm preservation of nanoscale integrity following cargo incorporation. Encapsulation efficiency was calculated as the percentage of siRNA retained in the purified BNV fraction relative to the initial siRNA input. siRNA loading content was calculated as the amount of loaded siRNA normalized to vesicle protein content or total formulation mass, as indicated. Custom siRNA synthesis was performed by GenePharma, China, and size-exclusion columns were obtained from Izon Science, New Zealand.

Quantification of encapsulation efficiency and siRNA payload

To determine siRNA incorporation, loaded vesicles were lysed under RNase-free conditions and the amount of encapsulated Il17ra siRNA was quantified either by fluorescence-based nucleic acid measurement or by RT-qPCR using an external standard curve. Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) was calculated as the proportion of input siRNA recovered within the vesicle fraction, whereas loading capacity was expressed either as the mass of siRNA per milligram of vesicular protein or as the percentage contribution of siRNA to the final formulation mass. Measurements were performed on freshly prepared samples to minimize degradation-associated bias.

Fabrication of alginate microcapsules encapsulating engineered BNVs

To improve gastrointestinal protection and prolong local retention after oral administration, siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs were incorporated into calcium alginate microcapsules by ionotropic gelation. Briefly, sodium alginate was dissolved in RNase-free sterile PBS to obtain a homogeneous alginate solution at a final concentration of 2.0% w/v. The solution was stirred overnight at room temperature until complete dissolution and then passed through a sterile filter to remove undissolved aggregates. Purified siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs were gently mixed with the alginate solution at an input amount of 1.0 mg BNV protein per mL alginate solution, corresponding to approximately 3.8–4.0 × 10¹⁰ particles mL⁻¹ and approximately 17 µg siIl17ra per mg BNV protein based on the optimized BNV/siRNA loading ratio. The mixture was gently pipetted to generate a uniform vesicle-containing alginate suspension while avoiding vigorous vortexing or sonication.

Microcapsules were fabricated using a syringe pump-assisted ionotropic gelation method. The BNV-containing alginate suspension was loaded into a sterile syringe and extruded through a 26-gauge blunt stainless-steel needle into a 100 mM CaCl₂ crosslinking bath under gentle magnetic stirring. The flow rate was maintained at 0.5 mL min⁻¹, and the distance between the needle tip and the CaCl₂ solution surface was approximately 8 cm. The resulting droplets were allowed to crosslink for 15 min at room temperature to form calcium alginate microcapsules. After gelation, the microcapsules were collected by gentle centrifugation or natural sedimentation, washed three times with sterile PBS to remove non-encapsulated vesicles, unassociated siRNA, and excess calcium ions, and then resuspended in sterile PBS. Freshly prepared microcapsules were used immediately for in vitro release and in vivo administration experiments or stored at 4 °C for short-term use within 24 h.

Microcapsule morphology was evaluated by bright-field microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. Mean capsule diameter was quantified from at least 100 microcapsules collected from multiple independent fields using ImageJ. Vesicle encapsulation was assessed by fluorescence labeling of BNVs, vesicle-associated protein detection, and indirect quantification of non-encapsulated vesicles in the supernatant after microcapsule collection. Microencapsulation efficiency was calculated as follows: microencapsulation efficiency (%) = (input BNV amount − unencapsulated BNV amount in the supernatant)/input BNV amount ×100. The amount of vesicle-associated siRNA retained in the microcapsule fraction was quantified under RNase-free conditions after microcapsule dissolution in sodium citrate buffer and was expressed as siRNA retention efficiency relative to the initial siRNA input. Loading content was calculated as the amount of encapsulated BNVs or siRNA normalized to the total wet microcapsule mass or formulation input, as indicated.

In vitro release and stability assay under simulated gastric and intestinal conditions

To evaluate the gastrointestinal stability and pH-responsive release behavior of siIl17ra from alginate-based microcapsules, in vitro release assays were performed under simulated gastric and intestinal conditions. Free siIl17ra, MC-siIl17ra, and MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs were dispersed in release media at an equivalent siIl17ra dose and incubated at 37 °C under gentle shaking. Simulated gastric conditions were established at pH 1.5 and pH 3.0 to mimic highly acidic and moderately acidic gastric environments, respectively. Simulated intestinal fluid was prepared at pH 6.8 to mimic the intestinal phase, in which phosphate ions and intestinal components were expected to promote Ca²⁺ exchange, matrix swelling, and erosion of the alginate network.

At predetermined time points of 1, 8, 24, 48, and 72 h, aliquots of the release medium were collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium to maintain sink conditions. The released siIl17ra content was quantified using a validated nucleic acid quantification method, and the cumulative release percentage was calculated relative to the total siIl17ra amount loaded in each formulation.

Cytocompatibility, live/dead staining, and apoptosis analysis

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated with BNVs, CD80-BNVs, or siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs at 20 µg mL− 1 for 48 h. Metabolic viability was then assessed with a Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and absorbance at 450 nm was acquired using a multimode plate reader. For fluorescence-based viability visualization, cells were stained with a Calcein-AM/propidium iodide live/dead kit and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Apoptotic responses were quantified after 48 h exposure using Annexin V/PI staining followed by flow cytometry. (CCK-8 kit, APExBIO, USA; live/dead staining kit, APExBIO, USA; Annexin V/PI apoptosis kit, BioLegend, USA; multimode plate reader, Agilent, USA; flow cytometer, Agilent, USA.)

EdU incorporation assay

Cell proliferation was examined by EdU incorporation. Cells were exposed to 10 µM EdU for 24 h, fixed, and processed using a click-chemistry-based EdU imaging kit. Fluorescent images were captured by confocal microscopy. EdU-positive nuclei and total DAPI-positive nuclei were counted from five randomly selected fields per well using ImageJ, and the proliferative index was calculated as the percentage of EdU-positive nuclei relative to the total number of nuclei. (EdU imaging kit, APExBIO, USA; confocal laser-scanning microscope, ZEISS, Germany.)

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or colonic tissues using a phenol/guanidinium-based reagent. RNA purity and concentration were measured by microvolume spectrophotometry. Reverse transcription was performed using 500 ng total RNA per reaction, and quantitative PCR was carried out with a SYBR Green master mix on a real-time PCR platform according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Relative mRNA expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method using an appropriate housekeeping gene as internal reference. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. (TRI Reagent, Molecular Research Center, USA; microvolume spectrophotometer, DeNovix, USA; reverse-transcription kit and SYBR Green qPCR reagents, Yeasen, China; real-time PCR instrument, Bio-Rad, USA.)

Western blotting

BNVs and engineered vesicle preparations were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay. Equal amounts of vesicular protein were loaded in each lane and separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Because purified vesicle preparations are not cellular or tissue lysates, conventional housekeeping proteins such as GAPDH, β-actin, or Tubulin were not used as loading controls. Membranes were blocked and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and expressed relative to CD80-BNVs. (PVDF membranes, Cytiva, USA; BCA kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; primary antibodies obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Abcam, UK, and Invitrogen, USA; chemiluminescent imager, Azure Biosystems, USA.)

Cellular internalization of vesicle formulations

For uptake studies, macrophages were cultured on glass-bottom confocal dishes and incubated with DiO-labeled BNVs, CD80-BNVs, or siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs for 4 h. Following extensive PBS washing, intracellular fluorescence was examined microscopically. Quantitative analysis of vesicle internalization was performed by flow cytometry, and the resulting data were processed with FlowJo software. (DiO membrane-labeling reagent, APExBIO, USA; flow cytometer, Agilent, USA; confocal microscope, ZEISS, Germany.)

To evaluate whether the enhanced uptake of CD80-BNVs was associated with CD80-mediated recognition, LPS-activated RAW264.7 macrophages were transfected with Cd80-targeting siRNA (siCd80) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, Cd80 knockdown efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence staining. For the uptake assay, siCd80-transfected macrophages were incubated with DiO-labeled CD80-BNVs for 4 h. After extensive washing with PBS to remove non-internalized vesicles, cellular uptake was quantified by flow cytometry and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence staining

For cell-based immunofluorescence, samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% donkey serum at room temperature. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed thoroughly, and exposed to fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI before imaging by confocal microscopy.

For tissue staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval prior to blocking and antibody incubation. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical microscope settings within each experiment. Antibodies against Ki67, IL-17RA, TNF-α, IL-10, IL-1β, ARG1, CD80, and phospho-NF-κB p65 were selected according to validated mouse applications. (Primary antibodies obtained from Abcam, UK; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Invitrogen, USA; and/or R&D Systems, USA; confocal microscope, ZEISS, Germany.)

In vitro macrophage polarization assay

RAW264.7 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. To establish an inflammatory phenotype, cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng mL− 1) alone or with LPS plus recombinant mouse IL-17 A (50 ng mL− 1) for 24 h. Cells were then treated with BNVs, CD80-BNVs, or siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs (20 µg mL− 1) for an additional 48 h. Polarization status was assessed by RT-qPCR for Tnf-α, Il-1β, Arg1, and Il-10, and by immunofluorescence for representative M1 and M2 markers. Cell culture supernatants were clarified by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C before cytokine analysis by ELISA. (RPMI 1640, fetal bovine serum, and penicillin/streptomycin, Gibco, USA; LPS, Merck, Germany; recombinant mouse IL-17 A, PeproTech, USA; plate reader, Agilent, USA.)

DSS-induced colitis model and in vivo treatment

Female C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were used to establish the DSS-induced acute colitis model. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature and humidity and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to standard chow and drinking water. Mice were allowed to acclimatize for 7 days before experiments.

Acute colitis was induced by providing mice with 2.5% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; colitis grade, molecular weight 36,000–50,000 Da; MP Biomedicals, USA) in drinking water for 7 consecutive days. Fresh DSS-containing drinking water was prepared and replaced every 1–2 days. Control mice received regular drinking water throughout the experiment. Mice were randomly assigned to the Control, DSS, and DSS + MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs groups using a random-number-based allocation procedure, with six mice in each group.

For in vivo treatment, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs were freshly suspended in sterile PBS and administered by oral gavage administered by oral gavage on days 1, 3, and 5 during DSS exposure. Each mouse received 200 µL of MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV suspension containing 200 µg BNV protein and approximately 3.4 µg siIl17ra per administration. Based on the body-weight range of 18–22 g, this corresponded to approximately 9.1–11.1 mg BNV protein kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ and 0.15–0.19 mg siIl17ra kg⁻¹ day⁻¹. Mice in the Control and DSS groups received an equal volume of sterile PBS by oral gavage according to the same schedule.

Body weight, stool consistency, fecal bleeding, general appearance, and survival were monitored daily throughout the experimental period. Disease activity index (DAI) was calculated as the sum of three clinical subscores, including body-weight loss, stool consistency, and fecal bleeding. Each parameter was scored from 0 to 4, resulting in a total DAI range of 0–12. Body-weight loss was scored as follows: 0, no weight loss; 1, 1–5%; 2, 5–10%; 3, 10–15%; and 4, > 15%. Stool consistency was scored as follows: 0, normal well-formed stool; 1, slightly soft stool; 2, loose stool; 3, semi-liquid stool; and 4, watery diarrhea. Fecal bleeding was scored as follows: 0, no bleeding; 1, trace bleeding; 2, mild visible bleeding; 3, obvious bleeding; and 4, gross rectal bleeding. Higher DAI scores indicated more severe disease activity.

Animal welfare was monitored daily. Humane endpoints were predefined as body-weight loss exceeding 20%, severe or persistent rectal bleeding, marked dehydration, inability to access food or water, persistent lethargy, or severe distress. At the predetermined endpoint, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation. Blood was collected under deep anesthesia when serum biochemical analysis was required. Mice were then euthanized by exsanguination under deep anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Colon tissues and major organs were collected immediately after euthanasia. Colon length was measured from the ileocecal junction to the proximal rectum, and distal colon tissues were processed for histological staining, immunofluorescence analysis, flow-cytometric analysis, RT-qPCR, and other downstream assays as indicated.

DAI assessment, colon length measurement, histological scoring, immunofluorescence quantification, flow-cytometric analysis, RT-qPCR data analysis, and image analysis were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation whenever applicable.

In vivo intestinal distribution and ex vivo mucosal retention

For biodistribution studies, siRNA was labeled with Cy7 and formulated either as free siRNA or as MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs. Mice were orally gavaged with equivalent siRNA doses, and whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed 1, 2, 6, 12, 18, 24 h later using an in vivo imaging system. After imaging, the gastrointestinal tract was excised and imaged ex vivo to compare signal distribution in the stomach, small intestine, cecum, colon, and rectum.

For ex vivo retention experiments, freshly isolated mouse ileum, cecum, and colon were cut into approximately 1 cm segments and gently everted to expose the mucosal surface. Free Cy7-siRNA or MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs containing the same amount of Cy7-labeled siRNA was evenly applied to the tissue surface and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After two gentle PBS washes, tissue-associated fluorescence was recorded. In selected experiments, the mucus layer was scraped, suspended in PBS, homogenized, and further analyzed to estimate retention within the mucus compartment. (Cy7-labeled siRNA, GenePharma, China; in vivo imaging system, Revvity, USA.)

Histological scoring of colonic injury

H&E-stained distal colon sections were evaluated in a blinded manner to quantify histological injury. Well-oriented sections showing the full mucosal structure from the epithelial surface to the crypt base were selected for analysis. Histological injury was scored based on four parameters: epithelial integrity, crypt architecture, inflammatory cell infiltration, and goblet cell preservation. Each parameter was assigned a score from 0 to 3, where 0 indicated normal histology and 3 indicated severe pathological alteration. The total histological injury score was calculated as the sum of the four parameters, with a maximum score of 12. For each mouse, multiple representative fields were evaluated and averaged to generate one biological replicate. Scoring was performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Flow sorting of colonic macrophages and RT-qPCR

Colon tissues were minced into small fragments and digested in RPMI 1640 medium containing collagenase IV and DNase I at 37 °C for 30–45 min with gentle agitation. The digested tissues were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. After red blood cell lysis and washing, cells were stained with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies against F4/80. F4/80⁺macrophages were isolated by flow sorting and collected for RNA extraction.

Total RNA was extracted from sorted F4/80⁺macrophages using an RNA extraction reagent or kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse-transcription kit, and RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green master mix. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method and normalized to Gapdh or Actb. The analyzed genes included Il17ra, Tnf-α, Il-1β, Arg1, and Il-10.

Serum biochemical analysis

At the experimental endpoint, mice from the Ctrl, DSS, and MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV-treated groups were anesthetized, and blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis. After clotting at room temperature, blood samples were centrifuged to obtain serum. Liver function-related indicators, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and total bilirubin (TBIL), as well as kidney function-related indicators, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA), were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer or commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The number of biological replicates or animals is indicated in each figure legend. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used when data were normally distributed. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was performed. For body-weight and DAI curves measured over time, two-way ANOVA with repeated measures followed by multiple-comparison correction was used when appropriate. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

Results and discussion

Engineering and characterization of macrophage-targeting CD80-BNVs and siIl17ra-loaded vesicles

We first established an engineered bacterial nanovesicle platform based on Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG), aiming to integrate cell-selective recognition with RNA interference-mediated immunomodulation. To this end, an anti-mCD80 nanobody was genetically fused to a secretion/surface-display module containing a signal peptide, a flexible linker, a Gram-positive anchoring motif, and a FLAG epitope, thereby enabling its presentation on the bacterial envelope and, consequently, on vesicles shed from the engineered strain (Fig. 1A). This design was intended to confer preferential affinity toward inflammatory macrophages with elevated CD80 expression, while preserving the intrinsic biocompatibility and membrane-associated bioactivity of probiotic-derived vesicles.

After vesicle isolation from conditioned culture medium, transmission electron microscopy revealed that the engineered nanovesicles retained a well-defined nanoscale morphology, appearing as rounded or slightly cup-shaped membranous structures with discernible boundaries (Fig. 1B). Such morphology is consistent with the general ultrastructural profile of extracellular vesicles and supports the integrity of the isolation procedure. Dynamic light scattering further showed a relatively narrow size distribution in the nanometer regime (Fig. 1C), indicating that the vesicles maintained a colloidally favorable size range for cellular interaction and cargo transport. To further strengthen the physicochemical characterization of the vesicle formulations, nanoparticle tracking analysis showed that the particle concentrations of BNVs and siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs were 4.1 × 1010 and 3.8 × 1010 particles mL− 1, respectively, indicating that nanobody engineering and siRNA loading did not markedly compromise vesicle recovery (Figure S1). In addition, BNVs and siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs exhibited negative zeta potentials of −21.85 and −24.69 mV, respectively, together with low PDI values, supporting their preserved surface charge characteristics and relatively homogeneous colloidal dispersion after engineering and cargo loading (Figure S1).

Western blot analysis detected the expected nanobody-associated fusion signal in vesicles harvested from engineered LGG, confirming successful transfer of the anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG construct to the vesicular fraction (Fig. 1D and Figure S2B). However, because immunoblotting requires vesicle disruption, this result alone could not determine whether the nanobody fusion protein was exposed on the outer surface of intact BNVs. To further assess surface accessibility, we performed an intact-particle anti-FLAG capture ELISA using equal particle numbers of different BNV formulations. Anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs showed a strong ELISA signal, with an OD450 value of approximately 1.52 ± 0.04, whereas blank wells, parental BNVs, FLAG-BNVs, no-detection-antibody controls, and irrelevant HRP-antibody controls showed only background-level signals. Notably, Proteinase K pretreatment markedly reduced the ELISA signal of anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG BNVs to approximately 0.29 ± 0.01 (Fig. 1E). Because intact BNVs were captured through the FLAG epitope and detected through the anti-mCD80 nanobody domain, these results indicate that both domains of the fusion protein were accessible on intact engineered BNVs. The marked signal decrease after Proteinase K treatment further supports the external exposure of the fusion protein rather than its exclusive localization within the vesicle lumen.

We next loaded Il17ra-targeting siRNA into the engineered vesicles to generate the therapeutic formulation siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs (Fig. 1F). The loading conditions were expressed as BNV protein-to-siRNA input ratios (w/w). Systematic evaluation of loading conditions showed that siRNA encapsulation efficiency increased as the BNV protein-to-siRNA input ratio increased, while siRNA loading content reached a peak at approximately 60:1 (w/w). This condition provided the most balanced formulation, combining high encapsulation efficiency with favorable siRNA loading content and preserved vesicle integrity. Therefore, the 60:1 (w/w) BNV protein: siRNA input ratio was selected for subsequent preparation of siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs.

From a materials-design perspective, this platform combines several features that are highly desirable for inflammatory disease nanomedicine: a biologically derived membrane interface, an active targeting module, and a nucleic acid therapeutic cargo. Recent work in the extracellular-vesicle field has emphasized that membrane-derived nanocarriers can retain biologically meaningful interfacial properties while functioning as modular delivery vehicles for therapeutic payloads [33]. In the context of intestinal inflammation, such a design is particularly attractive because it enables simultaneous exploitation of vesicle-mediated cellular communication and selective transcript-level intervention in inflammatory immune cells.

Favorable cytocompatibility, enhanced uptake by activated macrophages, and efficient Il17ra silencing

Based on the confirmed incorporation and surface accessibility of anti-mCD80-Nb-FLAG on engineered BNVs, we then examined whether this surface engineering translated into improved macrophage targeting. Western blot analysis verified the presence of the anti-mCD80 nanobody fusion protein in the engineered vesicle fraction (Fig. 2A and Figure S2C). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that all vesicle formulations could be internalized by RAW264.7 cells, reflecting the inherent phagocytic competence of macrophages (Fig. 2B). Notably, upon inflammatory activation with LPS, CD80-BNVs and siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs displayed substantially higher uptake than unmodified BNVs (Fig. 2C), consistent with the intended role of the anti-mCD80 nanobody in enhancing vesicle interaction with CD80-enriched inflammatory macrophages.

To determine whether the surface-displayed anti-mCD80 nanobody retained functional antigen-binding activity, we performed an mCD80-Fc binding ELISA using intact BNVs (Figure S3A). anti-mCD80 BNVs showed a strong binding signal when incubated with immobilized mCD80-Fc, with an OD450 value of approximately 1.68 ± 0.05. In contrast, only background-level signals were observed when anti-mCD80 BNVs were added to BSA-coated wells, Fc-only-coated wells, or mCD86-Fc-coated wells, with OD450 values of approximately 0.09 ± 0.003, 0.13 ± 0.006, and 0.18 ± 0.007, respectively. Similarly, parental BNVs and FLAG-BNVs showed low binding signals on mCD80-Fc-coated wells, with OD450 values of approximately 0.11 ± 0.004 and 0.15 ± 0.005, respectively. The no-detection-antibody and irrelevant HRP-antibody controls also remained close to background levels. These results indicate that engineered anti-mCD80 BNVs selectively bind immobilized mCD80-Fc, but not mCD86-Fc, Fc-only, or BSA controls, supporting the functional accessibility and antigen-binding specificity of the surface-displayed anti-mCD80 nanobody.

To further confirm the CD80-dependent binding specificity of anti-mCD80 BNVs, we performed a competitive blocking ELISA using immobilized mCD80-Fc (Figure S3B). anti-mCD80 BNVs showed strong binding to mCD80-Fc in the absence of competitor, with an OD450 value of approximately 1.70 ± 0.04, which was defined as 100% relative binding. Pre-incubation with soluble mCD80-Fc markedly reduced the binding signal in a competitor dose-dependent manner, decreasing relative binding to approximately 34% at 10× competitor and 12% at 50× competitor. Free anti-mCD80 nanobody also substantially inhibited BNV binding, reducing the relative binding to approximately 19%. In contrast, soluble mCD86-Fc, Fc-only protein, and irrelevant nanobody did not substantially block binding, with relative binding values remaining at approximately 91%, 94%, and 90%, respectively. Parental BNVs and FLAG-BNVs showed only background-level binding to immobilized mCD80-Fc, corresponding to approximately 3% and 5% relative binding, respectively. These results demonstrate that anti-mCD80 BNV binding to immobilized mCD80-Fc is specifically inhibited by soluble mCD80-Fc and free anti-mCD80 nanobody, but not by mCD86-Fc, Fc-only, or irrelevant nanobody controls, supporting CD80-dependent binding specificity.

To further determine whether this enhanced uptake involved CD80-mediated recognition rather than nonspecific phagocytosis alone, Cd80 was knocked down in LPS-activated RAW264.7 macrophages using siCd80. RT-qPCR confirmed a marked reduction in Cd80 mRNA expression, and immunofluorescence staining further verified decreased CD80 protein expression after siCd80 transfection (Figure S3C-E). Importantly, the enhanced uptake of CD80-BNVs observed in Fig. 2C was significantly attenuated after siCd80-mediated Cd80 knockdown in LPS-activated macrophages, supporting a CD80-dependent component in the internalization of engineered BNVs (Figure S3F). These results support the contribution of CD80-dependent recognition to the enhanced internalization of engineered BNVs, while indicating that baseline vesicle uptake may still occur through the intrinsic phagocytic activity of macrophages.

Consistent with their enhanced uptake, siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs produced a clear knockdown effect at both the transcript and protein-associated readout levels, as shown by qPCR and immunofluorescence analyses (Fig. 2D–F). These data indicate that the engineered vesicles are not merely internalized passively, but are also capable of delivering functional siRNA into recipient cells. This is an important distinction, as many nanocarriers exhibit robust cellular association without effective cytosolic availability of nucleic acid cargo. Here, the observed Il17ra suppression suggests that the BNV-based system supports biologically productive delivery.

Before evaluating therapeutic function, we assessed whether vesicle engineering and siRNA loading altered the biosafety profile of the nanoplatform. CCK-8 analysis showed that treatment with BNVs, CD80-BNVs, or siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs did not induce a measurable reduction in RAW264.7 viability (Fig. 3A). This finding was corroborated by live/dead staining, in which the overwhelming majority of cells remained calcein-positive and propidium iodide-negative across all treatment groups (Fig. 3B). Consistently, EdU incorporation analysis revealed no appreciable suppression of proliferative activity (Fig. 3C, D), and Annexin V/PI flow cytometry demonstrated an absence of overt apoptosis induction after vesicle exposure (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these results indicate that neither nanobody decoration nor siRNA incorporation imposed detectable acute cytotoxicity under the tested conditions.

The combination of minimal cytotoxicity, increased uptake by activated macrophages, and measurable target-gene knockdown establishes a strong functional foundation for the downstream anti-inflammatory studies. More broadly, these data support the concept that selective modulation of macrophage subpopulations may widen the therapeutic window by concentrating activity within pathogenic immune niches instead of indiscriminately suppressing the entire immune compartment. Given the central role of macrophage plasticity in both inflammatory amplification and tissue repair, such selectivity is likely to be especially advantageous in intestinal inflammatory disorders [34].

CD80-BNVs and siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs reprogram inflammatory macrophages toward a reparative phenotype

We next investigated whether the vesicle formulations could modulate macrophage phenotype under inflammatory conditions. In an LPS-driven macrophage activation model, treatment with BNVs already induced a degree of immunoregulatory remodeling, as reflected by partial suppression of pro-inflammatory markers and modest enhancement of reparative markers. This observation is notable, because it suggests that probiotic-derived vesicles are not inert carriers but possess an intrinsic immunomodulatory capacity, likely arising from their membrane composition and bacteria-derived bioactive constituents. Against this background, CD80-BNVs and siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs produced substantially stronger effects, leading to marked downregulation of the M1-associated genes Tnf-α and Il-1β and concurrent upregulation of the M2-associated genes Arg1 and Il-10 (Fig. 4B). ELISA measurements were in good agreement with the transcriptional data, showing decreased TNF-α and IL-6 secretion together with increased ARG-1 and IL-10 release (Fig. 4C). Immunofluorescence imaging further confirmed the attenuation of the pro-inflammatory TNF-α signal and the augmentation of IL-10 expression (Fig. 4D, E).

These findings indicate that nanobody-mediated targeting enhances the ability of LGG-derived vesicles to reshape macrophage behavior, likely by increasing the effective vesicle–cell interaction frequency within the inflammatory subset. Importantly, the magnitude of this phenotypic shift suggests that the engineered system is not simply dampening activation in a nonspecific manner, but actively redirecting macrophages toward a pro-resolution state. Such phenotypic repolarization is highly relevant in mucosal inflammatory diseases, where excessive persistence of inflammatory myeloid cells not only sustains cytokine production but also delays epithelial restitution and tissue recovery [1].

To further probe the contribution of the Il17ra silencing module, we challenged macrophages with LPS and IL-17 simultaneously, thereby creating a more stringent inflammatory context in which IL-17 signaling could participate directly in sustaining macrophage activation. Under these conditions, siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs outperformed CD80-BNVs, producing a more pronounced decrease in Tnf-α and Il-1β expression and a stronger induction of Arg1 and Il-10 (Fig. 5B). Secreted cytokine profiles mirrored these differences, with the siIl17ra/CD80-BNV-treated group exhibiting lower TNF-α and IL-6 but higher ARG-1 and IL-10 than the CD80-BNV group (Fig. 5C). Immunofluorescence imaging again confirmed weaker TNF-α staining and more robust IL-10 expression in the presence of siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs (Fig. 5D, E).

This result is mechanistically significant. Whereas nanobody decoration mainly improves cellular selectivity and delivery efficiency, Il17ra silencing addresses a specific inflammatory signaling node downstream of vesicle uptake. In other words, the formulation integrates targeted access and targeted intervention within one platform. The superior performance of siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs in the LPS/IL-17 co-stimulation model supports the notion that IL-17-related signaling contributes to stabilization of the inflammatory macrophage program in this setting. Thus, by interrupting this axis, the engineered vesicles can more effectively release macrophages from a self-reinforcing inflammatory state and shift them toward a reparative phenotype.

Alginate microencapsulation improves cargo retention, enhances gastric stability, and enables intestinal release

Although vesicle-based delivery improved macrophage targeting and intracellular gene silencing, oral administration imposes additional constraints, including gastric degradation, premature cargo leakage, and limited retention in the distal intestine. To address these barriers, we encapsulated siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs into calcium alginate microcapsules via ionotropic gelation, thereby generating a secondary protective matrix for gastrointestinal transit (Fig. 6A). Bright-field imaging showed that the microcapsules retained a regular spherical morphology after vesicle incorporation, without obvious collapse, fusion, or irregular fragmentation (Fig. 6B). This observation indicates that the microencapsulation procedure is structurally compatible with the vesicle formulation and does not induce gross physical destabilization of the carrier system.

(B) Representative bright-field images of alginate microcapsules before and after vesicle incorporation. (C) Mean diameter of MC and MC/siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs. (D) Comparison of encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) between direct siRNA encapsulation and vesicle-mediated siRNA loading followed by microcapsule formulation. (E) In vitro siIl17ra release profiles of free siIl17ra, MC-siIl17ra, and MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs under simulated gastric conditions at pH 1.5. (F) In vitro siIl17ra release profiles under simulated gastric conditions at pH 3.0. (G) In vitro siIl17ra release profiles under simulated intestinal fluid at pH 6.8. The mean diameters of the MC and MC/siIl17ra/CD80-BNV formulations were determined to be 421.8 μm and 428.7 μm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 6C. A direct comparison of cargo-loading strategies showed that pre-packaging siRNA into BNVs before microcapsule incorporation yielded clearly higher encapsulation efficiency and loading efficiency than direct entrapment of naked siRNA into the alginate matrix (Fig. 6D). This difference likely arises from the fact that free siRNA is highly susceptible to diffusion loss during microcapsule formation, whereas vesicle preloading provides an additional nanoscale compartment that retains nucleic acid cargo during the encapsulation process. Put differently, the BNVs function not only as biological delivery vectors but also as a secondary cargo-confinement unit within the polymeric matrix. This hierarchical loading strategy is conceptually important because it decouples nucleic acid retention from the limitations of polymer entrapment alone.

The release and stability assays further demonstrated the gastrointestinal protective function of alginate microencapsulation. Under simulated gastric conditions, acidic pH markedly suppressed premature siIl17ra release from the microcapsule formulations. At pH 3.0, MC-siIl17ra showed a sustained release pattern, with cumulative release increasing from 12.5 ± 0.9% at 1 h to 49.8 ± 2.7% at 72 h. In comparison, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs exhibited a slower release profile, reaching only 30.4 ± 2.2% at 72 h (Fig. 6F). This gastric-retention effect was further enhanced at pH 1.5, where MC-siIl17ra and MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs released 33.8 ± 2.2% and 20.4 ± 2.1% of siIl17ra at 72 h, respectively (Fig. 6E). By contrast, free siIl17ra displayed rapid diffusion-dominated release under acidic conditions and approached nearly complete release within 48–72 h.

Under simulated intestinal fluid at pH 6.8, siIl17ra release from the microcapsule systems was markedly accelerated. MC-siIl17ra released 46.1 ± 2.4% at 1 h, 81.9 ± 3.3% at 8 h, and 96.3 ± 1.9% at 24 h, followed by near-complete release at 72 h. MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs maintained a more sustained release profile but still achieved substantial intestinal release, increasing from 19.0 ± 1.4% at 1 h to 76.0 ± 3.6% at 24 h and 98.6 ± 1.4% at 72 h (Fig. 6G). These findings indicate that the alginate-based microcapsules effectively restrained premature siIl17ra leakage under gastric acidic conditions while enabling accelerated payload release under intestinally relevant conditions.

Mechanistically, this behavior is consistent with the pH- and intestinal-fluid-associated properties of calcium alginate networks. Under acidic gastric conditions, the alginate matrix remains relatively compact, thereby limiting swelling and cargo diffusion. In contrast, exposure to intestinally relevant pH and phosphate-containing medium may promote Ca²⁺ exchange, matrix swelling, and gradual loosening or erosion of the alginate network, resulting in enhanced siIl17ra release. However, these in vitro results should not be interpreted as evidence that pH is the only determinant of microcapsule degradation in vivo. After oral administration, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs are exposed to a considerably more complex intestinal environment, including gut microbiota, microbial enzymes, mucus, variable ionic strength, intestinal motility, and inflammation-associated biochemical cues. These factors may collectively contribute to microcapsule swelling, matrix relaxation, local degradation, and vesicle release in DSS-induced colitis. Therefore, the release behavior of MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs is more appropriately described as microenvironment-associated rather than purely pH-controlled. Future studies using microbiota-depleted or germ-free mice, ex vivo fecal fermentation/release assays, and inflammatory intestinal fluid-mimicking systems will be required to quantitatively define the individual contribution of microbiota- and inflammation-associated cues to alginate microcapsule degradation and vesicle release.

From a translational standpoint, this multistage design addresses several major obstacles in oral nucleic acid therapy simultaneously: instability of free siRNA, insufficient mucosal residence time, and poor localization to diseased intestinal segments. In inflammatory bowel disease, durable local exposure and mucosal healing are increasingly recognized as central therapeutic goals rather than secondary endpoints [1]. The microcapsule-assisted vesicle system developed here is therefore relevant not only as a formulation improvement, but as a disease-oriented delivery strategy that aligns with current therapeutic priorities.

MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs exhibit superior colorectal retention and therapeutic efficacy in DSS-induced colitis

We next assessed whether the formulation advantages observed in vitro translated into improved in vivo performance (Fig. 7A). Following oral administration of Cy7-labeled preparations, whole-body IVIS imaging demonstrated a substantially stronger abdominal fluorescence signal in the MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV group than in mice receiving free siRNA (Fig. 7B and Figure S8). Ex vivo imaging of the gastrointestinal tract further showed that fluorescence was preferentially retained in the colon and rectum in the microcapsule-treated group, whereas free siRNA produced only weak and sparse signal in the lower intestine (Fig. 7B). These results indicate that microencapsulation, together with vesicle-based packaging, markedly enhances the persistence of the therapeutic cargo in the distal gastrointestinal tract.

This redistribution is highly relevant for ulcerative colitis-oriented intervention, because disease burden is concentrated in the colon and rectum, and therapeutic benefit often depends on achieving sufficient residence time precisely in these segments [35]. The present biodistribution data therefore provide more than a formulation readout: they establish a plausible mechanistic link between materials design and therapeutic effect. Specifically, the microcapsules appear to protect the cargo during transit through the upper gastrointestinal tract and facilitate its delayed release in lower intestinal regions, thereby increasing the probability of local interaction with inflammatory immune cells.

Consistent with these imaging results, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs significantly alleviated disease manifestations in the DSS model. Compared with untreated DSS mice and the corresponding controls, mice receiving the engineered microcapsule formulation displayed markedly reduced body-weight loss, a lower disease activity index, and partial preservation of colon length (Fig. 7C-F). These improvements indicate that the therapeutic platform not only attenuates overt inflammatory symptoms but also mitigates structural consequences of colitis. Because colon shortening is a robust surrogate of inflammatory injury severity in DSS models, its preservation suggests effective suppression of tissue-destructive inflammatory processes rather than merely transient symptomatic relief.

Histological analysis reinforced this conclusion. MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment substantially reduced epithelial disruption, inflammatory infiltration, and crypt damage, while maintaining a more continuous mucosal architecture than that seen in DSS controls (Fig. 8A and Figure S4). In parallel, Ki67 immunofluorescence revealed increased proliferative activity within colonic tissue (Fig. 8B), suggesting enhanced epithelial regeneration. These findings are particularly important in light of the current emphasis on mucosal healing as a meaningful therapeutic benchmark in inflammatory bowel disease [1]. The present formulation therefore appears to act not only by dampening inflammation, but also by creating a tissue environment more permissive for epithelial restitution.

To avoid overinterpreting histological improvement as functional barrier restoration, we further examined a panel of barrier- and repair-associated epithelial genes in colonic tissues (Figure S5). DSS challenge markedly reduced the expression of tight-junction and epithelial-barrier genes, including Tjp1, Ocln, Cldn1, Cldn4, and Cdh1, together with mucus-layer and mucosal repair-associated genes such as Muc2 and Tff3. Treatment with MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs partially restored the expression of these genes, suggesting improvement of barrier-associated epithelial transcriptional programs. In addition, the epithelial proliferation marker Mki67 was increased compared with DSS-treated mice, supporting a repair-associated epithelial response. These results indicate that MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs promoted histological improvement and barrier-related gene recovery in DSS-induced colitis.

In vivo anti-inflammatory remodeling is associated with rebalanced macrophage programs and reduced NF-κB/caspase-associated inflammatory markers

To determine whether siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs effectively reduced target-gene expression in colonic macrophages in vivo, colon tissue-derived single-cell suspensions were prepared from DSS-treated mice, and F4/80⁺macrophages were isolated by flow sorting for RT-qPCR analysis. DSS challenge increased Il17ra expression in F4/80⁺macrophages, whereas MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment significantly reduced Il17ra mRNA levels, supporting effective target-gene silencing in the macrophage compartment (Figure S6).

We next examined how the therapeutic formulation reshaped the macrophage inflammatory phenotype in the injured colon. RT-qPCR analysis of sorted F4/80⁺macrophages showed that MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs markedly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, including Tnf-α and Il-1β, while concomitantly increasing the reparative macrophage-associated genes Arg1 and Il10 (Fig. 8C). Consistently, flow-cytometric phenotyping of colon tissue-derived immune cells showed a decreased proportion of CD86⁺ macrophages and an increased proportion of CD206⁺ macrophages within the F4/80⁺macrophage population after MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment (Figure S7). These results indicate that MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs not only reduced Il17ra expression in colonic macrophages but also shifted the macrophage compartment from a pro-inflammatory state toward a more reparative phenotype in DSS-induced colitis.

This observation is significant for two reasons. First, it provides in vivo confirmation of the macrophage-reprogramming effects observed in vitro, indicating that the vesicle platform remains functionally active after oral delivery and colonic deposition. Second, it suggests that the therapeutic outcome is unlikely to arise from simple cytokine suppression alone. Instead, the data are more compatible with a state transition in local immune regulation, in which inflammatory macrophage-associated programs are attenuated while reparative pathways are amplified. Given the well-established plasticity of macrophages and their central role in coordinating inflammatory escalation versus tissue repair, such phenotypic rebalancing offers a mechanistically coherent explanation for the simultaneous reduction in disease severity and improvement in epithelial recovery [34].

We further found that treatment reduced the colonic expression of Caspase-1 and Caspase-4 and markedly attenuated phospho-NF-κB p65 staining (Fig. 8G, H and Figure S9). These findings are consistent with an overall reduction in NF-κB- and caspase-associated inflammatory readouts after MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment. Because NF-κB activation and inflammatory caspase activity are important contributors to cytokine amplification and inflammatory tissue injury in colitis, these marker-level changes may partially explain the reduced inflammatory burden observed in treated mice. However, the present data are based on Caspase-1/4 transcript levels and phospho-NF-κB p65 immunofluorescence staining and therefore should not be interpreted as direct evidence of pathway inhibition or causality.

To preliminarily evaluate the in vivo biosafety of the therapeutic formulation, major organs were collected after treatment and examined by H&E staining. No obvious histopathological abnormalities, including inflammatory infiltration, tissue necrosis, or structural disruption, were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney of MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV-treated mice compared with control mice (Figure S10A). Serum ALT levels were increased in DSS-treated mice compared with Ctrl mice (61.10 ± 12.20 vs. 39.85 ± 9.66 U/L), while MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment reduced ALT levels to 42.43 ± 7.28 U/L. Serum AST levels were also elevated after DSS challenge (164.58 ± 21.47 U/L) compared with the Ctrl group (108.45 ± 12.70 U/L), and showed a decreasing trend after MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment (139.58 ± 29.41 U/L). Other hepatic indicators, including ALP and TBIL, showed no significant differences among groups. Renal function indicators, including BUN and CREA, also remained comparable across the Ctrl, DSS, and MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV-treated groups. Together, these serum biochemical results suggest that MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment did not exacerbate DSS-associated hepatic or renal biochemical abnormalities under the tested conditions (Figure S10B). These findings suggest that the oral administration of MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs did not induce apparent tissue-level toxicity in the major organs under the tested conditions.

Taken together, the in vivo data support a multistage therapeutic model. Following oral administration, alginate microcapsules protect the vesicle payload during gastrointestinal transit and prolong its retention in the distal intestine. CD80-directed vesicle recognition promotes preferential interaction with inflammatory macrophages, while the siIl17ra cargo contributes to attenuation of IL-17RA-associated inflammatory responses. These effects were accompanied by reduced NF-κB- and caspase-associated marker changes, restoration of a more reparative macrophage phenotype, and improved epithelial regeneration. Such an integrated effect is particularly attractive for colitis therapy, where disease progression is sustained by both immune dysregulation and defective barrier repair [35, 36].

Overall, this study establishes a hierarchically engineered oral nanotherapeutic system in which probiotic-derived vesicles, immune-cell targeting, RNA interference, and pH-responsive microencapsulation act in concert. Rather than functioning as independent modules, these components appear to reinforce one another: vesicles improve biological interfacing, nanobody decoration sharpens cellular selectivity, siRNA provides mechanistic specificity, and alginate microcapsules confer pharmacological robustness during oral delivery. The resulting platform therefore represents a promising strategy for localized immunomodulation in inflammatory bowel disease and, more broadly, a versatile blueprint for microbiota-inspired nanomedicine.

Although the present study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs in a DSS-induced colitis model, several limitations of this model should be acknowledged. The acute DSS model is primarily driven by chemically induced epithelial injury, disruption of the intestinal barrier, and secondary innate immune activation. Therefore, it is particularly useful for evaluating mucosal injury, inflammatory amplification, epithelial repair, and local drug-delivery performance in the inflamed colon. However, this model does not fully recapitulate the chronic, relapsing, and heterogeneous pathogenesis of human inflammatory bowel disease, which involves complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, adaptive immunity, intestinal microbiota, epithelial dysfunction, and environmental factors. Accordingly, the therapeutic effects observed here should be interpreted as evidence of efficacy in an acute colonic injury/inflammation setting rather than as a complete representation of chronic IBD.

Despite this limitation, the DSS model remains relevant for the present oral nanotherapeutic strategy because it reproduces several clinically meaningful features of ulcerative colitis-like intestinal injury, including body-weight loss, diarrhea, rectal bleeding, colon shortening, epithelial disruption, and inflammatory cytokine production. These features provide a useful experimental context for evaluating whether an orally administered vesicle-based formulation can survive gastrointestinal transit, accumulate in the distal intestine, modulate local inflammatory macrophage responses, and support mucosal repair. Future studies using chronic or relapsing DSS protocols, chemically induced immune-mediated models, and genetically susceptible colitis models will be necessary to further validate the durability, long-term safety, and translational robustness of this platform.

Compared with previously reported oral EV- or BNV-based strategies for intestinal inflammation, the present platform provides a more actively engineered and macrophage-oriented design. Native probiotic-derived EVs and other naturally occurring oral vesicle systems have shown therapeutic potential in experimental colitis, largely through their intrinsic immunomodulatory bioactivity, modulation of the gut microbiota, or interaction with intestinal epithelial and immune cells. However, these systems generally lack disease-cell-selective targeting and do not deliver a defined gene-silencing payload. In contrast, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs integrate alginate microencapsulation, anti-mCD80 nanobody-mediated macrophage recognition, and Il17ra-targeting siRNA loading into a single oral formulation. This design is intended to address several major barriers of oral RNA delivery, including gastrointestinal exposure, limited local retention, insufficient inflammatory-cell selectivity, and inefficient intracellular delivery.

Mechanistically, the revised data support a stepwise delivery-to-function relationship. The engineered vesicles exhibited enhanced uptake by LPS-activated macrophages, and this uptake was attenuated after siCd80-mediated Cd80 knockdown, supporting a CD80-dependent component in vesicle internalization. In vivo, flow-sorted F4/80⁺colonic macrophages showed reduced Il17ra expression after MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNV treatment, indicating target-gene silencing within the macrophage compartment. This was accompanied by reduced Tnf-α and Il-1β expression, increased Arg1 and Il-10 expression, decreased CD86⁺ macrophage proportion, and increased CD206⁺ macrophage proportion. Therefore, rather than acting only as a passive vesicle carrier, the engineered formulation links macrophage-directed delivery with target-specific Il17ra silencing and macrophage phenotype remodeling in the inflamed colon.

Nevertheless, the present study remains a proof-of-concept evaluation in an acute DSS-induced colitis model. Although the data show the evidence for macrophage-directed delivery, Il17ra silencing, and macrophage phenotype modulation, future studies using chronic or relapsing colitis models, direct intestinal permeability assays, long-term safety evaluation, and more stringent manufacturing-scale quality control will be necessary to further define the translational robustness of this oral engineered BNV platform.

Another limitation is that the present study did not quantitatively dissect how intestinal microbiota or the local inflammatory microenvironment influences alginate microcapsule degradation and vesicle release in vivo. Although the in vitro release study demonstrated pH- and intestinal-fluid-associated release behavior, the actual release process after oral administration is likely governed by multiple factors, including microbial activity, mucus interaction, ion exchange, intestinal fluid composition, peristalsis, and inflammation-associated biochemical changes. Future studies using microbiota-depleted or germ-free mice, ex vivo fecal fermentation/release assays, and inflammatory intestinal fluid-mimicking systems will be necessary to define the relative contribution of microbiota and local inflammatory cues to microcapsule degradation and BNV release.

Conclusion

A hierarchically engineered oral nanotherapeutic platform based on Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG-derived bacterial nanovesicles has been established for colitis therapy. By integrating anti-mCD80 nanobody-mediated inflammatory macrophage targeting, siIl17ra delivery, and calcium alginate microencapsulation, the resulting system achieves enhanced macrophage uptake, effective Il17ra silencing, favorable immunomodulatory activity, and pH-/intestinal-fluid-associated intestinal release after oral administration. In the DSS-induced colitis model, MC-siIl17ra/CD80-BNVs significantly mitigate disease severity, improve mucosal architecture, promote epithelial regeneration, and remodel the colonic inflammatory microenvironment toward a pro-repair state (Scheme 1). Collectively, this work highlights the therapeutic promise of combining probiotic-derived vesicles, surface bioengineering, RNA interference, and oral microencapsulation in a unified nanomedicine strategy for localized treatment of inflammatory bowel disease. Nevertheless, because the current in vivo evaluation was performed in an acute DSS-induced colitis model, further validation in chronic, relapsing, and immune-mediated colitis models will be required to define the long-term therapeutic durability and translational applicability of this oral nanotherapeutic platform.
