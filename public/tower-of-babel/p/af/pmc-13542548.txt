Research progress on agricultural water information sensing and applications in high-altitude cold regions
Authors: Yang Yang, Yikai Xu, Libin Su, Yonggang Guo, Yongtao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgricultural water management is essential for food security in high-altitude cold regions, such as the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, the Rocky Mountains, and the Alps. However, extreme environmental conditions—including low temperatures, intense radiation, and complex terrain—impede traditional hydrological monitoring. This narrative review systematically evaluates “space-air-ground” multi-source sensing technologies, summarizing recent advances in satellite remote sensing, low-altitude Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), and cold-resistant ground sensors to address these constraints. Beyond individual platforms, this study synthesizes how multi-source data fusion and edge computing enhance hydrological modeling and decision-making for regional drought monitoring, crop assessment, and precision irrigation. Finally, current challenges—such as insufficient cross-platform integration, limited data consistency, and poor model generalization—are critically analyzed, and future research directions are proposed to provide a theoretical framework for advancing smart irrigation.Keywords: high-altitude cold regions, multi-source remote sensing, smart water conservancy, precision irrigation, sensor adaptability, space-air-ground integration

1 Introduction

With the intensification of global climate change, the spatial-temporal maldistribution of water resources and regional food security issues in high-altitude cold regions have become increasingly prominent. 1 As ecologically fragile zones, these regions rely heavily on effective agricultural water management to ensure food supply. Furthermore, advancing this management requires linking hydrological practices to broader environmental monitoring challenges, such as mitigating water quality degradation and tracking agricultural contamination pathways under extreme climate stress.2–4 However, traditional manual monitoring and extensive irrigation can no longer meet the demands for the high-quality development of modern agriculture and animal husbandry. 5

In recent years, the rapid advancement of information technology has catalyzed a digital and intelligent transformation in agricultural water management. While the integration of the Internet of Things (IoT), Artificial Intelligence (AI), and remote sensing provides innovative tools,6,7 discussions in existing literature are often too general, assuming ideal lowland operational conditions. In high-altitude environments, AI must be specifically tailored for autonomous error correction to counteract freeze-thaw sensor drift, and IoT edge computing must be optimized for fragmented terrains with severely limited communication bandwidths.

The unique physical conditions of high-altitude cold regions pose formidable challenges to agricultural informatization. 8 Extreme environmental stressors—including frigid temperatures, freeze-thaw cycles, and intense radiation—frequently degrade monitoring equipment. Additionally, fragmented topography complicates device deployment and causes significant communication signal attenuation. 9

To address these imperatives, the “space-air-ground” integrated sensing system has emerged as a critical technological pathway. At the spatial scale, satellite remote sensing provides large-scale time series for soil moisture retrieval and drought monitoring. Regionally, low-altitude Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) compensate for satellite limitations in cloud-prone areas by enabling refined surface information acquisition. 10 At the ground scale, cold-resistant smart sensors and multi-source data fusion enhance the reliability of real-time data collection under extreme conditions. 11

Despite these advancements, persistent issues remain regarding multi-source data fusion complexity, sensor data drift, and poor model applicability in high-altitude microclimates 12 Furthermore, while existing reviews have documented smart irrigation frameworks for lowland agricultural systems, they typically treat “space-air-ground” components as isolated units. Therefore, the novelty of this review lies in its specific focus on the survivability and synergistic integration of multi-source sensing technologies under severe high-altitude environmental stressors, bridging the gap between theoretical remote sensing and actual hydraulic systems. 13

To address these gaps, this narrative review systematically evaluates agricultural water sensing in high-altitude cold regions. The explicit objectives are to: (1) analyze the physical limitations of current sensing hardware in plateau environments; (2) synthesize recent progress in space-air-ground multi-source sensing; and (3) critically evaluate how multi-source data fusion enhances drought monitoring and precision irrigation. This review is guided by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). 14 To ensure a comprehensive synthesis, our search strategy involved screening databases including Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and CNKI for literature published between 2015 and 2026, targeting keywords such as high-altitude agriculture, smart water management, and multi-source remote sensing.

This chapter aims to transition from the qualitative narrative of “environmental harshness” toward defining the specific “parameterizable constraints” that must be rigorously addressed in the contexts of sensor network deployment, remote sensing retrieval, and the construction of hydrological models. 15 These parameterizable constraints and typical hydro-meteorological variations on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau are illustrated in Figure 1.

2.1 Hydro-climatic constraints

Extreme climatic conditions fundamentally constrain the stable operation of sensing equipment, directly shortening hardware lifespan and causing data failures. 16

(1) Thermal Stress from Frigid Temperatures and Diurnal Fluctuations: Drastic diurnal swings (e.g., -40°C to 40°C) impair electronic insulation and induce severe zero-point drift. Physically, this drift arises from the temperature-dependent nature of semiconductor carrier mobility and the thermal expansion mismatch among internal components. 17 These thermal stresses alter the intrinsic resistance of the circuitry, causing a baseline signal shift that degrades the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and obscures true hydrological fluctuations. 18 Moreover, extreme cold inhibits chemical reactions in power systems (e.g., lithium batteries), causing sulfation, increased internal resistance, and sharp capacity loss—drastically reducing sensor node standby time. 19

2.2 Topographic complexity and remote sensing scaling constraints

The complex geomorphology of global high-altitude cold regions—from the Tibetan Plateau to the Andes and the Alps—imposes strict requirements on spatial scales and resolution for agricultural water information. 22

(1) Topographic Fragmentation and Rugged Terrain: These regions feature extreme altitudinal gradients and interlacing mountain-valley systems. Agricultural lands are mostly distributed in “small-patch” or “ribbon-like” patterns, showing high spatial heterogeneity with many small plots. 23

(2) Scale Effects and Resolution Limitations in Remote Sensing: In highly fragmented landscapes, traditional medium-to-low resolution satellite data (e.g., 30 m) suffer from severe mixed pixels, causing blurred boundaries, area extraction errors, and spectral confusion with adjacent land covers. In fragmented plateau farmlands, the use of 30 m resolution imagery (e.g., Landsat-8) is limited by significant scaling effects. Quantitative studies indicate that mixed pixels can reduce crop classification accuracy by 10–20% compared to homogeneous plains. 24 Furthermore, uncorrected topographic shading (slope-aspect effects) can induce over 25% deviation in surface reflectance values. 25 These errors directly impact hydrological retrieval; for instance, the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of soil moisture products has been observed to increase from 0.04 m3/m3 in flat areas to over 0.07 m3/m3 in complex terrains. 26 Additionally, persistent cloud/fog cover in mountainous microclimates severely limits optical satellite image availability.

2.3 Complexity of hydrological processes

The agricultural hydrological cycle in high-altitude cold regions is far more than a simplistic “precipitation-infiltration-evaporation” model; rather, it is a complex non-linear system profoundly intervened by cryospheric water transformations (the phase changes of solid-state water).

(1) Glacial Meltwater and Seasonal Runoff Dynamics: Rivers and agricultural irrigation sources in these regions are highly contingent upon glacial melt and seasonal snowmelt. Controlled by rising temperatures during the spring and summer, the meltwater process imparts a characteristically high temporal heterogeneity and seasonal pulsing to surface runoff. 28 Consequently, monitoring systems must possess high sensitivity to capture critical temperature thresholds and abrupt runoff fluctuations.

This violent energy exchange causes actual evapotranspiration (ETa) in high-altitude cold regions to exhibit exceptionally pronounced diurnal fluctuations. Traditional static empirical formulas, typically operating on daily or monthly scales, fail to accurately characterize these high-frequency dynamic processes. This necessitates the integration of high-temporal-resolution dynamic hydrological modeling with real-time observational data. 29

2.4 Constraints in information acquisition and transmission

In high-altitude pastoral regions with vast spans and fragile infrastructure, constructing a stable agricultural water IoT system faces dual bottlenecks in communication and energy supply. 30

(1) Communication Challenges in Complex Terrains: Physical obstruction in mountain-valley systems restricts line-of-sight transmission, and extreme cold worsens cable attenuation (e.g., coaxial loss spikes at -30°C). To ensure robust data backhaul, the system must shift to a heterogeneous self-healing hybrid network integrating Wireless Mesh and LPWAN (LoRa, NB-IoT). In dead zones, BeiDou Short Message Service (BDS-SMS) is essential for critical data relay. 31

3.Key “space-air-ground” integrated sensing technologies for agricultural water conservancy in high-altitude cold regions

To counteract the severe environmental and topographic constraints detailed in Section 1 that render conventional single-tier monitoring inadequate, constructing a heterogeneous synergetic sensing system—characterized by “Wide-area spatial coverage, refined aerial inspection, and precise ground-based point positioning”—represents the core methodology for breaking through regional information bottlenecks. 33 The integrated “space-air-ground” information sensing network is illustrated in Figure 2.

Integrated “space-air-ground” information sensing network. The multi-tier platform conceptualizes the synergy among space-based remote sensing, air-based UAV imagery, and ground-based IoT sensors, coupled with their respective heterogeneous data transmission links.

Space-borne sensing primarily leverages polar-orbiting and geostationary satellites to provide long-term, wide-coverage data support for regional water resource evolution and drought assessment.34,35

3.1 Soil moisture (SM) retrieval

To overcome persistent cloud/rain limitations of optical remote sensing in high-altitude cold regions, a synergistic active-microwave and optical retrieval strategy is commonly adopted. Using Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 optical data, researchers extract backscattering coefficients and vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI). The Water Cloud Model (WCM) —which conceptually simplifies the vegetation canopy as a uniform layer of water droplets—isolates canopy scattering by dynamically estimating Vegetation Water Content (VWC), while bare soil scattering models, such as the Oh model(an empirical semi-physical model relating radar backscatter to soil roughness and moisture), are coupled to compensate for surface roughness. For instance, when applying this coupled WCM-Oh framework to semi-arid meadow and dune environments—landscapes highly representative of plateau ecotones—studies have demonstrated significant reliability. Validated against field test data, this optimized inversion strategy achieved robust accuracy, yielding R2= 0.74 and RMSE = 0.045 m3/m3. 36

3.1.2 Vegetation growth sensing

Time-series NDVI from emergence to peak growth captures phenological differences across sowing dates. The Modified Soil Adjusted Vegetation Index (MSAVI) reduces soil background noise under intense radiation. Using Landsat 8/9 OLI NDVI dynamics and dual-threshold rules (growth slope and inter-temporal difference), fine crop states (e.g., early vs. late sowing of maize/sunflowers) are identified.

3.1.3 Drought monitoring

A national platform integrates the “precipitation-hydrology-moisture-agriculture-drought” chain, producing daily 1 km × 1 km drought maps. It fuses meteorological, hydrological, remote-sensing (Temperature Condition Index, NDWI) and soil moisture indicators. In the 2022 Yangtze River basin drought, the relative error for affected cropland area was 4.3%, greatly improving irrigation scheduling and drought relief decisions.

Critical Evaluation of Space-borne Sensing: Despite these multi-scale advancements, the application of satellite platforms in plateau environments is fundamentally constrained by the inherent trade-off between spatiotemporal resolution and signal penetration. Optical sensors suffer from prolonged data gaps due to persistent alpine cloud cover, rendering them unreliable for tracking rapid, snowmelt-driven hydrological events. Conversely, microwave sensors only reflect top-soil moisture (0–5 cm). Without robust assimilation frameworks to extrapolate these surface observations, satellite-derived indices remain physically decoupled from deep root-zone water stress, limiting their direct utility for precision irrigation.

3.2 Aerial sensing: Fine-grained near-surface inspection via Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs)

UAVs serve as the core of aerial detection in the “space-air-ground” framework, utilizing “below-cloud observation” to compensate for satellite limitations (e.g., low resolution and cloud interference). In fragmented high-altitude terrains like the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, UAVs offer indispensable flexibility, centimeter-level precision, and multi-payload integration for precision water management. 37

3.2.1 Synergetic monitoring via high-overlap multispectral and thermal infrared sensors

UAVs equipped with multispectral and thermal infrared (TIR) sensors acquire ultra-high-resolution imagery (1–5 cm/pixel) to monitor fragmented plots. To overcome the “salt-and-pepper effect” in single-band data, fusing vegetation indices with texture features significantly enhances accuracy. Notably, rigorous evaluations demonstrate that by fusing spectral and texture features to mitigate the “salt-and-pepper effect,” machine learning classifiers such as SVM (Support Vector Machine) can achieve a robust and realistic overall accuracy of approximately 92%. 38 For soil moisture, algorithms like Random Forest (RF) retrieve 0–20 cm moisture with high precision. Furthermore, TIR payloads capture canopy-ambient temperature differentials to compute the Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI), enabling meter-scale detection of early drought symptoms and irrigation micro-leakages.

3.2.2 3D modeling and micro-topography perception in complex terrains

High-altitude topographic relief renders 2D monitoring insufficient. UAV oblique photogrammetry generates high-fidelity Digital Surface/Terrain Models (DSM/DTM) to reconstruct mountain-valley micro-topography, reducing area survey errors to within 5%. These 3D models, especially when coupled with vegetation-penetrating LiDAR, are indispensable for calculating runoff directions, assessing snowmelt scour risks, and optimizing irrigation canal layouts.

3.2.3 Task-driven flexible response and early warning

UAVs provide high-frequency, task-driven inspections to mitigate satellite information lag during hazardous weather. Real-time conversion of UAV data into the Vegetation Condition Index (VCI) enables automated early-warning for crop vigor decline. Crucially, UAV operations offer 4–5 times the data acquisition efficiency of manual inspections—vital for sparsely populated plateaus. Integrating UAV-based inspection and plant protection can achieve water savings exceeding 30%.

Despite their unmatched spatial precision and task-driven flexibility, UAV applications face severe operational bottlenecks in extreme plateau conditions. The most critical limitation is the drastic degradation of lithium battery performance under low temperatures and hypoxic conditions, which slashes flight endurance and restricts the coverage area per sortie. Furthermore, the massive volume of high-fidelity data generated creates a significant computational bottleneck. Relying solely on off-site cloud processing is impractical due to the weak communication infrastructure in remote mountainous regions, dictating an urgent need for lightweight, on-board edge computing algorithms capable of real-time feature extraction.

3.3 Ground-based sensing: Intelligent sensor networks for extreme environments

As the core layer for data acquisition in the “space-air-ground” monitoring framework, the reliability and accuracy of ground-based sensing directly determine the overall system performance. To cope with the extreme conditions typical of high-altitude cold agricultural regions – such as low temperatures, intense radiation, large diurnal temperature fluctuations, freeze-thaw cycles, and weak power supply – systematic reinforcement is required. This includes careful selection of sensor components, low-drift compensation, multi-source energy management, and physical protection, all aimed at ensuring long-term autonomous operation of sensor networks in unattended environments. 39

3.3.1 Low-temperature tolerance and low-drift design

To withstand the extreme cold in plateau agricultural areas (down to -30°C), modern sensor nodes widely adopt military-grade electronic components (operating range -55°C to 125°C) to reduce temperature-induced drift at the source. Additionally, ultra-low-power self-heating diaphragms temporarily raise local temperatures during extreme cold spells, ensuring sensitive components survive freeze-thaw cycles. Although robust hardware provides the “ground truth,” merely deploying cold-resistant sensors is insufficient. Raw data acquired in high-altitude environments is frequently subjected to unique meteorological biases. For instance, plateau phenomena like the “cloud edge enhancement effect” can cause intense short-term spikes in solar radiation, while extreme thermal stress often induces severe baseline drift in field pyranometers and optical sensors. If these physical anomalies are not critically filtered via physics-aware error correction algorithms, errors propagate exponentially into downstream crop evapotranspiration (ETc) models.

3.3.2 Hybrid energy harvesting and low-power management

Given the weak power grid coverage and frequent prolonged cloudy or snowy weather in agricultural areas, existing solutions often adopt a hybrid energy harvesting architecture that combines wind, solar, and thermoelectric sources. For the solar component, low-temperature tolerant triple-junction gallium arsenide cells with anti-icing coatings are used. Wind turbines are typically of the vertical-axis design with low-temperature sealed bearings. Thermoelectric generation, based on the Seebeck effect, harvests temperature gradients by burying the hot side below the permafrost layer while exposing the cold side to the ambient air. The three sources are coordinated by an intelligent energy management module, with a low-temperature lithium battery pack serving as the energy storage buffer.

In terms of energy management strategies, most studies adopt a dynamic scheduling mechanism based on battery state of charge and weather forecasting. For example, during energy-sufficient periods (sunny daytime with moderate wind speeds), the sampling frequency is increased to once every 15 minutes. When the battery level drops below a threshold or when prolonged cloudy or snowy weather is forecast, the system automatically switches to a low-power mode (e.g., sampling once per hour and entering deep sleep between samples). The specific execution logic of this dynamic scheduling is illustrated in the energy management algorithm strategy (see Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material). With such strategies, the system can operate autonomously for more than five years without external power supply, effectively avoiding power interruptions under extreme weather conditions.

3.3.3 Physical encapsulation and fatigue resistance

The physical encapsulation of sensor nodes must withstand ultraviolet radiation, low-temperature embrittlement, and the repeated mechanical stresses caused by freeze-thaw cycles. In terms of materials, existing solutions often use titanium alloys (e.g., TC4) whose coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) matches that of common structural steel. This limits the deformation at encapsulation interfaces under large temperature swings (-40 °C to 40 °C) to within ±0.1 mm, preventing fatigue cracking of solder joints or sealing interfaces due to thermal mismatch. Regarding sealing techniques, a combination of laser welding and a fluororubber O-ring achieves an IP68 protection rating. Fluororubber remains elastic even at -60°C, effectively suppressing internal condensation caused by the “breathing effect”. Filling the sensor interior with high-purity argon (dew point below -70°C) further eliminates the risk of freezing due to condensation.

4. Multi-source data fusion and intelligent processing methods

In high-altitude and cold regions, extreme low temperatures, strong ultraviolet radiation, and rugged terrain often cause single-source sensing methods (e.g., ground-based monitoring alone or satellite remote sensing alone) to suffer from missing data or insufficient accuracy. This chapter focuses on how the integration of “space-air-ground” technologies, combined with edge computing and machine learning, can address the challenges of data acquisition in extreme environments. 40

Given the problems of cloud contamination in remote sensing images due to variable weather over high-altitude irrigation districts, and the poor representativeness of ground stations, multi-scale data synergy has become the core solution. 4.1.1 Image completion across spatial and temporal resolutions

Spatiotemporal adaptive fusion models (e.g., ESTARFM) bridge monitoring gaps by computing spatial weights from cloud-free base image pairs (e.g., MODIS and Sentinel-2) to predict high-resolution data on target dates. This reconstructs continuous vegetation index (e.g., NDVI) time series for short-cycle crops. However, its application in high-altitude regions is limited by the scarcity of cloud-free base images during rainy seasons and its linear assumption, which fails to capture non-linear surface dynamics like rapid snowmelt. 41

4.1.2 Point-to-area data assimilation

Recent research trends favor the use of ensemble Kalman filters (EnKF) to assimilate ground-truth point-scale soil moisture data obtained from low-temperature-resistant sensors into satellite-derived area-scale remote sensing models. This effectively corrects retrieval biases over complex terrain and significantly improves the robustness of water monitoring in high-altitude irrigation districts.

4.2 Edge sensing and data reliability in extreme environments

In remote high-altitude areas, expensive satellite bandwidth and network fluctuations caused by severe cold pose major technical bottlenecks.

4.2.1 Edge-cloud collaborative computing for bandwidth constraints

Transmitting raw, high-fidelity data via unstable, narrow-band plateau networks (e.g., BeiDou) is highly inefficient. Therefore, research has shifted from centralized cloud processing to “Edge AI.” By deploying lightweight feature extraction algorithms directly on terminal nodes, only critical water-demand indicators are transmitted. This reduces communication payload by over 60%, alleviating bandwidth bottlenecks and ensuring data reliability during extreme weather disruptions.

4.2.2 Link redundancy and heterogeneous networking

In extreme environments, a single communication link is highly prone to failure. A current optimization strategy is to build a heterogeneous network architecture combining LoRa, BeiDou, and 5G(their comprehensive performance under extreme environments is evaluated in Table S1 of the Supplementary Material). The literature review indicates that self-healing networking technologies with path-optimization capabilities can effectively overcome the damage to ground base stations caused by freeze-thaw cycles, thereby ensuring continuous transmission of water-related information.

4.3 Data-driven agricultural water modeling and decision making

Moving from traditional empirical formulas to data-driven models is an inevitable trend for improving the accuracy of water demand prediction in cold regions.

4.3.1 Application of machine learning in environmental parameter retrieval

Comparative studies show that Random Forest (RF, an ensemble learning method utilizing multiple independent decision trees) and Support Vector Machines (SVM, a classifier that maps data to a high-dimensional space to find an optimal separating hyperplane) outperform traditional regression in cold regions by intrinsically capturing non-linear, high-dimensional relationships among topographic and spectral features without strict parametric assumptions. While integrating multi-source parameters via advanced ensemble models significantly improves soil moisture content (SMC) retrieval, 42 as quantified by the comprehensive performance comparison across multiple algorithms in Table 3, these data-driven models face inherent limitations. Specifically, they are highly prone to overfitting when training samples are sparse—a common challenge in plateau environments—and fundamentally lack physical interpretability. 43

4.3.2 Characterizing crop water requirements under physical constraints

Given the unique physiological stresses in high-altitude regions, the latest modeling trend is to incorporate physical constraints (e.g., the Penman-Monteith equation) into deep learning models such as long short-term memory (LSTM) networks. This hybrid approach, combining a “black-box model” with physical mechanisms, can more accurately simulate crop transpiration changes under intense ultraviolet radiation and drought stress, thereby providing an algorithmic foundation for precision irrigation.

5.Typical application scenarios

With the deep integration of sensing hardware and intelligent algorithms, agricultural water information perception technologies have transitioned from theoretical research to practical application in several typical scenarios. In particular, in cold, high-altitude, and arid or semi-arid regions, these applications have effectively alleviated the constraints imposed by resource and environmental limitations on agricultural development.

5.1 Hydrological drought monitoring

Hydrological drought monitoring is the primary task of agricultural water management. Using multi-source remote sensing data to retrieve soil moisture and constructing drought indices enable large-scale early warning of drought conditions.

Soil Moisture Retrieval:Studies show that fusing active microwave (e.g., Sentinel-1 SAR) and optical remote sensing (e.g., Sentinel-2) data significantly improves retrieval accuracy. In a case study from the Hedong sandy area of Ningxia, researchers used the Water Cloud Model combined with a random forest (RF) algorithm to retrieve soil water content at depths of 5–20 cm. The coefficient of determination (R2) reached 0.75–0.85, effectively overcoming the limitations of a single sensor caused by surface roughness effects.

Drought Index Application:In emergency drought monitoring, the temperature-vegetation drought index (TVDI) derived from MODIS data has proven effective in reflecting regional drought distribution. For example, in the Tao River Basin, by calculating the TVDI range and overlaying it with agricultural land use vector data, it is possible to produce detailed maps and statistics of drought impacts on different land types – such as irrigated farmland, rainfed cropland, and grassland – thereby providing accurate decision support for emergency response.

5.2 Crop condition perception

Crop condition perception combines ground- and aerial-based sensing to obtain real-time information on crop growth, development, and pest/disease status. This serves as a prerequisite for precision pesticide application and variable-rate fertilization.

Growth Monitoring: Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) technology, with its high resolution and flexibility, has become a core tool for crop monitoring. By equipping UAVs with multispectral sensors, vegetation indices can be extracted and crop physiological parameter models can be constructed, enabling rapid assessment of crop coverage, leaf area index (LAI), and other traits, thereby promoting the digital transformation of agricultural production. 44

Disease and Pest Identification: Deep learning algorithms have demonstrated strong capabilities in identifying crop diseases and pests under high-altitude conditions. In a study on soybean pest holes in Nyingchi, Tibet (a high-altitude area), the researchers improved the YOLOv5s algorithm by introducing dilated convolutions to extract irregular edge information and incorporating a spatial attention mechanism to capture color features against complex backgrounds. Experimental results showed that the improved model achieved an average identification accuracy of 95.24 % for soybean leaf pest holes, while the model size was only 15.1 MB, making it easy to deploy on mobile monitoring devices. In addition, significant progress has been made in identifying rice leaf diseases using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). By employing data augmentation and transfer learning strategies, a good balance between identification accuracy and model parameter size has been achieved. 45

5.3 Precision irrigation decision making

Precision decision making converts sensed data into actionable instructions. In complex high-altitude environments, building a robust management system requires seamlessly integrating macroscopic hydrological sensing with field-scale agronomic applications.

Macroscopic Sensing and Data Integration: The foundation of sustainable water management lies in multi-dimensional data integration across highly heterogeneous catchments. Recent advances emphasize combining remote sensing with local environmental and socio-cultural data. For instance, in fragile high-altitude calcareous wetlands, integrating Sentinel-2 spectral indices (e.g., NDWI) with geochemical and hydrochemical assessments has proven crucial for detecting early-stage surface moisture degradation and evaluating the ecosystem’s buffering capacity46,47 Furthermore, operationalizing remote sensing for water security requires coupling biophysical Earth observations with hydrosocial governance frameworks, demonstrating that effective management depends on both technical monitoring and culturally embedded resilience. 48

Field-Scale Precision Execution: Built upon this catchment-scale hydrological context, field-scale models translate environmental data into precise agronomic instructions. Modern environment-driven models utilize real-time substrate monitoring (e.g., automatic weighing units) combined with meteorological parameters to dynamically regulate irrigation. Concurrently, integrated water-fertilizer drip technologies allow for precise nutrient supply during critical crop growth stages. Ultimately, bridging basin-scale ecological monitoring with plot-scale irrigation algorithms forms a closed-loop system, ensuring optimal water allocation in cold and high-altitude regions.

6. Challenges and future perspectives

6.1 Major challenges

Despite initial successes, the operational application of agricultural water perception in high-altitude cold regions faces severe challenges, primarily regarding platform coordination and data heterogeneity. For instance, the massive scale mismatch between coarse satellite microwave products (e.g., 36 km SMAP) and centimeter-level UAV imagery severely restricts synchronous observations, as current systems lack robust spatial downscaling mechanisms, particularly in highly heterogeneous cold alpine terrains. 49 Additionally, multi-source data heterogeneity—such as varying sensor viewing angles and solar illumination times—exacerbates Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF) effects during radiometric calibration. 50 These specific inconsistencies frequently result in geometric misregistration and ambiguous physical interpretations, confusing the spectral signatures of bare soil and tilled cropland in highly fragmented alpine terrains.

Poor adaptability of sensing devices to extreme environments.The typical conditions of high-altitude regions – such as low temperature, low air pressure, and strong ultraviolet radiation – have a profound influence on sensor materials, structures, and accuracy. Extremely low temperatures combined with repeated freeze-thaw cycles (from –40 °C to 40 °C) cause material embrittlement, frost-heave cracking of concrete, and failure of structural expansion joints. In addition, the low pressure and hypoxia at high altitudes significantly degrade the insulation performance and service life of ordinary electronic components.

Weak model generalization and interpretability. Physical models usually rely on complex inputs and struggle to adapt to areas with marked variations in climatic conditions and cropping systems. Data-driven models, although flexible, perform unstably when transferred across regions and lack sufficient interpretability. Furthermore, global climate simulation results over the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau still exhibit considerable uncertainty; limited by coarse spatial resolution, they cannot accurately capture local-scale micrometeorological and convective features.

High promotion costs and shortage of specialized talent.The relatively high initial investment required for smart water conservancy systems imposes a financial burden on farmers, and system installation and maintenance demand professional technical support. This field involves many advanced technologies such as the Internet of Things, big data, and artificial intelligence, resulting in a high technical threshold, while the capacity to accept and apply new technologies in vast rural areas is limited. The current training system for interdisciplinary professionals is insufficient, and the shortage of talent who possess knowledge of both water engineering and information technology is becoming increasingly acute, thereby constraining technological innovation.

6.2 Future perspectives

To address the above challenges, future research and applications of agricultural water information perception and intelligent management in cold and high-altitude regions should focus on the following breakthrough directions.

Building a standardized observation network with high spatiotemporal synergy. Remote sensing data acquisition will move more rapidly toward high spatiotemporal synergy, with emphasis on developing key band payloads such as hyperspectral, thermal infrared, and microwave. Low-power, high-density ground sensor networks will be deeply integrated with satellite microwave data, significantly enhancing the capability for dynamic and continuous monitoring of soil moisture and crop physiological status.

Developing specialized intelligent hardware and novel anti-freeze materials. To cope with deformation caused by extreme temperature differences, sensor housings should preferentially use titanium alloy (TC4) whose thermal expansion coefficient matches that of steel, thereby addressing structural mismatch problems from a material mechanics perspective. For hydraulic structures and foundation anti-seepage, locally available mineral resources can be exploited to develop low-cost basalt fiber reinforced polymer (BFRP), improving the durability of overall engineering under freeze-thaw cycles.

Deepening the hybrid paradigm of physical mechanisms and intelligent models. Remote sensing and hydrological modeling will move toward a fusion of mechanism and intelligence. For example, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) can embed physical laws such as energy conservation as constraints into a data-driven framework, thereby enhancing model generalization and interpretability. At the irrigation decision-making level, a combined model based on generative adversarial networks and gated recurrent units (GAN-GRU) can form a closed loop of data augmentation and time-series prediction, effectively filling missing sensor data and improving irrigation precision. The introduction of digital twin technology will also support virtual validation of irrigation schemes, promoting the digital and intelligent transformation of decision-making.

Improving data sharing mechanisms and socialized service systems. A national or provincial-level smart water conservancy data sharing platform should be established to enable centralized data management and shared application, break down departmental barriers, and increase data utilization efficiency. At the same time, it is necessary to strengthen the development of relevant specialties in universities and vocational colleges, optimize curricula, and carry out industry-university-research joint training to meet the demand for interdisciplinary talent in the development of smart water conservancy. On the business model side, innovative equipment leasing services and the development of agricultural socialized service organizations can lower the upfront investment threshold, thereby clearing the final channel for the transformation of scientific and technological achievements.

7. Conclusions

This review synthesizes the paradigm shift in agricultural water sensing in high-altitude cold regions, highlighting the transition from isolated monitoring to a multi-dimensional “space-air-ground” integrated framework. Severe environmental stressors dictate that isolated technical improvements are insufficient; instead, a synergistic design encompassing hardware adaptation, data assimilation, and algorithmic compensation is essential. Deeply coupling physical mechanisms with data-driven models is critical to overcome terrain-induced errors and data sparsity, thereby enhancing the accuracy of precision irrigation. Future research must prioritize three core directions to overcome cross-platform integration and generalization challenges: developing specialized extreme-environment materials to ensure long-term sensor stability under severe thermal and freeze-thaw stress; establishing synergistic satellite-UAV observation networks with robust spatial downscaling; and advancing decision intelligence by driving the dual “mechanism + artificial intelligence” paradigm toward functional Digital Twins. This integrated approach will catalyze smart water conservancy and climate adaptation in plateau agro-ecosystems.
