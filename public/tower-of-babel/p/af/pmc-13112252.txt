Edelweiss callus culture extracted exosome for chronic wound healing
Authors: Lucas Basmage Machado, Hee-Jin Ahn, Hosung Choi, Carlos Roberto Antonio, Alessandro Louza Alarcão, Shirley Nogueira Silva, Zeisa Teixeira Hohl, Jovian Wan, Kyu-Ho Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic lower-extremity ulcers remain difficult to treat, particularly in patients with metabolic and vascular comorbidities. This multicentre case series describes four patients with chronic lower-extremity ulcers that persisted for at least 6 months despite conventional management. The study evaluated topical application of edelweiss callus culture extract–derived exosomes (P198 Exo-HL FILCORE SB PLUS, Primoris International Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) used as an adjunct to standard wound care. All ulcers had persisted for at least 6 months despite conventional management. After wound cleansing and debridement as required, exosomes (0.5–1.0 mL; approximately 0.1 mL/cm²) were applied directly to the wound bed at monthly visits, followed by non-adherent sterile dressings. Clinical progress was documented by serial photography and wound measurements. Arterial and venous Doppler ultrasonography was performed at baseline and at 3-month intervals to assess changes in perfusion and venous reflux. Across the series, all patients demonstrated progressive granulation and re-epithelialisation without treatment-related adverse events. Early size reduction was observed within 15 days in one case, complete granulation and re-epithelialisation occurred within 60 days in another, and near-complete closure was achieved over 5–7 months in a third. In a severe post-infective ulcer for which amputation had been recommended, complete closure was achieved over approximately 6 months. Doppler parameters improved in all cases, including reductions in resistive index and venous reflux time and/or increases in peak systolic velocity. This small, uncontrolled series suggests that topical exosome therapy may be a feasible adjunct in refractory chronic ulcers, with concurrent improvements in clinical healing and Doppler-assessed vascular dynamics. Larger controlled studies are required to define efficacy and optimal treatment schedules.Keywords: Exosomes, Chronic wounds, Venous ulcer, Tissue regeneration, Doppler ultrasonography

Introduction

Chronic wounds are among the most difficult conditions encountered in outpatient and hospital-based wound practice. They impose a substantial burden on patients, caregivers, and healthcare systems because they are painful, functionally limiting, susceptible to infection, and frequently recurrent.1, 2, 3, 4 Venous leg ulcers, diabetic foot wounds, mixed arterial-venous ulcers, post-infectious soft-tissue defects, and pressure-related lesions are particularly challenging because they arise in compromised tissue environments where oxygen delivery, inflammatory resolution, matrix turnover, and cell migration are all impaired.1,2 The result is a wound that fails to move efficiently through the normal stages of repair and instead becomes trapped in a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation, bacterial burden, protease excess, and defective tissue reconstruction.3,4

Normal cutaneous wound healing depends on a tightly coordinated sequence of haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, angiogenesis, granulation, epithelial migration, and remodelling.5,6 Chronic wounds diverge from this sequence through persistent inflammatory signalling, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, fibroblast senescence, and inadequate extracellular matrix deposition.5, 6, 7 In practice, even high-quality standard care including debridement, compression, infection control, off-loading, vascular optimisation, topical dressings, and nutritional support may fail to achieve complete wound closure in a substantial subset of patients.1, 2, 3, 4,7 This therapeutic gap has encouraged exploration of biologically active adjuncts capable of reprogramming the wound microenvironment toward repair rather than persistence.

Extracellular vesicles, including exosomes, have emerged as promising biologic mediators in regenerative medicine because they act as nanoscale carriers of proteins, lipids, messenger RNAs, microRNAs, and other signalling molecules that can influence neighbouring and distant cells.8, 9, 10 In cutaneous injury, extracellular vesicles have been implicated in all major phases of healing, including inflammatory resolution, endothelial activation, fibroblast migration, collagen deposition, and matrix remodelling.8, 9, 10 Their biological appeal lies in the possibility of reproducing some of the paracrine effects of cell therapy without the practical and regulatory complexity of transplanting living cells. A growing body of experimental work suggests that extracellular vesicles can enhance re-epithelialisation, angiogenesis, and wound closure in preclinical models, although human clinical evidence remains limited and highly heterogeneous.8, 9, 10

Within this rapidly evolving field, plant-derived extracellular vesicles and plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles have drawn increasing attention. These vesicles are isolated from fruits, leaves, roots, callus cultures, and other plant tissues, and they share several functional similarities with mammalian extracellular vesicles while also carrying plant-specific lipids, proteins, metabolites, and small RNAs.11,12 For translational medicine, their potential advantages include ease of sourcing, scalability, relative physicochemical stability, and low apparent immunogenicity.11,12 Although nomenclature is still evolving, the terms plant-derived extracellular vesicles, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles, and plant nanovesicles are often used in the literature to describe these biologically active particles.11, 12, 13

Importantly, plant-derived extracellular vesicles are no longer an exclusively theoretical concept in wound care. Aloe vera peel-derived small extracellular vesicles have been shown to enhance keratinocyte and fibroblast migration while reducing reactive oxygen species in wounded skin-cell models.14 Aloe saponaria-derived extracellular vesicles have shown anti-inflammatory, proliferative, antioxidant, and pro-healing potential relevant to chronic wounds.15 Wheat-derived nanovesicles have demonstrated proliferative and migratory effects on endothelial, epithelial, and dermal fibroblast cells, together with enhanced type I collagen expression and endothelial tube formation.16 Tomato-derived nanovesicles have been shown to accelerate in vitro wound closure by promoting keratinocyte and fibroblast migration.17 Grapefruit-derived extracellular vesicles have been proposed as a cell-free wound-healing tool through enhancement of epithelial migration and angiogenic activity.18 Physalis peruviana fruit-derived nanovesicles have been reported to promote human dermal fibroblast proliferation, migration, and collagen-related remodelling.19 More recently, diabetic wound and hydrogel studies involving plant vesicles, including Aloe-derived and lotus-derived extracellular vesicles, have further expanded the translational landscape.20,21

At the same time, enthusiasm for exosome therapy should be tempered by several important realities. First, product standardisation remains inconsistent across the field. Different studies and commercial products vary in source material, isolation method, purification strategy, particle yield, storage conditions, and formulation. Second, many published reports remain preclinical, and relatively few human data exist for chronic wound indications. Third, even when clinical improvement is observed, it may be difficult to separate the effect of the exosome product from the effects of debridement, compression, glycaemic optimisation, infection treatment, and natural healing over time. These challenges are especially relevant for plant-derived formulations, where regulatory classification and quality control are still evolving.11,12,22,23

Nevertheless, the need for biologically active adjunctive therapies in chronic wound care remains pressing. This is particularly true in patients with overlapping risk factors such as diabetes, smoking, venous hypertension, or prior soft-tissue infection, all of which compromise perfusion and prolong inflammation. We therefore sought to document our clinical experience with a plant-derived exosome preparation derived from edelweiss callus culture extract in a small series of chronic wounds that had shown incomplete response to standard care. The aim of this paper is not to claim definitive efficacy, but rather to provide a professionally documented descriptive case series that integrates clinical evolution, representative photography, and supportive vascular observations. Materials and methods

This report was designed as a descriptive clinical case series of refractory chronic wounds managed in a wound-care setting. Written informed consent for treatment and publication of clinical data and photographs was obtained from all patients. Care was delivered according to routine clinical standards, and all interventions were undertaken in a manner consistent with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were included pragmatically rather than through a prospective trial framework. The common feature across all cases was the presence of a chronic or difficult-to-heal wound that had shown incomplete or unsatisfactory response to conventional therapy. The wounds represented the spectrum of complex ulceration encountered in clinical practice, including venous, diabetic, mixed vascular, and post-infectious tissue defects. The age range in this series was 42–62 years. Although the wound aetiologies were heterogeneous, all cases had been considered clinically refractory because they persisted despite prior standard-of-care measures directed at the underlying cause. The decision to include wounds of different aetiologies reflected the real-world context in which advanced regenerative adjuncts are typically considered: namely, after conventional wound management has plateaued without achieving satisfactory closure.

Before exosome treatment was initiated, each patient underwent re-evaluation of the wound bed, surrounding skin, vascular context, and systemic risk factors. Standard care was continued and optimised where feasible. Depending on the clinical context, this included debridement, compression-based venous management, off-loading, glycaemic control, antimicrobial therapy, vasodilator or vascular-directed care, local infection treatment, smoking cessation counselling, and nutritional support. The intent of exosome therapy in this series was therefore adjunctive rather than substitutive. The exosome product used in all cases was P198 Exo-HL FILCORE SB PLUS, Primoris International Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea, a commercially available plant-derived exosome preparation described by the manufacturer as containing vesicles derived from edelweiss callus culture extract. According to manufacturer information, the vesicles are isolated through high-pressure dispersion and ultracentrifugation and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline. Independent batch-level particle characterisation was not available in this series, and this should be regarded as a limitation. For clarity, we use the clinically familiar term “exosome therapy” throughout the manuscript while recognising that the more technically precise designation may be plant-derived extracellular vesicle or exosome-like nanovesicle preparation.11,12

The topical treatment protocol was standardised as far as possible but adapted to wound size and condition. At each session the wound was first cleansed thoroughly and debrided when necessary to remove non-viable tissue or excess slough. The exosome solution was then applied directly to the wound bed in a volume ranging from 0.5 to 1.0 mL per session, adjusted broadly according to wound surface area at an approximate target of 0.1 mL/cm². The solution was pipetted or dripped onto the wound surface and then distributed gently with a sterile applicator to ensure complete contact with the wound bed. A 5–10-min absorption interval was observed before the site was covered with a non-adherent sterile dressing. Patients were advised to avoid disturbing the dressing during the early post-application period and to continue all other wound-specific care instructions. Because wound size, logistics, and healing trajectory varied among patients, application intervals were individualised, usually occurring every 2–4 weeks.

Clinical wound assessment was performed at baseline and at each follow-up visit. Wound length, width, and visible depth were recorded using a sterile ruler or sterile measuring probe where appropriate. The wound bed was examined for granulation tissue, slough, necrotic tissue, epithelial edge advancement, exudate, peri‑wound inflammation, and signs of infection. Serial digital photographs were obtained using a measurement scale and a consistent attempt at maintaining image orientation and field of view. In the present manuscript, the most representative images from each case are shown in Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5.

Vascular evaluation with Doppler ultrasonography was used as a supportive adjunct in selected cases rather than as a uniform primary endpoint across the entire series. Where clinically indicated, arterial and venous Doppler imaging was performed using a colour Doppler ultrasound platform with a linear transducer. In cases in which vascular studies were available, waveform morphology, peak systolic signal, distal runoff, apparent peripheral resistance, and, where relevant, venous reflux patterns were reviewed as part of routine care. Because these studies were not performed at identical time points in all patients and were not collected under a prespecified imaging protocol for research purposes, the Doppler results in this manuscript are presented descriptively and qualitatively rather than as a formal statistical endpoint. Representative images are shown in Figure 6.

No formal hypothesis testing was planned for this case series. The objective of the report was descriptive: to document the clinical course of refractory wounds during adjunctive topical plant-derived exosome therapy and to identify patterns that may justify future controlled investigation.

Results

Five patients with chronic, difficult-to-heal wounds were treated with topical plant-derived exosome therapy after incomplete response to standard care. The group included three women and two men. The wounds varied in aetiology and duration, but all had demonstrated prolonged healing failure before exosome therapy was considered. Baseline characteristics, comorbidities, prior treatments, and treatment intervals are summarised in Table 1. Overall, all five patients showed progressive improvement in wound-bed quality after exosome therapy was introduced, with increasing granulation tissue, reduction in open wound area, and advancing epithelial closure over time. No patient required operative rescue after the treatment protocol began.

In Case 1, a 58-year-old woman with a history of diabetes and chronic venous insufficiency presented with a chronic ulcer that had been present for more than 6 months. Conservative and conventional non-surgical treatments had resulted in only partial improvement, and the wound remained stalled. After topical exosome therapy was initiated, serial photographs obtained 15 days apart showed a clear change in wound morphology. At baseline, the ulcer demonstrated substantial tissue breakdown, poor granulation, and a chronic inflammatory appearance. At follow-up, the wound bed had become visibly healthier, the ulcer area had contracted, and robust granulation tissue had emerged, suggesting a transition away from the chronic non-healing state (Figure 1A, B). Clinically, this was interpreted as an early reparative response after prolonged prior stagnation.

Case 2 involved a 62-year-old woman with chronic venous insufficiency and recurrent ulcers that had persisted for more than two years. Despite previous condition-specific management, the wounds had continued to recur and had not progressed to durable closure. After exosome therapy was commenced, substantial improvement was documented over a 60-day period. Baseline images showed recurrent chronic ulceration with poor tissue quality and limited spontaneous recovery. By follow-up, the wounds had undergone complete granulation and marked re-epithelialisation, with visibly improved peri‑wound skin quality and less inflammatory change (Figure 2A, B). This case was particularly notable because of the longstanding, recurrent nature of the disease and the abrupt change in healing trajectory after introduction of the new adjunctive therapy.

Case 3 was a 42-year-old man with chronic venous insufficiency and recurrent ulcers that had been present for more than 6 years. He had already undergone non-surgical conventional management with only partial improvement. Given the long disease history and inadequate response to standard treatment, exosome therapy was introduced as an adjunctive salvage measure. Serial images taken 45 days apart revealed meaningful early improvement. The baseline wound showed extensive tissue damage and chronic inflammatory change. At follow-up, the ulcer had contracted substantially, the wound bed was cleaner and better vascularised in appearance, and the surrounding skin displayed fewer inflammatory features (Figure 3A, B). Although the full treatment course extended beyond the interval displayed in the figure, the 45-day comparison captured the most visually compelling phase of early improvement.

Case 4 involved a 44-year-old woman who developed a severe chronic ulcer following local infection, most likely erysipelas or cellulitis. The wound extended from the dorsum of the foot to the left second toe and had been managed with repeated debridement and other condition-specific treatments, but only partial improvement had been achieved. Because of the extent of soft-tissue injury and ongoing failure of conservative management, amputation had been recommended. The patient refused amputation and elected to pursue exosome therapy. This was the most severe wound in the series and also the clearest test of whether a regenerative adjunct could alter the clinical course. The baseline image demonstrated severe tissue destruction and a high-risk wound bed (Figure 4A). At the intermediate stage, the wound showed contraction, healthier granulation, and reduced inflammatory burden (Figure 4B). By the final documented stage, obtained 5 months and 26 days after the start of the exosome-based approach, the wound had closed with restoration of skin integrity and no visible residual inflammation (Figure 4C). In practical terms, this was a limb-salvaging outcome in a patient who had otherwise been facing amputation.

Case 5 was a 54-year-old man with chronic venous insufficiency, diabetes, and ongoing tobacco use. He had been under treatment since early 2023 and had already received foam-based and optimised conventional management, yet the wound continued to evolve slowly without meaningful improvement. Continued smoking almost certainly compounded the impaired healing response by worsening microvascular dysfunction and local hypoxia. Exosome therapy was then initiated. Over 90 days, the wound demonstrated marked progression toward closure, with far more substantial epithelial recovery than had been observed during the preceding conventional course. The serial photographs show the baseline lesion and the later state after exosome therapy, by which time the wound had become nearly or completely closed (Figure 5A, B). This case is especially relevant because it illustrates improvement despite the persistence of a major behavioural risk factor.

Taken together, the photographic series in Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5 suggests a common pattern across cases: once exosome therapy was introduced, the wounds tended to shift from a refractory phenotype characterised by poor granulation and chronic inflammation to a more reparative phenotype characterised by healthier granulation tissue, contraction of the wound bed, epithelial edge advancement, and progressive closure. While such observations are not sufficient to establish causality, the temporal association was clinically striking in each case.

Supportive vascular imaging findings are illustrated in Figure 6. These Doppler studies were not available as a uniform quantitative dataset for all patients and therefore are presented as representative haemodynamic observations rather than as formal endpoints. In Figure 6A, the posterior tibial artery of a diabetic patient with upstream occlusions and a distal wound showed a low-amplitude monophasic pattern before exosome therapy, consistent with poor distal perfusion and persistent peripheral resistance. In Figure 6B, the popliteal artery of a smoker with critical upstream stenosis, imaged after exosome therapy, demonstrated a waveform with visibly improved amplitude and lower distal resistance. Figures 6C and 6D are especially informative because they provide an internal comparison in an obese diabetic patient with a mixed ischaemic-venous wound. Both posterior tibial arteries displayed monophasic flow due to upstream occlusions, but the side already exposed to exosome therapy for one week demonstrated noticeably better peripheral permeability and lower downstream resistance than the side not yet started on treatment. These Doppler differences are not proof of therapeutic angiogenesis, but they are compatible with improved local haemodynamics and are consistent with the clinical impression of better perfusion in the healing wound bed.

No serious adverse events attributable to exosome therapy were observed in this case series. No patient experienced wound deterioration, new necrosis, systemic infection, allergic reaction, or treatment-related complication requiring discontinuation. No patient required surgical intervention after exosome therapy was started, including the patient in Case 4 who had previously been advised to undergo amputation.

Discussion

This case series describes the use of topical plant-derived exosome therapy as an adjunctive intervention in five refractory chronic wounds. Although the series is small and uncontrolled, the clinical trajectory across all cases was directionally consistent: wounds that had plateaued under standard care began to show healthier granulation, reduction in wound burden, and progressive epithelial recovery after exosome therapy was introduced. These observations are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, but they are clinically important because they occurred in wounds complicated by diabetes, chronic venous insufficiency, active smoking, or severe post-infectious tissue loss, all of which are well-recognised barriers to wound healing.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Several mechanistic considerations may explain why exosome-based or vesicle-based therapies have attracted increasing interest in chronic wounds. Chronic wounds do not fail because of one isolated abnormality; rather, they persist because of simultaneous disturbances in inflammatory resolution, endothelial activity, fibroblast behaviour, oxidative stress, matrix turnover, and epithelial migration.5, 6, 7 A biologically active extracellular-vesicle preparation offers theoretical advantages because it can deliver a broad molecular cargo rather than a single growth factor or one pharmacologic target.8, 9, 10 Even when the precise active fractions remain incompletely defined, extracellular vesicles may influence several complementary processes at once, including macrophage phenotype, cytokine balance, endothelial migration, fibroblast activation, and matrix remodelling.8, 9, 10 This multitarget profile is particularly attractive in wounds where the pathology is multifactorial, as is almost always the case in real-world chronic ulcer practice.

The present series is also relevant because it focuses on a plant-derived preparation rather than a mammalian cell-derived exosome product. Plant-derived extracellular vesicles and exosome-like nanovesicles are being studied increasingly as alternatives to mammalian vesicle platforms because they may be easier to source, easier to scale, more stable under storage and transport conditions, and potentially less immunogenic.11,12 Their cargo is also biologically interesting, often including lipids, proteins, antioxidant compounds, and regulatory nucleic acids that may influence wound biology.11,12 For chronic wound therapy, this has created a growing translational niche in which plant-derived vesicles are being considered as cell-free regenerative adjuncts rather than as mere botanical extracts.

Recent literature supports the biological plausibility of this approach. Aloe vera peel-derived extracellular vesicles have been shown to reduce oxidative stress in keratinocytes and to enhance the migration of both keratinocytes and fibroblasts, thereby supporting wound closure under oxidative conditions relevant to chronic wounds.14 Aloe saponaria-derived extracellular vesicles have shown anti-inflammatory and pro-healing effects relevant to chronic wound repair.15 Wheat-derived nanovesicles have been reported to stimulate endothelial, epithelial, and dermal fibroblast proliferation and migration, increase collagen type I expression, and enhance endothelial tube-like structure formation, thereby linking plant vesicles to both matrix synthesis and angiogenic activity.16 Tomato-derived nanovesicles have been shown to promote keratinocyte and fibroblast migration and to accelerate wound closure in vitro.17 Grapefruit-derived extracellular vesicles have demonstrated cell-free wound-healing activity, including effects on keratinocyte migration and endothelial tube formation.18 Physalis peruviana fruit-derived nanovesicles have been associated with human dermal fibroblast regeneration and remodelling, including enhanced migration and collagen-related activity.19 Together, these studies support the concept that plant-derived vesicles may influence multiple aspects of wound healing, including oxidative balance, cell migration, fibroblast behaviour, and angiogenesis.

Newer studies have begun to move beyond simple cell-culture assays. Aloe-derived vesicle formulations have shown activity in diabetic wound models,20 and lotus-derived extracellular vesicles have been investigated for their anti-inflammatory and wound-healing potential.21 More broadly, recent reviews dedicated specifically to plant-derived extracellular vesicles in cutaneous wound healing now describe a rapidly expanding field in which vesicles are being incorporated into hydrogels, topical delivery platforms, and biomaterial systems designed to prolong residence time and improve wound-bed interaction.11,12,24 The present clinical series remains far below the evidentiary level of that translational ambition, but it fits within this emerging framework and offers real-world clinical observations that may help justify more formal investigation.

The clinical value of the current series lies not in statistical proof but in the pattern of response across markedly different wound phenotypes. Case 1 (Figure 1) showed rapid early granulation after more than 6 months of incomplete healing. Case 2 (Figure 2) demonstrated meaningful progress in a recurrent venous wound history spanning more than two years. Case 3 (Figure 3) showed improvement in a man with a 6-year background of recurrent disease. Case 4 (Figure 4) is arguably the most compelling because it documents progression from a severe post-infectious wound in which amputation had been advised to eventual closure after conservative limb-salvage management with adjunctive exosome therapy. Case 5 (Figure 5) is clinically relevant because improvement occurred despite the persistence of active smoking, a factor known to impair microvascular function and delay epithelial repair. In each case, standard wound care remained important, but the temporal shift in wound-bed behaviour after exosome introduction was sufficiently notable to merit documentation.

A further strength of the series is the inclusion of vascular observations. Chronic wounds are tightly linked to perfusion failure, whether from macrovascular occlusion, microvascular dysfunction, venous hypertension, or endothelial injury. In this context, the Doppler findings shown in Figure 6 are clinically meaningful. Before treatment, some arteries demonstrated low-amplitude monophasic flow with persistent peripheral resistance, patterns typical of compromised distal perfusion. After therapy, representative waveforms showed more prominent systolic peaks and lower downstream resistance. The internal right-versus-left comparison in Figure 6C and D is especially interesting because it suggests that even over a short interval, the treated side may exhibit better peripheral permeability than the untreated side in the same patient. These observations do not establish that exosomes directly induced angiogenesis, recanalisation, or improved endothelial function. Nevertheless, they are consistent with the broader mechanistic expectation that a pro-reparative wound environment should be accompanied by improved local vascular dynamics.

The present findings also reinforce a practical clinical truth: advanced biologic therapy cannot compensate fully for inadequate foundational wound care. Nutrition, glycaemic optimisation, compression when indicated, infection control, smoking cessation, and pressure relief remain essential.22,25 Chronic wounds are metabolically expensive lesions, and protein-energy deficiency, micronutrient insufficiency, and uncontrolled systemic disease all undermine the reparative capacity of the host.22,25 For this reason, exosome therapy in our clinic was never delivered as a standalone intervention. Rather, it was used after reassessment and optimisation of baseline wound care. This is especially relevant in Case 5, where persistent smoking likely remained a major impediment to complete healing even after the wound entered a more favourable reparative trajectory.

The regulatory environment for exosome-based therapies also warrants attention. Exosome products occupy a complex space between biologics, tissue-derived products, cosmetic adjuncts, and regenerative devices, depending on jurisdiction, source, manufacturing process, intended use, and route of administration.23 Regulatory agencies continue to develop frameworks for classification, manufacturing, quality control, sterility, potency testing, and clinical validation. For plant-derived vesicle products in particular, issues such as source variability, isolation reproducibility, particle stability, and contaminant exclusion remain highly relevant.11,12,23,24 Wider adoption in chronic wound care will therefore depend not only on encouraging case reports and mechanistic studies, but also on better product definition, rigorous safety monitoring, and well-designed comparative trials.

This study has several important limitations. First, it is a small uncontrolled case series and therefore cannot establish efficacy. Second, the wound aetiologies were heterogeneous, which improves real-world relevance but limits internal comparability. Third, all patients continued to receive standard wound care, making it impossible to isolate the independent effect of the exosome preparation. Fourth, Doppler studies were available only as supportive clinical imaging and not as a standardised quantitative research endpoint across all cases. Fifth, product characterisation was based on manufacturer information and did not include independent laboratory verification of particle count, purity, or cargo. Sixth, follow-up intervals varied across cases and were determined by clinical need rather than by a rigid protocol. Finally, spontaneous delayed healing cannot be excluded, even in wounds that had been clinically refractory beforehand.

Despite these limitations, the series is clinically useful. It documents not just isolated “before and after” snapshots, but a coherent pattern of reparative change in five chronic wounds that had previously shown poor response to standard care. The photographic record in Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, the supportive waveform changes in Figure 6, and the absence of serious treatment-related complications together suggest that plant-derived exosome therapy deserves more rigorous investigation. Future studies should adopt controlled designs, use prespecified wound-healing endpoints, include standardised digital planimetry and validated vascular imaging, and report full product characterisation, including particle enumeration by methods such as NTA, size profiling, purity assessment, and cargo analysis. Within such a framework, the true place of plant-derived exosome therapy in chronic wound management could be defined more clearly. At present, the most balanced conclusion is that topical plant-derived exosome therapy appears promising as an adjunctive approach in refractory wounds, but remains investigational and should be interpreted as such.
