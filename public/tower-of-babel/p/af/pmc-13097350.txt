Validity and Reliability Assessment of the Persian Version of a EORTC QLQ‐STO22 Questionnaire for Patients With Gastric Cancer Who Underwent Total Gastrectomy
Authors: Asma Mousavi, Leyla Sahebi, Seyed Amir Miratashi Yazdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe EORTC QLQ‐STO22 is a widely validated instrument for assessing health‐related quality of life in patients with gastric cancer. Given the high prevalence of gastric cancer and its specific risk factors in Iran, validating this tool for the Iranian population is essential. This study aimed to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Persian‐translated EORTC QLQ‐STO22.MethodsThis study assessed the content validity and reliability of a Persian translation of the 22‐item quality of life questionnaire (EORTC QLQ‐STO22) designed for patients with gastric adenocarcinoma post‐total gastrectomy. Content validity was evaluated by an expert panel using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR), as a result, 20 of the original 22 items were included in final version following panel review. Reliability was assessed through intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Cohen's Kappa. Construct validity was examined by correlating questionnaire scores with clinical variables such as age, tumor size, and T stage. Statistical analyses were performed using R software.ResultsThe study included 99 patients with a mean age of 60.1 years and 18.2% female. Content validity assessment by expert panel retained 20 of the original 22 items. Reliability analysis demonstrated excellent test‐retest consistency, with mean ICC of 0.999 and several items showing perfect agreement. Cohen's Kappa indicated strong to perfect agreement for most items, though two items showed lower consistency. Construct validity was supported by significant correlations between questionnaire scores and clinical variables, including a moderate positive correlation with age. Patient responses highlighted key quality of life challenges post‐gastrectomy, particularly concerns about health and future, weight issues, difficulty eating solid foods, and early satiety.ConclusionThe findings of this study confirm that the Persian version of the EORTC QLQ‐STO22 is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing quality of life in Iranian patients following total gastrectomy for gastric cancer.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global public health issue and ranks as the second leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. Among cancers worldwide, gastric cancer ranks as the fifth most common malignancy, with particularly high incidence in Eastern Asia, Eastern Europe, and South America, where it continues to present substantial challenges to public health [3, 4]. In Iran, gastric cancer is the third leading cause of cancer‐related mortality, responsible for 16.1% of all cancer deaths [5].

Patients with gastric cancer frequently experience a considerable reduction in their quality of life (QoL), stemming from both the physical and psychological impacts of the disease and its treatments [6]. Symptoms such as pain, digestive disturbances, fatigue, and weight loss are common, while anxiety and depression often accompany the cancer diagnosis and treatment process [7, 8, 9]. For those with advanced‐stage disease or where curative treatment is not possible, total gastrectomy may be required. This surgical approach, while sometimes necessary, introduces additional challenges [10]. Individuals undergoing total gastrectomy often face persistent issues such as nutritional deficiencies, changes in body image, altered gastrointestinal function, and difficulties resuming normal social and occupational roles, all of which can lead to diminished sense of well‐being [11, 12].

To comprehensively assess the QoL in cancer patients, the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Group developed a modular measurement system [13]. This system consists of a core questionnaire, the EORTC QLQ‐30, which assesses a broad range of health‐related QoL issues common to most cancer patients [14]. Crucially, the QLQ‐C30 is not adapted for different cancers; rather, it is used in conjunction with more specific supplementary modules designed to capture the unique challenges of a particular cancer type or patient group [15]. For gastric cancer the designated module is the EORTC QLQ‐STO22 [16]. Thus 22‐item module was developed through an international collaboration to assess symptoms and issues particularly relevant to patients with stomach cancer, such as dysphagia, eating restrictions, and dumping syndrome [12, 17]. However, the original development and psychometric validation of the QLQ‐STO22 primarily involved patient populations from Europe, and it is important to note that Iranian patients were not included in these initial phases. Since then, the questionnaire has been validated and utilized in various cultural contexts [18, 19].

Recognizing the potential for cultural and lifestyle differences to impact how patients interpret and respond to QoL questions, the EORTC Quality of Life Group is currently examining the cultural relevance of the QLQ‐STO22, particularly in regions where gastric cancer is highly prevalent [18]. Given the increasing incidence of gastric cancer in Iran and its profound impact on patients' lives, validating a culturally appropriate assessment tool is essential for both clinical practice and research. Therefore, this study aims to confirm the psychometric properties of the existing Persian version of the EORTC QLQ‐STO22. The objective is to formally evaluate its reliability and validity in a sample of Iranian patients with gastric cancer, ensuring it can effectively capture their unique experiences and provide a sound basis for improving patient care.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a psychometric validation design to assess the content validity and reliability of a Persian translation of a 22‐item questionnaire designed to evaluate the QoL in patients with gastric adenocarcinoma following total gastrectomy. The validation process comprised linguistic translation, expert review for content validity, and statistical analysis of reliability using test‐retest methodology (Supporting Information S1: Table 1 presents the original English version of the questionnaire). Patient responses were collected at two time points, with an interval of 10 days to 2 weeks between administrations. This study was conducted with prior approval from the Tehran University of Medical Sciences IRB (approval number: 1403‐2‐495‐72648).

Questionnaire Translation and Adaptation

The questionnaire translation and cultural adaptation followed a rigorous multi‐step process: 1.Forward translation: Two bilingual translators (A.M and M.H), both healthcare professionals with expertise in health and disability concepts, independently translated the original English questionnaire into Persian.2.Reconciliation and provisional version: The two forward translations were compared, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved in collaboration with the senior author (S.A., M.Y.). This iterative process resulted in a reconciled provisional Persian version of the questionnaire.3.Backward translation: A third independent translator, blinded to the original English questionnaire, translated the provisional Persian version back into English.4.Expert review and equivalence assessment: The backward translation was reviewed by the senior author (L.S.) to identify any conceptual or cultural discrepancies compared to the original English questionnaire. The research team then collectively reviewed both translations to ensure semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence.5.Expert panel for content validity: The finalized Persian version was submitted to a panel of 10 gastroenterology or surgery specialists, who were blinded to the original questionnaire. Each specialist assessed each of the 22 items for content validity using a three‐point scale: “necessary and effective,” “effective but not necessary,” and “not effective and not necessary.” A dedicated column was provided for specialists to suggest alternative wording or paraphrasing to enhance clarity and feasibility.

Data Gathering and Participants

This study recruited 99 adult patients diagnosed with adenocarcinoma of the stomach who had undergone total gastrectomy at Sina Hospital, Tehran, Iran, between 2016 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were: diagnosis of gastric adenocarcinoma, total gastrectomy, age ≥ 18 years, and willingness to participate in telephone follow‐up. Exclusion criteria included: partial gastrectomy, inability to complete follow‐up calls, refusal to participate, non‐adenocarcinoma pathology, and mortality prior to questionnaire completion.

Data collection included: Demographic and clinical data: Age, gender, tumor site, tumor size, tumor grade, T stage (AJCC TNM staging system), perineural invasion (presence/absence), lymphovascular invasion (presence/absence), surgical margin status (involved/clear), lymph node status (involved/clear). These data were extracted from patient medical records.Questionnaire data: Patient responses to the 22‐item Persian questionnaire were collected via two telephone interviews, conducted by a single trained interviewer (A.M.) to minimize interviewer bias. The interviews were conducted 10 days to 2 weeks apart. Patients were unaware of the test‐retest nature of the study during the first interview. The interviewer introduced herself, provided a detailed explanation of each question, and recorded responses on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = “not at all” to 4 = “most of the time”). Probing questions were used to clarify ambiguous responses, and efforts were made to maintain a neutral conversational style to avoid influencing patient answers. All participants provided verbal informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study, which was documented by the interviewer.

Sample Size Calculation

The required sample size was calculated using standard statistical parameters for continuous outcomes. Specifically, we set a significance level (alpha) of 0.05 (Z = 1.96), a statistical power of 0.8 (Z = 0.84), and assumed a moderate effect size of 0.3. Using the formula n=((Z1−α/2+Z1−β)/effect size)2 which yields a minimum sample size of approximately 99 participants to detect a moderate effect with adequate power and confidence [20]. This approach follows established methodology used in clinical research sample size calculation sufficient sensitivity to identify meaningful differences.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.2.3. The following R packages were utilized: Inter‐rater reliability [21] for reliability analyses intraclass correlation coefficients ((ICC) and Cohen's Kappa), psych for descriptive statistics and correlation analyses, and stats (base R package) for general statistical functions.

Content Validity

Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) for each of the 22 questionnaire items. The CVR was calculated using the formula: CVR=(ne−N/2)/(N/2) ne = number of subject matter experts who rated a question as “essential and effective”, N = total number of subject matter experts on the panel.

Items with a CVR value of 0.6 or higher were considered to have acceptable content validity and were retained in the final questionnaire. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize expert ratings and CVR values for each item [22].

Reliability

ICC: test‐retest reliability was evaluated using the ICC. A two‐way mixed‐effects model, absolute agreement, single measures ICC was calculated using the icc() function from the IRR package in R. ICC values were interpreted according to established guidelines [23]. Confidence intervals (95%) were calculated for each ICC value to assess the precision of the reliability estimates.

Cohen's Kappa: to assess the consistency of categorical responses between the two time points, Cohen's Kappa coefficient was calculated for each questionnaire item using the kappa2() function from the irr package in R. Kappa values were interpreted according to established guidelines for agreement [24]. Confidence intervals (95%) were also calculated for each Kappa value.

Construct Validity Analysis (Relationships Between Questionnaire Scores and Clinical Variables)

Construct validity was examined by assessing the correlations between questionnaire scores and relevant clinical variables. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to evaluate non‐parametric correlations between total questionnaire scores at Time 1 and Time 2, and the following clinical variables: age, tumor size, and T stage. Spearman's correlation was calculated using the cor.test() function in the stats package in R, specifying method = “spearman”. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak (ρ = 0.10–0.29), moderate (ρ = 0.30–0.49), or strong (ρ ≥ 0.50) [25]. These analyses were conducted using the cor.test() function with method = “spearman” in the stats package in R.

Patient Response Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation (SD), and range, were calculated for each questionnaire item score to summarize patient responses and identify items representing the most frequently reported challenges. These analyses were performed using base R functions.

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [26]. This methodology follows the SAMPL (statistical analyses and methods in the published literature) guidelines to ensure transparent and rigorous reporting of statistical methods [27].

The analyses described above were prespecified prior to data collection, except for the construct validity analysis, which was conducted post hoc based on the available data. No additional exploratory or post hoc subgroup analyses were performed,

Content Validity

The content validity of the Persian version of the questionnaire was evaluated by a panel of 10 gastroenterology or surgery specialists. The CVR was calculated for each of the 22 initial items. A CVR threshold of 0.6 was pre‐determined as the criterion for item retention. Following expert review, 20 items demonstrated acceptable content validity, meeting or exceeding the CVR threshold, and were thus retained in the final questionnaire. Two items failed to achieve the CVR threshold and were consequently excluded. Feedback from the expert panel also informed the refinement of the retained items through paraphrasing and minor modifications to enhance clarity and comprehensibility for the target population. Reliability

Intraclass Correlation Coefficient

The reliability of the Persian questionnaire was rigorously assessed using the ICC and Cohen's Kappa. ICC analysis revealed excellent reliability across all questionnaire items. Individual item ICC values were consistently high, with the majority demonstrating ICC values of 0.998 or greater. Several items exhibited perfect reliability with an ICC of 1.0. The mean ICC across all items was 0.999 further substantiating the questionnaire's excellent overall reliability. These findings indicate a high degree of consistency in questionnaire scores when administered across different time points. The narrow confidence intervals associated with each ICC value provide additional support for the robustness of the questionnaire's reliability (Table 3).

Cohen's Kappa for Consistency

Cohen's Kappa analysis was employed to evaluate the consistency of responses between two administrations of the questionnaire. Results indicated a mixed level of agreement across items. Items 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, and 18 demonstrated perfect agreement with a Kappa value of 1.0, signifying near‐perfect consistency in responses. Items 1, 4, 5, 6, 10, and 17 exhibited substantial agreement with Kappa values ranging from 0.838 to 0.893, indicating strong consistency. However, items 19 and 20 showed poor agreement with low Kappa values (0.237). These findings suggest that while the questionnaire generally demonstrates good to excellent agreement for the majority of items, items 19 and 20 warrant further investigation and potential refinement due to their lower consistency (Table 4).

Construct Validity

Construct validity was assessed by examining correlations between questionnaire scores and relevant clinical variables. Statistically significant correlations were observed, supporting the construct validity of the instrument. Specifically, a moderate positive correlation was found between age and questionnaire scores at Time 2 (r = 0.269–0.303, p< 0.05), suggesting a potential association between older age and higher questionnaire scores at the second time point. Additionally, the analysis revealed predominantly weak positive correlations between questionnaire scores, with correlation coefficients ranging from −0.17 to 1.0. The average correlation across all time points was approximately 0.25 (Tables 5 and 6, Supporting Information S1: Tables 2–3).

Patient Responses

Analysis of patient responses to individual questionnaire items revealed a range of mean scores from 109 to 221. Lower scores on the questionnaire are indicative of better QoL. The items with the highest mean scores, representing the most frequently reported challenges, were items 15, 18, 16, and 11. These items corresponded to concerns regarding health and future, weight concerns, difficulty consuming solid foods, and early satiety, respectively. These findings are consistent with existing literature and highlight the specific QoL challenges experienced by patients following total gastrectomy.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluated the validity and reliability of the Persian‐translated EORTC QLQ‐STO22 in a sample of 99 Iranian gastric cancer patients who had undergone a total gastrectomy. The findings confirm that the questionnaire is a largely valid and reliable instrument for this specific clinical population. The overall strong psychometric properties, including high CVR for most items and excellent test‐retest reliability, are consistent with findings from the module's international validation studies, which included patient cohorts from both European and Asian countries [16]. Our study contributes to this body of work by providing the first validation data from an Iranian cohort.

A key aspect of this validation was assessing content validity, where two items failed to meet the predetermined threshold. This may be attributed to our study's specific focus on a post‐total gastrectomy population. For instance, an item like “Have you lost any hair?” may have low relevance in a cohort not uniformly exposed to chemotherapy, a finding that has been noted in other QLQ‐STO22 evaluations. Similarly, certain questions may carry different cultural weight or interpretation in an Iranian context, which could explain the lower validity score. This highlights the importance of cultural and clinical context when applying standardized instruments.

Our surgically focused cohort contrasts with the original study's inclusion of diverse treatments (chemotherapy, palliative care). Compared to the FACT‐GA‐a tool emphasizing chemotherapy‐related symptoms, the EORTC QLQ‐STO22 better captures surgical sequelae like early satiety, a dominant concern in our cohort [28]. Regional adaptations, such as Korean KOQUSS‐40's emphasis on dysphagia post‐gastrectomy, highlight the value of culturally tailored items [18], though our results confirm the EORTC's broader cross‐cultural utility [29].

The prominence of health‐related anxiety and early satiety in our findings reflects global patterns of post‐gastrectomy QoL challenges [30]. While short‐term symptoms management interventions (e.g., exercise therapy) show promise, long‐term follow‐up data are critical [31, 32]. Studies suggest that QoL stabilizes over time as patients adapt, yet our limited follow‐up period precludes analysis of this trajectory [33, 34, 35].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our final sample size of 99 participants was modest, which may limit the statistical power for more granular subgroup analyses. Second, the sample was not fully representative of the broader gastric cancer population in Iran; specifically, there was a small number of female participants, which limits the generalizability of our findings to women undergoing gastrectomy. The study was also conducted at a single center, and the findings may not be generalizable to all regions of Iran. Finally, our cross‐sectional design and limited follow‐up period prevent us from analyzing the long‐term trajectory of QoL as patients adapt to life after surgery.

Future studies should include long‐term follow‐up to better understand changes in QoL over time. To make the questionnaire more relevant in different cultures, future research should focus on adding specific items based on EORTC Quality of Life Group guidelines instead of combining modules randomly. Using selected, validated questions from the EORTC Item Library under official guidance can help create an updated version that is both globally comparable and sensitive to local needs. We encourage future studies to optimize the instrument for use in various cultural settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the Persian version of the EORTC QLQ‐STO22 is a valid and highly reliable instrument for evaluating QoL in Iranian patients following total gastrectomy. The instrument showed excellent overall consistency and is suitable for use in both clinical practice and research to better address the needs of these patients in Iran. For future research, longitudinal studies are critical to track the evolution of QoL over time.
