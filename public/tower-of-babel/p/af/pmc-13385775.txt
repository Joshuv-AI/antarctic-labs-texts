Synergistic enhancement of athletic performance and psychological resilience through AI-driven personalized exercise regimens network
Authors: Huayang Kang, Yu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The reciprocal benefits of physical exercise on physiological performance and mental well-being are well-documented, yet conventional training paradigms seldom optimize for both outcomes simultaneously. This results in a missed opportunity for synergistic adaptation, where physical gains bolster mental fortitude and vice versa. Here, we introduce and validate an AI-driven framework for generating personalized exercise regimens that explicitly co-optimize for athletic performance and psychological resilience. Our system is built on three core innovations: (1) a Dual-factor adaptation model (DFAM), a novel mathematical formalization of the coupled dynamics of physical fitness, fatigue, psychological stress, and resilience; (2) a personalized regimen optimization algorithm (PROA), a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm that navigates the trade-off space between performance and resilience to generate Pareto-optimal training schedules tailored to an individual’s unique DFAM parameters; and (3) an adaptive feedback control (AFC) system that fine-tunes daily training loads in real-time based on wearable sensor data and subjective feedback. We evaluated this framework in a 12-week randomized controlled trial with 60 competitive athletes. The personalized group (PER) demonstrated markedly superior gains in a composite Athletic Performance Index (API) (+ 21.4% ± 3.8%) compared to a standardized training group (STD) (+ 12.0% ± 4.5%) and a non-training control (CTL) (+ 2.2% ± 1.5%). Concurrently, psychological resilience, quantified via the Connor–Davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC), increased significantly more in the PER group (+ 18.6% ± 2.5%) than in the STD (+ 8.3% ± 2.1%) and CTL (+ 1.1% ± 1.3%) groups (all p < 0.001). Ablation studies confirmed that both the dual-objective optimization and the adaptive feedback were critical for achieving these synergistic outcomes. Our findings suggest that an explicit, algorithmic co-optimization of physical and psychological states can significantly enhance holistic human performance, offering a promising new direction for intelligent training systems.

Introduction

The pursuit of peak human performance is a central theme in sports science, with training methodologies continuously evolving to push the boundaries of athletic achievement. Historically, these methodologies have centered overwhelmingly on the enhancement of physiological capacities such as strength, speed, and endurance1–3. While the psychological dimensions of sport—motivation, focus, and the ability to perform under pressure—are acknowledged as critical, they are often addressed through separate mental skills training rather than being integrated directly into the physical training regimen4. This bifurcation overlooks a profound and synergistic interplay: physical training does not merely sculpt the body; it also fortifies the mind. A robust body of evidence demonstrates that regular, structured exercise is a potent catalyst for enhancing psychological resilience5–7, the capacity to adapt successfully to stress and adversity8–10.

Despite this known dual benefit, the design of exercise programs remains largely rudimentary in its approach to personalization and multi-objective optimization. The dominant paradigm involves “one-size-fits-all” or group-based plans, which, even when periodized, fail to account for the vast inter-individual variability in physiological and psychological responses to training stimuli11. An identical training load may be optimal for one athlete, insufficient for another, and deleterious (leading to overtraining, injury, or burnout) for a third12. The advent of personalized medicine has highlighted the power of tailoring interventions to individual characteristics13, a principle that is directly applicable to athletic training. True personalization requires moving beyond simple heuristics to a data-driven, quantitative framework that can model and predict an individual’s unique adaptive trajectory.

Recent technological advances, particularly the proliferation of wearable sensors (e.g., smartwatches, heart rate monitors) and mobile applications, have opened the door to such a framework. These devices provide a continuous stream of high-fidelity data on an athlete’s internal and external load, physiological state (e.g., Heart Rate Variability, sleep quality), and subjective well-being14,15. This data deluge presents both an opportunity and a challenge. The opportunity lies in using this information to create truly adaptive, personalized training systems. The challenge is to develop the sophisticated mathematical models and algorithms necessary to translate this raw data into actionable, optimized training recommendations that account for multiple, often competing, objectives.

Existing work on intelligent training systems has made strides in optimizing for single objectives, typically physical performance. For instance, models based on the fitness-fatigue paradigm have been used to forecast performance and schedule training loads16. Reinforcement learning approaches have shown promise in learning optimal policies from data17. However, these models have not formally incorporated psychological resilience as a co-equal optimization target. Consequently, they risk designing regimens that maximize short-term physical gains at the expense of long-term mental well-being, potentially leading to burnout. There is a critical, unaddressed need for a unified framework that treats physical performance and psychological resilience not as separate silos, but as intertwined components of a single complex system to be optimized jointly.

This paper introduces such a framework. We propose and validate an AI-driven system that designs personalized exercise programs to simultaneously maximize athletic performance and psychological resilience. Our approach is built upon a tripartite structure of theoretical modeling, algorithmic optimization, and real-time adaptation.

We validated our integrated system through a 12-week, 60-participant randomized controlled trial. The results provide definitive evidence that this AI-driven, dual-objective personalization approach yields substantially greater improvements in both physical performance and psychological resilience than a high-quality, standardized training program. Our work not only presents a set of novel computational tools but also advocates for a paradigm shift in athletic development, moving towards a holistic, data-driven science of human performance enhancement.

Innovation beyond prior work. Different from prior intelligent training studies that focus primarily on a single physiological target (e.g., performance prediction or load scheduling), our contribution is a unified, closed-loop framework that explicitly treats psychological resilience as a co-equal optimization objective. Specifically, DFAM provides a coupled psycho-physiological state-space model, PROA performs constrained long-horizon regimen planning under dual objectives, and AFC implements short-horizon readiness-based adjustments using wearable and self-report inputs. The novelty lies not in replacing conventional statistics with “AI” for routine preprocessing, but in an algorithmic integration of (i) individualized model calibration, (ii) multi-objective long-term planning, and (iii) daily feedback control to co-optimize physical performance and resilience.

Methods

Our methodology is predicated on the integration of a theoretical model of adaptation, an optimization algorithm for long-term planning, and a feedback controller for short-term adjustment. This section details each of these components, followed by the design of the human-subject experiment used for validation.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Guangzhou Huashang College (Approval No. 2023-GZHC-IRB-028). All participants provided written informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was retrospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR). (Registration Status: Application submitted on February 14, 2025; Official Registration Number is pending administrative issuance and will be inserted prior to publication).

Sample size calculation The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 software based on an a priori power analysis for a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-between interaction). Based on a moderate effect size (f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, a minimum total sample size of 48 participants was calculated. To account for a potential dropout rate of 20%, we recruited 60 participants (20 per group).

Innovation 1: Dual-factor adaptation model (DFAM)

To move beyond traditional models that focus solely on physical performance, we developed the dual-factor adaptation model (DFAM). Our approach is conceptually grounded in the classic fitness-fatigue models pioneered by Banister et al.18, which posit that an athlete’s performance is the net result of two opposing factors: a positive fitness gain and a negative fatigue effect, both induced by training19. While powerful, these models are limited as they typically overlook the psychological dimensions of adaptation, which are critical for long-term progress and well-being20.

A substantial body of evidence demonstrates a strong, bidirectional link between physical exercise and psychological resilience—the capacity to adapt successfully to stress21. Structured training not only builds physical capacity but also serves as a form of stress inoculation, enhancing an athlete’s mental fortitude22. However, excessive training without adequate recovery can lead to mental stress accumulation and burnout. To our knowledge, no existing computational model formally integrates these psychological dynamics with physiological adaptation.

To bridge this gap, DFAM extends the dual-factor paradigm by incorporating psychological components. The model describes an individual’s psycho-physiological state as it evolves in response to training. Innovation 2: Personalized regimen optimization algorithm (PROA)

Given an individual’s calibrated DFAM parameters, the next challenge is to design an optimal training plan. This is a complex planning problem, as the effect of any single training session depends on the entire history of prior sessions. We formulated this as a multi-objective optimization problem and developed the Personalized Regimen Optimization Algorithm (PROA) to solve it.

The goal of PROA is to find a sequence of training loads for an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document}-week program, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{{W}_{1},{W}_{2},...,{W}_{N}\}$$\end{document}, that maximizes both the final predicted performance potential (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{final}$$\end{document}) and the final predicted resilience level (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{final}$$\end{document}). The daily loads \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{k}$$\end{document} within a week \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{i}$$\end{document} can be structured according to a pre-defined microcycle pattern (e.g., hard-easy-hard-medium-rest). The optimization variables are thus the weekly total loads. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{P}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{R}$$\end{document} are the objective functions for performance and resilience, which are simply the final values returned by the DFAM simulation for the given plan. Since these two objectives are often in conflict (e.g., an extremely aggressive plan might maximize \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{final}$$\end{document} at the cost of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{final}$$\end{document}), we seek solutions on the Pareto front. PROA uses a scalarization approach, combining the objectives into a single fitness function:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Fitness}={w}_{P}\cdot \mathrm{normalize}({P}_{final})+{w}_{R}\cdot \mathrm{normalize}({R}_{inal})$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{P}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{R}$$\end{document} are weights that can be tuned to reflect the desired priority (for our main experiment, we used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{P}={w}_{R}=0.5$$\end{document}).

The fittest plans are selected to produce a new generation of plans through crossover and mutation. This loop continues until a termination criterion (e.g., maximum generations) is met, at which point the best-performing plan is returned as the optimized, personalized regimen.

Innovation 3: Adaptive feedback control (AFC) system

While PROA generates an optimal macro-level plan, an athlete’s day-to-day readiness to train can fluctuate due to factors not captured in the long-term model, such as sleep quality, nutrition, or external life stressors. To account for this, we designed the Adaptive Feedback Control (AFC) system, a micro-level controller that adjusts the prescribed daily training load in real-time.

The AFC system operates as a simple closed-loop controller. Before each planned training session, it gathers feedback data from two sources: (1) Physiological Data (from a wearable sensor), primarily morning Heart Rate Variability (HRV) and Resting Heart Rate (RHR), compared against the individual’s rolling 7-day baseline. HRV is a sensitive indicator of autonomic nervous system balance and recovery status15; (2) Psychometric Data (from a brief daily questionnaire): Subjective ratings of sleep quality, muscle soreness, fatigue, and mood on a 1–5 scale.

The AFC algorithm then applies a set of expert-defined rules to modulate the planned training load for that day, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{planned}$$\end{document}. Experimental design and procedures

To validate our complete framework, we conducted a 12-week, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial.

Participants

We recruited 60 healthy, competitive amateur athletes (36 male, 24 female; age 25.1 ± 4.2 years) from local sports clubs. Participants were required to have at least two years of consistent training experience in either an endurance sport (running, cycling, triathlon) or a strength/power sport (weightlifting, sprinting, CrossFit). All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangzhou Huashang College.

Groups and interventions

Participants were stratified by gender and sport type (endurance vs. strength) and then randomly assigned to one of three groups (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=20$$\end{document} per group):

Personalized group (PER)

This group received training regimens generated and managed by our full AI framework (DFAM calibration, PROA optimization, and AFC daily adjustments).

Standard group (STD)

This group followed a standardized, periodized 12-week training program. The program was designed by certified strength and conditioning specialists and was considered high-quality, but it was identical for all participants in this group. Its total volume was matched to the average volume of the PER group’s plans to control for total workload.

Control group (CTL)

This group was instructed to maintain their regular lifestyle and refrain from any new structured training programs for the duration of the study. The allocation was concealed from the researchers enrolling participants. Furthermore, outcome assessors responsible for conducting the API and CD-RISC measurements were blinded to the group allocation of participants.

Outcome measures

A comprehensive set of measurements was taken at baseline (Week 0) and at the end of the intervention (Week 12).

Primary outcome 1

Athletic Performance. We created a composite athletic performance index (API). For endurance athletes, this was a weighted score of their VO2max and time-to-exhaustion on a graded treadmill test. For strength athletes, it was a weighted score of their 1-repetition max (1RM) in key lifts (e.g., squat, deadlift) and their peak power output on a 30-s Wingate test. Scores were normalized to a baseline of 100 for each participant to allow for aggregation and comparison of percentage changes.

Primary outcome 2

Psychological Resilience. This was measured using the Connor–Davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC), a widely validated 25-item self-report questionnaire with scores ranging from 0 to 10016. Higher scores indicate greater resilience.

Daily monitoring (for PER group)

Participants in the PER group used a commercial wearable (Polar Vantage V2) to track sleep and daily HRV/RHR. They also completed a 2-min questionnaire on a custom smartphone app each morning to provide the subjective inputs for the AFC system.

Ablation studies: deconstructing the AI framework

To isolate the specific contributions of our framework’s components, we conducted computational ablation studies using the PER group’s calibrated DFAM models. We simulated two counterfactual scenarios: (1) “No AFC,” where athletes followed their PROA plan rigidly without daily adjustments, and (2) “Perf-Only,” where PROA was re-run to optimize for performance alone (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{P}=1,{w}_{R}=0$$\end{document}).

The results, shown in Fig. 9 and Table 8, are striking. Removing the AFC system resulted in a predicted decrease in both outcomes. The API gain dropped from 21.4 to 19.1%, and the resilience gain dropped from 18.6 to 15.5%. This suggests that daily adaptation accounts for approximately 10–15% of the total benefit of the personalized system. Removing the resilience objective from PROA (“Perf-Only”) had a more dramatic and divergent effect. The predicted API gain was almost identical (21.1%), but the predicted resilience gain plummeted to just 7.9%—statistically indistinguishable from the STD group. Discussion

The findings of this study provide compelling evidence that an AI-driven, personalized approach to exercise prescription can simultaneously and synergistically enhance athletic performance and psychological resilience, far surpassing the outcomes of traditional, standardized training. Our integrated framework, comprising the DFAM model, PROA optimization, and AFC feedback system, represents a significant step forward in the application of machine intelligence to human performance.

Our first key finding is the sheer magnitude of the dual benefit. The personalized group achieved nearly double the performance gains of the standard group, while also realizing more than double the improvement in resilience. This confirms our central hypothesis: that co-optimizing for both physical and psychological outcomes is not a zero-sum game but a positive-sum endeavor. The strong correlation between individual gains in performance and resilience (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r=0.68$$\end{document}) suggests a powerful, mutually reinforcing relationship. We theorize that this synergy operates via several mechanisms. Enhanced physical fitness can reduce the physiological stress response to external pressures, increase self-efficacy, and improve mood, all of which contribute to resilience1⁷. Conversely, greater resilience allows an athlete to better tolerate the inherent stress of hard training, maintain motivation in the face of setbacks, and adhere more consistently to the program, thereby unlocking greater physical adaptation. Our framework is the first to algorithmically leverage this synergy.

The second critical insight comes from our ablation analysis. The “Perf-Only” simulation was particularly revealing: when our optimization algorithm was instructed to ignore resilience and focus solely on maximizing physical performance, it succeeded in that goal but completely failed to build resilience. The resulting resilience gains were no better than those from the generic, non-personalized standard program. This result is a stark demonstration that resilience is not an automatic byproduct of hard training; it must be an explicit target of the training design process. Our DFAM model and PROA algorithm provide the formal mechanism to do this. By modeling mental stress and resilience dynamics alongside fitness and fatigue, and by including resilience in the objective function, our system intelligently scheduled periods of lower-intensity work and recovery at times when the model predicted an individual would be approaching a state of excessive mental stress. This strategic modulation, invisible in a performance-only optimization, appears to be the key to fostering psychological adaptation without compromising physical progress.

The third major takeaway concerns the value of multi-scale adaptation. Our framework operates on two timescales: long-term planning with PROA and short-term adjustment with AFC. The ablation study showed that both are important. While PROA sets the overall strategic trajectory, AFC provides the crucial tactical flexibility to navigate day-to-day fluctuations in readiness. This prevented our athletes from overreaching on days they were fatigued and allowed them to train effectively and safely, as evidenced by the perfect adherence and zero-injury record in the PER group. This two-tiered architecture mimics the behavior of an expert human coach, who holds a long-term vision but makes daily adjustments based on how their athlete “looks” that day. Our system formalizes and automates this process, making elite-level, data-driven coaching scalable and accessible.

Our study has notable strengths, including its randomized controlled design, the use of objective performance measures, the validation of a comprehensive AI framework with multiple innovations, and the detailed ablation and subgroup analyses. However, we must also acknowledge its limitations. First, the 12-week duration is relatively short in the context of a full athletic season. While sufficient to demonstrate significant adaptation, longer-term studies are needed to assess the sustainability of these gains and the model’s performance over many training cycles. Second, our participant pool, while diverse in sport, consisted of healthy, competitive amateur athletes. The generalizability of our DFAM parameters and the overall framework to other populations, such as clinical patients, older adults, or elite professionals, requires further investigation. Third, while the CD-RISC is a validated instrument, resilience is a complex, multi-faceted construct, and our reliance on a single self-report measure could be supplemented in the future with more objective behavioral or physiological markers of stress coping. Finally, the “black box” nature of evolutionary algorithms like PROA can make the resulting plans difficult to interpret. Future work could focus on developing more explainable AI methods that provide clearer rationales for the prescribed training loads.

The implications of our work are far-reaching. For sports science, it provides a new, quantitative paradigm for holistic athlete development that moves beyond siloed physical and mental training. Coaches and organizations can use these principles to design smarter, safer, and more effective programs. For the technology industry, it provides a blueprint for the next generation of fitness and wellness platforms—systems that go beyond simply tracking activity to provide truly personalized, multi-objective, adaptive coaching. The potential applications extend beyond sport to corporate wellness, military training, and physical rehabilitation, where enhancing both physical capacity and mental resilience is paramount.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that by formally modeling, optimizing for, and adapting to both physical and psychological states, we can unlock a new level of human potential. The dual-promoting effect is real, quantifiable, and achievable through the intelligent application of machine learning and personalized modeling. The future of training is not just about getting stronger and faster; it is about getting stronger, faster, and more resilient, together.
