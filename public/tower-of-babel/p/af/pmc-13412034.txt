Balance impairment as a central correlate of falls, short sleep, and physical health in community-dwelling adults
Authors: Hammad Alhasan, Meral Alsayed, Jehan Alotibi, Rama Alharbi, Shouq Alharbi, Shmukh Alshareef, Sondous Redah, Afyaa Babtain, Aisha Aljabri, Farah Alalwani, Fidaa Hawsawi, Ammar Fadil, Mohamed Salaheldien Alayat, Abdulaziz Awali, Mansour Abdullah Alshehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a leading cause of disability in older adults and often co-occur with sleep disturbance, balance impairment, concern about falling and reduced health-related quality of life. However, these domains are usually studied separately. This exploratory cross-sectional analysis assessed the interrelationships among sleep quality and duration, concern about falling, clinical balance and functional performance, and health-related quality of life in community-dwelling adults aged ≥45 years using seven prespecified binary outcomes: falls, high concern about falling, balance impairment, short sleep duration, poor physical health-related quality of life, current smoking, and weak grip strength.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among adults aged ≥45 years (N=52; mean age 61.10±12.55 years). Trained physiotherapists collected sociodemographic and clinical data, medications, morbidities, smoking, and administered standardized measures: grip strength, 30-s Sit-to-Stand (STS), Timed Up & Go (TUG), Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Falls Efficacy Scale–International (FES-I), SF-12 Physical and Mental, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), sleep duration, and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Seven prespecified binary outcomes were modelled using logistic regression adjusted for age and sex.ResultsFalls were common (46.2%), as were BBS impairment (36.5%), weak grip strength (73.1%), short sleep (63.5%), and poor sleep quality (86.5%). Current smoking was reported by 26.9% of participants. Fallers were more often ≥65 years, smokers, and on ≥5 medications, and showed worse STS, BBS, SF-12 Physical/Mental, and FES (all p≤.041). Falls were strongly associated with age (rho=.58), smoking (rho=.71), BBS impairment (rho=.66), and higher FES concern (rho=.60) (all p<.001). In logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex, BBS impairment predicted falls (OR=5.88, 95% CI 1.06–6.67, p=.043) and high FES concern (OR=1.68, 95% CI 1.40–3.28, p=.004). Short sleep duration strongly associated with BBS impairment (OR=4.71, 95% CI 2.82–6.02, p=.008), having ≥1 fall was associated with current smoking (OR=2.86, 95% CI 1.02–3.74, p=.048) and high FES concern was linked to poorer SF-12 Physical status (OR=0.18, 95% CI 0.05–0.74, p=.017).ConclusionIn this cohort, balance impairment, fall concern, short sleep and lower physical health-related quality of life were observed to cluster. BBS was identified as a key independent correlate of both falls and concern about falling, while short sleep duration was strongly associated with balance impairment, and concern about falling independently predicted lower SF-12 physical scores. Given these results, multidimensional assessment including balance testing, concern-about-falling assessment, sleep and health-related quality of life screening is supported, and confirmation in larger longitudinal cohorts is required.

Introduction

The world’s population is ageing at an increasing rate, and fall-related injuries are now identified as a key driver of disability, functional decline, and mortality in older adults [1, 2]. Recent Global Burden of Disease analyses and extensive epidemiological evidence suggest that more than one in four older adults falls each year, with fall-related mortality rates continuing to increase despite advances in prevention [3, 4]. Falls rarely occur in isolation; they are often accompanied by other geriatric syndromes such as sleep disturbance, impaired quality of life and concern about falling, alongside wider vulnerability states including frailty and multimorbidity, and are now prominently featured in international guidelines for integrated care of older adults [5–7].

Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent from midlife onward and are linked to both functional decline and adverse events [8, 9]. Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies demonstrate that short sleep duration, poor sleep efficiency, and self-reported poor sleep quality are linked to a higher fall risk in community-dwelling older adults [10, 11]. Recent studies from diverse settings have confirmed that worse Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores and low self-rated sleep quality are associated with increased fall risk and impaired balance, highlighting sleep as a modifiable risk factor in fall-prevention strategies [12, 13]. Several mechanisms may help explain these associations. Poor sleep may negatively affect attention and executive function, impair the efficiency of sensory integration required for postural control and interfere with motor coordination and response timing, all of which may negatively affect gait stability and balance [14, 15]. These effects may be especially relevant in older adults, in whom age-related declines in physiological reserve may intensify the destabilizing consequences of insufficient sleep. Beyond its relationship with falls, poor sleep quality in older adults is also associated with cognitive decline, functional decline, and increased mortality, suggesting that sleep disturbances may be an early driver of wider vulnerability in older adults [16, 17].

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and concern about falling are closely related constructs in the context of falls, functional limitation, and healthy ageing. The 12-Item Short Form Survey (SF-12) is widely used to assess physical and mental HRQoL, and lower physical component scores are consistently linked to poor mobility and greater fall risk [18, 19]. Studies in older adults have demonstrated that risk factors such as impaired balance, reduced functional mobility, muscle weakness, and concern about falling are all related to poorer perceived physical health, highlighting the strong relationship between physical performance and perceived health [20, 21]. These associations are supported by plausible biological and behavioral mechanisms, because impairments in balance, mobility, and strength can compromise the capacity to perform daily activities safely and independently, while concern about falling may further limit activity, reduce confidence, and accelerate deconditioning, together contributing to worse perceived physical health and lower HRQoL [22, 23]. Concern about falling has increasingly been recognised as a highly prevalent geriatric concern that is independently linked with activity restriction, poorer quality of life, and mortality [24].

Objective evaluation of balance and functional mobility, together with assessment of concern about falling is fundamental to understanding fall risk. The Berg Balance Scale (BBS) is one of the most extensively validated clinical tools for assessing balance and predicting falls across multiple older adult populations [25]. Furthermore, functional mobility tests such as the Timed Up and Go (TUG) and subjective measures such as the Falls Efficacy Scale–International (FES-I) offer complementary information on balance, gait, and fall-related self-efficacy [26, 27]. Slower TUG performance has consistently been associated with higher fall incidence, reflecting impaired mobility and reduced capacity to respond to balance disturbances [25]. Higher FES-I scores capture concern about falling and activity restriction, both of which are associated with subsequent falls and functional decline [28].

Despite these observations, several gaps remain. The literature frequently investigates sleep, balance, concern about falling, and HRQoL in isolation, or focuses solely on adults aged ≥ 65 years [29, 30]. Fewer studies have examined these domains collectively within the same community-based cohort using a standardized assessment battery, particularly in adults across mid- to later life. Accordingly, the present study was designed to explore how these domains relate to one another within the same population. However, multidomain vulnerabilities related to sleep, balance, mobility, and fall-related concerns may develop earlier, during the transition from midlife to later life before more advanced functional decline becomes evident. For this reason, examining adults from age 45 and older may help identify early clustering of these risk-related factors.

The present exploratory cross-sectional analysis was conducted to address these gaps by exploring the associations among sleep quality and duration, health-related quality of life, clinical balance, functional performance, and fall-related concern in community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 45 years. Using a standardized assessment protocol and logistic regression modelling, the study evaluated seven prespecified binary outcomes: (1) falls, (2) high concern about falling, (3) balance impairment, (4) short sleep duration, (5) poor physical health-related quality of life, (6) current smoking, and (7) weak grip strength. The aim was to explore how these domains cluster and relate to one another in mid- to late-life and to inform multidomain assessment approaches for fall and frailty prevention in community settings.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Eligible individuals received a clear explanation of the study’s aims, procedures, and the potential benefits and risks of participation. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment after they had reviewed all study procedures. They were reminded that they could withdraw at any time without penalty, that their data would remain confidential, and were given contact details for inquiries or concerns. The protocol and consent process were approved by the Umm Al-Qura University Ethical Advisory Committee (approval no. HAPO-02-K-012-2024-11-2337). This study was conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and participants

This study used a cross-sectional observational design with a single assessment visit per participant. All data were collected in a university laboratory setting under standardized conditions. Participants were recruited through community announcements and targeted social media campaigns directed at adults aged 45 years and older. Recruitment focused on individuals who viewed and actively engaged with the study’s promotional materials such as by responding or reaching out to the research team ensuring that enrolment specifically reflected the intended age group.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were included if they were adults aged 45 years or older of either sex, ambulated independently without a wheelchair, had normal or corrected vision and normal hearing, had no diagnosed neurological disorder, and were able to understand instructions and communicate effectively.

Participants were excluded if they had any medical condition likely to compromise safety or balance during assessment or training (e.g., acute illness or unstable cardiopulmonary/musculoskeletal conditions); significant uncorrected visual or hearing impairment; a history of falls attributable to syncope or other acute medical events; or physical limitations preventing safe, effective use of the required devices. There were no exclusions based on sociodemographic characteristics, including education level, marital status, body mass index, employment status, living situation, physical activity level, or smoking status.

Data collection and outcome measures

Participants completed a structured one-hour assessment session during which trained physiotherapists conducted face-to-face interviews and standardized tests. All interviews and measurements were completed within the same visit.

Sample characteristics

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were collected through structured interviews. Information included age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), medical history, current medication use, smoking status, and presence of chronic conditions. Sex was ascertained by self-report using the study intake item: “What is your sex?” with response options male and female. Data on polypharmacy (use of multiple medications) and morbidities (presence of one or more chronic diseases) were also obtained. Cognitive function was screened using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) to categorize participants as having normal cognition, mild impairment, or moderate–severe impairment.

Functional and balance measures

Functional performance was assessed using standardized clinical tests. Grip strength was measured with a hand dynamometer, and lower-limb strength was evaluated using the 30-second Sit-to-Stand (STS) test. Mobility and dynamic balance were assessed with the TUG test. Functional balance was evaluated using the BBS. Falls within the last 12 months were recorded. A fall was defined as ’an unexpected event in which the participant comes to rest on the ground, floor, or other lower level ’ [31]. Concern about falling was assessed using the FES-I, providing insight into perceived fall risk and self-confidence during daily activities. The thresholds used to categorize these measures for analysis are outlined in the statistical analysis subsection on data preparation, operational definitions, and coding.

Lifestyle and health status measures

Lifestyle-related and psychosocial outcomes were also examined. Physical activity level was determined using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Sleep quality and sleep duration were evaluated through the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and a self-reported sleep-duration questionnaire. Health-related quality of life was measured using the SF-12, generating both physical and mental health component scores.

Sample size calculation

Primary analyses involved multivariable binary logistic regression models examining predictors of key outcomes (falls, concern about falling, balance impairment, reduced sleep duration, poor physical health, smoking, and low grip strength). Sample adequacy was evaluated using the events-per-variable (EPV) criterion (≥ 10 outcome events per estimated parameter; conservative target ≥ 20), which is widely recommended for logistic regression models. Each adjusted model included age, sex, and one primary predictor of interest; for predictors with more than two categories, additional indicator terms were entered as required. Therefore, the minimum number of outcome events required per model was approximately ≥ 50–120. With a total sample of N = 52, only participants with a prevalence ≥ 50% met the minimal EPV ≥ 10 criterion, indicating that adequate precision and stability were attainable primarily for common binary outcomes (e.g., falls, balance impairment, sleep duration, and grip strength). Conversely, models involving less frequent categories were underpowered, increasing the likelihood of wide confidence intervals and potential separation. To support this assessment, a post hoc power analysis was performed for logistic regression (Wald test) assuming a medium effect size (OR ≈ 1.8), α = 0.05, and five predictors. For outcomes with a prevalence of 0.50–0.60, the estimated statistical power ranged from 0.83 to 0.91, whereas for outcomes near 0.30, power fell below 0.75. This aligns with the EPV findings, confirming that the current sample size provides adequate power for common outcomes. Given the modest sample size and the number of interrelated domains examined, a single fully integrated multivariable model including all variables of interest simultaneously was not considered adequately stable; therefore, the analysis emphasized separate exploratory adjusted models.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27). Two-sided tests were used throughout with α = 0.05. Unless stated otherwise, results are presented as point estimates with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and p-values. Analyses followed four stages: (1) descriptive statistics; (2) descriptive between-group comparisons by fall status using χ²/Fisher’s exact tests; (3) bivariate correlations to prioritize candidate predictors; and (4) multivariable main-effects logistic regressions across the seven prespecified outcomes. Falls were used for the between-group descriptive comparison because they represented the most clinically interpretable and directly relevant event in the study framework; however, this comparison was intended to provide descriptive context rather than to establish a single primary outcome for the overall analysis.

Data preparation, operational definitions and coding

Continuous variables were examined for outliers, distributional shape, and plausibility; categorical variables were checked for appropriate levels and relabelled for clarity. For categorical analyses, variables were defined using predefined thresholds or standard scoring rules before being coded for statistical analysis. Age was categorized as ≤ 64 years versus ≥ 65 years. Sex was coded as male or female. BMI was categorized as normal/underweight, overweight, and obesity. Morbidities were coded as none versus one or more chronic conditions, and polypharmacy as no versus yes according to the use of multiple concurrent medications.

Falls was classified as none versus one or more falls in the previous 12 months. Balance BBS was categorized as normal versus impaired using a threshold of < 45/56 [32]. TUG performance was classified as normal mobility versus slow mobility using a threshold of ≥ 13.5 s [33]. Performance on the 30-second Sit-to-Stand (STS) test was categorized as normal, slow, or unable, with ‘slow’ indicating performance below age- and sex-referenced normative values and ‘unable’ indicating inability to complete the test [34]. Grip strength was classified as normal versus weak using sex-specific thresholds of < 27 kg for men and < 16 kg for women [35]. Concern about falling was categorized as low versus high concern using the FES-I, with scores ≥ 28 indicating high concern [36]. Sleep duration was categorized as normal versus short sleep using a threshold of < 7 h per night [37]. Sleep quality on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was categorized as good versus poor using a total-score threshold of > 5 [38]. SF-12 physical and mental health component scores were categorized as normal/high, moderate, or low/poor [39]. Physical activity was classified as low, moderate, or high according to the IPAQ scoring protocol [40].

Descriptive statistics and group comparisons

Participant characteristics were summarized as mean ± SD for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Between-group differences by fall incidence (fallers vs. non-fallers) were examined as an unadjusted descriptive step using Pearson’s χ² tests for categorical variables. These comparisons were intended to descriptively profile the sample by fall status rather than to serve as adjusted mean-difference analyses. Because the study was exploratory and included multiple descriptive comparisons, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied to these between-group analyses; therefore, these p-values should be interpreted with caution and primarily as descriptive signals rather than confirmatory findings.

Correlation analysis

Bivariate associations were explored to prioritize candidate predictors for multivariable modeling: Spearman’s rho was used for ordinal or non-normal pairs and for rank-based robustness. We summarized the full matrix and highlighted actionable correlations using the heuristic |rho| ≥ 0.30 and p ≤ 0.05 to nominate variables for modeling (e.g., BBS impairment, FES concern, SF-12 Physical, Sleep Duration, Smoking, Grip strength).

Logistic regression modeling

The primary analysis was designed as an exploratory multidomain assessment of prespecified binary outcomes. We fitted separate binary logistic regression models for each outcome to determine adjusted associations for individual candidate predictors. Regression analyses were conducted using variables that showed the strongest bivariate associations with each outcome. Each model included the common base covariates of age and sex along with one primary predictor of interest. This approach was chosen to provide clear adjusted estimates while limiting model instability in a modest sample with multiple interrelated variables. For reporting, we provide adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI, Wald p-values, and model performance metrics.

For each logistic model, we summarized overall fit using the omnibus likelihood-ratio χ², assessed calibration with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and reported explained variance via Nagelkerke R². Classification performance was described as overall accuracy at the 0.50 cutoff. To evaluate multicollinearity, we inspected tolerance/VIF from linear proxy models and considered VIF < 5 acceptable. We also monitored signs of separation or sparsity (e.g., very large standard errors, non-convergence, extreme odds ratios); when detected, we collapsed categories or simplified the model, and if instability persisted, we did not retain those terms and reported the parsimonious main-effects specification.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 55 individuals were screened for eligibility. Of these, 3 did not meet the inclusion criteria and were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 52 participants (mean age 61.10 ± 12.55 years; range 45–81). The flow of participants through the study is summarized in Fig. 1. Overall, the cohort showed a high burden of morbidity and polypharmacy, with marked mobility limitation on the Timed Up & Go and 30-s sit-to-stand tests and grip weakness. Falls were frequent and concern about falling was substantial. Lifestyle risks were evident as over half were low-active, with predominantly poor and short sleep. Health-related quality of life was skewed toward lower physical functioning, while mental health status was comparatively better (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Descriptive differences by fall status across morbidity, function, sleep, and quality-of-life measures

As a descriptive comparison anchored on the study’s most clinically relevant event, participants were also compared by fall status (non-fallers vs. fallers). Fallers were more likely to be ≥ 65 years (p < 0.001), current smokers (p < 0.001), and on ≥ 5 medications (p = 0.041). They also showed worse function and status: STS (overall p = 0.037), BBS (p < 0.001), SF-12 Physical (p = 0.003), SF-12 Mental (p = 0.002), and FES (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found for sex, BMI, grip strength, physical activity, sleep quality/duration, or TUG (all p > 0.05). Correlation patterns among clinical, functional, and lifestyle variables

Spearman’s rank correlations revealed several significant associations among clinical, functional, and lifestyle variables. Falls were strongly correlated with older age (rho = 0.58, p < 0.001), current smoking (rho = 0.71, p < 0.001), poorer balance on the BBS (rho = 0.66, p < 0.001), and higher concern about falling (FES-I; rho = 0.60, p < 0.001). Functional measures were inter-related, with significant positive correlations between STS performance, BBS impairment, and FES concern (all p < 0.001). Lower physical quality of life (SF-12 Physical) was moderately associated with worse balance (rho = − 0.58, p < 0.001) and greater fall concern (rho = − 0.45, p < 0.01), while mental quality of life showed weaker but similar trends (rho = − 0.40, p < 0.01). Higher PSQI scores were negatively correlated with sleep duration (rho = − 0.41, p = 0.002), indicating that poorer sleep quality was associated with shorter sleep duration (Fig. 2). These significant bivariate relationships informed the subsequent adjusted analyses used to identify independent predictors of falls, functional performance, and health outcomes.

Independent predictors of falls, balance, sleep, and physical function

Adjusted logistic regression models identified several consistent associations across the seven domains examined (Table 5). Impaired balance on the BBS emerged as the most robust predictor of falls (OR = 5.88, 95% CI 1.06–6.67, p = 0.043), indicating a substantially higher likelihood of falling among individuals with balance impairment. Balance impairment was also closely linked to higher concern about falling, with moderate balance impairment strongly predicting elevated FES scores (OR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.40–3.28, p = 0.004). Short sleep duration showed a pronounced association with balance impairment (OR = 4.71, 95% CI 2.82–6.02, p = 0.008), suggesting that inadequate sleep may be associated with balance difficulties. In terms of physical health status, greater fall concern was a significant predictor of poorer SF-12 Physical scores (OR = 0.18, 95% CI 0.05–0.74, p = 0.017), reflecting the detrimental impact of concern about falling on perceived physical functioning. Furthermore, moderate balance impairment substantially increased the likelihood of short sleep duration (OR = 5.74, 95% CI 2.91–8.49, p = 0.007), reinforcing the reciprocal relationship between sleep and functional balance.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This cross-sectional study in community-dwelling adults aged 45–81 years provides further evidence that sleep, balance, concern about falling, and physical health status are closely interconnected. The cohort was characterised by a high burden of multimorbidity and polypharmacy, pronounced mobility limitation, prevalent grip weakness, frequent falls, and considerable concern about falling. Physical HRQoL was clearly reduced, while mental HRQoL was comparatively preserved. Compared with non-fallers, fallers were more frequently ≥ 65 years, current smokers, and on ≥ 5 medications, and they demonstrated poorer performance on the STS and BBS, greater concern about falling, and lower SF-12 physical and mental scores.

In separate adjusted models, impaired balance was identified as a key correlate of both falls and concern about falling, while short sleep duration and balance impairment were strongly associated with each other. High fall concern independently predicted poorer SF-12 physical scores. No variables significantly predicted grip strength. Collectively, these results point to a multidimensional vulnerability pattern in which balance impairment, concern about falling and short sleep are closely connected to each other and to self-rated physical health.

Balance, concern about falling and falls

Our results emphasise that impaired balance as measured by the BBS is associated with fall history in this sample. Participants with lower BBS scores were nearly six times more likely to have reported a fall, independent of age and sex. This finding is consistent with evidence that balance measures are relevant to fall risk assessment; however, evidence for single physical performance measures, including the BBS as stand-alone tools for predicting falls remains mixed [25]. This point is especially important because falls in the present study were assessed retrospectively and therefore these findings should be interpreted as associations with previous falls rather than as evidence of true prospective prediction. Concern about falling was also closely linked with both balance and falls. High FES-I concern was strongly correlated with number of falls and balance impairment, and in multivariable models it independently predicted lower physical HRQoL. These findings are in line with earlier evidence linking concern about falling to decreased activity levels and poorer quality of life [41]. Studies in community-dwelling and home-care older adults have shown that concern about falling is more than a psychological after-effect of previous falls; it may also contribute to reduced participation and lower SF-12 physical scores [42].

The strong correlations observed between STS, BBS, and FES-I highlight that clinical physical performance and subjective concern about falling are strongly associated. This reflects earlier findings that poor balance and mobility performance are associated with higher concern about falling and lower HRQoL, suggesting a shared mechanism involving deconditioning and loss of confidence [43]. Our results reinforce the recommendation that comprehensive fall risk assessment should encompass both performance-based balance tests and concern about falling measures, even among adults as young as their mid-40s when other risk factors (e.g. smoking, polypharmacy) are present.

Sleep, balance, and physical health

One of the original aspects of this study is the demonstration of a strong link between short sleep duration and BBS impairment. While previous work has shown that poor sleep quality and insomnia symptoms are associated with impaired balance, most prior research has focused exclusively on adults aged ≥ 65 years and has typically examined sleep or balance independently rather than together [11, 44]. Although precision was limited, these bidirectional associations fit with a growing literature showing that moderate balance impairment markedly heightens the probability of short sleep.

Previous studies have demonstrated that both acute sleep deprivation and chronic poor sleep quality worsen postural stability [45]. Furthermore, chronic low-quality sleep may impair balance to a similar extent as total sleep deprivation [46]. Recent evidence indicates that the relationship is complex: in physically active adults, sleep deprivation may impair recovery rather than systematically worsen balance [47]. Our findings contribute to this literature by showing that short sleep duration is strongly linked with clinically relevant balance impairment and with self-perceived physical health. Those with higher fall concern were substantially less likely to report good SF-12 physical health, reflecting prior evidence that poor sleep quality is associated with worse function and greater disability [48]. Importantly, our results also showed a moderate negative correlation between sleep quality and sleep duration, suggesting that reduced sleep duration co-occurred with poorer self-rated sleep. Together, this clustering of short, poor sleep with impaired balance and lower physical HRQoL indicates that sleep disturbances may be a potential driver of emerging frailty.

Lifestyle and medication burden

The cohort was noted for numerous lifestyle and medical risk factors: over half were low active, had multimorbidity and polypharmacy, and falls were regularly reported. This is aligned with substantial literature that polypharmacy and fall-risk–increasing drugs are associated with fall incidence in older adults. Previous studies have underscored medication burden, chronic disease, and mobility limitations as key fall contributors [49]. Current smoking also showed a very strong correlation with falls in our data. Given the small sample size, we cannot draw definitive conclusions about smoking as an independent predictor once balance and age are adjusted for, but this pattern is consistent with broader evidence that smoking co-occurs with other adverse health behaviours and lower physical function [50]. In practice, the combination of smoking, low activity, and polypharmacy may identify a particularly high-risk subgroup in midlife that may benefit from integrated risk modification targeting both cardiometabolic and falls-related outcomes.

The lack of independent predictors for grip weakness may be due to limited power in our models, but it may also imply that muscle strength reflects cumulative exposures (e.g. nutrition, inflammatory burden) not fully accounted for in our limited set of covariates. Considering that grip strength is a well-recognised biomarker of ageing and future disability, its apparent inability to be fully explained in this small sample should not be interpreted as lack of clinical importance.

Clinical implications and recommendations

Clinically, our findings highlight the need to consider balance, concern about falling, and sleep as interrelated domains. A clinically practical assessment battery could include the BBS (or a similar balance test), concern about falling (e.g. FES-I), a brief sleep screen (e.g. PSQI), and SF-12 to flag individuals in whom poor sleep, impaired balance, and low physical HRQoL are already clustering. Multicomponent interventions that address balance training, confidence enhancement, sleep hygiene, and medication review may produce combined benefits for falls and broader functional outcomes.

From a research perspective, the strong associations observed here argue for moving beyond single-domain models toward multidomain frameworks incorporating clinical balance tests, concern about falling, HRQoL, and lifestyle behaviours. Future work should include larger, longitudinal cohorts to determine whether short and poor sleep causally contribute to balance decline and falls, or whether they predominantly signal the burden of existing functional impairment. Incorporating wearable activity monitoring during the night and more sensitive balance parameters in longitudinal designs may help clarify directionality and determine whether specific sway features mediate the relationship between sleep patterns and fall risk.

Limitations and future directions

Key strengths of this study include the integration of subjective, clinical and lifestyle/medical data within a single cohort of adults starting as early as age 45, as well as the use of standardized instruments widely adopted in gerontology and rehabilitation research. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference or temporal sequencing; we cannot establish whether short sleep causes balance impairment and falls, or whether these outcomes in turn lead to short sleep. Second, the modest sample size limits statistical power, yields wide confidence intervals, and increases the risk of model instability, especially for less common outcomes, raising the risk of type II error for underpowered outcomes and uncertainty for large odds ratios. In addition, the exploratory design included multiple descriptive and inferential comparisons without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons, which increases the likelihood of false-positive findings and means that statistically significant associations should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in larger studies. Furthermore, the analytic approach was exploratory and did not assess a single comprehensive model incorporating all domains simultaneously. Third, self-reported measures are subject to recall and reporting bias, and the convenience sampling strategy may over-represent individuals who are more health-conscious. Fourth, we also did not collect detailed information on fall circumstances (e.g., indoor vs. outdoor, environmental hazards) or pain severity, which can influence both sleep and balance. Fifth, the convenience sampling limits generalizability to the broader population, including individuals with lower digital access or those receiving institutional care. Sixth, the study was conducted in a single setting, which may limit generalizability to other regions or healthcare environments. Finally, although we controlled for age and sex and considered major clinical factors (e.g., morbidities, polypharmacy, smoking), residual confounding may persist. Important contributors such as depressive symptoms, anxiety, pain intensity, vestibular function and medication classes (e.g., psychotropics) were not fully characterised. Taken together, these limitations mean the results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of community-dwelling adults aged 45–81 years, a strong association was observed between impaired balance, high concern about falling, short sleep duration, and lower physical health-related quality of life. Balance impairment on the BBS was identified as a key correlate of both falls and concern about falling, while short sleep duration was found to be strongly associated with balance deficits. Greater concern about falling was associated with lower SF-12 physical scores, highlighting its impact on perceived function.

Although no independent predictors of grip weakness were identified, a multidimensional vulnerability pattern was noted, in which sleep disturbance, balance impairment, fall concern, multimorbidity, polypharmacy, and smoking showed a tendency to cluster from midlife onward. In light of this, the use of multidimensional assessment strategies combining balance tests, assessment of concern about falling, sleep screening, and HRQoL measures is supported for early identification of high-risk individuals. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples and objective sleep and postural-control metrics are essential to clarify causal pathways and to guide integrated interventions targeting sleep, balance, confidence, and medication burden to reduce falls and support healthy ageing.
