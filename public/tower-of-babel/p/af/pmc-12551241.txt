Racial disparities in clonal hematopoiesis and their impact on hematologic malignancies
Authors: Zijian Zhang, Chao Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is a known risk factor for hematologic malignancies (HM), but its distribution and clinical implications across diverse ancestries remain poorly characterized. In this study, we investigated CHIP and its progression to HM in a large, racially diverse cohort from the All of Us Research Program, comprising 245,388 participants. We identified 10,446 CHIP driver mutations in 9,476 individuals. Our analysis revealed clear racial disparities in CHIP prevalence and mutational profiles: African American (AA) individuals had higher odds of CHIP and exhibited distinct mutation patterns compared to White American (WA) individuals. Consistent with prior studies, CHIP was associated with an increased risk of HM, particularly myeloid malignancies. Notably, ancestry influenced the subtype of myeloid malignancy observed; CHIP was more strongly linked to myeloproliferative neoplasms in AA individuals compared with WA individuals. These findings demonstrated significant racial differences in CHIP biology and HM progression, highlighting the need for ancestry-informed approaches to CHIP risk assessment and HM prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13045-025-01740-z.

To the editor

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is age-related expansion of hematopoietic stem cell clones with specific somatic mutations [1]. Although typically asymptomatic, CHIP increase the risk of hematologic malignancies (HM) [2]. Most studies have been limited to single ancestry (e.g., Sweden [3] and Iceland [4]), or small sample sizes [5]. Large-scale cohorts like UK Biobank have advanced our understanding [6, 7], but findings are primarily based on European ancestries, limiting applicability to other racial and ethnic groups. The All of Us Research Program provides a racially diverse cohort [8], with over 20% identifying as “Black, African American, or African”. Leveraging this diversity, we investigated racial differences in CHIP mutations between African Americans (AA) and White Americans (WA), and their distinct associations with hematologic malignancies.

Following a previous study [9], we identified 10,446 CHIP mutations across 55 genes in 9,476 of 245,388 participants with their blood whole-genome sequencing data (Supplementary Table S1). The distribution of clonal sizes, number of variants per individual, and age trends closely matched prior reports [5–7] (Supplementary Figure S1). As expected, CHIP prevalence increased significantly with age. After adjusting for age, sex, and smoking, WAs exhibited a significantly lower CHIP prevalence than AAs (Odds Ratio OR = 0.936[0.878–0.997]; P = 0.040; Fig. 1a). At the gene level, WA individuals were less likely to carry DNMT3A mutations (OR = 0.74[0.68–0.80]; P = 8e-13), particularly the R882H hotspot variant (OR = 0.57[0.41–0.79]; P < 6e-04). In contrast, WAs had higher odds of harboring TET2 mutations (OR = 1.29[1.08–1.54]; P = 0.0048) and PPM1D mutations (OR = 1.65[1.13–2.49]; P = 0.012) (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Table S2). Although prior studies reported that the rs2887399-T allele increased the risk of DNMT3A CHIP and decreased the risk of non-DNMT3A-CHIP [10], WA remained significantly less likely to carry DNMT3A mutations, even after adjusting for this locus (Supplementary Figure S2a).Fig. 1Clonal hematopoiesis and its association with hematologic malignancy in the All of Us cohort. After excluding participants with prior HM, solid cancer, or possible polycythemia vera or essential thrombocythemia [11], we identified 770 incident HM cases: 311 myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), 61 myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), 29 acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and 324 lymphoid malignancies(Supplementary Methods). Using the date of blood sample collection as time zero, we calculated the time to HM diagnosis for each case. The cumulative incidence of HM was 2.44-fold higher in participants with CHIP compared to those without CHIP (Fig. 1c; 3.25% CHIP-positive vs 1.33% in CHIP-negative). In Cox regression analysis adjusting for age, sex, race, and smoking status, CHIP was significantly associated with increased HM risk (hazard ratio HR = 2.00[1.53–2.61]; P = 4e-7; Supplementary Figure S2b). At the gene level, mutations in JAK2, TP53, SF3B1, and PPM1D were strongly associated with increased HM risk (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Table S3). Stratified analysis by HM subtype revealed that CHIP was significantly associated with elevated risk for myeloid malignancies: MDS (HR = 4.42[2.47–7.91]; P = 6e-7), AML (HR = 3.93[1.44–10.76]; P = 0.0076), and MPN (HR = 2.05[1.32–3.17]; P = 0.0013) (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S4). We next performed ancestry-stratified survival analyses. CHIP was significantly associated with increased risk of myeloid malignancy in both populations: WA (HR = 3.17[2.18–4.59]; P = 1e-9) and AA (HR = 3.15[1.41–7.02]; P = 0.0050) (Supplementary Table S4). Although MPN comprised most cases in both WA (67% 163/242) and AA (87%,64/74), the association between CHIP and MPN was stronger in AA (HR = 3.62[1.61–8.14]; P = 0.0019) than in WA (HR = 1.84[1.01–3.36]; P = 0.047) (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table S4). Due to the limited sample size of AA, the racial difference in the risks of MDS and AML cannot be assessed. Further gene-level analysis revealed that the risk of myeloid malignancies progression associated with JAK2 mutations was markedly elevated in both groups but higher in AA (HR = 73.84[17.72–307.77]; P = 3e-9) than in WA (HR = 48.95[25.02–95.77]; P = 6e-30) (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S5). These findings highlight ancestry-related differences in CHIP-associated myeloid malignancy risk and potentially divergent pathogenesis between WA and AA individuals.

While the prospective design and racial diversity of the cohort are key strengths, several limitations remain, including limited power for gene- and ancestry-stratified analyses, unstable estimates for rare CHIP mutations and lack of longitudinal sequencing to monitor clonal evolution. Nonetheless, our findings reveal significant ancestry-related differences in CHIP mutation profiles and their links to specific myeloid malignancies, highlighting the importance of incorporating ancestry to improve risk stratification, surveillance, and prevention.
