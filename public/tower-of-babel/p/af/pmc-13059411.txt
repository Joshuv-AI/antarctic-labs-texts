Participants’ experiences and perceptions of community-based healthy lifestyle clubs in Cambodia: a qualitative study
Authors: Youngran Yang, Oknam Hwang, Sreypov Yuth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNoncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are emerging as a major public health challenge in low- to middle-income countries, including Cambodia. Although community-based health promotion programs have been emphasized to prevent these diseases, the importance of the programs is not evident among participants.We aimed to examine the participants’ experiences and perceptions of community-based health club programs in the Prek Pnov region of Cambodia.MethodsA qualitative research approach was used to assess the participants’ experiences. Thirty adult residents who had participated in such programs for 12–24 months, between 2022 and 2024, were recruited. They were stratified based on age, sex, and duration of participation, allocated evenly to six homogeneous focus groups, and interviewed on perceived program benefits and proposed improvements. Data were analyzed using a six-step thematic analysis, and a participant-informed logic model was developed to illustrate perceived pathways linking inputs, activities, outcomes, and impacts.ResultsAs a result of the data analysis, five main themes were identified: (1) perceived health and psychosocial benefits, (2) improved functional capacity and self-management, (3) social and community-level impact, (4) value perception and willingness to pay, and (5) suggestions for program improvement. Participants described notable improvements in their physical and psychosocial health, including increased energy, enhanced capacity for daily work, and enhanced self-efficacy in managing their health. The program also generated positive family and peer influence and contributed to community-level behavior change. The participants valued these benefits beyond monetary terms and expressed a willingness to pay for continued participation. Finally, they proposed several program improvements, including access to indoor exercise spaces and long-term support from governmental or non-governmental organizations. The logic model demonstrated how community-based activities and peer support contributed to increased NCD awareness, improved self-efficacy for NCD self-management, sustained health behaviors, and ultimately improved perceived quality of life.ConclusionsOur results revealed that community-based health clubs have the potential to be effective and sustainable strategies for NCD prevention and health equity in rural Cambodia. Moreover, these health clubs can serve as effective tools for enhancing community engagement in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26708-7.

Background

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and diabetes, are central to public health issues worldwide, with approximately 75% of all deaths caused by NCDs. Low- to middle -income countries account for 82% of premature deaths from all NCDs, indicating that the burden in these countries is greater [1]. Premature death and disability owing to NCDs have fatal effects on personal and community health as well as increase medical costs, reduce productivity, lower quality of life (QoL), exacerbate poverty, and hinder national economic development [2]. In Cambodia, 64% of all deaths were caused by NCDs in 2018; this rate is increasing annually, showing a double-burden pattern with infectious diseases [3]. Economic loss due to NCDs is estimated to account for 6.6% of the national gross domestic product (GDP), and expenditure for NCD treatment can be up to one-third of household income, creating a vicious cycle of poverty [4, 5]. Under these circumstances, NCD prevention and control have emerged as major public health challenges for developing countries, including Cambodia.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), up to 80% of early cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and diabetes can be prevented by controlling major behavioral risk factors such as smoking, lack of exercise, poor diet, and alcohol consumption [2]. Modifications in lifestyle habits, including regular exercise, a balanced diet, and abstinence from smoking and drinking, are an essential and effective strategy for the prevention and control of NCDs, as they improve metabolic risk factors such as blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels. However, such changes in health-related behaviors are difficult to sustain through individual efforts [6]. Poor lifestyle habits are formed over a long period and cannot be easily corrected [7]. However, lifestyle improvements are achieved more easily when accompanied by changes in physical or social environments [8, 9]. Particularly, when residents participate together at the community level to form an environment of mutual encouragement, it helps increase the practice of health behaviors and improve health equity [10, 11].

Therefore, community-based group programs have recently become a topic of interest in the public health sector. Community-based interventions are approaches to improving health behaviors using a non-clinical setting and adapting to the needs of the community. Residents meet in village units and form health clubs or self-help groups to exercise together or practice healthy diets. This approach has been highlighted as a method of improving adherence through social support networks [12]. Group education programs for patients with diabetes or obesity improve clinical and psychosocial indices, and enhance not only information delivery but also provide therapeutic benefits [13]. Furthermore, programs that integrate community-based peer support amplify lifestyle habit improvements by providing participants with mutual support and motivation [14]. Recent scoping reviews have provided additional evidence that group lifestyle habit interventions significantly improve metabolic syndrome-related risk factors [15]. These resident-led healthcare projects are an especially effective option for overcoming the lack of personal and geographical restrictions, and pursuing sustainable health improvements in settings without ample healthcare resources, such as rural areas [11].

In Cambodia, as part of the 2022–2024 Official Development Assistance project, community-based health club programs have been conducted for 3 years, funded by the Korea International Cooperation Agency (KOICA) to support adherence to a healthy lifestyle among residents with a high NCD risk. However, no studies have investigated the perceived value of these programs or satisfaction among participating residents. Efforts have been made to ascertain the needs and value of resident-led health promotion programs in some overseas regions. Previous studies from high-income countries, such as the United Kingdom and Spain, have identified key facilitators and barriers in community-led health promotion initiatives, providing valuable insight into community engagement and participation [16, 17]. In South and Southeast Asia, where NCD represent a major public health priority, community-based NCD prevention and management have also been implemented in countries such as Indonesia and Viet Nam, with evidence supporting their cost-effectiveness in reducing cardiovascular events and mortality [18, 19]. However, empirical evidence examining community-based NCD programs in resource-limited countries, particularly qualitative evaluations focusing on participant experiences and mechanisms of engagement, remains limited.

To overcome these limitations, we recruited residents who had participated in health club programs to practice a healthy lifestyle in a community in Cambodia, and qualitatively analyzed their experiences and perceptions of the program’s value. Thus, we aimed to derive insights for the effective development and management of resident-led community health promotion programs. The purpose of this study was to explore the experiences and perceptions of participants who joined a community-based health club program promoting healthy lifestyles in 2022–2024. We believe that our findings will serve as a foundation for the planning and sustainable management of community-based healthy lifestyle promotion programs for NCD prevention and management in developing countries such as Cambodia.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a qualitative design utilizing focus group interviews (FGIs) to investigate participants’ experiences and perceptions of a community-based health club program. FGIs are a form of group interview in which a small number of participants engage in guided discussion on a specific topic, allowing meanings, perspectives, and interpretations to be co-constructed through group interaction. FGIs were conducted in this study to generate rich data and gain an in-depth understanding of the topic, as they facilitated interactions and the exchange of ideas among participants who shared the common experience [20].

Community-Based Healthy Lifestyle Clubs Program

The Community-Based Healthy Lifestyle Clubs program was implemented as part of a KOICA-funded project between April 2022 and December 2024 in Prek Pnov, an urban district of Phnom Penh, Cambodia. Prek Pnou is an area of ​​extreme poverty. Due to the development of Phnom Penh, many people have migrated to the outskirts of the city and live in stilt houses. Depending on the commune within Prek Pnok, more than 80% of the population works in day labor, such as construction. Access to healthcare and social infrastructure is severely limited. Many residents work as day laborers in factories and other industrial settings. The intervention site was selected to address health inequalities among economically vulnerable populations facing barriers to healthcare access, in line with the principles of KOICA, the funding agency. The program aimed to improve healthy lifestyle practices and reduce the burden of non-communicable diseases through community-based group activities.

During the program, Club members met daily at predetermined times and participated in structured physical activities such as dancing, group walking, and following exercise videos (e.g., YouTube-based workouts). To enhance engagement and ownership, each club elected a representative who coordinated meeting schedules, established club rules, and assigned member roles. The clubs typically consisted of small community groups, and the activities were conducted in informal neighborhood settings, including temple courtyards, community centers, and other shared outdoor spaces within villages that are commonly used for community gatherings. The program was operated in collaboration with peer educators (PEs) and village health support group (VHSG) members, who received structured training from the project team and who played central roles as health club leaders. They were responsible for facilitating health club activities, communicating project-related announcements, and encouraging club members to participate actively in the activities. They were also involved in conducting monthly health assessments, including blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, body weight, and waist circumference, and entered the monitoring data into the project app system. Participation in the program did not incur any direct financial cost to members, as all activities were provided free of charge as part of the KOICA-supported community initiative; activities were conducted within participants’ neighborhoods, thereby minimizing transportation and other indirect costs.

Participants and Recruitment

Participants were eligible if they had joined the health club program and voluntarily agreed to share their experiences and opinions. The club members were adults aged 40 years and older residing in Prek Pnov who met at least two of the five metabolic syndrome criteria (blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, waist circumference, triglycerides, and blood glucose) based on community health screening results or had a history of hypertension or diabetes, agreed to participate in the health club activities, and remained engaged in the program for 1–2 years.

Village health support group members and peer educators who facilitated the health club activities were first informed of the purpose and procedures of the study, including group interviews. These community leaders explained the study to their group members and helped identify individuals who expressed interest in participating. Subsequently, one of the authors (S.Y.), who served as the local research assistant contacted each participant individually to provide information about the procedure and obtain written informed consent. Thirty participants were recruited from 655 individuals who participated in the health club program during the KOICA project period. Participants were divided into six groups, each consisting of five participants.

Focus Group Interview Procedures

Group interviews were conducted in accordance with the methodological guidance provided by Archer [21], who emphasized structured facilitation to maintain the quality and inclusiveness during FGIs. Each focus group interview was conducted jointly by a primary non-Khmer-speaking researcher (O.H.) and local Cambodian researcher (S.Y.). The sessions were conducted primarily in English, with real-time interpretation in Khmer provided by the local researcher (S.Y.) to ensure clarity and cultural sensitivity and to support participants in freely expressing their experiences and perspectives. Discussions involving culturally sensitive and personal health issues, such as perceptions of illness and stigma, financial constraints related to healthcare, and hesitancy in healthy lifestyle practices, were carefully facilitated by the interpreter to ensure participants’ comfort. Expert consultation was not undertaken; instead, the guide was refined internally by Y.Y. and S.Y. to ensure linguistic clarity, cultural appropriateness, and sensitivity to potentially delicate themes.

The semi-structured interview guide covered the following domains: (1) perceived usefulness and personal experience of the health club program, (2) acceptable payment ranges for continued program participation, (3) awareness of the program’s sustainability and potential impact on individuals and the community, and (4) suggestions for improving the program. Examples of questions included “What do you find most valuable about the health club program?”, “What challenges did you face in participating?”, and “What are your thoughts about paying for this service?”. The interview guide was initially developed in English by the first author (Y.Y.) and subsequently translated to Khmer by S.Y. Expert consultation was not undertaken; instead, the guide was refined internally by Y.Y. O.H., and S.Y. to ensure linguistic clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension. The moderator was the primary non-Khmer speaking but English-speaking researcher (O.H.), who read each question twice to allow time for comprehension and reflection and actively guided the discussion to ensure that all participants had equal opportunities to contribute, minimizing dominance by any single individual. Probing techniques were used to elicit deeper insights and clarify ambiguous responses, ensuring the richness and depth of the data. Examples of probes are “Would you explain further?”; “Would you give me an example of what you mean?”; “Would you say more?”; and “Is there anything else?” (Additional File 1).

Interviews were conducted in the seminar room of the Prek Pnov Operational Health District and Prek Pnov Referral Hospital between April 18 and 23, 2025. Each session lasted approximately 90–150 min, with an average duration of 120 min. The FGIs were audio recorded to clearly capture participant voices and visual expressions, while also distinguishing speakers facilitating accurate transcription and theme development. Additionally, participants were asked to complete a brief sociodemographic questionnaire that included items on sex, age, marital status, educational attainment, occupation, and monthly household income (Additional File 1). To acknowledge their time and contribution, each participant received a modest incentive of USD 20.

Data Management and Analysis

All FGIs were transcribed verbatim into English by a trained Cambodian-English bilingual researcher (S.Y.), who attended all FGIs to assist the moderator during the interviews. To ensure accurate transcription and capture the atmosphere, tone, and nuances expressed by the participants, the research team reviewed the audio recordings of the FGIs and verified the accuracy of the data.

The data were analyzed using a six-phase thematic analysis framework [22] for systematically identifying, analyzing, and interpreting recurring patterns (themes) within the data. First, the researchers familiarized themselves with the data through repeated readings of the transcripts to achieve immersion. The initial codes were generated by labeling the relevant features of the data line-by-line. These codes were organized into potential themes by identifying the patterns and connections among them. Next, the themes were reviewed and refined to ensure coherence and distinction between themes. The next phase involved defining and naming each theme by clearly articulating its meaning. Finally, the themes were reported in detail, supported by representative quotes from the data to support the key findings. Data were managed and analyzed using an Excel spreadsheet, where transcripts were organized, labeled, and manually coded. Coding, category development, and theme refinement were conducted manually by the research team, and an Excel-based matrix was used to compare patterns across participants and domains. Based on the findings, a participant-informed logic model was subsequently developed to illustrate perceived pathways linking program inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and longer-term impacts.

Ethical deconsiderations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jeonbuk National University (IRB No. JBNU-2025-04-004). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, the permission to publish the findings of this study was obtained in accordance with the KOICA Research and Survey Program Regulations, which govern the dissemination of studies involving participants from KOICA-supported projects. The interviews were conducted after the completion of the KOICA-funded project, which had obtained ethical approval from the National Ethical Committee for Health Research in Cambodia and from JBNU in South Korea. The interviews involved only non-identifiable perceptions, such as participants’ general perspectives, experiences, and reflections on the program. No names, personal identifiers, or sensitive medical information were collected. Therefore, additional local ethical approval was not required.

Participation in the study was voluntary and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, duration, audio recording, confidentiality measures, and right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Anonymity was protected by de-identifying transcripts, replacing participant names with codes, and analyzing and reporting data without individual identification.

Results

Characteristics of the Participants

Thirty participants were included in the study. The mean age was 55.1 years (standard deviation [SD] = 8.98; range 41–71 years). In terms of sex distribution, 53.3% were female and 46.7% were male. Most participants were married (86.7%), 40.0% had completed primary school, and 33.3% had completed secondary school. Notably, 13.3% of the participants reported having no formal schooling, meaning they had never attended any structured educational institution. In terms of occupation, 40.0% of participants did not engage in any income-generating work. All the participants were members of a community-based health club program. Among these, 70.0% had participated in the program for 24 months and 30.0% had participated for 12 months. Most individuals (66.7%) joined the program in 2022, and the remaining 33.3% joined in 2023. The average monthly household income before tax was $281 (SD = 143; range: $50–$665). Monthly household medical expenditures averaged $70 (SD = 83; range: $3–$400), whereas individual monthly health-related expenses averaged $30 (SD = 41; range: $2.5–$200). All participants resided in Phnom Penh, an urban setting. Except for the four individuals who reported having no formal schooling, all others were able to read and write (Table 1).

Satisfaction with the Health Club Program

All participants (n = 30) across the six focus groups reported being highly satisfied with the community-based health club program. Most participants highlighted physical exercise sessions as the most valued element. This was followed by health checkups, which were appreciated for providing accessible health monitoring in community settings. In addition to exercise and health checkups, a few participants mentioned other valued components, such as healthy dietary education, smoking cessation, and alcohol abstinence education, particularly in Groups 4 and 6.

Interview Data Themes

As a result of the data analysis, 5 main themes and 11 sub-themes were identified. These encompassed participants’ perceived benefits of the program, their willingness to pay (WTP) and the underlying factors shaping the perceived monetary value of the services, as well as their suggestions for program improvement (Table 1).

Theme 1. Perceived Health and Psychosocial Benefits

First, participants across all focus groups shared that they experienced notable improvements in physical health outcomes, which they attributed directly to the structured exercise and dietary guidance provided by the health club. These include a reduction in blood pressure and blood glucose levels, weight loss, improved digestion, and relief from chronic pain in areas such as the knees, shoulders, and lower back. One participant emphasized the degree of change by stating, “Before I started exercising, my blood sugar was never below 270. Just a few days ago, it reduced to only 151. After 3 months, my HbA1c dropped from 7% to 6, and now it stays at 5.3.” Another shared, “My blood pressure was 250, and now it is around 150–160. I was supposed to die.” These narratives reflect a widespread belief among participants that their health status had drastically improved, in some cases, preventing what they perceived as a life-threatening outcome.

In addition to measurable health outcomes, the participants reported significant changes in their knowledge, attitudes, and health-related behaviors. Several of them learned about the negative effects of excessive salt, sugar, fat, alcohol, and late-night eating and adjusted their diets accordingly. One participant noted, “I learned a lot about reducing salt and sugar… I now know more about eating vegetables and exercising regularly.”

Finally, participation in a health club was perceived to have psychosocial and emotional benefits. Several participants described feeling more relaxed and happier, and less stressed and angry, after participating in the club program. Improvements in sleep quality, mood, and emotional stability were frequently mentioned. They emphasized how engaging in group exercises created a sense of belonging and motivation, which helped them maintain their regular routines. One participant stated, “Meeting friends, sharing good experiences, and exercising together helped me mentally and physically.”

Theme 2. Improved Functional Capacity and Self-Management

Participants reported improved physical functioning and greater confidence in maintaining a healthier daily life after joining the health club program. These experiences included increased energy and enhanced capacity for daily tasks and strengthened self-efficacy for NCD self-management. The participants of all FGIs noted that they became more physically capable and productive in their daily lives after joining the health club. They described being capable of performing household tasks more efficiently, working longer hours without fatigue, and efficiently managing tasks such as farming, caregiving, and food vending. These improvements were often attributed to reduction pain and fatigue, as well as improved blood circulation resulting from regular exercise. One participant shared, “I used to get tired easily from work, but now I can work a lot both inside and outside the house.” Another explained, “I can now peel a 20 kg papaya in 10 min, and I no longer feel tired walking for 60 min.”

Additionally, they expressed a strong sense of increased self-efficacy in managing their health. Several reported positive improvements, such as regular monitoring of blood pressure and glucose, scheduling time for exercise, and encouraging family members to adopt similar behaviors. One participant stated, “Now I can monitor my blood sugar and blood pressure and take care of myself.” Overall, the program not only improved functional ability, but also instilled a sense of personal responsibility and capability for long-term self-management.

Theme 3. Social and Community-Level Impact

The participants’ involvement in the program contributed to broader changes in family dynamics, social relationships, and community health norms. The sub-themes include influence on family and peers and the ripple effects of behavioral change throughout the village. Several participants reported that positive transformation of their health and lifestyle habits motivated others in their households and villages to follow suit. One participant noted: “When the villagers saw me exercising, they wanted to exercise too, and they said that their health is better.” Others shared similar experiences in which their spouses, children, or neighbors began joining walks or adopted healthier eating habits after observing their changes, as shared by one participant: “My 90-year-old father-in-law now exercises daily. My children cook healthy meals and tell others about the importance of health.” At the community level, participants observed that their general attitudes toward health began to shift. A change of the unhealthy behaviors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, was also frequently reported as a community-wide change. “Now, they are ashamed of drinking alcohol or smoking, and are reducing or abstaining from it.” Moreover, the program helped strengthen social ties. Participants described feeling more connected to others in the village, which improved not only their physical well-being but also their social cohesion and mutual support.

Theme 4. Value Perception

Several participants expressed that the benefits they gained, such as reduced medical expenses, improved well-being, and prevention of serious illness, far exceeded the monetary costs. However, the way they translated this value into dollars or riel varied. Some could cite the exact amounts they were willing to pay based on prior medical spending, whereas others felt that health care was beyond monetary value. Participants stated, “I used to spend about $100 a month on medical bills, mainly on doctor consultations and medication, but now it’s significantly reduced. It’s hard to quantify happiness or health in monetary terms.”, “I think it’s worth $1,000 or even $2,000—because I spent a lot before on physical therapy.”

When participants were asked about their willingness to pay for a monthly subscription, the amounts they reported varied widely, ranging from approximately $2.50 to $40 per month. Several participants framed their response by comparing the cost to private gyms or hospital visits, and many considered $15–30/month to be reasonable if the program continued to offer exercise, education, and health check-ups. “$30, because I already pay that much for a gym, excluding training and health check-up.”

Importantly, a few expressed concerns that if the program became too expensive, poor residents would not be able to participate, thereby reducing the program’s accessibility and community cohesion.

Theme 5. Suggestions and Support Needs

Although most participants expressed high satisfaction with the program, they provided constructive suggestions to make it more effective and inclusive. The most commonly mentioned improvement was to ensure access to indoor or sheltered exercise spaces, particularly during the rainy season. Several participants also requested additional equipment and supplies such as blood glucose home test kits, blood pressure monitors, scales, and instructional materials for leaders. One stated: “We need an exercise space where we can go even when it rains.”

At the policy and institutional levels, participants called for continued or expanded governmental and non-governmental organization (NGO) support to help sustain the program, especially by covering resource-intensive components such as health check-ups, essential medications, and health education services. Some stressed the need for health education that specifically targets rural populations and low-literacy groups, as these residents may not understand the value of prevention unless actively engaged. One suggested; “Residents with low education levels don’t care about health and they seem to give up when they are sick or even dying. Go to their homes and bring them in.” These suggestions illustrate the participants’ desire not only to sustain and improve the health club model, but also for its expansion and support as a long-term community health resource.

Conceptual logic model derived from participants’ perspectives

Based on the qualitative findings, a conceptual logic model was developed to illustrate participants’ perceived pathways linking program components (Fig. 1). The model follows a logical progression. If core resources, including KOICA funding, the mHealth platform, and the support of peer educators and village health support group (VHSG) members, are utilized to implement regular community-based activities such as health screening, lifestyle club sessions, and monthly health assessments, then outputs can be achieved, including regular participation in health club activities and peer interaction. These activities were perceived as essential by participants; for example, ongoing peer support and encouragement enhanced motivation to participate in group exercise. If these outputs are achieved and program activities are maintained, then outcomes are expected to occur, including increased NCD awareness, enhanced self-efficacy for NCD self-management, and strengthened social connectedness. Participants perceived these outcomes as necessary mechanisms bridging initial participation and long-term impact, as increased awareness and self-efficacy supported a shift toward effective health management, while social connectedness fostered trust and sustained engagement in healthy behaviors. Ultimately, this pathway was perceived to lead to long-term impacts, including improved management of health conditions, reduced perceived NCD risk, and enhanced quality of life at both individual and community levels.

Discussion

Overall, this study demonstrated that community-based health club programs in Cambodia have multifaceted positive effects on residents’ physical health, psychosocial well-being, and functional ability. While participating in health club activities, the participants described having experienced improved physical health, including significant decreases in blood glucose and blood pressure levels, weight loss, improved digestion, and relief from ongoing chronic pain symptoms. The fact that community-based exercise programs can improve the indices of chronic diseases and lower health risks is consistent with the findings of several previous studies. For example, in Portugal, a 9-month community-based exercise program was administered to middle-aged and older patients with diabetes, and the regular exercise group showed significant improvements in glycated hemoglobin, fasting blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels, resulting in a decrease in the 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease [23]. Among older participants in Japan, a community-based exercise program was reported to show broad effects, including not only balanced improvements in physical, mental, and social health through regular group exercise but also improved functional health maintenance, happiness, and community bonding [24]. This evidence is consistent with the statements of participants in the present study and demonstrates why community-based physical activity promotion programs are rated as the ‘best-buy’ strategy for prevention of chronic disease [25]. The WHO also actively recommends a whole-of-community approach for the prevention of NCDs through lifestyle improvements [26].

In addition to physical benefits, the program participants reported acquiring health knowledge that most did not possess before and were able to improve their dietary and lifestyle habits based on this knowledge. In other words, health club activities aided the learning of preventive health strategies and attaining self-care skills for these participants. Aligning with this, in a qualitative study of community-based lifestyle habit improvement program in Singapore, participants reported improved health and nutrition knowledge that greatly helped them achieve their lifestyle habit goals [27]. In addition to knowledge acquisition, the participants in our study reported a large increase in their self-efficacy for NCD-management or belief in their ability to care for their health. Participants stated that the program gave them confidence and motivation to manage and take responsibility for their own health, including regularly checking their blood pressure and blood glucose, finding time in their daily schedule for exercise, and even encouraging healthy behaviors among their families. Improvements in psychological capabilities (empowerment) and self-efficacy have been noted as key outcomes of community-based interventions [28]. This shows that the presence of a peer group sharing information and exercising together at the health club, a devoted leader serving as a positive role model, and social motivator directed participants to continuously practice the desired behaviors [29]. This is consistent with insights from previous studies, in which solidarity and mutual support in community-based interventions were identified as important mechanisms promoting behavioral change [28].

After participating in the health club, the participants in the present study showed improved daily functioning and work abilities. Increased physical functionality and active abilities directly helped improve the QoL and productivity. In particular, in rural village communities in developing countries, where much of the population is economically active, improved labor ability due to health improvements can have positive downstream effects on household income and the local economy. Regular physical activity increases participants’ muscle strength and balance ability, and improves their performance in activities of daily living [24, 30]. Our findings provide further evidence to support this observation. Increased emotional well-being and self-contentment were also consistent with the psychiatric benefits reported by participants. The joy and psychosocial satisfaction provided by the program had major effects on the participants’ WTP and continued participation. Health clubs not only improved the physical health through exercise but also improved mental health by offering a place for social exchange. Stress relief is consistent with a community-based exercise program for Hispanic adults, which was reported to promote psychiatric health, social interaction, and stress relief [31]. These findings demonstrate the multidimensional value of community-based health promotion programs.

Another important observation was that the effects of health club participation extended to the broader community and surrounding society. The third theme, social and community-level impact, demonstrates the program’s potential to create sustainable community-driven health movements. Several participants testified that personal changes extended in the form of behavioral changes among family and neighbors, suggesting that the health clubs contributed to the formation of a healthy culture in the community. These ripple effects, also called multiplier effects, go beyond the original direct effects and cause a chain of additional effects in the surrounding environment [32]. In other words, health club participants triggered a domino effect through their positive role as ‘health champions’ in the family and community [33]. In this way, community-based interventions are not limited to short-term personal change but are a complex social process that can even change the culture and norms of the community [34]. Cambodia has developed as a collectivist/community-oriented culture and displays a strong social characteristic of social influence sensitivity that responds more sensitively to relationships with family and others than with individuals [35]. Therefore, these characteristics should be considered when developing policies for NCD prevention and management in Cambodia. In this context, the strong community-oriented culture may facilitate the sustainability of official development assistance (ODA) programs, as residents who have experienced the program’s benefits can play a central role in voluntarily mobilizing neighbors after the program ends. However, the challenges of maintaining leadership stability and securing institutional support during the transition to government-led or NGO-managed models will need to be addressed.

Another key theme of this study was the high perceived value of the program. All participants in the focus groups rated the health club’s value much higher than its monetary cost. When asked how much they would be willing to pay per month, the participants gave diverse responses ranging from 2.5 USD to 40 USD. In particular, many participants responded that the program would be sufficiently valuable at a cost of 15–30 USD per month, considering the costs of private fitness centers or hospital visits. This price range is much higher than that reported in a similar study conducted in Thailand. The WTP for a community-based exercise program was investigated among 472 middle-aged and older persons from Thailand, and 54% of the respondents wanted the program to be free, while the others were only willing to pay a small fee; the mean WTP was only 2 USD per month [25]. However, the group with previous experience of participating in organized exercise programs showed a significantly higher WTP, indicating that individuals who feel the direct benefits of the program will invest more actively in health. In another study of 268 participants in a community-based physical activity program for socially vulnerable groups in the Netherlands, the WTP was reported to be €9.6 (approximately 10 USD) per month; moreover, WTP was lower for participants with a lower income, while positive perceptions of health status, previous sports club experience, and the joy of physical activity had positive effects on WTP [36]. This shows that it is important to provide opportunities for positive experiences before investment, and that high WTP among participants can be interpreted as an indicator of high satisfaction with the program and certainty of effectiveness. However, if the program is too expensive, community solidarity can weaken, making it difficult for economically vulnerable residents to participate. Therefore, in the future, even if programs become monetarized, it will be important to maintain inclusivity and equity by implementing pricing policies that make allowances for vulnerable groups or provide financial support from the central or local government. The World Bank introduced the Health Equity and Quality Improvement Project, which provides support in the form of healthcare and transport costs to ensure health equity among vulnerable groups in Cambodia [37]. It is essential to support participation in health promotion programs for the practice of healthy lifestyle habits. This is consistent with proposals by previous researchers, who suggested that the outcomes of community-based health promotion programs should be reflected in policies to encourage public support [25].

Finally, we discuss the implications of participants’ suggestions regarding program improvements and the need for continued support. The most frequently proposed item was securing an indoor exercise space that could be used even under poor weather conditions. The current program was conducted outside or on vacant land in the village; however, considering the tropical climate in Cambodia, the program gets suspended during the monsoon season or in times of bad weather, which makes year-round continuity challenging. Preparing a space unaffected by weather, such as an indoor gymnasium or village hall, is important for maintaining participation rates and preventing accidents. From the perspective of Herzberg’s motivator-hygiene theory, program satisfaction and continued participation require not only motivating factors, such as enjoyment or a sense of achievement through exercise, but also the fulfilment of hygiene factors, including location, equipment, safety, and costs [30]. From an institutional/policy perspective, most participants wanted the program not to be a one-time program but to continue and improve over the long term. Many participants expressed the opinion that this would require continuous support from the government’s healthcare administration or NGOs to cover health examination costs drugs, and the preparation of educational materials, which are difficult to manage at the village level. Particularly notable was the need to visit health education centers and encourage participation among less-educated residents in rural villages. From a health equity perspective, the program should be accompanied by active and inclusive strategies to accommodate vulnerable groups. It is important to explore strategies to link the program with the local primary healthcare system and utilize healthcare workers (e.g., health professionals and village health volunteers). The 2019 NCD Strategy of the Cambodian Ministry of Health declared that “facility-based healthcare and community interventions would be run in parallel as part of a sustainable response to NCDs” [38]. They are currently reviewing strategies to improve the competencies of Village Health Support Groups employed in chronic disease prevention and management [3]. In this context, it is important to explore the policy expansion of health-club models as a case study of community-based NCD prevention and management. In the future, program staffing and supplies could be supported through local government budgets or international organizations, such as the WHO, UN, and World Bank. Strategies to integrate the program into the public healthcare system could be considered, such as regular education and health checks provided by local public health center professionals. If the program is made sustainable and extended to more communities, it could greatly contribute to chronic disease prevention strategies in developing countries such as Cambodia [28].

Although prior studies cited above have indicated that community-based health promotion programs improve social connectedness and self-efficacy [24, 28, 29, 31], few studies have investigated how participants conceptualize the value and mechanisms underlying behavioral change, particularly in community-led programs. This study extends the existing literature, suggesting that peer-driven motivation, collective accountability, and community-level norm shifts can be perceived as key mechanisms contributing to program effectiveness. Future NCD-related ODA programs in resource-limited settings can apply this mechanism by mapping the findings onto typical components of a logic model, including inputs (peer-led and community-based health club activities), mechanisms (group motivation and social accountability), and outcomes (improved healthy lifestyle practices, self-management, and functional capacity).

One of the strengths of this study is that, since we qualitatively explored the in-depth experiences of residents who participated in the health club for 1–2 years between 2022 and 2024, the results reflected their opinions on the actual effects and value of the program. In particular, because the program was not appraised based on a short-term intervention but reflects the experiences of participating for up to 2 years, we were able to observe not only the initial effects but also the established changes and continuation within daily living. In addition, by collecting opinions from 30 individuals of diverse sex, age groups, and health statuses across the six focus groups, we were able to derive core themes shared by and affecting several populations. All the participants were highly satisfied in the program, and we were able to obtain rich descriptions of their positive experiences.

However, these methods have certain inherent limitations. First, because the sample consisted of continuous participants in the program and individuals with high satisfaction levels, it is possible that the sample was biased toward successful cases. We were unable to reflect on the opinions of dissatisfied residents or those who dropped out; therefore, information on the challenges and negative aspects of the health club was limited. In future studies, it will be necessary to investigate the perspectives of non-participants and dropouts, as well as barriers to participation. Individual interviews may be considered in future studies to reduce the social desirability bias. Second, owing to the nature of qualitative studies, there were limitations in generalizing the results. As this was a case study conducted in one region on the outskirts of Phnom Penh, the results may not be directly applicable to other regions in Cambodia or rural areas in other countries. In addition, interviewing project staff and service providers could offer a more well-rounded understanding of the program’s implementation and perceived impact. Nevertheless, the main themes derived in this study are consistent with those reported in several previous studies (24,28–29,31,33–34), offering theme-specific insights for the implementation of similar community-based health promotion programs, particularly the emphasis on social connectedness, peer support, and self-efficacy. Considering the circumstances of the interviews, there is a possibility that participants responded positively out of gratitude to the individuals running the program. However, considering that the participants expressed relatively honest and critical opinions about potential improvements, we concluded that the expressions of satisfaction was at least partially grounded in actual experiences. Nevertheless, when interpreting our findings, it is essential to be aware of the bias that could have occurred due to the limitations of the study design.

The participant-informed logic model provides insight into how the community-based health club program functioned in practice from the perspectives of participants. The model highlights psychosocial and relational processes, such as peer support, social connectedness, and self-efficacy for NCD self-management, as central mechanisms linking program activities to sustained behavioral engagement. By articulating these pathways, the conceptual logic model extends existing evidence by clarifying how community-based NCD programs are experienced and internalized by participants, thereby offering practical implications for the design and adaptation of similar interventions in comparable contexts.

In future studies, the development of community nursing practice models could be improved by including not only participating residents, but also community nurses and primary healthcare workers, to explore the role these health clubs can fulfil and the effects their interventions on the self-care abilities and health outcomes of participants. Finally, we propose the need for programs that specialize in specific disease groups or targets. The health clubs in this study were aimed at middle-aged and older individuals at high risk of chronic disease. Future research could be extended to validate the effects of customized community-based programs for other populations, such as younger participants and pregnant women, and for people with specific diseases, such as hypertension or diabetes. Its applicability can be also broadened to evaluate the effectiveness of tailored programs in a variety of settings, such as corporate organizations, public institutions, and community facilities. If the health club model is reinforced and developed through these multifaceted studies, we anticipate that the programs will be firmly established as a strategy for sustainable, community-based health promotion to alleviate the growing chronic disease burden in Cambodia and other developing countries. Lastly, integrating routine monitoring of the implementation process and outcome assessment into ODA projects is essential for ensuring transparency and generating evidence that can guide program adaptation for specific population groups or implementing bodies.

Conclusions

In this study, we qualitatively demonstrated that health club programs in rural villages in Cambodia have positive effects on residents’ health outcomes, self-efficacy for NCD self-management, and community health culture. The participants’ experiences revealed that the program not only improved their physical health but also had multidimensional effects, helping with the formation of social support networks and promoting psychological well-being. In particular, the high WTP and demand for improvement provide evidence of the economic sustainability of the program and policy expansion. Future studies should reinforce and expand the current model by reviewing the perspectives of nonparticipants and dropouts, considering the roles of healthcare workers, and developing customized programs for each patient. Thus, the health-club model may be established as a realistic strategy to prevent NCDs and promote health equity in Cambodia and other resource-poor countries.
