Remote digital cognitive assessment in a trial‐ready Alzheimer's disease cohort: A scalable approach for early intervention studies

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTINTRODUCTIONEarly detection of cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease (AD), particularly in preclinical stages, is critical for evaluating therapeutic interventions. Traditional cognitive assessments often require lengthy in‐person visits, and may therefore limit scalability for younger, trial‐ready populations for primary and secondary prevention studies. We evaluated the feasibility, reliability, and validity of high‐frequency remote digital cognitive assessments in individuals with autosomal dominant AD (ADAD).METHODSOne hundred twenty‐three mutation carriers and non‐carriers from the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network Trials Unit from 20 international sites (Ages 19–66 years) completed remote assessments via personal smartphones, prompted four times daily for 7 days (≈ 3 minutes per session), and conventional in‐clinic cognitive testing at baseline. Adherence, between‐person reliability, test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficients [ICCs]), construct validity (confirmatory factor analysis), and sensitivity to clinical disease progression were evaluated.RESULTSRemote measures demonstrated excellent reliability (ICCs > 0.90 after just 10 sessions) and strong construct validity, with tasks loading onto memory, attention, and executive function domains. A composite of the remote tests slightly outperformed a traditional composite at separating mutation carriers from non‐carriers. Average adherence to the study protocol was 42%, lower that observed in previous studies of older adults.DISCUSSIONHigh‐frequency remote cognitive assessment is feasible, reliable, and valid in a relatively young international ADAD cohort. This approach offers substantial utility for clinical trials, including improved accessibility and enhanced reliability, supporting its integration into early‐intervention studies.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the accumulation of amyloid beta plaques and neurofibrillary tangles of the protein tau, which lead to progressive loss of cognitive function. It is widely accepted that the optimal time for pharmacological or behavioral intervention may be years before clinical symptoms manifest. Thus, studies have started to target either individuals who have biomarker evidence of AD in the absence of clinical impairment (secondary prevention), or even individuals who have yet to develop abnormal pathology (primary prevention). Demonstrating the cognitive benefits of treatment in secondary and primary prevention populations is rather difficult, however, as standard clinical and cognitive instruments, such as the Mini‐Mental State Examination, the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale Cognitive subscale, and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) show little to no change in cognitively normal populations. 1 , 2 , 3

It is becoming more common to examine the utility of in‐depth neuropsychological tests that form a comprehensive cognitive battery as alternative endpoints for demonstrating treatment efficacy, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 which have been shown to be sensitive to decline even in preclinical AD. 8 Nevertheless, cognitive change in preclinical samples remains very small, even when using sensitive cognitive test batteries. 8 Therefore, sample sizes in clinical trials need to be very large (> 2000 per treatment arm) to detect clinically meaningful effects, 9 presenting large burden on study infrastructure and greatly slowing the rate of treatment discovery. New assessment methodologies are clearly needed that can reach and assess large numbers of individuals at high scale. One approach to addressing this issue is to administer traditional cognitive testing remotely via videoconferencing 10 or with specialized digital testing smartphone applications, 11 both of which permit a robust cognitive assessment without the need for travel to a testing site and without the need for administration by research personnel, potentially increasing the speed with which new participants can be enrolled.

Some studies have taken the digital assessment approach one step further by administering cognitive tests multiple times over a short interval (e.g., several times per day for a week 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ), an approach that we term “high‐frequency cognitive assessment.” This enables researchers to fully capture cognitive performance across a range of contexts (e.g., time of day, sleep quality, mood, etc.), and affords access to alternative metrics of performance including learning curves 15 and day‐to‐day variability. 12 , 16 Extensive empirical work has shown that high‐frequency cognitive assessment using remote digital testing substantially improves test reliability; 17 is tolerable and feasible for older adults (typically aged ≥ 60 years) who are cognitively healthy or have mild impairment; 12 , 14 and is able to capture subtle effects associated with time of day, mood, and other factors. 13 , 18 Most importantly, detailed modeling of high‐frequency cognitive data may ultimately reduce the total sample size needed to detect intervention effects by averaging out day‐to‐day noise. 19 Ultimately, remote assessments (high frequency or otherwise) may negate the need for comprehensive in‐person assessment, allowing for cognitive assessments to be delivered at scale, and greatly facilitating recruitment of the large sample sizes that secondary and primary prevention studies will ultimately require.

Remote cognitive assessments are not without their limitations, however. There are established differences in technology access and preferences as a function of education and race 20 and technology familiarity is reduced among older (relative to younger) adults, 21 which may produce subtle biases in remotely collected outcomes. Furthermore, for self‐administered assessments, there is the risk for distractors in the testing environment to artificially impair performance. One report indicates that up to 25% of remote assessments were contaminated by interruptions which negatively impacted performance on key outcomes. 22 Thus, if remote digital testing is to be used to describe the cognitive progression of AD and ultimately as primary outcome in clinical trials, it is essential to establish the construct validity and feasibility of remotely delivered assessments. Extensive work has validated the use of remote assessments in older adults 12 , 14 , 23 yet much less work has been done in mid‐life with persons who may ultimately be enrolled in primary prevention studies.

The primary aim of the current study was to provide validation of remote cognitive assessments in younger participants compared to gold‐standard, in‐clinic cognitive assessments with known sensitivity to AD pathology. For remote testing to be valid it must pass (at least) two essential tests. First, remote outcomes must map onto the same underlying cognitive constructs that have been repeatedly established using in‐clinic testing. Second, it must show similar, or better, sensitivity to AD pathology as already established paper‐and‐pencil tests. We hypothesize that remote testing measures will load onto the same latent constructs as traditional paper‐and‐pencil testing and provide equivalent explanatory power in explaining differences in participants associated with AD pathology. In addition to establishing validity, it is essential to provide guidance on how many remote assessments are needed. Operating under the assumption that one should administer as few assessments as needed to meet study goals, we provide estimation of the number of tests that would be required to meet minimum reliability criteria.

We leverage a unique cohort of participants who have an autosomal dominant form of AD (ADAD). ADAD results from mutations in specific genes, and typically presents at a relatively young age (≈ 46 years of age) with clinical symptoms that are highly predictable in onset. 24 , 25 ADAD mutations are rare and comprise < 1% of all dementia cases, yet the predictability of clinical progression in this population has provided a useful model for the more common sporadic and late‐onset form of the disease. 3 , 24

METHOD

Participants

Participants for these analyses were drawn from the Cognitive Run‐In (CRI) study, a part of the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network Trials Unit (DIAN‐TU) clinical trial platform. 26 CRI was launched in 2019 with the goal of developing a trial‐ready ADAD cohort of participants who could be quickly randomized into treatment trials as they become available. Any individual from a family with known mutations interested in participating in a drug trial for AD were eligible to participate in the study. All individuals who participated in CRI were also enrolled in the remote testing. Importantly, participants in this cohort received a comprehensive clinical and cognitive evaluation, including a remote digital cognitive assessment (described below), as well as genetic testing to establish presence versus absence of specific ADAD mutations. As we are interested in quantifying the psychometric properties of our remote assessment in populations suitable for primary or secondary prevention studies, we restrict our analyses to individuals who are cognitively healthy (i.e., no evidence of dementia symptoms based on a standardized clinical interview, described below).

A total of 41 participants were asymptomatic (no evidence of dementia symptoms) and did not possess a causal mutation for ADAD (mutation non‐carriers or NCs) and 82 were asymptomatic and did possess a causal mutation for ADAD (mutation carriers or MCs). Non‐carriers are drawn from the same families as the mutation carriers and hence serve as an ideal healthy control population. Participants were recruited from across the DIAN‐TU, and its associated observational study DIAN‐Observational (DIAN‐Obs), international network. The expected number of years from symptom onset (EYO) was calculated based on the average age at onset of all participants who possess the same genetic mutation, 25 or in the case of NCs, the age at onset from family members with the same genetic mutation. This value is subtracted from the participant's current age to establish how many years remain until (or have elapsed since) symptoms are/were expected to develop. This metric serves as a biomarker‐independent indicator of disease progression. 3 Other available demographic information is summarized in Table 1.

Clinical and cognitive assessment

Presence of dementia symptoms was assessed using the Clinical Dementia Rating® (CDR®). 27 Participants completed a standard paper‐and‐pencil neuropsychological test battery, the Uniform Data Set (UDS) version 2.0, 28 which consists of standard measures of memory, attention, executive function, and language, as determined by a published factor analysis, the details of which are included in the supporting information. 29 The CRI battery also includes the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Task, 30 which is not traditionally included in the UDS. As DIAN‐Obs and DIAN‐TU are international studies, a variety of countries and languages are represented in the cohort. The DIAN studies have a standardized process to ensure the equivalent presentation and quality control of cognitive testing across languages. 31 Importantly, participants were always administered cognitive testing in their native language by a trained team member who is fluent in the same language. Nevertheless, the construct of “language” is difficult to assess in a uniform manner and typical outcomes such as picture naming, do not readily translate across languages. Thus, we limit our focus to the domains of memory, attention, and executive function, which also map onto the domains measured by our remote cognitive assessment.

Remote cognitive assessment

The Ambulatory Research in Cognition (ARC) smartphone application was specifically designed to assess cognition remotely and repeatedly in older adult and AD populations. 14 ARC consists of three cognitive tests: Symbols is a measure of processing speed, Grids measures spatial working memory, and Prices is a measure of associative memory. Tests are administered in a measurement burst design (four times per day for 1 week for a total of 28 possible assessments). We refer to each administration of the three tests as a “session.” The “bursts” of testing were repeated every 3 months, but some participants may have experienced a longer test–retest interval. Given the relatively small sample size and other design considerations (expanded upon in section 4) we do not consider assessment interval (i.e., the time between successive ARC bursts) in our analyses. Of course, this limitation only applies to longitudinal analyses, which constitute a relatively small portion of our analyses.

Details on the ARC tests, including adaptations made for international administration and adherence rates across different countries, are included in the supporting information. The ARC tests and the standard paper‐and‐pencil battery generally were administered within the same month (average difference in days = 20, standard deviation = 55) with the 10th, 30th, 50th, 70th, and 90th percentiles equal to 1.8, 3.1, 3.5, 4, and 56 days, respectively. Seven participants had an interval that exceeded 90 days.

Statistical analysis

Our analyses follow two major avenues. First, we establish the validity of the remote cognitive testing by (1) fitting a previously established confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model on the UDS 2.0 paper‐and‐pencil testing and evaluating whether the ARC tests load onto the same latent constructs and (2) using linear regression models to determine whether the ARC remote tests follow a similar trajectory as the gold standard UDS battery. We refer to these analyses as “Q1” in the results. For the CFA models, we begin by presenting the pairwise correlations among all the cognitive tests to promote transparency and reproducibility and then construct a model in which the ARC tests were forced to load onto their hypothesized underlying constructs (i.e., Symbols = executive function; Grids = attention; Prices = memory), and adequacy of the model was assessed using multiple metrics of global and relative model fit including the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), the comparative fit index (CFI), and the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI). We adopt the following cutoffs to establish acceptable model fit: RMSEA ≤ 0.06; SRMR ≤ 0.08; CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95. 32 For the linear regression models, differences in ARC test performance were analyzed as a function of mutation status with EYO at baseline included as a covariate. EYO was allowed to interact with mutation status to evaluate if group differences change as a function of increasing EYO. As EYO reflects progression toward onset of disease, it is expected that performance will decline as EYO approaches zero, particularly for the MCs. Both linear and quadratic terms for EYO were included to allow for non‐linear change in the outcomes. It is important to note that EYO is a metric of disease progression, yet this was a cross‐sectional analysis. To reduce the overall number of comparisons made in the regression models, we compared cognitive composite scores rather than individual tests. To form the “ARC composite,” available scores for each test were first averaged across the week and then the weekly averages were z scored to the mean and standard deviation of the entire cohort at baseline. The three z scores (Symbols, Prices, and Grids) were then averaged to form the composite. For the paper‐and‐pencil composite, we used the z scored average of Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test free recall, Trail Making Test Part B, Digit Symbol Substitution, and category fluency for Animals, consistent with our prior work 7 and very similar to established global cognitive composites that are in use in clinical trials (e.g., the Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite or PACC 6 ).

Our second avenue (referred to as Q2) of analysis was to jointly establish the feasibility and stability of the remote assessments. For feasibility, we calculate the adherence rates (percent of the 28 possible ARC tests that were completed) and compare them between the AD risk groups (quantified by presence [positive] vs. absence [negative] of a genetic mutation) using linear regression, with the EYO at baseline included as a covariate. The average adherence would present an upper limit on how many remote assessments can reasonably be administered in this design. For stability, we ask the question of how many remote tests are needed to achieve a sufficiently reliable estimate of mean performance. The between‐persons reliability of each ARC test was then calculated using published methodology 14 , 17 for the entire cohort as well as separately for each mutation group using intraclass correlations (ICCs). This ICC separates the amount of variance attributable to different people from the variance observed within individuals across the repeated assessments, 17 using the following formula ICC=VarBPVarBP+VarWPn where Var(BP) represents “between‐person variance” and quantifies the stable differences between individuals, Var(WP) represents the “within‐person variance” and quantifies how much scores vary within the same person over time, and n represents the number of measurements available on each person. This equation quantifies the extent to which the aggregated scores can consistently distinguish participants based on their average scores. Importantly, the within‐person variance can reflect both measurement error and true state‐level fluctuations over time. A high ICC indicates that a greater portion of the variance exists between persons compared to within person and therefore quantifies the reliability of distinguishing persons from one another. It does not, however, imply that within‐person variability is “bad” or “noise”; in fact, there may be meaningful signals in the within‐person variation. However, for our primary purpose of separating individuals into risk groups, we can consider a high ICC to be desirable. For ease of interpretation, we refer to published guidelines on ICCs, with values between 0.75 and 0.9 reflecting “good” reliability and values > 0.90 reflecting “excellent” reliability, 33 while recognizing these thresholds were developed in the context of test reliability and are not directly translatable to our variance partitioning approach. Hence, they serve as heuristics only. We calculate the ICC in five‐session increments; that is, for a single assessment, 5 assessments, 10 assessments, and so on up to the maximum of 25 individual assessments. The number of assessments needed to reach 0.90 reliability is then the number of tests that should be administered in future studies in this population.

The preceding analyses are all cross‐sectional in nature at the baseline burst. We conducted an exploratory third set of analyses (referred to as Q3) on a longitudinal subsample. In these analyses, we included only participants who had completed three or more ARC bursts at the time of data lock. This ensured that any adherence changes observed longitudinally are not due to differential drop‐out. We again calculated and compared the adherence rates at each burst. Finally, the ICC, using a two‐way random effects model with absolute agreement between the average score on each ARC test at baseline and the immediately sequential burst was calculated as a measure that is more similar to classic test–retest reliability.

DISCUSSION

Clinical trials in AD are targeting progressively younger participants in an attempt to prevent pathology as early in the disease course as possible. 35 Because cognitive change in young and cognitively healthy adults is very minimal, extremely large sample sizes are necessary to power successful trials. 9 , 36 It falls to neuropsychologists to define new and more sensitive methods of measuring cognitive change in these populations. Such innovation ranges from defining novel composite scores derived from gold‐standard cognitive test batteries, 6 , 7 , 34 , 37 to developing computerized testing batteries, 5 to exploring video based or remote assessment methods. 10 Each approach comes with its own set of benefits and limitations and may be more or less appropriate for any particular study design. Our goal in this study was to add one more tool to the neuropsychologist's already extensive toolbox, namely, the use of repeated cognitive assessments delivered remotely via smartphone applications. Like the prior innovations, if remote high‐frequency testing is to be used as a valid measurement paradigm in clinical trials, however, its feasibility and validity must be demonstrated in the population of interest. In this study, we examined characteristics of remotely administered high‐frequency cognitive tests in the DIAN‐TU CRI study, which consists of individuals who are relatively young, yet come from families that carry causal genetic mutations for AD.

Our study revealed several important findings which collectively establish basic yet necessary psychometric properties of ARC in this unique cohort. In terms of validity, the ARC measures loaded satisfactorily onto a published factor structure of the paper‐and‐pencil tests, the UDS 2.0, used in CRI. This is critical for demonstrating that the constructs being assessed with ARC are concordant with those measured in the clinic. In other words, Prices does indeed measure “memory” as typically conceived based on a rigorous, in‐person neuropsychological assessment. Additionally, we showed that the ARC cognitive composite was equally sensitive to disease processes as the analogous cognitive composite that is in wide use in the field. 5 , 6 , 7 Indeed, only the ARC composite detected an increase in the group differences with increasing EYO and ARC. This provides initial evidence that a high‐frequency cognitive composite may be useful to track cognitive decline in younger populations.

In terms of feasibility, the adherence to the testing protocol was lower than observed in other studies with older adult participants using an identical assessment paradigm. 12 , 13 , 14 The precise reasons for this reduction in adherence are unknown, but it can be speculated that a larger portion of the sample, relative to older adults, will have children or work full‐time jobs that the remote assessments must be taken around. There may also be differences in smartphone use habits across different cohorts. Despite a lack of data to identify mechanisms of lower adherence, it is equally important to note the increased reliability of these very brief cognitive measures compared to the reliability observed in older adult participants. In many cases, as few as 10 (or even 5) administrations of the ARC tests are sufficient to produce extraordinarily high between‐persons reliability (i.e., > 0.9) of multiple cognitive domains for just ≈ 10 total minutes of testing (compared to ≥ 90 minutes that are required for a typical neuropsychological test battery). Viewed in this light, the average adherence in this study is perfectly sufficient to obtain reliable estimates of the means for at least two of the three tasks and hence may not be need for undue concern. A reduction in total testing time would result in a large reduction in participant burden while simultaneously having cost‐saving implications for observational studies or clinical trials.

A few additional points regarding the remote testing are important to emphasize. First, remote cognitive testing has the potential for reduced participant burden as cognition can be assessed in participants’ homes without need for travel to a testing site. Nevertheless, given our relatively low adherence rates, it is clear that substantial optimization of user experience and engagement is needed prior to full‐scale implementation in clinical trials. Importantly, the version of the ARC application used in the CRI study also represented one of the very first iterations of the application and likely the only one in use in an international study of AD. Given the aim to recruit CRI participants directly into clinical trials, it was essential to have a “locked‐down” version of ARC that did not change to ensure comparability over time. This of course then prevented implementation of many user‐experience improvements that ARC has undergone over the intervening years, which includes participant‐accessible performance metrics (e.g., number of tests completed, number of tests remaining); a detailed rewards page that includes monetary compensation gained thus far in the study; and most importantly, motivating messages sent with each test notification (e.g., “Each test you complete helps us in the fight against Alzheimer disease!”). These improvements have been shown to improve adherence rates in this population. We believe newer versions of the application will greatly improve usability and hence adherence to the testing protocol. Finally, in the current report, we only examined mean performance from the ARC tests to establish validity comparable to commonly used composite measures of cognition like the PACC described in several studies. A critical advantage of using the ARC platform is the ability to not only model the mean performance across sessions, but to examine short‐term trends gleaned from the high‐frequency repeated measurement format including retest (practice) effects, 38 variability at different time scales 12 , 16 (momentary, hourly, daily), and comparison of time‐of‐day effects 18 and testing context effects like location and social surroundings, 12 , 39 all of which may reveal new insights into disease progression in this population that cannot be gleaned from single administrations of standard tests alone. Our research group is actively engaged in exploring these additional aspects of the data.

Given that these analyses are merely a first step in establishing the feasibility, reliability, and validity of remote digital cognitive testing, it is essential to emphasize that we are not suggesting that remote testing can now or ever fully replace traditional, in‐person cognitive testing. Rather, we view this framework as providing key supplementary characterization of cognitive performance (e.g., practice effects, cognitive variability, reliable mean estimates) and may provide cost savings or convenience in certain populations. One must of course balance these advantages against the limitations of remote testing, which include the need for compatible devices and the need to consider technology familiarity, as well as the robust advantages of paper‐and‐pencil testing, which include decades of research across many neurodegenerative disorders as well as the familiarity of these standard batteries to regulatory and funding agencies.

There are many strengths to this study including a very unique sample from the DIAN cohort, which includes a high percentage of Spanish‐speaking participants, comprehensive clinical and cognitive assessments, and a novel high‐frequency remote testing paradigm that was administered in conjunction with a standard in‐clinic neuropsychological battery. Nevertheless, some limitations merit mention. First, our sample size may be considered small for factor analysis techniques with some authors routinely recommending a sample of ≥ 300. 40 Relatedly, more detailed analyses of the impact of education, race, and language of administration on cognition was limited by small sample sizes and missing data. Our longitudinal dataset is limited to individuals who completed three sequential ARC assessments. Participants may have dropped out of CRI for a variety of reasons, including being recruited to participate in an intervention study and returning to the CRI study if that intervention study ended. This was a design feature of the CRI study and hence loss of participants over time does not necessarily indicate “drop‐out” as it would in a typical study. Of course, the longitudinal analyses must be interpreted within the lens of participants that remained in the CRI study. Moreover, we did not model true longitudinal cognitive trajectories in these analyses but rather inferred the trajectories from cross‐sectional data by leveraging EYO. In addition, our participants always completed the paper‐and‐pencil battery prior to engaging with ARC and thus there could be unmeasured order effects subtly affecting the data. Finally, although the links between ADAD and the more common sporadic form of AD have been well established, 24 , 41 it is nevertheless important to emphasize that we have not yet established how well our results will similarly generalize to sporadic AD as there may be critical differences in motivation, disease trajectory, family context, and certainly age of onset. Nevertheless, these analyses represent the first step in this line of research and critically demonstrate the feasibility and validity of ARC in the context of a global clinical trial with relatively young participants.

CONCLUSIONS

Accurately monitoring cognitive performance in the early stages of AD is critical for determining who is eligible for treatment and for tracking improvements over time. Repeated cognitive assessment in the clinic can be burdensome and expensive. Remote cognitive testing has the potential to alleviate this burden. We extend our previous findings by showing that remote cognitive testing is feasible, reliable, and measures similar constructs as a standard in‐clinic battery, in a younger to middle‐aged cohort. Thus, remote testing is a useful supplement to traditional measures with important, and potentially cost‐saving, implications for clinical trials.
