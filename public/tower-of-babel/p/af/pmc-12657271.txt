From Donation to Transplantation
Authors: Tania Kakoudaki, Caroline Patterson, Marius Berman, Paul Lincoln, Sai Bhagra
Published: 2025

We read with interest Venkatraman et al1 describing their successful initiative to improve donor heart utilization. There is a need to make the very best use of every precious deceased organ donor, and their work provides a way forward.

Between April 2021 and March 2024, we consistently recorded the lowest decline rates in the United Kingdom. We declined 60% of heart offers compared to the national average of 77%, and 48% of lung offers compared to 67% nationally, both reaching the lower 99.8% confidence limit.2 Our outcomes remain excellent with 144 heart transplants (2019-2023) achieving 91.6% 1-year survival3 and 126 lung transplants with 81.7% 1-year survival.4 The severe primary graft dysfunction rate post heart transplantation was 18% (2023/24).5

We use detailed dashboards displaying weekly data for all waitlist categories both national and center-specific, total offers, acceptance rates, and week-over-week trends. Similarly to the “weekly transplant waitlist status updates”, we monitor patients on our waitlist, tracking active versus on-hold patients and transplant volumes.

We also have weekly meetings in which we scrutinize every organ utilization decision with our comprehensive multidisciplinary team including transplant pathway nurses, organ retrieval team members, machine perfusion specialists, transplant physicians and surgeons. We review the donor characteristics in detail, discuss rationale for offer declines, and examine donation after brain death and donation after circulatory death offer conversion rates. We also examine logistics that might have affected the donation, retrieval or transplant, and provide feedback to relevant parties. Outcomes for organs declined by our center and used by another are reviewed for learning. We have a policy that no single individual declines an organ; at least 2 clinicians must be involved, with at least 1 consultant.

We believe scrutiny and peer review are valuable educational opportunities that ensure consistency in practice. Our significantly low decline rates suggest we have effectively minimized our own "missed viable opportunities," while maintaining safety and excellent outcomes. We believe these processes will benefit transplant programs more broadly.
