Hepatitis B vaccination status and associated factors among people living with HIV in Gulu, Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maria Nakabuye, Ivan Segawa, Kevin Ouma Ojiambo, George William Ndagga, Ponsiano Ocama, Pauline Byakika-Kibwika
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHepatitis B vaccination is highly recommended among people with HIV (PWH) because they have a poor prognosis when infected with Hepatitis B virus (HBV). Among PWH, HBV infection s are more likely to progress to chronic hepatitis and liver cirrhosis. It is therefore important that PWH are vaccinated against HBV. However, in Uganda, hepatitis B vaccination among PWH is not standard practice indicating that its uptake may be minimal. We aimed to determine the hepatitis B vaccination status among PWH in Gulu Regional Referral Hospital, Uganda and the associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 385 PWH in Gulu Regional Referral Hospital, Uganda. Proportions of PWH that are fully vaccinated against hepatitis B were reported with their 95% confidence intervals (CI). A modified Poisson regression model was used to determine factors associated with hepatitis B vaccination, estimating their Prevalence ratios (PR) and 95% CI.ResultsThe median age of the participants was 37 years, (interquartile range [IQR] 30-45years) and 30.9% (95% CI; 26.3–35.5) were fully vaccinated against HBV. Urban residence (adjusted PR [aPR] = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.17–2.17), family history of Hepatitis B infection (aPR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.06–2.61), and knowledge about HBV (aPR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.06–1.89) were associated with being fully vaccinated against hepatitis B.ConclusionWe found low coverage of hepatitis B vaccination among PWH attending Gulu Regional Referral Hospital. Hepatitis B vaccination programs should target PWH living in rural areas, with a low level of education and with no family history of hepatitis B to educate them about HBV vaccine to increase coverage.

Introduction

The World Health organization (WHO) estimated that globally, 7.4% of the 39.0 million people with HIV (PWH) are co-infected with hepatitis B [1]. Low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs), bear the greatest burden of HBV and HIV infection, accounting for 96% and more than 80% of HBV and HIV infections respectively [2, 3]. In Africa, 6.1% of the adult population is infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and 25.7 million people are living with HIV [4]. Since HIV and HBV are both transmitted through sexual contact, blood transfusion, and perinatal transmission, co-infections are common [5, 6]. This co-infection is especially high in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) where an estimated 2.6 million PWH are infected with HBV [7]. In Uganda, the national prevalence of HIV-HBV coinfection is at 3.9% [8] and 12 out of every 1,000 PWH acquire HBV annually [9].

PWH who are infected with HBV are six times more likely to rapidly progress to chronic infection compared to people without HIV [6, 10–13]. People with HIV-HBV coinfection suffer higher rates of liver-related complications such as liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma compared to those with either infection alone, and they incur a high mortality even when started early on Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) with HBV-active antiviral agents [14]. Also, HIV increases risk of re-activation of previously existing asymptomatic and chronic HBV [15]. Therefore, hepatitis B prevention among PWH remains a priority for WHO to reduce new hepatitis B infections by 90% and deaths by 65% between 2016 and 2030 [16]. Vaccination is the main preventive measure against HBV. Despite this, hepatitis B vaccination of HIV-infected adults in Uganda is currently not a routine practice [9]. Since routine hepatitis B vaccination among newborns was implemented in 2000 in SSA (2002 in Uganda), majority of Africans born before 2000 missed out on free vaccination as infants [17]. Adults in SSA, including Uganda have to seek the vaccine from either public hospitals or private facilities. The uptake of this service in public hospitals is however hindered by the inconsistencies in supply therefore patients have to pay for the vaccine in private facilities [18].

Hepatitis B vaccination among adults in SSA is dependent on a number of factors like cost of vaccine, availability of vaccine at health facilities, level of education, knowledge about hepatitis B virus, socio-economic status and living far (more than 5 km) from a health center [19–21].In addition, there are three interval doses to be obtained at 0, 1 and 6 months to complete vaccination which increases the likelihood of non-completion. Due to these factors, it was hypothesized that the hepatitis B vaccination coverage among PWH attending the ART clinic in Gulu Hospital is low.

There is little information about the current hepatitis B vaccination coverage among PWH in Uganda. This study will provide evidence to inform Ministry of Health (MoH) about the factors associated with the hepatitis B vaccination status among PWH. Knowledge of factors associated with vaccination status may be beneficial in identifying patients at risk for not completing vaccination and informing HBV prevention program in this group. This will help in designing targeted intervention programs for providers and patients. This study aimed to assess the hepatitis B vaccination status and factors associated with hepatitis B vaccination status among PWH attending ART clinic in a regional referral hospital in Northern Uganda.

Methods

Study design and setting

Between 1 st June and 29th July, 2022, we conducted a cross-sectional study at the ART clinic in Gulu Hospital. This is the largest public hospital in Northern Uganda and offers free referral and primary healthcare services including hepatitis B vaccine for Acholi sub-region. A study carried out in 2010 in Gulu district (one of the districts in Northern Uganda) estimated a prevalence of HBV among PWH at 7.9% in a busy hospital clinic [22]. Northern Uganda was the centre of a civil war from 1989 to 2006 [23], forcing inhabitants to seek shelter in camps with often poor living conditions, a situation that enhanced the spread of HBV due to lifestyle practices. Gulu district has a high HIV and HBV prevalence of 7.2% and 4.6% respectively, compared to the national HIV and HBV prevalence of 6.2% and 4.1%, respectively [24]. Hepatitis B vaccine uptake among adults is at 26.7% [25]. By the time the study was conducted, Gulu hospital’s ART clinic had a total of 15,842 PWH under their care. However, the hospital has not offered any routine hepatitis B vaccination services since 2014.

Participants and procedures

Participants were eligible for the study if they were 18 years and older, living with HIV, attended the ART clinic for routine care during the study period, and consented to participate in the study. However, they were excluded if they were too ill to participate. Approximately 320 patients visited the ART clinic in Gulu Hospital every week between March and April 2022. We determined a sample size of 385 study participants using Kish Leslie’s formula and assuming that 50% of PWH were fully vaccinated (to yield a maximum sample size), a sampling error of 5% and 95% CI. On each ART clinic day, we used systematic random sampling to select participants. The 1 st participant was selected using simple random sampling using a list of the first seven patients leaving the physician’s room as the sampling frame. Numbers one to seven were written on pieces of paper and the paper with the number seven was randomly selected. We selected every other seventh patient after that; if the seventh patient was ineligible or not interested in participating in the study, we selected the patient after them in the register. Selected participants were then individually approached in the triage centre to elaborately explain to them about the study and ask for their interest in participating in the study. Sampling method was adopted from Basics and clinical biostatistics textbook [26].

We interviewed patients who were interested using a questionnaire adopted from similar studies [27–30] and pretested among ten participants who were not included in the study population. We interviewed participants in English or the local language (Acholi), according to their preference, about their hepatitis B vaccination status, marital status, age, residence, family history of hepatitis B and knowledge of HBV. Hepatitis B vaccination status was self-reported. Residence was categorized as rural or urban (gazetted as urban by Ministry of Local Government and any trading center with more than 1,000 people) [31]. Knowledge about HBV was assessed by 16 questions including; routes of transmission, prevention and HBV vaccine. Participants were required to answer with ‘true’, ‘false’ or ‘I don’t know’, each correct answer was given a score of ‘1’ while an incorrect answer and questions answered as ‘I don’t know’ were given a score of ‘0’. Respondents who were able to answer 70% or more of the questions correctly were categorized as having “good knowledge”, while those with less were considered to have “poor/no knowledge.” Participants’ most current HIV viral load results were also abstracted from their facility records and categorized as suppressed (< 1,000 copies/ml) or unsuppressed (≥ 1000 copies/ml). Data were entered in Epidata v4.6 (The Epidata Association, Odense, Denmark). Unvaccinated participants were advised to seek full vaccination from private health facilities when vaccine is not available in government facilities.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was hepatitis B vaccination status (fully or not fully vaccinated); participants were considered fully vaccinated if they completed all three doses of the hepatitis B vaccine in the predefined MoH schedule of 0,1, and 6 months for adults [10] and not fully vaccinated if they did not complete all three doses. Hepatitis B vaccination status was reported as a proportion with 95% confidence interval (CI). Participants’ HIV viral load suppression status, family history of hepatitis B and other categorical characteristics were summarized as proportions. Age was summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). To determine the factors independently associated with hepatitis B vaccination status, we used modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors to estimate prevalence ratios (PR) with their 95% CI. Factors with p < 0.20 at bivariate were included in multivariable models. Interaction among variables was assessed using a likelihood ratio test. Variables which changed the PR by ≥ 10% were considered potential confounders and included in the final model. Using stepwise estimation, we dropped variables with p-values > 0.05. Variables with a p ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant and included in the final model. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from Makerere University School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (Mak-SOMREC-2021-218). Administrative clearance to conduct the study was obtained from Gulu Hospital (ADM/2017–18001).

Results

Participant characteristics

From the 2400 patients received at the ART clinic in Gulu Hospital during the study period, 389 patients were approached to participate in the study, out of these, 385 accepted and were considered in the analysis. We excluded 4 patients who were too ill to participate. Out of the 385 participants screened for hepatitis B, 13 self-reported having had a hepatitis B infection. The median age for the participants was 37 years (IQR 30–45). 96% (370) were Christian, 67% (257) were females, 62% (238) resided in rural areas, 60% (232) were self-employed, 53% (203) were married and 51% (196) had primary level education. 98% (364) of the participants had a suppressed HIV viral load (< 1,000 copies/ml), 97.4% (375) did not report a previous hepatitis B infection and 93.0% (357) did not report a family history of hepatitis B infection. About a third of the participants (136) had good knowledge about HBV (Table 1).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of nearly 400 PWH attending the ART clinic at Gulu Hospital, about a third (31%) were fully vaccinated against hepatitis B. Full hepatitis B vaccination status was associated with higher level education attainment, urban residence, good knowledge of HBV and family history of hepatitis B infection.

Despite the Uganda MoH recommendation of HBV vaccination for all PWH [32], only 30.9% were fully vaccinated at Gulu Hospital. Similar findings were reported among adults of Gulu city, 26.7% [33]. Hepatitis B vaccine uptake of 44.3% and 57.8% were reported among undergraduate students of Makerere University College of Health Sciences [27] and healthcare providers in Wakiso district, Uganda [34] respectively. This finding is slightly higher than ours, presumably due to healthcare providers’ perception of a high risk of occupational exposure to HBV and hence seeking hepatitis B vaccination. Findings in the Ugandan population are lower than that reported among PWH in Northern Greece (73.6%) [28] South Korea (75%) [35] and German (75.3%) [36]. Possible explanation for the difference in vaccination is, a low-income country like Uganda might struggle to maintain a consistent supply of vaccines due to financial constraints in purchase, transportation and administration of vaccines leading to a lower vaccine coverage. This lack of consistent vaccine supply might force patients to seek the vaccine from private facilities, which further limits access due to the financial burden on patients. Interestingly, cost of vaccine was the least reported reason for not getting vaccinated (1%). This could imply that the patients receiving services at the ART clinic may rely on public healthcare facilities where the vaccine is offered free of charge when available. However, the inconsistency in the supply of vaccines in public hospitals might leave patients unaware of the need to pay for it in private facilities, thus minimizing the focus on cost as a barrier. Additionally, the participants might prioritize other barriers such as the unavailability of vaccines at health centers especially if they were expecting the service to be freely provided as part of public health care. A study conducted in Uganda among university students who might not expect free vaccination services reported high cost of the vaccine as the main reason (63%) for incomplete vaccination [27]. Another possible explanation for the variation in vaccination rates is how differently vaccination status was determined. The study conducted in South Korea [35] used both vaccination records and anti-HBs tests whereas that in Greece [28] used vaccination cards, which both are more objective than recall of vaccination history. The low hepatitis B vaccine uptake highlighted by our study implies PWH are vulnerable to a potentially fatal liver disease. HBV testing among PWH should be prioritized at ART clinics and ensure all are tested freely and routinely. Hepatitis B vaccination among PWH should be offered free of charge at ART clinics among patients that are hepatitis B negative.

Poor vaccination rates for HBV infection could also be partly explained by inadequate HBV infection-related awareness and education among PWH. Participants who had adequate knowledge about HBV were more likely to have been vaccinated than those with no or little knowledge and this is supported by findings from similar studies [37–39]. Additionally, low education level was associated with non-completion to hepatitis B vaccine, which is consistent with Tsachouridou findings [28]. A possible explanation for the association between higher education attainment and hepatitis B vaccination status could be education’s effect on enabling participants acquire health knowledge to visit and use health resources reproductively [19]. More educated participants might be more likely to seek out reliable sources of information about vaccines, understand the science on vaccination safety and effectiveness and make informed decisions based on that information. Health professionals should have educational programs creating awareness about hepatitis B among PWH and sensitize patients in care about the importance of getting fully vaccinated for hepatitis B to enhance uptake of the vaccine.

Participants who reported a family history of hepatitis B were twice more likely to have been fully vaccinated than those who did not have a family history. This finding is similar to that reported among residents of Beijing, China [29]. These patients could have perceived themselves to be at risk or witnessed the prognosis of the disease and therefore believed the consequences of contracting the disease are severe and were more inclined to get vaccinated.

Study participants residing in urban areas were almost twice as likely to have received full dose vaccination than those who lived in rural areas. This could be attributed to HBV vaccine being inaccessible in rural areas due to limited cold storage facilities and resources which hinders distribution and administration of vaccines in remote areas. In addition, rural residents need to move long distances to access the vaccine in urban areas. Third most common reported reason for partial/incomplete vaccination was that the vaccine was inaccessible which supports our findings of rural residence being a predisposing factor for incomplete hepatitis B vaccination status. MoH needs to extend free hepatitis B vaccination services to PWH residing in rural areas where access to services is lower.

However, our study has some limitations. First, family history of hepatitis B infection and vaccination status were assessed based on self-report and prone to recall bias. Because many people do not test for hepatitis B, some participants may not have reported an accurate family history. Some participants may have failed to recall their vaccination history or some may have given false information on the doses they received. This might have led to over or under estimation of vaccination status. Second, there might have been selection bias since the sample was picked from a hospital setting indicating that the study population might not reflect the true population of PWH, results may not be generalized to PWH attending other ART clinics outside Acholi sub region. Third, we didn’t consider the time period in which routine hepatitis B vaccination services were offered in Gulu Hospital and when they were not. The potential impact of this discontinuation on vaccination coverage rates among PWH was not analyzed. Fourth, CD4 + count results were missing from patient files and this variable was not assessed for in this study. HIV Viral load was the only indicator for HIV management that was assessed for. Other potential confounders like patient’s income and physician’s awareness and vaccine recommendation were not studied. True prevalence ratios might be lower than depicted due to the effect of some uncontrolled confounders.

The study had some strengths. First the study site is located in a high HBV and HIV burden region, with a great need for intervention. Second, the study provides an evidence gap on the hepatitis B vaccination status of PWH, a group of people who are at higher risk of contracting HBV and fatal liver disease compared to the general population.

Conclusions

We found low coverage of hepatitis B vaccination among PWH attending Gulu Regional Referral hospital. Hepatitis B vaccination programs should target PWH residing in rural areas, with a low level of education and no family history of hepatitis B to educate them about HBV vaccine in order to increase coverage.
