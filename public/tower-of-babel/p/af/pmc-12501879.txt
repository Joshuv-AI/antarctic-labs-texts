Outcomes in Patients With Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Related Myopathy and Prolonged Follow-Up
Authors: Yahel Segal, Georgios Mangioris, Michel Toledano, Lisa Kottschade, Eoin P. Flanagan, Andrew McKeon, Ronen Stoff, Elie Naddaf, Sean J. Pittock, Divyanshu Dubey, Anastasia Zekeridou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesMyopathy is one of the most common neurologic immune-related adverse events (irAEs) reported after treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) cancer immunotherapies. Current knowledge on disease course relates to short-term outcomes, and long-term outcome data are lacking. The aim of this study was to evaluate the long-term outcomes of patients with ICI-related myopathy.MethodsWe reviewed Mayo Clinic patients diagnosed with ICI-related myopathy between 2013 and 2024 with at least 6 months of follow-up; we compared them with patients who died because of myopathy within 2 months from disease onset.ResultsTwenty-three patients were identified; the median follow-up duration was 13 months (range 6–76). The median age at presentation was 73 years (range 34–87), and 57% were male. The most common presentations were ocular involvement (78%, including oculomotor and ptosis), followed by proximal limb (74%), axial (61%), and bulbar (48%) weakness. Myocarditis was present in 35%. At disease nadir, 43% had an irAE grade >2. All patients were treated with corticosteroids; 61% received additional immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatment in the acute setting. The median treatment duration was 5 months (range 1–17). At the last follow-up, 87% of patients had a favorable outcome (irAE grade ≤2), yet residual symptoms/signs were common (48%). Gradual improvement was observed in most patients, even after immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatment was discontinued. The only patient with a truly refractory course had biopsy findings of nemaline rods. Compared with patients with long-term follow-up, patients who died within 2 months (N = 9) were more commonly men (57% vs 100%, p = 0.03) and more likely to have bulbar or axial involvement (p = 0.01 and p = 0.04, respectively).DiscussionApproximately half of the patients with ICI-related myopathy and long-term follow-up had some residual symptoms/signs, despite good functional outcomes. Symptoms continue to improve over 12 months regardless of immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatment duration. Male sex and axial and bulbar symptoms/signs were all associated with a terminal disease course. A prolonged refractory disease course might suggest an atypical pathology.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized cancer treatment, yet they are associated with significant immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Neurologic irAEs are reported in 1%–5% of patients, with myopathy being the most common.1 While ICI-related myopathy has high mortality rates, patients who survive seem to have a favorable myopathy outcome.1

Current treatment guidelines suggest discontinuation of ICI treatment, administration of high-dose corticosteroids with a gradual taper over 4–6 weeks, and consideration of additional immunosuppression/immunomodulation, depending on treatment response.2,3 However, despite recent data pointing to a chronic disease course in nearly 30% of patients,4 there are no consensus treatment guidelines for the long-term management of these patients and practices range from a short corticosteroid taper to prolonged maintenance immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatments.

We reviewed the medical charts of all patients with ICI-related myopathy and available long-term follow-up data of ≥6 months treated at our institution, with the goal of characterizing their long-term outcomes and informing long-term treatment choices.

Methods

All patients with a diagnosis of ICI-related myopathy treated at Mayo Clinic between 2013 and 2024 were screened (Figure 1). Patients with at least 6 months of follow-up (“long-term cohort”) were included. Six of these patients have been previously reported,5 yet extended follow-up is provided herein. As a comparison group, we included patients who died in hospital within 2 months of their ICI-related myopathy onset, with a cause of death related to their myopathy (“acute cohort”). Myopathy was defined as clinical presentation compatible with an ICI-related myopathy and supported by EMG findings, except for a single case of isolated ocular involvement where expert opinion was accepted for diagnosis. Cases were classified as definite, probable, or possible ICI-related myopathy according to the consensus definitions for neurologic irAEs of ICI.6 Myocarditis was determined according to the International Cardio-Oncology Society criteria.7 Patients were excluded if they had evidence of neuromuscular junction (NMJ) disorder, defined by >10% decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation or a definite clinical response to pyridostigmine. The irAE was graded based on the consensus definitions for neurologic irAEs of ICI.6 Poor outcome was defined as an irAE grade >2 at the last follow-up. Residual symptoms and signs were considered based on patients' reports and neurologic evaluation: Patients reporting residual symptoms were excluded if the last follow-up encounter lacked a detailed neurologic examination (n = 4); in patients with no residual symptoms reported, follow-up documentation by the treating oncologist was also accepted (Figure 1).

Comparisons between groups and associations between clinical and laboratory characteristics were evaluated using the Fisher exact (categorical variables) and Wilcoxon rank-sum (continuous variables) test; p values <0.05 were considered significant. The statistical software R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB 20–002897); patients gave consent for their records to be used for research.

Data Availability

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available on reasonable request from a qualified investigator.

Patients' median age was 73 years (range 34–87); 57% (13/23) were male. The median follow-up duration was 13 months (range 6–76). Most patients had ocular involvement (oculomotor abnormalities or ptosis, 18/23, 78%), followed by proximal limb (17/23, 74%), axial (14/23, 61%), and bulbar (11/23, 48%) weakness (Figure 2A). Approximately one-third of the patients had concomitant myocarditis (8/23, 35%). Twenty-six percent of patients had an isolated presentation of either ocular or proximal limb weakness while the rest had multifocal symptoms, most commonly a combination of ocular, axial, bulbar, and proximal limb weakness (generalized), encountered in 30% of patients (Figure 2B). When investigating clinical correlations, only axial weakness and myocarditis were associated with an older age at presentation among this cohort (p = 0.03 and p = 0.02, respectively). No additional associations were detected between clinical or demographic characteristics and myopathy phenotype.

All patients underwent EMG, and all but 1 had evidence of myopathic motor unit potentials; fibrillation potentials were noted in 78% (18/23). Three patients had a muscle biopsy performed at myopathy diagnosis. Of these, 2 had evidence of necrotic muscle fibers supporting a diagnosis of ICI-related myopathy while the third was not reviewed at our institution and details are not available.

Muscle acetylcholine receptor antibodies (AChR-Abs) were detected in 2 patients (2/22, 9%); both did not have clinical or electrophysiologic evidence of NMJ dysfunction. All patients with available data had elevated troponin T, and 78% (18/23) had creatine kinase (CK) elevations. Troponin I, more specific to the heart, was elevated in 57% (4/7 patients tested). Alanine transaminase (ALT) and/or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were elevated in 95% of patients (19/20); 52% of patients had elevations >2.5X upper normal limit; of those, 64% (7/11) had findings attributed to concomitant ICI-related hepatitis.

At nadir, 43% of patients (10/23) had an irAE grade >2 (median 2, range 2–4, Figure 3A). Table 2 provides patients' treatments and outcomes. All patients discontinued ICIs and were treated with corticosteroids at diagnosis. In addition, 61% (14/23) received further immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatment in the acute setting (within 3 months of onset) that included IV immunoglobulins (IVIgs), plasma exchange, and/or abatacept. Forty-three percent of patients (10/23) received additional maintenance treatment (>3 months from onset) beyond corticosteroid tapering, commonly with IVIg or rituximab (Table 2). The median duration of immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatment (a daily dose of ≥10-mg prednisone and/or any other immunosuppressive/immunomodulating agents) was 5 months (range 1–17, 4 patients were receiving ongoing maintenance at the time of data cut-off).

Residual Symptoms/Signs Were Reported in Nearly Half of the Patients at Last Follow-Up

At last follow-up, 87% of patients had a favorable outcome (irAE grade <3) and the median irAE grade was 0 (range 0–3). All patients with a maximal irAE grade of 2 at nadir showed clinical improvement at the last follow-up (4/13 continued to have an irAE grade of 2). However, 48% of patients (11/23) reported some residual symptoms/signs, most commonly ocular (22%, 5/23), followed by limb weakness or axial weakness (17%, 4/23 each) and bulbar weakness (9%, 2/23). The evolution over time is shown in Figure 3. In patients with at least 12 months of follow-up (N = 16), the rate of residual symptoms/signs was 38% and decreased further to 25% when examining patients with a longer follow-up duration (N = 12).

Symptoms Continued to Improve Over 12 Months of Follow-Up Regardless of Treatment Duration

Eight patients were treated with a short course of immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatment (≤3 months) while 15 were treated with a longer treatment course. Rates of residual symptoms/signs at 6, 9, and 12 months were comparable between the 2 groups (eTable 1). Patients who were treated for less than 3 months continued to improve after the 3-month cutoff even in the absence of treatment (Figure 3B, eTable 1).

As a comparator cohort, we reviewed data from patients who died in hospital within 2 months of their ICI-related myopathy onset (“acute cohort,” N = 9, eTable 2). Key comparisons between the 2 cohorts are provided in eTable 3. Compared with the long-term cohort, the acute cohort patients were slightly older (median age 75 vs 73 years, p = 0.04) and included a higher proportion of men (100% vs 57%, p = 0.03). Bulbar symptoms/signs and axial weakness were more common among the acute cohort (p = 0.01 and 0.04, respectively). AChR-Abs were more common in the acute cohort (p = 0.04).

Long-Term Patients With Poor Outcomes at Last Follow-Up

Only 3 patients were noted to have an irAE grade >2 at the last follow-up. Two of these patients showed clear and substantial improvement in their myopathic symptoms on treatment with high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory therapy. While both had an irAE grade of 3 at the final follow-up, this was contributed by minimal residual myopathic symptoms/signs combined with significant deterioration due to cancer progression.

Detailed Description of the Treatment-Refractory Patient

The third patient presented with an unusually refractory course: This is a 74-year-old woman treated with anti-PD1 therapy for melanoma. After her second cycle, she developed pruritus that responded well to low-dose oral prednisone, and ICI therapy was continued. Shortly after her seventh treatment cycle, pruritus recurred and she also developed elevated AST and ALT, necessitating oral prednisone treatment followed by taper and ICI discontinuation. She also developed ocular, axial, limb, and bulbar weakness including notable stridor while talking and breathing (this was approximately 155 days from her initial ICI dose). In addition, she developed severe stomatitis, requiring admission for parenteral feeding. She was diagnosed with ICI-related pemphigus vulgaris and treated with high-dose IV methylprednisolone and a course of IVIg, followed by prednisone taper and weekly IVIg. Peak CK was mildly elevated during initial presentation; there was no clinical or echocardiographic evidence of cardiac involvement. Due to insufficient treatment response (dermatologic symptoms improved yet neuromuscular symptoms remained largely unchanged), rituximab was added, approximately 7 months into her presentation, with no benefit. She was evaluated at Mayo Clinic 9 months into her disease course. Otolaryngology evaluation showed bilateral complete vocal cord immobility. Needle EMG performed 9 months from symptom onset showed myopathic units with occasional fibrillation potentials in proximal upper limb and axial muscles. At 15 months with weekly IVIg 0.5 gr/kg and prednisone 20 mg/daily, the patient had partial improvement in her limb weakness and stridor but persistent dropped head. In addition, symptoms were further aggravated on attempt to taper down daily prednisone dose below 20 mg and IVIg below 0.5gr/kg weekly. Due to her remarkably refractory disease course and unusual presentation with prominent stridor, MRI and muscle biopsy were pursued approximately 16 months into her disease course. Her biopsy was obtained from the left deltoid muscle that presented minor weakness. MRI revealed mild bilateral shoulder muscle atrophy; muscle biopsy showed occasional nonatrophic fibers harboring nemaline rods (Figure 4). There was mild upregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class 1 and no necrotic fibers or inflammatory infiltrates. The patient has no family history of myopathy, and her myopathic symptoms started after ICI exposure.

Discussion

In this cohort, we focused on long-term outcomes (≥6 months) of patients with ICI-related myopathy and found a generally favorable outcome despite high rates of residual symptoms/signs, regardless of prolonged immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory therapy in most of our patients. The clinical presentations of our patients are in line with previous reports emphasizing ocular involvement and proximal limb weakness as the most common features, with high rates of concomitant axial and bulbar weakness, and 30%–40% incidence of coexisting myocarditis.8-11 The combination of myopathy and myocarditis seems to be associated with an older age at presentation, as was recently reported.12 Nearly a fifth of all patients with myopathy in our study (6/31, 19%) had AChR-Abs, despite no clinical evidence of NMJ dysfunction. This is in accordance with previous reports11,13 and aligns with 2 recent studies, one suggesting that ICI-related AChR-Abs are associated with ICI-related myopathy rather than ICI-related myasthenia gravis (MG)11 and the other demonstrating that AChR-Abs from two-thirds of patients with a potential diagnosis of ICI-related MG showed no evidence of pathogenic activity in vitro, in contrast to antibodies from patients with idiopathic MG. Taken together, these findings suggest that AChR-Abs detected in the context of ICI use may lack classical effector functions and merely represent an epiphenomenon of a more complex immunologic process. In our study, although the numbers are small, AChR-Abs were more common in the acute cohort, suggesting that these may be related to a fulminant disease course, possibly due to tissue destruction resulting in increased epitope exposure, as was described with NMDA receptor antibodies among patients with herpes simplex virus encephalitis.14

Two-thirds of patients (70%, 16/23) had residual symptoms/signs at 6 months, and this number gradually declined to 40% (6/15) at 12 months (Figure 3B). Dissecting whether these symptoms/signs represent ongoing inflammation or simply residual damage to muscle tissue is challenging, but patients who stopped immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatments at 3 months continued to improve over time from a neurologic perspective. This suggests that the gradual improvement noted over the span of 12 months may represent slow muscle recuperation and compensatory mechanisms rather than active persistent inflammation. Nevertheless, reports of patients with long-term corticosteroid dependency, including a patient in our cohort who experienced an exacerbation on corticosteroid taper 4 months from symptom onset, suggest that some patients may experience chronic inflammation.4 Future studies prospectively following patients treated with a prolonged vs short treatment course would be necessary to identify optimal treatment duration but may be limited by the relative rarity of this disease and the multiple concomitant comorbidities that these patients have, which may affect both morbidity and mortality.

Most of our patients who survived the acute disease phase had generally favorable outcomes, similarly to other cohorts.1 In our cohort, the only patient with long-term follow-up who was truly refractory with limited treatment response had an atypical presentation of prominent stridor, dropped head, and, to a lesser extent, ocular and proximal limb weakness. Stridor has been rarely described as a presentation of myopathy, and in this patient, it was related to vocal cord immobility and was dependent on steroids and IVIg (would recur every time we attempted to decrease prednisone below 20 mg/d and IVIg below 0.5 gr/kg/wk). Due to her atypical response, a biopsy was performed late in her disease course (∼16 months from symptom onset) that revealed a few atrophic muscle fibers and occasional nemaline rods in nonatrophic fibers. These findings raised the possibility of sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy (SLONM). SLONM is an acquired adult-onset myopathy typically involving proximal and axial muscles, commonly associated with monoclonal gammopathy or HIV infection, with additional sporadic associations reported with autoimmune diseases.15 Histopathologic findings of inflammatory changes including sarcolemmal MHC class 1 upregulated expression and limited inflammatory infiltrates and treatment response to immune-therapies in some patients allude to inflammatory mechanisms, yet disease pathogenesis remains largely unknown.16,17 Our patient's biopsy findings are not typical of SLONM because nemaline rods in SLONM are found more abundantly and in atrophic fibers.15 In some patients with SLONM, nemaline rods may appear only occasionally16,18 and repeated biopsies may be required for diagnosis.19 In our patient, biopsy was obtained from a muscle with mild rather than maximal weakness. Therefore, it remains unclear whether this case represents SLONM or SLONM-like pathology activated by ICI, given that the presence of infrequent rods may be associated with various muscle disorders. An additional patient with a clinical presentation of ICI-related myopathy and evidence of sparse nemaline rods on muscle biopsy was also previously reported.20

In addition to cohort size, our study is limited by its retrospective nature. Furthermore, because we included only patients with available long-term follow-up, patients with extremely mild disease were likely discharged from follow-up early and, therefore, are underrepresented (Figure 1 shows the inclusion flowchart). However, our objective was to examine patients requiring long-term neurologic follow-up and provide insights into the characteristics and clinical outcomes of these patients. Our data support previous reports of a generally favorable outcome yet reveal the prolonged course of recovery and the extent of residual symptoms these patients experience.

These findings provide important tools for clinicians when discussing with patients their expected recovery course, suggesting that a short course of treatment may be sufficient for some of these patients, although further studies are required to determine optimal treatment durations and define biomarkers to guide such decisions. The gradual improvement in symptoms encountered across all but 1 patient in our long-term cohort highlights the importance of considering alternative pathologies in patients with an atypical clinical presentation or disease course.
