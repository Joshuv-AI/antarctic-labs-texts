Background

In the 1990s, the prognosis for acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) was very poor [1]. AMI is an uncommon but highly fatal clinical entity characterized by obstruction of blood flow to the intestines [2]. It typically presents with a group of non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain that is disproportionate to the degree of abdominal tenderness, nausea, vomiting, and anorexia [3]. The mortality rates persistently exceed 50%, despite advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [4]. Nevertheless, the small intestine exhibits a remarkable resilience, capable of withstanding a 75% reduction in mesenteric blood flow for up to 12 h [5]. The causes of AMI include embolic (which are the most common), thrombotic, and non-obstructive causes. In rare cases, infective endocarditis can also cause mesenteric embolism and acute mesenteric ischemia [6]. In cases of embolic mesenteric ischemia, the emboli most often originate from the heart and commonly occur in patients with atrial fibrillation or after a myocardial infarction [7]. Pneumatosis intestinalis (PI) and hepatic portal venous gas (HPVG) are radiographic indicators in 70% of patients with mesenteric ischemia [8]. In individuals with AMI, treatment options are often determined by physical and imaging findings. Open surgery is required if computed tomography shows evidence of intestinal infarction, necrosis, or overt peritonitis [9].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of extensive acute mesenteric ischemia complicated by extreme short bowel syndrome reported from Syria, a low‑resource setting. The combination of advanced age, multiple cardiac comorbidities, and the extent of bowel necrosis makes this presentation particularly uncommon. Reporting such cases from resource‑limited environments is essential to highlight diagnostic and therapeutic challenges and to contribute to global clinical understanding.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old female patient presented to the emergency department with an acute onset of generalized abdominal pain that progressively worsened in intensity, reaching a severity of 10/10 at the time of presentation. The pain was associated with poor oral intake, with the last reported meal approximately two days prior to admission. She also complained of nausea and vomiting. The patient had been symptomatic for approximately four days prior to presentation and had been transferred between several healthcare facilities, where she was initially evaluated and managed conservatively without a definitive diagnosis. She was a chronic smoker and not an alcohol consumer. Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, valvular heart disease, hypothyroidism on replacement therapy, recently diagnosed atrial fibrillation (AF), and a recent chest infection associated with pulmonary infiltrates. Her surgical history included coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery, repair of a femoral neck fracture, and prior ophthalmologic surgery. Her regular medications included levothyroxine, aspirin, metformin, rosuvastatin, ursodeoxycholic acid, and diuretics. When measuring the vital signs, we recorded the following, HR 83 beats/min, BP 110/60 mmHg, and SpO2 97%. During the clinical examination, the abdomen was soft but diffusely tender, with pain increasing gradually on palpation. The pain was disproportionate to the exam (soft abdomen with guarding, no abdomen distension or peritonitis). Laboratory tests revealed an elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) level of 85 mg/dL, a creatinine level of 1.42 mg/dL. Elevated glucose was also noted. LDH levels were markedly elevated (approximately 1050), along with elevated CK and ESR values (Table 1). A contrast-enhanced CT-scan was performed within two hours of the patient’s arrival to our hospital and was suggestive of extensive acute mesenteric ischemia (Fig. 1). Based on the overall clinical picture and imaging findings, the decision was made to proceed with urgent surgery. This was done after discussing the case with cardiology and obtaining informed consent. Under general anesthesia, the abdomen was opened by a midline laparotomy above and below the umbilicus. After exploration, the small intestine was found to be necrotic beginning approximately 40–50 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, while the other organs were normal. Free fluid was present. All necrotic bowel from this point to the ileocecal junction was resected. Due to her condition, a damage-control laparotomy approach was adopted, given the patient’s advanced age, significant cardiac comorbidities, recent pulmonary infection, lack of preoperative bowel preparation, and the substantial risk associated with prolonged anesthesia. A double-barrel jejunoileostomy was created, with approximately 40 cm of well-perfused proximal jejunum preserved distal to the ligament of Treitz as the proximal limb, while the remaining distal small bowel (ileum), which was severely ischemic, was exteriorized as the distal limb (Fig. 2) (Supplementary Video 1). After irrigation of the abdominal cavity with warm normal saline, good haemostasis and adequate suctioning were ensured. A tubular drain was placed in the pelvis, and the abdomen was closed and a sterile dressing poultice with colostomy bag was done. Postoperatively, the patient was conscious, responsive, and sensitive to time and place. The extensive bowel resection resulted in approximately 60–70 cm of remaining small intestine in total, leading to short bowel syndrome (SBS). Oral feeding was reintroduced 24 h after surgery in liquid form. Postoperative hydration consisted of 4 L per day (2 L dextrose-containing fluids and 2 L normal saline) during the first 24 h. With initiation of oral intake, intravenous hydration was continued at 2 L of normal saline daily for an additional 48 h. Serum electrolytes were monitored on a daily basis, and no clinically significant electrolyte disturbances were observed throughout hospitalization. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was initiated four days postoperatively due to limited regional availability, although earlier initiation was initially planned. During the early postoperative course, the patient developed bilateral pitting edema of the lower limbs, which was managed with intravenous albumin supplementation. The patient received omeprazole, enoxaparin sodium, prophylactic antibiotics (vancomycin and metronidazole), and regular blood glucose monitoring. Antidiarrheal medications were administered as part of SBS management. GLP-2 analogues were not administered due to unavailability and the patient’s limited socioeconomic status. She was transferred out of the intensive care unit (ICU) on postoperative day one, with progressive clinical improvement and normalization of laboratory parameters. She was discharged home with the following recommendations: a high-protein, high-fiber diet, daily TPN administration, assisted ambulation, and lower limb elevation at 15 degrees during sleep. During two weeks of follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, did not experience weight loss, and showed no significant changes in electrolyte levels, serum protein, or albumin values. She did not develop TPN-related complications during this period.

Discussion

AMI accounts for approximately 1% of hospital admissions for acute abdominal pain and the overall mortality rate can reach 50% to 90% if early treatment is not received [10]. Atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, valvular heart disease, and recent myocardial infarction serve as significant risk factors, as they heighten the probability of embolic events or thrombotic occlusion [11]. Furthermore, the administration of high-dose vasopressors can compromise mesenteric perfusion and subsequently trigger non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI) [12]. AMI remains a significant challenge with serious surgical consequences, especially in men over 60 years of age (3:1) [7]. In addition, it was found that 80-year-old patients have approximately tenfold higher risk of developing AMI compared to 60-year-old patients [4]. Ochi et al. found that the patient had infective endocarditis (IE), and even after ensuring the absence of risk factors such as coronary heart disease, valvular diseases, and arrhythmias, they found that candida endocarditis caused AMI [6]. Higashijima et al. reported a case of a 65-year-old woman who was diagnosed with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and hospitalized for chemotherapy, and AMI was suspected based on the aquarium sign [12]. Although the aquarium sign is a rare finding, its occurrence due to the flow of hepatic portal venous gas (HPVG) into the right atrium and ventricle is an important diagnostic sign of AMI, which is demonstrated using echocardiography [13]. According to Chandrakar et al., patients with COVID-19 related AMI symptoms may have shortness of breath, fever, abdominal discomfort, distention, constipation, loose stools, nausea, and vomiting [14]. The first line and most important imaging modality is computed tomography angiography (CTA) or contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis. These valuable methods are extremely helpful in determining the etiology behind AMI [15]. The mortality rate doubles rapidly with every 6 h of delay in diagnosis. The CTA’s significance lies in its ability to differentiate between vascular patterns of AMI, such as thrombotic, embolic, and occlusive, as well as to differentiate them from non-occlusive AMI [4] (Table 2). Unfortunately, angiography takes time, and in this case the patient should get the treatment as fast as possible and prolonged time treatment was not a choice. Another method that helps diagnose AMI is indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging, which is used to assess blood flow by monitoring the fluorescence emitted by the ICG dye after it is injected into the body. Reduced or absent fluorescence emission indicates that tissues or organs are suffering from reduced blood flow, suggesting ischemia [16]. In the case reported by Ritenour et al., the authors used a telescoping technique through the brachial artery, taking advantage of angiographic guidance [17]. Recent studies suggest that treating AMI with endovascular methods may lead to better outcomes than traditional open surgery. Patients who received endovascular treatment often needed fewer bowel resections and had lower death rates. However, these results might be influenced by the fact that healthier patients were more likely to be chosen for endovascular procedures, while those with more severe conditions were treated with open surgery [9]. Arthurs et al., compared the outcomes of endovascular therapies with conventional operational therapy and found that the length of resected bowel segments was shorter (52 cm vs. 160 cm) and the laparotomy rate was lower (69% vs. 100%) [18]. The main goal of surgical treatment of AMI is summarized in 3 axes: firstly, the removal of all necrotic parts of the small intestine; secondly, preserving the healthy parts of it; and finally, restoring blood supply to the ischemic intestine through various techniques, including the bypass technique in cases of superior mesenteric artery thrombosis at the origin of the aorta [4]. Overall, according to recent reports, European and international societies recommend that surgeons and emergency physicians include in their management both surgical techniques and endovascular recanalization [19]. SBS is a rare syndrome that occurs in approximately 15% of adults following bowel resection. Besides mesenteric ischemia, the other major risk factors for this syndrome are Crohn’s disease and malignant cancers, ultimately leading to reduced intestinal absorptive capacity and chronic intestinal failure [20]. In our case, the patient underwent a damage‑control laparotomy during which all non‑viable segments of the small intestine were resected based on intraoperative assessment of bowel viability. As a consequence of this extensive resection, she developed SBS in the postoperative period. This outcome was also reported by Islam et al. in a similar case of a 68-year-old woman diagnosed with ischemic necrosis of almost the entire small intestine. She underwent the same laparotomy surgery as our case and was also put on TPN for 32 days [21]. Drug therapy also plays a vital role in managing SBS, such as GLP-2 analogs, which contribute to enhancing intestinal adaptation and increasing nutrient absorption. However, it should be noted that these drugs are contraindicated in patients with intestinal malignancies because they stimulate mucus proliferation [22]. One promising therapeutic strategy that could revolutionize the field in the coming years and extend survival rates is the development of tissue‑engineered intestines generated from stem cells [23]. As for chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI), it is also a rare and insidious condition, often diagnosed in its later stages and developing over weeks to months. First described in 1918, the most significant cause is atherosclerosis (in 90% of cases), involving the proximal segments of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA), the celiac artery (CA), or the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) [24]. Studies have not demonstrated any benefit from chronic parenteral nutrition (CPN) in treating CMI; therefore, treatment focuses on revascularization through various methods, the most common of which is the endovascular approach using primary stents [25].

Conclusion

AMI is a rare but serious cause of acute abdominal injuries in emergency departments and requires rapid diagnosis and early treatment. A contrast-enhanced CT-scan or CTA should be performed quickly when AMI is suspected. It can help rule out differential diagnoses, identify underlying causes, and reduce mortality.
