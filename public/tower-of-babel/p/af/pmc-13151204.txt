Balloon detachment following bronchial blocker withdrawal: a case report of a rare complication
Authors: Songkai Long, Gaijun Huang, Huimin Liu, Anchao Li, Hu Yi

Background

The bronchial blocker (BB) is one of the commonly used airway management devices for achieving lung isolation and one-lung ventilation in thoracic surgery [1–4]. Compared with double-lumen endobronchial tubes, BBs offer greater clinical utility due to their simpler operation and reduced tissue trauma. However, with their widespread application, various complications have been increasingly reported, including malposition [5], bronchial mucosal injury [4, 6], and bronchial rupture [7]. Among these, balloon detachment is a rare but potentially severe mechanical complication. Should such an event occur, the detached balloon can cause airway obstruction, posing a life-threatening risk, and therefore warrants significant clinical attention. This report presents a case of an extremely rare balloon detachment during the use of a bronchial blocker and its successful handling. By combining a review of the literature, we conduct an in-depth analysis of its potential causes, management strategies, and preventive measures to enhance clinicians’ awareness and response capabilities regarding this rare event.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old male patient, 168 cm in height and weighing 60 kg, presented with a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. A chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a nodule in the medial segment of the right middle lobe, along with chronic bronchial changes and emphysema. Pulmonary function tests and other examinations did not show significant abnormalities. We planned to resect the right middle lobe nodule via thoracoscopic surgery for rapid frozen section analysis. A right middle lobectomy with lymph node dissection would be performed if the frozen section confirmed malignancy.

The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and anaesthesia was induced with 100 mg of lidocaine, 100 mg of propofol, 20 µg of sufentanil, and 10 mg of cisatracurium. Following muscle relaxation, a single-lumen endotracheal tube (ID 7.5 mm) was inserted under video laryngoscopy and secured 23 cm from the incisors. Two-lung ventilation was performed in volume-controlled mode (tidal volume of 8 ml·kg− 1, PEEP 3cmH2O, respiratory rate 12 times/min, FiO2 100%). After induction, a right thoracic paravertebral block was performed at the T5/6, T4/5, and T3/4 levels under the guidance of B-ultrasound. Anaesthesia was maintained using total intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and remifentanil administered by infusion pump, while cisatracurium was administered intermittently as needed.

The surgical incision was made through the 5/6th intercostal space on the right side of the chest. Following the skin incision, the BB balloon (9Fr) was deflated, and both the tracheal tube lumen and the balloon were thoroughly lubricated. Under direct fiberoptic bronchoscopy (TIC-SD-I, UE Medical) vision, the BB was then advanced into the right main bronchus, and the balloon was inflated with approximately 3 ml of air. Ventilation parameters were adjusted for one-lung ventilation (tidal volume of 6 ml·kg− 1, PEEP 5cmH2O, respiratory rate 16 times/min, FiO2 50%). Thoracoscopic exploration confirmed satisfactory right lung collapse; the patient did not cough, and the BB balloon remained in position throughout the procedure. Recruitment maneuvers were performed upon completion of the surgery, and the patient resumed some spontaneous breathing during chest closure(tidal volume is about 200-250 ml, respiratory rate is about 5–6 times/min). After deflating the BB balloon, resistance was encountered during its withdrawal. Moderate force was applied, suspecting the balloon might have caught on the distal end of the endotracheal tube, after which the balloon was found to have detached. Immediate fiberoptic bronchoscopy revealed the balloon lodged at the distal end of the tracheal tube. An attempt was made to remove the endotracheal tube and take it out together with the detached balloon, but the attempt failed. Inspection of the oropharynx found no balloon, considering the possibility of it falling into the airway. A No. 4 laryngeal mask was inserted to secure ventilation, a senior physician was summoned, and airway pressure parameters were closely monitored. Repeat fiberoptic bronchoscopy (TIC-13, UE Medical) confirmed the balloon had migrated into the left main bronchus, where it oscillated with respiration (Fig. 1. A, B). After several attempts, the balloon tip was grasped using disposable tissue sampling forceps (AMHBFA1.8 * 1200 mm, Anrei Medical) under bronchoscopic guidance and successfully extracted (Fig. 1. C, D). The patient recovered smoothly, was transferred to the PACU following laryngeal mask removal, and experienced no adverse postoperative complications. Discharge occurred on the fifth postoperative day.

Discussion and conclusion

Lung isolation and single-lung ventilation are essential for thoracoscopic surgery, as they provide a clear and stable operative field. This technique requires specialised airway isolation devices [4]. The double-lumen tube (DLT) is considered the clinical “gold standard” due to its extensive history and reliable performance [1, 8]. As an effective alternative, the BB offers several distinct advantages [1, 2, 6]: its smaller outer diameter facilitates placement in patients with abnormal airway anatomy where a DLT cannot be passed; it also avoids the need for tube exchange, benefiting patients with a difficult airway, those requiring prolonged postoperative ventilation, or those at risk of airway oedema; and it enables precise lobar or segmental occlusion rather than only complete lung isolation. Consequently, the BB is now widely used in thoracic surgery, particularly for pediatric patients and difficult airway management [4, 9]. Nevertheless, its increasing use has revealed several associated complications. BB complications primarily include local airway injury, inadequate lung collapse, malposition-related issues, and device technical malfunction.

This article reports an extremely rare device technical malfunction: the complete detachment of the BB balloon. Existing literature indicates this event has an exceptionally low incidence. By the end of 2025, only sporadic cases had been documented in the PubMed and Web of Science databases [10–14]. This not only confirms its rarity but also warns of the potential risk of serious complications with such devices, and also provides an important reference for future safety considerations in its applications in assisting airway foreign body removal [15].

Balloon detachment of the BB is a multifactorial complication. Its aetiology can be categorised into anatomical, device-related, and operational factors. Anatomical factors primarily involve abnormal airway anatomy or difficult airway. Device factors encompass occlusion of the balloon channel, manufacturing defects, and distal deformation of the endotracheal tube. Operational factors include the lack of bronchoscopic guidance, balloon over-inflation or incomplete deflation, excessive traction, and inadvertent passage of the balloon through the Murphy orifice of the endotracheal tube. Additionally, accidental interaction between the balloon and the surgical area is a high-risk factor for injury to the BB balloon. In thoracoscopic surgery using a cutting stapler, failure to withdraw the BB in time may lead to its suturing or transection by the stapler.

As shown in Table 1, most of the balloon-related complications reported in the previous literature were caused by accidental interaction between the balloon and the surgical area, that is, the balloon was sewn or cut by a cutting stapler, which often required the surgeon to remove part of the staple and repair the bronchial stump [12–14]; some literature reported that the balloon channel was blocked and unable to deflate when the BB was withdrawn, which was a functional failure of the balloon, and the treatment methods included overall extubation or puncture of the balloon [10]; other reports described structural damage during retraction, as the balloon was caught by the twisted tip of the endotracheal tube or Murphy’s hole, causing it to avulse and fall into the trachea [11]. Removal required biopsy forceps or other instruments.

This case differs from those previously reported in its aetiology, mechanism, and primary warning. Although the complications in this case also occurred during the withdrawal stage, the core reason clearly pointed to a violation of operating procedures: when withdrawal was blocked, the fiberoptic bronchoscope was not used as required, and efforts were intensified to withdraw the BB. Post-operative examination found that the balloon was generally intact, but with its white tip completely inverted into the balloon interior (Fig. 2A, B). This phenomenon is considered to be due to the violent pulling of the balloon through the endotracheal tube without being fully deflated, causing the balloon to fall off the shaft and stay in the airway. Based on the analysis of the complete detachment of the balloon, the possibility of manufacturing defects at the connection between the balloon and the shaft cannot be completely ruled out. This mechanism is different from the avulsion caused by “stuck in Murphy’s hole” reported in the literature [11]. Regarding management, a fully detached balloon is challenging to extract via a flexible bronchoscope owing to its size, smooth surface, and elasticity. In this case, the balloon was successfully removed by pinching its tip with biopsy forceps; alternatively, specialised grasping forceps or a retrieval basket may be attempted. When flexible endoscopic removal fails, the rigid fiberoptic bronchoscope remains the gold standard for removing foreign bodies.

Prevention remains central to medical safety. For rare yet serious complications like BB balloon detachment, a multi-level preventive strategy is essential. Preoperative evaluation and device inspection are primary. The anaesthesia assessment integrates the patient’s medical history and examination data to confirm the absence of airway malformations or stenosis, particularly proximal stenosis. When preparing the instruments, one must verify the integrity of the sterile packaging, ensure the balloon is undamaged, and confirm that the bronchial blocker and endotracheal tube tips are free of kinks or deformities. For procedures involving bronchial division, special care is required to confirm the bronchial blocker’s position, especially during bronchial transection and tracheal shaping, to prevent its accidental suturing or transection. During insertion, the bronchial blocker balloon and the inner wall of the endotracheal tube must be adequately lubricated, and the manoeuvre should be performed gently. Balloon inflation and deflation must be conducted under direct visualisation via fiberoptic bronchoscopy. If resistance is encountered, excessive force should not be applied, and the operation should be completed with fiberoptic bronchoscopic assistance.

Unfortunately, this study did not publish the manufacturer, model, and batch number of the relevant device, nor could it return the faulty equipment to the manufacturer for defect analysis to determine whether the balloon detachment was due to material fatigue, weak adhesion, design flaws, or pure operational damage. As a result, the opportunity to promote the quality improvement of the medical device was lost, which also had a certain impact on the rigour of this report.

In conclusion, BB balloon detachment is a rare but serious complication that demands an anesthesiologist’s vigilance. This case underscores the principle that prevention is superior to intervention. Strict preoperative assessment, thorough equipment inspection, sustained intraoperative vigilance, and operating with direct fiberoptic bronchoscopic vision are all critical for preventing such events. Furthermore, the use of continuously visible airway management equipment may offer an additional safeguard, enhancing overall clinical safety.
