Gut microbial metabolites, barrier dysfunction, and endotoxemia in men obstructive sleep apnea-associated hypertension
Authors: Chih-Yuan Ko, Li Zhang, Ying Lin, Yiming Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is frequently accompanied by hypertension, yet gut-related mechanisms remain unclear. We profiled fecal and plasma short-chain fatty acids, markers of intestinal barrier injury and endotoxemia, immune phenotypes, and the fecal microbiome in men classified as non-OSA, OSA, OSA with prehypertension, or OSA with hypertension, and reassessed a subset after three months of continuous positive airway pressure. OSA with hypertension was characterized by reduced fecal acetate and butyrate, elevated intestinal permeability and endotoxin markers, and a higher proportion of Th17 cells. Lipopolysaccharide tracked with blood pressure severity and showed inverse associations with fecal butyrate and positive associations with plasma propionate. Short-term CPAP exposure was accompanied by improved sleep metrics, reduced barrier injury and endotoxemia, increased microbial diversity, and altered systemic SCFA patterns. These findings support a gut barrier-endotoxin framework linking sleep-disordered breathing with hypertensive risk.Subject areas: health sciences

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by recurrent airflow limitation during sleep, leading to intermittent hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation.1 Beyond upper airway anatomy, accumulating evidence indicates that OSA is a biologically complex condition shaped by genetic susceptibility, neurobiological regulation of ventilatory control, and altered autonomic and arousal responses, rather than a purely mechanical disorder.2,3

The relationship between OSA and hypertension is likewise multifactorial and involves intertwined pathways linking intermittent hypoxia, sympathetic activation, metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction.4,5 Nearly half of individuals with OSA develop elevated blood pressure, making OSA one of the most common contributors to secondary hypertension, although causal mechanisms vary substantially across patients.6,7 In this context, increasing attention has been directed toward the gut microbiota as a potential integrative regulator of cardiometabolic and inflammatory homeostasis.8,9

Both OSA and hypertension have been linked to a depletion of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)-producing taxa and an enrichment of pro-inflammatory microbial species.9,10 SCFAs, such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate, are crucial for preserving intestinal epithelial integrity, sustaining immune balance, and controlling systemic inflammation.10,11 A reduction in these metabolites compromises barrier function, facilitates the entry of bacterial products like lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into circulation, and disrupts T cell homeostasis, particularly the balance between Th17 and regulatory T cells (Tregs).10,12 These immune-metabolic disturbances are thought to contribute to the pathogenesis of OSA-associated hypertension (OSAHTN).9,10

Under physiological conditions, commensal taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Ruminococcus, and Bacteroides ferment dietary fiber into SCFAs, which reinforce intestinal epithelial tight junctions, attenuate pro-inflammatory signaling, and contribute to vascular stability.10,11 By contrast, microbial dysbiosis characterized by impaired SCFA synthesis and enrichment of Gram-negative, endotoxin-producing bacteria has been associated with metabolic endotoxemia, chronic systemic inflammation, and vascular dysfunction.9,12,13

In OSA, chronic intermittent hypoxia and oxidative stress further destabilize gut microbial communities, leading to reduced SCFA production and increased intestinal permeability.9,10 The resulting leakage of LPS into the circulation activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated inflammatory pathways and promotes vascular remodeling.10,13 Immunological imbalance, particularly a predominance of Th17 cells with a relative reduction in Tregs, has also been observed in OSAHTN, further exacerbating hypertension risk.10,14

Recent prospective clinical evidence confirms that patients with OSA-associated hypertension exhibit distinctive microbial signatures, including reduced Faecalibacterium and increased Blautia and Fusobacterium compared with normotensive OSA patients.14,15 However, most available studies remain descriptive, focusing on taxonomic shifts without fully integrating microbial metabolic output, gut barrier biomarkers, and immune phenotypes.9,10 The extent to which fecal and circulating SCFAs mediate systemic endotoxin burden remains poorly understood, and discrepancies between local and systemic SCFA profiles suggest altered absorption or redistribution under hypoxic stress, mechanisms that require further clarification.10

To date, few studies have simultaneously examined gut microbial metabolites, intestinal barrier integrity, systemic endotoxin exposure, and immune dysregulation in the context of OSAHTN. Most available work has remained descriptive, emphasizing taxonomic changes in gut microbiota without integrating functional metabolic readouts or host immunological responses.9,10 Although clinical studies have confirmed that OSAHTN patients show significant alterations in microbial composition, including reduced Faecalibacterium and enrichment of Blautia and Fusobacterium, the causal links between these microbial shifts and vascular pathology remain insufficiently defined.14

Particularly, whether the combination of SCFAs depletion and LPS elevation constitutes a mechanistic bridge between OSA-induced dysbiosis and hypertension is still unresolved.10,12,16 Furthermore, discrepancies between fecal and circulating SCFA concentrations, frequently observed in patients under chronic intermittent hypoxia, suggest altered absorption, redistribution, or utilization of microbial metabolites, but the underlying mechanisms have yet to be clarified.10,17 Addressing these gaps should require integrative analyses that couple microbiome profiling with metabolic, barrier, and immune readouts to delineate causal pathways driving cardiovascular risk in OSA.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to longitudinally assess whether continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment is associated with changes in gut microbiome composition, SCFA, and related host inflammatory and metabolic signatures in patients with severe OSA-associated hypertension. To reduce biological and clinical heterogeneity and to ensure a high burden of hypoxia and sleep fragmentation, the study was restricted to male patients with severe disease, defined as an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) of 30 events per hour or higher.

In this study, male participants were divided into four groups: control (non-OSA), OSA, OSA with prehypertension (OSApHTN), and OSA with hypertension (OSAHTN). The diagnosis and severity of OSA were confirmed by overnight polysomnography (PSG) according to standard criteria.14,18,19 Fasting blood and stool samples were collected to quantify circulating and fecal SCFAs,20 evaluate intestinal barrier integrity using intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (i-FABP) and D-lactate (D-LA), and assess systemic endotoxin burden via LPS. Immune profiling included flow cytometric analysis of CD4+ T cell subsets and measurement of circulating cytokines.21 In a longitudinal subset, patients initiating CPAP therapy underwent repeat sampling after three months, with device-recorded adherence and residual AHI captured to verify treatment exposure. Statistical analyses combined cross-sectional group comparisons with paired tests and mixed-effects models to evaluate pre-post changes and to examine associations between variations in SCFA, barrier injury markers, endotoxin burden, and blood pressure.

Results

Fecal and plasma SCFA levels

The flowchart of participant enrollment and study group allocation is shown in Figure 1. Fecal acetate was significantly reduced in the OSApHTN (p = 0.012) and OSAHTN (p = 0.025) groups compared with the control group. Acetate was also lower in OSApHTN than in OSA (p = 0.035). Fecal butyrate showed a similar pattern, with significantly decreased levels in OSApHTN (p = 0.019) and OSAHTN (p = 0.031) relative to controls. In contrast, fecal propionate did not differ significantly among the four groups (Figures 2A–2C).

Intestinal mucosal injury and endotoxin levels

Plasma i-FABP and D-LA, both indicators of intestinal barrier injury, were significantly higher in the OSA, OSApHTN, and OSAHTN groups than in controls (all p < 0.001). Plasma LPS concentrations were likewise elevated across these groups relative to controls (all p < 0.001). Compared with the OSA group, the OSAHTN group showed additional increases in D-LA (p = 0.042) and LPS (p = 0.01). LPS levels were also significantly higher in OSAHTN than in OSApHTN (p = 0.002) (Figure 3).

Circulating cytokine and immune cell profiles

Plasma concentrations of interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and IL-17A did not differ significantly among the four groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 1). Flow cytometric analysis of CD4+ T cell subsets showed a higher proportion of Th17 cells, defined by IL-17A expression, in the OSAHTN group compared with both the control group (p = 0.028) and the OSA group (p = 0.031). The Th17 to Treg ratio was numerically higher in OSAHTN than in control and OSA groups, although this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 4).

Associations between gut-derived metabolites, intestinal barrier injury markers, and blood pressure in OSA

Multivariate ordinal logistic regression assessed the relationships of intestinal barrier injury markers (i-FABP and D-LA), LPS, fecal acetate, fecal butyrate, and plasma propionate with the risk of secondary hypertension in patients with severe OSA. Fecal propionate as well as plasma acetate and butyrate were excluded from the model because their levels did not differ significantly among groups. After adjustment for age and body mass index, each one-unit increase in LPS concentration (pg/mL) was associated with a 43.64-fold higher odds of moving to a higher blood pressure category (odds ratio = 43.64, 95% CI: 1.828–1042.107), indicating a strong association between elevated LPS and hypertension risk in this population (Table 2).

Discussion

This study integrates cross-sectional analyses with a short-term longitudinal subset to clarify gut-host interactions in OSA-associated hypertension. Cross-sectionally, patients with OSApHTN and OSAHTN had lower fecal acetate and butyrate, higher plasma i-FABP, D-lactate, and LPS, and a greater proportion of Th17 cells in OSAHTN, indicating impaired microbial metabolic output, intestinal barrier injury, endotoxemia, and immune imbalance. In multivariable models, LPS showed a directionally consistent association with blood pressure category, and fecal SCFAs were inversely correlated with LPS. Extending these observations, three months of CPAP in a subset improved sleep metrics (lower AHI and higher nadir SpO2), reduced i-FABP, D-lactate, and LPS, increased alpha diversity with a discernible community shift, and was accompanied by taxonomic changes (decreases in Firmicutes and Blautia, increase in Verrucomicrobiota). Fecal SCFAs did not change over this interval, whereas plasma propionate declined. These patterns suggest that ameliorating nocturnal hypoxemia can partially normalize barrier and endotoxin signals and modulate gut microbial ecology.

Our observation of reduced fecal acetate and butyrate concentrations in patients with OSA-associated hypertension is in line with prior reports suggesting that SCFA depletion contributes to cardiovascular dysfunction. SCFA-producing taxa are diminished in OSA, and this depletion correlates with impaired vascular tone and increased inflammatory signaling.9,14,21 SCFAs, particularly butyrate, are known to maintain mucosal integrity and modulate systemic immunity via G-protein coupled receptors.11,22 The lower SCFA levels in OSA patients were associated with reduced heart rate variability and increased systolic blood pressure,21,23 corroborating our findings. Moreover, Kim et al.24 found that butyrate levels inversely correlate with circulating LPS and inflammatory markers in hypertensive cohorts.

The increased levels of i-FABP, D-lactate, and LPS across OSA severity and hypertension strata reflect progressive compromise of the intestinal barrier and support the concept of gut-derived endotoxemia. Durgan16 proposed that intermittent hypoxia, a hallmark of OSA, impairs epithelial tight junctions, allowing translocation of bacterial endotoxins into systemic circulation. This hypothesis has since been supported by animal studies25,26 and confirmed in human cohorts.27,28 These studies, consistent with our data, show that LPS elevations are accompanied by increased permeability markers, underscoring the contribution of microbial translocation to vascular inflammation.29

The shift in immune balance toward a Th17-dominant profile in OSAHTN is biologically plausible and supported by previous research. IL-17-mediated activation of vascular smooth muscle cells in OSA patients, contributing to arterial stiffness and remodeling.30,31 Th17 polarization in OSA was correlated with oxygen desaturation severity and systemic inflammation.32 Similarly, experimental evidence that gut dysbiosis in CIH-exposed mice induces IL-6 and TGF-β, promoting Th17 differentiation.33,34 Our findings extend these results by integrating immunophenotyping with metabolic and barrier markers, offering a more comprehensive framework for understanding immune activation in OSAHTN.

Interestingly, we observed a divergence between fecal and plasma SCFA profiles, with fecal SCFAs reduced but plasma propionate elevated in OSA and OSApHTN groups. This dissociation may reflect altered SCFA absorption, redistribution, or utilization under hypoxic stress. Yan et al.35 proposed that low oxygen tension modulates microbial enzymatic activity and SCFA transport, while Kotlyarov et al.36 revealed hypoxia-induced upregulation of SCFA transporters (MCT1 and SMCT1) in intestinal epithelial cells. Our findings suggest that increased systemic SCFAs may not reflect enhanced microbial production but rather compensatory absorption or impaired local consumption due to epithelial damage.

The observed inverse association between SCFAs and LPS supports the notion that microbial metabolites play a protective role against endotoxin translocation. In our regression model, LPS remained a robust predictor of hypertension risk, even after controlling for age and body mass index (BMI). SCFAs, especially butyrate, inhibit TLR4 signaling and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production in the vasculature.13,28 This aligns with our data showing strong negative correlations between fecal butyrate and both i-FABP and LPS. Our findings are also consistent with the integrative model,15 where microbial composition, metabolic output, and host inflammatory responses converge to influence cardiovascular outcomes in OSA. Taken together, our study offers novel insights by integrating multi-dimensional biomarkers across microbiota-derived metabolites, intestinal barrier integrity, and immune polarization. Compared to previous descriptive studies focusing on taxonomic shifts, we provide mechanistic evidence supporting the functional consequences of dysbiosis in OSA-associated hypertension.

The longitudinal findings accord with the cross-sectional framework and place previously reported baseline microbiome features from the same cohort in context; those baseline data were published as Ko et al.14 and represent the pretreatment state. After three months of CPAP, improvements in AHI and nadir oxygen saturation were accompanied by reductions in i-FABP, D-lactate, and LPS, supporting a link among intermittent hypoxemia, epithelial injury, and endotoxin translocation described in prior human and experimental studies of barrier dysfunction in OSA.27,28 Although follow-up PSG was performed without CPAP and residual apnea-hypopnea events persisted in some individuals, CPAP was associated with significant improvements in nocturnal hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation, including reduced ODI, T90 percent, and WASO, together with improved sleep efficiency. These changes suggest that improvements in sleep quality and oxygen burden, rather than complete suppression of respiratory events, may be sufficient to influence downstream metabolic and inflammatory pathways. Beyond hypoxemia alone, improved sleep quality, reflected by reduced sleep fragmentation and arousals, and may further normalize autonomic and neuroendocrine regulation, providing a plausible explanation for microbiome-related changes despite residual respiratory events. In this context, the observed increase in alpha diversity and shift in community structure away from the baseline OSA pattern are directionally consistent with reports that hypoxic stress and sleep disruption perturb gut communities, whereas their mitigation attenuates dysbiosis signals.25,26 Taxonomic changes, including a decrease in Blautia and an increase in Verrucomicrobiota, align with literature indicating that microbial composition is sensitive to oxygen tension and metabolic milieu in sleep-disordered breathing.9,14,21 Notably, fecal SCFAs remained stable, whereas plasma propionate decreased, suggesting that short-term therapy may initially influence intestinal permeability, transport, or systemic handling rather than luminal SCFA production. This interpretation may help to explain the observed dissociation between fecal SCFAs and plasma propionate after CPAP, although direct assessment of intestinal transport and host clearance was not performed, and is compatible with prior studies linking hypoxia to altered SCFA transport and redistribution dynamics.35,36 Prior clinical evidence also indicates that CPAP can rebalance immune phenotypes in OSA, including improvement of Th17 to Treg balance.32 Collectively, these observations extend the baseline picture in Ko et al.14 by demonstrating within-subject movement of barrier and microbial indices toward a less perturbed state during CPAP exposure.

In our Chinese single-center cohort, our report showed that OSA-associated hypertension was characterized by reduced SCFAs-producing taxa (for example, Faecalibacterium, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Ruminococcus, and Bacteroides coprocola) and increased inflammation-associated genera such as Blautia and Fusobacterium.14 In European cohorts, Bikov et al. studied a predominantly Caucasian population from the Hungarian Twin Registry and reported higher Proteobacteria with lower Actinobacteria in patients with OSA, together with taxonomic associations linked to clinical variables.37 Szabo et al.38 investigated a European clinical cohort with OSA stratified by the presence of carotid atherosclerosis and reported atherosclerosis-related microbiome differences, including distinct genus-level signals between patients with and without carotid disease. Notably, these European studies are cross-sectional and therefore provide population context rather than treatment-associated trajectories. In contrast, the present study performs within-subject longitudinal assessment after CPAP, allowing us to test whether microbiome-related host signatures and selected microbial features show directionally consistent changes from the baseline dysbiosis pattern observed in our cohort.

These findings strengthen a gut centered framework for OSA-associated hypertension by linking lower fecal acetate and butyrate, higher circulating permeability and endotoxin markers, and a Th17 predominant immune profile with blood pressure strata. The short term changes during CPAP exposure suggest that improvement in nocturnal hypoxemia can shift intestinal barrier and endotoxin indices and alter community structure even when fecal SCFAs remain stable, while plasma propionate decreases. From a translational perspective, the consistent inverse associations between fecal butyrate and both i-FABP and LPS point to barrier supportive strategies and endotoxin lowering approaches as potential adjuncts to respiratory therapy, while the literature on SCFA signaling and TLR4 related inflammatory control provides a mechanistic basis for such hypotheses.11,13,22,28 The observation that microbial composition is sensitive to oxygen tension and metabolic milieu, together with the directionally favorable diversity and taxonomic signals during CPAP, supports further evaluation of combined interventions that stabilize nocturnal respiration and target gut derived pathways.9,14,21,25,26,27,28

In conclusion, in OSA associated hypertension, lower fecal acetate and butyrate, higher circulating i-FABP, D-lactate, and LPS, and a higher Th17 proportion track with blood pressure severity. LPS was the strongest predictor of higher blood pressure category, and fecal SCFAs were inversely related to endotoxin burden, highlighting impaired microbial metabolic output and barrier dysfunction as central features among OSA patients linked to hypertension. In a short follow up subset, CPAP improved sleep metrics, reduced i-FABP, D-lactate, and LPS, and shifted microbial diversity and composition, while fecal SCFAs were unchanged and plasma propionate decreased. These findings support a gut-related mechanism linking sleep disordered breathing to hypertensive risk and indicate that effective respiratory therapy can begin to normalize barrier and endotoxin signals.

Limitations of the study

Several considerations delimit the inferences that can be drawn from this integrative clinical study. Because the protocol combined overnight PSG with serial collection of biospecimens for microbiota profiling and host barrier, endotoxin, metabolite, and inflammatory readouts, participant burden, and ethics-approved feasibility constrained enrollment and the scale of longitudinal sampling. Consistent with these constraints, the longitudinal subset was modest, and follow-up spanned roughly three months, limiting conclusions about durability and hard clinical endpoints. In addition, blood pressure was classified using standardized office measurements rather than ambulatory monitoring, which may underestimate nocturnal or masked hypertension in OSA. Although CPAP treatment was confirmed via device downloads, residual night-to-night variability in usage and unmeasured lifestyle factors, including dietary intake (particularly dietary fiber) and physical activity, could have contributed to inter-individual trajectories in microbiome features and circulating biomarkers. Accordingly, the sample size was not powered for small effects or extensive multivariable modeling, resulting in model instability and wide confidence intervals for some estimates, and the analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Additionally, fecal SCFAs represent a distal compartmental snapshot and may not reflect production, transport, and host clearance; the absence of transporter expression and hepatic handling measures could explain why fecal SCFAs were unchanged while plasma propionate declined after CPAP.35,36 Immune profiling at follow-up was incomplete, and neither upstream regulatory pathways nor downstream effector programs were interrogated; prior evidence that CPAP may shift the Th17/Treg balance motivates more targeted mechanistic work in larger cohorts.32 Finally, baseline taxonomic profiles for this cohort have been reported previously as the pretreatment state; accordingly, to minimize redundancy, the present manuscript focuses on within-subject change relative to that baseline and integrates these changes with host readouts.14 Future studies that extend follow-up, incorporate standardized dietary monitoring, higher-resolution strain/function profiling, metabolite flux and transporter assessments, and ambulatory blood pressure phenotyping will be necessary to determine whether the observed biomarker shifts translate into sustained cardiovascular benefit.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study participants

Forty-seven men aged 20 to 60 years with a BMI of at least 24 kg/m2 and residence in Fujian Province were enrolled. A sex-restricted design was adopted to improve internal consistency of microbiome and cardiometabolic phenotyping. Because only male participants were enrolled, sex-based differences could not be evaluated, and the findings may not be generalizable to female patients with OSA. Individuals were eligible only if they had no history of diabetes, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease, malignancy, autoimmune disorders, or psychiatric conditions. Exclusion criteria included prior treatment for hypertension or OSA, use of antibiotics, probiotics, or immunosuppressive agents within the preceding month, and the presence of other primary sleep disorders.

PSG procedures were consistent with our previously published protocol.21 Overnight polysomnography was conducted in a dedicated sleep laboratory at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University by trained technologists using a computerized polysomnographic system. Recordings included electroencephalography, electrooculography, electromyography, electrocardiography, airflow, thoracoabdominal movements, and pulse oximetry. Sleep stages and respiratory events were scored using validated software according to the 2017 American Academy of Sleep Medicine criteria. Apnea was defined as a reduction in airflow of at least 90 percent lasting 10 s or longer. Hypopnea was defined as a reduction of at least 30 percent lasting 10 s or longer accompanied by either a 3 percent oxygen desaturation or an arousal. The apnea-hypopnea index was calculated as the total number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour of sleep.18

Blood pressure categories were assigned according to the 2018 European Hypertension, Blood pressure categories were assigned according to the 2018 European Hypertension Guidelines19 and were based on standardized office measurements rather than ambulatory monitoring, as repeated clinic-based assessments were integrated into the study protocol to ensure feasibility and consistency across visits.

Participants were stratified into four groups: non-OSA with AHI <5 events per hour and blood pressure <130/85 mmHg; severe OSA with AHI ≥30 events per hour and blood pressure <130/85 mmHg; OSApHTN with AHI ≥30 events per hour and blood pressure 130/85 to 140/90 mmHg; and OSAHTN with AHI ≥30 events per hour and blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg. This grouping approach mirrors previously published work using the same thresholds for AHI and blood pressure classification in similar clinical cohorts. The final sample comprised 12 participants in the non-OSA group, 11 in the severe OSA group, 13 in the OSApHTN group, and 11 in the OSAHTN group (Figure 1).

Ten patients received CPAP therapy, with adherence defined as use for at least 4 h per night over a three-month period. CPAP treatment was prescribed and initiated according to routine clinical practice. Because device selection could not be restricted in the clinical setting, devices from different manufacturers were used. Treatment exposure was verified using device-recorded data, including nightly usage duration and residual AHI, allowing confirmation of adherence and treatment effectiveness independent of device brand. Follow-up assessments were conducted at baseline and at three months and included polysomnography or device-derived AHI, blood pressure measurements, stool and blood sampling for targeted metabolite analyses, and immune profiling. Follow-up polysomnography was performed without CPAP; therefore, post-treatment AHI values reflect residual disease severity rather than on-treatment respiratory event suppression.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. 2020-160). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Method details

Sample collection and preparation

All participants underwent overnight polysomnography, and fasting specimens were obtained the following morning. Venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes, centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min, and plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis. Stool and plasma samples were prepared for short-chain fatty acid quantification and for assessment of intestinal barrier injury markers following procedures adopted in prior OSA–hypertension investigations.

SCFAs quantification

Concentrations of acetate, propionate, and butyrate were quantified in plasma and stool homogenates using gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-MS, Agilent 7890A, USA).20 For each run, 100 mg of stool or 100 μL of plasma was mixed with 50 μL of 15 percent phosphoric acid, 100 μL of internal standard solution at 125 μg/mL, and 400 μL of diethyl ether. After vortexing and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, the organic supernatant was injected into the GC-MS system. Calibration curves generated from serial dilutions of authentic standards were used for quantification. Results were normalized and expressed as 2μg/g for stool and pg/mL for plasma.

Intestinal mucosal damage and endotoxin measurement

Plasma i-FABP, D-LA, and LPS were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Amoy Lunchangshuo Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These analytes were selected a priori as indicators of intestinal epithelial injury and systemic endotoxin burden based on prior evidence that i-FABP reflects ischemic mucosal damage and that elevated D-LA and circulating LPS accompany barrier dysfunction in OSA cohorts.

Cytokines and immune cells analysis

Circulating cytokines, including IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17A, were measured using a human Th1/Th2/Th17 cytometric bead array.21 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation9 and analyzed by flow cytometry for CD4-positive T cell subsets including Th1, Th2, Th17, and regulatory T cells. The panel and gating strategy were selected to capture the Th17/Treg axis, which is frequently perturbed in OSA and mechanistically linked to inflammatory activation.

Gut microbiota analysis

Fecal samples were processed according to our previously validated protocol.21 Bacterial DNA was extracted, the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified, and libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform. Reads were denoised using QIIME2 with DADA2, and taxonomic assignment was performed using the SILVA reference database. Alpha diversity was assessed using Shannon and Chao1 indices. Beta diversity was evaluated using Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac distances. Community differences were tested by PERMANOVA. Differential abundance analyses were conducted with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Data distributions were examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median with interquartile range otherwise. In all analyses, n represents the number of independent human participants per group. No technical replicates were used in statistical modeling.

Group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance with Student–Newman–Keuls or least significant difference procedures for post hoc testing. When assumptions were not met, Welch’s ANOVA with Games–Howell post hoc comparisons was applied. Canonical correlation analysis was used as an exploratory approach to identify coordinated multivariate patterns between microbial features and host biomarker domains, providing an integrative overview rather than hypothesis-testing inference. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of hypertension among participants with OSA. Covariate inclusion was guided by biological plausibility and prior evidence, with parsimonious model specification adopted to limit overfitting and instability. Correlated biomarkers reflecting overlapping biological domains were not entered simultaneously into the same regression models.

Within-subject changes from baseline to three months were evaluated using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. Prespecified outcomes included bacterial taxa, SCFAs, and plasma biomarkers of barrier injury and endotoxin. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. Two-sided p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The study was feasibility-driven due to the intensive PSG-plus-biospecimen protocol; therefore, no formal a priori power calculation was performed, and effect sizes and confidence intervals are reported to support interpretation.
