Prognostic value of lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio in patients with small cell lung cancer: a meta-analysis
Authors: Fan Yang, Zhaofeng Wang, Minming Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) has been previously analyzed for its value in forecasting prognostic outcomes in patients with small cell lung cancer (SCLC); however, the findings remain conflicting. Therefore, we performed this meta-analysis to identify LMR’s precise role of the LMR in predicting the prognosis of SCLC.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library until April 10, 2025, and evaluated function of LMR in forecasting SCLC prognosis by determining aggregated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsNine articles involving 1701 cases included in the present study. As revealed by our pooled data, decreased LMR showed close relation to poor overall survival (OS) (HR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.16–1.91, p = 0.002) and inferior progression-free survival (PFS) (HR = 1.77, 95% CI = 1.16–2.70, p < 0.001) in SCLC. As indicated by subgroup analysis, prominent prognostic value of the LMR for OS remained unaffected by sample size, study center, or threshold point (p < 0.05), and that for PFS remained unaffected by country (p < 0.05). The stability of the results was also verified using sensitivity and publication-bias analyses.ConclusionAccording to our findings, LMR is markedly related to dismal OS and inferior PFS of patients with SCLC. Thus, LMR can serve as a candidate prognostic marker for SCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04041-4.

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is highly aggressive and accounts for 15% of all lung cancer cases [1]. The tumor stages of SCLC patients are assessed according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group classification system, including extensive-stage (ES) and limited-stage (LS), with approximately 70% of patients being in ES at first diagnosis [2]. Platinum-based chemotherapy continues to be the primary treatment for SCLC, with an initial response rate of approximately 60–65% [3]. The median overall survival (OS) in patients with LS SCLC patients is 16–24 months when treated, compared to just 6–12 months in patients with ES SCLC [4]. Moreover, most patients with SCLC develop disease recurrence despite an initial response to chemotherapy, and the 5-year OS rate is < 7% [5]. Consequently, the identification of novel and reliable prognostic markers for SCLC is crucial for improving survival outcomes.

Evidence suggests that host systemic inflammatory responses are essential for cancer onset and progression of cancer [6]. Inflammatory proteins and cells are essential for regulating cancer development and progression, and may be useful as prognostic indicators [7]. Blood-based markers are of significant value in forecasting cancer prognosis, including platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio [8], systemic immune-inflammation index [9], neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [10], C-reactive protein/albumin ratio [11], and prognostic nutritional index [12]. The lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) can be calculated by dividing the peripheral lymphocyte quantity by the peripheral monocyte quantity [13]. The LMR is widely suggested to be of prognostic significance for many solid tumors, such as biliary tract [14], bladder [15], breast [16], gastric [17], and esophageal cancers [18]. Previous studies have also investigated the prognostic value of LMR in SCLC; however, the results remain inconsistent [19–27]. For example, some studies have shown that a low LMR is significantly associated with a poor prognosis in SCLC [22, 23, 27]. However, other researchers have reported that LMR is not closely related to survival outcomes in SCLC [21, 25, 26]. Consequently, this meta-analysis focused on identifying accurate values of the LMR for forecasting SCLC prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study guideline

This meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Supplementary File 1) [28]. This meta-analysis has been registered in INPLASY (Registration No. INPLASY202570051). The protocol can be accessed at https://inplasy.com/inplasy-2025-7-0051/.

Search strategy

We screened PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library through April 10, 2025, using the following search strategies: (LMR OR lymphocyte-monocyte ratio OR lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio OR lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio) AND (Small cell lung cancer OR SCLC). The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary File 2. Only publications in English were included in the analysis. Reference lists of qualified articles were also checked to obtain eligible literature.

Eligibility criteria

The following studies were included: (1) those involving patients with a pathologic diagnosis of SCLC; (2) those reporting the relationship between LMR and SCLC survival; (3) those providing hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); (4) those with an identified LMR; and (5) published in English language. The following studies were excluded: (1) reviews, letters, meeting abstracts, comments, and case reports; (2) inclusion of duplicate cases; and (3) animal studies.

Data collection and quality assessment

Two investigators (F.Y. and Z.W.) independently extracted data from eligible studies, and all disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third investigator (M.W.). The following data were included: author, year, country, sample size, age, sex, study design, study period, study center, clinical stage, treatment, threshold, survival outcomes, survival analysis, follow-up, HRs, and 95% CIs. OS and progression-free survival (PFS) were the primary outcomes of this study. Meanwhile, we utilized Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for assessing study quality, and it covered three dimensions: selection, comparability, and outcome assessment (0–4, 0–2 and 0–3 points separately) [29], resulting in the NOS score being 0–9; ≥6 suggested high quality.

Statistical analysis

We determined the aggregated HRs and 95%CIs to evaluate LMR’s effect of the LMR on SCLC prognosis. The Cochrane Q test and I2 statistics were used to estimate the among-study heterogeneities. A fixed-effects model should be utilized to analyze data at p ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%, suggesting no obvious heterogeneity; or else, a random-effects model should be adopted. Subgroup analyses were performed to determine the source of heterogeneity. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of a single article on the overall HR for outcomes with notable heterogeneity. We used funnel plots and Egger’s and Begg’s tests to examine potential publication bias. Data obtained from individual articles were examined using the Stata software (version 12.0; Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). P < 0.05 stood for significant differences.

Results

Literature retrieval process

Through primary retrieval, we identified 67 studies, of which 42 were retained following exclusion of duplicates (Fig. 1). Through title and abstract screening, we eliminated 25 irrelevant articles. Subsequently, 17 articles were evaluated by full-text examination and eight studies were discarded for the following reasons: no survival data (n = 3), not on SCLC (n = 2), not on LMR (n = 2), and no cut-off value identified (n = 1). Finally, nine articles involving 1701 cases [19–27] were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Included study features

The included articles were published between 2017 and 2024 and were in English [19–27] (Table 1). Seven studies were conducted in China [19, 21–24, 26, 27], one in Turkey [20], and one in France [25] with sample size ranging between 53 and 707 (median, 113). Eight studies were of retrospective design [19, 20, 22–27] and one was a prospective trial [21]. Two studies included patients with LS [23, 27], five enrolled patients with ES [20–22, 24, 25], and two studies recruited patients with LS + ES [19, 26]. Eight were single-center study [19–23, 25–27], and one was multicentric [24]. Three studies treated patients with chemotherapy [19, 20, 25], two used chemotherapy plus immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [21, 24], two used chemoradiotherapy (CRT) [23, 27], and one each adopted targeted therapy [22] and ICIs [26]. The LMR thresholds was 1.61–8.33 (median, 3.26). All included studies used a receiver operating characteristic curve for threshold determination [19–27]. All nine studies reported role of the LMR in forecasting OS [19–27], whereas five showed its role in forecasting PFS [22–26] in SCLC. Five articles obtained HRs and 95% CIs using univariate regression [20, 22, 24, 25, 27], whereas four used multivariate regression [19, 21, 23, 26]. All included articles had NOS scores of 7–9, suggesting a high quality (Table 1).

LMR and OS

All nine studies recruiting 1701 patients [19–27] reported a relationship between the LMR and OS in SCLC. We used a random-effects model, considering the obvious heterogeneity (I2 = 68.2%, p = 0.001). The pooled results were: HR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.16–1.91, p = 0.002, suggesting the significant relation of decreased LMR to poor OS in SCLC (Fig. 2; Table 2). Subgroup analysis indicated that the significant prognostic value of the LMR for OS remained unaffected by sample size, study center, or cut-off value (p < 0.05; Table 2). Furthermore, the LMR significantly predicted OS in studies conducted in China (p = 0.006), retrospective studies (p = 0.001), LS stage (p < 0.001), CRT treatment (p < 0.001), targeted therapy (p = 0.003), and univariate analysis (p = 0.010) (Table 2).

LMR and PFS

Five articles involving 691 cases [22–26] analyzed role of the LMR in predicting the PFS of SCLC. We used the random-effects model, considering the obvious heterogeneity (I2 = 76.7%, p = 0.002). Based on our synthetized data, low LMR significantly predicted the dismal PFS of SCLC (HR = 1.77, 95% CI = 1.16–2.70, p < 0.001; Fig. 3; Table 3). Furthermore, according to the subgroup analyses, LMR’s role in forecasting PFS remained unaffected by country (p < 0.05; Table 3). Furthermore, LMR still significantly predicted the unfavorable PFS of sample size ≥ 120 (p = 0.022), LS stage (p = 0.002), single center studies (p = 0.006), and LMR threshold ≥ 3.5 (p = 0.034) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate whether our findings were stable by systematically eliminating one of the included articles. In this analysis, eliminating each article made no significant difference in the overall OS or PFS results, suggesting stable outcomes (Fig. 4).

Publication bias

We employed funnel plots and Egger’s and Begg’s tests to estimate publication bias. The symmetry of funnel plots was observed (Fig. 5). The results also indicated no significant publication bias for OS (p = 0.198/0.277 on Egger’s and Begg’s tests) or PFS (p = 0.221/0.124 on Egger’s and Begg’s tests) in this meta-analysis (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The LMR has been widely analyzed for its effect on forecasting SCLC prognosis, but its findings remain controversial. We aggregated information from 9 publications involving 1701 patients [19–27] to identify precise effect of the LMR in predicting SCLC. As a result, a decreased LMR significantly predicted dismal OS and inferior PFS in patients with SCLC. Moreover, based on subgroup analyses, the LMR had a stable effect on forecasting OS and PFS. Moreover, the findings were verified to be stable through sensitivity and publication-bias analyses. Collectively, decreased LMR serves as a stable biomarker to predict both short- and long-term survival outcomes in SCLC. To the best of our knowledge, the present meta-analysis provides the primary investigation of effect of the LMR on forecasting SCLC prognosis.

LMR is a prognostic indicator for different tumors, indicating systemic inflammation [30]. The reduced LMR is probably the result of a lower lymphocyte quantity and/or higher monocyte quantity. The mechanisms underlying prognostic role of LMRs in SCLC require further exploration; however, they are interpreted below. On one hand, lymphocytes are important for immune function, with strong anticancer activities that can impede the growth and spread of different tumors [31]. Specifically, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) may cause cytotoxicity and prevent cancer cell growth and movement by modulating immune interactions [32]. Growing evidence suggests that lymphocytes have specific cytotoxic effects against tumors and enhance the antitumor response by transforming into TILs [33]. Therefore, a reduction in lymphocyte count may suggest that tumor cells evade the immune system, potentially affecting the patient’s survival prognosis. In contrast, monocytes that circulate in the bloodstream can differentiate into tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and exert an essential effect on tumor-associated inflammation [34]. The dual functionality of TAMs involves attracting tumor-associated chemokines for peripheral blood monocyte recruitment and regulating regulatory T cells and innate immunity, thereby reducing anti-tumor immune responses and simultaneously enhancing angiogenesis and extracellular matrix breakdown [35]. Increasing evidence indicates that TAMs are abundantly present in tumor tissues, where they enhance tumor angiogenesis and counteract immune responses through producing TNF-α and vascular endothelial growth factor, ultimately leading to tumor development [36]. Therefore, the LMR could be an effective prognostic marker based on the biological functions of lymphocytes and monocytes.

Notably, subgroup analysis showed that the LMR remained a significant prognostic marker for patients undergoing CRT (p < 0.001) and targeted therapy (p = 0.003), but not for those receiving chemotherapy (p = 0.171), chemotherapy plus ICIs (p = 0.512), or ICIs (p = 0.215) (Table 2). These findings indicate that the LMR may not be a significant prognostic marker of outcomes in patients undergoing systemic treatment, especially immunotherapy. These results may be explained by several underlying mechanisms. First, the subgroups stratified by the treatment method had a limited sample size (Table 2). In this subgroup analysis, the patients were divided into five subgroups, each of which was relatively small. Therefore, the selection bias could be large in each subgroup. Second, the lower LMR was probably due to a decrease in lymphocyte numbers and/or an increase in monocyte numbers. However, in patients with SCLC receiving ICIs, anti-tumor activity is enhanced, and TILs usually increase in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [37, 38]. Therefore, the LMR did not decrease in patients with SCLC treated with ICIs. Third, patients receiving chemotherapy and systemic therapy have relatively severe side effects. Clinicians can monitor the complete blood count, and patients with low lymphocyte counts can be treated in a timely manner. Therefore, the LMR may not be low in patients receiving chemotherapy and systemic therapies.

Recently, a meta-analysis suggested that the LMR is of great significance in forecasting cancer prognosis [39–43]. Mei et al. showed that lower LMR levels were robustly correlated with an unfavorable prognosis regarding OS, PFS, and cancer-specific survival in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer in a meta-analysis involving 3089 cases [39]. Xiang et al. reported LMR as an independent risk factor that affected long-term prognostic outcomes in prostate cancer cases based on a meta-analysis involving six articles [40]. According to another meta-analysis involving 3377 cases, a low LMR is associated with poor prognosis in esophageal cancer [41]. Wei and colleagues reported a relationship between reduced LMR and worse disease-free survival, OS, and PFS in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma in their meta-analysis of 16 studies [42]. Additionally, in a recent meta-analysis, a decreased LMR was documented to be closely related to dismal OS of gliomas [43]. Our findings concur with results from other cancer types.

This study had some limitations. First, it was mostly retrospective in nature. Therefore, a selection bias may have existed. We did not restrict the design of the eligible studies. Second, some subgroups, including targeted therapy and ICIs, had relatively small sample sizes. Therefore, these interpretations should be considered with caution. Third, the pooled HRs and 95% CIs, not the data for each individual patient, were combined. Considering these limitations, future multi-center prospective studies should be conducted to validate the clinical utility of LMR.

Conclusion

In summary, this meta-analysis demonstrated that a reduced LMR was significantly associated with unfavorable OS and inferior PFS in patients with SCLC. Thus, LMR can serve as a candidate prognostic marker for SCLC.
