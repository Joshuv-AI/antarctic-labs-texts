Electrical Heterogeneity in Hispanic Background Subpopulations
Authors: Larisa G Tereshchenko, Kazi T Haq, Stacey J Howell, Evan C Mitchell, Jessica Hyde, Jesús Martínez, Cassandra A Ahmed, Genesis Briceno, Hetal Patel, Jose Pena, Akram Khan, Elsayed Z Soliman, João AC Lima, Samir R Kapadia, Anita D Misra-Hebert, Michael W Kattan, Mayank M Kansal, Martha L Daviglus, Robert Kaplan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Hispanic/Latino population is not uniform. Prevalence and clinical outcomes of cardiac arrhythmias in ethnic background subgroups are variable, but the reasons for differences are unclear. Vectorcardiographic Global Electrical Heterogeneity (GEH) has been shown to be associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to compare GEH in Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. We hypothesized that ethnicity category moderates an association of prevalent cardiovascular disease (CVD) with GEH.MethodsCross-sectional analysis of the HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos) included 15,684 participants (mean age 41 years; 38% Mexican, 20% Cuban, 16% Puerto Rican, 10% Dominican, 7% Central American, 5% South American, 4% mixed Hispanic/Latino background). Acculturation and socioeconomic data were collected. GEH was measured as spatial QRS-T angle, spatial ventricular gradient (SVG) azimuth, SVG elevation, SVG magnitude, and sum absolute QRST integral. Linear regression models included interaction terms of ethnic background category by CVD and were adjusted for age, sex, education attainment, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidemia, obesity, chronic kidney disease, physical activity, diet quality, heart rate, and rhythm.ResultsThe adjusted spatial QRS-T angle was significantly (P < 0.0001) narrower in Dominican background (−3.4°[95% CI −5.0° to −1.7°]) as compared to a total mean. SVG azimuth pointed farther posteriorly in Dominican (+2.9 [95% CI: 1.6-4.2]) and Puerto Rican (+3.8 [2.4-5.2]), but farther anteriorly in South American (−2.9 [95% CI: −4.4 to −1.4]) and Mexican (−3.5 [95% CI: −4.3 to −2.6]) vs total mean. An association of coronary heart disease with GEH was especially strong in Cuban background subpopulation.ConclusionsIn CVD-free Hispanic/Latino subpopulations, cardiovascular risk factors do not fully explain GEH differences across ethnic background categories, which likely reflect unmeasured health disparities.Key words: cardiovascular disease, ECG, health disparities, health equity, Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, vectorcardiogram

According to the 2020 census, 19% of the U.S. population, over 62 million Americans, identified themselves as Hispanic or Latino. Hispanic adults ≥20 years of age have a very high prevalence of obesity and a high incidence of stroke.1 Nevertheless, Hispanic adults have only one-half the prevalence and incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF) than non-Hispanic White persons.2 Hispanic/Latino adults are underrepresented in cardiac electrophysiology research, highlighting the importance of studying ethnic differences.3

Notably, the Hispanic/Latino population is not uniform. A few studies reported differences in clinical outcomes for Hispanic/Latino subpopulations, even after controlling for clinical and sociodemographic characteristics. For example, age-adjusted mortality for ischemic heart disease in foreign-born Puerto Rican Americans is higher than in foreign-born Cuban Americans and Mexican Americans.4 However, studies of the electrophysiological substrate in Hispanic/Latino subgroups are scarce. The HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos) is one of the very few studies that prospectively systematically collected data for Hispanic/Latino adults from various backgrounds.5,6 The HCHS/SOL study showed that Puerto Rican and Dominican individuals have more major ECG abnormalities (including prevalent AF) than Mexican participants.7,8

Vectorcardiographic (VCG) global electrical heterogeneity (GEH) phenotype9 is a set of 5 biomarkers, which, taken together, characterize a 3-dimensional spatial ventricular gradient (SVG) vector.10 GEH is associated with broadly defined cardiovascular disease (CVD),11,12 ventricular tachyarrhythmias,13 sudden cardiac death,14 stroke,15 anthracycline cardiotoxicity,16 and response to cardiac resynchronization therapy.17,18 As VCG GEH carries additional, complementary to 12-lead ECG information,19 hypothetically, it can help reconcile discrepancies in epidemiological findings of Hispanic/Latino adults. However, no study has been done on VCG GEH in Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted the study aiming to compare VCG GEH in HCHS/SOL study participants from various ethnic backgrounds, with and without CVD. We hypothesized that Hispanic/Latino background moderates the association of prevalent CVD with GEH.

Methods

The Institutional Review Boards approved the study at each participating institution, and all participants signed informed consent before entering the study.

Study population

The HCHS/SOL is a multicenter, community-based longitudinal cohort study of cardiovascular risk factors in Hispanic/Latino adults of 18 to 74 years of age.5,20,21 The study has been previously described.5,6,20,21 Enrollment was completed between March 2008 and June 2011 in the Bronx (New York), Chicago (Illinois), Miami (Florida), and San Diego (California). The study was designed to represent the Hispanic/Latino origin subpopulations: Mexican, Cuban, Puerto Rican, Dominican, Central and South American, and mixed/other (eg, European).

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of the data collected at the baseline examination. The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1. The HCHS/SOL participants with available digital 12-lead ECG and data on CVD status and cardiovascular risk factors were included (n = 15,684).

ECG and VCG analysis: GEH measurements

First, the resting 12-lead ECGs were analyzed as previously described, using the Minnesota Code. Then, the raw, fully deidentified digital ECG signal was analyzed in the Tereshchenko laboratory in a blinded manner.14,22, 23, 24 At least 2 physician investigators (S.J.H., E.C.M., L.G.T.) manually labeled each cardiac beat. The 12-lead ECG was transformed into XYZ ECG using Kors transformation. The origin of the heart vector was automatically identified, and the time-coherent global median beat was constructed using only one (dominant) type of beat.24 The normal (N) median beat category included normal sinus, atrial paced, junctional, and ectopic atrial median beats. The supraventricular (S) category included median beats of AF or atrial flutter with a dominant type of ventricular conduction. The ventricular paced (VP) median beat represented its own separate category. GEH was measured on N median beat in 99.8% (95% CI: 99.7%-99.8%) ECGs. The GEH (Figure 2) was measured by area-based spatial QRS-T angle (QRSTa, a.k.a. spatial mean QRST angle), SVG azimuth (SVGaz), SVG elevation (SVGel), SVG magnitude (SVGmag), and SVG scalar, sum absolute QRST integral (SAIQRST).22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 Investigators performed quality control of automated ECG analysis with the aid of a visual display (K.T.H.).

Prevalent CVD and cardiovascular risk factors

Prevalent CVD was defined as a self-reported history of coronary heart disease (CHD) or stroke, not counting angina or transient ischemic attack. Pure CHD history included a self-reported history of myocardial infarction, coronary artery bypass grafting, or percutaneous coronary intervention, not counting angina.

Cardiovascular risk factors were presented as categories, which permitted accurate reporting of weighted prevalence (per the study design). Age categories included 18 to 44, 45 to 64, and ≥65 years of age. The education level attained was categorized as less than high school, high school or equivalent, greater than high school with some college, and university or college. Untreated blood pressure (BP) categories included: normal BP <120/80 mm Hg; elevated BP 120 to 129/<80 mm Hg; hypertension stage 1 130 to 139/80 to 89 mm Hg; and hypertension stage 2 ≥140/90 mm Hg. The use of BP-lowering medications was a separate category. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, or self-reported use of antihyperglycemic medications for diagnosed diabetes. Cigarette smoking was categorized as former, current, or never. Hypercholesterolemia/dyslipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL, low density Lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol ≥160 mg/dL, high density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL, or use of lipid-lowering drugs.6 Obesity was defined as a body mass index ≥30 kg/m2. Diet quality was assessed by a score according to the sex-specific quintile of daily intake of saturated fatty acids, potassium, calcium, and fiber, with the score of 5 representing the most favorable quintile (ie, lowest quintile of intake for saturated fatty acids and highest quintile of intake for potassium, calcium, and fiber).28 The 4 scores were summed, and the higher 40 percentile was considered a healthier diet.6 Physical activity (moderate/heavy intensity work and leisure activities) in a typical week was assessed according to the 2008 physical activity guidelines,29 as meeting or not the guidelines level. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated from serum creatinine and cystatin C using the new chronic kidney disease (CKD)-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation fitted without race.30 CKD was defined as urinary albumin to creatinine ratio ≥30 and/or estimated glomerular filtration rate cr-cys(AS) <60 ml/min/1.73 m2.

Socioeconomic characteristics

U.S.-native status was defined as the place of birth in any of the 50 states and U.S. territories, including Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Virgin Islands. To reflect migration and acculturation patterns when compared to those born on the U.S. mainland (ie, 50 states), a proportion of the population lived in the 50 U.S. states ≥10 years is calculated. The participant immigrant generation status was based on a combination of the participant's country of origin and maternal and paternal parents and grandparents. The first generation is defined as foreign-born with foreign-born parents. Persons who are born in a U.S. territory are treated as foreign-born or first-generation immigrants. The 2nd generation is defined as U.S.-born or as foreign-born with at least one U.S.-born parent. The language subscore of the acculturation score was based on the language spoken at home. A score equal to 0 indicated “only Spanish,” 0.5– “more Spanish than English,” 1.0– “both languages equally,” 1.5– “more English than Spanish,” and 2– “only English.” Household income 2 categories include: 1) <$30,000; and 2) $30,000 or more, or refused to answer.

Statistical analysis

Survey design and analysis aspects

All analyses accounted for the HCHS/SOL design as a stratified 2-stage area probability sample.21 The normalized weight that sums to the enrolled study participants from all 4 field centers (n = 16,415) was used so that the degrees of freedom from the sum of the weights were not inflated. The sampling weights were calibrated to the U.S. 2010 Census within the prespecified HCHS/SOL target enrollment areas. The primary sampling unit clustering variable was a combination of the Field Center and selected block group identifier. The stratum was a combination of the Field Center, Hispanic/Latino Household Proportion (high, low), and socioeconomic status (high, low). Generalized linear model analysis of complex survey data used Taylor linearization-based variance estimators.

To appropriately handle excluded observations, the subpopulation of the HCHS/SOL study participants who met the eligibility criteria was analyzed (Figure 1). The excluded participants were a part of the target population and, therefore, contributed to the variance calculations. We estimated population means and 95% CIs for continuous variables. The population means of circular variables (QRSTa, SVGaz, SVGel angles) were normally distributed and were included in the analysis without transformations. Unadjusted categorical prevalence estimates were calculated using survey logistic regression conditional margins. Age-adjusted prevalence estimates were calculated as marginal means of outcome for levels of covariates with age handled as population-averaged.

Linear regression models

To determine whether Hispanic/Latino background category and CVD or pure CHD are associated with GEH, we constructed linear regression models with GEH measurements as outcome variables in individual models, each adjusted for known confounders: age, sex, education attainment, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hypercholesterolemia/dyslipidemia, obesity, CKD, level of physical activity, diet quality, the type of median beat (N, S, or VP), and average RR interval.14,25, 26, 27,31,32 A separate set of models was constructed with an interaction term of Hispanic/Latino background category with CVD or pure CHD status. Furthermore, yet another additional set of models included two-way interactions between sex, Hispanic/Latino background category, and CVD or pure CHD status. Stratified adjusted (as described above) analyses were also performed to determine whether GEH variables are associated with prevalent CVD in each Hispanic/Latino background subpopulation. Linear regression assumptions (normality, linearity, homoskedasticity, and absence of influential outliers) were tested and confirmed (Supplemental Figures 1 to 5). We observed some evidence of heteroskedasticity. Therefore, robust standard errors were calculated in all models. To account for multiple hypotheses testing (7 background categories by 5 GEH variables), P < 0.05/35, or P < 0.0014, was considered statistically significant.

Latent class analysis

A generalized structural equation model was used to model joint probabilities (multiple responses) of 5 continuous GEH variables (QRSTa, SVGaz, SVGel, SVGmag, and SAIQRST) as a mixture of 3 normal distributions/latent classes. As previously described, the model was adjusted by all the above-listed covariates.33 We compared the distributions of 3 latent GEH classes in Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations with and without CVD.

Results

Study population: CVD and cardiovascular risk factors

Among 15,684 participants, 37.8% (95% CI: 34.6%-41.1%) had Mexican background, 20.1% (95% CI: 17.0%-23.6%) Cuban; 15.6% (95% CI: 14.1%-17.2%) Puerto-Rican; 9.9% (95% CI: 8.6%-11.4%) Dominican; 7.4% (95% CI: 6.4%-8.6%) Central American; 5.0% (95% CI: 4.4%-5.6%) South American; and 4.2% (95% CI: 3.7%-4.8%) mixed/other Hispanic/Latino background.

As shown in Table 1, nearly 70% of the Mexican background subpopulation were young adults (age 18-44 years), and only 5% were at least 65 years of age. In contrast, the Cuban background subpopulation had a more uniform distribution of age, with approximately 20% of persons 65 years and older, 40% of young (18-44 years), and 40% of middle-aged (45-65 years) adults. Puerto Rican background subpopulation was characterized by a high prevalence of unhealthy habits: the highest proportion of current smokers (>30%) and obese (∼50%) individuals and those with diabetes (∼20%) but the lowest prevalence of healthy eaters (<20%). In contrast, the Dominican background subpopulation had one the healthiest lifestyles, with nearly 80% of neversmokers and only ∼30% of individuals having dis- or hypercholesterolemia. Mexican background subpopulation also ranked highly on healthy lifestyle: ∼70% followed a healthy diet and met 2008 physical activity guidelines standards, and had the highest proportion of normotensive (∼60%) but the lowest proportion of hypertensive persons (Table 1). Cuban background subpopulation had one of the most unhealthy profiles: the lowest proportion of persons who met physical activity standards (∼55%), but the highest proportions of those with discholesterolemia or hypercholesterolemia (∼50%), prediabetes or diabetes (∼55%), and hypertension (∼60%).

Among Hispanic/Latino background groups, the highest CVD prevalence was observed in Puerto Ricans (9.2% [95% CI: 7.7%-10.9%]). South Americans had one of the healthiest cardiovascular risk factor profiles, with the lowest proportions of persons with obesity (∼30%), diabetes (∼10%), hypertension (<40%), and CKD (Table 1). Age-adjusted CVD and CHD prevalence was nearly twice as high in the Puerto Rican than in the Mexican background category (P < 0.0001).

Socioeconomic characteristics of Hispanic/Latino subpopulations

The lowest proportion of U.S.-native individuals (<10%) was among South/Central American and Cuban background, whereas the highest was among Puerto Rican and Mexican background subgroups (Table 2). At least 50% of all participants lived in the United States ≥10 years. South American background subpopulation was highly educated; it included the greatest proportion (∼40%) of persons with college or university degrees and only ∼ 20% with less than a high school degree. In contrast, the Central American background subpopulation included ∼40% of individuals with less than a high school degree. The vast majority of South/Central American, Cuban, Dominican, and Mexican background subpopulations represented the 1st generation of immigrants. In contrast, more than 50% of Puerto Rican and mixed Hispanic/Latino background subgroups represented the 2nd generation of immigrants. At least 20% or more of Dominican and Mexican and 40% of Puerto Rican and mixed Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations used both English and Spanish languages equally at home. In contrast, more than 80% of Cuban and Central/South American background subpopulations used only Spanish or more Spanish than English language at home. More than half of the participants in nearly all subpopulations had a household income <$30,000, with the exception of mixed background Hispanic/Latino adults, who were more likely to have a higher income.

Comparison of GEH in Hispanic/Latino subpopulations

Unadjusted population means of GEH metrics are reported in Supplemental Table 1 and Figure 3. After adjustment for demographic characteristics, prevalent CVD, and cardiovascular risk factors, significant differences in GEH means among Hispanic/Latino subpopulations emerged (Table 3, Figure 4). The Dominican background subgroup had a narrower QRSTa, whereas Central American and Mexican subgroups had a wider QRSTa as compared to a total mean. SVG pointed farther posteriorly in Dominican and Puerto Rican, but farther anteriorly in Central/South American and Mexican and farther downward in Dominican, but farther upward in Mexican and Central American Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations compared to the total mean. Discussion

The main finding of this ancillary VCG study of the large prospective community-based epidemiological survey of 7 distinct Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations in the United States, the HCHS/SOL, was the observation of marked variations in GEH phenotype across Mexican, Cuban, Puerto Rican, Dominican, Central and South American, and mixed Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. The Hispanic/Latino population in the United States is not a monolith; it is distinguished by differences in acculturation, demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, behavioral traits and cultural habits, the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors and CVD, and VCG GEH manifestation. Notably, the differences in GEH manifested in the CVD-free population after controlling for well-known cardiovascular risk factors (age, sex, education, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyper- or dyslipidemia, obesity, level of physical activity, diet quality, and CKD), which implies unmeasured health disparities across ethnic subpopulations. Furthermore, we found evidence of the effect modification by the Hispanic/Latino background of an association of CHD with GEH, supporting the importance of personalized, ethnic subpopulation—specific cardiovascular prevention and medical care.

GEH and prevalence of CVD and cardiovascular risk factors among diverse Hispanic/Latino adults

The GEH (Figure 2) comprehensively describes 5 features of the SVG vector.10 The SVG vector points in the direction along which nonuniformity in excitation and repolarization is the most prominent. We quantify GEH by measuring 5 features of the SVG vector on orthogonal XYZ ECG beyond a well-known spatial QRSTa. Spatial QRSTa is a risk marker of ventricular tachyarrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, cardiovascular, and all-cause mortality.34,35 A comprehensive depiction of GEH achieved by measuring the SVGs’ 5 features carries additional predictive and prognostic value beyond QRS duration and morphology.11,13,18,23,32

Previously hidden disparities between Hispanic/Latino subgroups became recently recognized.4,36,37 Our findings of significant differences in VCG GEH across CVD-free Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations are consistent with previous studies reporting differences across the Hispanic/Latino background subgroups in the prevalence of healthy lifestyle,38 left ventricular hypertrophy and abnormal left ventricular remodeling,37 AF,8 major ECG abnormalities,7 an estimation of cardiovascular risk accuracy,36 acculturation,5 and CVD mortality.39 Underlying mechanisms of observed differences are likely multifactorial. Importantly, in our study, differences in GEH persisted after comprehensive adjustment for age, sex, education attainment, prevalent CVD, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyper- or dyslipidemia, obesity, CKD, level of physical activity, diet quality, and heart rate and rhythm. The VCG GEH is, in part, genetically determined, which can potentially contribute to the observed differences.40,41 Complex interactions between genetically determined differences in the electrophysiological substrate on one side and environmental exposures (acculturation, lifestyle, habits, socioeconomic characteristics, etc.) on the other side led to a mosaic presentation of GEH phenotype in ethnic subpopulations (Central Illustration). VCG GEH may be useful for personalized monitoring of CVD progression, which should be tested in future studies. The present study is an important milestone, as it reported reference values for GEH variables across the spectrum of Hispanic/Latino background categories.

Importantly, the Hispanic/Latino background modified an association of CHD with the GEH phenotype. Our HCHS/SOL findings are consistent with our previous results.12,42 A statistically significant interaction of ethnic background with CHD status after comprehensive adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors suggests that in different ethnicities, prevalent CHD manifests by different degrees of QRSTa widening and SVGmag reduction.

Furthermore, our study indirectly supports the “Hispanic resilience” hypothesis, suggesting that Hispanic/Latino individuals are especially resilient to harmful exposure to cardiovascular risk factors, in striking contrast to European/White (mixed, in this study) background populations. In a mixed/European background subpopulation, CVD (as compared to freedom from CVD) manifested by a much greater proportion of “abnormal” latent class III GEH. In contrast, in Central American, Mexican, Dominican, and Puerto Rican background subpopulations, CVD manifested by a greater proportion of “supernormal” latent class I.33 Future longitudinal studies are needed for appropriate interpretation of latent GEH profiles.

Strengths and limitations

The study's main strength is the prospectively designed stratified two-stage epidemiological survey providing a unique opportunity to compare the prevalence of critical socioeconomic characteristics, acculturation, and cardiovascular risk factors in 7 Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. However, while this study is the largest study of a diverse Hispanic/Latino population, the sample size of several subgroups was relatively small, and the study's negative findings must be interpreted with caution. Future studies are needed to understand the implication of the observed differences in GEH between CVD-free Hispanic/Latino background subgroups.

Conclusions

We observed significant differences in GEH phenotype between Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations, which were not explained by known cardiovascular risk factors. In CVD-free subpopulations, after controlling for age, sex, education attainment, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hypercholesterolemia/dyslipidemia, obesity, CKD, level of physical activity, diet quality, and heart rate and rhythm, substantial differences in GEH phenotype across ethnic subpopulations likely reflected health disparities, possibly due to complex relationships between genetic background and environmental exposures. Furthermore, we observed the effect modification by the Hispanic/Latino background of an association of CHD (but not CVD) with GEH.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Significant differences exist in VCG GEH phenotype across Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors do not explain ethnic background differences in GEH but reflect unmeasured health disparities. The prevalence of CHD is characterized by varying degrees of QRSTa widening and SVGmag reduction across different ethnic background subpopulations.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The VCG GEH phenotype reflects health disparities (beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors) across Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. The GEH is genetically determined, at least in part. Future studies are needed to untangle complex relationships between genetic background and environmental exposures in Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations. GEH can be easily and reproducibly measured on an inexpensive, noninvasive 12-lead ECG. Future studies are required to determine the usefulness of GEH for personalized monitoring of CVD progression in particular Hispanic/Latino background subpopulations.
