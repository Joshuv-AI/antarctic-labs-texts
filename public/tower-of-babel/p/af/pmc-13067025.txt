Identification and validation of a sphingolipid metabolism-related prognostic signature for predicting prognosis and immune microenvironment-related characteristics in ovarian cancer
Authors: Xin Lian, Hao Chang, Yun Yang, Yichen Guo, Lei Zhang, Xuemei Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian cancer (OV) is the leading cause of mortality among gynecological malignancies, often diagnosed at an advanced stage and prone to recurrence after treatment. In order to improve the prognosis, there is an urgent clinical need to identify novel strategies for early intervention and prognosis prediction. Sphingolipids are both important components of cell membranes and closely related to cell signaling. Key enzymes and intermediates of sphingolipid metabolism have critical roles in regulating biological processes such as proliferation and apoptosis of cancer cells, and some of the anticancer drugs targeting sphingolipid metabolism have already entered into clinical trials. However, the prognostic value of sphingolipid metabolism-related genes (SRGs) in OV remains unclear. This study aims to systematically evaluate the prognostic significance of SRGs in OV and construct a prognostic risk model to improve survival prediction.MethodsIn this study, we integrated transcriptomic profiles and corresponding clinical data of OV patients from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) databases. Through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, we identified five SRGs to construct a prognostic signature of OV. Based on the signature-derived risk scores, all samples were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. To further evaluate the signature’s clinical utility, we comprehensively assessed its associations with immune microenvironment, immunotherapy response and chemotherapy sensitivity. Finally, in vitro experiments were performed to validate the functional role of the key gene CERK in the model.ResultsPatients stratified according to risk scores exhibited statistically significant differences in survival outcomes. The robustness and predictive accuracy of this signature were consistently validated in both internal and external cohorts. Comprehensive analysis of the immune microenvironment and immunotherapy response revealed that patients in the low-risk group were more likely to derive clinical benefit from immunotherapy. Moreover, drug sensitivity analysis indicated that the low-risk group was more responsive to olaparib, whereas the high-risk group showed increased sensitivity to Topotecan. Critically, functional experiments demonstrated that CERK knockout in this model significantly suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion capacities in SKOV3 and ES-2 cell lines.ConclusionsWe developed a sphingolipid metabolism-related prognostic signature (SRPS), which demonstrated robust performance in predicting survival outcomes and guiding personalized therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Ovarian cancer (OV), sphingolipid metabolism, prognostic signature, immunotherapy response, CERK

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OV) ranks eighth in both incidence and mortality among female malignancies worldwide (1,2). Despite advances in biological studies of OV, patient prognosis remains poor, with age-standardized 5-year net survival rates ranging from 30% to 50% in most countries (2,3). This unfavorable outcome can be attributed primarily to two factors. First, due to the lack of precise diagnostic markers and the absence of significant clinical symptoms in the early stages, OV is often diagnosed at an advanced stage (4). Second, while cytoreductive surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy is currently the main treatment option for this disease, approximately 70% of patients relapse within 3 years, with progressively shorter treatment-free intervals culminating in platinum resistance (5). Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify effective and sensitive biomarkers relevant to the diagnosis, treatment and prognosis of OV.

Sphingolipids are essential structural and functional components of cell membranes, maintaining membrane integrity and fluidity (6). In addition, sphingolipids, as bioactive molecules, play important roles in tumorigenesis through modulating critical signaling pathways. Emerging evidence demonstrates their involvement in regulating multiple oncogenic processes, including growth, proliferation, migration, and invasion of cancer cells (7,8). Ceramide (Cer) and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) are the most extensively studied sphingolipids in cancer. Intriguingly, these two bioactive lipids exhibit diametrically opposed functions in tumor biology. Cer typically acts as a tumor suppressor by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting proliferation, whereas S1P promotes survival and proliferation of cancer cells (9). Under physiological conditions, sphingosine kinase (SK)—the enzyme responsible for S1P synthesis—maintains a dynamic balance between Cer and S1P levels, a regulatory mechanism often referred to as the “sphingolipid rheostat” (10). Dysregulation of this balance, particularly due to elevated SK expression, leads to increased S1P production and reduced Cer levels, contributing to uncontrolled cell proliferation and malignant transformation. Dysregulated SK isoforms have been implicated in multiple cancers. Specifically, SK1 is overexpressed in OV, breast cancer, renal cancer, gastric cancer, and gliomas (11), while SK2 has been found upregulated in colorectal cancer (6) and non-small cell lung cancer (12).

Currently, targeting metabolic pathways has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in oncology, with sphingolipid metabolism-related therapies demonstrating significant potential for improving clinical outcomes in cancer patients. For instance, Pitman et al. demonstrated that treatment with the anti-S1P antibody Sonepcizumab or the S1P receptor (S1PR) antagonist FTY720 significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in OV xenograft models, while administration of Cer liposomes (CL) effectively decreased peritoneal metastasis (13). Similarly, Muñoz-Guardiola et al. reported that ABTL0812, which elevated intracellular dihydroceramide levels, induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-mediated cytotoxic autophagy. This compound is currently undergoing Phase II clinical trials in patients with advanced endometrial cancer and squamous non-small cell lung cancer (14). In addition, previous studies showed that upregulation of glucosylceramide synthase led to cancer cells evading apoptosis and promoting drug efflux, thereby maintaining survival and proliferation, rendering chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin and cisplatin ineffective. Combining sphingolipid-targeted inhibitors with existing chemotherapy can synergistically enhance therapeutic efficacy (15). Despite these promising findings, the potential of sphingolipid metabolism as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker in OV remains incompletely characterized. Although accumulating evidence suggests dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism in OV, most studies have focused on individual metabolites or isolated enzymatic steps, lacking comprehensive profiling of the entire sphingolipid metabolic network. Targeting sphingolipid metabolism has demonstrated therapeutic potential in preclinical studies, and several agents modulating this pathway have advanced into clinical trials (16). Notably, tamoxifen, a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved standard-of-care agent for breast cancer with a mechanism that includes inhibition of sphingolipid metabolism, has also shown significant efficacy in OV models (16). Nevertheless, no therapy specifically engineered to precisely modulate key regulatory components of the sphingolipid metabolism pathway has yet received clinical approval for OV. Therefore, elucidating the molecular intricacies of this pathway and developing precision-targeted therapeutics hold significant promise for advancing the treatment of OV and overcoming current therapeutic limitations.

Immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) enhance endogenous antitumor immunity, offering potent tumoricidal efficacy, high specificity, and few side effects (17). ICIs have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy both as monotherapies and in combination with other cancer therapies (18,19). Accumulating evidence underscores the critical role of sphingolipid metabolism in immunoregulation. For instance, Liu et al. demonstrated that increased expression of S1PR1 in bladder cancer cells led to an accumulation of tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby suppressing antitumor immunity (20). Conversely, C2-Cer promotes polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward the M1 phenotype by upregulating protein kinase C zeta (PKCζ). This shift increases the proportion of activated CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME), enhances perforin and granzyme B levels, and boosts antitumor immune responses (21). Given the intricate interplay between sphingolipid metabolism and the immune system, constructing a sphingolipid metabolism-related prognostic signature (SRPS) to assess immunotherapeutic sensitivity could facilitate the precise selection of patients and improve clinical outcomes.

In this study, we performed comprehensive bioinformatics analyses utilizing data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) databases to explore the expression patterns, biological roles, and genetic alterations of sphingolipid metabolism-related genes (SRGs) in OV. Ultimately, we developed a novel SRPS based on five genes, providing a theoretical framework for predicting survival outcomes, guiding immunotherapeutic strategies, and informing targeted therapy development. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and TRIPOD reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2260/rc).

Methods

Data collection and preprocessing

We obtained RNA-seq data, mutation profiles, and clinical information for the TCGA-OV cohort from the TCGA database. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) for model construction and a validation set (20%) to evaluate model stability and predictive accuracy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. For independent external validation, we downloaded transcriptomic profiles and clinical data from the GSE140082 cohort in the GEO database (22). Samples lacking survival information or RNA-seq data in both TCGA and GEO databases were excluded. Normal ovarian tissue RNA-seq data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx; https://www.gtexportal.org/home/datasets) database served as controls. Batch effects were corrected using the “ComBat” function of the “sva” package in R (23).

Acquisition of SRGs

A comprehensive list of SRGs was obtained from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) database. Through stringent filtering, we identified 1,373 SRGs for subsequent analysis (see table online: https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2025-aw-2260-1.xlsx).

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

DEGs between OV tissues and normal ovarian tissues are likely to play critical roles in disease initiation and progression. To identify these DEGs, we conducted comparative analyses using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA; http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) database, which integrates transcriptomic data from TCGA tumor samples and GTEx normal controls. Genes with a |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥1 and a corrected P value ≤0.01 were defined as DEGs (see table online: https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2025-aw-2260-2.xlsx). A volcano plot was generated using the “ggplot” function of the “ggplot2” R package to visualize the distribution of DEGs. Intersection analysis between DEGs and SRGs yielded 445 differentially expressed SRGs (see table online: https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2025-aw-2260-3.xlsx). Subsequent univariate Cox regression in the TCGA-OV cohort identified 28 SRGs significantly associated with overall survival (OS; P value <0.05). To evaluate potential functional interactions among these 28 survival-associated SRGs, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING (https://string-db.org/) database (24).

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) analysis

We downloaded somatic mutation data for OV patients from the TCGA database and used the R package “maftools” to generate mutation waterfall plots for high- and low-risk groups (25).

Unsupervised cluster analysis of DEGs

Using the 28 prognosis-associated SRGs identified above, we performed unsupervised clustering to stratify patients into distinct subgroups (Appendix 1). An unsupervised cluster analysis was implemented using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package (26), with the following key parameters: the max cluster number (maxK) =10, repetitions (reps) =1,000, proportion of items to sample (pItem) =0.8, proportion of features to sample (pFeature) =1.0, cluster algorithm (clusterAlg) = k-means, and distance = euclidean. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF), and relative change in area under the CDF curve. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis was performed using the “survival” and “survminer” R packages to compare survival outcomes across the identified clusters.

Functional enrichment analysis

To explore the biological functions of sphingolipid metabolism, we analyzed the expression of DEGs among different clusters using the R package “limma”, with a selection criterion of |log2 FC| ≥1 and P value <0.05. These DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the R package “clusterProfiler”, with P value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Construction and validation of SRPS

After excluding samples lacking clinical or gene expression information from the TCGA database, 398 OV patients were included and randomly divided into training (80%) and validation (20%) cohorts. To minimize model overfitting, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was conducted on the 28 SRGs identified through univariate Cox analysis using the R package “glmnet”. The optimal penalty parameter lambda (λ) was selected through 10-fold cross-validation, yielding 11 genes (27). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed, and according to the minimal Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), five genes and their corresponding regression coefficients (Coei) were ultimately determined for constructing the optimal SRPS. The risk score for each patient was calculated based on the normalized expression (Expi) of these genes and their Coei. The formula for calculating risk score is as follows: Risk score=∑i=1N(Expi×Coei). Patients in the training cohort were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score for survival assessment. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using the R package “survivalROC” to evaluate predictive accuracy. Internal validation was carried out using the TCGA validation cohort, while external validation was achieved by utilizing the GSE140082 cohort to ensure the robustness of our model.

Nomogram development and validation

The R package “rms” was utilized to integrate clinical indicators with risk scores for the construction of a nomogram (28). The clinical utility of the nomogram was assessed through ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Immune microenvironment analysis

We calculated immune scores, stromal scores, ESTIMATE scores, and tumor purity for OV samples using the R package “ESTIMATE” (29). Based on previous research (30), we identified gene sets marking 16 immune cell types and 13 immune-related molecules. Using the single sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm in the R package “GSVA”, we obtained enrichment scores for immune cells and molecules in each sample. We employed seven algorithms (TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCP-COUNTER, XCELL, and EPIC) through the R package “IOBR” to estimate immune cell infiltration levels in OV samples (31). Furthermore, we processed the transcriptomic profiles of the TCGA-OV cohort using the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE; http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) platform to derive TIDE, Dysfunction, and Exclusion scores (32).

Immunotherapy response prediction

The immunophenoscore (IPS) from the Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA; https://tcia.at/) was utilized to predict the immunotherapeutic responses (anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA4) in high- and low-risk groups (33).

Drug sensitivity prediction

The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) database provided drug sensitivity information for cancer cell lines (34). We employed the R package “oncoPredict” to estimate drug sensitivity for OV patients in the TCGA database (35), using the “calcPhenotype” function to determine sensitivity scores for each patient.

Cell culture

This study utilized the human normal ovarian epithelial cell line IOSE-80 (cat. no. AW-CH0170, AnWei-sci, Shanghai, China) and the human OV cell lines SKOV3 (cat. no. SCSP-5214, National Collection ofAuthenticated Cell Cultures, Shanghai, China) and ES-2 (cat. no. SCTT1074, Wuhan Zishan Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in either 5A medium (cat. no. KGL1702-500, KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China) or RPMI-1640 medium (cat. no. KGL1503-500, KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (cat. no. A5256701, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (cat. no. PC0315, ProteinBio, Nanjing, China). All cultures were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection

CERK-targeting siRNAs and the negative control siRNA were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfection was performed using the siRNA-mate plus transfection kit (cat. no. A00001-A05060, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol when OV cells in 6-well plates reached 60% confluence. Transfection efficiency was validated by real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis. The siRNA sequences for CERK were designed based on the human CERK mRNA sequence (NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_022766.6). The siRNA sequences targeting CERK (Homo sapiens) are listed in Table S1.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (cat. no. 15596018CN, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed with HisyGo qRT Red Super Mix (cat. no. RT101-01-AC, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was carried out using ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (cat. no. Q312-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on an ABI StepOnePlus system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with GAPDH serving as the internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primers were designed based on NCBI Reference Sequence NM_022766.6 (CERK) and NM_001256799.3 (GAPDH, housekeeping gene), synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and are provided in Table S1. All RT-qPCR experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates, with three technical replicates per condition in each experiment.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA lysis buffer (cat. no. G2002, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (cat. no. 78425, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The cell lysates were mixed with 5×SDS-PAGE protein loading buffer (cat. no. P0015L, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and then heated at 95 ℃ for 5 min to denature the proteins. A total of 20 µg of protein sample per lane was loaded onto an 8% SDS-PAGE gel (cat. no. E302-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) for electrophoretic separation. Protein samples were electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (cat. no. IPVH00010, Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) after separation by PAGE gel electrophoresis. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies diluted in primary antibody dilution buffer (cat. no. P0023A, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After washing three times with TBST, the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 2 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody diluted in secondary antibody dilution buffer (cat. no. P0023D, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Finally, protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (cat. no. 36208ES76, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and imaged with the Tanon-4600 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China) and the ChemiDocMP imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The following antibodies were used: CERK polyclonal antibody (cat. no. 25731-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) (900 µg/mL, diluted 1:1,000), Recombinant Anti-beta Actin antibody (HRP Conjugated) (cat. no. ZB15001-HRP, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) (diluted 1:1,000, used as loading control), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (cat. no. GB23303, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) (diluted 1:10,000). All Western blot experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chambers (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA). For invasion assays, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (cat. no. 356234, Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA). SKOV3 and ES-2 cells were seeded into the upper chambers at densities of 6×104 and 3×104 cells/well, respectively. Following 48-hour incubation at 37 ℃, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. Migrated or invaded cells were quantified by counting cells in five randomly selected fields per chamber under an inverted light microscope. Transwell assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, with three technical replicates per condition in each experiment.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2×103 cells/well. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (cat. no. G4103, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 ℃ in the dark for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Synergy H4 microplate reader (Bio-Tek, Winooski, VT, USA). Cell proliferation assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, with six technical replicates per condition in each experiment.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well tissue culture plates at a density of 800 cells/well and incubated at 37 ℃ for 14 days. Following incubation, the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. The number of colonies formed was manually counted. Colony formation assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, with three technical replicates per condition in each experiment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R software (v 4.2.2). In the bioinformatics analysis section, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for comparisons among three or more groups. Additionally, survival differences among groups were examined via Kaplan-Meier analysis. In the experimental validation section, all comparisons involved three groups. Depending on the data distribution and sample size, if the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were satisfied, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Detailed methods for Figures S1-S3 are provided in Appendix 1.

Results

Identification of dysregulated SRGs in OV

Figure 1 illustrates the study workflow. Through an in-depth exploration of the GeneCards database, we identified 1,373 SRGs as candidates. Principal component analysis (PCA) based on these genes successfully discriminated OV tissues from normal ovarian tissues, suggesting a pivotal role of sphingolipid metabolism in the development and progression of OV (Figure 2A,2B). To comprehensively investigate the impact of sphingolipid metabolism on OV, we compared OV tissues from the TCGA-OV cohort with normal ovarian tissues from the GTEx database, identifying 7,641 significantly dysregulated genes (|log2FC| ≥1 and corrected P value ≤0.01) (Figure 2C). By intersecting the 1,373 SRGs with these 7,641 dysregulated genes, we identified 445 SRGs that showed significant differential expression. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on these differentially expressed SRGs within the TCGA-OV cohort, initially screening out 28 candidate genes significantly associated with OS (P value <0.05) (Figure 2D and Table 1). The mutation landscape of these 28 SRGs in OV tissues is shown in Figure 2E, with the top 5 most frequently mutated genes being RYR1, LRP1, EGFR, B4GALT5, and LPIN3. Additionally, copy number variation (CNV) analysis of these 28 SRGs revealed notable amplification trends in B4GALT5, CDKN1B, LPIN3, LSR, and RYR1, while CERK, POLDIP3, PPARA, SBF1, and SREBF1 displayed significant deletion tendencies (Figure 2F). Chromosomal mapping of CNV loci is detailed in Figure 2G. PPI network analysis revealed functional connectivity among the 28 SRGs (Figure 2H), with correlation analysis confirming interaction robustness (Figure 2I).

Identification of sphingolipid clusters in OV and their correlation with biological functions

Based on the 28 prognosis-related SRGs, we employed an unsupervised clustering algorithm to classify OV samples (Figure 3A and Figure S1A,S1B). The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating CDF and relative change in area under the CDF curve (Figure S1C,S1D), resulting in three clusters: Cluster A (n=132), Cluster B (n=127), and Cluster C (n=139) among the 398 OV samples. PCA confirmed distinct heterogeneity among clusters (Figure S1E). K-M survival analysis revealed significant differences in OS across the three clusters (P value <0.05), with Clusters B and C demonstrating superior prognosis compared to Cluster A, indicating a close association between sphingolipid metabolism and patient outcomes (Figure 3B). A heatmap illustrating the age, stage, grade, clinical outcome, and SRG expression across the three clusters highlighted significant heterogeneity in clinical characteristics and gene expression profiles (Figure 3C). Box plots further depicted differential SRG expression among the clusters (Figure S1F), with Cluster A exhibiting elevated risk-gene expression and Clusters B/C showing higher protective-gene expression, aligning with their prognostic differences. To elucidate biological mechanisms underlying cluster heterogeneity, we identified common DEGs across all clusters (Figure S1G) and performed GO annotation, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and GSEA (Figure 3D-3F). Through integrated GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, we identified four significantly overlapping biological processes between the two datasets. Notably, actin cytoskeleton organization and its regulation, as well as cell adhesion, are closely associated with malignant behaviors including tumor migration and invasion (36). Concurrently, endocytosis and lysosome-related activities have been implicated in immune evasion, immunotherapy resistance, and chemoresistance (37,38). These findings collectively suggest that SRGs may function as multifunctional nodal molecules, engaging in crosstalk with other signaling pathways to collectively shape the invasive phenotype and therapeutic resistance in OV. GSEA identified several oncogenic pathways enriched in Cluster A, reinforcing the finding that Cluster A has the poorest prognosis.

Construction of SRPS

To construct the SRPS, we randomly divided 398 patients from the TCGA-OV cohort into a training set (n=318) and a validation set (n=80) at an 8:2 ratio. Initially, LASSO regression analysis was performed on the 28 prognosis-related SRGs in the training set, narrowing down to 11 key genes (Figure 4A,4B). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis identified five independent prognostic factors for constructing the optimal SRPS (Figure 4C), including four risk genes and one protective gene. K-M survival analysis confirmed that high expression of risk genes (UNC5C, CERK, CDKN1B, TRPM2) was significantly associated with shorter OS, while high expression of the protective gene (LPAR3) predicted better outcomes (Figure 4D-4H).

Validation of SRPS in internal and external cohorts

To evaluate the generalizability of the SRPS constructed based on the training set, we first conducted internal validation using the validation set of the TCGA-OV cohort and further validated externally using the GSE140082 cohort. In both validations, patient risk scores were calculated according to the aforementioned formula. The results showed that high-risk group patients had significantly shorter OS in both the internal (Figure 5D) and external validation set (Figure 5E), with moderate but consistent predictive performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival (Figure 5F,5G). In the external validation cohort, optimal cutoff analysis based on the maximum Youden index further evaluated the model’s discriminative performance. For 1-year survival prediction, sensitivity was 64.1% and specificity was 53.8% (Youden index =0.179); for 3-year survival prediction, sensitivity was 63.2% and specificity was 63.5% (Youden index =0.267); and for 5-year survival prediction, sensitivity was 78.7% and specificity was 52.7% (Youden index =0.314) (Figure S2A). Notably, the Youden index showed a monotonically increasing trend with increasing prediction time horizons. This pattern not only indicates a robust association between the model features and disease prognosis but also suggests a possible time-dependent cumulative effect—namely, that the influence of the relevant biological features on long-term survival outcomes may become more pronounced as time progresses. Risk factor triptychs in the internal (Figure 5H) and external validation set (Figure 5I) exhibited consistent trends with the training set, demonstrating the robust prognostic stratification capability of SRPS across different data sources and platforms.

Development and evaluation of nomogram

To enhance the accuracy of OS prediction for OV patients, we integrated age, grade, stage, and risk score in the TCGA-OV training set, and constructed a prognostic nomogram based on the “rms” R package (Figure 6A). We then comprehensively evaluated this nomogram in both the TCGA-OV cohort and the GSE140082 cohort, and used calibration curves, ROC curves, and DCA to assess its accuracy, predictive power, and clinical utility. For the TCGA-OV cohort, the calibration curve demonstrated excellent agreement between the nomogram’s predicted probabilities and actual observations for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Figure 6B). ROC analysis further confirmed its predictive performance, and the nomogram’s AUC values in the TCGA cohort were 0.701, 0.692, and 0.666 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively, which were significantly higher than those of individual variable models (Figure 6C). Moreover, DCA showed that the nomogram provided greater net clinical benefit compared with any single clinical factor or the risk score alone, indicating superior clinical utility (Figure 6D). In the GSE140082 cohort, due to limitations in data availability, we could not obtain 5-year clinical follow-up information for patients, but the calibration curve likewise showed good agreement between the nomogram’s predicted probabilities and actual observations for 1- and 3-year OS (Figure 6E). ROC analysis yielded AUC values of 0.625 and 0.663 for 1- and 3-year OS, respectively (Figure 6F). DCA similarly demonstrated that the nomogram yielded higher net benefit than individual clinical factors or the risk score alone, suggesting favorable clinical applicability in this validation set (Figure 6G). In the GSE140082 cohort, to further evaluate the discriminative ability of the nomogram model, we determined the optimal cutoff value based on the Youden index. The results showed that the sensitivities at both 1 and 3 years exceeded 80.0% (80.0% and 80.9%, respectively), representing an improvement of over 15 percentage points compared to the prognostic model and significantly reducing the risk of missed diagnosis, while the specificities were 47.8% and 48.9%, respectively (Figure S2B). This pattern of high sensitivity and low specificity is reasonable in clinical decision-making for severe prognostic events, as it prioritizes avoiding false negatives to ensure timely intervention for high-risk patients. The Youden index (0.278 at 1 year and 0.298 at 3 years) also improved compared to previous results, indicating enhanced overall discriminative ability of the nomogram.

Association between SRPS and the immune microenvironment

To further investigate the relationship between the SRPS-based risk score and the immune microenvironment in OV samples, we compared multiple immune-related features between the high- and low-risk groups. First, we employed seven algorithms—TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCP-COUNTER, XCELL, and EPIC—to analyze immune cell infiltration in the TCGA-OV cohort. A heatmap illustrated the infiltration levels of various immune cells across different algorithms (Figure S3A), and Pearson correlation analysis was then applied to assess the association between the risk score and immune cell abundance (Figure S3B), revealing significant differences in immune cell composition between the two groups. Building on these preliminary findings, we further evaluated the immune score, stromal score, estimate score, and tumor purity in the high- and low-risk groups using the ESTIMATE algorithm. Additionally, ssGSEA was applied to assess the enrichment levels of 29 immune-related signatures, including 16 immune cell types and 13 immune-related molecules. The overall distribution of these features is summarized in Figure 7A. Specifically, the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher immune, stromal, and estimate scores but lower tumor purity than the low-risk group (Figure 7B,7C). These findings suggest that the high-risk group contains fewer cancer cells but a higher proportion of infiltrating immune and stromal cells. Further analysis demonstrated that the high-risk group had elevated infiltration levels of multiple immune cells, including B cells, CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages (Figure 7D). Moreover, most immune-related molecules showed positive correlations with risk scores (Figure 7E), indicating that SRPS effectively predicts immune microenvironment characteristics in OV tissues.

Immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity analysis based on SRPS

Given the critical role of immune checkpoints in immunotherapy efficacy, we compared the expression of key immune checkpoint genes between high- and low-risk groups. Significant differences were observed in the expression of CTLA4, PDCD1, and other immune checkpoint genes between the two groups (Figure 7F). Since CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibitors are two major classes of ICIs, we predicted response to these therapies using the IPS from the TCIA database. The low-risk group exhibited higher sensitivity to anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1, and combination therapies (Figure 7G), suggesting a greater likelihood of clinical benefit from ICIs. Based on the aforementioned findings (Figure 7B-7E), the high-risk group exhibited significantly elevated immune scores, greater enrichment levels of immune cells, and enhanced activity of immune-related functions or pathways compared to the low-risk group, suggesting an immune microenvironment characteristic of a “hot tumor” (39). However, the IPS indicated a poorer response to immunotherapy in the high-risk group (Figure 7G). Hegde et al. have classified T-cell infiltration into “effector-inflamed” and “dysfunctional-inflamed” subtypes (40). We therefore proposed that the high-risk group likely corresponded to the latter, wherein despite high levels of T-cell infiltration, these cells were functionally impaired due to exhaustion. To reconcile this apparent paradox and validate our hypothesis, we compared TIDE, Dysfunction, and Exclusion scores between the risk groups. The results revealed higher TIDE scores in the high-risk group, indicative of reduced immunotherapy sensitivity, which was consistent with the IPS findings (Figure 7H). Furthermore, both Dysfunction and Exclusion scores were significantly elevated in the high-risk group (Figure 7H), implying that even within a “hot tumor” context with substantial immune infiltration, the immune cells were either functionally defective or physically excluded to the tumor periphery, thereby promoting immune evasion. In contrast, the low-risk group, despite potentially lower overall immune infiltration, may harbor more functionally competent immune cells, rendering it more likely to benefit from immunotherapy.

Given the importance of pharmacotherapy in OV management, we investigated whether SRPS could guide personalized treatment strategies. Using the GDSC database, we predicted the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 198 anticancer drugs and observed significant heterogeneity in drug sensitivity between risk groups. Among FDA-approved drugs for treating OV, the high-risk group was more sensitive to topotecan (Figure 8A), whereas the low-risk group was more sensitive to olaparib (Figure 8B). Among the 10 drugs with the most significant sensitivity differences, the low-risk group showed greater sensitivity to WEHI-539, ABT737, navitoclax, AZD4547, pevonedistat, and axitinib, while the high-risk group was more sensitive to PD0325901, ERK_6604, AZD5153, and MN-64 (Figure 8C-8L). Although these drugs have not yet been approved by FDA for OV treatment, their clinical potential is under investigation. Using the DrugBank (https://www.drugbank.ca) database, we identified the target genes for these therapies (Table S2) and found significant differences in the expression of most target genes between high- and low-risk groups (Figure 8M). These findings highlight the potential of integrating SRPS with pharmacogenomic data to optimize precision medicine in OV.

Experimental validation of CERK

Among the five core genes included in our prognostic model, CERK exhibited the highest hazard ratio (HR) [HR =1.210, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.037–1.412, P=0.02] in univariate Cox regression analysis (Figure 2D). This indicated that elevated CERK expression was most strongly associated with poor prognosis in OV patients. Furthermore, CERK, localizes to a critical branch point of the sphingolipid metabolic network. It facilitates a pivotal signaling switch by Cer to ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P), thereby transducing a pro-apoptotic signal into pro-survival and pro-migratory outputs (41,42). Therefore, given its prominent prognostic value and central role in sphingolipid metabolism, we prioritized CERK as the primary target for functional validation. Consistent with its proposed oncogenic role, CERK expression was significantly elevated in OV cell lines (e.g., ES-2, SKOV3) compared to the normal ovarian epithelial cell line IOSE-80 (Figure 9A). To investigate the role of CERK in OV, we performed gene knockdown of CERK in the SKOV3 and ES-2 cell lines, which resulted in significant reductions of CERK at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure 9B,9C). In CCK-8 cell proliferation assays and colony formation experiments, we observed that the proliferative capacity of SKOV3 cells with CERK knockdown was significantly reduced compared to the control group, with similar results seen in ES-2 cells (Figure 9D,9E). In transwell assays, the migration and invasion abilities of SKOV3 and ES-2 cells were markedly decreased following CERK knockdown (Figure 9F,9G). These findings indicate that CERK can promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of OV cell lines, thereby facilitating cancer progression. Our previous analyses have shown that patients with high CERK expression have poor prognosis, serving as an adverse factor for survival (Figure 4F). Our experimental results confirm the accuracy of our preceding bioinformatics research.

Discussion

OV represents the most lethal gynecologic malignancy, posing a significant threat to women’s health globally (43). Approximately 75% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages with metastatic disease (16); the 5-year survival rate remains at only 10–40% (44). Although cytoreductive surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy remains the gold standard of treatment, the majority of patients experience recurrence and ultimately succumb to chemoresistance (4). While emerging therapies such as poly adenosine diphosphate ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors (e.g., olaparib) and anti-angiogenic agents (e.g., bevacizumab) have shown promise in improving progression-free survival (PFS) (4), OS has not significantly improved over the past decade (13).

Sphingolipids, a critical class of lipids, include key bioactive molecules such as sphingosine (Sph), Cer, S1P, sphingomyelin (SM), and glycosphingolipids (GSL). These molecules play essential roles in regulating diverse biological processes, including organelle function, membrane homeostasis, cell-cell interactions, cell fate determination (45). Notably, OV tissues exhibit marked upregulation of SK1, S1P, and S1PR1-5 alongside depleted Cer levels compared to normal tissues (13,16). Zhu et al. revealed that elevated levels of SK1 are associated with platinum/paclitaxel resistance in OV (46). Huang et al. demonstrated that decreased levels of Cer were correlated with paclitaxel insensitivity in OV (47). These findings suggest that dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism serves as a pivotal driver of OV progression and therapeutic failure. Currently, various risk signatures have been established to investigate the prognostic value of metabolism-related genes in OV. However, the role of SRGs remains largely unexplored. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of SRGs is urgently needed to assess their prognostic value and potential impact on treatment sensitivity in OV.

In this study, we identified 445 differentially expressed SRGs using the GEPIA database. Through univariate Cox regression, LASSO regression, and multivariate Cox regression analysis, we selected five key genes to construct a prognostic signature. Applying this signature to the training set of the TCGA-OV cohort, we stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups. Survival analysis revealed significantly better outcomes in the low-risk group. Similar results were observed in the validation set of the TCGA-OV cohort and the GSE140082 cohort, demonstrating the robustness of the signature. Time-dependent ROC analysis further confirmed its predictive accuracy. To enable individualized risk prediction and guide personalized therapeutic strategies, we integrated the risk score with clinical parameters (age, grade, and stage) to develop a nomogram. The clinical utility of the nomogram was evaluated using ROC curves and DCA, which collectively demonstrated superior predictive performance and clinical applicability compared to individual clinical factors alone.

This study identified five prognostic signature genes, all of which have been previously implicated in the progression of various cancers. Lysophosphatidic acid receptor 3 (LPAR3), a G protein-coupled receptor that primarily binds lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), participates in multiple cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and migration (48). In OV, LPAR3 has been identified as the only differentially expressed member among the six LPA receptors and promotes tumor growth and metastasis through the G protein alpha inhibitory subunit (Gαi)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway (48). The second member of the TRPM ion channel subfamily (TRPM2) mediates Ca2+ influx and plays critical roles in oxidative stress, autophagy, and DNA repair (49). Lin et al. reported that TRPM2 enhances pancreatic cancer cell migration and invasion by regulating the Protein kinase C (PKC)/MAPK Kinase (MEK) signaling pathway (50). Cer Kinase (CERK) is a lipid kinase that phosphorylates Cer to generate C1P. Vu et al. demonstrated that in KRAS-mutant non-small cell lung cancer, CERK promoted mitochondrial translocation of Protein Kinase B (AKT), which enhanced voltage-dependent anion-selective channel (VDAC) phosphorylation to mitigate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby sustaining tumor cell viability (51). Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1B (CDKN1B) is a member of the Cip/Kip family of cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors (52). p27kip1 encoded by CDKN1B is closely associated with cell growth and differentiation, cell cycle regulation, and cell motility (53). Mutations in CDKN1B are frequently observed in breast cancer, prostate cancer, thyroid cancer, and neuroendocrine tumors (53). Viotto et al. found that in luminal breast cancer, CDKN1B mutations and CNVs were associated with tumor aggressiveness (54). Uncoordinated 5 Homolog C (UNC5C), a member of the UNC5H family of transmembrane receptors, functions in a netrin-1-dependent manner. In the presence of netrin-1, UNC5C promotes proliferation and metastasis, while inducing apoptosis in its absence (55). Yuan et al. showed that suppressing the expression of UNC5C enhanced netrin-1/integrin α6/β4 signaling and thereby promoted tumor growth and metastasis in breast cancer (56). Although these genes have been extensively studied individually in cancer, integrated analysis of multiple genes may offer more comprehensive and clinically relevant prognostic information. For example, Zheng et al. developed a mitochondrial gene-based prognostic model for OV (57), and Xiong et al. established a histone modification-related prognostic signature (58). In this study, we constructed the first SRPS for OV.

To explore the biological roles of sphingolipid metabolism in OV, we classified patients into three clusters using unsupervised clustering based on the 28 prognosis-associated SRGs. Functional enrichment analyses of common DEGs across the three clusters revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to actin cytoskeleton organization, cell adhesion, endocytosis, and lysosomal activity, as shown by GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. These findings suggest that SRGs may influence OV prognosis through modulating tumor metastasis, immune landscape, and therapeutic sensitivity.

OV has long been considered an immunogenic tumor, with the “immunoreactive” molecular subtype being associated with favorable clinical outcomes, suggesting that some OV patients may benefit from immunotherapy (59). However, no reliable biomarkers currently exist for accurate patient stratification and treatment selection. Emerging evidence indicates that sphingolipid metabolism regulates immune cell differentiation and function, playing a vital role in reshaping the immune microenvironment and influencing tumor immunotherapy responses (60,61). In our study, bioinformatics analysis revealed significant differences in immune cell infiltration and immune-related molecule expression between high- and low-risk groups, further supporting the regulatory impact of the SRPS on the immune microenvironment. Significantly, based on bioinformatics prediction, the low-risk group exhibited greater sensitivity to CTLA4positive/PD-1positive treatments, suggesting that SRPS can effectively identify immunotherapy-responsive individuals. Beyond immunotherapy, we evaluated the predictive value of SRPS for responses to 198 anticancer agents. Drug sensitivity analysis based on large-scale pharmacogenomic datasets revealed that the low-risk group exhibited higher sensitivity to olaparib, WEHI-539, ABT-737, navitoclax, AZD4547, pevonedistat, and axitinib, whereas the high-risk group was more responsive to Topotecan, PD0325901, ERK_6604, AZD5153, and MN-64. These findings collectively demonstrate that SRPS can guide personalized treatment strategies for OV patients.

CERK, a critical gene in our prognostic model, demonstrates a strong association with poor OV outcomes in survival analysis. Shi et al. reported that CERK promotes the conversion of Cer to C1P in prostate cancer, thereby enhancing tumor cell proliferation (62). Similarly, Bhadwal et al. identified CERK as a potential biomarker for metastasis and drug resistance in breast cancer (63). In our functional assays, CERK was shown to significantly promote OV cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, further validating the clinical relevance of this gene and supporting the potential utility of our prognostic signature.

The SRPS that we developed exhibits moderate but consistent discriminative performance and robustness across training, internal, and external validation cohorts. When integrated with clinical variables in a nomogram, the model’s predictive performance is further improved. Thus, the SRPS and the biological pathways it highlights provide a crucial theoretical foundation for identifying novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets in OV. While our study highlights the potential clinical utility of SRPS, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the prognostic model was constructed and validated using retrospective data from public databases, lacking prospective research to fully assess its clinical applicability. This limitation may affect the generalizability and predictive accuracy of the model. Second, our findings are based on bioinformatics analyses and require further experimental validation to refine the model and confirm its biological mechanisms. Future investigations should integrate large-scale prospective cohort studies, mechanistic experiments, and clinical validation to overcome these limitations and facilitate clinical translation.

Conclusions

Using data from the TCGA and GEO databases, we systematically investigated SRGs associated with OV and developed a novel prognostic model. This model has been rigorously validated as a reliable tool for predicting both patient survival and treatment sensitivity in OV. Furthermore, we experimentally validated the functional role of CERK, a key gene in this model, in OV pathogenesis. In summary, our integrated bioinformatics and experimental approach not only establishes a robust prognostic model for OV but also functionally validates CERK as a key molecular driver.
