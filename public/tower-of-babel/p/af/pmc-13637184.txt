Mechano-reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages by engineering nanogel stiffness for suppression of tumor development
Authors: Haochen Lin, Zheng Li, Ao Hua, Sijing Wu, Qingfu Zhao, Shiyou Li, Shuya Zhang, Xing Wang, Jiayuan Li, Qingyuan Deng, Ze-yang Ding, Bi-xiang Zhang, Sergey M. Deyev, Xiangliang Yang, Zifu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanoparticle mechanical properties, as critical factors in tumor targeting drug delivery, have recently been revealed to regulate cellular biological functions. While macroscopic mechanical stimuli are demonstrated to reprogram tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), it is unclear how TAMs respond to microscopic stimuli from nanoparticles. Herein, we demonstrated that the stiff 15%NGs presented stronger interactions with integrin than the soft 2%NGs, promoting the polymerization of actin and the activation of yes-associated protein 1. Therefore, the pro-inflammatory phenotype of TAMs was enhanced and the secretion of tumor necrosis factor α was increased. In synergy with high liver enrichment, 15%NGs exhibited superior tumor suppression by reprogramming TAMs in orthotopic liver tumors. Crucially, the pro-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages reprogrammed by 15%NGs significantly induced the apoptosis of tumor cells in ex vivo hepatocellular carcinoma tissues from patients. This study proposes a mechanical signaling pathway to reprogram TAMs by manipulating nanogel stiffness and provides new insights for rational design of cancer nanomedicines.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04793-5.

Introduction

Nanomedicine, which is widely utilized in cancer therapies, typically consists of therapeutic agents for antitumor effect and nanoparticles as their carriers for tumor targeting delivery. Although the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, including size, shape, surface chemistry, etc., are known to significantly influence the efficiency of tumor targeting drug delivery, such properties endow nanoparticles with specific biological functions and affect antitumor efficacy [1–5]. Recent studies have revealed that the mechanical properties, as intrinsic attributes of nanoparticles, markedly modulate the processes of tumor accumulation and cellular internalization [6, 7]. Furthermore, the critical role of mechanical signals has been demonstrated in various biological processes and effects, such as the regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation [8, 9]. Consequently, exploring whether nanoparticles can modulate the cellular functions of tumor tissues via their mechanical properties, and in turn, augment antitumor efficacy, holds significant clinical value for the design of cancer nanomedicines.

It is widely recognized that the tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) with anti-inflammatory and protumor phenotypes, which are non-negligible elements of the suppressive tumor immune microenvironment, induce both immune evasion and tumor progression [10–12]. Therefore, reprogramming anti-inflammatory TAMs to pro-inflammatory phenotypes is considered a promising immunotherapy strategy to overcome immunosuppression and inhibit tumor growth [11, 13, 14]. To this end, many approaches have been pursued, among which the mechanical stimuli have been explored as a promising method, given the fact that macrophages can be reprogrammed by the stiffness of substrate or tumor extracellular matrix [15–18]. Moreover, our previous study revealed that regulating the stiffness of nanogels could decrease the transcriptional level of clathrin and suppress endocytotic capacity of RAW264.7 macrophages [2]. As for the immunologic function of macrophages, the impact of nanoparticle mechanical properties on macrophage reprogramming still lacks investigation.

It is indistinct how macrophages achieve different biological functions through sensing and responding to nanoparticle mechanical properties. The macrophage membrane is interspersed with a large number of mechanosensitive proteins such as integrins, Piezo1, etc., which can recognize external mechanical stimuli to stimulate or deactivate downstream signaling pathways [19–21]. Additionally, yes-associated protein (YAP), as one of the representative intracellular mechano-signaling molecules, can subsequently convert mechanical signals into biochemical signals and regulate biological functions of macrophages [16, 22–24]. It has been reported that macrophages can be reprogrammed by macroscopic mechanical stimuli like matrix stiffness and stretch force, via various signaling pathways, including CD11b, Piezo1, YAP, and TAZ [15, 16, 24, 25]. However, it is unclear how macrophages respond to microscopic stimuli of nanoparticle mechanical properties and exert different biological functions, which could be clarified by exploring mechanotransduction pathways. Such explorations would contribute to developing new strategies for TAM reprogramming and nanomedicine design.

According to our previous observation that stiff nanogels affected the biological functions of macrophages such as the suppression of clathrin-mediated endocytosis [2], and the fact that macrophages could also be reprogrammed by external mechanical stimuli, we hypothesized that nanoparticle mechanical properties were potential factors of TAM reprogramming for constrained tumor growth. To this end, poly(N-isopropylmethacrylamide-disulfide bond-methacrylic acid) (P(NIPMAM-ss-MAA)) nanogels are utilized as model nanoparticles, whose stiffness was regulated by controlling the crosslinking density [26, 27]. Our results revealed that the stiffness of nanogels played a significant role in reprogramming TAMs. In detail, compared to the soft nanogels (2%NGs), the stiff nanogels (15%NGs) were more efficient in reprogramming anti-inflammatory macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotype with higher expression of CD86 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). Mechanistically, 15%NGs interacted with integrin to trigger actin polymerization and activate the YAP signaling pathway, thereby reprogramming TAMs (Fig. 1A). As a result, the secretion of tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) by macrophages and the apoptosis of tumor cells were promoted by 15%NGs. Furthermore, benefitting from high liver accumulation, 15%NGs exhibited superiority over 2%NGs in reprogramming TAMs and suppressing tumor growth in orthotopic H22 liver tumor models. The reprogramming of TAMs and apoptosis of tumor cells were also demonstrated in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues excised from patients. This study explored the potential of regulating nanogel stiffness for reprogramming TAMs and suppressing tumor progression. For rational design of cancer nanomedicines, the mechanical properties and intrinsic biological effects of nanoparticles should be taken into consideration.

Results

Influence of nanogels with different stiffness on reprogramming macrophages

To explore the influence of nanogel mechanical properties on macrophage reprogramming and reveal the associated mechanism, two kinds of P(NIPMAM-ss-MAA) nanogels with different stiffness were synthesized according to our previous study [2, 28], including the soft nanogel with crosslinking degree of 2% (designated as 2%NGs) and the stiff counterparts with crosslinking degree of 15% (designated as 15%NGs). Consistently, both 2%NGs and 15%NGs were monodispersed with a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 220 nm in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at 37°C (Figure S1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) images further confirmed the monodispersity and spherical morphology of nanogels (Fig. 1B, C). As reported previously, the Young’s modulus of nanogels was positively correlated with the molar ratio between the crosslinker N, N’-bis(acryloyl)cystamine (BAC) and the monomer N-isopropylmethacrylamide (NIPMAM) [2, 28]. Specifically, the Young’s modulus of the soft 2%NGs was 64.98 ± 35.27 kPa, while that of the stiff 15%NGs was 551.48 ± 93.04 kPa (Fig. 1D). So far, two kinds of nanogels with varied stiffness but similar hydrodynamic diameter were obtained.

Before assessing whether nanogels with varied mechanical properties could reprogram RAW264.7 macrophages, the effect of nanogel stiffness on cell viability of RAW264.7 macrophages or cancer cells was excluded with cell counting kit-8 (Figure S2). To mimic TAMs, RAW264.7 macrophages were preferentially reprogrammed towards anti-inflammatory phenotype by using IL-4 and IL-13 [29]. Subsequently, the anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages were incubated with either soft 2%NGs or stiff 15%NGs. The results revealed that 15%NGs were more effective than 2%NGs in reprogramming macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotype, with increased levels of mRNA transcription (Fig. 1E) and protein expression (Fig. 1F) of CD86 and iNOS which were classical markers of pro-inflammatory macrophages [30, 31]. Consistently, the flow cytometry analysis also confirmed that the expression of CD86 was significantly increased by 15%NGs (Fig. 1G). Because of the elevated level of iNOS, the marked increase in secretion of nitric oxide (NO) by RAW264.7 macrophages was detected after incubation with 15%NGs (Figure S3). To thoroughly comprehend the mechano-induced reprogramming of macrophages, typical anti-inflammatory markers, including CD206 and Arg-1, were also investigated [32, 33]. No significant variations were observed, suggesting that 15%NGs effectively reprogrammed RAW264.7 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype instead of anti-inflammatory phenotype (Figure S4). Similar results were obtained with bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), in which 15%NGs obviously promoted the pro-inflammatory phenotype of BMDMs, manifesting in upregulated CD86 and iNOS (Figure S5 and S6). Moreover, given that internalization efficiency is an important factor in regulating cellular biological functions, the cellular uptake of nanogels with different stiffness was evaluated. The result demonstrated that 2%NGs were internalized more efficiently by RAW264.7 macrophages than stiff 15%NGs, manifesting as higher fluorescence intensity (Figure S7). Despite this, 15%NGs exhibited a stronger capacity for reprogramming RAW264.7 macrophages, as evidenced by higher expression of CD86 and iNOS, indicating that the internalization efficiency was not the dominant factor for macrophage reprogramming, while the stiffness of nanogels made a much greater contribution. These results corroborated that the mechanical properties of nanogels were non-negligible factors in mediating biological functions of macrophages, especially the reprogramming process. The stiff 15%NGs demonstrated stronger capability of reversing anti-inflammatory macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotype, compared to soft 2%NGs.

YAP1 signaling is essential for the reprogramming of RAW264.7 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype induced by nanogels with different stiffness

Although some studies have expounded the effect of macroscopic mechanical stimuli on macrophage reprogramming [16, 17, 24, 35], the mechanisms by which macrophages recognize and react to the microscopic stimuli such as mechanical properties of nanomaterials still lacks thorough comprehension. YAP1, as a widely-investigated protein converting upstream mechanical signals to downstream biochemical signals, plays a key role in modulating biological functions of cells including inflammatory reactions and immune responses [22, 36]. Accordingly, YAP1 was determined as the starting point for uncovering the mechanism of macrophage reprogramming induced by nanogel stiffness. The results exhibited that the increment of mRNA (Fig. 2A) and protein (Fig. 2B, C) level of YAP1 in anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages induced by the stiff 15%NGs was much stronger than that by the soft 2%NGs. Additionally, the significant reduction of the phosphorylation level of YAP1 which was regarded as the activation of its function [37], was detected via western blot (Fig. 2B, C) and flow cytometry (Figure S8 and S9) analysis after treatment by 15%NGs. For further verification, the western blot analysis presented increased expression and decreased phosphorylation of YAP1 in primary BMDMs treated by 15%NGs, compared with those treated by PBS or 2%NGs (Figure S10). Subsequently, the activated dephosphorylated YAP1 translocated to the nucleus to perform the regulatory function of reprogramming macrophages, while phosphorylated YAP1 was typically retained and degraded in the cytoplasm, instead [38, 39]. After labeling the nucleus and YAP1 in RAW264.7 macrophages, it was observed that 15%NGs promoted the translocation of activated YAP1 to nucleus, leading to higher co-localization coefficient (Fig. 2D, E). Meanwhile, the elevated fluorescence intensity of YAP1 in the entire cells and nucleus was induced by 15%NGs, while the fluorescence signal in cytoplasm was almost constant (Fig. 2F). Therefore, 15%NGs contributed to higher ratio of fluorescence intensity of YAP1 in nucleus to cytoplasm (Figure S11). This conclusion was further substantiated by western blot analysis after nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation (Fig. 2G, H), indicating more efficient nuclear translocation by 15%NGs than 2%NGs. These results suggested that the stiff 15%NGs possessed superiorities in mobilizing the function of YAP1 including increased expression, promoted dephosphorylation, and enhanced nuclear translocation than the soft 2%NGs.

To validate whether YAP1 was essential in mechano-induced macrophage reprogramming, Verteporfin (VP), a YAP inhibitor that disrupted the interaction between YAP and TEA domain transcription factor (TEAD) in nucleus, was introduced [40]. VP effectively suppressed the reprogramming of RAW264.7 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype induced by nanogels. Particularly, the transcription (Fig. 2I) and expression (Fig. 2J and Figure S12) of iNOS and CD86 were reduced to a greater extent by VP in the 15%NGs-treated group, compared to 2%NGs-treated group.

Collectively, YAP1 played a critical role in reprogramming RAW264.7 macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotype by nanogels with different mechanical properties.

Stiff nanogels activate YAP1 signaling through the polymerization of actin

To further understand the mechanism for the activation of YAP1 in macrophages, actin as an important link involved in intracellular delivery of mechanical signals [41, 42], of which the polymerization was closely associated with YAP1 activity [23], was investigated. The western blot analysis demonstrated that the influence of nanogel mechanical properties on total amount of actin in RAW264.7 macrophages was negligible (Figure S13). However, the stiff 15%NGs obviously increased the content of F-actin which was polymerized by G-actin in anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages, after fluorescence labeling of F-actin (Fig. 3A, B). This phenomenon was further corroborated by flow cytometry analysis (Figure S14). After isolating F-actin and G-actin of RAW264.7 macrophages, it was found that the ratio of F-actin to G-actin was elevated after treatment with 15%NGs, indicating enhanced polymerization of actin (Fig. 3C, D), which was further verified in primary BMDMs (Figure S15).

To assess the necessity of actin polymerization for nuclear translocation of YAP1 and reprogramming of anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotype, an inhibitor of actin polymerization Latrunculin A (Lat-A), was utilized [43]. It was confirmed that in the presence of Lat-A, the expression and the dephosphorylation of YAP1 induced by nanogels, especially 15%NGs, were suppressed (Fig. 3E and Figure S16). Moreover, the nuclear translocation of YAP1 was impaired by Lat-A, indicating weakened activation of YAP1 signaling (Fig. 3F and Figure S17). As a result, Lat-A inhibited the process of reprogramming anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotype by 15%NGs, manifesting as the significant decrease in mRNA (Fig. 3G) and protein (Fig. 3H) level of iNOS and CD86. Collectively, the stiff 15%NGs are more potent in facilitating the polymerization of actin, which is closely associated with the activation of YAP1 to reprogram anti-inflammatory macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype.

Nanogels with different stiffness interact with integrin to initiate the reprogramming towards pro-inflammatory phenotype

The mechanism by which macrophages sense the mechanical properties of nanogels and induce the polymerization of actin awaits further investigation. It has been reported that integrin αMβ2 is an important transmembrane protein for sensing and transmitting extracellular mechanical stimuli, undergoing conformational variation to interact with actin via the β2 subunit (ITGB2) [20, 25, 44, 45]. The qPCR and western blot analysis revealed that the transcription and expression of ITGB2 in anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs were increased with stiffness of nanogels, signifying the potentially enhanced mechanical responsiveness after treatment by stiff 15%NGs (Fig. 4A, B and Figure S18). Furthermore, 15%NGs significantly strengthened the binding between ITGB2 and actin via co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments (Fig. 4C). As for the interaction between integrin and nanogels, stronger co-localization of fluorescence signal of ITGB2 and 15%NGs was observed, compared to 2%NGs (Fig. 4D, E). To further validate this conclusion, nanogels were incubated with integrin αMβ2 for native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis. Only unbound integrin αMβ2 could be detected, rather than the bound counterparts with nanogels which could not be separated due to the large molecular weight of nanogels. The significantly attenuated band of unbound integrin αMβ2 led to the stronger binding of integrin αMβ2 with 15%NGs than 2%NGs (Fig. 4F and Figure S19). To verify the necessity of interaction between ITGB2 and nanogels for the reprogramming of RAW264.7 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype, a neutralizing antibody against ITGB2 (anti-CD18) was utilized [46]. In the presence of anti-CD18, the polymerization of actin induced by nanogels was hindered, manifesting as the decreased ratio of F-actin to G-actin (Fig. 4G, H). Furthermore, the marked decrease in expression and activation of YAP1 was observed (Fig. 4I, J). As a result, the pro-inflammatory characteristics of RAW264.7 macrophages were weakened, including decreased expression of iNOS and CD86 (Fig. 4K and Figure S20 and S21). While the functional variation of integrin induced by nanogels has been clarified, the increased transcription and expression of ITGB2 might be explained as the result of activated function of YAP1 in nucleus, which could be suppressed by the inhibitor of YAP1, VP (Figure S22 and S23). Collectively, the abovementioned results showed that the stiff 15%NGs exhibited stronger interaction, than the soft 2%NGs, with integrins and underscored the critical role of integrins in triggering the polymerization of actin, activation of YAP1, and the reprogramming of macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype, eventually.

Stiff nanogels suppress the development of 4T1 tumors by activating TNF signaling pathway of macrophages

To further explore the antitumor potential of macrophages with pro-inflammatory phenotype, transcriptomics analysis was performed on anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages that were treated by nanogels with different mechanical properties. A total of 845 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2FoldChange| > 1; P value < 0.05) were induced by stiff 15%NGs compared to soft 2%NGs (Figure S24 and S25), which were mainly enriched in various inflammatory signaling pathways according to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (Figure S26). The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further confirmed the significant activation of TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 5A, B), which is closely associated with antitumor effect of macrophages [47, 48]. Consistently, 15%NGs led to stronger transcription (Fig. 5C) and secretion (Fig. 5D) of TNF-α in RAW264.7 macrophages than 2%NGs, implicating that stiff nanogels would exhibit better antitumor performance than soft counterparts. The cytotoxicity of TNF-α towards murine 4T1 breast cancer cells was investigated firstly (Figure S27). As we expected, the supernatant of 15%NGs-treated RAW264.7 macrophages obviously inhibited cell viability (Fig. 5E) and markedly promoted apoptosis (Fig. 5F and Figure S28) of 4T1 cancer cells, whereas 2%NGs only made limited contribution. In the presence of Etanercept (Etan), which competitively binds to TNF-α receptors on the membrane of tumor cells, the cytotoxicity of the supernatant from RAW264.7 macrophages treated by nanogels was significantly diminished (Fig. 5G) [49]. These results collectively demonstrated that the stiff 15%NGs activated the antitumor effect of macrophages by promoting TNF signaling pathway.

Subsequently, in vivo tumor inhibition capacity of nanogel-treated RAW264.7 macrophages was evaluated with subcutaneous tumor models of female Balb/c mice by co-inoculation of RAW264.7 macrophages with different phenotypes and 4T1 cancer cells at a 2:1 ratio. For this purpose, five phenotypes of macrophages were induced and named as M2, M0, M1, 2%NGs, and 15%NGs. In detail, M0 representing untreated RAW264.7 macrophages, together with M2 for macrophages treated by IL-4 and IL-13 and M1 for macrophages treated by lipopolysaccharides (LPS), jointly constituted the references for positive correlation between tumor inhibition capacity and the pro-inflammatory characteristics of macrophages [50]. Tumor volume on Day 13 decreased from 495.4 ± 88.8 mm3 (M2) to 257.7 ± 83.0 mm3 (M0) and 114.0 ± 87.7 mm3 (M1). Meanwhile, 2%NGs and 15%NGs stood for anti-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages treated by soft 2%NGs or stiff 15%NGs, respectively. Figure 5H showed that 15%NGs led to stronger suppression of tumor growth with tumor volume of 113.4 ± 57.8 mm3, relying on more efficient reprogramming of RAW264.7 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype, compared to 2%NGs with tumor volume of 215.6 ± 110.5 mm3. The photo and weight of harvested tumors further confirmed that 15%NGs exhibited better antitumor performance than 2%NGs (Fig. 5I, J). The immunofluorescent staining of tumor tissues demonstrated that stronger signal of CD86-positive RAW264.7 macrophages could be detected along with the progression of pro-inflammatory phenotype and the increase of nanogel stiffness.

Additionally, to evaluate the reprogramming of TAMs in situ and inhibition of tumor growth by nanogels with varied stiffness, the intratumoral injection of 2%NGs or 15%NGs in subcutaneous 4T1 tumor models was performed. Consistently, 15%NGs significantly retarded tumor growth (Figure S29), as a result of increased percentage of CD86+ macrophages and promoted the reprogramming of TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype (Figure S30).

In summary, the stiff 15%NGs successfully reprogrammed TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype in situ and effectively suppressed the progression of subcutaneous 4T1 tumors.

Stiff nanogels suppress orthotopic liver tumors relying on tumor accumulation and reprogramming of TAMs

After confirmation of in vivo reprogramming macrophages and suppressing tumor growth in 4T1 tumors, nanogels were administered systemically via intravenous injection for the consideration of clinical requirement for tumor therapy, instead of intratumoral injection. Our previous work demonstrated that via intravenous injection, the soft 2%NGs accumulated more in subcutaneous tumor, while the stiff 15%NGs exhibited superior enrichment in liver [2, 28]. Accordingly, orthotopic liver tumor should be a more compatible type of cancer with the advantages of 15%NGs in liver accumulation. Ex vivo imaging of orthotopic H22 tumor models illustrated that 15%NGs accumulated more in both liver tissue and tumor site relative to 2%NGs (Figure S31).

The suppression effect of stiffness-induced pro-inflammatory macrophages to H22 cancer cells was first evaluated in vitro. Consistent with the results in 4T1 cancer cells, the treatment with 15%NGs enhanced the cytotoxicity of supernatants from RAW264.7 macrophages and promoted cell apoptosis of H22 cancer cells relying on secreted TNF-α (Figure S32 and S33), which could be inhibited by Etan (Figure S34 and S35). Furthermore, the enhanced phagocytosis of pro-inflammatory RAW264.7 macrophages towards H22 cancer cells was observed after treatment by 15%NGs (Figure S36). So far, the inhibition effect of pro-inflammatory phenotype of RAW264.7 macrophages against H22 cancer cells has been verified. Then, as illustrated in Fig. 6A, the subcutaneous co-inoculation of H22 tumor cells with RAW264.7 macrophages at a 1:1 ratio demonstrated that 15%NGs delayed tumor development more efficiently and led to lower tumor volume of 47.6 ± 43.5 mm3 and tumor weight of 0.03 ± 0.03 g than the volume of 216.1 ± 166.7 mm3 (Fig. 6B, C) and weight of 0.09 ± 0.07 g (Fig. 6D) after treatment by 2%NGs, due to greater extent of pro-inflammatory characteristics of RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 6E and Figure S37). Afterwards, to verify the contribution of liver-targeting ability of 15%NGs to the regulation of HCC development, nanogels with different stiffness were injected intravenously in orthotopic H22 liver tumor models (Fig. 6F). By utilizing H22 cancer cells expressing luciferase, the tumor growth could be monitored via in vivo bioluminescence imaging. It could be observed that the intravenous injection of 15%NGs effectively inhibited the development of orthotopic H22 tumors, manifesting as the lowest luminescence intensity among all groups (Fig. 6G, H). After harvesting livers, the volume (Fig. 6I) and weight (Fig. 6J) of tumors treated by 15%NGs were obviously lower than the PBS group, while the treatment by 2%NGs exerted negligible inhibition effect. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that 15%NGs significantly upregulated the expression of CD86 in TAMs, while 2%NGs only made limited contribution to the reprogramming of TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype in orthotopic H22 tumors (Fig. 6K and Figure S38), which was further confirmed by immunofluorescent staining of tumor tissues (Fig. 6L, M). Finally, the blood biochemical analysis and the routine blood examination corroborated the safety of nanogels with different stiffness via intravenous injection (Figure S39) and no obvious damage was observed in hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs (Figure S40).

In summary, benefitting from the advantages in reprogramming of macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype and liver accumulation, the stiff 15%NGs achieved more efficient suppression of orthotopic H22 tumors than soft 2%NGs, exhibiting great potential for cancer therapy against HCC.

Stiff nanogels contribute to the reprogramming of TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype and apoptosis of tumor cells in HCC tissues from patients

Not only in mice, ITGB2 and YAP1 also played non-negligible roles in the regulation of HCC development in humans [51, 52]. The Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) 2.0 database proposed a positive correlation between the expression of ITGB2 or YAP1 and the infiltration of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) (Fig. 7A). Kaplan-Meier analysis further revealed that the male HCC patients with high expression of ITGB2 (ITGB2high) or YAP1 (YAP1high) exhibited prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 7B). These observations emphasized that targeting ITGB2-actin-YAP1 signaling pathway would be a promising strategy for suppressing the progress of HCC. To validate the activation of this pathway, the anti-inflammatory human THP-1-derived macrophages were treated by nanogels with different stiffness. Among them, the stiff 15%NGs consistently increased the expression of ITGB2 (Fig. 7C) and promoted the polymerization of actin (Fig. 7D and Figure S41), leading to the activation of YAP1, manifesting as increased expression and reduced phosphorylation of YAP1 (Fig. 7E and Figure S42). As a result, 15%NGs reprogrammed THP-1-derived macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotype with elevated level of iNOS and CD86 (Fig. 7F and Figure S43) and facilitated the secretion of TNF-α (Fig. 7G), endowing THP-1-derived macrophages with stronger inhibition effect against HepG2 and Huh7 cancer cells (Fig. 7H, I), than 2%NGs.

To assess the therapeutic potential of 15%NGs in suppressing human liver tumors by reprogramming TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype, HCC samples from two male patients were acquired for evaluation (Figure S44 and Table S1). Afterwards, ex vivo organotypic slices excised from HCC tumor tissues were employed and incubated with nanogels with different stiffness for 24 h (Fig. 7J). Flow cytometry analysis presented that 15%NGs significantly enhanced the apoptosis of tumor cells (Fig. 7K and Figure S45A, B). Moreover, higher mean fluorescence intensity of CD86 signal (Fig. 7L and Figure S45C) accompanied by elevated production of TNF-α (Fig. 7M) indicated that 15%NGs reprogrammed TAMs to pro-inflammatory phenotype more effectively relative to 2%NGs. The immunofluorescence staining of HCC tissues also confirmed the superior reprogramming of TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype induced by 15%NGs than 2%NGs (Fig. 7N, O). The increased apoptosis of tumor cells was further validated (Figure S46) relying on promoted expression of CD86 (Figure S47A, B) and production of TNF-α (Figure S47C) of organotypic slices derived from another patient with HCC.

In summary, depending on stiffness-activated ITGB2-actin-YAP1 signaling pathway, the stiff 15%NGs were feasible for reprogramming TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype and outperformed soft 2% NGs in eliminating tumor cells in human HCC tissues.

Conclusion

For the purpose of fulfilling the biological potential of nanoparticles, especially in regulating cellular functions, P(NIPMAM-ss-MAA) nanogels with different stiffness were prepared as model nanoparticles to investigate the impact of mechanical properties of nanoparticles on TAMs reprogramming and tumor development. It was revealed that in RAW264.7 macrophages, stiff nanogels (15%NGs) presented stronger interactions with integrin and upregulated the expression of subunit ITGB2, compared to soft counterparts (2%NGs). Subsequently, the binding of actin to ITGB2 and the polymerization of actin were enhanced, promoting both the expression and functionalization of YAP1. As a result, the activated YAP1 was translocated into nucleus to improve the pro-inflammatory phenotype of RAW264.7 macrophages with elevated expression of CD86 and TNF-α, which were also confirmed in rodent BMDMs and human THP-1-derived macrophages. Owing to the synergistic effect of more efficient reprogramming capacity and higher liver accumulation, 15%NGs exhibited more advantages in retarding tumor progression of orthotopic liver tumor than 2%NGs. Most importantly, the antitumor effect in human liver tumors was confirmed via the ex vivo organotypic slices of HCC tumor tissues derived from two patients, of which the percentage of CD86+ macrophages and apoptotic cancer cells was significantly increased by 15%NGs.

Herein, in addition to macroscopic mechanical stimuli, microscopic mechanical property of nanogel stiffness was also proved as an important factor in reprogramming macrophages. Furthermore, a more clarified ITGB2-actin-YAP1 signaling pathway was established as a significant mechanism by which nanogel stiffness reprograms macrophages. In detail, integrins on the membrane of macrophages, as a potential mechanical sensor of nanogel stiffness, transmitted the external mechanical signals to YAP1 via actin. And the mechanical signals were converted to biochemical signals by YAP1 to achieve the reprogramming of TAMs towards pro-inflammatory phenotype. This mechanobiological mechanism provides a new perspective for explaining the impact of nanogel mechanical properties on cellular functions. Moreover, by fully utilizing the biological effects of reprogramming macrophages in synergy with stiffness-dependent liver accumulation, a specific strategy for orthotopic liver tumor therapy relying on nanogel stiffness was established. In summary, the mechanical properties and intrinsic biological effects of nanoparticles were non-negligible and should be taken into consideration for rational design of nanomedicines and optimization of strategies for cancer therapy.

Nevertheless, there are still several interesting but challenging issues deserving further investigation. First, the binding site between nanogels with different stiffness and integrin and the conformational variation of integrin await exploration for thorough comprehension of nanoparticle-protein interactions. Second, nanoparticles with different mechanical properties might affect other biological effects such as the antigen presentation of dendritic cells and the function of nerves in tumor tissues for additional therapeutic applications. Third, in vivo tumor suppression effect of nanogels with different stiffness should be evaluated in HCC patients to achieve clinical translation and tumor therapy.

Preparation of p(NIPMAM-ss-MAA) nanogels with different stiffness

P(NIPMAM-ss-MAA) nanogels with different stiffness were synthesized via emulsion polymerization, employing BAC as the crosslinker [2, 28]. The precise quantities and molar ratios of the components relative to NIPMAM are provided in Table S2. The monomers NIPMAM and MAA, along with the surfactant SDS, were dissolved in 80 mL of water within a three-necked round-bottom flask. BAC, pre-dissolved in ethanol, was subsequently introduced to the mixture. The solution was subjected to vacuum treatment for 10 min to remove oxygen and ethanol, followed by purging with argon. This process was repeated for three times. The mixture was then heated to 80 °C, and an aqueous solution of KPS was injected to initiate polymerization. The reaction was maintained at 80 °C under an argon atmosphere for 6 h with continuous stirring, after which it was cooled to room temperature. The obtained nanogels were purified by ultrafiltration using a membrane with molecular weight cutoff of 10,000 Da to remove unreacted monomers and other small molecules, followed by three times of purification with deionized water. The nanogels were subsequently concentrated to 20 mg/mL and stored at 4 °C, labeled as 2%NGs and 15%NGs.

5 g Rhodamine B (10.45 mmol), 75 mg 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP; 0.615 mmol), and 2.6 g N, N’-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC; 12.6 mmol) were dissolved in 52.5 mL anhydrous dichloromethane (CH₂Cl₂) and oxygen was removed under vacuum. The mixture was stirred under an argon atmosphere for 30 min, after which 1.55 mL 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA; 12.5 mmol) was added. The solution was stirred at 20 °C for 25 h under argon protection. The product was purified by column chromatography on silica gel using a 90:10 dichloromethane/methanol (DCM/MeOH) eluent and concentrated using a rotary evaporator to yield a dry purple powder (RhB-HEMA). For the synthesis of Rhodamine-labeled poly(N-isopropylmethacrylamide-co-methacrylic acid) (P(NIPMAM-ss-MAA)), 255 µg RhB-HEMA, corresponding to a 5% molar ratio relative to the NIPMAM monomer, was dissolved in water along with other monomers, followed by the same synthetic procedure [2, 28]. The Rhodamine-labeled nanogels were subsequently concentrated to 20 mg/mL and stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of nanogels

10 µL of nanogels with different stiffnesses (2%NGs and 15%NGs) were dispersed in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The hydrodynamic diameters were then measured with dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern, Zetasizer Nano-ZS, UK) at 37 °C, with an equilibrium time of 15 min.

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, JEM-1230, Japan) analysis, the nanogels were dispersed in ultrapure water to a concentration of 0.01 mg/mL. 10 µL of each dispersion were applied to a 400-mesh carbon-coated copper grid and dried at room temperature. Subsequently, 10 µL of a 1% phosphotungstic acid aqueous solution was added for staining for 2 min. Excess stain was removed by absorption with filter paper along the edge of the carbon support film, followed by washing with 10 µL of ultrapure water for 1 min. After removing excess water with filter paper and drying the grid at room temperature, the morphology of nanogels was observed using TEM.

For atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker, Multimode 8, Germany) analysis, glass coverslips were immersed in a 1% polyethyleneimine aqueous solution for 24 h to modify the surface with positive charges. Nanogels were dispersed in ultrapure water to a concentration of 0.01 mg/mL, and 100 µL of dispersion was applied to the coverslip for electrostatic adsorption over 10 min. Excess dispersion was removed, and the sample was rinsed with 300 µL of ultrapure water to remove unabsorbed nanogels. AFM was then performed in a liquid environment, with imaging conducted in contact mode and Young’s modulus measured using tapping mode.

Cytotoxicity of nanogels

The cytotoxicity of 2%NGs and 15%NGs on various cell types was evaluated with the CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 8,000 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing 2%NGs or 15%NGs at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. After 24 h of incubation, the culture medium containing the nanogel was removed and 100 µL of new culture medium containing 10 µL of CCK8 reagent was added for an additional 2 h of incubation. The absorption value was measured with microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Flex Station 3, USA) at 450 nm and the cell viability was calculated accordingly.

Cellular uptake of nanogels

Rhodamine-labeled nanogels with different stiffness were used to evaluate the cellular uptake efficiency of RAW264.7 macrophages. RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated for 12 h. The culture medium was then removed and replaced with complete medium containing Rhodamine-labeled nanogels at a concentration of 50 µg/mL. After incubation for 0.5, 1.5, 3, and 4.5 h, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove free nanogels. Subsequently, the cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 cells with an RNA extraction kit and subsequently reversely transcribed into cDNA with a PCR thermal cycler. Gene transcription level was assessed by RT-qPCR, with relative transcription levels quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Quantitative analysis was conducted after normalization to the control group. The primer sequences for RT-qPCR are provided in Table S3.

Western blot

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed using lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Affinibody LifeScience). Adherent cells were scraped and the lysate was collected. Following centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected. The lysate was mixed with 5× SDS sample buffer and heated at 95–100 °C for 5 min. Prepared samples were loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane.

The membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST) for 5 min at room temperature and blocked with 25 mL of blocking buffer for 1 h. Incubation with primary antibodies was performed overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was then washed with TBST for 20 min and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). The ratio of the grayscale intensity of the target protein band to that of the housekeeping protein band was calculated. Semi-quantitative analysis was conducted after normalization to the control group.

Flow cytometry

After digestion into single-cell suspension, cells were labeled with specific antibodies, and then detected with flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX, USA). The protein expression was determined by median fluorescent intensity (MFI).

Immunofluorescence staining of YAP1

Following stimulation, RAW264.7 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. The fixed cells were washed with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS to facilitate YAP1 staining. After additional washes with PBS, the cells were blocked with 2% BSA and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequent washes with 2% BSA were performed, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies in 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and Hoechst (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), both diluted 1:1000 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. After thorough washing with PBS, imaging was captured with FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, FV3000, Japan). The mean or total fluorescence intensity of each cell was quantified using ImageJ software (Ver. 2.0). Relative intensity YAP1 was calculated after normalization to the control group.

Separation and identification of F-actin and G-actin

Anti-inflammatory macrophages were incubated with culture medium containing PBS, 2%NGs (200 µg/mL), or 15%NGs (200 µg/mL) for 24 h. Subsequently, following the protocol provided by the G-Actin/F-Actin In Vivo Assay Kit (Cytoskeleton, Denver, USA), the cells were washed with PBS and lysed in a detergent-based lysis buffer designed to stabilize and preserve both G-actin and F-actin. This buffer selectively dissolves G-actin instead of F-actin. Centrifugation was performed to precipitate F-actin, with G-actin retained in the supernatant. The supernatant and precipitation were analyzed with western blot. Relative F-actin/G-actin was conducted after normalization to the control group.

Colocalization of nanogels and integrin

RAW264.7 cells were incubated with antibodies against ITGB2 for 2 h at room temperature. The cells were then washed repeatedly with 2% BSA and incubated with CoraLite647-labeled secondary antibodies in 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Following thorough washing with PBS, 100 µg/mL of Rhb-labeled NGs (Alexa Fluor 561) was added. Images were acquired with confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000, Japan) and the colocalization coefficient was calculated.

Nanogels-integrin affinity assay

Human integrin αMβ2, comprising ITGAM and ITGB2 subunits with 74.8% similarity to murine ITGAM and 81.8% similarity to murine ITGB2, was utilized in the experiments [53]. The 2%NGs and 15%NGs, with concentrations of 200 µg/mL or 1000 µg/mL, were incubated with 20 ng/mL human integrin αMβ2 on ice for 6 h. In addition of non-reduced native gel sample loading buffer, native-PAGE was performed to separate unbound integrin to nanogels. Western blot was subsequently conducted using ITGB2 antibody and the binding capacity of nanogels to integrin was inversely proportional to the grayscale intensity of the integrin bands.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) of ITGB2 and actin

The combination of actin to ITGB2 was investigated with Co-IP. In detail, Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were thoroughly resuspended and blocked with PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 h. The prepared Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were incubated with 25 µg/mL antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Magnetic separation was conducted, and the Protein A/G Magnetic Beads bound to the corresponding antibody were collected. These beads were subsequently mixed with the antigen sample and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h. Magnetic separation was performed again, and the supernatant was discarded. A volume of 25–50 µL of 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added to the beads, mixed thoroughly, and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Following another magnetic separation, the supernatant was collected for SDS-PAGE analysis.

Transcriptomic analysis

Anti-inflammatory macrophages were incubated with 2%NGs or 15%NGs. The treated cells were subsequently collected and sent to Metware (Wuhan, China) for transcriptome analysis. mRNAs bearing polyA tails were enriched using Oligo (dT) magnetic beads. The purified mRNAs were fragmented into small segments using fragmentation buffer at an appropriate temperature. First-strand cDNAs were synthesized through reverse transcription with random hexamer primers. During second-strand synthesis, dUTP was substituted for dTTP, resulting in the incorporation of dUTP into the second-strand cDNAs. This procedure also encompassed end repair and the addition of dA tails. Sequencing adapters were subsequently ligated, followed by purification with magnetic beads and fragment selection to yield a library containing insert fragments of 250–350 bp. The ligated products were amplified via PCR, subjected to an additional round of magnetic bead purification, and dissolved in nuclease-free water. The initial library was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, and fragment sizes were assessed with a Qsep400 high-throughput bio fragment analyzer. Following successful library quality control, the libraries were pooled according to their effective concentrations and target sequencing output, then sequenced on an Illumina platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. The sequencing process entailed simultaneous synthesis and sequencing, employing four fluorescently labeled dNTPs, DNA polymerase, and adapter primers introduced into the sequencing flow cell for amplification. As the complementary strand extended, each incorporated fluorescently labeled dNTP emitted distinct fluorescence, which was detected by the sequencer. These optical signals were subsequently converted into sequencing peaks by computational software, yielding the sequence information of the analyzed fragments.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The collected samples were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate cellular components. The supernatant was diluted to concentrations of 1/1, 1/10, and 1/50. Biotinylated antibody working solution was incubated with samples or standards at various concentrations in plate wells. The liquid was then discarded from the wells, and the wells were washed. Subsequently, streptavidin-HRP working solution was added and incubated in the wells, followed by discarding the liquid and washing the wells again. TMB substrate was then added and incubated in the dark. Stop solution was added thereafter, and the results were measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Flex Station 3, USA).

Cytotoxicity of supernatant from macrophages against cancer cells

Anti-inflammatory macrophages including RAW264.7 macrophages and THP-1-derived macrophages were exposed to PBS, 2%NGs (200 µg/mL), or 15%NGs (200 µg/mL) for 24 h. The supernatants were then replaced with serum-free medium and cultured for another 24 h to obtain conditioned media. The conditioned media were obtained to treat H22, 4T1, Huh7, and HepG2 cancer cells for 24 h. Cell viability was measured with CCK-8 assay (Yeason, Wuhan, China), and apoptosis was assessed with cell apoptosis detection kit (40302ES60, Yeason, Wuhan, China).

Phagocytosis of cancer cells by reprogrammed anti-inflammatory macrophages

anti-inflammatory macrophages were treated with PBS, 2%NGs (200 µg/mL), or 15%NGs (200 µg/mL) for 24 h. The treated macrophages were labeled with CD11b and subsequently co-cultured with DiD-labeled H22 cancer cells at a 1:4 ratio for 8 h at 37 °C. The phagocytosis of macrophages towards H22 cancer cells was evaluated with flow cytometry.

Co-implantation of macrophages and cancer cells

Anti-inflammatory macrophages were cultured separately in medium, medium containing 200 µg/mL of 2%NGs, or medium containing 15%NGs for 24 h. Subsequently, macrophages were mixed with H22 cancer cells at a 1:1 ratio (2 × 106 macrophages and 2 × 106 cancer cells per mouse) or were mixed with 4T1 cancer cells at a 2:1 ratio (4 × 106 macrophages and 2 × 106 cancer cells per mouse) and subcutaneously co-implanted into the dorsal region of BALB/c mice, designated as day 0. Starting from day 0, tumor volume was measured and recorded. On day 13, after measurement, the mice were sacrificed, tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. Tumor burden was calculated based on tumor volume and weight. Tumors were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence staining. The percentage of the area of CD86+ cells within tumor tissues was analyzed with ImageJ software (Ver. 2.0).

The accumulation of nanogels in orthotopic liver tumors

A suspension of H22-luc cancer cells was mixed with Matrigel matrix at a 1:1 volume ratio, and 1 × 106 H22-luc cancer cells were inoculated into the liver tissue in a volume of 25 µL in male BALB/c mice to establish orthotopic H22-luc liver cancer models. 7 d post-inoculation, the mice were randomly divided into two groups including 2%NGs and 15%NGs. The nanogels were fluorescently labeled with Rhodamine B and injected via the tail vein. At 2 h after injection, the mice were sacrificed and livers were excised. Fluorescence intensity at the tumor site was detected with in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina XR, USA), and semi-quantitative analysis was performed with Living Image (Ver. 4.0) software.

In vivo antitumor effects

A 4T1 subcutaneous tumor model was established by injecting 2 × 106 4T1 cancer cells into the dorsal region of female BALB/c mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 200 mm3, designated as Day 0, the mice were randomly divided to three groups including PBS, 2%NGs, and 15%NGs. Mice were intratumorally injected with PBS, 2%NGs, or 15%NGs at a dose of 0.5 mg/100 mm³ on Day 1 and 8. Tumor volume was measured and recorded daily from Day 0, with a vernier caliper and calculated with the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. On Day 13, mice were sacrificed and tumors were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence staining. The percentage of the area of CD86+ cells within tumors was analyzed with ImageJ software (Ver. 2.0) to evaluate the reprogramming effects of nanogels with different stiffness on TAMs.

For the H22-luc orthotopic liver tumor model, the day of tumor implantation was designated as Day 0. On Day 7, mice were randomly divided into three groups: PBS, 2%NGs, and 15%NGs. On Day 7, 10, and 13, mice were administered PBS, 2% NGs, or 15% NGs via tail vein injection at a dose of 200 mg/kg. On Days 7 and 14, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 3 mg of luciferin substrate per mouse, and bioluminescence images of livers were captured with in vivo imaging system for semi-quantitative analysis with Living Image (Ver. 4.0) software. On Day 13, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. Tumors were minced and resuspended in medium containing 0.8 mg/mL Type I collagenase and 5 µg/mL DNase, followed by digestion at 37 °C for 1 h and filtration through 40 μm cell strainers to obtain single-cell suspension. Cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with FVS780 dye, CD45 (Alexa Fluor® 700 anti-mouse CD45), CD11b (FITC anti-mouse/human CD11b), F4/80 (Brilliant Violet 421™ anti-mouse F4/80), and CD86 (PE anti-mouse CD86) antibodies. FVS780 dye was used to distinguish viable cells. All antibodies were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated with cells for 30 min at room temperature. Tumor cells were identified as CD45− cells, while TAMs were identified as CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ cells with flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX, USA). The extent of pro-inflammatory characteristics of TAMs was determined by the MFI of CD86. Another portion of the tumor was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence staining. The percentage of the area of CD86+ cells in tumors was analyzed using ImageJ software (Ver. 2.0) to assess the reprogramming effects of nanogels with different stiffness on TAMs.

Bioinformatics analysis

For survival analysis, RNA-seq data were sourced from the Kaplan-Meier Plotter dataset. To evaluate the association between YAP1 or ITGB2 expression and pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage infiltration, RNA-seq data from the TIMER 2.0 dataset were analyzed.

Human organotypic slice culture

Fresh HCC tissue was obtained from two cancer patients undergoing routine surgical resection at Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). Patient demographic and clinical information is provided in Table S1. The study was approved by the Clinical Trial Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (project number: 2025S016), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Tumor tissues were sectioned into approximately 5 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm slices and incubated in RPMI 1640 medium containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 200 µg/mL 2%NGs, or 200 µg/mL 15%NGs at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. The tumor slices were washed with PBS three times, minced, and digested in RPMI 1640 medium containing 0.8 mg/mL Type I collagenase and 5 µg/mL DNase I at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with CD45 (Alexa Fluor® 700 anti-human CD45), CD11b (FITC anti-mouse/human CD11b), CD14 (Brilliant Violet 421™ anti-human CD14), and CD86 (PE anti-human CD86). All antibodies were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated with cells for 30 min at room temperature. Tumor cells were identified as CD45− cells, while TAMs were identified as CD45+CD11b+CD14+ cells with flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX, USA). The extent of pro-inflammatory characteristics of TAMs was determined by the MFI of CD86.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as specified, and were analyzed using an unpaired two-sided Statistical significance between two groups was calculated using unpaired two-sided Student’s t test. Statistical significance among multiple groups was calculated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software. The correlation between YAP1 or ITGB2 expression levels and pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage infiltration was assessed using Pearson’s correlation test. Survival rates were evaluated by the log-rank test. The co-localization coefficient between YAP1 and the nucleus or integrin and nanogels was determined using Manders’ correlation test.
