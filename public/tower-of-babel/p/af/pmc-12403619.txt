Associations of child-to-adult body size change with risk of hypertension and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: a population-based cohort study of 226,420 individuals
Authors: Fengyuan Tang, Wenqianzi Yang, Weijia Wu, Shengxuan Jin, Xuemei Zhen, Hengjin Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationships between body size changes and the risk of hypertension and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) are still unclear. This study examined the independent and combined influences of child and adult body size on the risk of adulthood hypertension and MASLD.MethodsWe included 226,420 participants from the UK Biobank who were free of hypertension and liver diseases. Child-to-adult body size categories were identified based on self-reported childhood body size and measured BMI in adulthood. Cox proportional hazard regression models were applied to assess the associations between body size categories and the incidence of hypertension and MASLD. Mediation analyses to address the effect of anthropometric measures and metabolic risk factors were also performed.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of approximately 13 years, 28,662 individuals had developed hypertension (N = 27,624) and/or MASLD (N = 1,692). Low body size in childhood increased the risk of adulthood hypertension and MASLD, regardless of adult body size. Individuals with low child body size and high adult body size exhibited the highest risk for both hypertension (HR 2.01, P < 0.001) and MASLD (HR 3.28, P < 0.001). Individuals who had high child body size but average body size in adulthood had risks of the two diseases that were similar to those of persons who had an average body size consistently (all P > 0.05). Body size changes influenced hypertension through significant natural direct effects and indirect effects via anthropometric and metabolic factors, while their impact on MASLD was primarily mediated by these factors without significant direct effects.ConclusionsChildhood leanness exacerbates the risk of hypertension and MASLD in adulthood. Within the follow-up period, normal adult body size would mitigate the detrimental effects of childhood obesity. Thus, attention should be given to reinforcing weight management in children by implementing evidence-based strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24317-4.Keywords: Hypertension, MASLD, Obesity, Pediatric obesity, Body size change

Introduction

Hypertension and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [1]) are both significant metabolic abnormalities [2]. Hypertension represents a significant aspect of the metabolic syndrome, with MASLD regarded as a hepatic manifestation thereof [3]. Hypertension is a key driver of cardiovascular events, while MASLD serves as a precursor to severe liver conditions, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [4, 5]. Both hypertension and MASLD are becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide, posing significant challenges to public health and placing immense pressure on healthcare system [6, 7]. Furthermore, given their shared pathophysiological mechanisms and complex bidirectional relationship, hypertension and MASLD frequently occur together as co-morbidities [8], underscoring the necessity for early prevention strategies.

Childhood obesity, a growing public health concern with the global rate increasing fourfold from 1990 [9], plays a crucial role in the development of both hypertension and MASLD [10, 11]. Obesity during childhood is strongly associated with long-term changes in body size, as overweight children are more likely to become obese adults [12]. The change in body size from childhood to adulthood reflects not only individual growth and metabolic patterns but also long-term risks for adverse health outcomes [13]. Evidence suggests that child-to-adult body size changes are independently linked to the development of both hypertension and MASLD in adulthood [14, 15].

However, the relationships between changes in body size from childhood to adulthood and the risk of developing hypertension and MASLD are inconsistent. Regarding hypertension, it is debated whether being underweight during childhood contributes to a higher risk of hypertension in adulthood [15, 16]. Notably, some studies indicated that children who remain obese into adulthood might experience the highest rates of hypertension [17, 18], while other studies indicated that individuals who become obese only in adulthood might be at the greatest risk [15, 19]. Additionally, it remains unclear whether individuals who were obese in childhood but later lost weight in adulthood face a similar risk compared to those who were never obese [20, 21]. For MASLD, most studies investigating its link with body size have primarily focused on the effect of adult body size [22]. As a result, limited studies have explored how changes in body size from childhood to adulthood might influence the risk of subsequently developing MASLD, and findings from existing studies are inconsistent [14, 23]. Moreover, most previous studies involved relatively small participant numbers and lacked sufficient follow-up, particularly among older age groups, which could affect the reliability and generalizability of the results.

Given the complex correlation between hypertension and MASLD, coupled with the unclear relationship between these two conditions and body size changes, this study aimed to investigate whether independent child or adult body size and child-to-adult body size changes are associated with hypertension and MASLD in adulthood.

Methods

Study participants

This was a prospective cohort study utilizing data from the UK Biobank (UKB). a large population-based cohort study with over half a million participants aged between 37 and 73 years. During the baseline survey conducted between 2006 and 2010, the participants completed a touchscreen questionnaire and a verbal interview, took physical measurements, and provided biological samples at assessment centers. Participants were followed up through study visits, linkage to national health records, death registers, and primary care records [24]. This study has obtained ethical approval from the UKB Research Ethics Committee and the Human Tissue Authority, and each participant provided written informed consent.

A total of 502,402 individuals completed the baseline survey and remained during follow-up. We excluded participants based on several criteria: (1) those with self-reported or diagnosed pre-existing hypertension, as well as those taking antihypertensive medication at baseline (N = 140,419); (2) those diagnosed with other liver disease or suffering from alcohol/drug use disorder at or before the beginning (N = 2,149) [25]; and (3) pregnant women at baseline (N = 136). To mitigate reverse causality effects, we also ruled out MASLD participants who experienced events within the first two years of follow-up (N = 76) [26]. Furthermore, participants with missing values on covariables were omitted (N = 115,029). Consequently, the final participant count stood at 226,420 (Fig. 1).

Child‑to‑adult body size categories

The categorization of childhood body size was derived from participants’ responses to a baseline touchscreen questionnaire. Participants were asked,“When you were 10 years old, compared to the average, would you describe yourself as thinner, about average, or plumper?” The responses provided served as the basis for dividing the study population into three distinct groups with “thinner” representing low child body size (ChildLow), “average” representing average child body size (ChildAverage), and “plumper” representing high child body size (ChildHigh). This measure has been validated by prior research investigating the correlation between polygenic scores and measured body mass index (BMI) in British and Norwegian birth cohorts [27, 28].

During the enrollment assessment visit, adult participants’ body weight and height were accurately measured by trained personnel. Participants were required to remove their shoes and any heavy outerwear before the measurement [29]. Body weight was determined using a Tanita BC-418MA scale (Amsterdam, Netherlands), while standing height was recorded with a Seca 202 device (Birmingham, UK), ensuring that the participants were barefoot. Subsequently, the body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the individual’s weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters.

The World Health Organization’s criteria for defining overweight and obesity were initially applied to categorize the participants [30]. However, only 0.6% of the total population (n = 1,385) was identified as having a low adult body size, including those with a BMI of 18.5 or below. Moreover, the majority of adults were classified as overweight (high body size), with 60.3% of participants having a BMI of 25 or higher. This categorization can result in the formation of extreme groups with an imbalanced disease prevalence and sample sizes. To address this issue, we aimed to make adult body size as comparable as possible to child body size as previous research [28]. BMI residuals calculated form a model, in which age and its squared and sex were served as predictors (BMI ~ age + age2 by sex), were used to estimated adult body size [31]. Three adult body size groups encompassing low adult body size (AdultLow), average adult body size (AdultAverage), and high adult body size (AdultHigh) were established by matching the corresponding proportion of child body size groups. Subsequently, nine child-to-adult body size categories were formulated by amalgamating child and adult body size groups, namely ChildLow−AdultLow, ChildLow−AdultAverage, ChildLow−AdultHigh, ChildAverage−AdultLow, ChildAverage−AdultAverage, ChildAverage−AdultHigh, ChildHigh−AdultLow, ChildHigh−AdultAverage, and ChildHigh−AdultHigh. These nine categories represented the transition of an individual’s body size from childhood to adulthood.

Incident hypertension and MASLD

The incident hypertension was defined as hospitalization or death due to hypertension using the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes I10-I15 [32, 33]. Similarly, MASLD referred to hospitalization or death due to MASLD or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). According to the Expert Panel Consensus Statement [25], MASLD was defined as ICD-10 code K76.0 (fatty [change of] liver, not elsewhere classified) and K75.8 (other specified inflammatory liver diseases) [34]. The dates of hospital admission and their causes were verified through the linkage of electronic health records with Health Episode Statistics (England & Wales) and Scottish Morbidity Records (Scotland). Mortality data was obtained from death certificates maintained by the National Health Service Information Center (England & Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register Scotland (Scotland) [24].

The time-to-event was calculated as the period between the date of the UKB center assessment and the earliest occurrence of hypertension, MASLD, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up on February 1, 2022.

Covariates and baseline indicators

In terms of socioeconomic factors, we considered covariates such as age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, and Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI) at recruitment and assessment centers. Based on the preceding national census output areas, TDI was calculated immediately before the participant joined the UKB. We also accounted for various lifestyle factors, including smoking status, drinking status, diet, physical activity time, sedentary time, and sleep duration (Supplementary Methodologies).

Furthermore, we reported baseline anthropometric measures and metabolic risk indicators in serum including fat-free mass (FFM), fat mass (FM), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol, and Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and so on. FFM and FM were measured by and Tanita BC418MA body composition analyser. Metabolic indicators were analyzed with Beckman Coulter AU5800 (UK), except for HbA1c, which was assayed using Bio-Rad Variant II Turbo (Hertfordshire, UK).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for the nine child-adult body size categories were presented using means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and counts and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in continuous variables were assessed using one-way ANOVA, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests.

The Kaplan − Meier estimator was used to assess the relationship between childhood and adulthood groups, as well as the nine child-adult body size categories and the incidence of hypertension and MASLD. After adjusting for potential confounding factors previously mentioned, Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to quantify the association and calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with individuals who had average child and adult body size consistently serving as the reference.

We also performed mediation analyses to explore the effect of anthropometric measures and metabolic risk indicators in serum on the association between chid-to-adult body size change and the risk of hypertension and MASLD. The mediation analyses were conducted using the R package “medflex”, which fits natural effect models for nested counterfactuals [35]. The total effect of child-to-adult body size change on hypertension and MASLD was decomposed into estimates of the natural direct effect (NDE) of child-to-adult body size change (not mediated by anthropometric measures and metabolic risk indicators) and the natural indirect effect (NIE) of child-to-adult body size change (mediated by anthropometric measures and metabolic risk indicators). Mediator models were also adjusted for potential confounding factors. 95% CIs were calculated by non-parametric bootstrap with 1,000 replications for all mediated models.

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure the robustness of the results, several sensitivity analyses were performed. Firstly, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using the broader MASLD definition that includes ICD-10 codes K76.8, K76.9 K74.0, K74.1, K74.2, and K74.6 [26]. Secondly, we conducted sensitivity analyses with additional controls for family history of hypertension and metabolic risk factors including (1) hyperglycemia/diabetes defined as HbA1c ≥ 42 mmol/mol or physician-diagnosed diabetes, (2) high triglycerides defined as ≥ 1.7 mmol/L and (3) low high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-cholesterol classified as < 1.3 mmol/L for women and < 1.0 mmol/L for men [36]. Thirdly, stratification by sex and age groups was performed to examine the influence of body size changes from childhood to middle-aged or older adulthood. Finally, we included prevalent cases to determine if the results differed from the analysis of incident disease using logistic regression. By stratifying the analyses by sex and recreating the nine child-adult body size categories if needed, the results were compared accordingly.

The data were processed and analyzed using R version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). The significance level was set at two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Following the aforementioned categorization methodology, child body size was divided into three groups with ChildLow comprising 32.6% of the cohort, ChildAverage accounting for 52.2%, and ChildHigh making up the remaining 15.2%. Accordingly, three adult body size groups were identified in the same proportions as in children, namely AdultLow (32.6%), AdultAverage (52.2%), and AdultHigh (15.2%). During a median follow-up period of 12.79 years (IQR 11.89 − 13.57 years) for hypertension and 12.95 years (IQR 12.23 − 13.65 years) for MASLD, 28,662 individuals (12.7%) were diagnosed with hypertension (N = 27,624) and/or MASLD (N = 1,692). The incidence rates were 10.07 per 1,000 person-years for hypertension (95% CI 9.95, 10.19) and 0.59 per 1,000 person-years for MASLD (95% CI 0.56, 0.62).

Regarding trends in body size changes, 46.3% of individuals maintained the same body size category from childhood to adulthood (ChildLow−AdultLow, ChildAverage−AdultAverage, and ChildHigh−AdultHigh). However, 26.7% transitioned to a higher category (ChildLow−AdultAverage/AdultHigh and ChildAverage−AdultHigh), while 27.0% shifted to a lower category (ChildHigh−AdultLow/AdultAverage and ChildAverage−AdultLow) (Table 1). Specifically, 19.2% experienced an increase from a low childhood size to an average or high adulthood size (ChildLow−AdultAverage/AdultHigh), and 10.7% exhibited a decrease from a high childhood size to a low or average adulthood size (ChildHigh−AdultLow/AdultAverage). Those with a high body size in childhood (ChildHigh) were generally associated with a higher BMI in adulthood. It was observed that individuals with a low body size in childhood (ChildLow) had similar or, in women, higher WHR in adulthood compared to those with a high body size in childhood (ChildHigh) (Tables S1-S3). Table S4 showed the differences of basic characteristics between excluded and included populations.

Incidence of diseases in child or adult body size groups

For child body size, a higher hypertension incidence was observed in individuals with either low (ChildLow, HR 1.07 [95% CI 1.04, 1.09]) or high (ChildHigh, HR 1.20 [95% CI 1.16, 1.24]) child body size compared to those with average child body size (ChildAverage) (Figure S1). The correlation noted for MASLD was consistent with and greater than that observed in hypertension, with both low (ChildLow, HR 1.15 [95% CI 1.03, 1.28]) and high (ChildHigh, HR 1.42 [95% CI 1.24, 1.61]) child body size associated with higher risk. The distribution of adult BMI across various child body size groups, stratified by sex and age tertiles is detailed in Table S5.

In adulthood, individuals with a low adult body size (AdultLow) exhibited a lower incidence of hypertension (HR 0.66 [95% CI 0.64, 0.68]), whereas those with a high adult body size (AdultHigh) showed a higher incidence (HR 1.81 [95% CI 1.76, 1.86]) when compared to individuals with an average adult body size (AdultAverage) (Figure S2). Similarly, but more pronounced, the results indicated that low adult body size (AdultLow) was associated with a lower incidence of MASLD, whereas high adult body size (AdultHigh) was associated with a higher incidence of MASLD. This was reflected in an HR of 0.37 (95% CI 0.32, 0.44) for AdultLow and 2.71 (95% CI 2.44, 3.00) for AdultHigh.

Incidence of diseases in child-adult body size categories

After controlling for socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, Cox regression analyses demonstrated similar associations between body size changes and hypertension and MASLD. Among nine child-to-adult body size categories, all individuals with a high adult body size (ChildLow/ChildAverage/ChildHigh−AdultHigh) were at the greatest risk of developing the two diseases, and those with low adult body size (ChildLow/ChildAverage/ChildHigh−AdultLow) were associated with the lowest risk of both hypertension and MASLD compared to individuals who keep an average body size (Figs. 2 and 3). Within each adult body size group, individuals with low child body size (ChildLow) exhibited a higher incidence of hypertension and MASLD than those with average or high child body size (ChildAverage/ChildHigh). In detail, individuals having low child body size and high adult body size (ChildLow−AdultHigh) had the highest incidence compared to those having average body size consistently with the HR for hypertension was 2.01 (95% CI 1.90, 2.12), and the HR for MASLD was 3.28 (95% CI 2.74, 3.92). This was higher than those with high child body size (ChildHigh−AdultHigh, hypertension HR 1.87; MASLD HR 2.71, all P < 0.001) or average child body size (ChildAverage−AdultHigh hypertension HR 1.75; MASLD HR 2.55, all P < 0.001) and high adult body size. For individuals with high child body size, those having average body size in adulthood (ChildHigh−AdultAverage) had similar risk (all P > 0.05) compared to those having persistent average body size, and those with low body size (ChildHigh−AdultLow) in adulthood had lower disease risk (hypertension HR 0.74; MASLD HR 0.53, all P < 0.001). This trend was consistently observed for both genders (Figures S3 and S4).

The mediation analysis revealed that various anthropometric measures and metabolic risk factors significantly mediated the association between child-to-adult body size change and the risks of hypertension and MASLD. For hypertension, significant NDE was observed for all body size change categories except ChildHigh−AdultAverage (Table 2). FFM demonstrated the highest proportion mediated (32.81%), followed by WHR (20.13%), with triglycerides (3.14%), HbA1c (4.98%), and FM (3.40%) showing relatively more minor proportions. For each mediating variable (excluding HbA1c), the direction was consistent with the NDE as long as it exhibited a significant mediating effect. However, for MASLD, the NDE did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.171) (Table 3). All NIE of mediator except FM reached statistical significance (P < 0.05), indicating that anthropometric measures and metabolic risk factors primarily mediate the influence of child-to-adult body size change on MASLD. The largest proportion was mediated by FFM (37.55%), followed by WHR (16.07%), FM (13.73%), and triglycerides (5.27%), with all directions of the significant mediatorconsistent with the corresponding NDE.

Sensitivity analyses

When sensitivity analyses were conducted using the broader definition of MASLD, 2,941 participants were identified with the condition, and similar results were observed (Figure S5). Sensitivity analyses, accounting for hypertension family history and metabolic risk factors such as hyperglycemia/diabetes, high triglycerides, and low HDL-cholesterol showed similar results comparable to those adjusted solely for socio-economic and lifestyle factors (Figure S6). Furthermore, age-stratified sensitivity analysis demonstrated no significant differences from primary analyses (Figure S7). The inclusion of prevalent cases also maintained analytical outcomes unchanged (Figure S8).

Discussion

While body size in adulthood has a notable influence on the onset of hypertension and MASLD, the long-term implications of childhood body size on health outcomes are of greater public health importance from an early prevention and whole life circle perspective. The findings indicated that low child body size was linked to an elevated risk of hypertension and MASLD within each adult body size group. Individuals with low body size during childhood and high body size in adulthood exhibited the highest disease risk. Additionally, individuals who had high child body size but normal adult body size had risks of the two diseases that were similar to those of persons who had an average body size consistently. Those with high body size and low adult body size had lower disease risk. Various anthropometric measures and metabolic risk factors significantly mediated the association between child-to-adult body size change and the risks of hypertension and MASLD. These associations were observed independently of confounding variables, across all subgroups examined, and remained consistent in nearly all sensitivity analyses.

Limited research investigated the link between changes in body size and the likelihood of hypertension development in adulthood [11, 16–18]. The majority of these studies focused on the effects of overweight or obesity, with minimal consideration for leanness. A study utilizing longitudinal data from 5,276 participants in the China Health and Nutrition Survey showed that individuals who transitioned from being thin to overweight, as well as those who moved from thin to normal body size, had an increased risk of hypertension compared to those who consistently maintained a normal body size [16]. Similar findings were observed in the British birth cohort, whereby weight gain among individuals in the lowest 10% of BMI was associated with the highest adult risk of hypertension at both age 11 and age 16 [37]. These study results align with our findings. However, our analyses further categorized adult body size and confirmed the adverse effects of childhood leanness on hypertension in adulthood, particularly in individuals with high adult body size.

In addition to childhood leanness, we have also investigated the correlation between childhood obesity and the subsequent development of hypertension in adulthood. Our findings indicated that individuals who maintain a high body size from childhood into adulthood have a higher risk of hypertension compared to those who were normal-sized in childhood and adulthood. However this elevated risk could be mitigated if a normal or low body size is restored in adulthood. These results remained consistent across analyses stratified by age and sex and were unaffected by the inclusion of family history of hypertension and metabolic risk indicators in serum. These findings are further supported by numerous empirical studies [18, 20, 38] and two meta-analyses encompassing sixteen high-quality studies [19, 39].

Furthermore, our study indicated a prospective association between changes in body size and MASLD, which was independent of socioeconomic factors, lifestyle habits, and risk biomarkers. Given the prospective nature of the study and the consistency of the results obtained using a two-year landmark analysis and different outcome definitions, it is unlikely that the observed associations are due to reverse causation [40]. A Danish study comprising 244,464 individuals revealed that an increase in BMI in childhood was associated with an elevated risk of MASLD, regardless of the initial or attained BMI [41], which is consistent with our findings. The present study additionally revealed that body size gain was the most significant risk factor for MASLD in individuals who were thin in childhood and obese in adulthood like the results of another study [23]. However, these findings are at odds with those observed in 2,020 participants of the Cardiovascular Risk in Young Finns Study [14], which indicated that the risk of MASLD was highest in those who were overweight in both childhood and adulthood. This may be attributed to the fact that the study did not distinguish between childhood leanness and other body types, but instead grouped them under the category of “normal.” Furthermore, the study revealed that the risk of MASLD in individuals who were able to eliminate childhood obesity in adulthood was comparable to or lower than that of individuals who kept a normal body size.

The effects of child-to-adult body size change on hypertension and MASLD are analogous, though the impact on hypertension is more pronounced. This may be attributed to the fact that hypertension and MASLD have a shared underlying etiology [42, 43].Various metabolic and pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the association. It is evident that obesity-related metabolic dysfunction, characterized by adipocyte proliferation and hypertrophy, is associated with hypertension and progressive liver disease [44, 45]. Adipocyte hyperplasia serves as a protective mechanism against the metabolic dysfunction that can result from adipocyte hypertrophy [45]. Children exhibit a greater capacity for adipocyte hyperplasia than adults, and a leaner child’s body type may result in a lower number of adipocytes and hypertrophy, potentially increasing their vulnerability to metabolic dysfunction when they gain weight [46, 47]. Individuals with low body size had similar or higher (for females only) WHRs compared to those who were larger in childhood size, despite having a slightly smaller BMI, suggesting an impaired ability to store subcutaneous fat, which supports our interpretation to some extent. Furthermore, excessive weight gain may accelerate the development of liver pathologies during childhood, when the liver may be more susceptible to metabolic disturbances [44]. Moreover, obesity is associated with an elevated level of sympathetic nervous system activity [48], which increases the risk for cardiovascular disease by impairing endothelial, renal, and cardiac functions [49]. Nevertheless, weight loss restores the ability of the cardiovascular system to regulate stress responses and improves metabolic and inflammatory markers, with fewer associated physiological obstacles [50–52]. Such changes can mitigate morbidity in adults who successfully transition from childhood obesity.

The mediation effect is defined as the product of the effect of exposure on the mediator variable and the effect of the mediator variable on the outcome. Body size growth frequently indicates adverse changes in anthropometric measures and metabolic risk factors, especially FM. The mediation analyses yielded a hierarchical pattern, with FFM mediating the largest proportion of the effect of child-to-adult body size change on hypertension and MASLD, followed by WHR. In contrast, FM and metabolic markers played much more minor roles. The absence of significant NDE for MASLD (P = 0.171) implied that its association with body size change is almost entirely mediated by the mediators. In contrast, hypertension retained significant direct effects, possibly due to unmeasured mediators (e.g., sympathetic nervous system activation) or residual confounding One plausible explanation for the dominant mediating role of FFM is that it primarily reflects skeletal muscle mass, which can influence hemodynamic load through muscle pressor reflexes and cardiovascular physiological changes, thereby contributing to elevated blood pressure [53, 54]. Compared with FM, FFM has a much higher metabolic demand [55]. Researches in adults and children has demonstrated that FFM is a more significant predictor of blood pressure levels than FM [56]. Meanwhile, the process of body size change may be characterized by the phenomenon of myosteatosis [57], which reinforces the association of free FM with hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance, increasing the risk of MASLD [58]. WHR predominantly captures visceral adiposity, which is strongly linked to cardiometabolic diseases. Adipose tissue can release interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and leads to adiponectin reduction, resulting in the development of inflammation and insulin resistance [46], which in turn leads to hypertension [47] and MASLD [48]. Although the mediating proportion of WHR was lower than that of FFM, it still exceeded that of FM and other metabolic risk factors, especially in hypertension, suggesting a more significant mediating effect of fat distribution status than of FM on changes in body size, which is also consistent with the aforementioned phenomenon that WHR is similar or higher in adulthood in children with low body size compared with those with high body size. The weak mediation by triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and HbA1c (2–5%) suggests that these factors may act as downstream biomarkers rather than primary mediators because dyslipidemia and hyperglycemia often result from upstream disruptions in body composition [59]. Further research is necessary to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

While our findings emphasize the importance of maintaining healthy child-to-adult body size, weight management strategies must be approached cautiously given the limited success of existing interventions. Studies show that monitoring body size throughout the life cycle, coupled with early-life strategies such as promoting balanced nutrition and physical activity, facilitates the identification of potential risks and assists children in maintaining a healthy weight [60, 61]. Future research should explore effective, sustainable interventions and examine their impact on these outcomes in diverse populations, addressing gaps in implementation science and real-world effectiveness.

This study used long-term follow-up data from a large cohort, adjusting for a large range of potential confounders and selecting objective and accurate disease endpoints. Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of the results. However, this study is not exempt from limitations. Firstly, the child body size was determined by recall, which may be subject to bias. Nevertheless, the self-reported child body size variable exhibited a certain degree of consistency with the objectively measured child BMI [28]. Secondly, although previous studies have employed methodologies analogous to the present study for the categorization of body size in childhood and adulthood, they may be devoid of practical relevance and comparability with other studies. Thirdly, the exclusion of individuals with hypertension at baseline may result in an underestimation of the risk of early onset due to childhood obesity, given the strong association between childhood BMI and blood pressure. Fourthly, incident diseases were defined based on registers, which may lead to the omission of individuals who are not documented in the medical or death registries and those in pre-diagnostic phase. Fifthly, body size changes may lead to additional health concerns and more clinical examinations, potentially overestimating the risk of alterations in body size. Finally, the relatively healthy baseline metabolic status of the included population and the study’s focus on a predominantly Caucasian UK cohort limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations.

Conclusions

Our findings additionally demonstrate that child leanness exacerbates the risk of both hypertension and MASLD, with individuals who had low child body size and high adult body size exhibiting the highest disease risk. Within the follow-up period, adults who overcome childhood obesity in adulthood would mitigate the detrimental effects. This study indicate the necessity for a greater focus on maintaining healthy body size from childhood and the exploration of effective and sustainable management strategies is warranted.
