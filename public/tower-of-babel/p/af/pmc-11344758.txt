Simultaneous removal of malachite green and lead from water by consortium dry-biomasses of Bacillus licheniformis AG3 and Bacillus cereus M116
Authors: Md Imran Hasan, Surajit Bag, Dipankar Halder, Sutapa Bhowmik, Anindita Chakraborty, Alok Ghosh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic textile dye malachite green (MG) and heavy metals present in industrial wastewater are hazardous to the ecosystem. Bioremediation of dyes and heavy metals using dry-biomasses has advantages over chemical methods. This study screened an acclimatized, heavy metal-resistant, and dye-degrading Gram positive Bacillus licheniformis AG3 strain from the textile wastewater near Kolkata, West Bengal. The EDXRF analysis of this colored wastewater effluent showed 36.33 mg/L lead, significantly higher than the WHO recommendation. Previously, Bag et al. showed bioremediation of synthetic dyes using dry-biomass of Bacillus cereus M116 from an aqueous solution (Bag et al. Arch Microbiol 203(7):3811–3823, 2021). Here, a consortium of dry-biomasses of B. licheniformis AG3 and B. cereus M116 strains (1:1 w/w ratio) was prepared for the simultaneous removal of lead and MG from wastewater. Statistical optimization determines that the pH, initial concentration of contaminants, and dry-biomass concentrations are critical for bioremediation under batch procedures. Further, optimization using the response surface methodology showed that 0.01% consortium dry-biomasses eliminated a maximum of 99.35% MG and 96.01% lead (II) within 6 h. SEM–EDS and FTIR confirmed a strong surface biosorption. Furthermore, a fixed-bed biofilter column of the consortium dry-biomasses was prepared, which was able to remove 98.1% MG and 98.5% lead at the 0.5–1 mL/min flow rate. Together, this study developed a biofilter with a consortium dry biomasses of B. licheniformis AG3 and B. cereus M116 for the simultaneous removal of MG and lead from wastewater.Keywords: Consortium dry-biomasses, Malachite green, Lead (II), RSM, Fixed-bed biofilter column, Bacillus licheniformis AG3Subject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology, Environmental sciences, Engineering

Introduction

Dyes impart color in life. Currently, more than ten thousand dyes are commercially used in the textile, pharmaceutical, food, cosmetics, and paper industries1,2. The textile or tannery industry is the largest purchaser of dyestuffs. At the time of coloration, 10–16% of textile dyes cannot bind with the product and are disposed of into the water bodies. These water contaminants are further mixed with sewage, rivers and thus destroy the ecosystem. Moreover, dyes may also alter or degrade to form highly carcinogenic products3,4. A cationic synthetic dye, malachite green (MG) is frequently used in the textile industry5. MG and leucomalachite green (an MG metabolite), promotes the formation of reactive oxygen species generation that leads to cause chromosomal alteration, cancer, renal and pulmonary toxicity6,7.

In addition to dyes, contamination of heavy metals is another major toxicant of wastewater. Heavy metals encompassing aquatic systems undergo food chain accumulation and cause affliction in higher trophic levels. It tends to be impossible to decontaminate heavy metals from wastewater8. Many industries such as textile, tannery, automobile use various types of heavy metals, including lead (II)9, which cause irreversible damage to the kidney, nervous, and reproductive system. Chronic exposure to low levels of lead in children may decrease their intelligence capacity. The permissible limits of lead in drinking water according to the US EPA is 0.05 mg/L (0.05 ppm) and according to WHO 0.01 mg/L. The acceptable limits of lead in wastewater according to the US EPA and the WHO are 5 mg/L and 0.01 mg/L, respectively10.

Past studies have shown that bioremediation through microorganisms is a cost-effective and environment-friendly measure to eliminate dyes11, and heavy metals. Several studies focused on individual microorganisms biosorbing the dyes or heavy metals from wastewater1,5,12. Algae, bacteria, fungi, and yeasts are potential metal biosorbents. Previously research article showed dry biomass of Bacillus sp. is promising as a biosorbent for the bio-removal of dyes such as acridine orange, MG or heavy metals like chromium, lead from wastewater13. The Bacillus sp. cell wall is comprised of an outer thick peptidoglycan layer containing carboxyl, hydroxyl, phosphate, amino, and amide groups14. Also, Bacillus sp. contains an overall negative charge on their cell surface15, which plays an effective role in the biosorption of cationic dyes like acridine orange and malachite green5,16. Although consortium of the bacteria and other microbes has been successfully used as biofertilizers, pesticides, biofuel production, lignocelluloses degradation based on augmentation of biological activity of the symbiotic relationship in the concerned consortium17–19 but efficiency for the simultaneous biosorption of both dye and heavy metal has not been tested. Recently, viable algae and bacteria together were used as a consortium for the bioremediation process20,21. However, dead dry biomasses of Bacillus sp. or other microbial species consortium to remove both carcinogenic dye and toxic heavy metal from wastewater have not been explored. Dead dry biomasses have many advantages, including high environmental resistance, greater toxicity tolerance, reasonably fast regeneration, reuse, and safely can be used without serious biohazards22.

This study found that the textile effluents in West Bengal, India are heavily contaminated with dyes and heavy metals lead (Pb). A salt-tolerant and dye degrading Gram positive Bacillus licheniformis AG3 strain was isolated from the textile wastewater effluent and deposited in MTCC (accession no. 13371). As wastewater contaminants vary significantly among various places; a single bacterium may not be ideal for the bioremediation of a broad spectrum of dyes and heavy metals. To overcome, this limitation a consortium of dead dry biomasses of our newly identified B. licheniformis AG3 and previously reported Bacillus cereus M116 strains1 were prepared to remove the MG and lead in a one-step biosorption process. The goals of this study were to statistically optimize the potential of consortium B. licheniformis and B. cereus dry biomasses as the biosorbents for the simultaneous removal of MG and lead from water using PBD and followed by RSM under the batch procedure. The novelty of the current study is articulated in establishing a consortium dry biomasses-based biofilter for simultaneous removal of dyes and lead from wastewater with superior biosorption capacity.

Results

EDXRF elemental analysis of the textile wastewater

Colored textile wastewater were collected from a textile industry at Howrah, near Kolkata, West Bengal, and the analysis of the elements was performed by a Micro EDXRF analyzer. We found seven major elements were present in the hot air oven-dried wastewater sediment, where chlorine (Cl) about 1025 mg/L concentration with 84.13%, potassium (K) about 283 mg/L with 9.17%, sulfur (S) about 98 mg/L with 4.41% and iron (Fe) about 42.15 mg/L concentration with 0.28% were listed in Fig. 1. Among toxic heavy metals or metalloids, lead (Pb), and arsenic (As) were detected. The concentrations of cytotoxic lead and arsenic were measured at about 36.33 mg/L (0.12%) and 7.04 mg/L (0.06%), respectively. According to WHO and US EPA, the desired limit of wastewater lead is 0.01 mg/L and 5 mg/L10, and for arsenic, it is 0.05 mg/L22 in the wastewater. Hence, screening of the heavy metals resistant and MG decolorizing microorganisms from this wastewater was performed.

Isolation, identification, and screening of potential dye decolorizing and heavy metal-resistant bacteria

In the present study, at least 32 microbial colonies were isolated from textile wastewater, among them one lead resistance halophytic, lichen-like biomass forming B. licheniformis AG3 was selected for bioremediation because of its highest MG removal capacity, salt and heavy metal tolerance, rapid biomass forming abilities. Gram staining confirmed that the microorganism is a rod-shaped, Gram-positive bacteria (Supplementary Fig. S1a–e). The isolated B. licheniformis AG3 has multi-heavy metal resistant and halotolerance properties. This strain was able to grow in 8% NaCl and 1000 mg/L of Pb2+, Hg2+, Cr6+ or MG concentrations at 37 °C (Supplementary Fig. S1f). 16S rRNA gene sequence was compared in the NCBI database where the strain B. licheniformis AG3 had 100% query coverage with Bacillus licheniformis strain P8 B2; SRCM 103583; ATCC 14580; SRCM 103529 with E-value of 0.00. Further, the evolutionary relationship of the strain was assumed using the Neighbor-joining tree method using Tajima-Nei model (1000 bootstrap). Bacillus licheniformis strain P8 B2 (96,382–97,887 bp) and B. licheniformis strain AG3 were found to belong to the same group in the phylogenetic tree, indicating that the two strains are homologs of one another. The GenBank accession no. OR166328 and MTCC accession number 13371 confirmed that the isolated strain is B. licheniformis and 99% similarity with other NCBI strains.

Evaluate the biosorption of MG and lead (II) by dry biomasses of consortium bacteria

We previously showed dry biomasses of the B. cereus M116 strain isolated from the Kolkata industrial area is an excellent biosorbent for carcinogenic acridine orange dye1. Other laboratories also showed B. cereus M116 strain can bioremediate multiple heavy metals and dyes5. Therefore, in this study, we prepared a consortium dry biomasses of our newly isolated B. licheniformis AG3 and previously used B. cereus M116 strains (schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 2) and determined the best parameters for simultaneous bioremediation of MG and lead (II) from water.

Selection of parameters for decolorization of MG using PBD matrix

Plackett–Burman design (PBD) is an effective statistical matrix to determine the variables that significantly affect any process. Here, we performed PBD models for MG/lead removal by consortium dry biomasses with the two-level fractional factorial design using Minitab software. The influence of five parameters i.e., pH, MG concentration (mg/L), biomass dosage, temperature, and contact time were analyzed. Every independent variable consisted of lower (− 1) and higher (+ 1) values (Supplementary Table S1a).

The PBD design with the Experimental and the Predicted values of MG biosorption are shown in Supplementary Table S1b. The maximum decolorization of MG was found 94.8% at pH 7, MG concentration 200 mg/L, biomass dosage 50 mg/L. The PBD model was significant with a p-value of 0.000 (p-value < 0.05). Multiple regression analysis followed a first-order polynomial equation.

Statistical optimization of MG and lead (II) bio-removal by consortium bacteria

Response surface methodology (RSM) is the best mathematical tool for optimizing the values of the biosorption process. Significant parameters i.e. pH, initial MG concentration (mg/L), and biomass dosage (mg/L) were obtained from the PBD matrix and further optimized by central composite design (CCD) design. Three independent parameters- pH, initial MG concentration, and biomass dosage (Supplementary Table S3a) with experimental and predicted values after MG decolorization are shown in Supplementary Table S3b. We observed a synergistic effect between MG decolorization and three variables in Supplementary Fig. S2a. The maximum 99.32% decolorization of MG was found at pH 7 when initial 400 mg/L MG concentration and 50 mg/L biomass were present.

Simultaneously biosorption of MG and lead (II) by consortium dry biomasses and validation in the industrial effluent

Individual RSM analysis showed that MG and lead (II) removals occurred optimally at the basic pH 9.86 and acidic pH 3.29, respectively. But pH variations had limited effect on lead biosorption compared to MG biosorption (Supplementary Figs. S2–S3). Thus, changing the pH from acidic to basic range has less impact on lead removal. On the contrary, MG removal drastically decreases at the acidic pH. Therefore, we tried to perform RSM analysis at pH 9.86, but the addition of lead (II) into the MG solution at pH 9.86 immediately precipitated both contaminants. So, we conduct simultaneous biosorption under neutral pH 7 where both contaminants are stable and showed significant up to 99% MG removal.

ANOVA analysis (Table 3) with p-value < 0.0001 for MG decolorization (%) indicates that the RSM model is highly significant. There is a positive relationship between the parameters and the decolorization, as indicated by the correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9837.

The numerical optimization of simultaneous removal of both MG and lead (II) was MG concentration 400 mg/L, lead (II) concentration 150 mg/L, and biomass dosage 110 mg/L. The conditions result in the bio-removal of 97.3% and 90.15% with DF of 1.00 for MG and lead (II) respectively (Fig. 4g).

Further, we validated optimized conditions in the raw colored textile wastewater effluent. Spectrophotometric wavelength scan of green colored textile effluent showed absorption maxima at 617 nm which is similar to the pure MG solution (Himedia, GRM952). The removal of textile dye and lead (II) by consortium dry biomasses was up to 99.01% and 97.75% at pH 7.0, respectively, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4a–b with p-value < 0.05. Together, we found biosorption of textile effluent by consortium dry biomasses was extraordinarily effective in batch process.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectrums of control dry biomasses shown in Supplementary Fig. S5a. The MG-loaded, and lead (II) loaded consortium dry biomasses were shown in Supplementary Fig. S5b–c. The surface of control consortium dry biomasses comprises phosphorus monoxide, hydroxyl, and amino groups. The broad stretching vibration frequency of –OH was observed near 3781 cm−1 and 3604 cm−123. The peak at 3519 cm−1, 3444 cm−1, and 3284 cm−1 is involved in –OH, –NH stretching vibration24. The peak at 2934 cm−1 is the indicator of the alkyl chain –CH stretching vibration25. The sharp peak observed around 1657 cm−1, is the characteristic of C=O stretching of amide groups26. The peak at 1451 cm−1 is known as –NH binding of the amine group. The peak at 1232 cm−1 may be attributed to phosphorus monoxide (P=O)25. The peak at 1068 cm−1 is known as –C–O symmetric stretching. Each functional group has a specific biosorption band, thus producing an indisputable peak. The shifted peak after MG and lead (II) removal and the corresponding possible functional group are listed in Table 5. Also, a new peak that arises in the lead (II) loaded biomass at 2112 cm−1 represents the acetylene group (C≡C)26, and MG loaded biomass at 2370 cm−1 represents the imines, or Schiff bases group (C=N)27. Therefore, FTIR spectra of consortium dry biomasses confirmed the involvement of hydroxyl, amine, phosphorus monoxide, and carbonyl groups for the biosorption of MG and lead (II).

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of consortium dry biomasses after MG and lead (II) biosorption

Consortium dry biomasses morphology was characterized by SEM–EDS before and after simultaneous biosorption of MG and lead (II) from aqueous solution with initial concentrations of 400 mg/L and 150 mg/L. Figure 5a,b showed SEM–EDS of the pristine and the MG and lead (II) loaded biomass after biosorption. Pristine biomass showed smooth cell surfaces, whereas significant binding of lead (II) on cell surfaces of dry biomasses was noticed in Fig. 5b. Quantitative analysis showed that in aqueous solution only 0.1–0.2% of residual MG and lead (II) were present after biosorption. Further, we observed that even after washing with double distilled water, dry biomasses retained up to 98% and 96% MG and lead on the cell surface as shown in Supplementary Fig. S6a–d.

Simultaneous removal of MG and lead (II) by the continuous column

To develop the continuous column based biosorption, a fixed-bed biofilter column was prepared by mixing the consortium dry biomasses of B. licheniformis and B. cereus and glass beads (5:2 w/w ratio) as shown in Fig. 6. Biosorption experiments were carried out as the function of initial MG and lead (II) concentrations, column diameter with the volume of effluent to determine the maximum exhaustive capacity of consortium dry biomasses column. We found that in the first cycle MG and lead (II) removed up to 87.2% and 82.5% with a specific flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. In the second cycle MG and lead (II) removed up to 98.1% and 98.5% with a flow rate of 1 mL/min as shown in Supplementary Fig. S7a–b. After the experiment was done the column was also washed with 5 column volume of double distilled water and no dye or heavy metals were eluted. We also prepared a glass bead column without adding dry biomasses as a negative control, and no biosorption was observed. Hence fixed-bed biofilter column of consortium dry biomasses removed both dye and heavy metal contaminates from industrial wastewater.

Discussion

Several heavy metal resistance viable microorganisms including Halomonas xianhensis SUR30828, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ASU6a29, showed biosorption properties by biosorbent heavy metals on extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), binding of heavy metal by siderophores, etc30. Further, dry biomasses of bacteria also adsorb heavy metals and dyes and has been used as a green method since low-valuable nontoxic material is used as simple one-step batch procedure. The advantages of dry biomass over viable cells include higher toxicity tolerance properties, greater metal biosorbance, easy statistical modeling for biosorbent reactors, reuse biomass, and ready to apply in industrial wastewater31. A consortium dry biomasses are more efficient as different microorganisms are used to increase their efficiency for decontamination wastewater.

This study developed a statistically RSM modeling of simultaneous removal of MG/lead (II) from wastewater. Our newly developed consortium dry biomasses showed 96–99% removal efficiency in both aqueous and real textile wastewater within 6 h at room temperature under the batch procedure. In comparison the single bacterium strain of B. licheniformis AG3 were removed both MG and lead (II) about 75% and 62% and B. cereus M116 about 82% and 85% from aqueous solution (data not shown). We also prepared a fixed-bed biofilter, which showed ~ 98% efficiency in removing both MG and lead (II) simultaneously from an aqueous solution. The possible mechanism of electrostatic interactions (pH dependent) between the positively charged MG and negatively charged biomass may be the underlying cause of efficient biosorption32,33. Removal of heavy metals like lead (II) by EPS on their cell surface is also possible as our consortium showed a positive polysaccharides test followed by the Dubois method shown in Supplementary Fig. S8. Till now consortium dry biomasses of Bacillus sp. never been tested as biofilter for removal of both MG and lead (II) simultaneously from wastewater. Our consortium dry biomasses capable of removing both MG and lead (II) significantly in compare to other biosorbent reported earlier (Table 6).

Conclusion and future prospective

In this study, consortium dry biomasses of B. licheniformis AG3 and B. cereus M116 strains (1:1 (w/w)) were used to optimize the maximum biosorption efficiencies of the consortium to simultaneously eliminate MG and lead (II) from aqueous solution. RSM modeling showed that in single biosorption under batch procedure conditions, MG biosorption capacities (with initial 1.09 mM or 400 mg/L) ranges from 0.35 to 0.88 mmol g−1 and lead (II) biosorption capacities (with initial 0.6 mM or 200 mg/L) ranges from 0.16 to 0.45 mmol g−1, respectively. Further, validation of the biosorption capacities in real textile wastewater showed up to 99.01% and 97.75% biosorption of dyes and lead, respectively. In a dynamic system, fixed-bed biofilter column removed both MG and lead (II) with capacities found to be 0.69 mmol g−1 and 0.55 mmol g−1, respectively with a flow rate of 0.5–1 mL/min. MG and lead (II) bioremediation have been studied for the last decades. However, limited success was achieved and removal percentage was reported from 40 to 95% in individual biosorption as listed in Table 6. In compare to other biosorbent our biosorbent for the first time eliminate both MG and lead (II) simultaneously from aqueous solution up to 99% and 96% with more significant than the other biosorbent. Future research should be performed in scaling up and estimate the reusability of the fixed-bed biofilter columns production, so that can be used in the industry as a green eco-friendly and cost-effective method to decontaminate both dyes and heavy metals.

Materials and methods

EDXRF analysis

The textile wastewater was sampled from the industrial location in Howrah, West Bengal, located close to Kolkata. The water sample was aseptically collected in a 500 ml high-grade plastic bottle and taken to the lab for further analysis. To elucidate level of heavy metal present in textile wastewater EDXRF was performed. The elements present in wastewater were measured from the water sediments prepared by oven drying followed by a run in a Micro EDXRF (HORIBA Scientific XGT-7200, Kyoto, Japan) with the Rhodium (Rh) anode target X-ray tube (maximum voltage 50 kV, current 1 mA). The transmitted X-ray detector is Nai (Tl) scintillator, and the fluorescent X-ray detector is a peltier-cooled silicon drift detector (SDD). The analysis was carried out with an air chamber between the source and sample (Max 300 mm × 300 mm × 80 mm under vacuum).

Morphological and molecular identification of bacterial strain

The heavy metal and dye degrading microorganism was screened as described previously46. Briefly, microorganisms from wastewater were cultured in 2% Luria–Bertani agar supplemented with 1000 mg/L MG solution and 1000 mg/L K2Cr2O7 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). The plates were then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The pure culture was obtained by repeated streaking on an agar plate at 37 °C for 24 h. Isolated colonies were screened to identify multi-heavy metal resistant and dye-degrading properties.

Molecular identification of the isolate was performed by sequencing 16S rRNA using universal primer 8F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-CGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Briefly, genomic DNA is isolated by the Qiagen kit. The PCR reaction total volume of 10 µL contains, master mix 5 µl (Promega, Madison, USA), Forward primer (8F) of 1 µL, Reverse Primer (1492R) of 1 µL, DNA sample of 3 µL. The reaction set up initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 50 °C for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1.30 min, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min with 30 cycles. The amplified DNA fragments were separated on agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the Qiagen gel extraction kit. The purified PCR samples were sent to Eurofins Genomic India Pvt. Ltd for sequencing. The DNA sequence was processed in BLASTN, NCBI GenBank database to determine the evolutionary relationship of the strain. 16S rRNA sequence of AG3 is shown in Supplementary Table S5. MEGA 11 was used for multiple sequence alignment by MUSCLE programming and the construction of a phylogenetic tree (Neighbor-joining method). The sequence was submitted to the NCBI GenBank database and the Microbial Type Culture Collection (MTCC), Chandigarh, India.

Preparation of consortium biosorbent

Isolated B. licheniformis AG3 strain was cultured on LB medium. The B. cereus M116 (MTCC 5521) cells were cultured on modified nutrient medium having composition g/L: beef extract 1.0; peptone 5.0; yeast extract 2.0; sodium chloride 5.0; pH 6.5, according to Bera et al. Liquid cultures of the individual strains was grown at 37 °C. Cells were pelleted separately and washed with double distilled water and dry biomass was obtained by oven drying at 55–60 °C until it reached stationary followed by powdering in a mortar pestle. The dry biomasses of two bacteria mixed in a 1:1 (w/w) ratio to prepare a consortium dry biomasses19.

The biosorption under batch procedure

The biosorption was conducted in 100 mL flasks with 25 ml working volume to evaluate the efficiency of consortium dry biomasses for removal of MG and lead (II) from aqueous solution. The initial pH has been adjusted with the addition of 0.1 N NaOH or 0.1 N HCl. The quantity of dye and heavy metal uptake by the dry biomasses was calculated using the removal percentage.

Preparation of MG and lead solution

Extra pure MG (Himedia, India. Catalog no. GRM952) dye stock solution of 1000 mg/L was prepared by dissolving 1000 mg of MG in 1000 mL of double distilled water and diluting the dye solution for the desired concentration during experiments. The concentration of MG in the solution was measured by UV–Vis at 617 nm (λmax). The molecular structure of MG is shown in Supplementary Fig. S9a. Lead (II) stock solution of 1000 mg/L was prepared by dissolving Pb(NO3)2 in 1000 mL of double distilled water. The concentration of lead (II) in solution was measured with atomic absorption spectroscopy (Agilent 200 series AA, USA). The molecular structure of lead (II) is shown in Supplementary Fig. S9b.

Optimization of individual parameters using PBD for MG and lead (II) bio-removal

PBD is statistical method for selecting the independent variable from multiple number of parameters that affect any process34. Five independent variables pH, Temperature (°C), initial MG concentration (mg/L)/lead (II) concentration (mg/L), biomass dosage (mg/L), and contact time (min) were selected at a 95% confidence level (p-value 0.05). The PBD independent variables were selected by using Minitab statistical software (version 20.3). Twelve experimental trials of five parameters were performed to find out the independent variables that significantly influence the bio-removal of MG and lead (II) by consortium dry biomass.

Evaluate statistical significance by RSM using CCD of biosorption of MG and lead (II)

For assessing the most important variables and identifying best optimized conditions, RSM is the most effective statistical method35. Using a three-way ANOVA test, statistically significant tests were used to further assess the experiment's validation. All the calculations and the graphical 3D models of three independent interaction parameters were determined using the Design-Expert software (version 13.0.2.0).

Determination of functional group present in consortium dry biomasses surface by FTIR

FTIR is the tool for understanding the functional group present on the surfaces of any kind of living and non-living thing. FTIR assembles spectral data in a wide range at a time14,36. Consortium dry biomasses were freeze-dried (lyophilized) to make sure no moisture was present in the sample, and the pellet was mixed with KBr (FTIR grade) in a 1:1000 ratio in an agate motor, and absorbance was recorded near 4000–500 cm−1 using a FTIR (PerkinElmer Frontier, USA). FTIR spectra were analyzed using Origin Pro 8.5 software.

Determination of morphological changes of consortium bacteria by SEM–EDS

SEM is used for the study of consortium bacterial morphology to produce an image by scanning the surface of biomass with a focused electron beam37and the presence of lead in biomass surface was determined by EDS38. Simultaneous MG (400 mg/L) and lead (250 mg/L) loaded pellet were collected by centrifugation at 5000 g for 15 min and washed with double distilled water. Control consortium biomasses were taken as control. The biomasses (control, MG-loaded, lead (II)-loaded) were freeze-dried (lyophilized) to make sure no moisture was present in the sample. Samples were gold coating and visualized under SEM–EDS (HITACHI SU3800, Tokyo, Japan).

Experimental design of fixed-bed biofilter column

The column biosorption experiment was performed in a 3 ml syringe column with a bed height of 2 cm and an internal diameter of 1 cm. The column was packed with biosorbent between two layers of glass wool. A Whatman filter paper was placed at the bottom of the column to prevent any loss of biosorbent and provide mechanical support to the biosorbent bed during the biosorption process. The column was filled with bed mass (w/w) of consortium dry biomasses and glass bead (Sigma, Catalog no. G8772) about 500 mg and 200 mg, respectively. A peristaltic pump was used to maintain a constant flow rate during the biosorption process. A rubber cork was provided at the top of the column to support the inlet pipes. After setting up the column, the double distilled water 5 column volume was passed through the column about 0.5 ml/min of flow rate. Then, 25 ml of MG and lead (II) solution of known concentration, 400 mg/L and 200 mg/L, at pH 7 was run through the column at the flow rate of 0.5–1 ml/min. The filtrated water was collected from the lower bottom and reloaded on the top of the column. The MG and lead (II) concentrations from the flow through were measured using UV/Vis and atomic absorption spectroscopy.
