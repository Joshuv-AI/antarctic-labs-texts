American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy best practice statements on the sharing of endoscopic images and videos on social media
Authors: Walter W. Chan, Rashmi R. Advani, Mohammad Bilal, Aline Charabaty, Karl K. Kwok, Janese S. Laster, Alexander Perelman, Shazia M. Siddique, Judy Trieu, Mfoniso D. Umoren, Austin L. Chiang, American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Social Media Task Force
Published: 2024

Introduction

Social media and its host platforms have evolved in many ways since its inception, from its original purpose to the supported media formats. The purpose of social media in medicine has transformed beyond casual exchanges to both structured and ad hoc education.1, 2, 3, 4 Seemingly overnight, the COVID-19 pandemic created a reliance on remote learning and underscored the impact of social media as an educational tool for remote audiences.5,6 Moreover, social media has also been found to be an effective tool for dissemination of research,7, 8, 9 with investigators, institutions, and scientific journals increasingly engaging in social media to promote new discoveries and innovation.

As technology has improved with time, media formats supported online and on social media platforms have advanced to include images and videos. This has allowed health professionals to pair clinical discussions with visual aids, often with the intent of educating each other and the general public. Many of these images and videos within gastroenterology are derived from clinical care, including radiographs and endoscopic footage. The use of these images has introduced, among other considerations, a question around balancing the importance and need for education on social media with the imperative to protect patient privacy.

Along with the growing functionality of social media for communication and education within the profession, spillover to the general public raises a number of key concerns. Despite even the best of intentions, at times medical education content on social media can quickly extend beyond a professional audience. In fact, patients often use social media to gain medical information and aid in disease management,10, 11, 12 and social media is increasingly leveraged as a tool for patient education as well.13, 14, 15, 16 However, the rapidity and brevity of social media can potentially lead to a lack of appropriate context and misinterpretation of the presented information in various ways and may even contribute to unintended perpetuation of health misinformation.17, 18, 19

Despite the widespread use of social media and its rapid adoption by health professionals, there remains a lack of guidance among the professional gastroenterology community, particularly with regard to digital media contents. This may be in part due to the rapid evolution and growing number and complexity of social media platforms. In a recent study of Twitter/X (X Corp, Bastrop, Texas, USA) posts with endoscopic images and videos, issues of patient confidentiality and potential conflicts of interests were found to be insufficiently addressed in an important proportion.20 Overall, there may be several major issues and pitfalls associated with professional social media use by gastroenterologists, including inadvertent disclosure of protected health information and violation of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA), misinterpretation of content aimed at other professionals by the lay public that may lead to misinformation, and limited transparency related to potential conflicts of interest. The aim of this study was to confront gaps in our understanding of how to approach social media education by developing consensus statements specifically with regard to the sharing of endoscopic images and videos. This was executed by using a modified Delphi process, convening a panel of experienced social media content creators within the gastroenterology community diverse in clinical experience, practice setting, gender, race, and ethnicity.

Methods

The RAND/University of California, Los Angeles Delphi Appropriate Methodology was applied to develop the expert-based best practice statements on sharing of endoscopic images and videos on social media. A modified Delphi approach was used with the assistance of the members of the ASGE Social Media Task Force, which comprised invited gastroenterologists who are experienced and regular educators on different social media platforms. The Delphi process was organized by a lead investigator (W.W.C.) who did not participate in the voting process. The Chair of the Task Force (A.L.C.) and the lead investigator composed the initial statements for voting by the panel. The voting process was conducted using the SurveyMonkey system (San Mateo, Calif, USA), which allowed each participant to complete the survey only once. The results of each voting round were collected and analyzed by the nonvoting lead investigator. The Task Force agreed a priori to organize the Delphi process through a maximum of 4 voting rounds. After each voting round, the Task Force convened to discuss the results and revise the statements that did not reach the predetermined agreement threshold. With each iteration, statements that failed to reach agreement were either improved until consensus thresholds were met or ultimately discarded.

Formation of expert voting panel

The voting panel comprised the members of the ASGE Social Media Task Force. A diverse group of gastroenterologists who are experienced users and recognized educational content creators on various social media platforms were invited to participate in the Task Force and serve as voting members of the panel. To ensure representation of the wide-ranging backgrounds of social media users in gastroenterology, the panel included 9 voting members with diverse experiences (trainees and board-certified attendings), age, race and ethnicity, subspecialties, and practice settings (private practice and academic institutions). All members participated through the final round of the study.

Formulation of potential consensus statements

The initial potential statements were conceived by the lead investigator (W.W.C.) and Chair of the Social Media Task Force (A.L.C.) based on the extensive review of existing medical literature, review of social media policies of other fields, and legal requirements on patient protected information, such as the HIPAA. The statements aimed to focus on practical issues related to the posting of endoscopy-related contents such as images and videos on social media that would be relevant to both practicing gastroenterologists and trainees, given its increasing use in education and distribution of medical information. The lead investigator and Task Force Chair composed 20 candidate consensus statements in total covering topics related to patient privacy, consent, clinical context, professionalism, bias, attributions, conflict of interest, and institutional policies.

Voting and analysis of statement validity

During each voting round, panelists were asked to score each proposed statement using a visual analog scale with scores ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 100 (strongly agree). Statements with rating of ≥80/100 by at least 80% of expert panelists were considered as reaching consensus and, therefore, accepted to the final best practice guidelines. Statements receiving 60% to 80% of votes with rating ≥80/100 were discussed during scheduled meetings of the panel in between voting rounds, and revised statements were formulated for additional voting based on panelists’ feedback and comments. If statements failed to reach at least 60% consensus for rating ≥80/100, they were discarded and not subjected to further revision or voting. Results

The expert panel comprised 9 members, including 3 trainees, 3 private practice gastroenterologists, 2 academic clinical faculty, and 1 health services researcher. There were 5 (55.6%) women and 4 (44.4%) men, with 5 (55.6%) Asian, 2 (22.2%) white, and 2 (22.2%) black members. Three panel members were actively engaged in medical education, including 2 currently serving as associate fellowship program directors. The most common social media platforms with active professional engagements by panel members were Twitter/X (9/9, 100%), Instagram (6/9, 66.7%), and LinkedIn (5/9, 55.6%) (Supplementary Table 1). The expert panel voted initially on the 20 proposed statements. In round 1, consensus agreement was reached in 16 statements with high validity in at least 80% of votes, whereas 1 statement failed to reach 60% of votes with rating ≥80/100 and was discarded. There were 3 borderline statements, with 66.7% of votes reaching consensus agreement. These 3 statements were discussed during a teleconference by the panel, further refined, and transferred to round 2 for additional voting. During round 2, all 3 statements reached consensus agreement with high validity.

Overall, 19 statements reached consensus agreement and were accepted, covering 8 important areas of social media use by gastroenterologists. These include institutional policies (1 statement), patient privacy (5 statements), consent (2 statements), clinical context of social media posts (3 statements), professionalism (2 statements), bias (1 statement), appropriate attribution (3 statements), and financial disclosures (2 statements). Discussion

Social media has been increasingly adopted by health care professionals for medical education, research, health promotion, and other public health efforts. Although benefits and opportunities have been identified with health care social media, there are also potential risks and pitfalls with regard to liability and ethical implications in the areas of patient confidentiality, consent, attribution, professionalism, and conflicts of interest.21, 22, 23, 24 Some medical organizations and societies have provided some general guidance on the professional use of social media for health care providers,25, 26, 27 although no best practice guidelines currently exist that are specific to gastroenterologists. Given the unique and important role of endoscopy in gastroenterology and the rapidly evolving capabilities and complexity of social media platforms, the development of best practices on the sharing of endoscopic media contents on social media is needed to help guide digestive health professionals. To address this unmet need, the ASGE Social Media Task Force used the modified Delphi process to formulate a set of consensus best practice statements on the sharing of endoscopic images and videos on social media, which discussed issues ranging from patient privacy and consent to professionalism and conflicts of interests. It is important to note that these best practice statements represent expert consensus to provide general guidance and do not serve as legal advice.

Best practice statements

Institutional policies

(1)Health professionals should adhere to their institutional social media policies when posting endoscopic images or videos.

(1)Health professionals should adhere to their institutional social media policies when posting endoscopic images or videos.

Health professionals should adhere to their institutional social media policies when posting endoscopic images or videos.

Health systems and medical practices ultimately govern social media practices for their employees. Although these best practice statements aim to provide guidance for health care providers in sharing endoscopic content on social media, particularly for those whose hospital/practice does not have formal policies, clinicians should be aware of and ensure full compliance to any institutional policies that exist, even if they differ from these statements. In the events where institutional policies and these statements offer different guidance, it may be most prudent to follow the stricter and more conservative of the two, as long as there is no violation of institutional policies, which should take precedence. Open dialogue with those who govern institutional social media policies is encouraged to ensure a mutual understanding of the educational intention of one’s social media platform. To support this intention, institutional support may be available to craft appropriate processes and consent forms to minimize individual and institutional liability. In recent years, some licensing boards have also adopted their own general social media guidelines. Health care providers engaging in professional social media use should also consult and be familiar with any existing guidelines of their respective licensing board to ensure compliance.

The HIPAA was enacted before most mainstream social media platforms used today were in existence.28 The Department of Health and Human Services Office of Civil Rights enforces compliance with privacy and security rules laid forth by HIPAA. HIPAA explicitly outlines identifiers protected by its De-Identification Standard.

In addition, patient identity may potentially be revealed through other references not included in the De-Identification Standard, including temporal descriptors (date and time of procedure or hospitalization, including timestamps on video footage) and/or other visible cues on imaging such as rare jewelry pieces (eg, family heirlooms, unique necklaces, and piercings) or anatomic characteristics (eg, anatomic variants, fractures, postsurgical anatomy, distinct postural findings, and dental work) specific to an individual. In a study of 956 tweets containing endoscopic images or videos, 177 (18.5%) were found to be at risk for confidentiality noncompliance, although only 13 (1.4%) included information clearly protected by the HIPAA De-Identification Standard, whereas the vast majority of at-risk posts involved the inclusion of or reference to the procedure date.20 There have also been examples of disciplinary and legal action taken as a result of media outlets being able to deduce patient identity from context clues and circumstantial information,29 even leading to patient safety concerns. Should the educational benefit of specific images be deemed significant, they can only be used if appropriate patient consents have been obtained. In the cases where patients explicitly permitted the use of specific images or any identifiable information, such consents should be disclosed in the relevant posts with short statements such as “photo posted with patient consent.”

Because engagement (eg, likes, comments, saving, or sharing content) on social media increases the visibility of social media content, these actions should be avoided to prevent perpetuation of the material if patient confidentiality has been compromised. Furthermore, reinforcement of such behavior through positive engagement with this content should be avoided.

Consultation with institutional communications and medicolegal teams may be required to determine content monitoring practices, to craft patient consent disclosures that may potentially be overlaid on content, or if a public response is required for privacy-related complaints (either publicly on social media platforms or directly submitted to the institution). If a HIPAA-related complaint is submitted online in response to a social media post, the content should first be removed immediately.

As is performed with endoscopic images for journal publication, appropriate patient consent should be obtained before public dissemination. However, in contrast with images intended for scientific publication, not all standard consent forms include a specific clause explicitly seeking permission for social media use. Consent forms should reflect the intention to share images on public platforms beyond a limited professional viewership. Therefore, not only should consent be obtained, but specific clauses should be drafted for social media purposes. This does not have to be a separate consent form or document. Rather, specific statements seeking permission for social media use can be integrated into the institutions’ existing clinical consent forms. Consent should be obtained in a culturally appropriate manner in the patient’s preferred language to ensure the greatest amount of transparency to the patient about the potential sharing of their images.

Before posting any images/videos on social media, a physician should consult with their institution to ensure that the informed consent contains language seeking permission for social media use. If a physician is not employed with an institution, then a review of the informed consent with outside counsel is recommended to ensure that language seeking permission for social media use is included.

When images and videos are used for educational purposes, appropriate clinical context is necessary to inform the learner of the applicability of the presented scenario. Without appropriate clinical context, lessons learned may be applied incorrectly and lead to potential patient harm. In practice, the nuances of clinical decision-making may depend on varying circumstances (eg, family history, concomitant medications, procedural history, previous attempts with therapies, social support, local resources, cultural considerations, and more) that may otherwise be omitted in abridged social media posts. Furthermore, special attention should be paid toward controversial subject matter where recommendations may differ among professional society guidelines.

Interesting procedural footage and radical endoscopic approaches are often shared on social media to introduce new ways to address specific, and often difficult, situations. However, these approaches can often fall outside of guideline recommendations, or involve investigational or off-label use of medical devices or tools. This can inadvertently lead patients to feel as though they are not receiving the best care if they have not been offered a procedure with an off-label medical device, when in reality it is still investigational and potentially unavailable to the patient. Therefore, appropriate disclaimers are warranted. Furthermore, given the global reach of social media, there may be additional disclosure necessary to account for inconsistencies in device approvals to discern on-label versus off-label use in different countries.

(13)Appropriate warnings should be considered in the post for potentially sensitive or graphic endoscopic videos or images.

In recent years, there has been growing use of humor and lighthearted approaches toward content creation to improve the relatability and reach of educational social media content. Given the public nature of social media, individuals should approach humor carefully and maintain professionalism when delivering educational content. Even in the absence of humor, any narration, text, or audio overlay should be mindfully executed with respect for the patient in mind. It is important to remember that procedural interventions in medicine are often unfamiliar and at times feared by the public. Levity in narration may inadvertently minimize the impact that a procedure has on the patient, which can be particularly problematic if one’s own patient videos are used.

Moreover, elements of procedural videos such as visible blood, other tissue trauma (eg, percutaneous interventions, tissue resection, or cautery), or fecal matter may be triggering for some nonphysician viewers. Social media platform algorithms that determine placement of social media content on feeds may present these unsolicited images or videos to the unsuspecting viewers. Many social media platforms have built-in mechanisms to mask or provide a warning to viewers before proactively activating the content to view. In the absence of this functionality, messaging via text overlay or captions may be considered. Moreover, general statements may be developed to be used for any endoscopy-related images or videos to indicate that the posted content may be upsetting to unintended viewers.

Bias

(14)Social media posts with endoscopic images or videos should avoid perpetuating racial, gender, and/or cultural biases.

(14)Social media posts with endoscopic images or videos should avoid perpetuating racial, gender, and/or cultural biases.

Social media posts with endoscopic images or videos should avoid perpetuating racial, gender, and/or cultural biases.

Structural racism and historical bias in scientific investigation, medical education, and health care delivery have led to significant distrust among marginalized racial and ethnic groups. Education on social media should therefore be approached thoughtfully to avoid explicit and implicit bias that could lead to further distrust. Historically, minorities have been under-represented in educational materials in medicine.30,31 In addition to including this notion as part of cultural competency training, incorporating multiple examples from various population subgroups may promote inclusivity in education via social media. Gender bias has also been previously noted in medical illustrations, implying male anatomy as the standard.32

Although endoscopic images and videos rarely reveal gender, race, or cultural background, the way the clinical scenario or condition is described may perpetuate bias. Clinical scenarios should be presented in a manner that avoids misrepresentation of gender, race, or other groups. Misrepresentation of race may reference concepts of inaccurate race-based diagnostic bias or antiquated race-based practice guidelines.33 When possible, granular ethnic categories referencing country of origin, as outlined by the National Academy of Medicine, should be used.34 Racial or cultural context should be included only when clinically relevant, after historical/political context are taken into consideration, and presented in a manner that avoids perpetuating stereotypes.

Endoscopic footage discussing interesting or novel procedures on social media often references specific medical devices. Some gastroenterologists posting these videos may be key opinion leaders who are not only influential online but also serve in an advisory role or receive research funding from device vendors. Although the same physicians may serve on professional society guideline committees that require disclosure of financial conflict of interest, these conflicts are frequently undisclosed online.24 In a study of 95 tweets containing endoscopic images or videos with mention of the name of a medical device, over one-third were posted by physicians who had received payments from the manufacturer of the device mentioned, but none disclosed these financial relationships.20 Without such transparency, social media consumers may be unaware of potential bias in what may otherwise seem to be an objective case description. This is also important for other non–endoscopy-related general educational posts on social media. Although there is no formal guidance on how to disclose these relationships, there should be clear and apparent disclosure mentioning the nature of all relevant conflicts.

In recent years, various companies have approached physicians and other health professionals to sponsor social media posts for product endorsement, educational campaigns, or other promotions. Should endoscopic content on social media be sponsored on social media, The Federal Trade Commission specifically outlines ways to disclose sponsored social media content.35 These recommendations include using simple language, clear text overlays on images, or verbal disclosure on videos in a manner that is apparent.

Conclusions

The reach of social media has been leveraged for rapid dissemination of educational content in the form of endoscopic procedural images and videos, especially in pandemic-related lockdown situations and remote regions that limit easy access to educational opportunities. However, the benefit of sharing endoscopic content must be weighed with potential risk to patients, health professionals, employer, and the field of gastroenterology. In an era of significant distrust in physicians and health care, it is important to construct educational content around endoscopic footage while actively considering the potential implications with respect to patient privacy, ensuring proper attribution, mitigating bias, and appropriate financial disclosures.
