Can ChatGPT-5 estimate dental age? A comparative study with Demirjian and Willems methods in paediatric dentistry
Authors: İsmail Haktan Çelik, Farhad Salmanpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess, in an exploratory manner, the performance of ChatGPT-5, a general-purpose multimodal large language model (LLM), in estimating dental age (DA) from panoramic radiographs (PRs), and to compare its estimates with those obtained using the Demirjian and Willems methods.Materials and methodsA total of 900 PRs from children aged 5.00–13.99 years, stratified into nine balanced age/sex bands, were evaluated. The Demirjian and Willems DA were calculated via blinded stage scoring, while ChatGPT-5 produced DA estimates directly from the images. Performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), mean error (ME), mean squared error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), and Bland–Altman analysis. Age-group classification performance was also assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Non-parametric tests were used for between-method comparisons.ResultsThe overall MAE was lowest for the Willems method (0.63 years), followed by the Demirjian method (0.71 years) and ChatGPT-5 (0.80 years) (p < 0.001). The Willems method significantly outperformed both other approaches. Bland–Altman analysis showed the smallest bias and narrowest limits of agreement for the Willems method, whereas ChatGPT-5 demonstrated wider variability. Although the Willems method showed the highest overall accuracy, ChatGPT-5 demonstrated competitive performance in the 6 to 7-year age groups (Groups 2 and 3). However, the model showed significantly higher error rates in older age groups, particularly in Group 9.ConclusionsChatGPT-5 showed exploratory potential for DA estimation, particularly in younger children; however, its overall performance remained inferior to that of the validated Willems and Demirjian methods. At present, general-purpose multimodal LLMs should be regarded as adjunctive decision-support tools rather than as replacements for established manual methods.Keywords: Dental age, Paediatric dentistry, Panoramic radiography, Demirjian, Willems, ChatGPT-5

Introduction

Estimation of chronological age (CA) in children is an important clinical tool in pediatric dentistry and orthodontics, as it facilitates the assessment of the progression of different dental malocclusions and optimal timing of intervention based on craniofacial growth patterns [1, 2]. Analyses of skeletal maturation and dental development are among the advanced medical techniques commonly employed for age estimation [3]. However, chronic diseases and nutritional deficiencies during growth and development may affect skeletal maturation, leading to deviations in age estimation. Compared with skeletal maturation, dental development is less influenced by environmental factors [4], largely because it is more tightly regulated by genetic factors [5]. Thus, under specific clinical circumstances, approaches based on dental development may be considered a more reliable indicator of age.

Adult assessment considers factors such as tooth wear, secondary dentin formation, and color changes [6–8], whereas pediatric estimation methods are predominantly based on the radiographic analysis of dental calcification stages [9–12]. These methods code and score the calcification stages identified on panoramic or periapical radiographs according to standardized criteria. The resulting scores are combined based on a predetermined scoring system to establish the DA.

DA estimation method proposed by Demirjian et al. in 1973 is one of the most widely used and recognized methods. It involves examining the calcification of the seven permanent teeth in the left mandible on a panoramic radiograph (PR). These teeth are classified into developmental stages A–H using charts developed by Demirjian. Each tooth score is determined according to the developmental stage, and the sum of these scores (ranging from 0 to 100) is converted into DA using a corresponding Table [10].

In 2001, Willems et al. adapted the Demirjian method based on data derived from a Belgian population [11]. This method introduced new sex-specific charts. Compared with the charts by Demirjian, these charts contain the sum of the scores that directly yield the DA; thus, they do not require a conversion process.

These traditional DA estimation methods rely on the manual assessment of dental development stages and are widely regarded as valid in this field. However, they are limited by observer dependency, time-consuming scoring and conversion procedures, and the need for population-specific calibration [12].

Recent advances in computing power and software have accelerated the development of deep learning-based approaches for dental age estimation. Artificial intelligence (AI) systems may improve the efficiency, objectivity, and precision of age estimation from PRs while reducing reliance on manual assessment. Despite these developments, the literature has predominantly focused on specifically designed artificial neural network architectures, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and ResNet-based models [13–15]. Based on the accessible literature, no previous study has specifically investigated the use of a general-purpose multimodal LLM for dental age estimation from panoramic radiographs in paediatric dentistry.

OpenAI’s latest models, such as ChatGPT-4 and ChatGPT-4o, offer built-in multimodal capabilities, which enable them to accept and reason over visual inputs in addition to text. This aspect has been explicitly stated in OpenAI’s technical reports and product announcements [16]. According to OpenAI, ChatGPT-5, introduced in August 2025, demonstrates improved performance over earlier generations across a range of tasks, including visual perception and healthcare-related applications [17]. In medical imaging, previous studies have shown that ChatGPT-4 and ChatGPT-4o can perform tasks such as radiographic finding extraction, positioning-quality assessment, and interpretation of chest or abdominal radiographs. However, they do not replace expert radiologists and remain limited in subtasks such as visual matching and localization [18, 19]. These findings suggest that multimodal LLMs may serve as complementary tools within clinical decision support settings [20]. In this context, ChatGPT-5 is of interest because it combines visual and textual processing within a general-purpose multimodal framework. However, it is not a task-specific dental AI model trained or validated for dental age estimation. Therefore, its use in the present study should be interpreted as exploratory.

Recent studies have begun to evaluate the ability of general-purpose multimodal LLMs and other image-based artificial intelligence systems to interpret radiographic and visual data in orthodontics and growth assessment [21–24]. In hand-wrist radiographs, ChatGPT-5 based models have been shown capable of generating bone-age and growth-stage estimates directly from images [21], while a broader comparative analysis of multimodal LLMs for pediatric bone-age assessment demonstrated that such models can produce radiograph-based age estimates without task-specific training, although their current error levels remain too high for clinical or medico-legal use [22]. In parallel, image-based artificial intelligence has also been applied to other orthodontic interpretation tasks, including landmark-independent classification of vertical skeletal pattern on lateral cephalometric radiographs [23] and AI-driven scoring of orthodontic aesthetic treatment need using the IOTN-AC [24]. Collectively, these studies suggest that multimodal and vision-based artificial intelligence systems are increasingly being explored for direct interpretation of dentofacial images; however, their outputs should currently be regarded as supportive rather than substitutive [22, 24].

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the performance of ChatGPT-5 in estimating dental age from panoramic radiographs in pediatric patients and to compare its estimates with those obtained using the established Demirjian and Willems methods. Given the general-purpose nature of the model, this comparison was intended as an exploratory contextual assessment rather than an equivalent benchmark against validated population-based methods.

Materials and methods

This study was reviewed and endorsed by the Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Ethics Committee for Scientific Research (Approval no. 2025/608). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles delineated in the Declaration of Helsinki. In this retrospective, cross-sectional, and single-center study, PRs belonging to 2234 pediatric patients, aged 5–13.99 years, who presented to the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University, between June 1, 2024, and January 1, 2025, were retrospectively retrieved from the archives. Of these patients, 900 (450 males, 450 females) were included in the final analysis, applying pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients must be systemically healthy according to their medical records; diagnostic-quality digital PRs must be available; the images must be free of head positioning errors (e.g., asymmetry or head tilt), motion artifacts, or exposure/contrast deficiencies; all seven permanent teeth in the left mandibular quadrant (either as tooth germs or erupted) must be clearly visible on the PR; and these teeth must be free of any conditions that could interfere with the assessment of calcification stages, such as caries, restorations (e.g., stainless steel crowns), endodontic treatment, or periapical lesions.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: presence of any systemic disease, chronic condition, genetic syndrome, or metabolic disorder (e.g., endocrinopathies, growth hormone deficiency, and rickets) known to affect dental or skeletal development, as documented in the patient records; presence of numerical or structural dental anomalies in the region of interest, such as hypodontia, supernumerary teeth, fusion, gemination, microdontia, or macrodontia; and a history of trauma, surgery, or cystic pathology in the maxillofacial region.

A power analysis was performed using G*Power v3.1.9.2 (Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, NRW, Germany). Because the primary analysis compares three repeated measures (Demirjian, Willems, and ChatGPT-5 methods) on the same participants, a conservative small-to-moderate effect size estimate was used (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.30; f ≈ 0.15). Using either the “ANOVA: repeated measures, within factors” setting (f = 0.15, measures = 3, correlation among measures = 0.5) or the paired-t approximation (d = 0.30), the required total sample size was estimated as ≈ 90 participants for 80% power at α = 0.05.

The CA of all individuals was calculated by the second researcher (F.S.) based on the total number of days between the date of birth and the date of radiographic examination. For comparative analysis, this total number of days was converted into decimal years using the following formula:

CA (years) = (date of radiographic examination − date of birth) / 365.25.

To enhance the statistical power of the study and examine age-related differences, the final sample (N = 900) was stratified by the second researcher (F.S.) according to CA and sex. Individuals were divided into nine distinct age groups based on their CA (group 1: 5.00–5.99 years; group 9: 13.00–13.99 years). The sample size was allocated equally between males and females participants, with each age group containing 50 males and 50 females, yielding a total sample of 100 participants.

Assessment of panoramic radiographs

Digital PR images were obtained using a Planmeca Proline XC (2009; Helsinki, Finland) X-ray unit. The images were saved using a standard positioning technique in alignment with the manufacturer’s prescribed recommendations, with exposure parameters of 65 kV, 5 mA, and 14 s.

For expert assessment and AI-based prediction, all PRs were first converted from DICOM to PNG format. Before any external processing, all radiographs were de-identified. During DICOM-to-PNG conversion, all embedded DICOM metadata were removed. In addition, any burned-in identifiers visible on the radiographs (e.g., patient name, identification number, date, or institution labels) were cropped or masked to ensure that no direct identifiers were present in the image content. Each PR was assigned a study-specific code; the linkage key between study codes and patient identities was stored separately in a password-protected file accessible only to the investigators. De-identified image files were stored on a secured institutional computer, and access was restricted to the study team. A standardized preprocessing procedure was implemented using Python and OpenCV to ensure the images were optimized for AI processing while preserving their diagnostic value for manual evaluation. This preprocessing protocol was designed to ensure compatibility and consistency for the diagnostic tasks of the ChatGPT-5 model while preserving clinically meaningful image quality for the evaluators.

Manual assessment was conducted using a two-stage, blinded protocol designed to minimize observer bias and provide a fair basis for comparison with the AI model. In the first stage, the preprocessed PRs (1024 × 512 pixels, 200 dpi, histogram-normalized PNG) were provided in random order to the first researcher (İHÇ), a pediatric dentist with at least 5 years of experience, in their unannotated form (i.e., without tooth numbering or color coding). Annotated copies of the same preprocessed images were used only for the ChatGPT-5 sessions to standardize tooth referencing. The researcher, blind to the patients’ CA and sex, determined and recorded the calcification stages (A–H) of the seven left mandibular permanent teeth for each image. In the second stage, these raw staging data were transferred to the second researcher (F.S.), who had access to patients’ sex (required for the sex-specific reference tables) to compute DA using the Demirjian and Willems methods. Using these staging records, F.S. performed two separate DA calculations: one according to the Demirjian method [10] (by scoring the stages and converting the total score to DA using a sex-specific table) and one according to the Willems method [11] (by directly summing the stage scores using sex-specific tables).

In the next phase, the aim was to estimate DA using ChatGPT-5. ChatGPT-5 was used in its multimodal (vision-enabled) mode via the ChatGPT web interface (paid subscription) at https://chat.openai.com. Only de-identified PNG images were uploaded to the platform; no DICOM files, metadata, patient names, dates of birth, record numbers, or any other identifiers were shared. The prompt contained no identifying information, and the only case-specific non-image input was the patient’s recorded sex. The evaluation was conducted using the August 2025 release of ChatGPT-5. Because the web interface did not display a more specific reportable version identifier to the user, the exact internal model build could not be documented. To address reproducibility concerns associated with web-based interfaces, several standardized procedures were implemented. Although the web interface does not permit manual adjustment of internal inference parameters, all assessments were performed using the system’s default settings within a concentrated 7-day window (October 6–13, 2025) to minimize temporal model drift. In addition, each image was processed in a new Temporary Chat session with the Memory and Model Improvement features disabled to reduce session-based carryover effects. This approach helped standardize the inference environment by preventing access to prior evaluations during the study period.

No manual radiographic description, tooth staging scores, or exact chronological age was provided to the model. The prompt was finalized before the evaluation period and was not modified at any point during data collection or analysis. The only accompanying non-image inputs were the standardized prompt (Table 1) used verbatim for all cases and the patient’s recorded sex; the overall study age range was stated in the prompt. Sex was included because the Demirjian and Willems methods rely on sex-specific reference tables; therefore, the same case-specific information was provided to ChatGPT-5 for comparability. Accordingly, a total of 900 PRs were submitted in random order. Each PR was evaluated in a new independent session (Temporary Chat), and no follow-up prompts were used after the initial standardized prompt.

Structured prompt utilized for dental age estimation using the ChatGPT-5 multimodal model

To reduce variability in tooth localization and to standardize identification of the target teeth across cases, annotated copies of the preprocessed panoramic radiographs were created only for the ChatGPT-5 sessions, with primary teeth numbered in yellow and permanent teeth in white. This methodological step was used to support consistent referencing of the target teeth (FDI 31–37) in the AI arm, given that ChatGPT-5 is a general-purpose multimodal model rather than a task-specific dental system. In contrast, the human examiner was an experienced pediatric dentist for whom tooth identification on standard panoramic radiographs represents a routine clinical task; therefore, overlays were not used in the manual assessment arm. The annotations were used solely for tooth referencing and did not include any developmental stage labels, age-related cues, or diagnostic information. The DA predictions generated by ChatGPT-5 were recorded verbatim by the second researcher (F.S.), and the final numeric estimate reported in years was extracted as the predicted DA. When the model returned an age range, the midpoint of the range was used as the predicted DA. The prompt used was optimized using the chain-of-thought (CoT) method to ensure maximal performance and elicit the most appropriate responses from ChatGPT-5. To maintain methodological consistency and prevent data contamination, the data controls for the paid ChatGPT-5 sessions used in the analyses were specifically configured. Throughout the analysis period, the “Improve the model for everyone” setting was disabled, which prevented the content from these sessions from being used in the training of OpenAI models. Additionally, each evaluation was conducted in “Temporary Chat” mode—wherein content is not saved to the conversation history, is not included in model training, and is automatically deleted from the system within 30 days—and with the “Memory” feature disabled. This approach served two purposes: to prevent patient data from being used for model training or improvement and to minimize potential recall effects. Accordingly, the 90 PRs re-evaluated 2 weeks later for the reproducibility assessment [intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC)] were processed in new, memory-free chat sessions that were fully independent of the initial evaluations. To assess repeatability, a random sample of 90 PRs (10% of the dataset) was selected and re-evaluated after a 2-week interval. The same randomly selected 90 PRs were used for both manual re-examination (İ.H.Ç.) and the second ChatGPT-5 run, using identical preprocessing, the same standardized prompt (Table 1), the same rule-based sex entry, and new independent Temporary Chat sessions.

As both overestimation and underestimation were possible, absolute error (AE) values were also calculated: .

Mean absolute error (MAE) and standard deviation (SD) were used to summarize the performance of each method in the entire sample and within sex-specific and age-specific subgroups. In addition, mean error (ME), mean squared error (MSE), and root mean square error (RMSE) were calculated to further evaluate model performance and to assess potential systematic bias. Statistical comparisons between methods were performed based on MAE values. Furthermore, Bland–Altman analysis was conducted to assess agreement between predicted age and chronological age and to evaluate potential systematic bias.

To further assess classification performance across age groups, additional metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated, and confusion matrices were constructed to visualize the agreement between predicted and actual age groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Intra-examiner repeatability for the estimated DAs obtained with the Demirjian, Willems, and ChatGPT-5 methods was assessed using ICCs. The distribution of continuous variables was examined with the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated that the MAE values did not meet the normality assumption. Accordingly, nonparametric methods were used to compare the MAE values across methods (Demirjian, Willems, and ChatGPT-5). The Friedman test was applied to examine overall differences among the three methods in the full sample and evaluate whether accuracy varied by sex (males/females) and age group. Where the Friedman test indicated a significant difference, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was assessed at two levels. For global comparisons (e.g., Friedman test, Shapiro-Wilk test) and sex/age subgroup differences, a standard alpha level of p < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. To control the Type I error rate during multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied for the three-way pairwise tests between methods (Demirjian vs. Willems vs. ChatGPT-5), resulting in a more stringent significance threshold of p < 0.0167 for these specific comparisons. For analyses with Bonferroni correction, the corresponding adjusted thresholds were used.

Results

ICC analysis showed the highest agreement for the Willems method (ICC = 0.91), followed by the Demirjian method (ICC = 0.87) and ChatGPT-5 (ICC = 0.81). In the total sample (N = 900), the MAE values were 0.71 ± 0.60 for Demirjian, 0.63 ± 0.51 for Willems, and 0.80 ± 0.65 for ChatGPT-5. The Friedman test revealed a significant difference among methods (χ² = 24.23, df = 2, p < 0.001). Post hoc Wilcoxon tests indicated that Willems performed significantly better than both Demirjian (p = 0.007) and ChatGPT-5 (p < 0.001) and that Demirjian outperformed ChatGPT-5 (p = 0.002) (Table 3).

Discussion

DA estimation is a widely utilized method for assessing an individual’s level of biological maturity, particularly during childhood and adolescence. Other biological age indicators, such as skeletal development and pubertal signs, are more susceptible to environmental and hormonal factors. However, dental development follows a more genetically stable process and is therefore considered a more reliable indicator for age estimation [3, 25]. Traditionally, DA is determined by experienced specialists who make a judgment based on a radiographic assessment of dental developmental stages. However, this approach is time-consuming and subjective, which can lead to inconsistencies and differences in interpretation among observers.

Integrating AI into dentistry has shown promise in mitigating these limitations. In particular, deep learning–based models, such as CNNs, can perform automatic age estimation from dental radiographs, thereby improving accuracy, efficiency, and objectivity. These advances represent meaningful progress for orthodontic treatment planning, growth–development monitoring, and timing of pediatric surgical interventions and forensic applications [2]. Nevertheless, many CNN models have been trained on restricted age ranges and limited datasets; consequently, their accuracy and generalizability across diverse ethnic or geographic populations may be constrained. Moreover, the manual labeling required during model development remains labor-intensive and susceptible to human errors. In this context, multimodal LLMs such as ChatGPT-5 may have a complementary role in dental image interpretation. However, ChatGPT-5 is a general-purpose multimodal model rather than a task-specific dental AI system trained and validated for dental age estimation. For this reason, the present study should be interpreted as an exploratory evaluation of whether such a model can generate clinically plausible age estimates from panoramic radiographs when compared with established methods. Although ChatGPT-5 can produce structured textual justifications for its outputs, these narratives should not be interpreted as a direct representation of the model’s internal decision-making process. Accordingly, multimodal LLMs should currently be viewed as exploratory or adjunctive support tools rather than as validated alternatives to established dental age-estimation methods [22, 26].

Despite this potential, the use of LLMs in clinical applications requires careful consideration. In particular, the model’s propensity to occasionally generate out-of-context or non-factual outputs, a phenomenon described in the literature as “hallucination,” represents an important methodological limitation [27]. Therefore, this study used a structured prompting strategy to standardize response format and encourage explicit reporting of image-based observations. This approach was intended to improve procedural consistency and to obtain more structured textual outputs for qualitative review. However, such outputs should not be interpreted as direct evidence of the model’s internal decision-making or latent computational processes. Rather, they represent output-level explanations and do not establish true mechanistic explainability. Although structured prompting may help reduce variability in response format, it does not eliminate platform-level uncertainty or fully resolve the risk of non-factual outputs [28, 29].

In the present study, the Willems method demonstrated the best performance in dental age estimation, with a significantly lower MAE than both the Demirjian method and ChatGPT-5. These findings are in agreement with previous reports from different regions of Türkiye. Studies in Turkish pediatric populations have consistently shown that the Demirjian method tends to overestimate chronological age, whereas the Willems method provides more accurate estimates with lower error rates and better compatibility with the target population [30]. Furthermore, data from the Thrace region showed that the Willems method yielded lower deviations than the Demirjian method in both males and females and offered greater clinical accuracy [31]. These findings are highly consistent with both international and Turkish literature. In contrast, ChatGPT-5 showed higher error rates than the Willems method, which may be explained by the population-specific calibration and direct scoring structure of the Willems approach. This discrepancy highlights an important distinction between current AI modalities. Whereas convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are specifically trained to detect pixel-level visual patterns, multimodal LLMs function primarily as general-purpose semantic processors. Our findings suggest that, despite its visual capabilities, ChatGPT-5 currently lacks the level of visual discrimination required to distinguish subtle developmental stages as accurately as task-specific models or calibrated human observers.

In hand-wrist radiograph studies, ChatGPT based models and other multimodal LLMs were able to estimate bone age and developmental stage directly from images [21, 22]; however, their agreement with reference standards remained limited, and the magnitude of error was considered insufficient for independent clinical or medico-legal use [22]. A similar pattern has been reported in orthodontic aesthetic assessment, where GPT-5 demonstrated significant correlations with reference IOTN-AC scores but only moderate classification performance, again supporting a supportive rather than autonomous role for these systems [24]. In contrast, purpose-built image-analysis models trained for specific orthodontic radiographic tasks, such as landmark-independent deep learning classification of vertical skeletal pattern on lateral cephalograms, have achieved substantially higher task performance [23], underscoring the advantage of domain-specific optimization. Against this background, the lower overall accuracy of ChatGPT-5 relative to the Willems and Demirjian methods in the present study appears both technically and clinically plausible.

The performance of DA estimation methods across sex groups is an important consideration because biological developmental patterns vary between males and females. Sex-specific differences in dental mineralization may directly influence the accuracy of these methods. Dental development is generally completed earlier in females, whereas it tends to take longer and show greater variation in males [2]. These biological differences may contribute to lower age-estimation accuracy in males. Previous studies have also shown that the Demirjian method tends to overestimate CA in males, whereas the Willems method, which incorporates sex-specific scoring tables, yields more consistent results in both sexes [11, 30, 31]. In the present study, significant differences in method performance were observed in males, with the Willems method showing the best accuracy. In females, ChatGPT-5 yielded accuracy comparable to that of the Demirjian method. This pattern suggests that the model does not yet adequately capture sex-specific biological variation, although it provides a baseline level of performance comparable to some conventional approaches. The superior performance of the Willems method across sexes is plausibly attributable to its sex-specific scoring tables and population-based calibrations. In this context, incorporating sex-related differences not only enhances the accuracy of traditional methods but also delineates priority areas for the future development of AI-based models.

The performance of AI-based models for DA estimation should be evaluated not only on the basis of overall accuracy but also with respect to age-group-specific variation. Because developmental patterns change with CA, this issue is particularly important for multimodal systems, whose ability to distinguish age-dependent features may vary across developmental stages. In the present study, ChatGPT-5 showed different levels of accuracy across age groups. Notably, the lowest MAE values were observed in the younger groups (G2 and G3), suggesting that the model was better able to recognize the more distinct stages of dental maturation seen in early childhood. This finding accords with previous reports indicating that radiographic indicators are more conspicuous at younger ages, enabling AI-based approaches to produce more precise estimates [15, 32]. By contrast, error rates increased substantially in the older age bands (particularly G8 and G9). One possible explanation is that the subtle radiographic features associated with late-stage root development and apical closure may be more difficult for a general-purpose multimodal model to distinguish consistently than the more conspicuous developmental changes observed in younger age groups. Based on these findings, although ChatGPT-5 demonstrates promising performance for DA estimation in early childhood, its use at later ages without corroboration from conventional methods may compromise accuracy. These observations underscore that age-sensitive modeling strategies are a fundamental requirement for AI-driven clinical decision support in pediatric dentistry.

Bland-Altman analysis revealed that the Willems method demonstrated superior performance with minimal and consistent bias (− 0.15 years). The positive bias value of the Demirjian method (+ 0.49 years) is consistent with the literature and exhibits systematic overestimation in the Turkish population [33]. The increasing bias observed in adolescents (1.04 years) indicates that this method should be used with caution in dentistry applications such as legal age threshold determination. The ChatGPT-5 method’s wide limits of agreement (± 3.91 years) and age-dependent variable bias limit its reliability in individual age estimation. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive validation of AI-based methods prior to clinical use [34]. For clinical applications, the Willems method is recommended due to its minimal bias and high consistency.

Comprehensive performance metrics and confusion matrix analysis revealed marked differences in age group classification performance among the three methods, with the ChatGPT-5 method showing significant limitations. The ChatGPT-5 accuracy (33.22%) was substantially lower than Willems (49.44%) and only marginally superior to Demirjian (31.44%). More concerning, the ChatGPT-5 F1-score (29.76%) was 38% lower than Willems (48.01%), indicating both reduced accuracy and poor reliability as a classifier.

A critical weakness of the ChatGPT-5 method was its inconsistent age-dependent performance. While demonstrating acceptable accuracy in specific early childhood groups (Groups 2–3: MAE 0.34–0.44 years), the method’s performance deteriorated dramatically in adolescent populations (Group 9: MAE 1.81 years, representing a 5.3-fold increase from Group 3). This instability contrasts sharply with the Willems method, which maintained robust performance across all age groups and achieved exceptional accuracy in late adolescence (Group 9: 92%). The Willems method demonstrated the highest overall accuracy (49.44%), with balanced precision (49.06%) and recall (49.44%) indicating consistent, reliable performance.

Confusion matrix analysis addressed potential concerns about systematic bias in all methods. Misclassifications were symmetrically distributed around the diagonal for all three methods, including ChatGPT-5. For example, in the Willems method, 7% of Group 5 patients were misclassified to lower groups while 37% were assigned to higher groups, demonstrating balanced error distribution. High specificity values exceeding 91% across all methods further confirm the absence of systematic lower age group labeling bias, as such bias would have manifested as elevated false-positive rates in upper age groups and reduced specificity.

The observed superior performance in early childhood groups reflects the inherent distinctiveness of dental development characteristics in these age ranges rather than methodological bias [35]. Similarly, increased accuracy in late adolescent groups (Willems Group 9: 92%) corresponds to the re-emergence of characteristic features during dental development completion. Literature consistently demonstrates that the Willems method exhibits superior accuracy and reduced systematic error compared to both Demirjian and emerging AI-based approaches [36].

ChatGPT-5 may have potential as an exploratory adjunctive decision-support tool rather than as a standalone dental age estimation method. Its outputs may assist with the structured organization of radiographic observations and may be useful in educational or preliminary assessment settings. However, given its lower overall accuracy and its general-purpose architecture, any clinical interpretation should remain supplementary and should be considered alongside validated conventional methods.

However, this study has several limitations. First, although ChatGPT-5 can process visual data directly, it is a general-purpose multimodal model rather than a task-specific system trained for dental image interpretation, which may have constrained its accuracy on panoramic radiographs. Second, because the model was accessed through the web interface, the exact internal model build and inference parameters could not be fully controlled or documented; although several standardization procedures were implemented, exact technical reproducibility cannot be guaranteed. Third, although the structured prompt elicited textual rationales, these narratives represent output-level explanations rather than verified access to the model’s internal decision pathway. Fourth, this was a single-center study conducted in a Turkish pediatric population, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Fifth, tooth-number overlays were provided only in the AI arm to standardize tooth referencing; although these overlays did not include any developmental staging labels or age cues, they may have reduced localization-related variability relative to the unannotated manual assessment.

Conclusion

The Willems method remained the most accurate approach in this population and showed significantly better performance than the tested LLM. Although ChatGPT-5 showed potential during the early mixed dentition stage, it lacked the level of visual discrimination required for older age groups, particularly in the evaluation of root apex closure. However, unlike traditional scoring methods, the model generated structured textual responses alongside its outputs. These responses may support readability and educational interpretation, but they should not be taken as a direct representation of the model’s internal reasoning process. As a general-purpose multimodal LLM rather than a task-specific dental AI model, ChatGPT-5 should currently be regarded as an adjunctive and exploratory tool rather than a replacement for established dental age estimation methods. Future research should focus on task-specific validation and on improving performance in the assessment of late developmental stages.
