Electrical stimulation as an emerging strategy in bone repair: Mechanisms, applications, and advances
Authors: Xue Wang, Huilin Hu, Nana Xiong, Shaoke Gu, Juan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of critical-sized bone defects remains a substantial clinical challenge in orthopaedics. Electrical stimulation (ES), which mimics the endogenous bioelectric and piezoelectric properties of bone tissue, has emerged as a promising non-invasive modality to facilitate bone regeneration. This review elucidates the multifaceted mechanisms through which ES coordinately enhances osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and inflammation resolution, primarily via the regulation of key signalling pathways and modulation of the immune microenvironment at molecular and cellular levels. We also highlight recent advances in the integration of ES with electroactive biomaterials, enabling more targeted and efficacious therapeutic outcomes. Robust clinical evidence supports the ability of ES to significantly accelerate fracture healing, underscoring its translational relevance and potential for broad clinical adoption. By synthesizing these developments, this review aims to inform and inspire the design of next-generation bone regeneration strategies.The translational potential of this articleES offers a non-invasive or minimally invasive therapeutic modality that can significantly accelerate fracture healing and bone regeneration. This review synthesizes current mechanistic understanding and clinical evidence, highlighting ES as a potential alternative to secondary revision surgery for nonunion fractures. By integrating ES with electroactive biomaterials and emerging self-powered implantable devices, future strategies can be translated into personalized, intelligent bone repair systems, reducing patient morbidity and healthcare costs.Keywords: Bioelectricity, Bone regeneration, Electrical stimulation, Fracture healing, Translational medicine

Introduction

With the intensifying global aging population, bone defects have become a significant challenge in the medical field [1]. Bone defects not only impact patients’ quality of life but also impose a substantial burden on societal healthcare resources. However, large bone defects caused by bone tumors, trauma, infection, and other bone diseases often exceed the natural healing capacity of bone tissue. Restoring their structure and function requires bone grafting or other reconstructive approaches [2].

Current conventional treatments for bone defects primarily include autogenous bone grafting, allogeneic bone grafting, metal implants, and bioceramic materials [3]. Although these methods are widely used clinically, each still has significant limitations. Consequently, developing novel bone repair strategies that overcome these limitations has become a research priority. This urgency is especially pronounced for critical-sized bone defects (CSBDs), which are typically defined as defects that exceed the natural self-healing capacity of bone tissue. These defects often arise from severe trauma, tumor resection, or infection and fail to heal spontaneously, leading to prolonged disability and repeated interventions.

In recent years, ES has emerged as an innovative therapeutic approach attracting growing attention from researchers. As early as the 19th century, scientists observed that electrical phenomena within living organisms are closely linked to tissue physiological functions. Specifically, the application of electricity to address bone-related conditions dates back to early clinical reports in the 1800s; for instance, the successful use of ‘electric shocks’ to treat a tibial nonunion was documented as early as 1841, with further corroboration by mid-19th-century reports on galvanic current for delayed unions and nonunions [4]. As an inherent physiological characteristic of the human body, endogenous electrical signals play a pivotal role in bone metabolism and repair. With advancements in biomedical engineering and electrophysiology, exogenous ES has been demonstrated to effectively promote bone healing, soft tissue repair, and cellular behavior regulation through non-invasive or minimally invasive approaches [5]. Compared with autologous bone grafting, which requires a secondary surgical site, carries risks of donor-site complications, and has limited supply, ES offers distinct advantages. It can be applied repeatedly without additional surgical trauma, is not constrained by graft availability, and can be tailored to the patient's healing response. Moreover, ES restores the endogenous bioelectric microenvironment that is often disrupted in critical-sized defects, thereby reactivating dormant reparative pathways. These features make ES a promising non-invasive strategy, particularly for CSBDs where traditional grafting is insufficient.

Bone defect healing and conventional treatments

Bone defects, caused by trauma, infection, or tumor resection, compromise skeletal integrity and function. Their healing proceeds through three overlapping phases: hematoma-inflammatory, callus formation, and remodeling. The inflammatory phase initiates repair, the callus phase bridges the defect with new tissue, and the prolonged remodeling phase restores native bone architecture and strength [6] (Fig. 1).

Hematoma inflammatory reaction period

Following bone defect formation, external loading impacts cause skeletal fractures. Due to the rich vascular supply of both bone tissue and bone marrow, substantial blood leakage occurs post-fracture, forming a hematoma at the defect site. Within hours, this blood coagulates to create a temporary natural scaffold, providing a foundation for subsequent bone cell attachment. During this process, platelets release multiple signaling factors (e.g., PDGF, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and chemokines) that recruit circulating progenitor and precursor cells, including monocytes and hematopoietic progenitor cells, to the bone defect site. These cells work synergistically to clear apoptotic cells and necrotic tissue while initiating the formation of new blood vessels. The newly established tissue and vascular network lay the foundation for the subsequent recruitment of chemokines and the delivery of nutrients. At the molecular level, this phase is characterized by the rapid upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which activate downstream signaling pathways including NF-κB and MAPK, thereby orchestrating immune cell recruitment and early osteogenic signaling cascades. Overall, the inflammatory response is a critical early event in bone repair, typically peaking within a short period before gradually subsiding.

Bone callus formation and repair period

Following the resolution of the inflammatory response, the bone repair process enters the callus formation phase. This stage primarily occurs atop the newly formed extracellular matrix (ECM). The ECM progressively differentiates into fibrous callus and bony callus, ultimately forming new trabecular bone structures through the coordinated action of various bone cells and chemokines. Mechanistically, this stage is dominated by osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation regulated by key signaling pathways such as BMP/Smads, Wnt/β-catenin, and TGF-β. These pathways promote the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into chondrocytes and osteoblasts, while angiogenic factors such as VEGF further enhance vascular invasion into the callus [7]. From a mechanical perspective, this callus provides essential structural support to the defect site, creating a stable microenvironment for subsequent cellular bone repair. At this stage, the bone structure at the defect site approaches integrity, transitioning into the prolonged callus ossification phase, which may persist for several months.

Bone callus formation and shaping period

This stage constitutes the longest phase of bone healing, typically beginning around weeks 4 to 6 after injury and continuing for 6 to 12 months. During this period, osteoclasts and osteoblasts work in coordination to remodel bone: under sustained mechanical loading, the initial callus is progressively resorbed and replaced with structurally mature bone. At the molecular level, bone remodeling is tightly regulated by the RANK/RANKL/OPG signaling axis, which controls osteoclast differentiation and activity, as well as by mechanotransduction pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin that respond to mechanical loading and regulate bone formation [8].

The bioelectrical basis of bone healing and the rationale for electrical stimulation

Notably, the aforementioned phased healing cascade is orchestrated not only by biochemical signals but also by endogenous bioelectrical cues. Bone tissue inherently exhibits piezoelectric and streaming potential properties: mechanical loading during physiological activities generates localized electrical fields that direct cellular migration, proliferation, and differentiation. In the context of critical-sized defects or nonunion fractures, however, this intrinsic electrical signaling network is disrupted or attenuated, contributing to impaired regeneration. This pathophysiological insight provides a compelling rationale for the therapeutic application of exogenous ES. By recapitulating the native bioelectrical microenvironment, ES can reactivate dormant reparative pathways and compensate for signaling deficiencies at the injury site. Given the fundamental role of ES in potentiating the cellular and molecular events described above, the following section will comprehensively examine the mechanistic basis, biological effects, and clinical applications of ES in bone repair.

Grafting strategies for bone defect treatment

Current section note: Before reviewing traditional grafting approaches, it is essential to recognize that the limitations of these static structural replacements have motivated the exploration of bioactive physical modalities, specifically ES, which is comprehensively addressed in the preceding section.

Autologous bone grafting

Autologous bone grafting involves harvesting bone tissue from the patient's own iliac crest, proximal tibia, fibula, or other sites and transplanting it to the bone defect area. It is considered the “gold standard” for bone repair due to the excellent biocompatibility, osteogenic potential, and osteoinductive properties of autologous bone. However, autologous bone grafting also has significant limitations. First, harvesting autologous bone requires additional surgical procedures, which not only increase patient discomfort and surgical risks but may also lead to donor site complications such as infection, bleeding, nerve damage, and chronic pain. Second, the supply of autologous bone is limited, making it difficult to meet the demands of repairing large bone defects.

Allogeneic bone graft

Allogeneic bone grafting involves the use of bone tissue obtained from another individual. This type of bone is typically sourced from cadaveric donors or bone banks and undergoes stringent processing and sterilization to minimize the risks of immune rejection and disease transmission. A major advantage of allogeneic bone grafts is that they eliminate the need for additional surgery to harvest bone, thereby avoiding donor site complications. Moreover, allogeneic bone is relatively more abundant, making it suitable for repairing larger bone defects. Although allogeneic bone is treated through inactivation and decellularization processes, which substantially reduce immune responses, it remains foreign tissue and may still elicit certain degrees of immune rejection, potentially resulting in graft failure.

Artificial bone material transplantation

With the rapid advancement of biomaterial science, artificial bone materials have emerged as a significant research focus in bone defect repair. These materials are synthetically manufactured or processed to serve as alternatives to autologous and allogeneic bone. They generally possess favorable biocompatibility, osteoconductivity, or osteoinductivity, and are capable of facilitating the regeneration and repair of bone tissue [9]. Among them, calcium silicate-based ceramics have been widely studied for bone regeneration due to their ability to release bioactive ions such as Ca2+ and SiO44−, which can promote osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis. In addition, decellularized ECM scaffolds, derived from native tissues, retain the natural composition and bioactive factors of the extracellular matrix, thereby providing a favorable microenvironment for cell adhesion and osteogenesis. These materials complement the existing biomaterial systems and further expand the design strategies for bone regeneration.

Conventional treatments include autografts, allografts, and artificial materials. Autologous bone grafting is the “gold standard” due to its osteogenicity and biocompatibility, but it requires a second surgical site and carries donor-site morbidity and limited supply. Allogeneic grafts avoid donor-site surgery but risk immune rejection and lower bioactivity. Artificial biomaterials, including biometals such as titanium, magnesium, and zinc, bioceramics such as hydroxyapatite and tricalcium phosphate, and polymer composites such as PLGA, PLA, and PEG, offer scalability yet face challenges such as mismatched elastic modulus or insufficient osseointegration. These limitations have driven the search for alternative strategies, among which ES has emerged as a promising non-invasive modality that mimics the endogenous bioelectric microenvironment of bone [9]. Application of novel bone repair materials

Current section note: Building upon the mechanistic framework of ES, this section further explores how the integration of ES with advanced electroactive biomaterials can yield synergistic therapeutic outcomes, representing a paradigm shift from passive scaffolds to bioactive, electrically-responsive constructs.

Angiogenesis-promoting bioactive materials

As a central strategy in the design of next-generation bone repair materials, third-generation bone repair materials address the limitations of traditional materials. Addressing issues such as poor biocompatibility, low bioactivity, and insufficient self-repair efficiency in existing materials, third-generation bone repair materials enhance repair efficacy by modulating specific cellular responses, focusing on overcoming the bottleneck of inadequate angiogenesis capacity. As shown in Fig. 3a, Miao et al. found that composite materials can induce macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype and promote the secretion of high levels of VEGF and PDGF-bb cytokines, indicating strong angiogenic potential. When such materials are combined with ES, the polarization effect can be further enhanced, leading to accelerated bone repair. Qiao et al. designed a bio-membrane for preparing genetically functionalized matrices (GFM) that modulate angiogenesis and neurogenesis. Integrating ES with such GFM could further optimize the neurovascular coupling, although this remains an underexplored direction.

Biomimetic composite materials

Although hydroxyapatite and calcium phosphate bioceramics share certain compositional similarities with the inorganic components of human bone matrix, the fundamental structural unit of natural bone is mineralized collagen, which consists of type I collagen and nano-hydroxyapatite arranged in a specific spatial configuration. Jin et al. developed a composite nanofiber based on type I collagen peptides that not only maintains excellent cell compatibility but also effectively promotes osteogenic differentiation of stem cells [32]. Liu et al. discovered that polyvinylimide-modified eggshell membrane composites enhance cell adhesion, proliferation, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, and type I collagen expression. As shown in Fig. 3b, Zhang et al. developed a bio-derived collagen membrane through citric acid pretreatment and carboxymethyl chitosan-mediated mineralization, demonstrating potential in promoting bone healing [30].

Functionalized composite materials

To overcome these shortcomings, researchers have developed injectable bioactive scaffolds with hierarchical degradation properties to mimic the creeping replacement process of autologous bone repair. The core of functionalized composite materials lies in integrating multiple biological functions, which can promote angiogenesis, antimicrobial activity, or immunomodulation to optimize the bone regeneration microenvironment [33]. Ye et al. developed an injectable, hierarchically degradable bioactive scaffold that functionally mimics the natural creep-substitution process of autogenous bone repair. Additionally, research has focused on other properties required during osteogenesis, such as antimicrobial and anti-infective capabilities. Antimicrobial functionality stands as a key characteristic of third-generation bone repair materials, effectively controlling infection while promoting bone regeneration [34]. Tang et al. designed a nanomaterial that, for the first time, integrates sonodynamic antimicrobial activity, accelerated bone repair, and near-infrared real-time imaging, offering a comprehensive solution for infected bone defects [35]. The combination of such antimicrobial materials with ES could provide dual benefits: infection control via material properties and tissue regeneration via electrical cues, though this combination requires further investigation.

Emerging material systems and manufacturing technologies

DNA-based materials, particularly DNA hydrogels, have demonstrated potential in promoting bone repair during biomineralization. Meshry et al. discussed four mechanisms through which DNA-based materials exert bone-conducting capabilities, and proposed degradation models for these materials along with their relationship to osteoconductive properties [36]. Additionally, conductive materials such as graphene possess exceptional mechanical properties, electrical conductivity, and large specific surface area (Fig. 3c), making them attractive candidates for bone repair scaffolds [31]. As will be summarized below, conductive materials like graphene represent a key class of electroactive scaffolds that can directly synergize with ES.

Summary of electroactive biomaterials for ES integration

Electroactive biomaterials are designed to generate, conduct, or respond to electrical signals, thereby enhancing the effects of exogenous ES. They can be classified into three main categories. Firstly, the conductive materials, such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, polypyrrole, and polyaniline, which facilitate electron transfer and promote cell adhesion and differentiation under ES. Secondly, the piezoelectric materials, including BaTiO3, PVDF, and P (VDF-TrFE), which generate surface charges under mechanical load, thereby mimicking the endogenous bioelectricity of bone. Thirdly, the self-powered systems, such as triboelectric and piezoelectric nanogenerators, which enable wireless, implantable ES delivery without external power sources. When integrated with ES, these materials improve spatiotemporal control over electrical cues, enhance osteogenesis and angiogenesis, and modulate immune responses, for example by promoting M2 macrophage polarization. Recent advances in 3D and 4D printing as well as electrospinning allow precise fabrication of patient-specific, electroactive scaffolds. Together, the bioactive materials discussed above, when combined with ES, yield synergistic therapeutic outcomes for bone regeneration.

Effects of ES on bone growth

Promotion of fracture healing

The beneficial role of ES in fracture healing has been widely investigated and applied in clinical practice. This pro-healing effect has also been supported by recent clinical trials. In a prospective, double-blind, sham-controlled randomized pilot study, patients with acute distal radius fractures treated with pulsed electromagnetic field (PEMF) stimulation showed improved recovery-related outcomes compared with the sham group, suggesting that non-invasive electromagnetic stimulation may be beneficial even during the early stage of fracture healing [37]. Consistent with this, a prospective multicentre randomized controlled trial published in 2024 reported that capacitive biophysical stimulation improved the healing course of vertebral fragility fractures, indicating that the clinical benefits of ES may extend beyond appendicular fractures to osteoporotic axial skeletal injuries [38]. As introduced in Section 4, ES can also act synergistically with third-generation bone repair materials to further enhance angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Promotion of Osteoblast proliferation and mineralization

Osteoblasts play a crucial role in the process of fracture healing. ES has been shown to significantly enhance the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts, thereby accelerating bone tissue regeneration. Research indicates that both constant direct current and pulsed direct current can increase calcium ion permeability through modulation of cell membrane potential, which in turn promotes osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Furthermore, pulsed electromagnetic fields are also known to facilitate the fracture healing process by stimulating osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Sham et al. co-cultured human pre-osteoblasts isolated from patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty in a medium while applying continuous ES at both high and low electric field intensities for 31 days. It was observed that both stimulation levels enhanced osteoblast activity by upregulating osteogenic genes such as Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and increasing osteocalcin (OCN) secretion, ultimately promoting osteoblast mineralization and bone defect healing [39]. Wu et al. found that the group of mice with bone defects treated with ES recruited more mouse pre-osteoblast (MC-3T3) cells compared to the group without ES. Furthermore, ALP and alizarin red S (ARS) staining results indicated that osteogenic differentiation was significantly enhanced in the electrically stimulated group [40]. Staehlke et al. observed a notable trend of cell spreading in human osteoblast-like cells (MG-63) following 24 h of continuous ES. Subsequent quantification using flow cytometry further confirmed that ES facilitates the attachment and initial adhesion of MG-63 cells [41]. Importantly, the efficacy of ES is highly dependent on stimulation parameters such as intensity, frequency, and duration. For example, Reininger-Mack et al. applied pulsed ES to MC3T3-E1 preosteoblasts cultured on Ti6Al4V implants using two different pulse amplitudes (10 μA and 20 μA) and two frequencies (50 Hz and 100 Hz) for 72 h. They found that asymmetric charge-balanced pulsed stimulation improved cell survival and collagen production in a dose-dependent manner, demonstrating that parameter selection directly determines osteogenic outcomes [42]. Similarly, Zhou et al. systematically examined sinusoidal electromagnetic fields at 1.8 mT across frequencies from 10 to 100 Hz. They observed that 50 Hz most strongly promoted osteogenic differentiation and mineralization, while frequencies of 30-60 Hz inhibited proliferation, and 10, 70, 80 Hz enhanced proliferation, revealing a clear “frequency window effect” that involves primary cilia elongation and protein kinase A activation [43]. Regarding stimulation duration, studies in mechanobiology have shown that continuous mechanical loading leads to desensitization of osteocytes through ion channel inactivation and negative feedback loops, resulting in “diminishing returns” where prolonged stimulation yields progressively weaker anabolic responses [44]. Given the shared signaling pathways (e.g., calcium channels, MAPK) between mechanical and ES, it is plausible that excessively prolonged continuous ES may similarly cause desensitization and reduced efficacy. These findings collectively indicate that ES parameters are not arbitrary but must be carefully optimized based on the specific bone defect type, scaffold material, and treatment goals.

Beyond cellular proliferation and gene expression, ES also directly enhances matrix mineralization. Studies have demonstrated that ES promotes the deposition of calcium phosphate minerals and increases the formation of mineralized nodules in osteoblast cultures, as evidenced by enhanced ARS and von Kossa staining. This pro-mineralization effect is mediated in part by increased ALP activity, which elevates local phosphate concentrations and facilitates hydroxyapatite crystal nucleation and growth on collagenous matrices.

Modulation of Osteoclast activity

Osteoclasts play a vital regulatory role during fracture healing. In the early phase of bone remodeling, mature osteoclasts are responsible for resorbing damaged and aged bone tissue at the injury site. Additionally, decreased osteoclast activity can affect the maturation and mineralization of osteoblasts, thereby promoting new bone regeneration in the mid to late stages of bone repair. Yao et al. discovered that ES can regulate osteoclast activity by altering the pH of the extracellular microenvironment. Under ES, the reduction reaction at the cathode increases local alkalinity, thereby inhibiting osteoclast activity and enhancing bone repair by promoting osteoblast differentiation. Conversely, the oxidation reaction at the anode acidifies the local environment, favoring osteoclast activation to remove aged and damaged bone tissue [45]. Zaniboni et al. found that ES can promote bone remodeling during orthodontic treatment with palatal expansion by increasing the number of osteoclasts [46].

Promoting angiogenesis

Adequate blood supply is critical for fracture healing, as it delivers necessary nutrients and oxygen to the fracture site and enables the efficient removal of metabolic waste generated by cells [47]. Studies have demonstrated that effective vascularization enhances the expression of neuropeptide receptors at early stages, thereby accelerating bone defect repair through the provision of a favorable environment for neural regeneration [48,49]. Zhao et al. employed low-intensity pulsed ultrasound-assisted piezoelectric stimulation to establish an electrical microenvironment conducive to bone defect healing. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression level of VEGF increased 4.3-fold in the group exposed to the electro-microenvironment compared to the control group that did not receive this stimulation [50]. Wang et al. developed a fully implantable bone defect ES (BD-ES) system utilizing self-powered technology. A critical-sized bone defect model was created in the medial femoral condyle of SD rats, followed by precise injection of conductive hydrogel into the defect area and simultaneous implantation of the BD-ES device. Animals in the experimental group were subjected to daily treadmill exercise at a speed of 1 km/h for 30 min per day. After four weeks, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a significantly enhanced co-expression intensity of the vascular endothelial marker vascular endothelial cadherin (CD31) and the vascular smooth muscle marker α-smooth muscle actin compared with the control group [51]. These findings suggest that ES plays an important role in promoting angiogenesis during bone defect repair.

Prevention and treatment of osteoporotic bone defects

Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disorder characterized by decreased bone density and impaired bone microarchitecture, mainly resulting from reduced osteoblast activity and increased osteoclast activity. ES modulates bone metabolism through multiple mechanisms, thereby contributing to the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis. Research has shown that ES can reduce bone loss and prevent osteoporotic bone defects by promoting a decrease in the secretion of calcitonin [52]. Mi et al. found that implanting electrodes into the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) at the L3 and L4 vertebrae of rats revealed that ES can upregulate calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) synthesis in the DRG by activating the Ca2+/CaMKII/CREB signaling pathway. It also triggers the rapid release of CGRP from the nerve terminal vesicle pool via action potentials. Furthermore, studies have indicated that ES alone does not significantly promote healing in osteoporotic bone defects [53]. Future studies may need to explore the combination of ES with bone repair materials to achieve enhanced bone regeneration outcomes.

Regulation of osteocyte signaling pathways

ES promotes bone growth and repair largely through modulating key osteocyte signaling pathways. As mechanosensory cells embedded in bone matrix, osteocytes orchestrate bone metabolism and remodeling via multiple signaling cascades. Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a highly conserved signaling system involved in tissue development and regeneration. Exogenous ES can enhance its activation, making it a promising target for accelerating bone healing. This pathway promotes osteogenesis and angiogenesis while inhibiting osteoclastogenesis. Furthermore, it drives bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) toward osteoblastic rather than adipocytic differentiation—a fate decision critical for bone integrity.

Key osteogenic factors in this pathway, such as osteoprotegerin (OPG), RUNX2, and osterix (OSX), are upregulated during osteoblast differentiation. Studies in β-catenin knockout mice reveal adipocyte accumulation and osteoporotic phenotypes at the osteochondral junction, underscoring the role of β-catenin in directing BMSC lineage commitment [54]. Additionally, electroconvulsive therapy elevates transcription factor 7, a Wnt pathway regulator, further supporting the link between ES and Wnt/β-catenin signaling in osteogenesis.

BMP signaling pathway

The BMP/Smads pathway is a central and specific regulator of osteoblast differentiation. BMPs, members of the TGF-β superfamily, stimulate new bone formation, with BMP-2, -4, and -7 being particularly important in bone repair research.

Upon BMP binding to membrane receptors, Smad proteins are phosphorylated, activating transcription of osteogenic genes such as Runx2. Runx2 and OSX subsequently induce expression of differentiation markers like ALP, OCN, and OPN, enhancing the osteogenic potential of mesenchymal stem cells (Fig. 5). Fredericks et al. administered direct current ES to 30 New Zealand white rabbits following bilateral posterolateral intertransverse fusion surgery. Real-time quantitative PCR results indicated that ES significantly increased the expression of osteogenic factors such as BMP-2, 6, and 7. In addition, a marked elevation in VEGF expression was observed, collectively contributing to enhanced bone formation and development [55]. Srirussamee et al. demonstrated through in vitro experiments that continuous ES of osteoprogenitor cells for three days upregulates mRNA expression of BMP2 and secretory phosphoprotein 1, suggesting ES may represent one potential mechanism underlying electrically induced osteogenesis [56]. In addition to the signaling pathways discussed above, the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathway also plays a crucial role in bone defect healing. Activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway has been shown to promote osteoblast proliferation, survival, and differentiation, as well as inhibit apoptosis, thereby contributing to new bone formation. Mechanistically, PI3K/Akt signaling can regulate downstream targets such as mTOR and GSK-3β, and it also exhibits significant crosstalk with other osteogenesis-related pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Furthermore, the PI3K/Akt pathway has been reported to enhance angiogenesis by upregulating VEGF, which is essential for coupling osteogenesis with vascularization during bone regeneration. Therefore, incorporation of biomaterials or bioactive factors that can modulate PI3K/Akt signaling may further improve the therapeutic efficacy of bone repair strategies [[57], [58], [59]].

MAPK signaling pathway

The MAPK pathway, a serine/threonine kinase cascade, positively regulates osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. Its core three-tier module (MAPKKK–MAPKK–MAPK) includes subfamilies ERK, p38, JNK, and ERK5, which mediate cell growth, stress responses, and inflammation.

ES upregulates MAPK protein expression, activating downstream transcription factors and promoting osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Specifically, increased ERK1/2, p38, and JNK activity enhances transcription of osteogenic genes [60]. Interestingly, while p38 typically mediates apoptosis and inflammation, ES at the “Huantiao” acupoint in sciatic-nerve-injured rats suppressed p38/MAPK activation, reducing neuronal apoptosis [61]. Konstantinou et al. found that microcurrent stimulation promotes cell proliferation and migration in an ERK 1/2- or p38-dependent manner in fibroblasts and osteoblast-like cells. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that microcurrents enhance the transcriptional activation of genes involved in the MAPK signaling pathway, demonstrating that electrical stimulation can activate key MAPK family members and regulate downstream gene expression [62].

NF-κB signaling pathway

NF-κB is a classic pathway involved in inflammation, immunity, and cell survival, and it plays a role in osteoporosis treatment [63]. Under pathological conditions such as estrogen deficiency or chronic inflammation, persistently activated NF-κB promotes excessive osteoclast production by upregulating RANKL while simultaneously suppressing osteoblast function and survival, thereby shifting the bone remodeling balance toward net resorption.

Research indicates that ES suppresses the upstream production of key cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which trigger NF-κB activation [64]. By inhibiting NF-κB signaling, ES transforms the local microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory, osteoclastogenic state to one conducive to regeneration. This shift is characterized by reduced osteoclast formation and activity, alongside increased survival of osteoblasts and osteocytes, potentially achieved through modulation of anti-apoptotic factors like Bcl-2 [65]. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization promoted by ES is closely associated with an alternative NF-κB signaling pathway, which further enhances the pro-healing immune environment, thereby synergistically supporting angiogenesis and osteogenic processes [66] (Fig. 5).

In addition to osteoblast-mediated bone formation, osteoclasts also play a critical role in bone healing by regulating bone resorption and remodeling. Osteoclasts, which originate from the monocyte–macrophage lineage, are essential for maintaining the balance between bone formation and resorption during the repair process. Emerging evidence suggests that ES may also influence osteoclast differentiation and activity. Specifically, electrical cues have been reported to inhibit osteoclastogenesis by modulating the RANKL/OPG signaling axis and suppressing NF-κB activation, thereby reducing excessive bone resorption. Moreover, the coordinated regulation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts under ES is considered crucial for achieving optimal bone regeneration outcomes [67].

Crosstalk mechanisms among signaling pathways

While the above subsections describe how ES individually activates the Wnt/β-catenin, BMP/Smads, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways, an important question is whether these pathways function independently or interact with each other. Emerging evidence indicates that extensive crosstalk exists among these signaling cascades, forming a complex regulatory network that fine-tunes the cellular response to ES.

For instance, the BMP/Smad pathway and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway are not linearly independent; rather, Smad proteins can physically interact with β-catenin to co-activate osteogenic gene transcription, and ES has been shown to promote this interaction [68]. Similarly, MAPK signaling (especially ERK1/2) can phosphorylate and stabilize β-catenin, thereby amplifying Wnt-mediated osteogenesis under ES [69]. Conversely, activation of NF-κB by inflammatory stimuli interferes with both Wnt and BMP signaling by promoting β-catenin degradation and suppressing Smad nuclear translocation. ES can inhibit this interference by downregulating upstream inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β), thus relieving NF-κB-mediated suppression of pro-osteogenic pathways [70]. Furthermore, the p38 MAPK pathway, which is activated by ES, can also modulate the activity of Smad-independent BMP signaling, adding another layer of complexity [71]. Taken together, ES does not simply turn on individual pathways in isolation; instead, it orchestrates a network of interconnected signals, with key nodes such as β-catenin, Smads, and p38 serving as hubs for crosstalk. A deeper understanding of these interactions may reveal novel targets for enhancing bone repair.

Ion channel activation and extracellular matrix remodeling

Beyond the signaling pathways discussed above, a growing body of evidence indicates that ES exerts its pro-osteogenic effects through the direct regulation of ion channels, particularly voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) and stretch-activated calcium channels (SACCs), as well as through the remodeling of the ECM microenvironment. ES hyperpolarizes the cell membrane, leading to the opening of VGCCs and SACCs, which triggers Ca2+ influx from the extracellular space. This Ca2+ influx subsequently activates the Ca2+/calcineurin/NF-AT signaling cascade, promoting the expression of osteogenic genes such as TGF-β and BMP [72]. For instance, dynamic piezoelectric signals generated under low-frequency mechanical loading have been shown to activate VGCCs, triggering Ca2+ influx and intracellular redistribution, thereby directly driving osteogenic commitment of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells [73]. Similarly, capacitively coupled electric fields promote transmembrane calcium translocation via VGCCs, followed by activation of phospholipase A2 and increased prostaglandin E2 synthesis, which collectively enhance bone cell proliferation [74].

Mechanosensitive ion channels, particularly Piezo1 and Piezo2, have recently emerged as key mediators of the cellular response to ES. ES has been shown to upregulate the expression of Piezo1 and Piezo2, and the activation of Piezo1 induces Ca2+ influx, which in turn activates downstream pathways such as Akt/GSK3β/β-catenin to promote osteogenic differentiation [75]. Moreover, Piezo1 activation has been linked to the immunomodulatory effects of ES, where macrophage polarization toward the pro-repair M2 phenotype is enhanced [76].

Taken together, the regulation of ion channels, particularly VGCCs, SACCs, and Piezo1/2, and the remodeling of the ECM represent critical yet often underexplored mechanisms by which ES promotes bone repair. These insights not only deepen our understanding of ES mechanobiology but also provide valuable theoretical references for the design of electroactive biomaterials and the optimization of stimulation parameters.

Regulation of immune response

Modulation of inflammatory response

The inflammatory response is the initiating phase of the bone repair cascade, and its timely resolution is essential for successful regeneration. Through targeted regulation of immune cell activity and inflammatory factor networks, ES facilitates the transition from a pro-inflammatory microenvironment to a pro-reparative state, thereby establishing an immune microenvironment favorable for bone tissue repair and regeneration. Research indicates that ES significantly reduces local levels of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β, while upregulating the expression of anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 and TGF-β, thus promoting an osteogenesis-conducive immune homeostasis [77]. Additionally, ES markedly suppresses the IL-17/NF-κB signaling axis, thereby reducing inflammatory response [78], and alleviates inflammation through modulation of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α [79].

Regulation of immune cell metabolism

Beyond directly modulating inflammatory responses, ES significantly impacts the metabolic activity and functional status of immune cells (Fig. 6). It has been demonstrated that ES effectively activates key pro-angiogenic pathways, such as the VEGF signaling pathway, markedly improving hypoxia and inadequate nutrient supply in bone defect regions resulting from trauma and inflammation [80]. Qi et al. also indicated that ES can directionally modulate macrophage polarization, driving it toward the pro-repair M2 phenotype [81]. Wu et al. reported that the piezoelectric effect of polarized scaffolds promotes M2 macrophage polarization and osteogenic differentiation of mouse embryonic osteoblasts. Additionally, under ES, a higher proportion of M2 macrophages was observed in the peri-scaffold tissue [40]. Chen et al. found that ES increased vascular density in the treated region by 40%, along with a rise in the proportion of M2 macrophages, which together facilitated callus maturation [82]. This synergy between pro-angiogenic effects and immunomodulation is considered a key mechanism that accelerates bone healing.

ES synergistically enhances bone regeneration by coordinating immunomodulation and angiogenesis. It profoundly influences immune cell metabolism and function, activating the VEGF-PI3K-ERK pathway to promote angiogenesis, alleviate local hypoxia, and improve nutrient supply. Furthermore, ES guides macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, establishing a repair-favorable immune microenvironment. Importantly, growth factors represent critical downstream mediators linking ES-induced immune cell metabolic reprogramming with angiogenesis and osteogenesis. During the transition from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to a pro-reparative M2 phenotype, macrophages can increase the secretion of angiogenic and osteogenic growth factors, including VEGF, PDGF-BB, TGF-β, and BMP-related mediators. These factors promote endothelial cell migration, vascular maturation, mesenchymal stem/stromal cell recruitment, and osteogenic differentiation, thereby establishing an osteoimmune microenvironment favorable for bone regeneration. For example, Miao et al. reported that macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype was accompanied by increased VEGF and PDGF-BB expression, which enhanced early angiogenesis and promoted bone repair [29]. Similarly, ES-induced macrophage M2 polarization around piezoelectric BaTiO3-coated Ti6Al4V scaffolds was associated with improved bone regeneration, suggesting that electrical cues may amplify immune-regulated growth factor signaling during bone healing [40]. Therefore, the effect of ES on immune cell metabolism should not be interpreted solely as suppression of inflammation, but also as a growth factor-mediated osteoimmune regulatory mechanism that coordinates vascularization, callus maturation, and new bone formation [49,51].

In addition to macrophages, other immune cells such as T cells and B cells also participate in bone healing and may be influenced by ES. T cells have been reported to regulate osteogenesis through cytokine secretion and interaction with osteoblasts, while B cells can modulate bone remodeling via the production of OPG. Although current studies mainly focus on macrophage polarization, emerging evidence suggests that ES may also affect the function and metabolic activity of these immune cell subsets, thereby contributing to the overall immunomodulatory microenvironment during bone regeneration [83].

Synergistic coupling of osteogenesis, angiogenesis and inflammation resolution

The preceding sections have separately discussed how electrical stimulation promotes osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and inflammation resolution. However, these three processes are not independent. They are tightly coupled and mutually reinforcing during ES-mediated bone regeneration. Osteogenesis and angiogenesis are intrinsically linked because new bone formation requires a concomitant blood supply. ES-induced VEGF production not only stimulates endothelial cell proliferation but also directly enhances osteoblast differentiation [82]. Angiogenesis and immunomodulation are also interdependent. M2 macrophages secrete pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF and PDGF-BB, which promote vessel ingrowth, while newly formed blood vessels facilitate immune cell trafficking and the clearance of inflammatory debris [29]. Furthermore, inflammation resolution and osteogenesis are directly coupled. The shift from M1 to M2 macrophages reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-1β, which suppress bone formation [40]. At the same time, anti-inflammatory factors derived from M2 macrophages, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, create a pro-osteogenic microenvironment. Thus, electrical stimulation simultaneously activates a tripartite network that includes osteogenic pathways like BMP/Wnt/Runx2, angiogenic pathways like VEGF/PI3K/Akt, and immunomodulatory pathways such as M2 polarization mediated by NF-κB and Piezo1 [56,76,77]. The therapeutic efficacy of ES lies in its ability to orchestrate this triangular synergy, in which each process amplifies the others. This integrated perspective underscores that optimal bone regeneration cannot be achieved by targeting any single process alone. It also provides a rationale for combination strategies, for example, using ES together with pro-angiogenic scaffolds or anti-inflammatory biomaterials to maximize healing outcomes.

Preparation strategies of electrical stimulation materials for bone regeneration

To better translate the biological effects of ES into practical applications, the design and preparation of electroactive biomaterials have attracted increasing attention in bone tissue engineering. Typically, these materials are composed of biocompatible scaffolds integrated with conductive or piezoelectric components, enabling the generation or transmission of electrical cues at the defect site. Common conductive materials include carbon-based nanomaterials (e.g., graphene and carbon nanotubes) and conductive polymers such as polypyrrole (PPy) and polyaniline (PANI), which can facilitate electron transfer and enhance cell–material interactions. In addition, piezoelectric materials (e.g., barium titanate and polyvinylidene fluoride, PVDF) can generate electrical signals in response to mechanical loading, thereby mimicking the endogenous bioelectrical microenvironment of bone. From a fabrication perspective, various strategies such as electrospinning, 3D printing, and surface functionalization have been employed to construct electroactive scaffolds with tunable electrical properties and structural features. These approaches allow precise control over porosity, mechanical strength, and electrical conductivity, which are critical for promoting osteogenesis and angiogenesis. Overall, the rational design of electroactive biomaterials provides an effective platform for enhancing bone regeneration through ES [84].

Electrical stimulation and mechanotransduction in bone regeneration

Mechanotransduction is a fundamental process in bone regeneration, through which mechanical stimuli are converted into biochemical signals that regulate cellular behavior. Increasing evidence suggests that ES can interact with mechanotransduction pathways to influence bone healing. Specifically, electrical cues may enhance the sensitivity of cells to mechanical signals by modulating mechanosensitive ion channels, cytoskeletal organization, and focal adhesion formation. In addition, ES has been reported to activate key mechanotransduction-related signaling pathways, such as integrin/FAK and YAP/TAZ signaling, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation and matrix production. These findings indicate that ES and mechanical signaling are closely coupled processes, and their synergistic effects may provide new strategies for improving bone regeneration [85].

Comparative analysis of electrical stimulation protocols and advantages over traditional treatments

Different ES protocols, including direct current, pulsed direct current, and pulsed electromagnetic fields, have been shown to promote bone healing, but their efficacy varies depending on parameters such as intensity, frequency, and duration. For example, Sham et al. reported that continuous ES at both high and low intensities enhanced osteoblast activity and mineralization [39]. In contrast, Staehlke et al. observed that pulsed ES facilitated cell adhesion and spreading [41]. While direct comparisons remain limited, available evidence suggests that pulsed modalities may offer better tissue penetration and patient compliance due to their non-invasive nature. Clinical trial data from other fracture types also support the therapeutic relevance of pulsed electromagnetic stimulation. In a randomized controlled trial on mandibular fracture healing, pulsed electromagnetic field treatment improved clinical recovery-related outcomes compared with conventional management alone, indicating that PEMF may serve as a useful adjunctive modality across different skeletal sites [86]. Importantly, the optimal protocol likely depends on defect characteristics, such as size, location, and chronicity.

When compared with traditional treatments such as autografts, allografts, and artificial materials, ES offers several distinct advantages. Unlike autografts, ES avoids donor-site morbidity and secondary surgery [87]. Unlike allografts, ES carries no risk of immune rejection or disease transmission [3]. Furthermore, ES is non-invasive or minimally invasive, can be applied repeatedly, and is not limited by bone graft supply. However, ES also has limitations. Its efficacy may be influenced by patient-specific factors such as osteoporosis, and the optimal stimulation parameters have not been standardized. In some cases, such as large segmental defects, ES may not fully substitute for bone grafting but can serve as a valuable adjunct. Overall, ES represents a promising alternative or complementary strategy, particularly for patients at risk of delayed union or nonunion, or those who wish to avoid additional surgery.

Integration of ES with electroactive biomaterials

While the preceding subsections focus on ES applied directly to bone defects, a growing body of research combines ES with electroactive biomaterials to achieve more targeted and efficient bone regeneration. In this approach, the biomaterial such as conductive scaffold or piezoelectric membrane serves as a substrate that delivers, distributes, or amplifies the applied electrical signals, rather than merely being a passive filler. For instance, when a conductive hydrogel is implanted into a bone defect and connected to an external power source, the ES is conducted uniformly throughout the scaffold, promoting cell migration and osteogenic differentiation in three dimensions [88]. Similarly, a piezoelectric scaffold subjected to low-intensity pulsed ultrasound generates surface charges that mimic endogenous bioelectricity, and when combined with exogenous ES, the two electrical cues can synergistically enhance angiogenesis and callus formation [76]. Thus, the integration of ES with electroactive materials is not a simple addition but a synergistic strategy that exploits the electrical properties of the scaffold to optimize the spatiotemporal distribution of ES. A systematic analysis of how material parameters such as conductivity, porosity, and impedance influence the optimal ES settings including intensity, frequency, and duty cycle remains an urgent research need [89].

Summary

ES has emerged as a promising strategy for bone regeneration, offering significant advantages over traditional therapies by promoting osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation through multiple signaling pathways. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that electrical cues can effectively regulate cellular behaviors and enhance bone repair outcomes, particularly when combined with advanced biomaterials and tissue engineering approaches. Although traditional treatments for bone defects have achieved certain clinical efficacy, they still face numerous limitations, including insufficient osteointegration efficiency, immune rejection of allogeneic materials, and trauma associated with secondary surgeries. In recent years, ES has emerged as a promising non-invasive strategy that addresses many of these shortcomings. Moreover, different ES protocols can be tailored to specific defect types, although optimal parameters require further standardisation. The critical role of bioelectrical phenomena in regulating bone tissue metabolism has been increasingly elucidated. ES technology, leveraging mechanisms such as the piezoelectric effect, ion channel activation, and electric field-mediated cellular responses, has demonstrated unique potential in bone regeneration. By mimicking the physiological electrical microenvironment of bone, ES provides an innovative approach to bone defect repair that overcomes the constraints of conventional bone grafts and fixation devices.

Despite these advances, several research gaps remain. First, the field suffers from substantial heterogeneity in stimulation parameters. Variables such as field strength, frequency, stimulation duration, and electrode placement vary widely across studies, making it difficult to compare outcomes or establish standardised clinical protocols. Second, our understanding of the precise molecular mechanisms remains incomplete. While studies have identified several signalling pathways involved in electrically induced bone repair, including the MAPK, Wnt/β-catenin and BMP pathways, it remains unclear how these pathways are coordinated across different cell types and how their activation patterns evolve over the course of healing. Third, the long-term safety of implantable ES devices requires systematic evaluation. Issues such as electrode corrosion, chronic inflammation and off-target effects remain understudied in large-animal models. Fourth, most studies have been conducted on otherwise healthy bone defects, whereas clinical scenarios frequently involve comorbidities such as osteoporosis, diabetes or chronic infection. The efficacy of ES under these conditions remains poorly characterised.

Notably, the majority of existing preclinical studies investigate ES in healthy or simplified models. There is an urgent need for further research employing clinically relevant comorbid disease models such as osteoporosis, diabetes, and infection to rigorously assess ES efficacy and safety across these challenging pathological contexts. Moreover, the progression from bench to bedside mandates well-designed large animal studies and multicenter randomized controlled clinical trials that can establish standardized stimulation protocols, confirm therapeutic efficacy, and evaluate long-term safety. Addressing these gaps is essential to fully translate ES into routine clinical orthopaedic practice.

Looking forward, we propose several future directions. Firstly, standardisation of stimulation protocols requires systematic dose-response studies to define optimal parameters for different defect types and patient populations. Secondly, smart and closed-loop systems that integrate ES with real-time biosensors and self-powered technologies such as triboelectric or piezoelectric nanogenerators could enable adaptive stimulation that responds to the dynamic healing microenvironment. Thirdly, synergy with electroactive biomaterials can be achieved by combining ES with conductive scaffolds like graphene, PPy or piezoelectric ceramics and employing 3D or 4D printing techniques to enable spatiotemporally controlled personalized bone regeneration. Fourthly, translation to comorbid conditions means preclinical studies using osteoporotic, diabetic or infected defect models are urgently needed to validate the efficacy of ES in clinically relevant settings. Fifth, large-animal and clinical trials with well-designed multicentre randomised controlled trials and standardised endpoints will be essential to establish evidence-based guidelines.

Feasibility and challenges must also be acknowledged. The miniaturisation and wireless powering of implantable ES devices are technically feasible but require rigorous biocompatibility testing and regulatory approval. Patient compliance with non-invasive ES (e.g., daily sessions over weeks to months) may be a barrier. Cost-effectiveness analyses will determine whether ES can be widely adopted. Nevertheless, given the robust preclinical and clinical evidence accumulated to date, it is anticipated that therapeutic systems incorporating ES will be further refined and translated into routine orthopaedic practice in the near future, offering promising outcomes for patients with bone defects. In summary, ES represents a versatile and powerful tool in bone tissue engineering. With continued interdisciplinary efforts, it is expected to play an increasingly important role in the development of next-generation therapies for bone defect repair.
