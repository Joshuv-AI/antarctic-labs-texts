Magnetron sputtering to enhance bone integration of tantalum-coated titanium implants: an in vitro and in vivo analysis
Authors: Jialiang Li, Yuxin Gong, Yuewen Xue, Zhengtuan Guo, Chong Xie, Fangfang Xu, Guangbin Zhao, Zhengjie Lin, Xijing He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTi6Al4V (TC4) widely used in bone implants, has good mechanical properties but unremarkable bone-forming capacity. Tantalum (Ta) features excellent biocompatibility and suitability for osteogenesis, albeit with a significantly higher elastic-modulus. In this study, we combined the strengths of both materials to optimize implant materials. Magnetron sputtering was applied to deposit a Ta coating onto the TC4 surface (Ta-C-TC4). Surface characteristics were assessed via scanning electron microscope (SEM). Cell adhesion was assessed using SEM and cytoskeletal staining, while live/dead staining was used to evaluate cell viability and biocompatibility on the material surfaces. For proliferation analysis, fluorescence transfection and CCK-8 assay were utilized, while quantification of substance and qRT-PCR were employed to assess the osteogenic differentiation. In vivo, fluorescence labelling, VG, and Goldner staining were employed to evaluate bone integration. A 550 nm-thick Ta coating was successfully achieved on Ta-C-TC4, and its elements and morphology closely resembled Ta. Cells exhibited more pronounced proliferation and differentiation on Ta and Ta-C-TC4. More extensive encasement of new bone was observed around Ta and Ta-C-TC4. Ta-C-TC4 exhibits biocompatibility on par with Ta and demonstrates superior bone integration compared to TC4. Introduction

Bone implant materials play a pivotal role in determining their mechanical attributes and bone integration capabilities. Among these materials, titanium alloys (Ti6Al4V, TC4) have gained widespread acceptance in orthopedics due to their capacity to swiftly establish robust support post-implantation, averting the risk of surrounding bone collapse and promptly reinstating bone stability. Furthermore, foundational research has revealed TC4’s ability to support cell growth without incurring biological corrosion [1], thereby giving rise to a profusion of TC4 implants. Nevertheless, with the passage of time, several investigations have unveiled that TC4 implants do not always fulfil expectations concerning bone formation. Furthermore, numerous instances of implant failures have arisen, potentially attributable to their limited bone integration prowess and the production of metal fragments through wear and tear [2, 3].

In recent years, research into metallic materials has revealed tantalum (Ta) to possess remarkable corrosion resistance, wear resistance, and exceptional biocompatibility [4–6]. Furthermore, when compared to TC4, Ta lacks potentially toxic ions, such as aluminum and vanadium ions, making it an increasingly prominent focus of research. Studies have showcased the impressive osteogenic potential of porous Ta to the extent that it has been proposed for use as metallic bone struts [7–10]. However, Ta faces a significant challenge due to its greater weight than TC4 and a higher elastic modulus. Biomechanical research has indicated that an excessively high elastic modulus, leading to stress shielding, may have an adverse impact on bone formation [11]. Therefore, considering the standalone application of Ta as a bone implant is considered impractical.

Despite its favorable mechanical properties and corrosion resistance, Ti-6Al-4 V (TC4) alloy exhibits suboptimal biological integration, especially in the early stages following implantation. This can result in insufficient bone-implant contact, poor early fixation, and long-term loosening or failure. On the other hand, although tantalum is known for its excellent biocompatibility and osteoconductivity, its high elastic modulus and manufacturing limitations restrict its widespread application in load-bearing implants. Moreover, the elastic modulus mismatch between dense metal implants and surrounding bone can lead to stress shielding, bone resorption, and eventual implant destabilization. Therefore, developing a surface modification strategy that imparts Ta-like bioactivity onto a TC4 substrate—while preserving favorable mechanical compatibility—may help overcome these challenges and improve clinical outcomes.

Consequently, adopting the concept of material composites and amalgamating the strengths of TC4 and Ta is deemed a viable approach to enhancing bone implant materials [12]. The interface between implant materials and bone plays a crucial role in determining implant stability [13], and surface coating treatment offers a logical means of uniting these two elements. By capitalizing on the benefits of both materials and employing Ta for coating TC4, it becomes possible to retain the excellent mechanical properties of TC4 while harnessing Ta’s superior bone integration capabilities. Compared with conventional techniques such as anodization or electrochemical deposition, magnetron sputtering offers distinct advantages, including precise control of coating thickness, excellent film uniformity, and strong interfacial adhesion [4]. Unlike thermal spray or PVD methods that may involve higher processing temperatures or generate less uniform coatings, magnetron sputtering operates under relatively low temperatures and allows for conformal coating of substrates with complex geometries [14]. These characteristics make it more suitable for biomedical applications requiring nanoscale surface control and clinical scalability.

This study utilized 3D printing technology for the fabrication of TC4 and Ta and employed magnetron sputtering to apply a Ta coating onto the TC4 surface (Ta-C-TC4). Comparative characterization of pure materials and coated materials was carried out to verify the potential of magnetron sputtering for surface modification in bone implant materials. Additionally, in vitro and in vivo biological experiments were conducted to analyze and validate whether Ta-C-TC4 demonstrates superior osteogenic performance and its potential for broader applications.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Ta coated materials

Titanium alloys (Ti6Al4V, TC4) specimens were fabricated using a selective laser melting (SLM) 3D printing system (BLT-S200, Bright Laser Technologies, China). The processing parameters were as follows: laser power, 225 W; laser scanning speed, 1500 mm/s; powder layer thickness, 0.03 mm; and hatching distance, 0.1 mm. The Ti-6Al-4 V (Grade 5) powder used in the process was also provided by Bright Laser Technologies, with a particle size distribution of 15–53 μm(Fig. 1a). After 3D printing, the support structures were removed mechanically using a high-speed rotary tool. The printed specimens were then subjected to stress-relief heat treatment at 800 °C for 2 h under vacuum to reduce residual thermal stress. No hot isostatic pressing (HIP) was performed. Following heat treatment, the samples were ground using SiC papers (grit #400 to #1200) to achieve a uniform surface finish prior to sputtering. These steps ensured consistent baseline surface conditions for all groups. After fabrication, the samples were polished to achieve a consistent surface finish and ultrasonically cleaned in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water (10 min each). A tantalum (Ta) coating (Fig. 1b)was deposited on selected TC4 samples using a direct current (DC) magnetron sputtering system (JGP500A, SKY Technology Development Co., Ltd., China). The working pressure was maintained at 0.8 Pa, the argon flow rate was 45 sccm, and the sputtering power was set to 120 W. The sputtering time was adjusted to obtain coatings of different thicknesses (e.g., 250 nm, 500 nm, and 750 nm). During deposition, the rod-shaped samples were mounted on a rotating holder (25 r/min) to ensure uniform coating on all exposed surfaces. All specimens were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 30 min prior to biological experiments.

(a-b) Schematic diagram of the SLM 3D printing and Ta coating prepared by magnetron sputtering. (c-d) The surgical process of implantation and postoperative X-ray detection of rods

For experimental grouping, uncoated TC4 samples served as the TC4 group; bulk porous tantalum cylinders (commercially obtained from Zhongbang Medical, China) served as the Ta group; and TC4 specimens coated with tantalum via magnetron sputtering were designated as the Ta-C-TC4 group.

Surface characterization

Material samples were placed in a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JEOL, Japan) to examine surface morphology and coating characteristics at varying magnifications. Additionally, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) functionality was employed to detect surface elements. The adhesion strength between the tantalum (Ta) coating and TC4 titanium alloy substrate was evaluated using a scratch test. The experiment was performed using a CSM Revetest Scratch Tester (CSM Instruments, Switzerland) to measure the critical load at which the coating detached from the substrate.A diamond indenter with a tip radius of 200 μm was used. The scratch test was performed with a constant load of 50 N cm⁻¹, and the indenter was dragged along the surface of the coated samples at a speed of 1 mm/s for a total distance of 5 mm. The load was gradually increased during the test to assess the critical load at which the coating detached from the substrate.

Cell compatibility and proliferation assessment

For this experiment, MC3T3 cells (subclone 14) and green fluorescent protein (GFP)-transfected MC3T3 cells (Genechem, China) were utilized. Cells were directly seeded onto the surface of sterilized samples placed in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per sample and cultured in α-MEM (containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S) (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every other day. After 24 h of incubation, the material samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 8 h, followed by dehydration in a graded ethanol series and vacuum drying. Surface cell morphology was analyzed using SEM. Cell viability was assessed using a Calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide (PI) live/dead staining kit (Solebo, China). After culturing, samples were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adherent cells. A working solution was prepared by diluting the stock solution to 1× concentration according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 500 µL of the 1× working solution was added to each well, and samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. After staining, samples were gently rinsed with PBS before imaging under a fluorescence microscope. Cell viability was assessed under an inverted fluorescence microscope. The samples were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton-X100 and stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-phalloidin and DAPI (Solarbio Science & Technology, China) to observe cell cytoskeletal morphology. After removing the culture medium, each sample was gently rinsed with PBS to eliminate non-adherent cells and residual serum. Then, 500 µL of highly sensitive CCK-8 working solution (diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions) was added to each well of the 24-well plate, ensuring complete immersion of the samples. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in the dark. After incubation, the reaction mixture was thoroughly mixed, and 100 µL aliquots were transferred to a 96-well plate. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Blank controls (wells containing CCK-8 solution without cells or material) were included, and their average OD values were subtracted from the experimental readings to correct for background. Quantitative evaluation of cell viability was performed using the CCK8 method on days 1, 4, and 7 of cell culture. Additionally, the proliferation of GFP-MC3T3 cells was monitored (n = 3).

Osteogenic differentiation evaluation

The induction medium was based on α-MEM (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10⁻⁸ mol/L dexamethasone, 10 mmol/L β-sodium 3-phosphoglycerate (β-glycerophosphate), and 50 mg/L vitamin C (ascorbic acid).Osteogenic differentiation medium(Gibco, USA) was added on day 7 after cell seeding. Differentiation-related assays were then performed on day 10 and day 20 post-seeding, corresponding to day 3 and day 13 of induction, respectively. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was performed using RNA extracted from three independent biological replicates (n = 3 per group), each representing a separately cultured and induced cell population. For each replicate, qPCR reactions were performed in technical triplicates, and the mean value was used for further statistical analysis. Alkaline phosphatase (Alp) activity and extracellular matrix mineralization levels were assessed at both early (day 10) and late (day 20) differentiation stages (n = 3). After induction for 10 and 20 days, cells were harvested and lysed. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). ALP activity was measured using an alkaline phosphatase assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was read at 405 nm using a microplate reader, and ALP activity was normalized to total protein content (U/mg protein). After 21 days of osteogenic induction, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed with PBS, and then stained with 2% Alizarin Red S solution (pH 4.2, Solarbio, China) for 30 min to label calcium deposits. The stained samples were gently washed with distilled water to remove excess dye. For quantification, the bound dye was extracted using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride in 10 mM sodium phosphate solution and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Simultaneously, qRT-PCR was employed to examine the expression of osteogenic-related genes, including Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh), Alkaline phosphatase (Alp), Bone morphogenetic protein 2 (Bmp2), Osteopontin (Opn), and Osteocalcin (Ocn) genes (n = 3), with the primer sequences detailed in Table 1.

Surgical implantation procedure

All animal studies were done in compliance with the regulations and guidelines of Xi’an Jiaotong University institutional animal care and conducted according to the AAALAC and the IACUC guidelines. For this experiment, twelve adult male New Zealand rabbits (provided by the Animal Centre of Xi’an Jiaotong University) were utilized. Following anaesthesia (urethane, 1.3 g/kg), the anesthetic method is intravenous injection. For adult rabbits (weighing approximately 2–3 kg), the dose of urethane was 1.3 g/kg, with effects occurring 20–30 min after injection. The experiment was conducted once the animals showed no voluntary movement, exhibited stable breathing, and the corneal reflex had disappeared. An incision was made on the lateral condyle of the femur, and subcutaneous tissues were progressively separated to expose the femoral condyle. After drilling, the specimens (TC4, Ta, and Ta-C-TC4 rods) were individually implanted (n = 8), and the incision was sutured and disinfected (Fig. 1c). Post-surgery, the anesthetic effect of urethane typically wanes gradually over several hours. During recovery, appropriate anesthetic recovery measures were taken, including maintaining a warm environment and monitoring cardiopulmonary function to ensure the animals regained full consciousness. X-ray examinations were conducted to verify the implantation sites (Fig. 1d). Daily disinfection and cefuroxime antibiotic injections were administered for 7 days post-surgery. Sampling for subsequent assessments was performed at 4 and 8 weeks post-operation.

In vivo fluorescence labelling and histological staining

To label newly formed bone at different time points, calcein and alizarin complexone (Sigma, USA) were intravenously injected at 3 and 6 weeks post-surgery. Calcein (10 mg/kg) and alizarin complexone (30 mg/kg) (both purchased from Sigma, USA) were used for double fluorescent labeling of mineralized bone. The dyes were administered via intramuscular injection into the lateral thigh muscles of each rabbit. Tissue samples were procured and processed to produce approximately 30-micron-thick hard tissue sections (LEICA, Germany). These sections were subsequently observed for fluorescent labelling of new bone surfaces under inverted fluorescence and laser confocal microscopes (OLYMPUS). Additionally, hard tissue sections underwent Van Gieson (VG) staining and Goldner staining to observe newly formed bone on the material surfaces. The tissue sections were prepared and cleaned using an ultrasonic cleaning system to ensure no contamination. The procedure for VG staining was as follows: The tissue sections were immersed in 1% formic acid for 3 min, followed by washing with tap water for 3 min. Sections were then soaked in 20% methanol for 2 h, followed by rinsing with water for 3 min. The tissue was stained with methylene blue solution (heated) for 3 min, followed by washing in warm water until no further color leached. The final staining solution was prepared by mixing 1% acidic ponceau with 1.22% bitter acid (saturated aqueous solution), and sections were stained for 15 min. Afterward, sections were washed with absolute ethanol and dried. Finally, the sections were observed under stereomicroscopy and optical microscopy. For Goldner’s trichrome staining, the tissue sections underwent a gradient ethanol rehydration process before staining: Sections were stained in Weigert’s iron hematoxylin solution for 5 min, followed by washing with water for 1 min. Acid differentiation was performed for 5 s, followed by 5 min of washing in water. Sections were then stained with Acid Ponceau solution for 5 min, followed by a wash with weak acid working solution for 15 s. The tissue was subsequently stained with Orange G solution for 5 min and washed again with the weak acid working solution for 15 s. Finally, sections were stained with bright green solution for 5 min, washed with the weak acid solution for 15 s, and then rinsed with distilled water.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using SPSS (version 19.0, SPSS Inc., USA). A repeated-measures analysis of variance (Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed for comparison of the three groups. P-value < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Surface characteristics of materials

As shown in Fig. 2a at 100x magnification, the TC4 surface appears relatively smooth, while the Ta surface exhibits a porous and loosely structured morphology. Further magnification at 10,000x confirms the porosity of Ta, and the Ta-C-TC4 surface showcases the overlay of Ta with its distinctive porous architecture (Fig. 2b). Additionally, Fig. 2b indicates that the thickness of the Ta coating on Ta-C-TC4 is approximately 550 nm. Elemental analysis via EDS (Fig. 2c) reveals a composite elemental composition of TC4 and Ta metal for the Ta-C-TC4 material, with Ta being the dominant surface metal element (Table 2). The results of the water contact angle experiments confirmed that TC4 is around 71˚, Ta is around 68.2˚ and 69.5˚ for Ta-C-TC4, whose contact angles are less than 90 degrees. Thus, the samples can be attributed to their high hydrophilicity (SFigure 1). To evaluate the adhesion strength between the sputtered tantalum (Ta) coating and the TC4 substrate, a scratch test was conducted. The critical load at which coating delamination and visible failure occurred was recorded as 39.184 N (SFigure 2). This value indicates a strong bonding between the Ta coating and the TC4 surface, which supports our conclusion that the sputtered Ta coating adheres well to the titanium alloy substrate. The AFM results can be summarized as follows (SFigure 3): TC4 exhibited a relatively smooth surface with a mean roughness (Ra) of approximately 0.25 ± 0.03 μm.Ta-C-TC4 showed intermediate roughness at 0.42 ± 0.05 μm, consistent with a fine-grained, sputtered coating. Bulk Ta displayed a significantly higher roughness of 0.85 ± 0.07 μm, attributable to its inherent porous microstructure.

Cell adhesion and biocompatibility

SEM observations (Fig. 3a) depict uniform cell adhesion across all material surfaces, with more extensive adhesion observed within the porous interstices of Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces. The porous structure enhances the available surface area for cell contact. Living/dead staining (Fig. 3b) exhibits that the living cells predominantly distributed, although some dead cells could be seen on the surface of each material, possibly linked to the staining process. Examination of cell cytoskeletal morphology (Fig. 3c) demonstrates robust cell adhesion on all material surfaces, with cells adopting a polygonal shape conducive to proliferation. Notably, cell density appears lower on TC4 surfaces when compared to Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces. Furthermore, early-stage cells on Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces display extensive cellular connections.

Cell proliferation

Observations of GFP-MC3T3 cells (Fig. 4a) illustrate an expanding cell proliferation area over time, with more pronounced cell confluent on Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces. Quantitative fluorescence analysis (Fig. 4b) further corroborates the larger cell areas on Ta-C-TC4 and Ta surfaces (P < 0.05). Quantitative proliferation analysis (Fig. 4c) reveals that on the first day of cell culture, Ta-C-TC4 surfaces host a greater number of cells compared to TC4 surfaces (P < 0.05). With extended culture time, cell numbers increase on all material surfaces. However, on days 4 and 7, TC4 surfaces consistently support fewer cells than the other materials (P < 0.05), with no significant difference observed between Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces (P>0.05).

Cell differentiation

As depicted in Fig. 4d, during the early stages of differentiation, Ta-C-TC4 displays a higher ALP concentration compared to TC4 (P < 0.01), with no significant disparity observed between Ta and Ta-C-TC4. Over time, extracellular matrix mineralization undergoes maturation, with TC4 showing lower maturity in the initial stages compared to Ta and lower maturity in the later stages compared to Ta-C-TC4 (P < 0.05). There are no noteworthy distinctions of substances related to osteogenic differentiation between Ta and Ta-C-TC4. As shown in Fig. 4e, the expression of osteogenesis-related genes exhibited distinct temporal patterns corresponding to different stages of osteogenic differentiation. On day 10, Alp (alkaline phosphatase) and Bmp2 (bone morphogenetic protein-2), which are indicative of early-stage osteogenic commitment and matrix maturation, were significantly upregulated in both the Ta and Ta-C-TC4 groups compared to TC4. This suggests that these surfaces promote early osteoblast differentiation. By day 20, the expression levels of Ocn (osteocalcin) and Opn (osteopontin), which are markers of late-stage differentiation and mineralization, increased significantly, particularly in the Ta-C-TC4 group. This shift in gene expression pattern—from early to late markers—reflects a continuous progression of osteogenesis supported by the surface-modified materials. The Ta-C-TC4 group demonstrated both enhanced early commitment and sustained mineralization activity, indicating its superior capacity to guide cells through the full osteogenic process.

Histological staining of new bone

As presented in Fig. 5a, VG staining illustrates the presence of bone formation on all material surfaces. At 4 weeks post-surgery, TC4 surfaces exhibit intermittent new bone formation, whereas Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces display more continuous bone formation. With an extended implantation duration, at 8 weeks, TC4 rods are still partially uncovered by new bone, whereas Ta and Ta-C-TC4 rods exhibit more comprehensive coverage with improved interconnectedness. Goldner staining (Fig. 5b) reveals visible mature mineralized bone on TC4 surfaces, although the coverage remains incomplete. In contrast, Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces exhibit a substantial presence of mature bone tissue which represents better integration activity.

Discussion

The bone integration ability, encompassing support for cell growth and differentiation as well as the promotion of bone tissue growth on the surface, stands as a pivotal attribute among materials used in bone implants [5]. Presently, implant materials encompass a variety of substances, including metals, ceramics, and polymers [6]. Calcium-phosphate ceramics, such as hydroxyapatite (HA) and bidirectional calcium phosphate (BCP), have been favoured in earlier experiments as bone implant materials due to their resemblance of the inorganic elements of bone [7]. Nevertheless, their brittleness renders them unsuitable for autonomous use as bone implants [8]. Conversely, more pliable polymers like polyethylene and polyether ether ketone (PEEK), although it has good mechanical properties, exhibit diminished support for cell growth and a propensity to generate wear debris, which can incite local inflammation [9, 10].

Metal materials, owing to their commendable compatibility with skeletal mechanics, are the most widely employed, with Ti alloys, typified by TC4, being particularly prominent [11]. These alloys possess ductility, corrosion resistance, and the capacity to sustain cell growth [12]. Nevertheless, despite the manifold advantages of Ti alloys, research into their application as implants has uncovered issues such as the generation of wear debris and suboptimal bone-inducing properties [13]. These challenges constitute pivotal factors influencing osteointegration capability and, consequently, the enduring stability of implants [14]. Recent investigations have disclosed that Ta naturally incorporates a porous environment and even surpasses traditional metal materials in biocompatibility [15]. Nevertheless, due to the constraints imposed by high processing conditions, utilizing Ta as a standalone internal implant proves to be a formidable task. Moreover, Pure Ta (186 GPa) exhibits an excessively high elastic modulus compared to TC4 (110 GPa), potentially instigating stress shielding effects which may cause resorption of adjacent bone [16]. Thus, amalgamating the advantages of both materials emerges as a potentially efficacious approach to enhancing the performance of implant materials.

The integration of functions holds significance in scientific research, and surface coating, as one of the methods for material integration, represents a pivotal approach for harnessing the advantages of two disparate materials [17]. Traditional surface treatment techniques, such as immersion, sandblasting, and chemical treatments, often yield coatings with inadequate adhesion, thereby posing the risk of detachment [18, 19]. In contrast, magnetron sputtering, originating from industrial methods for treating metal surfaces, can yield compact metal coatings on the substrate’s surface characterized by their superior adhesion [20]. Consequently, it emerges as one of the feasible techniques for amalgamating TC4 and Ta.

Since the primary purpose of bone implants is to restore mechanical support [21], the selection of TC4 as the substrate material stands as a logical choice. The presence of a Ta coating on the TC4 surface does not exert a substantial influence on the overall mechanical properties of the material [22]. Consequently, magnetron sputtering emerges as a promising method for amalgamating TC4’s robust mechanical attributes with Ta’s remarkable biocompatibility. Within the scope of this experiment, TC4 and Ta were effectively manufactured through SLM 3D printing, culminating in the successful production of a Ta coating with a thickness measuring 550 nm on TC4 surface, achieved through magnetron sputtering. The surface of the Ta-C-TC4 boasts a heightened Ta concentration, and its surface structure closely mirrors that of pure Ta, manifesting as porous textured. This structural characteristic enhances cell adhesion when contrasted with the flat TC4 surface [23, 24].

In vitro experiments, focusing on the observation of cell morphology on the material surfaces, consistently corroborate the superior cell adhesion capabilities inherent to Ta-C-TC4. Furthermore, viability staining results affirm that all materials support cell growth, a fundamental criterion for the clinical applicability of both Ta and TC4 materials [25]. Additionally, the heightened cell fusion observed on the surfaces of Ta and Ta-C-TC4 underscores the favourable impact of Ta on cell functionality, presumably attributable to its distinctive micro-porous attributes [26]. The presence of surface roughness and micro-porosity significantly amplifies the cell contact area, thereby fostering three-dimensional cellular behaviours [27, 28]. Proliferation assays conducted on the material surfaces yield analogous outcomes, with cells manifesting more vigorous growth on both Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces. Furthermore, mineralization assays reveal that cells on Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces accumulate bone mineralization substances more extensively. Consequently, Ta likely exerts influence over the biological processes governing cell proliferation and differentiation. Earlier reports have indicated that Ta impacts the integrin-β1-activated AKT/MAPK signaling pathway, thereby affecting tissue adhesion [29]. Additionally, Ta might wield influence over osteogenesis through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, BMP signaling pathway, and TGF-β signaling pathway [30]. The present study also unveils the upregulation of early differentiation genes, specifically ALP and BMP-2 genes, along with mineralization genes OCN, in response to the presence of surface Ta.

In vivo experiments have revealed the presence of bone formation on the surface of TC4, accompanied by mineralization. However, at a later stage, the new bone formation on TC4 surfaces remained sporadic and discontinuous. In contrast, on both Ta and Ta-C-TC4 surfaces, irrespective of the early or late post-surgery periods, there was a more abundant presence of newly formed bone, densely mineralized and enveloping the materials. This strongly implies that Ta and Ta-C-TC4 exhibit superior osteogenic capabilities in comparison to TC4. Interestingly, in vivo fluorescent staining indicated that early and late-stage new bone formation on Ta-C-TC4 surfaces was even more pronounced than on Ta. This phenomenon can be attributed to the prolonged implantation period, during which the high elastic modulus of Ta might have influenced bone growth [31–33]. Previous reports have emphasized the significance of mechanical stimulation as a crucial factor in bone growth [34–36]. The excessive elastic modulus of Ta could induce stress shielding effects, resulting in a relatively reduced bone stress stimulus around the material, subsequently leading to less conspicuous new bone formation when compared to Ta-C-TC4. It can be inferred that with extended time, the impact of Ta on the surrounding bone might become more pronounced.

This study successfully achieved the surface deposition of a Ta coating on TC4 and confirmed the excellent biocompatibility and remarkable bone integration capabilities of Ta-C-TC4 material (Fig. 7). Although Col1a1 is a critical early marker of osteoblast activity and collagen matrix formation, it was not analyzed in the present study and will be included in future investigations to further characterize early-stage osteogenesis [37–39]. Nevertheless, this study is not without its limitations. Due to the dense nature of Ta and the characteristics of the deposition process, it is inevitable that Ta-C-TC4 surfaces are morphologically close to Ta, which may affect cellular behavior and osteointegration effects in conjunction with their chemical compositions [40]. Additionally, postoperative evaluation in this study focused solely on X-ray imaging to confirm implant positioning. Other clinically relevant parameters—such as bone damage, implant migration, or postoperative infection—were not systematically assessed [41, 42]. Although no overt complications were observed during the 4 and 8week periods, future studies should incorporate comprehensive postoperative monitoring, including clinical scoring, micro-CT analysis, and histological evaluation, to ensure a more complete assessment of implant safety and performance. Due to the high density of Ta, imaging examinations may produce significant metallic artefacts, posing challenges in quantitatively analyzing the bone volume using imaging techniques such as micro-computed tomography. Furthermore, the mechanisms underlying Ta’s influence on osteogenic cells still require further investigation. In conclusion, magnetron sputtering proves to be a reliable approach for amalgamating the advantages of different metal materials. The findings of this study hold relevance for the modification of bone implant materials.

Conclusion

This study, addressing the issue of relatively weak osteogenic capabilities in traditional metal materials, relied on magnetron sputtering technology to deposit a Ta coating on the surface of TC4, thereby enhancing its bone integration performance. The comprehensive findings reveal that:

Magnetron sputtering technology facilitates a robust bonding between TC4 and Ta, resulting in a porous rough surface morphology similar to Ta.
