Efficient and Accurate Modeling of Anisotropic Electrostatic Landscapes in Amorphous Organic Semiconductor Films
Authors: Yoonki Kim, Jiho Son, Geongi Moon, Hyeonsik Choi, Jaeyoung Gil, Jay-Hak Lee, YounJoon Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise modeling of the energetic landscape is a prerequisite for predicting the charge transport properties of organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs). However, a significant gap remains between highly accurate but computationally prohibitive self-consistent field (SCF) calculations and efficient but often oversimplified models. In this work, we propose an accurate and effective electrostatic framework with high computational efficiency that encompasses these complex polarization effects through an anisotropically screened dielectric function augmented by a position-dependent background potential. Optimized for the archetypal host material 4,4′-Bis­(N-carbazolyl)-1,1′-biphenyl (CBP), our model accurately reproduces the microscopic details, including the polarization-induced stabilization and the surface-reduced energetic disorder, while maintaining high transferability across independent morphological realizations and film thicknesses down to D ≈ 4 nm. Kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations further confirm that the model faithfully replicates the reference mean squared displacement (MSD) and current–voltage (JV) characteristics, whereas simple image charge models significantly underestimate the current density by failing to describe the downhill gradient at the interface. This framework offers a practical pathway for generating realistic energy distributions for large-scale device simulations, effectively bridging the tradeoff between physical accuracy and computational efficiency.

Introduction

Organic Light-Emitting Diodes (OLEDs) have emerged as a cornerstone technology for next-generation displays and high-efficiency solid-state lighting markets. Their prominence stems from the inherent advantages offered by their self-emissive properties, including superior contrast ratios, rapid response times, and the capability for flexible form factors. , Recently, driven by the escalating demand for high color gamut and high-luminance devices, extensive research has been directed toward maximizing device performance through sophisticated multilayer architectures and the introduction of novel materials. However, the fundamental internal processes, such as charge carrier injection, transport, and exciton recombination, involve highly complex physical mechanisms inherent to disordered organic semiconductors. Relying solely on empirical trial-and-error approaches to analyze and optimize these intricate systems is often prohibitively time-consuming and costly. Consequently, the role of theoretical modeling and numerical simulation has become paramount to reliably predict microscopic physical phenomena within the device and to provide essential guidelines for efficient device design.

Among various modeling approaches, kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations have become widely accepted due to their unique ability to embody the stochastic nature of charge carrier dynamics at a microscopic level. − Unlike macroscopic continuum models, such as the drift-diffusion equation, KMC explicitly addresses the disordered nature of amorphous organic semiconductors, where charge transport occurs via hopping between localized states. In this framework, the site energy, the electronic energy level assigned to each localized molecule or segment, serves as a fundamental governing factor. The energy landscape, defined by the statistical distribution and spatial correlation of these site energies, directly determines the hopping rates and the probability of charge trapping or detrapping events. Therefore, the precise modeling of site energy is not merely a numerical parameter but a critical prerequisite for predicting charge carrier mobility and the current–voltage (JV) characteristics of OLEDs. Complementary to the electrostatic modeling approach pursued here, semiempirical methods have also proven effective for describing energy and charge transfer in large amorphous organic systems, including Marcus-theory-based charge transport in donor–acceptor polymers and nonadiabatic excited-state molecular dynamics simulations of low-bandgap conjugated materials. ,

The most rigorous and accessible method for calculating site energies involves SCF polarization calculations utilizing the Thole model and Distributed Multipole Analysis (DMA). However, due to its unfavorable computational scaling with system size, this approach has primarily been restricted to small 3D periodic systems for predicting charge carrier mobility. To simulate device-level data such as JV characteristics, it is essential to employ a 2D periodic slab geometry that incorporates image charges and polarization effects induced by parallel electrodes. In such geometries, calculating the infinite series of image interactionseven with necessary truncationimposes a computational cost several times higher than that of 3D periodic systems. To overcome this computational bottleneck, conventional approaches have predominantly relied on lattice-based models, ,,,, often invoking the Gaussian Disorder Model (GDM) to describe the statistical distribution of site energies. In these frameworks, the potential landscape is typically constructed by superimposing a stochastic, spatially uncorrelated Gaussian disorder onto a deterministic electrostatic potential. Furthermore, to render the boundary-induced electrostatics tractable, molecules are simplified as point particles, restricting the calculation to the interactions between permanent multipoles and their image charges.

However, full SCF calculations in slab geometries, which account for induced polarization, reveal a site energy landscape that fundamentally deviates from these simplified models. In a model considering only permanent electrostatic interactions, the energy is lower near the electrodes due to image stabilization and increases toward the center, forming a mound-shaped profile that plateaus in the bulk region. In contrast, the full SCF results exhibit an overall bowl-shaped trend. The decomposition of the SCF energy into permanent and polarization components reveals that while the permanent electrostatic component indeed follows the aforementioned concave behavior, the induction energy provides a more significant stabilization that increases in magnitude toward the center of the system. Because this induced polarization effect outweighs the permanent electrostatic interaction, the resulting total site energy profile forms a potential well that stabilizes toward the center. This potential well represents a critical physical feature that simplified point-particle approximations fail to depict.

To bridge the gap between computational feasibility and physical accuracy, previous efforts have focused on developing distance-dependent isotropically screened dielectric functions. ,− These approaches are based on the phenomenon of polarization saturation, where the dielectric response is spatially dependent: the dielectric constant approaches that of a vacuum (εr ≈ 1.0) in the immediate vicinity of a charged molecule and converges to the bulk valueoften estimated by the Clausius-Mossotti relationat greater distances. In the present work, we propose an effective electrostatic framework that accounts for anisotropic screening and boundary-induced potentials. We demonstrate its efficacy by optimizing it for 4,4′-Bis­(N-carbazolyl)-1,1′-biphenyl (CBP), a prototypical host material in OLED devices. By fitting the model parameters against a representative set of full SCF calculations for CBP morphologies, we ensure that the screening function reproduces the specific polarization physics of this molecular species within a device-level environment. This approach not only mimics the energy landscapes of CBP slab geometries but also maintains high transferability, allowing for its application across diverse morphologies of the same material without the need for exhaustive recomputation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section details the computational methodologies, including the morphology generation protocol, KMC simulation framework, and the theoretical formulation of the proposed anisotropic screening model. In Section , we first analyze the physical origins of the energy landscape based on full SCF calculations to elucidate the role of induced polarization. We then validate the accuracy of the proposed model in reproducing these energy characteristics and demonstrate its robustness through transferability tests across independent morphologies and varying film thicknesses. Furthermore, the impact of the landscape model on macroscopic device performance is assessed by comparing simulated current–voltage characteristics with reference data. Finally, Section summarizes the main findings and discusses the broader applicability of our framework for efficient modeling of site energies.

Methods and Computational Details

Morphology Creation

The amorphous morphology of CBP was generated using a molecular dynamics (MD)-based physical vapor deposition (PVD) simulation protocol developed by Degitz et al., which considers the collective molecular movements and local packing characteristics of thin-film growth. While following the general framework of the original protocol, several modifications were introduced: all MD simulations were carried out with the GROMACS software package , utilizing the OPLS-AA force field to describe intramolecular and intermolecular interactions, and a graphene substrate composed of 5,800 carbon atoms was used. The simulation was initiated by placing this graphene substrate, with planar dimensions of 12.354 nm × 12.3 nm, at the bottom of the box, with two-dimensional periodic boundary conditions applied in the xy plane to represent a continuous film environment. A total of 2,400 CBP molecules were deposited sequentially onto the substrate, with each insertion followed by a simulated annealing cycle to facilitate efficient packing. During these cycles, the system temperature was briefly raised to overcome local energy barriers before being gradually cooled to the target substrate temperature of 300 K. Following the completion of the deposition process, the entire morphology underwent a final equilibration phase at 300 K to ensure the stability of the amorphous structure. The resulting bulk density of the CBP film was approximately 1.14 g/cm3, in a good agreement with the experimentally measured value of 1.18 g/cm3.

Kinetic Monte Carlo Simulations

To investigate the charge transport properties of the generated morphologies, we performed KMC simulations based on a stochastic hopping model. The simulations explicitly take into account two types of electronic effects: (1) intermolecular charge transfer between neighboring molecules and (2) charge injection and extraction at the organic-electrode interfaces.

The intermolecular hopping rates (k if ) between site (molecule) i and site f were described using Marcus theory, which represents the polaronic nature of charge carriers in organic semiconductors: kif=2πℏ|Jif|214πλkBTexp{−(ΔEif+λ)24λkBT} 1 where J if is the electronic coupling (transfer integral) between two sites, λ is the reorganization energy, k B is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature. The driving force ΔE if is defined as the sum of the site energy difference and the energy change induced by the external field and electrostatic interactions: ΔEif=(Esite,f−Esite,i)+ΔEfield,if 2 where E site,j denotes the site energy of molecule j. The term ΔE field,if is the potential energy change due to the external electric field and specific electrostatic contributions. Specifically, ΔE field,if is computed using a hybrid scheme that combines a mean-field 1D Poisson solver for the long-range electrostatic potential with an explicit short-range correction within a cutoff radius of 3 nm to recover carrier-carrier correlations. The detailed derivation and functional form are provided in Section S1 of Supporting Information.

For the charge injection and extraction processes at the organic-electrode interfaces, we first identified the “interfacial molecules” capable of exchanging carriers with the electrodes. This was performed using Voronoi tessellation, where molecules whose Voronoi cells share a face with the simulation box boundaries (z-direction) were defined as the injection/extraction sites. The rates for these interfacial events were modeled using the Miller-Abrahams expression: kinj/ext,i={ν0exp(−2γd−ΔEikBT)ifΔEi>0ν0exp(−2γd)ifΔEi≤0 3 where ν0 is the attempt-to-hop frequency, γ is the inverse localization length, and d is the distance to the electrode. This distinction in rate equations allows for a more refined treatment of the interface dynamics, where tunneling and thermal activation at the electrodes follow a different physical regime than the self-trapped hopping between organic molecules. To model an Ohmic contact regime, we defined the intrinsic injection barrier relative to the tail of the density of states at the interface. The effective energy barrier ΔE i for an interfacial molecule i is given by: ΔEi=(Esite,i−Eminsurf)+ΔEfield,i 4 Here, E min surf is the minimum site energy among all the interfacial molecules. This formulation sets the intrinsic barrier to zero for the most energetically favorable injection pathway. The field-dependent term ΔE field,i is consistent with the definition provided above for intermolecular hopping.

The temporal evolution of the system is governed by a rejection-free stochastic algorithm. In each simulation step, all possible electronic eventsincluding intermolecular charge transfers, carrier injections, and extractionsare identified based on the current state of the system. To determine which event occurs, a random number u 1 is drawn from a uniform distribution in the interval (0, 1]. The m-th event is selected if it satisfies the following condition: ∑p=1m−1kp<u1∑p=1Neventkp≤∑p=1mkp 5 Once an event is selected, the positions of the charge carriers and the local electrostatic potential are updated accordingly. The time increment for the step, Δt, is determined using a second independent random number u 2 ∈ (0, 1]: Δt=−ln(u2)∑p=1Neventkp 6

The electronic parameters used in the Marcus rate expression were obtained as described in Section . The internal reorganization energy λ was calculated to be 0.1308 eV for CBP. For the Miller-Abrahams injection and extraction rates (eq ), the attempt-to-hop frequency ν0 and inverse localization length γ were set to 0.01 fs–1 and 0.2 nm–1, respectively. All KMC simulations were initialized with an empty system without any preplaced charge carriers. Charge injection from the electrodes was included as a possible event at every simulation step for each unoccupied interfacial molecule. Each simulation was run for a total observation time of 10 μs, during which the number of charge carriers present in the system was monitored. Once this quantity reached a steady-state plateau, the carriers injected subsequently were used to compute the mean squared displacement (MSD) and the current density. To ensure statistical reliability, 100 independent KMC runs were performed for each morphology, and the reported MSD and JV characteristics represent averages over these runs.

Electronic Parameters for Marcus Rates

All quantum chemical calculations in this study were performed using the TeraChem software package. For both monomer and dimer configurations, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were executed using the B3LYP hybrid functional − with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction and the 6-311++G­(d,p) basis set. −

Electronic Coupling J if

The intermolecular electronic coupling, J if = ⟨ϕ i |H|ϕ f ⟩, was determined using the Dimer Projection (DIPRO) method. This projective approach calculates the transfer integral by forming the expectation value of the dimer Fock operator with the frontier orbitals of two neighboring monomers. Specifically, the monomer molecular orbitals are projected onto the manifold of the dimer orbitals within a common basis set. The resulting Hamiltonian and overlap matrices are then transformed into a standard eigenvalue problem in terms of an orthonormalized basis to identify the transfer integrals. This procedure bypasses the nonorthonormality of monomer orbitals, providing a more reliable description of charge transport parameters in disordered systems compared to simple energy-splitting approaches.

Reorganization Energy λ

The internal reorganization energy, λ, was calculated using a four-point procedure, outlined in Bredas et al. This framework determines λ by evaluating the total energies of the neutral and charged states at both their respective optimized geometries and the geometries of the alternative state: λ=(UnC−UnN)+(UcN−UcC) 7 where U sG denotes the energy of state s (neutral n or charged c) at geometry G (neutral N or charged C). In our KMC simulations, a consistent average value of λ was adopted for each material, as internal reorganization energy typically shows less sensitivity to local morphological variations than site energy.

Site Energy Calculations

Self-Consistent Field Calculation of Polarization

The site energy of a specific molecule i is defined as the change in the total electrostatic energy of the system upon placing a charge carrier on that site. This is calculated as the difference between the total energy of the system when molecule i is in a charged state U i tot) and the energy of the reference neutral system (U 0 tot): Esite,i=Uitot−U0tot 8 The total electrostatic energy U tot typically consists of the interaction energy between permanent multipoles (U perm) and the induction energy arising from induced multipoles (U ind). Consequently, the site energy can be decomposed into the contribution from the change in permanent electrostatic interactions and the contribution from the induced polarization: Esite,i=(Uperm,i−Uperm,0)+(Uind,i−Uind,0) 9 To describe the electrostatic potential of the molecules, we employed DMA implemented in the GDMA software. Following the rigorous framework established by Stone, the molecular charge distribution obtained from DFT calculations was partitioned into a set of atomic multipoles. These distributed momentsincluding charges, dipoles, and quadrupolesprovide a significantly more precise representation of the anisotropic molecular electrostatic field compared to simple point-charge models.

The permanent electrostatic energy, U perm, represents the interaction between the permanent multipoles of the molecules. Using the Thole-damped interaction tensor Ttuab , which incorporates the direct and image-mediated interactions (up to the 10th order image based on the reflection rules), U perm is expressed as: Uperm=12∑a,b∑t,uQtaTtuabQub 10 The induction energy, U ind, represents the stabilization resulting from the induced multipoles, ΔQ t a , created by the total electric field at each site. Consistent with the SCF treatment of induction, the induced moments are determined by the linear response to the total local electric field F t a : Fta=∑b∑uTtuab(Qub+ΔQub) 11 as ΔQta=−∑a′∑t′αtt′aa′Ft′a′ 12 where α tt′ aa′ is the atomic polarizability, adopted from Van Duijen et al. The resulting induction energy is evaluated as: Uind=12∑a,b∑t,uΔQtaTtuabQub 13 This formulation ensures a consistent and physically precise treatment of many-body electrostatic effects within the device architecture.

To construct the spatial energy profiles (as presented in Figures –), the simulation box was discretized along the surface normal (z-axis) into contiguous bins (slices) of thickness 0.4 nm. Molecules were assigned to specific bins based on the z-coordinate of their center of mass. Within each spatial bin, we computed the statistical moments of the site energy distribution: the first moment yielding the spatially dependent mean site energy, E(z), and the square root of the second central moment yielding the local energetic disorder, σ­(z). This slicing resolution of 0.4 nm corresponds approximately to the intermolecular spacing of CBP and was chosen to be sufficiently fine to resolve the steep gradients at the interfaces while ensuring adequate statistical sampling within each slice.

Anisotropic Screened Dielectric Function

We developed a modified screening model that involves the spatial anisotropy inherent in OLED thin films, based on CBP molecules as a representative example. We first consider the isotropic screening function previously proposed for 3D periodic bulk systems. , This model is based on the distance-dependent dielectric response, εiso(r), which captures the effect of polarization saturation near a charge carrier: εiso(r)=εr−(εr−1)(1+sr+12s2r2)exp(−sr) 14 where εr is the bulk dielectric constant and r is the distance from the charged site. While this form precisely reproduces site energy difference distributions in bulk morphologies, its direct application to a 2D periodic slab geometry is limited due to effect of metallic electrodes which significantly destabilizes the site energies of molecules.

To satisfy the site energy profile of the slab geometry, our approach explicitly addresses the interplay between permanent electrostatics and induced polarization. In organic semiconductor thin films, it is physically expected that the collective many-body polarization provides a dominant stabilizing effect in the bulk, which diminishes near the interfaces due to the reduction of the polarizable medium. This suggests that the total site energy should be modeled not merely by geometric reflection of permanent charges, but by a combination of screened interactions and a position-dependent background potential that includes the induction gradient.

To capture these spatial characteristics, we partitioned the total site energy into two distinct components: an electrostatic term and a background contribution. Esite(ri)=ECoul(ri)+Eofs(zi) 15 Here, E Coul represents the screened electrostatic interactions between a charged site and its surrounding environment within the slab geometry. To calculate this, we constructed an anisotropic screened dielectric function, εaniso(r), by introducing a modification to the isotropic reference εiso(r). Recognizing that the screening efficiency is significantly enhanced due to the polarization of the nearby metallic electrodes, we adopted a multiplicative scaling that depends on the proximity to the boundaries: εaniso(r)=εiso(r)×[1+β(rRL+rRR)] 16 where R L and R R represent the distances from the interaction center to the left and right first images, respectively, and r is the interaction distance. In this formulation, β acts as an amplification factor for the electrode screening effect. This multiplicative form ensures that the effective dielectric constant increases as the interaction pair approaches the electrodes (small R L, R R) or as the interaction distance r increases, thereby strongly suppressing the long-range Coulomb interactions in the thin-film limit.

To reduce the computational cost of iterative parameter optimization, the electrostatic interactions in the proposed model were evaluated using a coarse-grained (CG) representation of the CBP molecule, in which the full atomic charge distribution is reduced to 6 effective interaction beads based on its symmetric chemical substructure. The effective charge of each bead was determined by summing the DMA partial charges of its constituent atoms, with the interaction center placed at the center of mass of the grouped atoms. The details of this mapping are described in Section S2 of the Supporting Information.

The term E offset(z i ), however, is not a simple scalar correction but a composite term that addresses the physics missing from the point-charge approximation. It is constructed as the superposition of a deterministic mean energy and a stochastic intrinsic disorder contribution: Eofs(zi)=E−ofs(zi)+δEofs(zi;σint) 17 where E̅ ofs(z i ) describes the systematic shift in the average energy level due to the polarization volume loss near the interfaces, and δE ofs(z;σint) represents the random fluctuation arising from local structural variations. The magnitude of this fluctuation is governed by the position-dependent width profile, σint(z), such that δE ofs(z;σint) is drawn from a distribution with a standard deviation of σint(z i ) at a given depth z i . As defined in eq , the deterministic component of the background offset, E̅ ofs(z i ), reflects the degree of polarization stabilization. Ideally, the local site energy varies with the distance from the electrodes due to the reduced number of polarizable neighbors at the interfaces. This variation creates an energy landscape where the site energy is higher near the electrodes compared to the bulk. We approximated this background energy profile E̅ ofs(z i ) using a smooth, symmetric decay function: E−ofs(zi)=E−ofsbulk+(E−ofssurf−E−ofsbulk)×[exp(−ziλofs)+exp(−D−ziλofs)] 18 where E̅ ofs bulk and E̅ ofs surf represent the asymptotic site energies in the bulk and at the interface, respectively, and λofs characterizes the depth of the interfacial effect.

While the screened Coulomb interactions hold the long-range electrostatic fluctuations, they do not fully consider the short-range variations arising from intramolecular distortions and local packing heterogeneities. The stochastic component, δE ofs(z i ;σint) in eq , factors in the residual energetic roughness. We modeled its spatial variation using the intrinsic disorder profile, σint(z), which defines the local disorder width. Notably, this residual disorder incorporates spatial correlations rather than being modeled as uncorrelated white noise, reflecting the coherent nature of local structural features, such as variations in packing density and molecular orientation. Furthermore, the magnitude of this correlated disorder is not spatially uniform.

To quantitatively model this variation, we adopted an empirical function analogous to the background offset potential E̅ ofs(z i ). The intrinsic disorder profile, σint(z i ), is parametrized by its asymptotic values in the bulk (σbulk) and at the surface (σsurf). To maintain the simplicity of model and reflect the common structural origin of both the mean energy shift and the disorder reductionnamely, the depth of the interfacial packing variationwe constrained the decay length of the disorder profile to share the same parameter λofs used for the background offset: σint(z)=σbulk+(σsurf−σbulk)×[exp(−zλofs)+exp(−D−zλofs)] 19

To generate the specific realization of the stochastic background term δE ofs(z i ;σint) for each molecular site i consistent with this profile, we generated a spatially correlated noise sequence by convolving a standard Gaussian white noise with a Gaussian kernel. This approach was chosen because the Gaussian kernel convolution generates a spatially correlated noise field in a computationally straightforward manner, with the correlation length directly controlled by the kernel standard deviation. By setting this correlation length equal to λofs, we impose the physical constraint that the extent of microscopic structural coherence arising from local packing variations near the electrode interface decays over the same characteristic length scale as the interfacial polarization effects. Subsequently, this normalized fluctuation sequence was multiplicatively scaled by the position-dependent disorder amplitude, σint(z i ). This scaling step ensures that while the spatial coherence is governed by λofs, the local magnitude of the energetic roughness correctly follows the target bell-shaped profile defined in eq . Finally, to ensure that the intrinsic disorder remains statistically independent of the electrostatic landscape, the generated noise vector was orthogonalized to the precalculated screened Coulomb energy vector using a weighted Gram-Schmidt process. This procedure prevents accidental correlations that could distort the target disorder, guaranteeing that the variances of the electrostatic and intrinsic contributions are strictly additive (σtotal 2 = σCoul 2 + σint 2) across the entire film thickness.

To calibrate the proposed anisotropic model against the full SCF landscape, we performed a global optimization of the model parameters. The optimization involved a total of seven variables: the screening inverse length s, the amplification factor β, the asymptotic limits for the background potential (E̅ ofs bulk, E̅ ofs surf) and the intrinsic disorder (σbulk, σsurf), and their common decay length λofs. The optimal values were determined using the L-BFGS-B algorithm, implemented in SciPy. This quasi-Newton method is particularly well-suited for this nonlinear least-squares problem, as it efficiently handles bound constraints to ensure that physical parameters remain within valid ranges. Prior to the optimization, the reference full SCF profiles were symmetrized with respect to the center of the film (z = D/2). This step implies that the model is trained on the idealized physical symmetry of the device architecture, smoothing out asymmetric fluctuations arising from the finite sampling of the specific morphology. Consequently, the objective function was defined as the weighted sum of squared differences between the model-generated and the symmetrized reference profiles, simultaneously minimizing the errors in both the mean value and the disorder.

For comparative validation, we calculated the site energies using a simple image charge framework, hereafter referred to as the SIC Model. Consistent with the definition of site energy as the energy change associated with carrier occupation, the site energy for a molecule i was calculated as the difference between the total electrostatic energies of the charged (hole) and neutral states. Specifically, the intramolecular contribution was determined by calculating the interaction between the molecule i’s charge distribution (coarse-grained interaction centers) and its own induced image charge series for both states and taking their difference. The intermolecular electrostatic contribution was computed based on the variations in partial charges (Δq) of the coarse-grained interaction centers resulting from charge carrier occupation, covering interactions with all other molecules as well as their respective image charges. The infinite series of image charges was truncated at the 100th reflection order. Finally, all Coulombic interactions were screened using the isotropic dielectric function, εiso(r), where the screening inverse length, s, was set identical to the optimized value obtained from our proposed anisotropic model to ensure a consistent comparison.

It should be noted that the SIC model employed here already incorporates a distance-dependent isotropic dielectric function εiso(r) to phenomenologically account for polarization saturation near a charge carrier, as well as a truncated infinite series of image charges up to the 100th reflection order. However, this class of models intrinsically cannot capture the anisotropic polarization environment induced by the metallic electrodes, as it does not account for the position-dependent reduction of the polarizable medium near the interfaces. Furthermore, the raw site energies predicted by the SIC model exhibited a significantly narrower disorder width compared to the full SCF results. The SIC site energies were therefore linearly rescaled to match the global energetic disorder of the reference SCF data, in order to isolate the effect of the energy profile curvature from confounding differences in disorder magnitude. Without the rescaling, the narrow density of states in SIC model would yield unphysically high current densities in KMC simulations.

Results and Discussion

Energy Landscape of Dielectric Slab within Parallel Electrodes

Before evaluating the performance of the proposed model, we first analyze the fundamental physical origins of the energy landscape in the CBP thin film using the full SCF calculations. Figure decomposes the total SCF site energy profile into its two constituent parts: the permanent electrostatic interaction (U perm) and the induction energy (U ind).

The results in Figure a clearly illustrate why conventional models fail. The permanent electrostatic component (blue line) exhibits a concave shape, where energy increases from the electrode interface toward the bulk and levels off at the middle of the slab. This is the expected behavior for simple image-charge interactions of static multipoles. However, the induction energy (red line) shows a significantly stronger stabilization effect that increases in magnitude toward the center of the film. This trend arises because molecules in the bulk are surrounded by a complete shell of polarizable neighbors, whereas molecules near the interface lack polarizable medium on the electrode side. Since the magnitude of the induction stabilization outweighs the permanent electrostatic variation, the total site energy (black line) follows the convex distribution of the induction term. This confirms that the potential well structure is a direct consequence of many-body polarization effects.

Similarly, Figure b shows the decomposition of the energetic disorder. The induction component again dominates the spatial characteristics, showing a significant reduction in disorder near the interfaces. This analysis confirms that any effective screening model must explicitly account for this depth-dependent polarization resulting in both the mean energy shape and the surface-reduced disorder.

To investigate the physical origin of the surface-reduced energetic disorder (σsurf < σbulk), we analyzed the structural transition of CBP molecules near the interface. Specifically, we calculated the orientational order parameter (OOP), defined as ⟨P 2(cosθ)⟩ = ⟨(3cos2θ – 1)/2⟩, where θ is the angle between the vector connecting the two nitrogen atoms in a CBP molecule and the electrode’s normal vector. To capture the distance-dependent structural changes, the OOP was evaluated as a statistical average within 0.4 nm thick slices along the z-axis.

As shown in Figure , the OOP reaches approximately −0.5 in the slice directly contacting the electrode surface, indicating that molecules are oriented nearly parallel to the substrate. As the distance from the electrode increases, the OOP rapidly rises and stabilizes around −0.08 beyond approximately 2 nm, consistent with the surface-induced orientational ordering previously reported for vapor-deposited CBP films. This strong surface-induced structural ordering suppresses the fluctuations in the local electrostatic environment, resulting in a more uniform energetic landscape and a corresponding reduction in energetic disorder compared to the isotropic bulk region.

Reconstruction of the Energy Landscape

The primary objective of our model was to accurately reconstruct the site energy landscape of the amorphous CBP film sandwiched between electrodes, using a computationally efficient framework. Figure presents a direct comparison between the site energy profiles obtained from the reference full SCF calculations and those generated by our optimized model.

Figure a clearly shows the formation of a distinct energy valley in the full SCF profile, stabilizing toward the film center. This valley-like feature underscores induced polarization as the primary stabilizing factor. This behavior, which contrasts with the mound shape typical of SIC models, confirms that induced polarization from the surrounding organic medium is the dominant stabilizing factor. Our model (red dashed line), incorporating the background offset potential E̅ ofs(z), reproduces this trend with remarkable accuracy. The successful reconstruction of the potential well depth and the interfacial gradients demonstrates that the position-dependent offset function closely mimics the effect of polarization volume loss and molecule alignment effect in induction energy at the boundaries.

Simultaneously, Figure b illustrates the site energy disorder. The SCF results reveal a bell-shaped profile, where the disorder is largest in the bulk (∼0.25 eV) and significantly reduced near the interfaces (∼0.10 eV). This reduction is physically attributed to the planarization and increased structural ordering of CBP molecules induced by the flat electrode surfaces. Our model, utilizing the z-dependent intrinsic disorder σint(z), faithfully reproduces this spatial variation. The agreement across both the mean and disorder profiles indicates that the decomposition of site energy into screened Coulomb interactions and intrinsic structural disorder is a valid physical approximation.

It is crucial to clarify the fundamental nature of this agreement. Our model is designed to reproduce the statistical properties of the site energy distribution rather than to predict the exact site energy value of every individual molecule in a one-to-one correspondence. While the full SCF calculation provides a deterministic energy value for each specific molecular configuration based on its unique local environment, our model generates a thermodynamically and statistically equivalent landscape. This statistical reconstruction is sufficient and appropriate for device-scale simulations such as KMC, where macroscopic transport observables are governed by the global features of the energy landscape and the statistical distribution of states, rather than the accidental energy values of specific individual sites.

Analysis of Optimized Parameters

The physical validity of the model is further corroborated by the values of the optimized parameters, summarized in Table . To ensure the physical robustness of the electrostatic description, the bulk dielectric constant εr was not treated as a fitting parameter; instead, it was fixed at 2.17, a value derived from the Clausius-Mossotti relation using the molecular polarizability and film density. Consequently, the optimization involved seven free variables: the screening inverse length s, the amplification factor β, the common decay length λofs, and the asymptotic limits for the background potential (E̅ ofs bulk, E̅ ofs surf) and intrinsic disorder (σbulk, σsurf).

Of particular interest is the amplification factor, β, which converged to a value of 7.79. It is instructive to interpret this magnitude in the context of the method of images. The amplification factor β in eq is introduced to account for the collective electronic polarization of the molecular environment, which is often underestimated in standard continuum-based image charge models. While the classical approach determines the strength of image charges solely based on the dielectric mismatch at the interface, our framework incorporates β as an effective scaling factor to capture the enhanced many-body response of the amorphous organic medium. From a physical perspective, β represents the deviation of the microscopic screening response from the idealized macroscopic description. This scaling approach allows us to map the complex, nonlocal polarization effects observed in full SCF calculations onto a computationally efficient functional form in our model. By adjusting the weight of the image potential terms, the model effectively bridges the gap between the discrete molecular nature of the film and the continuous boundary conditions of the electrodes without the need for prohibitive computational costs. We attribute the large optimized value of β ≈ 7.8 primarily to the strong self-polarization effects induced by the proximal electrodes, where the intense electric field from the image charges induces a significant redistribution of the molecular electron density. This self-interaction creates a highly nonlinear, short-range screening effect that suppresses the potential much more strongly than what is predicted by simple geometric reflections of charges.

The decay length λofs was optimized to approximately 0.87 nm. Since this parameter describes the spatial extent of both the mean energy shift and the disorder reduction, its convergence to a physically meaningful length scale is significant. A value of ∼0.87 nm corresponds to the thickness of approximately one to two molecular layers of CBP. This suggests that both the reduction in polarization stabilization and the increase in structural ordering share a common physical origin: the packing perturbation caused by the rigid electrode interface. The ability of a single geometric parameter to describe two distinct energetic phenomena underscores the consistency of our effective electrostatic approach.

Validation of Transferability

To verify the robustness and transferability of the proposed approach, we assessed its performance on independent morphological realizations that were not used during the parameter optimization phase. While the model parameters listed in Table were calibrated exclusively using the first morphology (Morph 1), they were subsequently applied to four additional, independent CBP morphologies (Morph 2–5) without any readjustment. It serves to distinguish whether the model captures the intrinsic material properties of CBP or merely overfits to the specific geometric idiosyncrasies of a single snapshot.

Figure compares the averaged site energy profiles of the full SCF reference and the model predictions across the five morphologies. Due to the slight variations in film thickness (∼1 Å) inherent to the PVD simulation process, the spatial coordinates were normalized to the average thickness for consistent averaging. It is noteworthy that the full SCF energy profiles fluctuate across different microscopic realizations. These sample-to-sample variations are inherent to disordered organic semiconductors, arising from the stochastic nature of local molecular packing and orientation.

Despite these microscopic fluctuations, our model (red line) consistently follows the averaged trend, yielding predictions that lie well within the statistical variance of the reference (blue line) data. This agreement demonstrates that the proposed anisotropic screening function and the z-dependent disorder profile robustly mimic the fundamental energy landscape driven by the electrostatic boundary conditions and material-specific polarization response. By successfully predicting the energetics of unseen morphologies, the model proves its high transferability, confirming its suitability for generating reliable inputs for device-scale charge transport simulations.

To further challenge the robustness of the proposed framework, we examined the size transferability of the model by applying it to systems of varying thicknesses. Using the same PVD protocol, we generated three thinner CBP films consisting of 300, 600, and 900 molecules, corresponding to film thicknesses (D) of approximately 1.8, 3.2, and 4.6 nm, respectively. The model parameters utilized here are identical to those optimized for the thickest film (N = 2400, D ≈ 11.6 nm).

Figure displays the mean site energy and disorder profiles plotted against the normalized position (z/D). For the films with intermediate thickness (N = 900), the model (dashed lines) exhibits good agreement with the reference SCF calculations (solid lines), closely predicting the deepening of the potential well and the disorder saturation as the film thickness increases. This confirms that our anisotropic screening framework and the depth-dependent background potential are robust across standard device-relevant scales (D > 4 nm).

The primary organic semiconductor layers in practical OLED devices are well above this threshold, and thus this limitation does not impose a practical constraint on device-level simulations. For organic layers approaching the ultrathin regime, a separate optimization of the Coulomb interaction parameters against reference SCF calculations for the specific film thickness would be required, and represents an important direction for future work.

Charge Transport Characteristics

To elucidate the microscopic origin of the charge transport behavior, we analyzed the MSD of the charge carriers along the direction of the applied electric field (z-axis). Figure compares the MSD profiles obtained from KMC simulations under an external field of 0.2 MV/cm. The MSD characteristics provide direct insight into the transport regimes and the impact of the overall landscape on carrier mobility.

MSD of holes obtained from KMC simulations. The proposed model (red) closely reproduces the MSD profile of the reference full SCF calculations (blue). In contrast, the SIC model (gray) shows a significantly suppressed MSD, indicating that carrier motion is hindered by the artificial interfacial barrier.

As shown in Figure , the MSD profile predicted by our model (red line) exhibits agreement with the reference Full SCF results (blue line) in terms of magnitude. Both profiles display a sublinear power-law dependence (MSD ∝ t α, α < 1) across the simulated time window. This deviation from ideal diffusive behavior (α = 1) is a hallmark of dispersive transport in disordered organic semiconductors, where charge carriers undergo continuous energetic relaxation into the tail states of the density of states. The ability of our model to reproduce this dispersive feature confirms that it correctly captures the depth and spatial distribution of the energetic disorder that governs the bulk transport dynamics.

Conversely, the SIC model (gray line) yields an MSD that is drastically lowerby nearly 2 to 4 orders of magnitudethan the reference. Interestingly, despite the low magnitude, the slope of the SIC model is steeper than that of the reference model in the intermediate time regime. This behavior highlights a fundamental difference in the limiting transport mechanisms between the models. In the reference and proposed models, which operate under a bulk-limited regime, carriers are efficiently injected and rapidly explore the bulk; however, their motion is subsequently hindered by trapping events as they relax into deep-lying states, leading to the observed dispersive slowing down. In stark contrast, the SIC model is governed by injection-limited transport due to the high interfacial barrier. Here, carrier motion is restricted not by bulk relaxation but by the rare probability of thermally activating over the interfacial barrier. Once a carrier overcomes this barrier, it drifts with a constant velocity, resulting in a linear MSD slope but with a severely suppressed effective velocity. This analysis demonstrates that the convex downhill landscape incorporated in our anisotropic model is essential for facilitating efficient carrier injection and enabling the correct bulk-limited transport regime, whereas the SIC model assumption leads to an injection-limited bottleneck.

Finally, to validate the impact of the energy landscape on the macroscopic device performance, we performed KMC charge transport simulations using the site energies generated by the three different approaches. Figure displays the simulated JV characteristics for the single-carrier CBP device. Remarkably, the JV curve obtained from our model (red line) is in a good agreement with the full SCF data (blue line) over the entire voltage range. This indicates that our effective electrostatic approach successfully describes the critical features of the energy landscape governing charge injection and bulk transport.

Simulated JV characteristics. The proposed model (red) reproduces the full SCF reference (blue), while the SIC model (gray) underestimates the current. This is attributed to the steep uphill barrier in the SIC model, where the low interfacial disorder further restricts carriers to the potential well, hindering penetration into the bulk.

Conversely, the SIC model (gray line) significantly underestimates the current density. This discrepancy arises from the concave profile which creates a steep uphill gradient at the charge injection interface. As shown in Figure , the SIC model predicts that the site energy is lowest at the electrode boundary and rises sharply toward the bulk. This reduced current is further exacerbated by the overall low disorder. The narrow density of states confines charge carriers to the energetic minimum near the electrode. This lack of broadening reduces the probability of carriers finding accessible higher-energy sites, pinning them at the interface and preventing efficient penetration into the bulk, even in the presence of external field. By contrast, the SCF landscape (and our model) exhibits a convex potential well with a downhill gradient, which naturally facilitates the drift of carriers from the interface into the bulk region, thereby supporting a much higher current density. The failure of the SIC model underscores that the reliable description of the interfacial energy gradientdriven by anisotropic polarizationis a prerequisite for the quantitative prediction of OLED device characteristics.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a computationally efficient and physically motivated model to accurately describe the site energy landscape of amorphous organic semiconductor thin films sandwiched between metallic electrodes. Recognizing that conventional SIC models fail to encompass the complex polarization physics in slab geometries, we introduced a refined approach that explicitly accounts for the anisotropic dielectric response and the spatial nonuniformity of the energetic environment.

Our analysis of full SCF calculations revealed that the site energy profile in a thin-film geometry is governed by a delicate balance between permanent electrostatic interactions and induced polarization. We identified that the induced polarization effect creates a convex potential well that stabilizes charge carriers in the bulka feature diametrically opposed to the concave profile predicted by simple image-charge approximations. To integrate the physical behavior into the model, we formulated an anisotropic screened dielectric function augmented by a position-dependent background potential. The optimization of the model parameters for the host material CBP yielded a large amplification factor (β ≈ 7.8), highlighting the dominance of strong electrostatic confinement and molecular polarization effects over simple geometric reflections.

The validity and robustness of the proposed model were thoroughly validated. The proposed framework demonstrated its capability by faithfully replicating the microscopic energetic details of the full SCF reference, capturing both the bowl-shaped mean energy and the arching disorder distribution. Beyond fitting a single structure, the model exhibited robust transferability; parameters calibrated on one morphology accurately predicted the landscapes of independent realizations without readjustment. Ultimately, the validity of these energetic inputs was substantiated through kinetic Monte Carlo simulations, which confirmed excellent agreement with the reference current–voltage characteristics. In contrast, the SIC model significantly underestimated the current density, proving that describing the downhill energetic gradient and its steepness at the injection interface is critical for predictive device simulation.

In conclusion, our work bridges the gap between high-accuracy quantum chemical calculations and large-scale device modeling. By providing a physically motivated yet computationally inexpensive method to generate realistic site energy landscapes, this approach paves the way for the efficient optimization of next-generation OLED materials and multilayer device architectures. While the specific parametrization presented here is optimized for CBP case, we anticipate that the underlying protocolfitting the anisotropic screening function and background offset against a representative set of reference SCF calculationsprovides a transferable workflow for other amorphous organic semiconductors. The functional forms employed here may serve as a reasonable starting point, though material-specific fitting will be required to capture the distinct polarization physics of different molecular species. Preliminary calculations for Bathocuproine (2,9-Dimethyl-4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline, BCP), an electron-transport material with distinct molecular geometry, reveal that while the quantitative landscape differs from CBP, the characteristic electrode-induced polarization pattern near the interfaces is preserved across independent morphologies (see Supporting Information), suggesting that the physical picture underlying our framework may extend to other amorphous organic semiconductors.

We note that while the physical picture of electrode-induced polarization anisotropy and surface-reduced disorder is expected to be a general feature of amorphous organic semiconductor films, the specific functional forms employed here are phenomenological in nature and may require revision for different molecular systems. Material-specific parametrization against reference SCF calculations will therefore be necessary when applying this modeling protocol to other materials. The present framework is also limited to amorphous organic semiconductors where static energetic disorder governs charge transport; for crystalline systems where dynamic disorder and transient localization effects are dominant, a different theoretical treatment would be required.

Furthermore, we envision a hybrid modeling strategy for simulating realistic multilayer device architectures. Since the polarization-induced anisotropy is predominantly localized near the metallic contacts, it may be computationally advantageous to apply our anisotropic screening model exclusively to the layers adjacent to the electrodes, while utilizing standard isotropic screening functions for the intermediate bulk-like layers. Realizing this scheme will require a careful treatment of the boundary conditions at the heterointerfaces, specifically how to smoothly match the distinct screening behaviors of adjacent layers. Addressing this dielectric coupling at internal boundaries represents a critical and exciting direction for future development in full-device organic electronics simulation.
