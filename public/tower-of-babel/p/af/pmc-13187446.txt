Ecofriendly synthesis and characterization of copper chitosan nanoparticles (CuChNPs) and assessing combined effect of nanoparticles and bismerthiozol against bacterial leaf blight of rice
Authors: Rumana Akter, Lutfur Rahman, Omar Kayess, A. K. M. Sahfiqul Islam, Rakibul Hasan, Mohammad Abdul Latif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rice bacterial blight pathogen, Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo), poses a major threat to global rice production. While chitosan and copper-based nanoparticles have been explored for plant disease control, their potential to function as synergistic boosters for reducing conventional agrochemical dosage remains largely underexploited. Keeping this in mind and risks associated with traditional chemical treatments, this study focused on the eco-friendly synthesis of crystalline copper–chitosan nanoparticles (CuChNPs) designed to function as a sustainable synergistic booster enhancing antibacterial efficacy against Xoo. To evaluate the effectiveness of CuChNPs alone and in combination with Bismerthiozol (0.15%, 0.125%, and 0.1%) at CuChNPs: dH₂O ratios of 6:4 and 4:6 under net-house conditions, ten treatments were compared against controls (Bismerthiozol 0.2% and CuChNPs 10:0). All treatments were applied as foliar sprays before and after inoculation, with the 6:4 and 4:6 ratios selected due to their lower dosage requirement and superior in vitro performance. The successful synthesized of crystalline shaped nanoparticles were confirmed by UV–visible spectroscopy. SEM and TEM analyses revealed a distinct morphology with an average particle size of 110.23 nm and zeta potential of -23.1 mV indicating strong colloidal stability. In vitro assays showed strong antibacterial activity of CuChNPs against Xoo, while CuChNPs with bismerthiozol (0.15%) demonstrated notable disease reduction (63.78% at 14 days and 58.09% at 21 days) in net-house conditions. Field trials confirmed this superior performance, with the same treatment achieving 54% and 57.88% disease reduction at 14 and 21 days, respectively. Overall, these findings conclude that CuChNPs serves as an effective, stable and eco-friendly alternative for managing rice bacterial blight, while promoting sustainable rice production and global food security through reduced hazardous agro-chemical usage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44356-0.

Introduction

Bangladesh is an agrarian country, and its socio-economic development heavily dependent on the agricultural sector1. Agriculture is the key economic driver which plays a well-established role in shaping the nation’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP). In recent years, agriculture in Bangladesh has confronted numerous challenges, like climate change, rapid urbanization, the resurgence of pests, and the emergence of plant diseases2. To mitigate yield losses caused by insect pests and pathogens, extensive use of chemical pesticides has been employed3.

To date, thirty-two distinct rice diseases have been reported in Bangladesh9, with the most prevalent being blast, bacterial blight, sheath blight, and tungro4–7. Bacterial leaf blight (BLB), caused by Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo), is a widespread rice disease that can cause yield losses of 10–20% in moderately resistant varieties and up to 50% in susceptible varieties under favourable conditions8,9. Bangladesh’s warm, humid monsoon climate developing this persistent disease pressure10 and also triggered by heavy nitrogen use under existing intensive rice cultivation system. Thus, it creates socio-economic threat by reducing due to severe yield losses during epidemics11 as this pathogen proliferates quickly under suitable environmental conditions, underscoring the need for timely and effective management strategies12.

Currently, no bactericide has been officially recommended for managing BLB in Bangladesh due to its environmental hazardous role13. Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising alternative in agriculture by overcoming the limitations and environmental concerns of conventional bactericides, as metallic nanoparticles enhance the efficacy of natural compounds while reducing reliance on harmful synthetic pesticides14,15. It provides multiple benefits, such as increased surface area coverage, improved efficacy at reduced dosages, optimal size-to-volume ratios, and greater precision in targeted delivery16.

Copper is recognized as non-toxic to humans17 and possesses notable antimicrobial properties18. The synthesis of CuChNPs has gained significant interest due to their high surface energy and the role of phytochemicals in plant extracts as natural binding and stabilizing agents12,14. Moreover, these biomolecules can reduce metal salt precursors from their oxidized states to zero-valent forms, promoting nanoparticle nucleation and crystallization16, supporting the goal of sustainability and green chemistry.

The evergreen Azadirachta indica (Neem) is a member of the Meliaceae family, is widely distributed across America, Africa, and Asia19 and is considered one of the richest sources of pharmacologically active compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, catechins, gallic acid, limonoids, phenolics, carotenoids, steroids, and ketones20. Extracts from various parts of it have been used to synthesize sustainable silver, gold, and iron oxide nanoparticles with diverse biological functions, exhibiting enhanced antimicrobial and antioxidant activities21 is used for the phytofabrication of chitosan nanoparticles, as an alternative eco-friendly procedure.

Biopolymer’s alternatives have been extensively studied due to their high affinity for metal ion adsorption, attributable to the abundance of hydroxyl (− OH) and amine (− NH₂) functional groups. Among these, chitosan has acquired considerable attention22, as it as an eco-friendly, naturally derived, multifunctional biopolymer primarily sourced from the exoskeletons of crustaceans (e.g., shrimp and lobsters) as well as insects, fungi, and algae, and it exhibits antimicrobial properties, recyclability, and biocompatibility, immunomodulation, non-toxicity, biodegradability, anti-carcinogenic potential, and degradation into non-toxic low-molecular-weight compounds both in vivo and in vitro making it highly attractive for diverse agricultural applications23,2425. Copper has long been used in pesticide formulations for its antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-biofilm activities, though its tendency to oxidize into intermediate species like CuO or Cu₂O during synthesis and storage can limit nanoparticle stability26,27. Several studies have demonstrated that copper–chitosan nanoparticles effectively inhibit the mycelial growth of Alternaria solani and Curvularia lunata, as well as the spore germination of Fusarium oxysporum28, highlighted the promising application of copper–chitosan nanoparticles in the effective management of plant pathogenic bacteria29, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella bovismorbificans, Salmonella typhi, and Escherichia coli30.

Excessive and indiscriminate use of synthetic pesticides has led to the development of pesticide resistance and caused adverse effects on ecosystems and public health31. In this context, metallic copper nanoparticles have emerged as a viable alternative to overcome the resistance barrier32,33. Notably, CuChNPs have recently been reported to exhibit anticancer activity against bladder cancer34,35, which also helps to maintain sound human health. However, nanoparticles alone still face challenges due to pathogen adaptation and incomplete suppression under high disease pressure, highlighting the need for synergistic disease management strategies.

Bismerthiazol, a thiazole-based bactericide widely used against BLB, inhibits bacterial cell division and DNA synthesis but suffers from declining efficacy due to resistance development and repeated field application36. The combination of CuChNPs with bismerthiazol represents a largely unexplored yet highly promising strategy, where both agents may act through complementary and mutually reinforcing mechanisms. CuChNPs can disrupt bacterial cell membranes, increase membrane permeability, and induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, thereby weakening cellular defense systems37,38, which may facilitate bismerthiazol penetration into bacterial cells, while nanoparticle-induced oxidative stress synergistically enhances its bactericidal activity by overwhelming bacterial antioxidant defenses, thereby enabling effective control at lower doses and reducing the likelihood of resistance development. Such a synergistic nano–chemical approach has the potential to improve disease control efficiency while minimizing chemical input, environmental toxicity, and resistance evolution, thereby representing a novel and sustainable strategy for managing BLB in rice.

This study hypothesizes a novel synergistic interaction between copper–chitosan nanoparticles (CuChNPs) and bismerthiazol, aiming to develop a high-efficacy, low-dosage, and eco-friendly antibacterial formulation for the sustainable management of bacterial leaf blight evaluated under both in vitro and in vivo conditions.

Materials and methods

Preparation of leaf extracts

Leaves of Azadirachta indica (neem) were used for their bio-reductive properties in the synthesis of CuChNPs. For this, fresh neem leaves were collected from the Bangladesh Rice Research Institute campus in Gazipur, Bangladesh. To ensure purity, the leaves were thoroughly washed under running tap water to remove debris and extraneous organic matter, followed by three successive rinses with distilled water. The cleaned leaves were then air-dried at ambient room temperature. Approximately 30 g of finely chopped leaves were immersed in 450 ml of distilled water in a beaker, and the mixture was heated at 80 °C for 30 min to extract bioactive compounds. After cooling to room temperature, the extract was filtered twice using Whatman No. 1 filter paper to remove particulate matter and obtain a clear filtrate. The resulting neem leaf extract was stored at 4 °C until further use in nanoparticle synthesis.

Biosynthesis process

This study employed a modified synthesis method for CuChNPs based on the protocol described by Abdallah et al.39 and Zungu et al.40 with minor modifications as illustrated in Fig. 1. Briefly, a 3% (w/v) chitosan solution was prepared in 5% (v/v) acetic acid using 50 ml of purified water and stirred continuously at room temperature. Subsequently, 12 ml of 1 M ascorbic acid was added, raising the pH of the mixture to 3.5. Separately, a 0.1 M solution of CuSO₄·5 H₂O was prepared in 25 ml of distilled water, yielding a transparent blue solution. The formation of CuChNPs was indicated by a color change to brick brown when the copper solution was gradually added to the continuously stirred chitosan-ascorbic acid mixture, maintained at 60 °C for 60 min. The synthesis of nanoparticles was further facilitated by the addition of 13 ml neem leaf extract. To justify the CuChNPs ratios used in subsequent experiments, a preliminary in vitro screening was conducted using a broad range of CuChNPs: dH₂O ratios (10:0, 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, and 2:8). Ratios were selected based on their antibacterial efficacy against Xoo, colloidal stability, and reduced copper dosage. The ratios 6:4 and 4:6 consistently demonstrated superior antibacterial activity while minimizing copper input and were therefore chosen for detailed in vitro, net house, and field evaluations. The resulting nanoparticles were collected and utilized in both solution and powdered forms for subsequent physicochemical characterization and efficacy assessments.Fig. 1Schematic representation of the steps involved in the green synthesis of CuChNPs. Characterization

The synthesized CuChNPs were characterized using a range of analytical techniques to elucidate their structural, morphological, and physical properties. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of the nanoparticles was assessed via UV–visible spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-2600i, Japan), measuring light absorption across wavelengths from 500 to 600 nm over time to determine their optical characteristics and peak absorption values41.

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) on a Smart Lab diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) operated at 40 kV and 50 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 5° to 90° at a scanning speed of 30° per minute to identify the crystalline phases of the CuChNPs.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to investigate the functional groups involved in the formation of CuChNPs, using a PerkinElmer Spectrum II spectrometer equipped for FTIR imaging. Before FTIR analysis, the nanoparticle solution was dried in an oven for 24 h to obtain solid samples suitable for examination.

The FTIR analysis was specifically used to identify functional groups associated with chitosan, neem phytochemicals, and surface-bound copper species. The presence of a band around 550 cm⁻¹ was attributed to Cu–O vibrations arising from surface oxidation or coordination with oxygen-containing functional groups, which is common in biologically synthesized copper-based nanoparticles and does not necessarily contradict the presence of metallic copper cores as confirmed by XRD analysis.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed to determine the particle size distribution and heterogeneity of the colloidal suspension. Zeta potential, which reflects the surface charge or electrokinetic potential of the particles, was measured using the same instrument as a key indicator of colloidal stability. The stability and interfacial properties of the nanoparticle suspension were assessed via zeta potential analysis using a Litesizer 500 (Anton Paar, United Kingdom).

The morphology of the synthesized nanoparticles was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JCM7000 instrument. Samples were gold-coated prior to imaging, and an accelerating voltage of 15 kV was applied during analysis. Elemental composition was determined through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) integrated with the SEM system. EDX analysis was used to obtain the complete elemental composition of the CuChNPs, including Cu, C, O, and N, confirming the contribution of chitosan and neem-derived biomolecules in nanoparticle stabilization in addition to copper. Additionally, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; JEOL-7600 F) was utilized for detailed characterization at the Plant Pathology Division of the Bangladesh Rice Research Institute (BRRI), Gazipur, Bangladesh.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted at the Materials Science Division of the Bangladesh Atomic Energy Commission, Savar, using a Talos F200X G2 (Thermo Fisher, Czech Republic) to analyze the size, shape, and dispersion of the CuChNPs. Samples were dried, mounted on carbon-coated copper grids, and examined under TEM. Elemental composition was further confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with the TEM system.

Elemental composition and spatial distribution were further validated using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with TEM, providing complementary confirmation of copper and associated non-metallic elements within the nanoparticle matrix.

Antibacterial properties.

Antibacterial activity of CuChNPs in vitro and the assessment of bacterial growth

The antibacterial efficacy of CuChNPs at various ratios against Xoo was investigated in vitro to compare with control treatments. The Xoo bacterial culture was previously isolated and propagated on Pentose Sucrose Agar (PSA) medium. Prior to experimentation, the pathogenicity of Xoo was confirmed. Antibacterial activity of the CuChNP composite against Xoo was assessed using the agar-well diffusion assay. To minimize experimental bias, treatments were randomly assigned to wells, and inhibition zone measurements were conducted by an independent observer blinded to the treatment identity. The intended final concentration of CuChNPs was 15 ml per 10 ml (v/v), followed by serial dilutions. Working samples were prepared by diluting the CuChNP solution in sterile distilled water to obtain a range of concentrations (10:1, 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, 2:8) of CuChNPs to distilled water (dH₂O).

Control treatments included neem leaf extract, 3% chitosan, 0.1 M copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO₄·5 H₂O), and sterilized water. Ten treatments in total were tested and each replicated thrice. On PSA plates, 100 µl of a three-day-old Xoo culture with a concentration of ~ 1 × 10⁸ CFU ml− 1 was uniformly spread using a sterile glass rod. Subsequently, 15 µl of each treatment was introduced into wells on the agar surface using a micropipette. Plates were incubated at 28 °C ± 1 °C for 48 h. Zones of inhibition were measured at 48 and 72-hours after-inoculation to evaluate antibacterial activity.

Data were collected at seven-day intervals up to 28 days. The microbial growth inhibition rate was determined by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zones using a ruler placed above the Petri dish. To calculate the precise bacterial growth inhibition zone, the well diameter was subtracted from the measured diameter, and the following formula was applied = √(×)/4.

Effect of CuChNPs on bacterial leaf blight disease under net house conditions

A total of 27 pots were prepared and filled with naturally dried soil for the experiment. The BLB susceptible rice variety BRRI dhan51 was used as experimental material where the seedlings were grown in the seedbed at the Plant Pathology Division, Bangladesh Rice Research Institute (BRRI), Gazipur, Bangladesh. After thirty days, healthy seedlings were transplanted into the pots, with three seedlings were allocated per pot. Plants were inoculated at the maximum tiller stage using the leaf clipping method outlined by Kauffman42, and disease development was subsequently monitored. Treatment allocation to pots was randomized, and disease assessments were performed by evaluators blinded to treatment identity to reduce observer bias. To determine whether CuChNPs alone or in combination with Bismerthiozol at three different dosages, viz. 0.15%, 0.125%, and 0.1% at different ratios [CuChNPs: dH2O(6:4) and CuChNPs: dH2O(4:6)] were effective in lessening lesion length in BB disease or not under net house settings. The efficacy was evaluated in relation to a healthy control, a disease control, and a standard control with Bismerthiozol (0.2%) and CuChNPs (10:0). Total ten treatments were tested on BLB disease under net house conditions. The treatments were T1: healthy control, T2: control, T3: CuChNPs (10:0), T4: Bismerthiozol (0.2%), and T5: Bismerthiozol (0.15%)+CuChNPs: dH2O(6:4), T6: Bismerthiozol(0.125%)+CuChNPs: dH2O(6:4), T7: Bismerthiozol(0.1%)+CuChNPs: dH2O(6:4), and T8: Bismerthiozol(0.15%).+CuChNPs: dH2O(4:6), T9: Bismerthiozol(0.125%)+CuChNPs: dH2O(4:6), and T10: Bismerthiozol(0.1%)+CuChNPs: dH2O(4:6). Each treatment was applied via foliar spray at the specified concentrations three days before inoculation and repeated twice three days after inoculation. The CuChNPs: dH₂O ratios of 6:4 and 4:6 were selected based on their lower dosage and superior in vitro performance. Disease severity was assessed at 14 and 21-days after inoculation using the International Rice Research Institute43 scale, which ranges from 0 to 9 based on the percentage of diseased leaf area where score 1 indicates high resistance with 1–5% leaf area infection, 3 represents resistance with 6–12% infection, 5 denotes moderate resistance showing 13–25% infection, 7 corresponds to susceptibility with 26–50% infection, and a score of 9 indicates highly susceptible plants with 51–100% leaf area infection. Two separate in vivo experiments were conducted, employing the same methodology but utilizing two distinct treatment sets in each.

Effect of CuChNPs on bacterial leaf blight disease under field conditions

This study was conducted in the experimental field of the Plant Pathology Division of BRRI, Gazipur, Bangladesh. The susceptible rice variety BRRI dhan51 was used for the experiment. Each plot measured 3 m × 1 m, and the experimental design followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Treatments were randomized within each block, and disease severity scoring was performed independently by two trained evaluators blinded to treatment identity. Thirty-day-old seedlings were transplanted at a spacing of 20 cm × 20 cm. Inoculation was performed using the leaf clipping method as above-mentioned concentration. The treatments applied were identical to those used in the pot experiment, with foliar sprays administered twice per plot. CuChNPs were sprayed at varying concentrations three days before inoculation and again three days after inoculation. Disease severity was measured as mentioned above.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data set was examined three times for statistical analysis, and the obtained values were calculated as mean ± SD. Mean separation test was done by using the STAR software. The Excel software was used to calculate the standard deviation and standard error.

Confirmation of CuChNPs’ Biosynthesis and UV–vis absorbance

A colloidal suspension of uniformly colored copper nanoparticles with a brick-brown hue was produced by a combination reaction. Notably, the addition of ascorbic acid to the opaque chitosan-acetic acid solution (pH 3.5) did not induce any apparent color change. However, the subsequent introduction of light blue CuSO₄0.5 H₂O resulted in an immediate color shift, first to greenish-yellow and then to a final brick brown. This color change (colorless to reddish-brown, as shown in Fig. 1) served as the initial indicator of CuChNPs formation within the reaction mixture. FTIR spectra of CuChNPs

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was utilized to investigate the chemical interactions and functional groups involved in the synthesis of CuChNPs. The analysis provided insights into the role of neem leaf extract as a reducing and stabilizing agent, as well as the nature of the interactions between copper nanoparticles and the chitosan matrix. The FTIR spectra, presented in Fig. 6, revealed several distinct absorption peaks at 3394 cm⁻¹, 1635 cm⁻¹, 1466 cm⁻¹, 1383 cm⁻¹, 1149 cm⁻¹, 1076 cm⁻¹, 901 cm⁻¹, and 550 cm⁻¹.Fig. 6FTIR spectra of CuChNPs, chitosan and Azadirachta indica leaf extract.

FTIR spectra of CuChNPs, chitosan and Azadirachta indica leaf extract.

The broad peak at 3394 cm⁻¹ was attributed to O–H stretching vibrations, indicative of hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl groups presents in alcohols and phenolic compounds from the plant extract. A peak at 1635 cm⁻¹ corresponded to the stretching vibrations of the amide I band (CO–NH₂), confirming the presence of chitosan. Peaks at 1466 cm⁻¹ and 1383 cm⁻¹ were assigned to C = O stretching and C–N bending, respectively, while the absorption at 1149 cm⁻¹ was related to C–H bending vibrations. The peak observed at 1076 cm⁻¹ was indicative of C–O stretching vibrations, typical of primary alcohols.

Importantly, a pronounced peak at 550 cm⁻¹ was attributed to Cu–O stretching vibrations, confirming the formation of copper oxide bonds within the nanoparticles. The same peak also suggested strong interactions between copper nanoparticles and chitosan, implying effective encapsulation of the metal particles by the polymer matrix. Additionally, the peak at 901 cm⁻¹ was associated with C = C bending vibrations, typically found in alkenes.

Four dilution ratios of CuChNPs to distilled water (dH₂O), namely 10:1, 6:4, 4:6, and 2:8, was evaluated using the plate assay technique. Effect of CuChNPs on bacterial leaf blight disease under net house conditions

Several treatments showed notable effectiveness in reducing disease severity when compared to both the standard disease control and healthy control groups. As expected, the healthy control exhibited no disease symptoms, while the disease control group showed the highest lesion development, confirming the virulence of the Xoo pathogen under experimental conditions. After 14 days of treatment, all tested formulations showed a significant reduction in disease severity, with percent reductions ranging from 30.68% to 63.78%. This trend continued at 21 days post-treatment, where reductions ranged from 28.12% to 58.09%, indicating sustained efficacy over time. Among the treatments, T5: Bismerthiozol(0.15%)+CuChNPs: dH₂O (6:4) demonstrated the highest disease suppression, achieving a 63.78% reduction in lesion length at 14 days, followed by T6: Bismerthiozol(0.125%)+CuChNPs: dH₂O (6:4) with a 53.71% reduction. Both treatments maintained their effectiveness through day 21, with lesion length reductions of 58.09% (T5) and 52.46% (T6), respectively. This sustained suppression suggests their potential applicability as long-term disease management strategies. In addition, T3: CuChNPs (10:0) also significantly reduced BLB lesion development, with 47.58% and 35.59% reductions at 14 and 21 days, respectively, when compared to the disease control group. Effect of CuChNPs on bacterial leaf blight disease under field conditions

This study evaluated the field efficacy of a combined treatment using CuChNPs and Bismerthiozol against BLB disease. Various treatment combinations of CuChNPs and Bismerthiozol were tested, including CuChNPs alone (10:0), and compared against both disease and healthy controls. In the disease control group, progressive lesion development was observed, with lesion lengths reaching 12.83 nm and 19.56 nm at 14 and 21-days after inoculation, respectively (Fig. 10). The healthy control group exhibited no lesion formation at either time point. The treatment T3 (CuChNPs 10:0) demonstrated moderate efficacy, reducing lesion lengths by 47.49% and 39.83% at 14 and 21 days, respectively. Notably, treatment T5 (Bismerthiozol (0.15%)+CuChNPs dH2O (6:4)) ratio recorded the highest effectiveness, achieving lesion length reductions of 62.54% and 57.88% at 14 and 21 days, respectively. Treatment T6, consisting of Bismerthiozol (0.125%) with CuChNPs: dH₂O (6:4), also exhibited substantial efficacy, reducing lesion lengths by 52.22% and 48.02% at the same intervals. Overall, a dose-dependent response was evident, with higher concentrations of Bismerthiozol generally leading to greater reductions in disease severity. Among all treatments, T5 emerged as the most effective in mitigating BLB symptoms. However, the results from T3 (sole CuChNPs) are promising, suggesting the potential of nanoparticle-based treatments as standalone or complementary approaches with possibly fewer side effects. Discussion

Copper chitosan nanoparticles were synthesized through an eco-friendly green synthesis approach. In this method, copper salts acted as the precursor, while chitosan functioned as a stabilizing and capping agent. Chitosan formed a complex with Cu²⁺ ions, where the interaction between CuSO₄·5 H₂O and chitosan yielded a CS–CuSO₄ complex44. Subsequently, neem leaf extract served as the reducing agent, facilitating the nucleation of Cu²⁺ ions within the chitosan-copper complex to produce elemental copper (Cu⁰) nanoparticles45. To prevent the unintended formation of copper oxides such as Cu₂O, CuO, and Cu(OH)₂, ascorbic acid was introduced as an antioxidant50.

The formation of CuChNPs was initially indicated by a distinct brick-brown coloration observed upon mixing the copper sulfate solution with the plant extract and chitosan matrix. The color transformation initially from light blue to greenish-yellow, and subsequently to brick-brown (Fig. 1) corroborated the successful synthesis of CuChNPs. This observation was further confirmed by UV–visible spectrophotometry. The characteristic brick-brown color aligns with previous studies that synthesized CuChNPs using various plant-based extracts46, indicating the reproducibility of the method. The visual change in color is attributed to surface plasmon resonance (SPR), a phenomenon arising from the collective oscillation of free conduction electrons in Cu nanoparticles upon interaction with incident electromagnetic radiation. This resonance governs the specific light absorption properties of the nanoparticles, thereby determining the color of the colloidal solution47.

The chemical reduction of metal salts remains one of the most convenient and promising synthetic approaches for obtaining metallic nanoparticles, offering the advantage of relatively low-cost experimental setups. However, unlike noble metals such as silver (Ag), gold (Au), or platinum (Pt), the synthesis of copper nanoparticles (Cu-NPs) via simple reduction in aqueous solution is considerably more challenging due to copper’s high susceptibility to oxidation into CuO and Cu₂O48.

The SPR of the synthesized copper–chitosan nanoparticles exhibited a distinctive absorption peak at 595 nm in the UV-Vis spectrum, consistent with earlier reports [44, 49, 50,]. As reported in the literature, copper–chitosan nanoparticles typically demonstrate absorption bands within the 500–600 nm range41, further validating the successful formation of CuChNPs in this study (Fig. 2).

The particle size and homogeneity of the synthesized CuChNPs were analyzed using the dynamic light scattering (DLS), which revealed a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 110.23 nm (Fig. 3a). This value is larger than the crystallite size estimated from XRD analysis (~ 77.5 nm), which reflects the fundamental difference between the two techniques. XRD measures the size of coherent crystalline domains, whereas DLS captures the hydrodynamic diameter of nanoparticles, including the chitosan coating, adsorbed biomolecules, and the solvation layer. Therefore, the observed discrepancy is expected and indicates effective polymeric encapsulation rather than aggregation. Similar size deviations between DLS and XRD/TEM measurements have been widely reported for polymer-stabilized nanomaterials50. Furthermore, the zeta potential of the CuChNPs was measured to be −23.1 mV at pH 12.0 with a 10:0 ratio (Fig. 3b), Although this value lies close to the commonly cited ± 25 mV threshold for electrostatic stability, the presence of chitosan provides additional steric stabilization, which compensates for borderline electrostatic repulsion. Such combined electrosteric stabilization is advantageous for short- to medium-term storage and field application, particularly when nanoparticles are freshly prepared or formulated immediately before use. Nevertheless, for large-scale storage and commercialization, further optimization such as pH adjustment, cross-linking of chitosan, or incorporation of secondary stabilizers may be required to enhance shelf-life stability. This finding corresponds well with the findings of Manikandan & Sathiyabama29. Although the value does not exceed the ± 25 mV threshold commonly associated with colloidal stability46, the moderately high negative zeta potential still suggests a fair degree of electrostatic repulsion among particles, contributing to the formulation’s overall stability.

The biosynthesized CuChNPs in this study exhibited heterogeneous and polymorphic shapes, as revealed by SEM and FESEM analyses. These observations are consistent with previous findings reported by Chowdhury et al.33 and Sivasuriyan et al.51. Transmission Electron Microscopy further demonstrated that the CuChNPs were well-dispersed, predominantly spherical, with an average particle size of approximately 110.23 nm. EDX spectroscopy confirmed the presence of elemental copper within the nanoparticles, thereby verifying their purity. Importantly, the detection of additional elements such as carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen reflect the contribution of chitosan and neem-derived phytochemicals in surface functionalization, which plays a crucial role in stability, bioactivity, and controlled copper release. This finding aligns with the EDX results reported by Mahmoud et al.52 for similarly synthesized CuChNPs. It is important to note that DLS measures the hydrodynamic radius, which typically results in slightly larger particle size values compared to those obtained from TEM micrographs, as explained by Huang et al.53.

Size-controlled synthesis of copper nanoparticles can be achieved through the use of carrier materials, such as polymer matrices. In this study, chitosan was employed as a capping agent due to its superior copper-binding properties54,55. The surface chemistry and size distribution of nanoparticles are significantly influenced by capping with biocompatible surfactants56,57. Chitosan functions as both a reducing and stabilizing agent during Cu-NP formation, effectively inhibiting agglomeration and minimizing oxidation reactions, thereby restricting particle growth56. Consequently, the use of appropriate capping agents such as chitosan significantly reduces nanoparticle agglomeration over extended periods41. Overall, such size-controlled synthesis is particularly relevant for agricultural applications, where nanoparticle dispersion, adherence to leaf surfaces, and sustained antimicrobial activity are critical for efficacy.

The FTIR analysis revealed the potential involvement of alcohols, aromatic compounds, carboxylic acids, alkenes, amines, and phenols as reducing and capping agents during the synthesis of copper chitosan nanoparticles. A distinct peak observed at 3394 cm⁻¹ could be attributed to hydrogen-bonded O–H groups present in alcohols and phenols or to N–H stretching in amides, consistent with previous findings49. The most prominent peak at 500 cm⁻¹ corresponded to Cu–O stretching vibrations. This signal likely originates from surface-bound copper–oxygen interactions or partial surface oxidation, which is common in biologically synthesized copper nanoparticles and does not contradict the presence of a metallic copper core, as confirmed by XRD analysis. Rather, such surface oxidation may enhance antimicrobial activity through controlled copper ion release. Additional absorption bands at 1466 cm⁻¹, 1383 cm⁻¹, and 1149 cm⁻¹, associated respectively with C = O stretching, C–N bending, and C–H bending, corroborated results reported in chitosan-mediated copper oxide nanoparticle synthesis using Eupatorium adenophorum leaf extract58. The presence of a peak at 550 cm⁻¹ indicated interactions between Cu nanoparticles and chitosan, signifying nanoparticle encapsulation by the polymeric matrix, consistent with observations by Usman et al.41 and Dang et al.59. Furthermore, characteristic peaks at 1635 cm⁻¹ corresponded to CO–NH₂ groups60, while absorption at 901 cm⁻¹ was assigned to C = C bending vibrations in alkenes41. These spectral features align well with previous studies, supporting the current findings52,53,59.

The XRD pattern of CuChNPs (Fig. 7) exhibited diffraction peaks at 46.034°, 50.47°, and 77.23°, corresponding to the face-centered cubic (fcc) crystal planes (111), (200), and (311) of copper, respectively53. This confirms the crystalline nature of the synthesized nanoparticles, consistent with prior reports52,53,59.

Copper-based nanomaterials have demonstrated potential as cost-effective alternatives for managing bacterial infections in rice61. Metallic nanoparticles exert their antibacterial effects by increasing bacterial cell permeability, ultimately leading to cell death62. These nanoparticles adhere to the microbial cell wall, inducing high-energy transduction that damages cell membranes and results in microbial mortality63. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the antibacterial activity of CuChNPs remain incompletely understood, their reduced size-to-volume ratio enhances their ability to penetrate microbial membranes, thereby inhibiting growth41,64. Due to their unique physicochemical properties, nanoparticles exhibit greater biological activity at lower concentrations compared to their bulk counterparts43,65. Yoon et al.66 described the primary mode of action of copper nanoparticles as the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which disrupt bacterial metabolic processes and inflict multifaceted damage to cellular structures. The combined action of copper and chitosan is particularly advantageous, as chitosan enhances nanoparticle adhesion to bacterial cell walls, while copper induces oxidative and metabolic stress, resulting in synergistic antimicrobial activity. Similarly, Mahendra et al.67 reported that the adherence of nanoparticles to bacterial surfaces alters membrane properties, leading to cell death. Enhanced efficacy of CuNPs against Gram-negative bacteria has also been documented68. These findings align with observations that chitosan significantly inhibits bacterial growth under in vitro conditions, attributed to its increased solubility at pH ≤ 6.569. In this study, copper chitosan nanoparticles were synthesized using naturally derived chitosan, environmentally friendly ascorbic acid (Vitamin C), and neem leaf extract to reduce the reliance on conventional bactericides in rice production.

The concentrations of CuChNPs (CuChNPs: dH₂O) exhibited promising inhibition zones ranging from 32.9 nm to 17.04 nm after 5 days of plating. These findings are supported by Chowdhury et al.33, who reported inhibition zones of 11 nm and 8 nm at copper nanomaterial concentrations of 500 ppm and 250 ppm, respectively, in vitro. Chitosan alone has also been documented to inhibit the growth of several phytopathogenic bacteria, including Xanthomonas oryzae, Pseudomonas syringae, Agrobacterium tumefaciens, and Erwinia carotovora, as well as various fungal species70. Furthermore, at 1000 ppm, CuChNPs effectively suppressed the growth of three fungal pathogens, namely Magnaporthe oryzae, Rhizoctonia solani, and Phytophthora capsici, with Rhizoctonia solani exhibiting the greatest sensitivity49. Multiple studies have demonstrated that copper nanoparticles possess broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy against pathogens such as Salmonella, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus mutans, and Staphylococcus species29. Mehta et al.46 reported that CuChNPs achieved 70% suppression of Fusarium sp., 65% suppression of Aspergillus sp., and complete (100%) inhibition of Bacillus cereus. Additionally, Mehta and colleagues46 demonstrated that CuChNPs, even at a low concentration of 7 ppm, completely eradicated Alternaria sp. and Pythium sp. within 72 h in vitro.

In the net house experiment, all treatments demonstrated disease reductions ranging from 30.68% to 63.78% after 14 days and 28.12% to 58.09% after 21 days compared to the disease control, indicating their effectiveness. The lesion length of bacterial blight was notably shorter in treatments T5: Bismerthiozol(0.15%) + CuChNPs: dH₂O (6:4) and T6: Bis(0.125%) + CuChNPs: dH₂O (6:4) compared to the disease control. These results were corroborated by field experiments, where the T5 treatment again exhibited superior disease management, reducing lesion length by 54% and 57.88% at 14 and 21 days, respectively. Treatment T3: CuChNPs (10:0) also significantly decreased lesion length, showing reductions of 47.58% and 35.59% at 14 and 21 days in the pot experiment, and 47.49% and 39.83% at 14 and 21 days under field conditions. Supporting these findings, Choudhary et al.33 reported that CuChNPs elicited substantial growth promotion and defense responses against Curvularia leaf spot of maize, evidenced by increased activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and peroxidase, as well as defensive enzymes like polyphenol oxidase and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase. Their study observed disease reductions ranging from 0.04% to 0.16% and 0.12% to 0.16% under pot and field conditions, respectively. Although all treatments showed disease suppression, further research is needed to optimize dosage and combinations for sustained long-term management, particularly given the limited physiological insights available.

From an economic and practical perspective, the synthesis of CuChNPs using inexpensive and readily available materials such as chitosan, neem leaf extract, and copper sulfate supports their feasibility for large-scale agricultural application. Their effectiveness at reduced dosages indicates potential cost savings for farmers while simultaneously decreasing chemical inputs and environmental load in rice production systems.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations should be acknowledged. The evaluation of CuChNPs was largely restricted to laboratory assays, net-house experiments, and a single-location field trial, which may not fully represent diverse agro-ecological conditions. Moreover, cytotoxicity assays and long-term assessments of nanoparticle persistence, transformation, and accumulation in soil–plant systems were not conducted. Although no visible phytotoxic effects were observed at the tested concentrations, the impacts of repeated applications and multi-season exposure remain uncertain. Copper nanoparticles may undergo dissolution or complexation in soil, potentially affecting microbial communities and nutrient cycling. Therefore, future studies should focus on chronic phytotoxicity and cytotoxicity evaluations, copper accumulation in plant tissues, soil persistence, and multi-location, multi-season field assessments to ensure environmental safety and sustainable deployment.

Conclusions

Bacterial leaf blight increasingly threatens global rice production, highlighting the urgent need for eco-friendly alternatives to costly and environmentally harmful synthetic pesticides. This study used neem leaf extract as a reducing agent and chitosan as a stabilizer to synthesize copper–chitosan nanoparticles, which were crystalline with an average size of approximately 110.23 nm and a narrow size distribution. The zeta potential measurements indicated good colloidal stability in suspension. In vitro assays showed strong antibacterial activity of CuChNPs, with inhibition zones ranging from 20.25 to 32.90 nm, while in vivo net-house trials revealed disease reduction of 30.68–63.78% at 14 days and 28.12–58.09% at 21 days. The combination of Bismerthiozol (0.15%) with CuChNPs: dH₂O (6:4) consistently achieved the highest efficacy, recording up to 63.78% reduction in net-house conditions and 57.88% under natural field conditions. Despite these promising outcomes, several challenges remain for the broader agricultural adoption of CuChNPs, including formulation stability during long-term storage, scalability of green synthesis, cost optimization, and potential nanoparticle transformation or accumulation in soil–plant systems. Furthermore, comprehensive evaluations of phytotoxicity, cytotoxicity, and long-term environmental fate across diverse agro-ecological conditions are essential to ensure biosafety and sustainable field application. Future research should therefore focus on multi-location and multi-season field validation, formulation optimization for enhanced shelf life, and integrated disease management strategies combining CuChNPs with reduced chemical inputs. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for translating CuChNP-based approaches into sustainable, economically viable, and environmentally safe solutions for bacterial leaf blight management, ultimately contributing to resilient rice production and global food security.
