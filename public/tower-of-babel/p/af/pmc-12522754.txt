Nonoperative treatment of neonatal appendicitis
Authors: Yakun Liu, Shuai Chen, Yang Li, Lulu Chen, Shungen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal appendicitis (NA) is a rare but potentially fatal condition with diagnostic challenges and limited management evidence. This study evaluates the efficacy of nonoperative treatment compared to surgical intervention.MethodsA retrospective study included infants ≤ 28 days with clinically and sonographically confirmed NA treated at four institutions between January 2010 and May 2022. The nonoperative group received antibiotics and supportive care, compared to a control group of 34 surgically treated neonates. The primary outcome was nonoperative treatment failure (death or delayed appendectomy), while secondary outcomes included severe infectious complications, hospital stay, costs, and readmission.ResultsTwenty-eight neonates (median age 11 days; 35.7% female) received nonoperative therapy. The failure rate was 25%, with 3 deaths (10.7%) and 5 delayed appendectomies (17.9%). Severe infectious complications occurred in 28.6% of cases, higher than the surgical group, but not statistically significant (P = 0.10). Hospital costs were significantly lower for the nonoperative group (P < 0.05). No recurrence was observed over 6.2 years of follow-up.ConclusionsNonoperative treatment of NA was associated with a high failure rate, mortality, and complication rate, despite lower costs. Given limited power for several comparisons, our findings suggest early surgical intervention may be preferable.Keywords: Neonate, Newborn, Appendicitis, Conservative treatment, Antibiotic treatment

Background

Neonatal appendicitis (NA) is a rare yet potentially fatal condition, with a high male-to-female ratio of approximately 3:1[1, 2]. Its nonspecific presentation, subtle clinical signs, and limited diagnostic tools frequently lead to delayed diagnosis, often resulting in perforation [3, 4]. Across early childhood, perforation is strongly age-dependent and highest in the youngest: 100% in children < 1–2 years, 70–83% at 2–5 years, and 47% at age 5, highlighting the narrow diagnostic window in neonates and infants [5]. Historically, NA was associated with mortality rates as high as 78%, though advances in medical care have reduced this to 5%–23%[2–4].

Appendectomy has long been the standard treatment for appendicitis across all age groups [6]. However, the diagnostic challenges in neonates, particularly for uncomplicated NA, often preclude timely surgical intervention. Consequently, NA is frequently diagnosed late, occasionally misidentified as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) or identified intraoperatively [3, 7, 8]. In recent years, nonoperative management has emerged as an alternative for uncomplicated appendicitis in children and adults [9, 10]. Although surgery remains the standard recommendation in small children with appendicitis for its high perforation rate, some reports suggest that nonoperative management with antibiotics and supportive care can achieve favorable outcomes, possibly due to advancements in diagnostics, antibiotics, and critical care [11]. However, these findings may be influenced by selection bias.

Despite these observations, evidence for nonoperative management of NA remains sparse and primarily anecdotal. For neonates diagnosed with appendicitis after several days of antibiotic therapy or those who are clinically stable, the choice between surgical and continued nonoperative management remains uncertain. Thus, this study evaluates the efficacy and outcomes of nonoperative treatment in neonates with appendicitis, aiming to offer insights into managing this rare and serious condition.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted across four institutions: a national center for children’s health, two regional children’s hospitals, and a tertiary medical center. Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) at each site approved the study, waiving informed consent due to its retrospective design. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.

Patients

Eligible patients were identified through systematic searches of institutional databases. Eligible participants were infants aged ≤ 90 days diagnosed with acute appendicitis between January 2010 and May 2022. Final inclusion was restricted to neonates with symptom onset ≤ 28 days. No restriction was placed on gestational age; both preterm (gestational age < 37 weeks) and full-term neonates were included. Exclusion criteria included: (1) symptom onset > 28 days of age; (2) diagnosis without ultrasonographic confirmation or based solely on clinical assessment; (3) surgical treatment post-appendicitis diagnosis; (4) concurrent Meckel’s diverticulitis, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), or intestinal perforation; and (5) comorbid immunodeficiency or severe congenital anomalies. Although all included patients were ≤ 28 days of age at the time of symptom onset, nonoperative treatment could continue beyond the neonatal period (i.e., beyond 28 days of age) in some cases if clinically appropriate, until the patient’s condition stabilized or surgical intervention was deemed necessary.

The nonoperative treatment group comprised neonates who initiated or continued antibiotic therapy after an appendicitis diagnosis. A control group of 34 surgically treated neonates with surgically confirmed appendicitis, previously reported, was included for comparison [3]. Of the 37 surgically treated neonates from the prior report, three initially received nonoperative treatment but required surgery due to treatment failure and were thus included in the nonoperative treatment group.

Definitions and outcomes

Appendicitis was diagnosed based on both (1) clinical and/or laboratory evidence of intra-abdominal or systemic infection; and (2) ultrasound findings of an appendiceal diameter >3.5 mm with direct signs (e.g., appendiceal wall discontinuity, thickening, or intraluminal fluid) or indirect signs (e.g., localized right lower quadrant peritoneal thickening, periappendiceal abscess, or fluid accumulation) [12, 13]; and (3) absence of NEC-specific findings (e.g., pneumatosis intestinalis or portal vein gas). Patient age was determined by the onset of symptoms or signs relevant to NA, as documented in medical records and verified by two independent investigators.

Ultrasound examinations were performed by pediatric radiologists/sonographers at each site as part of routine care. At the time of clinical scanning, radiologists were not blinded to bedside information. For the present analysis, we applied centralized operational definitions across centers. Ultrasound variables were re-abstracted using a standardized template. Where available, original images were reviewed to confirm measurements; otherwise, standardized data were extracted from radiology reports. We did not mandate a contemporaneous, site-wide scanning protocol during the study years; standardization was achieved at the analysis stage via uniform definitions and two-investigator verification.

The primary outcome was the failure rate of nonoperative treatment, defined as all-cause death or delayed appendectomy within 3–30 days after diagnosis.

Secondary outcomes included severe infectious complications, bowel obstruction, length of hospital stay, hospital costs, and 30-day readmission rates. Severe infectious complications included sepsis, septic shock, meningitis, bacteremia, or organ abscesses following appendicitis.

We re-applied the current study’s eligibility (symptom onset ≤ 28 days) and outcome definitions to the surgical dataset and re-abstracted all variables de novo using the same standardized template. Surgical/pathologic confirmation served as the diagnostic reference; therefore, preoperative ultrasonography was not mandatory and was unavailable for some cases.

Data on clinical, laboratory, and ultrasound findings were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized template by one investigator and verified by another. Clinical signs and symptoms were assessed based on documented physical examinations (e.g., abdominal tenderness or distension via palpation), vital signs (e.g., fever), and patient history (e.g., vomiting, diarrhea), as recorded by attending neonatologists or pediatricians during admission and follow-up. Follow-up data were obtained from outpatient records or telephone contact for patients in both group lacking outpatient follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Before analysis, the distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If they did not meet normality in at least one comparison group, they were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Inter-group comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U test or chi-square test, as appropriate. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp. Armonk, New York). A two-sided α of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of 38 neonates who initially received nonoperative treatment, three were excluded for missing ultrasound imaging and seven for non-visualization of the appendix on ultrasound, yielding an exclusion rate of 26.3%. Thus, 28 neonates were included in the nonoperative treatment group. The median age was 11 days (IQR, 5.3–14.0 days), and 35.7% were female. All patients were < 1 month old at symptom onset. The clinical picture was characterized by non-specific symptoms, including fever (n = 14, 50%), abdominal distension (n = 12, 42.9%), vomiting (n = 6, 21.4%), and diarrhea (n = 1, 3.6%). The control group comprised 34 surgically treated neonates. Their characteristics, symptoms, and laboratory findings were similar to the nonoperative treatment group, except for lower white blood cell counts in the surgical group.

Reasons for choosing nonoperative treatment included localized right lower quadrant inflammation on ultrasound (16 cases), marked improvement in symptoms and signs after prior antibiotic therapy (5 cases), mild symptoms and signs (3 cases), and other reasons (4 cases).

Of the included patients, intravenous antibiotics were started early (median, one day after symptom onset). Nineteen patients were admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). The median time from symptom onset to appendicitis diagnosis was five days in the nonoperative group versus three days in the surgical group (P = 0.69). Twenty-two patients received combination antibiotic therapy. The most commonly used antibiotics were cephalosporins (22 cases), metronidazole (15 cases), and meropenem (11 cases). One patient required mechanical ventilation.

Primary outcome occurred in 7/28 (25.0%) neonates, including 3 deaths (10.7%) and 5 delayed appendectomies (17.9%). Three patients died (including one after surgery), a lower rate than in the surgical group, though not statistically significant. No post-mortem examinations were performed. Five patients underwent delayed appendectomy within 30 days: three during the initial hospitalization and two after readmission within 30 days. Of these, four underwent successful appendectomy, while one had severe adhesions preventing appendectomy. Histopathological reports for the four delayed appendectomies confirmed appendicitis in all cases (suppurative appendicitis in 1 case and perforated appendicitis in 3 cases).

Severe infectious complications occurred in eight patients (sepsis n = 4, bacteremia n = 3, organ abscess n = 1) in the nonoperative group, a higher rate than in the surgical group, but the difference was not statistically significant. Hospital costs were significantly lower in the nonoperative treatment group (P < 0.05).

Among patients with successful nonoperative treatment, no recurrence of appendicitis was observed during a median follow-up of 6.2 years (IQR, 3.6–10.3 years).

Discussion

This retrospective study demonstrated a 25% failure rate for nonoperative treatment of neonatal appendicitis within 30 days, with a 10.7% mortality rate and a 17.9% risk of delayed appendectomy. Severe infectious complications occurred in 28.6% of nonoperatively managed neonates, though hospital costs were significantly lower compared to the surgical group (P < 0.05). These findings address a critical gap in the evidence for management of neonatal appendicitis.

Historically, neonatal appendicitis diagnosis has relied on surgical confirmation [8, 14]. This is largely due to the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria for neonatal appendicitis. The variable position and small size of the neonatal appendix, combined with interference from intestinal gas, make ultrasound detection challenging [1, 12]. Two prior studies described ultrasound characteristics of neonatal appendicitis, including direct signs (e.g., appendiceal diameter >3.5 mm, wall discontinuity, intraluminal fluid, or wall thickening) and indirect signs (e.g., localized right lower quadrant abscess, periappendiceal fluid, or peritoneal thickening) [3, 12]. However, these features lack external validation for diagnostic accuracy. In this study, we applied stringent ultrasound-based diagnostic criteria to ensure accuracy, which excluded patients diagnosed solely by clinical findings or computed tomography (CT), as well as those with non-visualized appendices on ultrasound. Notably, non-visualization of the appendix on ultrasound is common. A prior case series reported a visible rate of 32.1% in neonates with clinical or surgical diagnosed appendicitis, which may indicated that additional cases may have been overlooked beyond those excluded in patient selection [15].

To further address diagnostic overlap with NEC, our stringent ultrasound criteria (appendiceal diameter >3.5 mm with specific signs) and clinical exclusion reduce the likelihood of including early-stage NEC (Bell I/II), which often mimics NA but shows smaller appendiceal diameters on ultrasound, as demonstrated in a previous study (median appendiceal diameter 3.3 mm in NEC vs. 4.8 mm in NA; AUC 0.84 for differentiation) [12]. This helps lower the risk of misclassification in our study. However, it should be noted that this study cannot exclude the possibility that the nonoperative group included patients with Bell stage I/II NEC.

Ultrasound performance in neonatal appendicitis is imperfect and operator-dependent, and serial examinations are often required; in this retrospective study, repeat ultrasonography was not standardized. Acquisition and interpretation varied across centers and radiologists were not blinded to bedside information at the time of clinical scanning, which may introduce inter-observer variability [13]. To enhance consistency at the analysis stage and improve diagnostic specificity, we applied centralized operational definitions (appendiceal outer diameter ≥ 3.5 mm plus at least one direct/indirect sign) and excluded cases with a non-visualized appendix; these choices likely increased specificity at the expense of sensitivity and may have excluded some true NA. We did not conduct a blinded central reread in this study; prior neonatal data suggest high between-reader agreement for appendiceal outer diameter [13]. Prospective studies should standardize scanning protocols and timing of serial ultrasound, incorporate blinded central adjudication, and quantify sensitivity–specificity trade-offs to minimize inter-observer effects.

A previous report of 40 neonates treated nonoperatively for appendicitis noted only one death, attributed to parental abandonment for financial reasons [11]. However, the diagnosis mainly relied on ultrasound report without reporting strict diagnostic criteria. Another systematic review of 242 infants ≤ 3 months with appendicitis, including 200 neonates (83%), found that 63 neonates initially received nonoperative treatment, with most eventually requiring surgery [16]. The reported mortality rate was approximately 12%, with a declining trend with time. Notably, the systematic review was based on case report studies. However, the declining mortality in neonatal appendicitis likely reflects advances in life support and diagnostic techniques, which may partly explain the lower mortality in our study compared to historical reports. Other contributing factors may include intensive antibiotic regimens and enhanced supportive care.

Appendicitis in older children and adults is commonly caused by fecaliths or lymphoid obstruction [17]. The short, conical shape of the neonatal appendix makes obstruction unlikely [1]. Neonatal appendicitis may represent an isolated form of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) or be linked to immune compromise, such as in preterm infants [18]. However, the proportion of preterm infants in this study and prior case reports is far lower than in NEC cases (70%–90%), suggesting prematurity is not a primary driver [19, 20] Perinatal asphyxia, cardiac anomalies, or other low-flow states causing vascular insufficiency may also contribute to neonatal appendicitis [8, 18]. Additionally, intestinal obstruction (e.g., meconium ileus or Hirschsprung’s disease) could increase pressure at the appendiceal base, leading to inflammation or perforation [21]. However, no such cases were observed in our cohort. These findings suggest that the etiology of neonatal appendicitis is complex and may involve unique pathological mechanisms or neonatal-specific risk factors, warranting further investigation.

During a median follow-up of 6.2 years, no recurrence of appendicitis was observed among neonates successfully managed nonoperatively. In contrast, recurrence rates for nonoperatively treated uncomplicated appendicitis in older children range from 20% to 40%, often associated with fecaliths or higher pain levels [22, 23] These factors are rare or difficult to assess in neonates. Possibly owing to distinct etiologies or the small sample size, the recurrence rate was much lower than that reported in older children. It is also possible that potential triggers for recurrence grow with age.

In this study, the decision of nonoperative treatment was primarily based on localized right lower quadrant inflammation or abscess, significant symptom improvement after prior antibiotics, or stable vital signs. The failure rate of nonoperative treatment reached 24%. A study of 50 children ≤ 3 years with appendiceal phlegmon or abscess reported an 8.7% failure rate for nonoperative treatment in those without fecaliths [24]. Another study of 89 children with appendiceal abscess treated nonoperatively found a 10% appendectomy rate and a 13% readmission rate over 5.1 years [25]. Similar to our findings, most appendectomies and readmissions occurred within one month of discharge. However, the nonoperative treatment failure rate in our neonatal cohort was higher. Combined with the comparison to our surgical control group, these results suggest that neonates with appendicitis have a higher failure rate with nonoperative management even among those with improved or stable condition. Hence, early surgical intervention may be preferable for patients with neonatal appendicitis.

No Amyand’s hernia was identified in our nonoperative group. The discrepancy with Raveenthiran et al. likely reflects publication/selection bias, as case reports/series preferentially feature rare, educational entities such as Amyand’s hernia, inflating its apparent proportion [26]. Moreover, Amyand’s hernia typically presents with an incarcerated inguinal mass and is triaged to urgent surgery rather than conservative treatment, so these cases are inherently underrepresented in nonoperative series and our study cannot estimate its frequency.

The study is underpowered for several between-group comparisons; thus, non-significant findings should not be taken as evidence of no difference. The wide 95% CIs reflect imprecision typical of rare diseases and leave room for clinically relevant effects (i.e., risk of Type II error).

This study has several limitations. First, diagnoses were not surgically confirmed, the current gold standard. Although the ultrasound and clinical criteria were made to ensure accuracy, stringent diagnosis criteria may have excluded some true cases. On the other hand, because NEC may present without classical imaging signs, complete exclusion of NEC in conservatively managed neonates is not possible. Therefore, residual misclassification cannot be ruled out. Second, the small sample size and retrospective design limit generalizability. Third, cases without sonographic visualization of the appendix or with missing ultrasound images were excluded. While this reduces misclassification risk, it may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Fourth, because surgical/pathologic confirmation was the reference standard in the surgical group, whereas ultrasonographic criteria were required in the nonoperative group, differential ascertainment may introduce bias. Additionally, we did not have reliable denominator data across participating centers; therefore, we did not attempt to calculate center-specific or period incidence, which would also be vulnerable to diagnostic misclassification in this age group. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate ultrasound diagnostic criteria and compare standardized nonoperative protocols against surgery.

Conclusion

Nonoperative treatment of neonatal appendicitis was associated with a high failure rate, mortality, and severe complication rate, despite lower hospital costs. These findings underscore the challenges of conservative care, even among clinically improving or stable infants. Given the retrospective design, small sample size, and the lack of surgical confirmation in the nonoperative group, non-significant between-group differences may reflect limited power, and results should be interpreted with caution. Early surgical intervention may be preferable to minimize complications and mortality. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate diagnostic criteria and define evidence-based treatment guidelines for this rare but serious condition.
