Climate and regional plant richness drive diet specialization in butterfly caterpillars
Authors: Collin P Gross, Akito Y Kawahara, Barnabas H Daru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies of coevolution, ecosystem processes, and latitudinal diversity gradients are improved by understanding variation in resource specialization. Insect herbivory is one of the most ubiquitous terrestrial ecological associations, and is important for understanding the evolution of both plants and insects, yet the processes underlying global variation in diet breadth remain poorly understood. Here, we use global datasets of butterfly and plant distributions to investigate the patterns and drivers of butterfly larval diet breadth. Diet breadth showed a negative relationship with plant family richness, but this was offset by a direct effect of temperature acting in the opposite direction. Islands generally harbor species with broader diets, but islands with higher endemism had narrower diets than average. Our study provides a global baseline for understanding how plant and herbivore interactions structure ecological communities in the face of global environmental changes.Subject terms: Biogeography, Macroecology, Community ecology

Introduction

Variation in the magnitude, direction, and breadth of interspecific interactions influences multiple ecological and evolutionary processes. Differences in dietary specialization have received attention for their effects on organisms, ecological communities, and ecosystems at multiple spatial, temporal, and taxonomic scales. For instance, variation in specialization can affect resource competition and coexistence1,2, species extinction risks and responses to disturbance3–5, stability of interaction networks6,7, and top-down effects on primary productivity8. At broad scales, generalism (feeding on multiple disparate food sources) may facilitate species range expansion5. More specialized interactions near the equator relative to the poles may influence the latitudinal diversity gradient, that is, in low-latitude tropical habitats, ecological specialization can allow for “niche packing” of more species with narrow niches, but specialization itself may also evolve in response to intense competition from greater numbers of species at lower latitudes9–11. Furthermore, variation in diversification rates among lineages can be attributed to differences in dietary specialization12,13.

Insect herbivory is one of the most ubiquitous and consequential species interactions in terrestrial ecosystems worldwide14,15. Unlike vertebrate herbivores, insect herbivores are typically found directly in or on the plants they feed on at one or more stages of their life cycle, leading to their classification as parasites in many cases16. Because of this close association, insect herbivores are best known for narrow, specialized diets. However, while the global distribution of diet breadth in insect herbivores is characterized by a predominance of specialized diets, in large part as a function of the fitness costs of host switching16,17, there is also a long, thin tail of highly generalist species18.

One possible explanation for the maintenance of both highly specialized and generalist species is variation in plant species richness along latitudinal gradients19–21, representing a range of discrete resources that can promote diversification and specialization22,23. In areas of high resource diversity (such as the tropics), we expect a negative relationship between resource diversity and consumer diet breadth when generalism carries a fitness cost24,25. This can drive adaptive radiations into multiple specialized niches driven by consumer competition26–28. Thus, if tradeoffs between niche breadth and fitness are widespread, we hypothesize that lower latitudes harbor more specialized insect herbivores because of the diversity of plant hosts, while at the same time, the most speciose plant lineages allow for the evolution of a greater number of specialists9,18.

Plant species richness itself varies along global climate gradients21,29, which themselves may directly impact insects. For example, highly seasonal climates have limited growing seasons and can therefore limit herbivore resource availability30. In regions where insect and hostplant climate niches differ, we might expect selection for broader insect diets in seasonal habitats as search time for the “correct” hostplant becomes costly31. If several hostplants are available, insects in seasonal habitats may opt to specialize on hosts that maximize growth and minimize development time32,33. However, hostplant use is often plastic, meaning that this specialization may vary among populations, or within populations across seasons according to temperature- or precipitation-dependent hostplant availability or benefits to herbivores31. Realized diet breadth across populations may thus be broader than expected. While previous studies have examined global variation in insect diet breadth along latitudinal gradients in resource richness (e.g.18), how this variation is associated with resource availability vs. direct and indirect effects of climate remains relatively unexplored.

Although the latitudinal diversity gradient of high richness in the tropics and low richness at the poles may hold for plants in mainland continental regions19, the same may not be true for islands, a discrepancy largely driven by their small size and isolation from mainland species pools of both plants and their mutualistic partners34–36. Insect lineages colonizing islands where plant resources are unexploited may be driven towards specialization over evolutionary time37–39. On the other hand, the reduced plant species richness on islands relative to mainland regions may limit the evolution of specialized herbivore diets, and a lack of competition may allow for ecological release and generalism40,41. Which of these patterns prevails in island habitats is likely a function of the insect assemblages on islands. Assemblages composed of largely dispersal-limited island-endemic species are likely to be more specialized on island plants, while assemblages composed of wide-ranging species whose ranges include but are not limited to islands will likely be more generalist on average42,43.

Here, we investigate plant-feeding butterfly caterpillars (Papilionoidea), for which the ecological and evolutionary consequences of dietary specialization are particularly well-studied since Ehrlich and Raven’s foundational work on the evolutionary arms race between caterpillars and their chemically-defended hostplants18,23,44,45. Specifically, we investigated how climate, habitat, and resource availability affected the larval diet breadth of local butterfly assemblages and individual species, as well as how island-endemic butterflies differ from mainland ones in their larval diet breadths. While previous studies have explored global patterns in diet breadth (e.g.,18), they have largely focused on describing broad correlative patterns in diet breadth and its relationship to resource diversity. This leaves the direct and indirect effects of putative biotic and abiotic drivers of this variation unexplored. Moreover, the geographic distributions dataset for both butterfly and plant species necessary to investigate these interactions have been unavailable until recently19,46. We use structural equation models to examine global patterns and direct and indirect drivers of diet breadth.

We hypothesize that caterpillar diet breadth will primarily be influenced by resource availability (host plant richness), while climate effects will be mostly indirect and mediated through their effects on plants. We therefore predict a negative relationship between diet breadth and latitude, with more specialized species in low-latitude regions with greater plant diversity. We additionally predict that island-endemic butterfly species will have narrower diets than non-island species, and that the diet breadth of island assemblages of butterflies will be negatively correlated with the proportion of endemic species.

Results

Latitudinal gradients

A generalized additive model (GAM) showed that plant family richness peaked primarily near the equator and declined towards the poles, with a secondary peak around latitude 36°N (Fig. 1, Table S1). Assemblage-level (including all species in 100 × 100 km2 grid cells) hostplant specialization closely tracked plant family richness, with a primary peak in specialization near the equator and a secondary peak near latitude 39°N (Fig. 1A). Because diet breadth distributions tend to be strongly right-skewed18, we used the shape parameter (α) of a discrete truncated Pareto power distribution to describe hostplant specialization in these assemblages. The pattern for mean phylogenetic diversity (PD) was less clear-cut (Fig. 1B), while specialization (low mean PD) peaked near the equator, we also observed comparable peaks in specialization at latitudes 34°S and 50°N, suggesting additional factors driving larval diet breadth.

Assemblage-level diet breadth

After correcting for butterfly community phylogenetic structure and species richness, we found positive relationships between caterpillar hostplant specialization (α) and plant family richness, net primary productivity (NPP), percent tree cover, precipitation of the warmest quarter, and mean annual precipitation (MAP; Table S2), indicating that these factors are correlated with increased diet specialization. We also found strong negative relationships between mean PD vs. climate and vegetation predictors, and a strong interaction between precipitation seasonality and mean annual temperature (MAT; Fig. 2). To examine direct and indirect effects of climate and vegetation on larval diet breadth, we built a series of structural equation models (SEMs; Fig. 3; Fig. S1 and S2). Because of our large sample sizes (n = 10,669 grid cells for mean PD and n = 10,654 for α), traditional SEM fit statistics (χ2 and Fisher’s C) indicated poor model fit. We therefore focus on partial R2 values for larval diet breadth and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) values (with other fit measures in Table S3, Supplementary Data 1;47), because these generally provide better indications of fit and explanatory power for the response variables of interest48,49.

Our SEM of hostplant specialization (α; χ23 = 220.936, p < 2e-16, RMSEA = 0.083, partial R2 = 0.57) showed a significant direct positive effect of plant family richness (standard effect size [SES] = 0.943, p < 0.001), indicating that this factor decreased assemblage-level larval diet breadth; whereas MAT had a strong negative effect on α (SES = -0.214, p < 0.001), indicating an increase in diet breadth (Fig. 3). However, MAT exerted a strong positive indirect effect (SES = 0.438) on α mediated through plant family richness that was greater in magnitude than its direct (negative) effect. We also found weaker but significant indirect effects of MAP (SES = −0.0150) and precipitation of the warmest quarter (SES = 0.200), mediated by plant family richness and NPP. For mean PD (χ25 = 1625.253, p < 2e-16, RMSEA = 0.174, partial R2 = 0.33), we found similar direct effects of plant family richness (SES = −0.750, p < 0.001) and MAT (SES = 0.648, p < 0.001) indicating decreases and increases in larval diet breadth, respectively. Specifically, we found an indirect negative effect of MAT on mean PD through plant family richness (SES = −0.386), although it was not nearly as large as the same indirect effect on α. We also observed weaker indirect effects of precipitation of the warmest quarter (SES = -0.165), precipitation seasonality (SES = -0.167), and a MAT × precipitation seasonality interaction (SES = 0.327) on mean PD. Specifically, warm areas with low precipitation seasonality had higher plant richness and productivity, while cold areas with low precipitation seasonality had the lowest richness and productivity (Fig. 2). When we applied a stricter threshold for hostplant records (10 vs. 2 records indicating a plant family’s use as a host by a given species), the direct and indirect effects of climate and vegetation on diet richness were similar (Fig. S2; Table S3, Supplementary Data 1), although we observed better model fits (α: RMSEA = 0.060, mean PD: RMSEA = 0.143) due to smaller sample sizes48. Notably, the direct effect of NPP on α hostplant specialization shifted from weakly negative to positive, although it was still dwarfed by the large direct effect of plant family richness.

We also investigated whether caterpillar assemblages on islands differed in diet breadth from those in mainland habitats. Island assemblages had greater diet breadth on average than main assemblages, an effect that was significant for both mean PD and α with the 643 butterfly species for which at least 10 larval hostplant associations were recorded (model averaged ANOVA; mean PD: z = 11.851, partial R2 = 0.025, p < 2e-16; α: z = 8.563, partial R2 = 0.066, p < 2e-16; Fig. S3A, C), but significant only for mean PD with the broader set of species for which at least 2 hostplant associations were recorded (model averaged ANOVA; mean PD: z = 5.641, partial R2 = 0.0208, p < 2e-16; α: z = 1.089, partial R2 = 0.00152, p = 0.276; Fig. 4A, C). Within islands, however, the relationship between assemblage-level larval diet breadth and island endemism was highly sensitive to the species included. With the broader set of species, we found a negative relationship between larval diet breadth and endemism – as island assemblages were more dominated by endemic species (325 island endemic species), mean PD declined (ANOVA: F1,443 = 34.47, R2 = 0.070, p = 8.51e-09) and α increased (ANOVA: F1,443 = 20.68, R2 = 0.043, p = 7.01e-06), even after accounting for latitude (Fig. 4B, D). In contrast, when we restricted our dataset to the narrower set of 643 butterfly species, the relationship flipped, that is, as island assemblages were dominated by endemic species (40 island endemic species), mean PD increased (ANOVA: F1,442 = 86.53, R2 = 0.162, p < 2e-16) and α decreased (ANOVA: F1,440 = 119.7, R2 = 0.212, p < 2e-16; Fig. S3B, D).

Species-level diet breadth

When we modeled diet breadth using individual butterfly species as replicates (n = 2570), we found significant direct positive effects of plant family richness on species-level larval diet breadth. Even after accounting for butterfly range size, we found that the interaction between MAT and precipitation seasonality affected plant family richness, which in turn directly affected larval diet breadth (hostplant specialization p < 0.001, host PD p < 0.001; Fig. S4). When we excluded hostplant families with fewer than 10 records (n = 643), direct effects on larval diet breadth were overall much smaller and less significant (Fig. S5). For the subset of island and mainland species with comparable ranges, island caterpillar species did not differ significantly in diet breadth (hostplant specialization p = 0.537; host PD p = 0.0741).

Our SEMs did not explain much variation in larval diet breadth (hostplant specialization partial R2 = 0.03; host PD partial R2 = 0.03). We observed direct positive effects of within-range plant family richness and MAT on both larval diet breadth metrics, indicating that species with large numbers of plant families in their range and those in hotter climates had broader diets on average. The combined direct and indirect effects of MAT mediated through plant family richness were greater than the direct effect of plant family richness on larval diet breadth (Fig. S4). By contrast, at the assemblage level, the combined direct and indirect effects of plant family richness were such that larval diet breadth decreased with increasing plant family richness, and these effects exceeded the combined direct and indirect effects of MAT on diet richness (Fig. S1).

Discussion

As expected, we observed indirect effects on both α and mean PD mediated through plant family richness, supporting the idea that MAT increases diet specialization by increasing the number of discrete resources available to consumers18. However, our SEMs revealed direct effects of MAT on α and mean PD of the same order of magnitude as the direct effects of plant family richness, albeit in the opposite direction (Fig. 3A, Fig. S2A). These results may reflect greater plasticity and intraspecific variation in hostplant use in hotter climates. Numerous studies have demonstrated that butterflies and other insects are able to alter their choice of hostplant in extreme temperatures in the interest of increasing heat tolerance, to accommodate physiological changes associated with increased temperatures, or to accommodate differences among hostplants in heat tolerance50–52. Alternatively, extreme heat, especially in areas with low tree cover, may limit flight time and female hostplant choice31.

Effects of plant diversity on caterpillar diet breadth

While MAT was the strongest individual climate predictor of diet richness, its direct effects were largely masked by the effects of plant family richness, the strongest direct driver of larval diet breadth (Figs. 2, 3A, Fig. S2A). Plant family richness increased with MAT, leading to increased diet specialization up to a point. For mean PD, we found that precipitation seasonality interacted with MAT to affect plant family richness; when precipitation seasonality was high, plant family richness was moderate across all temperature regimes. However, when precipitation seasonality was low, plant family richness peaked at high temperatures, reflecting the warm and wet equatorial tropics, where we observed the maximum average plant family richness (Fig. 1, Fig. S1), minimum mean PD, and maximum α. These results are corroborated by the strong positive effect of precipitation of the warmest quarter on plant family richness, indicating that the positive influence of MAT on plant family richness only translates to increased dietary specialization when conditions are sufficiently wet53.

In areas of high plant diversity, particularly in forests or other areas of high tree cover21,53, specialization is thought to be favored for several reasons. First, high plant diversity allows herbivores to be more selective of their hostplants, favoring specialization in the interest of reducing competition for food and oviposition sites at high densities26–28. High herbivore density may itself be a possible function of high primary productivity54,55, which also exerted small but significant negative effects on diet richness (Fig. S2A, B). Higher-intensity trophic interactions in diverse tropical habitats can select for strong and distinct plant chemical defenses56,57, in turn selecting for specialist herbivores uniquely equipped to metabolize and/or sequester those defenses44,58, while fitness costs of host-switching tend to favor narrow diets16,59.

While plant family richness was a strong direct predictor of assemblage-level larval diet breadth, it was less informative for individual species (Table S4, Supplementary Data 1; Fig. S4–S5). Although we accounted for species’ range sizes in our models, we necessarily used a single measure of larval diet breadth for each species rather than accounting for variation among populations within species, some of which occur over large geographic ranges and exhibit wide variation in hostplant use60,61. This meant that we could not detect signals of natural selection on larval diet breadth in response to hostplant availability, which acts on individual populations at scales usually smaller than the range of the entire species. Additionally, we used the total number of plant families pooled across entire species ranges, potentially smoothing over variation in plant family richness that might better predict species-level diet breadth.

Does island endemism drive specialization?

We show that caterpillar assemblages on islands appeared to be much less specialized on their hostplants than those in mainland areas on average (Fig. 4A, C), consistent with the hypothesis that most assemblages on islands that we observed consisted of non-endemic species that are generalist throughout their range43. Butterfly populations at range edges often feed on distinctive or novel hostplants in a way that expand the species-average niche breadth43. Because our hostplant dataset is aggregated across multiple populations, expansion into a wider geographic area equates to broader niches on average across the range of the entire species, despite narrow diet breadth that may occur in marginal populations42,43. Even on islands with well-documented endemic species, endemism may drive assemblage-level generalism (Fig. S3B, D). Limited availability of interaction partners has been proposed as a mechanism of reduced diversity on islands relative to mainlands, even for islands at low latitudes35, and may also contribute to reduced specialization as well. On the other hand, reduced competition for plant resources among smaller assemblages of island species may lead to ecological release, allowing island species to expand into broader, more generalist diets40,41.

Interestingly, our investigation into assemblage-island endemism and larval diet breadth revealed that the relationship between these variables is highly sensitive to how commonly observed hostplant associations are; in the strict dataset of only 643 species with at least 10 hostplant association records, just 40 species are island endemics, compared to 325 species in the full dataset. Assemblages with a larger proportion of island-endemic species in the full dataset tended to be more specialized (Fig. 4B, D), potentially indicating that island endemism influences assemblage-level specialization, while the strict dataset showed that increased proportions of island-endemic species reinforced the high degrees of diet generalism found on islands (Fig. S3B, D). This raises two alternative possibilities for the larval diet breadth of island assemblages. First, poorly-documented (those with fewer than 10 hostplant records) “specialist” endemics may actually be generalist species for which few hostplant records exist, and more extensive investigation of hostplant associations in these species would reveal that the majority of island endemic butterfly species are generalists as larvae, due to limited availability of hostplants and/or competition35,41.

Alternatively, generalist endemics may be more likely to have well-documented diets, either because they are more abundant in their ranges and hence more detectable62–64, or because they are more easily reared in feeding assays, so our results for the stricter dataset reflect a biased piece of the broader picture in which islands dominated by endemics actually tend to be more specialized. Island endemics may drive specialization due to longer histories of isolation from mainland habitats. Non-endemic island species likely represent recent colonization events in marginal habitats; they may be specialized at the population level within their broader range43, but they have had insufficient time to become reproductively isolated as distinctive “neo-endemic” species37,39. Furthermore, the geographic isolation and small size of islands with high endemism contribute to limited availability of hostplants34,36, which may also drive specialization. Limited hostplant availability is provided as one explanation for why there are multiple examples of carnivory in Lepidoptera on islands65,66, while it is comparatively much rarer on mainlands. Species endemic to larger islands with historic connections to continents likely represent “paleoendemic” lineages (phylogenetically distinct members of older clades with origins that predate isolation), and the mechanisms that drive specialization in these regions are less clear.

Our results reveal that global patterns in plant diversity, which are affected by climate, play a key role in shaping caterpillar diet breadth, while there also exist direct effects of climate on diet breadth that are comparable in magnitude to the direct effects of plant diversity. A prior study18 found that the effect of latitudinal variation in plant richness on specialization was approximately 25% of the direct effect of latitude on the global trend in specialization. While latitude is a useful proxy for several factors that may influence larval diet breadth, including climate and evolutionary history, it does not provide any insight into the mechanisms driving global patterns of species interactions10,11. Our study breaks down latitude into several covarying environmental predictors that offer more direct relationships with larval diet breadth. Although an underlying mechanism driving diet breadth (plant family richness) is consistent with Forister et al.18, our results for the global pattern of diet breadth differ. We attribute this difference largely to differences in methodology; while Forister et al.18 dealt with a relatively small number of empirical point samples of insect communities, we used 10,669 assemblages of co-occurring butterfly species predicted using species distribution models46, which allow us to fill in gaps that would otherwise be excluded due to poor geographic sampling.

The roles of temperature and its interaction with precipitation and precipitation seasonality are testament both to the importance of climate in dictating the evolution and ecology of terrestrial ectotherms67–69, and to the vulnerability of butterfly and other insect communities to global change as these climate regimes shift. Similarly, the influence of endemism and range size on diet breadth of island caterpillars emphasize the potential impact of range expansions and species invasions on insect communities and food web dynamics in these isolated habitats70,71. While our results represent a broad-scale exploration of patterns and processes that have their roots in the deeper evolutionary histories of butterflies and plants, we anticipate that our work will provide valuable understanding of how these processes shape butterfly and hostplant responses to global change.

Methods

Caterpillar and plant distributions

We used comprehensive species-level native distribution maps for 10,372 species of butterflies of the world and 151,508 species of plants19,46. Briefly, these distribution maps were modeled based on point data available from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; 10.15468/dl.5fwp2a accessed 15 October 2023). We modeled species distributions as a function of environmental conditions and sampling intensity as background points46. The models were calibrated to expected accessible areas, defined using clade-specific dispersal rates fitted using a phylogenetic spherical Brownian motion model72, and species distribution models using maximum entropy. Each species range map was stacked at the resolution of 100 km × 100 km grid cells to generate a presence-absence matrix for species across 13,316 grid cells using the polys2comm function in the R package phyloregion v.1.0.973. Below, we reproduce the process for butterfly distributions as described by Daru46, while the process for plants is similarly described by Daru19.

We used comprehensive species-level native distribution maps for 10,372 butterfly species worldwide and 151,508 plant species. To build butterfly distribution maps, we used 87 million records from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), accessed on 15 October, 2023. From these point occurrences, we reconciled taxonomic names using the comprehensive checklist of country-level occurrences by Pinkert et al.74. This checklist provides accepted names for extant butterflies along with their native ranges within broad political divisions. We then matched names from point occurrences to those in Pinkert et al.74 and only retained species named in Pinkert et al.74, thereby removing potential naming errors in the point data. We also removed duplicate records, those without coordinate information, points in the centroids of political divisions, points in the ocean, points at (0°,0°), and points within a 100-meter radius of biodiversity institutions (zoos, museums, universities, etc.) from the filtered data using the R package CoordinateCleaner v.3.0.175.

This procedure resulted in a total of 10,393 species names and 9.747,480 records, excluding 77,647,783 records (89%) with unmatched species names and/or without coordinate information. While acknowledging the exclusions, we focused only on the data retained after cleaning for downstream analysis. Next, we characterized species occurrences more finely so that they capture the species’ distributions in their known native ranges. To do this, we overlaid the point occurrences with the country-level maps from Pinkert et al.74 of the same species and retained only points that were delimited within the corresponding species country-level maps for further analyses using the crop function in the R package terra v.1.7-5576.

After cleaning the data, we spatially thinned records with more than 4,000 occurrences by subsampling records across 5-arc-minute grid cells using the function gridSample in the R package dismo v.1.3-1677. To incorporate the cleaned point records into species distribution ranges, we generated alpha hull polygons corresponding to the maximum geographical extent of each species78–80. We generated alpha hulls for species with ≥ 3 non-linear unique points, and buffered 10 km outwards around point localities for species with fewer than 3 locations per IUCN sampling standards81 to account for potential measurement and spatial uncertainties. We then incorporated the thinned point records into polygon maps by modeling with alpha hulls using the R package rangeBuilder v.2.182 with the initial alpha set to 2. For each species, we increased the alpha hyperparameter incrementally until a range was formed encompassing at least 99% of the occurrence records. We clipped the resulting polygons to only the terrestrial part of the range using a basemap from Natural Earth83. Finally, we created pseudo-occurrence records for each species derived from the alpha hull polygons and point buffers by converting to raster at a resolution of 5 arc minutes (~10 km at the equator), then to spatial points, thinning them to a maximum of 500 records per species to reduce spatial autocorrelation84.

Predicting a species’ habitat suitability and ability to occupy space should be informed by the species’ dispersal ability85. To this end, we fit a phylogeographic spherical Brownian motion (SBM) model72, which quantifies the dispersal of a clade over time as a diffusion-like process based on a single diffusion coefficient D, while accounting for Earth’s spherical geometry86,87 using the function fit_sbm_const in the R package castor v.1.7.872,88. This yields a diffusivity score D, measured in km2 Ma-1, based on maximum-likelihood and independent contrasts between sister lineages72. The fit_sbm_const function calculates diffusivity using a rooted phylogenetic tree and geographic coordinates for the tips of the tree. We then computed species dispersal rates as the expected dispersal distance traveled by a species per year (km yr-1) using the function expected_SBM_distance. For our phylogeny, we used a time-calibrated tree of 2,254 species from Kawahara et al.89 representing all families, all subfamilies and tribes, and 92% of described genera. To complete the tree, we grafted the remaining species that were not in the original tree onto the backbone topology using the function phylo.maker in the package V.Phylomaker2 v.0.1.0 under scenario 290, in which new tips are bound to randomly selected nodes at and below the genus- or family-level basal node. We created 100 random trees based on this method and averaged them to create a consensus tree for downstream analyses. The tip coordinates were estimated by taking the centroid of each species’ alpha hull polygon. Finally, we used species dispersal rates to define a calibration area by buffering the dispersal rates around the alpha hull polygons of each species before intersecting the buffered zones with the terrestrial ecoregions occupied by the species91 to predict habitat suitability for each species.

To account for biased sampling of butterfly occurrences92,93, we generated background points as nulls for species distribution modeling using a vector of probability weights. These weights represent the likelihood of a butterfly occurring in a particular area, based on factors such as habitat suitability and distance from known occurrences. Specifically, we used the function backg in phyloregion73, which takes a species’ accessible area and a Gaussian kernel density94 to estimate the density of butterfly occurrences across the landscape, and then scaled this density between 0 and 1 for modeling95. Finally, we used this density grid to randomly select 10,000 background points for each species within its respective accessible area, representing the natural areas where butterflies could potentially occur.

Building on the success of IUCN expert-range polygons in generating species distribution models (SDMs) across various taxonomic groups84,96–98 and their demonstrated agreement with point data99, we used alpha hulls for modeling butterfly distributions in the absence of expert-range polygons for butterflies. We sampled the alpha hull polygons to generate 500 points per species for input into the SDM as in previous studies84,96–98 rather than using raw and biased point occurrences. To this end, we used a regular sampling procedure that systematically aligned samples by transferring values associated with the alpha-hull polygons' geometries to a raster at a resolution matching the environmental data, then thinning to 500 points per species.

Using the occurrence data as input, we employed a 75% random sample for model development, while keeping the remaining 25% for model evaluation. To predict the distribution of species into unsampled areas, we used SDMs to predict the relationship between the observed occurrences and environmental factors while restricted to the calibration area defined above. We used environmental predictors from the WorldClim database100, which represent historical climate layers for a total of 19 bioclimatic variables at a spatial grain of 5 arc minutes. We also included elevation from WorldClim as an additional variable. We then computed variance inflation factors (VIFs) to identify and remove highly correlated predictor variables using the car package v.3.1-3101, selecting only variables with VIFs less than 5. We excluded pixels corresponding to inland water bodies from Natural Earth83 and then masked them to each species’ calibration area.

We used maximum entropy (MaxEnt) to model butterfly species distributions because it generally outperforms other methods based on predictive accuracy and does not generate response curves that may lead to unpredictable behavior when applied to novel climatic conditions102. MaxEnt is also widely used for species distribution modelling with presence-only data and works well for several taxonomic groups. For model settings, we considered important decision steps in the input data and parameter settings by using the sdm function in phyloregion, which we used to evaluate the model with a range of different complexities in terms of the feature classes and regularization multiplier settings in MaxEnt, including linear, threshold, and hinge responses; and tested a set of regularization multiplier values (2, 5, 10, 15, 20 using a 5-fold spatial block cross-validation design103.

For each species, we generated five sets of models and reported the median to account for uncertainties across different model runs. The model prediction consisted of a range map stored in raster format at a 5 arc minute grid cell resolution. We quantified the predictive accuracy of the models using the area under the curve (AUC104), True Skill Statistic (TSS105) and Boyce Index106,107. We did not exclude models with lower test scores because while such values may mean poor prediction108, we found that model performance was generally high across the evaluation metrics with a median AUC of 0.91 (ranging from 0.36 to 0.999), Boyce Index of 0.87 (-0.55 to 0.99), and TSS of 0.42 (-0.16 to 0.88). These high scores indicate that our models' predictions are a reliable indicator of where the butterfly species are likely to be found. We provide an ODMAP standard report of our protocol109 in Daru46.

We converted the suitability of the models for each species into binary presence-absence maps by using the 95% quantile of the suitability values extracted from the underlying occurrence records as a presence threshold. To represent species distributions as continuous surfaces analogous to the extent-of-occurrence range maps used by the IUCN, we dissolved each spatial raster to a polygon in terra76. In the final step, we smoothed the outlines of the polygons to make the maps more visually appealing in accordance with IUCN mapping standards for polygons81. Additionally, smoothing the outlines helped to reduce visual clutter and make it easier to see overall patterns in the data. To smooth the outlines, we used spline interpolation of vertices in the R package smoothr v.1.0.0110.

Caterpillar diets

We characterized caterpillar hostplants at the family level, which has been adopted as the standard taxonomic rank for examining host use evolution18,22,89. Larval diet breadth data for 4,127 butterfly species were obtained from multiple sources, including LepTraits111 (n = 3,643), Kawahara et al.89 (n = 1027), and the NHM HOSTS database112 (n = 36), and filtered to retain only those for which at least two independently observed records of feeding on a hostplant were documented (n = 2,570). Because closely related hostplant families may share similar profiles of secondary metabolites, caterpillars may be more likely to feed on more closely related families44,58. Thus, in addition to examining the number of hostplant families used by caterpillar species, we also used a family-level phylogeny of plants based on the supertree by Jin & Qian90 to inform diet breadth. Specifically, we measured hostplant phylogenetic diversity (PD) as the total branch lengths shared by all host families113. When caterpillars were only documented to feed on one hostplant family, we included the most distant tips of the tree for the family as a way of measuring the phylogenetic distances within the family. For further information on diet breadth metrics and the selection of butterfly species, see Fig. S6. To account for the sensitivity of our results to the number of hostplant records, we also conducted our analyses with diet breadth metrics calculated with a minimum of 10 records per species-hostplant pair.

Predictor variables

To examine biogeographic drivers of caterpillar diet breadth, we focused on climate and vegetation predictors (Fig. S7). For climate, we selected four bioclimatic variables from WorldClim100 that capture both seasonal variation and mean values of temperature and precipitation: mean annual temperature (MAT, °C), mean annual precipitation (MAP, mm), precipitation of the warmest quarter (mm), and precipitation seasonality (annual coefficient of variation). For vegetation, we included net primary productivity (NPP, kg C m-²) and percent tree cover data from MODIS114,115. Increased productivity can support greater numbers of competing herbivores and potentially influence their diet breadth as a result24,27,54,55; vegetation type and percent cover can affect both primary productivity and microhabitat diversity, affecting plant diversity21,53. We additionally included plant richness at the family level, the taxonomic resolution that matched our diet breadth measures. For climate and vegetation we used the value corresponding to the centroid of each grid cell at a resolution of 10 min of a degree (~18.5 km at the equator), while for plant family richness, we summed across all families with ranges overlapping the grid cell.

We additionally investigated how isolation influences caterpillar diet breadth by comparing butterfly species found on islands with those on the mainland. A species was classified as an island endemic if its distribution was restricted to an island or group of islands, regardless of historical connection to mainland habitats. An exception were the Sunda Islands and New Guinea, in which several species were also present at the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula, but were considered to be island species116,117. We used similar criteria to classify grid cells as island or mainland and characterized the proportion of species in these grid cells that were island endemics.

Data analyses – latitudinal gradients

To assess latitudinal trends in caterpillar diet breadth, we built generalized additive models (GAMs) of diet breadth as a function of latitude across 13,316 different 100 × 100 km2 grid cells. We also modeled the family-level richness of plants in the same grid cells, because past studies have shown that insect diet breadth often shows a negative relationship with hostplant richness18. We plotted maps of assemblage-level diet breadth using the R packages sf v.1.1-0118 and maptools v.1.1-9119.

Data analyses – assemblage level

For both diet dispersion and diet richness, we examined the effects of climatic, habitat, and geographic predictors in two ways: 1) using diet breadths of caterpillar species occurring in each 100 × 100 km2 grid cell as response units (“assemblages”; n = 10,669 in which all predictors were available), and 2) using individual species’ diet breadth as response units (n = 2570). Diet breadth distributions in herbivore assemblages tend to be strongly right-skewed18, so we opted to describe assemblage-level hostplant specialization as the shape parameter (α) of a discrete truncated Pareto power distribution18, where high values indicate narrower diets (fewer families). We were not able to calculate a shape parameter (α) value for assemblages of one species with a known diet breadth, so the sample size for this dataset was slightly smaller (n = 10,654). We used the mean diet family-level phylogenetic diversity of assemblages to describe assemblage-level PD, such that higher values indicate broader diets. For our analyses with a threshold of 10 records per hostplant-caterpillar pair, sample sizes were 10,203 and 10,495 for hostplant specialization and mean PD, respectively.

Assemblage-level larval diet breadth reflects a combination of historical factors – natural selection on species and deeper phylogenetic nodes, historical dispersal and vicariance – and contemporary factors – ecological selection, dispersal limitation, ecological drift – that act together on the trait distributions of co-occurring species120,121. Here, we focus mainly on natural and ecological selection by climate and vegetation variables within grid cells. On the other hand, species-level larval diet breadth reflects lineage-specific historical evolutionary forces acting across geographic area122. Together, sampling units of grid cells and species provide a holistic picture of the forces that shape larval diet breadth at a global scale.

We first modeled each larval diet breadth variable at the assemblage level in response to caterpillar species richness and phylogenetic structure (mean pairwise distance) to control for sampling effects from the global species pool and phylogenetic signal in larval diet breadth. We used residuals from these initial models as responses to our 7 predictor variables. For our phylogeny, we used the same consensus tree as in our species distribution modelling protocol. While more explicit methods of accounting for spatial and phylogenetic signal in the analysis of assemblage and community-level trait variation exist (e.g. phylogenetic [generalized] linear mixed models123), the number of grid cells and species in our dataset made this approach computationally infeasible.

While caterpillar diet breadth may be directly influenced by climate or the availability of hostplants, the latter is likely influenced by climate. Therefore, we built a series of structural equation models (SEMs; Fig. 3, Fig. S1, Fig. S2) to examine direct and indirect effects of climate and vegetation on larval diet breadth using the package piecewiseSEM v.2.3.0.148. We selected our set of SEM predictors based on standard effect sizes of our initial set of predictors on each response variable separately in linear models, with a cutoff of 0.25, and narrowed these down to a subset that had VIFs less than 5, indicating minimal collinearity, using the car R package v.3.1-3101. For α (hostplant specialization), we included MAT, MAP, and precipitation of the warmest quarter as exogenous predictor variables and vegetation variables as mediating variables. For mean PD, we included MAT, precipitation seasonality, their interaction, MAP, and precipitation of the warmest quarter as exogenous predictors, with the same mediating variables. We compared nested SEMs according to AIC and multiple goodness-of-fit metrics, including χ2, Fisher’s C, and RMSEA to account for large sample size47,48, testing only paths that made biological sense a priori. We modeled all variables in general linear models, except for plant family richness, which we modeled in a generalized linear model as a Poisson-distributed variable with a log link function.

To examine whether caterpillar assemblages on islands differed in diet breadth from those in mainland habitats, we compared residual larval diet breadth in island grid cells (n = 445) to that in a random set of 445 mainland grid cells, which we averaged based on AIC weights. Because island grid cells in our dataset are not uniformly distributed across latitudinal bands, apparent differences between island and mainland assemblages may simply reflect a broader latitudinal gradient in larval diet breadth, underlaid by climatic and vegetation features as described above. Thus, in comparing island assemblages to mainland assemblages, we randomly selected paired mainland grid cells within the same absolute latitudinal band as the corresponding island grid cell and modeled larval diet breadth with grid cell pair as a covariate; to visualize latitude-corrected diet breadth, we used residuals from GAMs of larval diet breadth against latitude. Furthermore, the breadth of larval diet assemblages on islands may vary depending on whether they are composed of island-endemic species. Therefore, we additionally examined the relationship between diet breadth and the proportion of caterpillar species on the island that were endemic to the island or archipelago.

Data analyses – species level

At the species level, we modeled larval diet breadth as a function of climate, habitat, and plant family richness within each species’ range polygon. For each species, we overlaid butterfly range polygons onto rasters of climate and habitat data and averaged across the range, and summed the total number of plant families present within the range polygon. We narrowed our initial predictor pool down to five with VIFs less than 5: MAT, precipitation seasonality, and their interaction, NPP, and plant family richness. To initially account for phylogenetic signal in larval diet breadth, we modeled the response of species-level diet breadth to our five predictors in phylogenetic least squares models with the R package phytools v. 2.4-4124, averaging across models built with each of our set of 100 random trees. Because of greater variation in range size among butterfly species than among grid cells as replicates, we first modeled larval diet breadth as a function of range size in a pgls framework, and then used the model residuals in our tests of climate and vegetation effects. Controlling for range size addressed two key factors: the tendency for large areas to host a greater diversity of plant families34,36,125, and the observation that species with broad range size are more likely to exhibit generalist feeding behaviors across their range5,42,43.

We also separately modeled the effect of island endemism on larval diet breadth using phylogenetic least squares. Recognizing that the full breadth of mainland ranges is much greater than that of island areas, we restricted these analyses to encompass only those mainland species whose range areas fell within 1.5 times the interquartile range of island range areas (560 island and 1,369 mainland species).

From these initial models, we derived a final set of species-level predictors for use in SEMs of species-level larval diet breadth. We built SEMs including MAT, precipitation seasonality, and their interaction as exogenous predictors, and plant family richness as an endogenous predictor of diet richness (defined as the number of hostplant families per species or as PD) for the full set of species. We compared nested SEMs according to AIC, χ2, RMSEA, and Fisher’s C. Our full pipeline from selecting butterfly species through the species- and assemblage-level analyses of climate and vegetation effects on larval diet breadth can be seen in Fig. S6.
