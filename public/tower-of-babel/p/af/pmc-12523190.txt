Cholesterol metabolism on stem cell fate determination: a frontier horizon
Authors: Boyang Xu, Ling Li, Qian Wei, Yuli Wang, He Huang, Tao Pang, Zhijian Wei, Guoqing Zheng, Yue Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCholesterol, a key steroid in cell membranes and organelles, is vital for processes like migration and apoptosis, regulated by biosynthesis, uptake, and esterification. Its metabolism significantly impacts stem cell fate via pathways like AKT/FOXO1 and Notch, influencing proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Thus, cholesterol emerges as a promising therapeutic target. This review aims to shed light on the impact of cholesterol metabolism on stem cells and explores cholesterol-targeting drugs’ potential in regenerative medicine. Our purpose is to elucidate cholesterol’s role in stem cell function and guide therapeutic development.Graphical abstract Keywords: Cholesterol metabolism, Fate determination, Stem cell

Introduction

Cholesterol, a key sterol in mammals, is essential for cell membrane integrity, protein structure, and signaling [1, 2]. It aids in cell proliferation, embryonic signaling, and is a precursor for bile acids, oxysterols, and steroid hormones. Its interaction with transmembrane proteins is vital for their stability and regulation [3]. Stem cells, with their self-renewal and differentiation abilities, are promising for regenerative medicine and disease treatment [4, 5]. Cholesterol’s biosynthesis, uptake, and efflux are critical in stem cell differentiation, cell cycle, proliferation, oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial function through pathways like Delta-Notch and AKT/ERK [6, 7]. Cholesterol metabolism dysregulation significantly affects stem cell fate [8], proliferation, and differentiation [9]. Regulating cholesterol metabolism holds promise for enhancing stem cell therapies (Table 1), but our understanding of cholesterol’s specific roles in these processes is limited. Improving knowledge of the biological processes and signals involved in stem cell fate can reveal how cholesterol metabolism influences various stem cell types and inform targeted stem cell therapies.

The role of drugs targeting cholesterol metabolism

This review delves into the regulatory effect of cholesterol metabolism on various physiological processes in tendon stem cells (TDSCs), intestinal stem cells (ISCs), dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs), bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs), hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs), and cancer stem cells (CSCs). Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) reside in the stroma of various organs and tissues and are a diverse group of cells. These isolated MSCs are not uniform clonal cells but include stem cell subpopulations with multipotent differentiation potential, as well as progenitor and mature cells at different stages of differentiation [10, 11]. It highlights cholesterol’s role and mechanisms in regulating stem cell proliferation and differentiation, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic tool in disease (see Table 2).

Regulation of cholesterol metabolism—functional effects and molecular mechanisms in different cell types and species

Cholesterol metabolism

Cellular cholesterol levels are regulated by biosynthesis, uptake, export, and storage [12]. Mainly located in the cell membrane, cholesterol affects the membrane’s stiffness, fluidity, and permeability. We have summarized the key molecular pathways in cholesterol metabolism, highlighting its interaction with stem cells (Graphical abstract).

Classical cholesterol metabolic pathway in stem cells

Cholesterol biosynthesis is mediated by the mevalonate pathway (MVA). Initiated by the rate-limiting enzyme HMGCR, the pathway converts acetyl-CoA to HMG-CoA, then to mevalonate, which is further metabolized to farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) via farnesyl-PP synthetase. FPP is a precursor to squalene, leading to cholesterol formation, and to GGPP, which modifies small G proteins [13, 14].

Mammalian cells acquire cholesterol by synthesizing it or absorbing it from outside sources [15]. They take up cholesterol through the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), which breaks down circulating Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) into free cholesterol (FC) in lysosomes [16].

To prevent toxic FC buildup, ABCA1/ABCG1 transporters remove excess cholesterol, forming high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particles [17–19]. Excess FC is either distributed within cells or stored as cholesterol ester (CE) or lipid droplets (LD) by acyl-CoA-cholesterol acyltransferase (ACAT) [20, 21]. Sterol regulatory element binding proteins (SREBPs) and liver X receptors (LXRs) are transcription factors that regulate cholesterol levels (Fig. 1) [20]. SREBP2-SCAP controls cholesterol in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [22], detaching from insulin-induced gene 1 protein (INSIG1) and moving to the Golgi when ER cholesterol is low, then activating genes like squalene epoxidase (SQLE), HMGCR, and LDLR in the nucleus [23]. High ER cholesterol activates LXRs, inducing ABCA1 and ABCG1 to promote cholesterol efflux [24, 25]. SQLE and HMGCR are essential for actively proliferating stem cells, such as cancer and neural stem cells [7, 26], that depend on endogenous cholesterol synthesis and isoprenylation signaling.

Acetyl-CoA and other rate-limiting enzymes participate in the enzyme-linked reaction to synthesize cholesterol. B Cholesterol uptake. Exogenous cholesterol uptake in cells is mediated by LDLR. Regulation of stem cell fate through cholesterol metabolism

Cholesterol metabolism is crucial for stem cell regulation across species by balancing biosynthesis, uptake, and efflux. For cholesterol biosynthesis, CSCs influence organ size, survival, proliferation, and migration by regulating Rho/YAP/TAZ signaling via the Hippo pathway’s core effector [27]. DPSCs influence osteogenic and neuronal differentiation via the Ras/PI3K/AKT/ERK and EGF/bFGF/AKT/ERK pathways [28, 29]. BMSCs promote adipogenic differentiation through the SREBP2/PPARγ pathway and regulate proliferation and osteogenic differentiation via the BMP2/Runx2/ALP pathway [30–32]. ISCs rely on Delta-Notch signaling for proliferation, which accumulates in lipid rafts [33]. For cholesterol uptake, BMSCs use TGFβ/BMP and WNT pathways for osteogenic differentiation [34, 35], while mechanisms in mouse NSPCs and ISCs are not yet known. TDSCs manage apoptosis and autophagy through the ROS/AKT/FOXO1 pathway [36]. Cholesterol efflux in HSPCs influences their proliferation, differentiation, and mobilization through the IL-3/RAS-ERK pathway [37, 38], suggesting that cholesterol metabolism controls stem cell fate via specific signaling networks.

Dynamic regulation of stemness by cholesterol metabolism in stem cells

Stem cells show distinct metabolic traits compared to other cells [39]. A key question is whether there are shared metabolic regulatory mechanisms specific to stem cells from various tissues, which do not generally apply to other cell types.

Recent studies on cholesterol metabolism’s role in stem cell fate have shown that cholesterol affects membrane remodeling in CSCs, influencing self-renewal by maintaining receptor localization for pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and Hedgehog [40, 41], and regulating membrane stiffness to impact YAP/TAZ pathways, balancing proliferation and differentiation [42]. In HSPCs, cholesterol modulates ferroptosis resistance through non-membrane signaling or epigenetic regulation, enhancing antioxidant capacity via the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis to inhibit ferroptosis and reduce ROS, creating a “cholesterol-ferroptosis resistance” equilibrium [43]. Single-cell metabolomics has shown that HSPC subpopulations within the same tissue have varied metabolic activities, with hematopoietic stem cells displaying greater self-renewal and lower metabolic activity [44]. Studying metabolic molecule expression in stem cells related to cholesterol metabolism enhances understanding of stem cell fate regulation, potentially uncovering disease mechanisms and aiding regenerative medicine.

Cholesterol metabolism in stem cells

Cholesterol and TDSCs

TDSCs originating from multipotent cells within the paratenon demonstrate multilineage potential, differentiating into tendon fibers, tendon cells, and other cell types [45]. Aberrant proliferation and differentiation of TDSCs contribute to the heightened risk of pathologies such as osteoarthritis (OA) [46] and tendinitis [47, 48]. Excessive cholesterol intake has been implicated in augmenting ROS production [49], subsequently activating the ROS-dependent AKT-FOXO1 signaling cascade showed that enhanced AKT phosphorylation leads to hyperphosphorylation and nuclear accumulation of FOXO1 in a mouse model with a high-fat diet (HFD), thereby impairing TDSC proliferation and promoting apoptosis in vitro (Fig. 2) [36]. Elevated cholesterol intake boosts ROS generation, facilitating AKT activation and subsequent FOXO1 phosphorylation. Additionally, the elevation of ROS induced by cholesterol affects the NF-κB pathway in vitro and in vivo, thereby compromising TDSC differentiation in a mouse model with HFD [50], resulting in cell cycle arrest that restrains migration and proliferation capabilities [36]. These detrimental effects negatively impact TDSC functionality in post-injury tendon repair, ultimately exacerbating tendon pathology. Research has shown that cholesterol buildup causes endoplasmic reticulum stress, activating the unfolded protein response and impacting protein synthesis and folding in TDSCs [51]. Future studies should explore the link between cholesterol metabolism and TDSC function to create new treatments for tendon injuries.

Cholesterol and DPSCs

DPSCs derived from the dental pulp exhibit the capacity to differentiate into odontoblasts and osteoblasts, and maintaining this balance is crucial for dental repair [52, 53]. Cholesterol biosynthesis regulation is pivotal in this differentiation process [28, 29]. Statins and other competitive inhibitors can reduce cholesterol production in human DPSCs by suppressing HMGCR, the key enzyme in cholesterol synthesis [54]. Inhibiting cholesterol synthesis stimulates the RAS signaling pathway, leading to AKT and ERK1/2 phosphorylation [55], which increases the expression of osteogenic genes like Runx2 and BMP2, promoting osteogenic differentiation of DPSCs(Fig. 3) [55]. Conversely, it raises dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) levels, enhancing odontogenic commitment and suppressing proliferation of human dental pulp stem cells both in vitro and in vivo [56]. However, cholesterol’s role in differentiation, especially in odontoblasts and osteoblasts, remains debated, affecting therapeutic outcomes [57].

Reduced cholesterol synthesis promotes osteogenic signal expression and differentiation by modulating the PI3K/AKT/MAPK or ERK signaling pathway. Recent studies suggest that cholesterol metabolism may influence growth factor secretion, impacting the neuronal differentiation of DPSCs from healthy human teeth [58]. Enhanced cholesterol efflux dampens the activation of the AKT-ERK1/2 pathway induced by epidermal growth factor (EGF) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), thereby influencing nerve regeneration and neurological disorders in vitro [59, 60]. In summary, the role of cholesterol in the multipotential differentiation of DPSCs is intricate and pivotal. Its metabolic regulation maintains the balance between odontogenic and osteogenic fates and influences neurodegenerative processes. The findings emphasize the interplay between cholesterol and the AKT-ERK1/2 signaling pathway both in vitro and in vivo [61], suggesting that future therapeutic strategies targeting specific signaling pathways to fine-tune DPSC function differentiation. Cholesterol synthesis likely influences hDPSCs differentiation and proliferation through various pathways.

Cholesterol and NSPCs

NSPCs generate numerous neurons throughout life, crucial for brain development [62, 63]. These versatile cells can self-renew and differentiate into various brain cells, including neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes [64]. Although cholesterol metabolism appears to affect brain function, its role in NSPCs is unclear [65]. Investigating this could reveal how NSPCs influence cognitive function.

Cholesterol, especially LDL accumulation, hinders neuron stem cell (NSC) migration and proliferation in the subventricular zone (SVZ), reduces hippocampal neurogenesis, and leads to cognitive impairment without affecting microglia numbers in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease in vivo [66]. This indicates that the accumulation of cholesterol, particularly LDL, impairs cognitive function by suppressing NSC proliferation and neuronal differentiation. Similarly, increased cholesterol intake, even with restricted overall caloric intake, is associated with cognitive impairment in Ldlr mutant mice, reducing NSC proliferation and neuronal differentiation in the hippocampus subgranular zone (SGZ) [67]. In vitro studies also show that reduced cholesterol efflux impairs neuronal cell proliferation and reduces neurogenesis in the DG region of Ldlr−/− mice [68]. These results indicate that high cholesterol may impair memory and learning, but cholesterol synthesis is crucial for the growth and brain development of embryonic NSPCs [69, 70]. Thus, managing dietary cholesterol could help preserve hippocampal neurogenesis and prevent age-related cognitive decline. In summary, these findings highlight cholesterol’s importance for cognitive function through its effect on NSCs in different brain areas. The intricate relationship between cholesterol metabolism and NSPCs has introduced a new research perspective in the field of neuroscience.

Cholesterol and ISCs

ISCs are vital for the rapid renewal of the intestinal lining, supporting intestinal function and a healthy environment (Fig. 4A) [71, 72]. They follow a controlled differentiation process that produces various intestinal epithelial lineages, primarily diverging into nutrient-absorbing enterocytes and enteroendocrine cells [73, 74], along with other specialized cells like Paneth, goblet, and tuft cells. Cholesterol plays a dual role in this process: moderate intake boosts ISC proliferation and differentiation into enteroendocrine cells via the Notch pathway in a Drosophila midgut model [75]. Notch signaling is regulated by the interaction between the Delta ligand and the Notch extracellular domain (NECD) [76, 77]. The interaction between NECD modulates the Notch signaling cascade. Upon elevated cholesterol intake, it disturbs the integrity of the Delta-NECD complex, leading to a suppression of Notch signaling, which in turn facilitates the expansion and differentiation of ISCs towards endocrine lineage (Fig. 4B-C) [33, 78]. Excessive cholesterol production disrupts ISC proliferation and differentiation, causing intestinal epithelium hyperproliferation in Lpcat3-deficient mice [79]. Cholesterol metabolism is closely associated with gastrointestinal cancer risk [75]. Although the role of statins in reducing intestinal cancer risk is debated, dietary cholesterol is widely recognized to increase this risk. Lowering intracellular cholesterol might reduce cancer risk by affecting ISCs, but more research and clinical trials are needed. LDL receptor deficiency enhances ISC proliferation and hinders their differentiation into goblet and Paneth cells through the PPAR pathway and fatty acid β-oxidation(FAO), suggesting that both dietary and circulating lipids affect ISC function [80].

Studies have shown that in obese mice on a high-fat diet, the cholesterol transporter Scarb1 is upregulated, boosting the expression of genes like Lyz and Def1a in Paneth cells through the intestinal epithelial cell insulin receptor (IEC-IR) [81]. More research is needed to understand how cholesterol influences the differentiation of ISCs into Paneth cells.

Cholesterol and HSPCs

HSPCs give rise to blood cells of all lineages throughout life [82], and primarily reside in the bone marrow. The delicate balance between self-renewal and differentiation is essential for preserving hematological integrity [83]. With advancing age, HSPCs exhibit enhanced proliferation capacity and become more susceptible to external stimuli, especially inflammation [84]. Under inflammatory conditions, HSPCs deviate from their normal differentiation path, contributing to immune cell production [85], which accelerates chronic inflammatory diseases like atherosclerosis [86, 87]. In normative conditions, quiescence is a fundamental characteristic of HSPCs, preserving long-term regenerative potential [88]. In a mouse arthritis model, cholesterol buildup on HSPC membranes induces IL-3-Rβ and GM-CSF receptor expression, activating Ras signaling and boosting ERK1/2 phosphorylation in vitro, or increases Abca1 and Abcg1 expression in vivo (Fig. 5) [37, 38]. Consequently, this dysregulation fosters abnormal proliferation, migration, and differentiation of HSPCs into pro-inflammatory monocytes and neutrophils, exacerbating atherosclerosis progression [89]. Therefore, strategies aimed at regulating cholesterol metabolism to restore HSPC function and inhibit excessive consumption and inflammatory cell differentiation under inflammatory conditions might hold promise as a therapeutic approach for managing atherosclerotic disorders [6].

Cholesterol efflux regulates the migration, proliferation and differentiation of HSPCs by mediating GM-CSF/IL-3/IL-3-Rβ/Ras/ERK signaling pathway. On the cell membrane or lipid raft of HSPCs, ABCA1, ABCG1 and APOE jointly regulate cholesterol efflux. In hypercholesterolemia model mice, the defect of Abca1/Abcg1/ApoE regulating cholesterol efflux leads to the accumulation of cholesterol in the cell membrane and lipid rafts of HSPCs, which activates the expression of receptors in rafts, then activates the expression of downstream Ras/ERK1/2, and promotes the gene induction of targeting genes for cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration

Cholesterol and CSCs

CSCs are believed to originate all tumor cells in malignant tumors [90], playing a key role in cancer progression and treatment resistance [91, 92]. Thus, targeting CSCs is a research priority [93, 94]. Cholesterol, essential for cell membranes and hormone synthesis [95], supports CSC activities [96]. Clinical studies have shown that statins, which inhibit cholesterol synthesis, can indeed reduce cancer incidence [97]. Elevated cholesterol levels are linked to the maintenance of CSCs, and the activation of key cholesterol biosynthesis target SQLE enhances the expression of stemness markers like c-Myc, KLF4, and SOX2, fueling BCSC proliferation. Suppressing SQLE significantly curtails BCSC expansion [98]. In one of the CSCs, specifically in low-grade glioma initiating cells (BTICs), increased expression of alpha-hydroxyvaleric acid (AHVA) synthase leads to a dose-dependent decrease in Hedgehog (Hh) signaling. This inhibition affects cholesterol synthesis and impedes the growth of glioblastomas [99]. Disruption of Lpcat3 phospholipid balance boosts cholesterol production, increases ISC proliferation, and accelerates tumor growth in Apcmin mice [79]. Elevated LDL cholesterol boosts CRC stem cell regulatory gene expression and migration [100]. Cholesterol synthesis may also activate carcinogenic pathways like Hippo, Hedgehog (Fig. 6), and Wnt/β-catenin, influencing stem cell behavior [40, 41, 79]. The Hippo signaling pathway is a conserved kinase cascade pathway involving tumor suppressor kinases MST1/2, LATS1/2, and adaptor proteins SAV1, MOB1, and MOB2 [101]. Key downstream effectors YAP and TAZ influence various cellular functions [102]. Notably, the MVA pathway activates Rho family small GTPases, which bind to TEAD transcription factors to initiate transcription, leading to BCSC proliferation and speeding up YAP/TAZ nuclear entry (Fig. 6) [27, 42]. However, the precise impact of this pathway on CSCs remains unclear. In summary, although numerous studies link high cholesterol levels to CSCs, the exact impact of cholesterol biosynthesis on CSC traits and drug resistance is not well understood. Clarifying this is crucial for developing targeted cancer treatments.

Cholesterol and BMSCs

BMSCs are bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells that can become chondrocytes, osteoblasts, or adipocytes, essential for bone homeostasis [103]. Osteoporosis mainly arises from an imbalance in the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs [104, 105].

Research shows that physiological cholesterol levels influence BMSC fate by promoting Runx2, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and bone morphogenetic protein-1 (BMP-1) production [34]. However, excessive cholesterol hinders osteogenic differentiation and proliferation due to oxidative stress. Likewise, Ldlr−/− mice with impaired cholesterol uptake exhibit reduced osteogenesis and osteoblastic differentiation [106]. These results suggest that cholesterol-rich diets may reduce bone formation or increase bone remodeling, likely due to cholesterol-inhibiting osteogenic differentiation. Rats on a high cholesterol diet show lower ALP levels and reduced osteogenic differentiation due to increased expression of genes in the Wnt and TGF-β/BMP pathways, such as Wnt5a, β-catenin, and Smad (Fig. 7) [34, 35]. This suggests cholesterol uniquely influences osteogenesis and BMSC differentiation. It is hypothesized that cholesterol synthesis may lead to skeletal diseases by promoting fat cell formation and hindering bone cell differentiation in MSCs due to the regulatory effects between SREBP2 and PPARγ [30]. Strong evidence indicates that high cholesterol levels negatively impact bone health by influencing the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

Conclusion and perspectives

This review summarizes recent findings on how cholesterol metabolism affects various stem cell populations. Cholesterol levels, whether too high or too low, negatively affect stem cell growth, repair, and differentiation, with effects differing by stem cell type and their microenvironments [107]. Despite recognizing cholesterol’s role in stem cell behavior, three key questions persist: how cholesterol metabolism governs differentiation across stem cells, how distinct niches affect this, and how specific molecular pathways in cholesterol metabolism interact with other metabolic pathways to jointly regulate stem cell fate.

ISCs generate diverse epithelial lineages like goblet cells, tuft cells, and enterocytes, but how cholesterol affects their differentiation is largely unknown and needs further study. While research has explored cholesterol’s role in stem cell differentiation, it hasn’t covered all differentiation pathways. HSPCs regulate cholesterol synthesis through the AIBP/Srebp2 pathway for normal function [6]. The specific signals that regulate the AIBP/SREBP2 pathway in normal conditions are still unclear. The disruption of age-related niche cholesterol in BMSCs indicates a need to examine cell-specific mechanisms. Stem cells adapt to different environments by changing cholesterol-related gene expression, but the niche signals that initiate these changes and their tissue-specific variations remain unclear.

Interactions between stem cells, other cells, cytokines, and matrices in the niche significantly influence cholesterol metabolism. Understanding the complex relationship between cholesterol and disease-related molecular processes helps elucidate disease mechanisms and offers new therapeutic insights. The AKT signaling pathway, a crucial signal transduction cascade, regulates cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, and survival, and controls cholesterol biosynthesis via the PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 axis. This review highlights how the AKT signaling pathway influences cholesterol levels, offering neuroprotective and tendon repair benefits by affecting the proliferation and differentiation of DPSCs and TDSCs [36, 60]. Pathological gene defects in neural stem cells can cause abnormal proliferation and differentiation, altering AKT phosphorylation [108]. This raises the possibility that such changes could disrupt cholesterol metabolism, especially in conditions like cancer where there’s a link between cholesterol dysregulation and abnormal stem cell behavior. Understanding these mechanisms could help identify new therapeutic targets and strategies to enhance stem cell differentiation and maintain pluripotency. While insights have suggested therapeutic strategies for corneal epithelial stem cells in treating corneal blindness, the limited research on cholesterol’s role in certain stem cells hinders their clinical application [109, 110].

Future research should investigate the impact of altered cholesterol metabolism in various diseases on stem cell biology. This requires interdisciplinary collaboration and diverse methods, like single-cell spatial metabolomics for mapping cholesterol distribution and organoid-on-a-chip (OOC) models to simulate niche nutrient gradients, to advance the field.
