A multimodal ROS logic-gated therapeutic platform disrupts the vicious cycle of senescence to promote aged bone defect repair
Authors: Xueheng Sun, Shangyu Xie, Ya Fang, Deteng Zhang, Wenhao Wang, Zeyue Sun, Jiaju Lu, Xiaokun Yue, Weize Kong, Dehao Fu, Xiang Xu, Kerong Dai, Jinwu Wang, Jie Wei, Xin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impaired regeneration of aged individuals presents major challenges for repairing bone defects. Within the senescent microenvironment (SME), excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), chronic inflammation, the accumulation of senescent cells and local infection form a self-reinforcing vicious cycle that hinders healing. Here, we developed a multimodal ROS logic-gated therapeutic platform by integrating magnesium- and manganese-doped bismuth oxide with oxygen vacancies nanoparticles (MMBOx) embedded in a rapamycin (Rapa)-loaded, 3D-printed hydrogel scaffold. In vitro study, this platform combines pH-responsive peroxidase, oxidase-like activity and photothermal-enhanced sonodynamic effects, enabling on-demand ROS generation for efficient antibacterial elimination. Simultaneously, MMBOx and the hydrogel scavenge excess ROS, while Rapa promotes cellular autophagy to remove damaged mitochondria, enhances ROS regulation, and delays stem cell senescence. Moreover, MMBOx enhances glutathione (GSH) synthesis and metabolism by modulating the Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby boosting the intracellular GSH pool and increasing ROS tolerance to support a more youthful cellular phenotype. In vivo studies confirm that this platform alleviates infection in the infected skin defect model. Furthermore, it attenuates SME-associated chronic inflammation and cellular senescence, and promotes bone regeneration in the aged rat calvarial defect model. These findings offer a promising strategy for addressing deteriorated SME and enhancing bone repair in the elderly.Keywords: Age-related bone defect, Senescence microenvironment, Antibacterial therapy, ROS homeostasis, Glutathione metabolism

Introduction

The aging global population has led to a marked rise in osteoporosis and the incidence of geriatric fractures, yet bone regeneration in elderly individuals remains significantly impaired [[1], [2], [3]]. Aged individuals often experience delayed fracture healing and persistent bone defects, which are primarily attributed to the accumulation of senescent stem cells, the formation of a senescent microenvironment (SME), and aggravated chronic inflammation [[4], [5], [6]]. In parallel, age-related immune decline leads to increased susceptibility to infection, further complicating clinical outcomes [[7], [8], [9]]. Although tissue engineering offers promising tools for bone defects, current strategies rarely simultaneously address infection control and SME modulation, both of which are critical to restoring bone regeneration in aged patients [4,[10], [11], [12]].

The SME is characterized by stem cell senescence, excessive oxidative stress, and sustained inflammatory responses [[13], [14], [15]]. In this context, excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a central role in driving cell senescence. On the one hand, aged tissues accumulate ROS, disrupting mitochondrial function, impairing autophagy, and amplifying chronic inflammation signaling through the secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. On the other hand, bacterial infections further elevate ROS accumulation and aggravate cellular senescence through atypical DNA damage and inflammatory responses, especially under antibiotic-resistant conditions. In turn, senescent cells further exacerbate both senescence and infection by overexpressing ROS and secreting additional SASP factors. Collectively, the above processes form a vicious loop involving excessive oxidative stress, heightened inflammation response, and accelerated stem cell senescence, which is aggravated by bacterial infection. Therefore, a bidirectional ROS regulation strategy, capable of eradicating infection, restoring redox homeostasis, rejuvenating senescent cells, and restoring bone regenerative capacity, may be key to resolving bone defects in the elderly.

To combat infection and oxidative stress in bone defects, numerous antibacterial and antioxidant biomaterials have been developed, including antibiotic-loaded scaffolds, metal or metal-oxide nanoparticle-modified implants, and photothermal, photodynamic, or sonodynamic systems. In particular, ROS-based sonodynamic and photodynamic therapies show promise in disrupting bacterial biofilms and overcoming antibiotic resistance, while ROS-scavenging hydrogels, nanozymes, and antioxidant-loaded scaffolds alleviate oxidative stress to protect cells [4,[10], [11], [12]]. However, these approaches remain largely unidirectional, emphasizing either bactericidal ROS generation or ROS elimination without considering the delicate balance required for senescent tissues. Meanwhile, senescence-targeting strategies using senolytics, senomorphics, or autophagy activators such as rapamycin (Rapa) have been shown to rejuvenate bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and restore their osteogenic potential [[17], [18], [19]]. Yet, systemic administration suffers from off-target effects and immunosuppression, and only a few studies have incorporated such agents into infection-responsive biomaterial systems. Moreover, existing designs rarely integrate antibacterial capability with precise, bidirectional ROS modulation and the rejuvenation of senescent cells.

Here, we developed a multifunctional therapeutic platform comprising a magnesium (Mg)/manganese (Mn)-doped bismuth oxide with oxygen vacancies nanoparticles (MMBOx) integrated into a Rapa-loaded, ROS-responsive 3D-printed hydrogel scaffold, designed for synergistic antibacterial activity and rejuvenation of aged bone defects (Fig. 1A). This platform integrates four synergistic functions by through a logic-gated therapeutic design to achieve efficient and stage-adaptive ROS regulation for prompting age-related bone defects repair (Fig. 1B): (i) by sensing the infected microenvironment, the platform triggers pH-responsive enhancement of peroxidase (POD)- and oxidase (OXD)- like activities, synergized with external photothermal-enhanced sonodynamic effects, enabling on-demand ROS generation for targeted antibacterial elimination; (ii) after infection control, the antioxidation responses mediated by phenylboronic ester chemistry and MMBOx's intrinsic catalase (CAT)- and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities restore redox homeostasis via keap1-Nrf2 pathway activation; (iii) sustained Rapa release promotes autophagy, restores mitochondrial function, and enhances cellular antioxidant capacity; (iv) these coordinated effects rejuvenate senescent BMSCs, promote osteogenic differentiation, and accelerate new bone formation in aged rat calvarial defect models. Together, these findings establish a microenvironment-targeted strategy to treat age-related infected bone defects, offering a promising potential for bone regeneration in aging individuals.

Results

Structural and electronic characterization of BOx and MMBOx

Bismuth (Bi) oxide with oxygen vacancies (BOx) and MMBOx were synthesized via a hydrothermal method, exhibiting similar morphologies with particle sizes around 50 nm, as observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2A1 and B1). High-resolution diffraction mapping (HRTEM) confirmed disordered lattice defects in BOx and MMBOx attributed to oxygen vacancies and ion doping (Fig. 2A2 and B2) [20]. The diffraction plane of (200) in both materials, with a crystal spacing of 0.285 nm for BOx and 0.283 nm for MMBOx (Fig. 2A3 and B3). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) further demonstrated uniform elemental distribution of Bi and O in both samples, as well as homogeneous dispersion of Mn and Mg in MMBOx (Fig. 2A4 and B4). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of both BOx and MMBOx matched well with the reference pattern for Bi2O3 (JCPDS #47-1057) (Fig. 2C) [21]. Compared to BOx, a slight decrease in crystallinity was observed in MMBOx, likely due to the introduction of additional oxygen defects by Mg and Mn doping [22]. Compared with the standard Bi2O3 diffraction peak at 28.20°, BOx exhibited a slight shift toward a lower angle (28.09°), indicating oxygen vacancy formation (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, the corresponding peak in MMBOx shifted to a higher angle (28.12°), which attributable to lattice contraction caused by the substitution of Bi3+ (108 p.m.) with smaller of Mn2+ (80 p.m.), Mn3+ (66 p.m.), and Mg2+ (65 p.m.), resulting in lattice distortion and enhanced polarity [23]. The total X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further revealed the presence of Bi and O in both BOx and MMBOx, with additional Mn and Mg signals in MMBOx (Supplementary Fig. 2). Quantitative analysis revealed Mn and Mg doping levels of 1.52% and 3.35%, respectively. High-resolution Mn 2p spectra displayed characteristic peaks corresponding to Mn2+ (641.75 eV, 653.89 eV), Mn3+ (643.03 eV, 656.99 eV), and Mn4+ (645.84 eV) (Fig. 2D). The Mg 1s peak at 1304.19 eV indicated divalent Mg2+ species existed (Supplementary Fig. 3A) [24]. Bi 4f spectra indicated Bi3+ states in BOx, with Bi4f5/2 and Bi4f7/2 peaks located at 163.97 eV and 158.95 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3B) [25]. High-resolution O 1s spectra of BOx displayed two primary components at 529.57 eV and 530.87 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (Ol) and oxygen vacancy (Ov), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3C) [26]. After doping with Mn and Mg, the Ov peak intensity increased markedly and shifted to a higher binding energy (531.57 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 3D). The oxygen vacancy concentrations of BOx and MMBOx were calculated to be 49.53% and 63.1%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3C and D) [27]. Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy further validated the presence of oxygen vacancies, revealing a characteristic signal at g = 2.003 in BOx, corresponding to unpaired electrons associated with the generation of Ov (Fig. 2E) [28]. The signal intensity was further enhanced after doping, consistent with the XPS results. Collectively, these results suggest that Mn and Mg doping induce lattice distortion and further introduce oxygen vacancies, thereby disrupting the centrosymmetric structure.

Thermal properties of BOx and MMBOx

Oxygen vacancies enhance light absorption across the UV-NIR range by acting as electron traps with high electron affinity [29]. These vacancies can convert the absorbed light into heat through a localized surface plasmon resonance effect, enabling an efficient photothermal response [30]. UV-vis spectra revealed that both BOx and MMBOx exhibit strong absorbance in the 600-900 nm range, indicating their potential for NIR light harvesting and photothermal conversion (Supplementary Fig. 4). To evaluate their photothermal properties, BOx and MMBOx were irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser, a wavelength chosen for its superior tissue penetration and minimal phototoxicity compared to UV light [31]. Under dry and wet conditions, both materials exhibited a time-dependent temperature increase upon irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). In a dry environment, BOx increased rapidly from 22.5 °C to 104.7 °C within 200 s, while MMBOx reached 109.0 °C over the same period. Under a wet environment, the temperatures increased from 25.2 °C to 47.8 °C for BOx and 57.0 °C for MMBOx. MMBOx further demonstrated stable photothermal performance over five NIR laser on/off cycles, confirming excellent photothermal reversibility and stability (Supplementary Fig. 5C).

Synergetic NIR-US- enhanced ROS generation by BOx and MMBOx

Compared with NIR irradiation alone (BOx + NIR and MMBOx + NIR), US irradiation significantly enhanced ·OH and ·O2− production from BOx and MMBOx (BOx + US and MMBOx + US), as evidenced by decreased absorbance of methylene blue (MB, ·OH indicator) and 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF,·O2− indicator) after 5 min of US exposure, with MMBOx showing superior activity (Supplementary Fig. 6). Importantly, combining US with NIR irradiation (MMBOx + US + NIR) further amplified ROS generation, leading to the lowest MB and DPBF absorbance. The comparable ROS levels observed under MMBOx + US + NIR and MMBOx + US at 55 °C confirmed that the improved ROS yield was driven by the photothermal effect of NIR irradiation. Electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis corroborated these findings, with MMBOx + US + NIR exhibiting the strongest ·OH and ·O2− signal (Fig. 2F and G), demonstrating the superior synergistic ROS generation efficiency of MMBOx under combined US + NIR activation.

Mechanism of synergistic US-NIR-enhanced antibacterial activity of nanoparticles

UV-vis-DRS analysis showed that Mn/Mg doping and oxygen vacancies reduced the bandgap (Eg) of MMBOx from 2.49 to 2.36 eV compared to BOx (Fig. 2H). Valence band (EVB) determined by VB-XPS were 1.86 eV for BOx and 1.64 eV for MMBOx (Fig. 2I and J), while the conduction band edges (ECB) were calculated by the formula ECB = Eg - EVB as −0.63 eV and −0.62 eV, respectively [32]. Mott-Schottky analysis confirmed both materials as N-type semiconductors (Supplementary Fig. 7) [33]. The flat band potentials (EFB) versus Ag/AgCl were measured as −1.02 eV (BOx) and −0.97 eV (MMBOx). Converting to standard hydrogen electrode (NHE) using EFB (NHE) = EFB (Ag/AgCl) + 0.199 V + 0.059 × pH, with pH = 7, the EFB values were determined as −0.40 eV (BOx) and −0.36 eV (MMBOx), approximately 0.1 - 0.3 eV above the calculated conduction band edge, consistent with theoretical expectations [34].

Piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) demonstrated that MMBOx possessed enhanced piezoelectric properties compared to BOx (Supplementary Fig. 8). Both BOx and MMBOx exhibited typical butterfly amplitude curves and 180° phase switching (Fig. 2K and L; Supplementary Fig. 9), confirming their piezoelectricity [35]. Notably, MMBOx displayed a higher piezoelectric coefficient (d33 = 139.5 p.m. V−1) than BOx (59.87 p.m. V−1), attributed to lattice distortion and increased dipole polarization from ionic substitution and oxygen vacancies [[36], [37], [38]]. These modifications suggest that MMBOx can act as an efficient piezoelectric sonosensitizer.

Efficient separation and migration of electron-hole pairs are crucial for the generation of ROS in piezoelectric sonosensitizers. Improved charge migration and separation were further confirmed by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and ultrasonic transient current measurements (Fig. 2M and N), with MMBOx showing higher current density (0.939 μA cm−2) than BOx.

Based on these results, a synergetic US-NIR activation mechanism is proposed (Fig. 2O). The narrow bandgap of MMBOx (2.36 eV) promotes US-induced carrier excitation compared with BOx (2.49 eV). While piezoelectric polarization under ultrasonic force induces band bending, shifting the valence band (1.64 eV) above the oxidation potential of H2O/·OH (+1.99 eV vs. NHE) and maintaining the conduction band (−0.62 eV) more negative than O2/·O2− (−0.33 eV), thereby supporting ·OH and ·O2− formation [20,28,39]. Oxygen vacancies further enhance carrier density and suppress electron-hole recombination [40].

Although MMBOx cannot initiate ROS generation by NIR-induced photodynamic effects due to its band gaps (>1.53 eV) exceeding [41], NIR irradiation serves as a photothermal enhancer. Localized photothermal heating under NIR irradiation accelerates electrons and holes in acoustic sensitizers, amplifying US-triggered ROS generation [42]. Collectively, these structural and electronic modifications enable MMBOx to serve as a US-NIR-responsive catalytic platform for enhanced ROS generation and effective antibacterial therapy.

Mimetic enzyme activities of nanoparticles

Combined oxygen vacancies with the Mn multivalency endowed BOx and MMBOx may exhibit mimetic enzyme activities, including OXD-, POD-, CAT-, and SOD-like activities. Under acidic conditions (pH = 5.5), MMBOx exhibited superior OXD-like enzyme activity compared to BOx, as evidenced by higher 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) absorption (Fig. 3A). US irradiation (MMBOx + US) further enhanced the absorption, due to acoustic-pressure electrocatalysis driving electron migration [43]. The OXD-like enzyme activity was significantly increased under US + NIR irradiation (MMBOx + US + NIR), comparable to thermal activation at 55 °C (MMBOx + US+55 °C), verifying a photothermal effect mediated enhancement. The OXD-like activity of MMBOx showed clear pH-responsiveness, with increasing activity as pH decreased from 7.4 to 4.5 (Fig. 3B), suggesting that MMBOx is enable to intelligently trigger ·O2− production in an acidic environment and is enhanced by US and NIR irradiation. Similarly, MMBOx exhibited enhanced POD-like enzyme activity under acidic conditions in the presence of H2O2-containing (Fig. 3C and D), and the POD-like enzyme activity was significantly amplified by US and NIR irradiation. CAT-like enzyme activities were investigated under pH = 7.4, where MMBOx produced the highest O2 release (ΔO2 = 22.2 mg L−1 in 60 min) (Fig. 3E). This activity remained stable over five catalytic cycles (Fig. 3F). The calculated Km and Vmax of MMBOx were 11.29 mM and 3.36 mg L−1 min−1, respectively (Fig. 3G) [44]. Even at pH = 5.5, MMBOx maintained CAT-like activity (ΔO2 = 8.6 mg L−1), which increased to 10.2 mg L−1 under US irradiation and 11.1 mg L−1 under US + NIR irradiation, similarly to that at MMBOx + US+55 °C (ΔO2 = 11.6 mg L−1) (Fig. 3H).

For SOD-like activities, MMBOx were achieved 86.58% ·O2− clearance (compared to 72.43% for BOx) at 500 μg mL−1 (Fig. 3I), with stable activity over multiple cycles (Fig. 3J) and a calculated activity of 112.5 U mg−1 (Fig. 3K). Total radical scavenging, by using DPPH assay, further confirmed MMBOx's superior radical scavenging ability (Fig. 3L). To elucidate the pH-gated switchable activity of MMBOx, we compared the Km (reflecting substrate affinity for H2O2) of its POD-like and CAT-like activities across a pH range of 4.5 to 7.4. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, at pH values below 6.28, the Km of POD-like activity is lower than that of CAT-like activity, indicating a higher affinity for H2O2 in acidic conditions. This suggests that within the pH range of 4.5-6.28, MMBOx primarily functions as a POD-like nanozyme, which is consistent with the acidic infection microenvironment and facilitates the generation of ROS for antibacterial therapy. Conversely, at pH > 6.28, the Km for CAT-like activity becomes lower than that for POD-like activity, signifying a shift in the predominant catalytic mode. This transition aligns with the microenvironment of tissue repair, where MMBOx effectively scavenges ROS to promote tissue regeneration.

The multi-enzyme activity of MMBOx can exert from Mn valence cycling (Mn4+/Mn3+/Mn2+), oxygen vacancies, and external driving (US and NIR irradiation) (Fig. 3M). Under acidic environment, Mn ions participate in reversible valence transitions that mimic multi-step enzyme-like activities: CAT-like enzyme activity decomposes H2O2 into O2, and the resulting O2 then converted into ·O2− via OXD-like activity; in parallel, POD-like activity enables the direct conversion of H2O2 into ·OH [45]. In addition, US activation and NIR-induced thermal effects accelerate electron transfer and reaction rate [38,46]. Therefore, in an acidic environment and external stimuli (US and NIR), MMBOx can generate abundant ROS through enhanced multi-enzyme activities, while CAT- and SOD-like activities regulate excess ROS during tissue repair through valence cycling and oxygen vacancies [47].

Construction of composite ROS-responsive 3D printable hydrogel scaffold

To translate the catalytic and antibacterial properties of nanoparticles into a functional therapeutic platform, BOx and MMBOx were incorporated into a ROS-responsive 3D-printable hydrogel scaffold. Methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) hydrogel was modified with phenylboronic acid (PBA) to form GelMA-PBA (GP) (Supplementary Fig. 11), introducing ROS-responsive boronate ester linkages [48]. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), a cis-diol-containing polymer, can pre-crosslink with PBA and form a dynamic primary network, while UV-induced photocrosslinking of GelMA established a secondary covalent network (Fig. 3N). This dual-network GelMA-PBA-PVA (GPP) hydrogel provided both structural stability and ROS responsiveness. In addition, BOx and MMBOx loaded into hydrogels are denoted as BOx@GPP and MMBOx@GPP, respectively.

3D printing performance was enhanced by PVA-mediated pre-crosslinking, which improved filament edge smoothness and adaptability during extrusion (Fig. 3O). Rheological analysis revealed a temperature-dependent sol-gel transition by a dynamic shear rheometer, supporting temperature-controlled printability (Fig. 3P; Supplementary Fig. 12). After photocrosslinking, GPP hydrogels demonstrated an enhanced compressive strength of 1.6 MPa in stress-strain analysis, while BOx or MMBOx incorporation had minimal impact on mechanical integrity (Fig. 3Q). In addition, MMBOx and BOx slightly increased swelling ratios and pore size (Fig. 3R). SEM confirmed the porous architecture of GP and GPP scaffolds (Supplementary Fig. 13). Degradation tests show approximately 90% of the scaffolds degraded within 30 days (Supplementary Fig. 14). Notably, ROS-scavenging assays confirmed the superior H2O2-elimination capacity of MMBOx@GPP (Fig. 3S). Overall, this 3D-printed scaffold combines structural integrity, printability, and ROS responsiveness, establishing a versatile therapeutic platform for infection-complicated aged bone defects.

In vitro and in vivo antibacterial effects of therapeutic platform

To evaluate the antibacterial potential of the therapeutic platform, both in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted. In vitro, a low concentration of H2O2 was used to mimic the inflammatory microenvironment of infected tissues. GPP, and MMBOx@GPP exhibited negligible or limited antibacterial ability even upon exposure to US or NIR irradiation (Fig. 4A and C; Supplementary Fig. 15). MMBOx@GPP + H2O2 showed significant increased antibacterial efficacy owing to its intrinsic POD-, and OXD-like activities. Upon US stimulation, MMBOx@GPP + US reduced bacterial viability because of piezoelectric-enhanced sonodynamic performance. Combined H2O2 with US irradiation further decreased survival rates to 24.5% for Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and 20.4% for Escherichia coli (E. coli), attributable to the synergistic effects of enzyme-like activities and sonodynamic performance. Under NIR irradiation, MMBOx@GPP + NIR showed decreased survival rates due to photothermal antibacterial ability [49]. When combined H2O2 with NIR, MMBOx@GPP + H2O2+NIR obviously reduced survival rates caused by photothermal effects and enhanced enzyme-like activities. Notably, the simultaneous application of US and NIR irradiation resulted in the lowest bacterial survival rates (3.3% for S. aureus and 1.3% for E. coli) in MMBOx@GPP + H2O2+US + NIR group, indicating enhanced synergistic antibacterial effect arising from the integration of photothermal enhancement, enzyme-like catalysis, and piezoelectric-enhanced sonodynamic activity. To further evaluate the platform's efficacy against clinically challenging biofilm-associated infections [50,51], its anti-biofilm activity was assessed by crystal violet staining and live/dead imaging. Crystal violet staining results showed that both E. coli and S. aureus formed intact biofilms, and neither US nor NIR could effectively disrupt the biofilms (Supplementary Fig. 16). Furthermore, staining and 590 nm absorbance confirmed that biofilm biomass decreased to about 20% of control in the MMBOx@GPP + H2O2+US + NIR group (Fig. 4B and D; Supplementary Fig. 17). Live/dead imaging revealed reduced bacterial viability and fragmented biofilm structures (Fig. 4E). SEM images confirmed membrane rupture and intracellular leakage in bacteria treated with combined US + NIR irradiation (Fig. 4F; Supplementary Fig. 18). In contrast, partial structural damage and extracellular protein leakage were observed in the MMBOx@GPP + H2O2 group, as well as in both the MMBOx@GPP and MMBOx@GPP + H2O2 groups under individual US or NIR treatment. Furthermore, under combined US and NIR irradiation, most bacteria in both groups exhibited severe surface rupture and leakage of intracellular contents beyond the cell membrane, indicating significantly enhanced antibacterial efficacy through dual-modality activation.

In vivo, using a S. aureus-infected full-thickness skin defect model on the dorsal side of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. MMBOx-loaded hydrogels were implanted and treated with US and (or) NIR to evaluate their ROS-modulated antibacterial and wound-healing effects (Fig. 4G). Compared with the control group, the MMBOx@GPP + US + NIR treatment resulted in an approximately 90% reduction in bacterial burden at the wound site after 3 days, as evidenced by representative bacterial colony images (Fig. 4H) and corresponding quantitative CFU analysis (Supplementary Fig. 19). In addition, wound images showed progressive closure in all treatment groups, with the smallest wound area by day 9 in the MMBOx@GPP + US + NIR group (Fig. 4I and J). HE staining revealed reduced cell infiltration, while Masson staining demonstrated increased collagen deposition in the MMBOx@GPP + US + NIR group (Fig. 4K).

Collectively, the MMBOx@GPP platform achieves effective antibacterial properties by ROS (·OH, ·O2−)-mediated bacterial killing and biofilm disruption, addressing infections in wounds (Fig. 4L). This timely infection control provides a critical foundation for rejuvenating senile bone regenerative capacity. Beyond its therapeutic effects, MMBOx@GPP enables MRI-guided monitoring and visualization of therapeutic activity through positive T1-weighted contrast enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 20). ROS-responsive changes in the valence state of Mn ions enhance T1 signal intensity, thereby enabling visualization of ROS-associated therapeutic responses. At baseline, GPP hydrogels exhibited low T1 signal intensity (∼2060), whereas MMBOx@GPP and MMBOx@GPP + H2O2 hydrogels showed elevated signals of 2313 and 3046, respectively. Upon US stimulation, further increases were observed in both MMBOx@GPP + US and MMBOx@GPP + H2O2+US groups, consistent with ROS-triggered Mn valence state change. A subsequent decline in T1 signal intensity in the MMBOx@GPP + H2O2+US group suggested ROS clearance over time. Together, these results highlight the therapeutic platform's ability: (1) low T1 signals in physiological, non-infected microenvironments; (2) elevated signals in ROS-rich infected sites, indicating the presence of infection and therapeutic demand; and (3) US-triggered enhancement of ROS-associated T1 signals, allowing real-time guidance and modulation of treatment intensity. These findings establish MMBOx@GPP as a promising theranostic implant capable of integrating ROS sensing, therapeutic activation, and regenerative support for precision infection management and bone repair.

Therapeutic platform for ROS regulation and antioxidant enhance in senescent BMSCs

Beyond infection control, chronic oxidative stress remains a major obstacle to tissue regeneration. Therefore, the antioxidant capacity of the platform was evaluated in H2O2-induced senescent BMSCs. H2O2-induced senescence models recapitulate key pathological features of physiological aging, including cell-cycle arrest, aberrant SASP activation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, and have therefore become a reliable in vitro platform for investigating aging mechanisms and anti-aging interventions [11,16,[52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57]]. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining confirmed that 100 μM H2O2 for 3 days reliably induced cellular senescence (Supplementary Fig. 21). To improve cell autophagy and enhance ROS regulation, Rapa was incorporated to prepare MMBOx/Rapa@GPP. All formulations of hydrogels loaded with up to 5 mg mL−1 nanoparticles exhibited good biocompatibility without cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 22 and 23). Furthermore, MMBOx/Rapa@GPP can sustain the release of Rapa for 21 days (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Compared with GPP and MMBOx@GPP, MMBOx/Rapa@GPP significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels in senescent BMSCs (Fig. 5A–C). Flow cytometry confirmed lower 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence, indicating effective ROS scavenging in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group (Fig. 5D–G), while JC-1 assays demonstrated improved mitochondrial polarization (Fig. 5H and I). At the protein level, MMBOx/Rapa@GPP promoted autophagy, as evidenced by reduced LC3-I/LC3-II ratio and decreased p-mTOR expression (Fig. 5J–L), suggesting that enhanced mitochondrial clearance contributed to oxidative stress reduction by improving antioxidant capacity. These results suggest that MMBOx/Rapa@GPP not only scavenges ROS directly but also promotes autophagy-mediated mitochondrial quality control to alleviate oxidative stress in senescent BMSCs.

RNA-seq analysis identifies potential pathways involved in MMBOx-Mediated BMSCs rejuvenation

To further elucidate the mechanism by which MMBOx delays BMSCs senescence, RNA-seq analysis was performed on senescent BMSCs treated in vitro with MMBOx@GPP or GPP. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified 441 upregulated and 195 downregulated genes in the MMBOx@GPP group compared to the GPP group (Supplementary Fig. 27). MMBOx treatment suppressed genes associated with inflammation and osteogenesis inhibition, while upregulating genes involve in senescence delay and mitochondrial function (Fig. 8A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses indicated that MMBOx enhanced pathways associated with cell division, DNA replication, telomere maintenance, oxidative stress response, and macroautophagy, while suppressing inflammatory response and SASP-related processes (Fig. 8B–D; Supplementary Fig. 28 and 29). Notably, glutathione (GSH) metabolism pathways were significantly upregulated in the MMBOx@GPP group compared with the GPP group, including genes involved in GSH biosynthesis, GSSG recycling, and antioxidative function.

Furthermore, biochemical assays showed that MMBOx treatment significantly increased intracellular total GSH content and the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 8H and I; Supplementary Fig. 33A), indicating enhanced redox homeostasis. Morphology improvement of senescent BMSCs further supported the anti-senescent effect (Supplementary Fig. 33B). In the GSH depletion experiment, intracellular GSH levels in the MMBOx@GPP group were significantly elevated compared with the GPP group, whereas buthionine sulfoximine (BSO) treatment markedly reduced GSH levels (Supplementary Fig. 34A). Senescence-associated staining and quantitative analyses (Supplementary Fig. 34B-E) further revealed that the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells and the expression of p21 were significantly decreased in the MMBOx@GPP group compared with the GPP group. These effects were substantially reversed following BSO treatment. Collectively, these results reveal that MMBOx rejuvenates senescent BMSCs in vitro via activating the Keap1-Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and enhancing GSH metabolism, thereby maintaining intracellular redox balance and delaying senescence (Fig. 8J).

Therapeutic platform promote calvarial defect regeneration by modulating the aged microenvironment

Based on the in vitro evidence of senescence attenuation and osteogenic restoration, the therapeutic efficacy of the platform was further evaluated in vivo using a calvarial defect model in aged rats. In 20-months-old male SD rats, local anti-senescent effects were assessed seven days post-implantation. Immunofluorescence staining showed reduced γ-H2A.X expression in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group, suggesting alleviated DNA damage and delayed cellular senescence (Supplementary Fig. 35A and F). The MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group also downregulated the autophagy marker p62, suggesting promoted autophagy activity in damaged local cells (Supplementary Fig. 35B and G). In addition, the expression of SASP, including MMP2, IL-6, and TNF-α, was significantly suppressed, reflecting effective inhibition of SASP factor secretion and attenuation of chronic inflammation within the SME (Supplementary Fig. 35C-E and H-J). Markers of cell cycle arrest were also assessed, with reduced p16 and p21 expression, and enhanced Cyclin D1 levels, further supporting the local anti-senescence effect (Supplementary Fig. 36).

At 12 weeks post-treatment, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis demonstrated significantly enhanced bone regeneration in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group (Fig. 9A). Quantitative micro-CT analysis revealed that bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group were 12.6-fold and 4.0-fold higher, respectively, compared to the Control group (Fig. 9B and C). In contrast, the MMBOx@GPP group showed 9.5-fold and 3.1-fold increases in BV/TV and BMD, respectively, relative to the control group. Histological analyses (H&E and Masson's trichrome staining) confirmed increased new bone and collagen deposition in MMBOx-treated groups, with the most extensive regeneration observed in MMBOx/Rapa@GPP groups (Fig. 9D and E). Moreover, immunofluorescent staining revealed elevated expression of bone formation markers RUNX2 and OPN in newly formed bone in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group (Fig. 9F–I). Angiogenesis is essential for effective bone healing. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the expression levels of vascular markers CD31 and α-SMA were significantly upregulated in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group compared with the control group, indicating enhanced neovascularization within the defect region (Supplementary Fig. 37). In addition to promoting bone formation, the platform modulated local immune microenvironment. CD163/CD68 dual immunofluorescence staining indicated a higher CD163/CD68 ratio in the MMBOx/Rapa@GPP group, suggesting effect in promoting polarization of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 38). Histological evaluation of major organs confirmed good biocompatibility with no evident systemic toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 39).

Discussion

Repairing aged bone defects remains challenging due to the SME, characterized by excessive oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and stem cell senescence. Specifically, ROS accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction lead to sustained SASP secretion and regenerative BMSCs exhaustion. These processes form a self-sustaining vicious cycle, further aggravated by drug-resistant infections.

Numerous tissue-engineering strategies have been developed to promote regeneration of aged bone defects or to treat infection-associated bone defects [4,[58], [59], [60], [61]]. For example, He et al. reported a hydrogel incorporating Rapa-loaded nanomicelles for aged bone regeneration [4]. This system effectively scavenged excessive ROS, improved mitochondrial function, and promoted bone repair in aging conditions. In another representative study, Xu et al. constructed a GSH-modified silk fibroin/methacrylated pectin/MXene-TiO2 composite hydrogel for infected bone regeneration [58]. Under ultrasound stimulation, this hydrogel generated abundant ROS to eradicate bacteria, while cessation of ultrasound enabled the hydrogel and its degradation products to eliminate residual ROS, thereby accelerating bone regeneration. While these studies elegantly address either oxidative stress-associated senescence or infection-associated bacterial burden, they primarily focus on single-stage or stimulus-dependent ROS regulation. To date, few studies have explicitly designed a biomaterial system that integrates multimodal ROS regulation with a therapeutic logic framework to dynamically break the vicious cycle linking infection, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence in aged bone defects.

To address this unmet need, we developed a multimodal ROS logic-gated therapeutic platform by integrating MMBOx nanoparticles and Rapa into a 3D-printed ROS-responsive hydrogel scaffold. Unlike existing approaches, our platform enables pH-adaptive and bidirectional ROS regulation on-demand ROS amplification for antibacterial therapy during infection (pH ∼5.5), followed by ROS scavenging and senescence rejuvenation during the regenerative phase (pH 6.5-6.9) [62,63]. This logic-gated and temporally controlled strategy allows coordinated modulation of redox homeostasis, autophagy, and stem cell function, thereby providing a comprehensive solution for aged bone defect repair.

MMBOx, doped with Mg and Mn and enriched with oxygen vacancy via a hydrothermal synthesis, enabled dual functionality: catalytic ROS generation under US and NIR stimulation and intrinsic antioxidant activity via SOD- and CAT-like effects. Mg, an essential trace element in the human body, plays a critical role in bone metabolism and osteogenesis. Beyond its well-established osteogenic effects, emerging evidence has demonstrated that Mg also exhibits outstanding anti-aging properties [10,55]. Mn possesses multiple ionic valence states (Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+), which enable versatile redox activity and confer enzyme-mimetic functions. In addition, Mn-based materials exhibit excellent T1-weighted MRI contrast capability, providing opportunities for noninvasive imaging and therapeutic guidance [64,65]. From a materials engineering perspective, ion-doping is a simple and advantageous modification strategy to tailor crystal structure, chemistry, and composition of materials [66,67]. In this study, a straightforward Mg-Mn dual-doping strategy was employed to synergistically enhance the asymmetry of BOx, increase Ov concentration, and reduce the bandgap, thereby improving the piezoelectric response, sonodynamic performance, enzyme-like activities, and photothermal conversion efficiency.

Several studies have demonstrated that functional hydrogels capable of scavenging excessive ROS or modulating inflammatory responses can effectively promote tissue regeneration [[68], [69], [70]]. For example, Qi et al. developed a hydrogel that disrupts the ROS-inflammation cycle through ROS scavenging, oxygen generation, and near-infrared-triggered hyperthermia, thereby accelerating diabetic wound healing. Despite these advances, most existing hydrogel systems focus primarily on a single regulatory dimension and are mainly applied to non-aging models. Our system does not merely target inflammation or oxidative stress, but effectively breaks the vicious cycle of senescence by reprogramming the SME. The platform constructed from phenylboronic ester-modified GelMA and PVA, formed a dual-network architecture that provided ROS-responsive degradation, sustained Rapa release, excellent printability and mechanical compatibility. This rational design allowed the therapeutic platform to dynamically shift between bactericidal and regenerative phases, offering precise spatiotemporal control of ROS levels and supporting both infection resolution and bone regeneration. This functional integration enables the platform to respond adaptively to the dynamic microenvironment of infected aged bone defects.

Postoperative infection impedes bone defect repair in aged individuals, as it aggravates SME deterioration and accelerates BMSCs senescence. Our platform demonstrates pH-responsive POD-like activity and photothermal-enhanced sonodynamic, leveraging the acidic microenvironment during infection to enhance ROS-mediated antibacterial effects. As infection resolves, the platform transitions to an antioxidant state, protecting host cells from excessive ROS. This spatiotemporal ROS gating, together with ROS-responsive T1 contrast enhancement, supports both therapeutic efficacy and real-time theranostic monitoring. In vitro and in vivo studies confirmed the platform's efficacy in biofilm eradication, bacterial inhibition, and skin tissue repair in infected models of rat.

ROS dysregulation is a defining feature of the SME that impairs stem cell proliferation and differentiation [71]. Our results demonstrated that MMBOx exhibits robust SOD- and CAT-like activity, and when combined with the ROS-responsive hydrogel, effectively scavenges intra- and extracellular ROS. This restores redox balance in senescent BMSCs, reduces SA-β-gal activity, and suppresses SASP expression. SASP, a proinflammatory secretome released by senescent cells, amplifies local inflammation and propagates senescence to neighboring cells, further deteriorating the SME [15,16]. In addition, chronic oxidative stress further reinforces cell-cycle arrest in BMSCs via telomere shortening, DNA damage response, and CDKN2A derepression. Our platform alleviated this arrest, as evidenced by increased S phase in cell cycle, downregulation of p16, p21 and p53. Beyond redox modulation, mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy are central to age-related senescence [72]. Rapa, an mTOR inhibitor, is known to promote autophagy and mitochondrial function [16]. In our study, sustained Rapa release from the platform activated autophagy of BMSCs and improved mitochondrial membrane potential. MMBOx and Rapa exhibit synergistic anti-aging effects through coordinated regulation of redox homeostasis and autophagy. MMBOx initially scavenges excess ROS, thereby alleviating oxidative stress. This is followed by Rapa-mediated clearance of damaged, ROS-generating mitochondria, which restores mitochondrial regulatory of ROS and contributes to long-term oxidative homeostasis [4,16]. In addition, MMBOx alleviates ROS-induced damage, preserves lysosomal membrane integrity, and facilitates autophagosome-lysosome fusion, thereby maintaining intact autophagic flux and serving as a prerequisite for Rapa to exert its autophagy-inducing effects [73].

To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, RNA-seq revealed that MMBOx-treated senescent BMSCs upregulated genes involved in cell division, DNA replication, telomere maintenance, and antioxidant defense. Notably, pathways related to GSH metabolism were significantly enriched, including genes regulating GSH biosynthesis, utilization, and redox cycling. This was accompanied by increased anti-oxidant capacity by activating Nrf2 pathway and elevated intracellular GSH levels, which collectively promote redox homeostasis and delay cellular senescence. Our findings are consistent with the previous studies in aging stem cells, where Nrf2 activity and high GSH content support a youthful phenotype through enhanced ROS resilience, autophagy, and cell cycle progression [74,75]. This provides new guidance for biomaterial-based modulation of the aging target GSH.

Despite the promising outcomes demonstrated in this study, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, this study was primarily conducted in aged rat models. Future investigations necessitate the use of large animal models for aging. Second, while the therapeutic platform exhibited good biosafety in short-term studies, its long-term metabolic clearance and systemic effects require further evaluation. Finally, although GSH metabolism emerged as a key anti-senescence mechanism, its precise role in therapeutic value warrants deeper mechanistic investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this logic-gated ROS-modulating scaffold integrates antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-senescence effects to promote aged bone defects repair. By reprogramming the SME through targeted modulation of redox balance, autophagy, and GSH metabolism, this platform offers a comprehensive and translationally relevant strategy for regenerating aged bone tissue.

Synthesis of BOx and MMBOx

BOx and MMBOx were synthesized using a hydrothermal method. Briefly, 1.40 g of NaBiO3, 1.12 g of NaOH, 0.02 g of MgCl2, and 0.04 g of MnCl2·H2O were dispersed in 30 mL of deionized water. The suspension was stirred for 2 h and then transferred into an autoclave and hydrothermally reacted at 180 °C for 5 h. After cooling, the product was washed three times with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol via centrifugation and subsequently dried for 12 h. To obtain small-sized MMBOx, the product was redispersed in a deionized water-ethanol mixture and ultrasonicated for 4 h. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm to remove larger particles, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at 8000 rpm to collect the precipitate. The precipitate was dried to obtain the final MMBOx. The control BOx was synthesized using the same procedure without the addition of MgCl2 and MnCl2·H2O.

ROS generation under US irradiation

To evaluate ROS generation, 300 μg mL−1 of BOx and MMBOx were dispersed in MB (10 mg L−1) or DPBF (10 mg L−1), then stirred in the dark to ensure adsorption equilibrium, and irradiated by US irradiation (WED-100, Weld, China) for 5 min. The supernatant was obtained by centrifugation, and the supernatant absorbance changes were obtained by UV spectrophotometer (Lambda 950, PerkinElmer, USA). The signal peaks of ·OH (aqueous system) and ·O2− (ethanol system) were obtained through ESR by using DMPO as the trapping agent.

Photothermal and sonodynamic effects

A laser thermal imager (LSR808H-4W, Yuanming, China) was used to monitor the photothermal behavior of BOx and MMBOx (3 mg mL−1). Photothermal performance was evaluated using an infrared thermal imager under 808 nm laser irradiation at 1.4 W cm−2. Photothermal-enhanced sonodynamic performance was assessed by simultaneous laser and US irradiation, with a 55 °C water bath group as a thermal control.

Enzyme-like activities

For US group, the parameter of US treatment is 1.5 W cm−2 for 3 min. For NIR group, NIR treatment was performed at the same time as US irradiation. For the water bath group, under 55 °C water bath conditions were performed.

POD-like enzyme activity was assayed by monitoring the oxidation of TMB in the presence of 30 mM H2O2. 300 μg mL−1 of BOx and MMBOx were dispersed in 1.2 mM of TMB solution under nitrogen to eliminate dissolved oxygen. Reactions were conducted under varying pH conditions, and supernatants were collected by centrifugation for UV spectrophotometric analysis. OXD-like activity was performed as the same protocol as POD-like activity, but excluded H2O2 and nitrogen protection.

CAT-like activity was performed by measuring the dissolved oxygen concentration with the addition of 10 mM H2O2 at different time using a dissolved oxygen meter (JPB-607A, Yidian, China). Preparation of phenylboronic acid-modified GelMA

Briefly, 2 mmol of DMTMM and 2 mmol of 4-CPBA were separately dissolved in 6 mL of water and 9 mL of DMF. The DMTMM solution was then slowly added into the 4-CPBA solution and activated for 30 min. The 4-CPBA solution was then added dropwise to 2% (w/v) GelMA solution, and the pH was adjusted to 4.5 with hydrochloric acid. The reaction was maintained at 25 °C for 24 h, followed by dialysis for 3 days and lyophilization to obtain GP. Hydrogen nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra of GP were acquired using a 400 MHz spectrometer (AVANCE AV400 MHz, BRUKER, Switzerland).

Bioinks characteristics and 3D bioprinting

Bioink Preparation: The bioink was formulated in PBS containing 0.25% (w/v) LAP, 7.5% (w/v) GelMA-PBA, 2.5% (w/v) PVA, and 5 mg mL−1 BOx or MMBOx.

Rheological Characterization: A dynamic shear rheometer (Discovery HR 20, TA Instruments, USA) was used to characterize the rheological properties. The mixed bioink was loaded between parallel plates (1 mm gap) at 25 °C. Viscosity was recorded under steady shear (1 s−1) as temperature decreased from 25 °C to 5 °C (1 °C min−1). Simultaneously, a cooling oscillatory scan (1 Hz, 0.1% strain) was performed to monitor storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) versus temperature.

Mechanical Testing: Cylindrical samples (Φ10 mm × 5 mm) were prepared by injecting bioink into molds and photocuring. Compression tests were performed using a universal testing machine (WDW-5, Bairoe, China; compression rate: 1 mm min−1), and yield strength was determined from the stress-strain curve at the elastic limit.

Swelling Behavior: Freeze-dried cylindrical samples were weighed (W0), then immersed in PBS at 37 °C. At set time points, samples were blotted and weighed (Wx). Swelling ratio (%) = (Wx - W0)/W0 × 100%.

Biodegradability Testing: Similarly, freeze-dried cylindrical samples were weighed (Wdry) and incubated in PBS at 37 °C. At scheduled intervals, samples were freeze-dried and weighed (Wt). Degradation rate (%) = (Wdry - Wt)/Wdry × 100%.

H2O2 Scavenging Assay: Bioink samples (500 μL) were incubated with 3 mM H2O2 at 37 °C with shaking (180 rpm). At set time points, supernatants were collected, and H2O2 content was measured at 560 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite M200 Pro, Tecan, Switzerland), following the hydrogen peroxide detection kit manufacturer's instructions.

3D Bioprinting: The prepared bioink was loaded into a sterile printer cartridge (Bio-Architect; Regenovo, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) and printed through a 300 μm nozzle at 15-20 °C, under an extrusion pressure of 0.05-0.15 MPa. The receiving platform temperature was controlled at 8-12 °C. Printing was conducted according to the designed printing path. 3D-printed scaffolds were photocrosslinked at 405 nm blue light (3 W) for 180 s. Three types of scaffolds were fabricated: GPP: GelMA-PBA/PVA (7.5% GP and 2.5% PVA, w/v) hydrogel; BOx@GPP: GPP hydrogel supplemented with BOx (5 mg mL−1); MMBOx@GPP: GPP hydrogel supplemented with MMBOx (5 mg mL−1). The macro-surface characteristics of different scaffolds were observed using SEM (Sigma 300, Zeiss, German).

Drug Loading and Release: Hydrogels containing 20 μM Rapa were prepared using the above-described method. Then, hydrogels were incubated in pH = 7.4 acetic acid ammonium acetate buffer at 37 °C. At designated time points, supernatants were collected, and drug concentrations were quantified based on a standard Rapa curve tested by HPLC (1260/UV, Agilent, China).

In vitro antibacterial performance

S. aureus (ATCC 29213) and E. coli (ATCC 25922) were used as model Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively. S. aureus and E. coli were purchased from American type culture collection.

For planktonic bacteria, 3D-printed hydrogel scaffolds were immersed in bacterial suspension containing 100 μM H2O2, followed by external field interventions (US: 1.5 W cm−2, 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 10 min; NIR: 808 nm, 1.4 W cm−2, 10 min). Following the intervention, the treated bacterial suspension samples (20 μL) were spotted onto pre-coated agar plates and incubated in a constant temperature shaking incubator for 24 h, the samples were photographed, and colonies counted using ImageJ software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA).

For bacterial biofilms analysis, bacterial suspensions (S. aureus or E. coli) were inoculated into LB medium and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h to form mature biofilms, followed by washing with PBS and scaffold treatment under external field (US, NIR) intervention. Biofilms were assessed via crystal violet staining and live/dead staining. After incubating at 37 °C for 12 h, biofilms were stained with 200 μL of 50% crystal violet for 5 min, washed thrice with PBS, dried, and imaged. Crystal violet was then eluted with anhydrous ethanol, and absorbance was measured to quantify biofilm disruption. For morphological analysis, treated bacteria were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated via graded ethanol, and observed using SEM (Zeiss).

In vivo infection model establishment and antibacterial evaluation

Healthy male SD rats (8 weeks) were anaesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% sodium pentobarbital. After dorsal skin preparation, 10 mm × 10 mm full-thickness wounds on the rat's back were created and inoculated with 106 CFU of S. aureus. After 12 h, hydrogel platforms were implanted into the full-thickness skin defect sites of the rats, followed by external field interventions. PBS-treated rats served as controls. On day 3, rats were euthanized, and fresh skin tissue was homogenized and sonicated in PBS to prepare bacterial suspensions for plate counting. For other rats, wound healing was monitored and photographed every 2 days, with wound area quantified using ImageJ software (Media Cybernetics). The rats were euthanized on the 9th day. For histological evaluation, skin samples were fixed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E and Masson trichrome using standard protocols. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of East China University of Science and Technology (ECUST-2025-136).

BMSCs isolation, culture and in vitro experiments

BMSCs were isolated from femurs and tibias of 4-week-old male SD rats. Briefly, after humane euthanasia by cervical dislocation, femurs and tibias were harvested, and surrounding tissues were carefully removed under sterile conditions. Incise both ends of the epiphysis, rinse the bone marrow cavity three times with α-MEM medium to collect bone marrow cells. Cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS (v/v) and 100 U mL−1 penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 3 days, non-adherent cells were removed, and adherent cells were expanded. Passage 3 BMSCs were used for further experiments.

To evaluate cytocompatibility, BMSCs were cultured with various concentrations of MMBOx or different composite hydrogels. Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 on days 1, 4, and 7 according to the manufacturer's protocol. The absorbance of each group was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite M200 Pro, Tecan, Switzerland). Additionally, after 3 days of culture, cells were stained using calcein-AM and PI. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica, STELLARIS 5, Germany) to evaluate cell viability.

SA-β-gal activity was detected using a β-galactosidase staining kit after 100 μM H2O2 stimulation for 3 days to induce cellular senescence. After treatment, cells were fixed and washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the SA-β-gal staining working solution was added, and cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. Positive staining (blue) was observed under an optical microscope.

To assess the intracellular oxidative stress status, after 3 days of culture with 100 μM H2O2, ROS were detected using 10 μM DCFH-DA and 5 μM DHE staining solutions. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 5 min, and fluorescent images were acquired using CLSM (Leica). Additionally, adherent BMSCs were harvested, incubated with DCFH-DA or DHE at 37 °C for 20 min, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (LSRFortessa, BD Biosciences, USA) to quantify intracellular ROS and superoxide anion levels.

Intracellular mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed by JC-1 staining. BMSCs were incubated with JC-1 dye at 37 °C for 20 min following the manufacturer's instructions, cells were washed and immediately analyzed by flow cytometer. Red (aggregates) and green (monomers) fluorescence signals were visualized by flow cytometer (LSRFortessa). The mitochondrial membrane potential of each sample was evaluated by calculating the red/green fluorescence intensity ratio.

To further explore the underlying protective mechanisms of MMBOx, protein expression levels of key autophagy-related and oxidative stress-related markers were examined. After 3 days of treatment, BMSCs were lysed using RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit, and equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated on 4-20% gradient SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at 25 °C, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig; 1:50,000), anti-LC3 (Proteintech, 14600-1-AP; 1:3000), anti-mTOR (Proteintech, 66888-1-Ig; 1:25,000), and anti-phospho-mTOR (Proteintech, 67778-1-Ig; 1:6000). After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech: rabbit SA00001-4, 1:5000; mouse SA00001-1, 1:5000) for 1 h at 25 °C. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system (XF101, Epizyme, China), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (Media Cybernetics) with GAPDH as the loading control.

The anti-senescence effects of the materials were assessed by evaluating cellular senescence markers. After 3 days of culture, quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to quantify mRNA expression of cell cycle inhibitors (p16, p21, p53) and SASP factors (Ccl2, Mmp2, Il-6, Tnf-α). For qRT-PCR, total RNA was extracted from BMSCs using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions and quantified with a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 μg of total RNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit. Amplification reactions were conducted with SYBR Greenon a Real-Time PCR Detection System (CFX Connect, Bio-Rad, USA). The 2−ΔΔCt method was employed for relative quantification, with Gapdh serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Protein expression of TNF-α was also detected by western blotting, while p53 (Affinity, AF0877; 1:1000) expression was assessed using immunofluorescence staining and CLSM (Leica) imaging.

The anti-senescence effects of the materials were assessed by SA-β-gal staining after 100 μM H2O2 stimulation for 3 days to induce cellular senescence. The therapy effects of different groups were assessed by positive staining (blue). Furthermore, cell cycle distribution analysis was conducted by propidium iodide staining and flow cytometry after ethanol fixation (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coutler, USA).

To assess the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs, BMSCs were co-cultured with therapeutic hydrogel platform in osteogenic induction medium (α-MEM, 10% FBS, 50 μg mL−1 ascorbic acid, 100 nM dexamethasone, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate) containing 100 μM H2O2. ALP activity was measured at day 7 using a commercial kit, while mineralization was assessed by ARS staining on day 21. Osteogenic gene expressions (Alp, Runx2, Opn and Osx) were quantified at day 10 using qRT-PCR. Protein expression of RUNX2 (Proteintech, 20700-1-AP; 1:500) and OPN (Abcam, ab63856; 1:500) were detected by immunofluorescence staining on day 14, and fluorescence intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (Media Cybernetics).

Mechanism exploration and validation

To further investigate potential molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-aging and osteogenic effects of MMBOx, RNA sequencing was performed. After BMSCs co-cultured with GPP or MMBOx@GPP in α-MEM medium containing 100 μM H2O2 for 3 days, total RNA was extracted. RNA purification, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). RNA quality and integrity were assessed prior to quantification and library construction. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150 mode). Reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression quantification was performed with HTSeq. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (p.value < 0.05&|log2FoldChange|>1). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using ClusterProfiler. PPI networks were constructed using the STRING database.

Based on RNA-seq results, antioxidant signaling pathways, particularly the Nrf2 pathway and glutathione metabolism, were selected for further validation. qRT-PCR was performed to evaluate the expression of Nrf2 pathway-related genes (Nrf2, Hmox1, Nqo1, and Srxn1) and glutathione metabolism-associated genes (Gclc, Gstp1).

Western blotting was employed to evaluate protein expression of key targets, including Keap1 (Affinity, AF5266; 1:1000), Nrf2 (Proteintech, 16396-1-AP; 1:1000), Nqo1 (Abcam, ab80588; 1:10,000), Gclc (Proteintech, 12601-1-AP; 1:25000) and GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig; 1:50,000).

To measure intracellular GSH levels, BMSCs were collected by trypsinization and resuspended in 5 μM ThiolTracker Violet, and incubated in a 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence intensity was detected using flow cytometry (LSRFortessa, AmCyan channel) to quantify intracellular GSH content.

For further assessment of intracellular redox status, total Glutathione (GSH + GSSG) was extracted BMSCs after 3 days of cell culture. Total Glutathione and GSSG contents were measured using a GSH/GSSG Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. GSH content was calculated using the following formula: GSH = Total Glutathione-GSSG × 2. This quantification provided a direct evaluation of the intracellular GSH/GSSG ratio, reflecting the cellular antioxidant capacity.

To directly evaluate whether MMBOx-induced enhancement of GSH contributes to the alleviation of cellular senescence, BMSCs were treated with the GSH inhibitor BSO to deplete intracellular GSH levels. Intracellular GSH levels following GPP, MMBOx@GPP, and MMBOx@GPP + BSO treatment were quantified, as described above. Senescence-associated markers were further assessed by SA-β-gal staining and immunofluorescence staining for p21 following the previously described protocols.

In vivo evaluation of therapeutic effect and mechanism

Animal Model and Scaffold Implantation: A critical-sized calvarial defect model was established in naturally aged (20-months-old) male SD rats to simulate age-related impaired bone regeneration. Rats were anaesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium. After skin disinfection with povidone iodine, bilateral circular defects (5 mm in diameter) were created symmetrically on both sides of the sagittal suture using a trephine drill. The defects were treated with 3D-printed scaffolds, including GPP, MMBOx@GPP, or MMBOx/Rapa@GPP, while untreated defects served as the control group.

Early-Stage Mechanistic Analysis: At one-week post-implantation, calvarial samples were harvested to evaluate local senescence and oxidative stress status. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with EDTA, dehydrated in ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Tissue sections were stained for γ-H2A.X (Servicebio, GB111841, 1:500), p62 (Affinity, AF5384, 1:500), p16 (Santa Cruz, sc-81156, 1:200), p21 (Affinity, AF6290, 1:300), Cyclin D1 (Proteintech, 26939-1-AP, 1:200), MMP-2 (Servicebio, GB11130, 1:500), IL-6 (Servicebio, GB11117, 1:200), and TNF-α (Servicebio, GB115702, 1:200) following standard protocols. Fluorescent images were acquired using a panoramic digital slide scanner (VS200, Olympus, Japan).

Micro-CT Analysis: At 12 weeks post-surgery, rats were euthanized, and calvarial specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Bone regeneration was evaluated using a micro-CT (PerkinElmer Quantum GX2, Japan). Three-dimensional reconstructions of defect areas were generated. BV/TV and BMD were quantified using analysis software.

Histological and Immunofluorescence Staining: After micro-CT scanning, samples were decalcified, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. H&E and Masson's trichrome staining were performed to evaluate tissue morphology and collagen deposition. Immunofluorescence staining was conducted with antibodies against OPN (Abcam, ab63856, 1:100), RUNX2 (Proteintech, 20700-1-AP, 1:500), CD68 (Servicebio, GB113109, 1:100), CD163 (Servicebio, GB113751, 1:500), CD31 (Servicebio, GB113151, 1:100), and α-SMA (Servicebio, GB121364, 1:200) to assess osteogenesis, macrophage polarization, and angiogenesis. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) of elderly rats euthanized 12 weeks after scaffold implantation were also harvested for H&E staining to assess long-term biosafety. All sections were imaged using a panoramic digital slide scanner (VS200, Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± SD; Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test and t-test. Statistical significance was defined as ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.
