Association Between Junk Food Consumption and Behavioral Outcomes in 10–15-year-old Children: A Cross-sectional Questionnaire-based Study
Authors: Baba S Markapurapu, Veena Balakrishnan, Satyam Martha, Sri R Maganti, K Haritha, Harshitha Yaramala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDietary habits and food consumption patterns have long been a focus of interest for both researchers and healthcare professionals. In recent years, growing awareness of the role of nutrition in overall well-being, along with a noticeable rise in diet-related health problems, has intensified this focus. Furthermore, increased concern is surfacing regarding the effect of junk food has on the behavior patterns among children; however, there is limited evidence. The present questionnaire study aims to investigate the association between junk food consumption and behavioral issues among 10–15-year-old children attending schools in and around Rajanagaram, Andhra Pradesh.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1,500 students using a validated dietary questionnaire and the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) adolescent self-report version. Junk food consumption (based on IAP guidelines) and behavioral categories were evaluated from the reported questionnaires, and the association between the two variables was analyzed statistically using Chi-square and Pearson correlation tests.ResultsModerate junk food consumption was reported by 71.3% of children, with 11.8% reporting severe intake. SDQ revealed 81.6% had abnormal behavior scores, predominantly hyperactivity (54.78%) and conduct problems (67.02%). Although Pearson correlation was statistically nonsignificant (p = 0.963), a trend of increased behavioral issues with higher junk food intake was observed.ConclusionA high prevalence of behavioral disturbances was seen among children with moderate to severe junk food consumption. Introduction

Over the last two decades, the global food environment has undergone a substantial transformation, largely driven by the rapid expansion of the fast-food industry and the easy availability of ultra-processed food products. In India, this dietary shift is especially evident among school-aged children, who frequently choose packaged snacks, sugar-sweetened drinks, and ready-to-eat meals due to their affordability, accessibility, and sensory appeal.1 While these trends have already been linked to rising rates of childhood obesity and metabolic disorders, growing evidence also indicates potential effects on emotional and behavioral health.2

According to a recent survey conducted by the Centre for Science and Environment (CSE), over 90% of Indian children consume packaged food, and nearly 68% regularly drink sugary beverages more than once a week.3 Despite this widespread exposure, there is only limited literature that specifically investigates the psychological or behavioral consequences of such eating patterns in children in India.

Current research suggests a possible association between the frequent intake of high-fat, high-sugar, and high-salt (HFSS) foods and an increased risk of hyperactivity, aggression, irritability, and reduced concentration.4,5 The Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) defines “junk food” as including items such as chips, biscuits, fried street foods, sugar-loaded drinks, and fast foods such as pizza and burgers. These foods often lack essential nutrients such as omega-3 fatty acids, iron, and B vitamins, all of which are important for optimal neurological development and mood regulation.6

The school environment plays a critical role in shaping children's eating habits, especially in government schools where nutritional awareness, food regulation, and access to healthy alternatives may be limited. Children from economically disadvantaged families are particularly susceptible due to fewer resources, limited parental guidance, and the easy availability of unhealthy food vendors near schools.7

So, the present study was undertaken to investigate the potential link between junk food consumption and behavioral outcomes in 10–15-year-old students in schools in and around Rajanagaram, Andhra Pradesh. By utilizing standardized tools for dietary and behavioral evaluation, this research aims to contribute to evidence-based strategies that promote healthier food habits and improved mental well-being among children.

Methods

The cross-sectional observational research was carried out in January 2025 at Rajanagaram, Andhra Pradesh. Prior to the commencement of the research, formal approval was acquired from the Institutional Ethical Committee, Lenora Institute of Dental Sciences, Rajanagaram. Informed consent from the parents of the children involved was also acquired.

The age group of 10–15 years was specifically chosen because it represents a critical developmental period, late childhood to early adolescence, during which children undergo significant cognitive, emotional, and behavioral changes. This stage is also when independent food choices begin to emerge, often influenced by peer pressure, media exposure, and school environment. Studies have shown that children in this age group are more likely to consume high-fat, high-sugar foods such as packaged snacks, sugary beverages, and fast foods due to increased autonomy and spending power.8 Moreover, behavioral problems such as hyperactivity, emotional instability, and conduct disorders often become more noticeable and measurable during this transitional phase, making it an ideal period to assess the influence of diet on psychological health.9 The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), used in this study, is also validated for this age range, ensuring age-appropriate and reliable self-reporting of behavior.10

Based on a pilot study conducted among 50 children, where approximately 50% showed behavioral disturbances linked to junk food consumption, a minimum sample size of 384 was calculated using standard statistical methods (n = z2pq/d2) for cross-sectional studies. To enhance reliability, allow age-wise subgroup analysis, and account for potential non-responses, the sample size was increased to 1,500 schoolchildren, ensuring stronger statistical power and broader representativeness of the target population.

Each item is scored on a three-point Likert scale, with cumulative domain scores interpreted as normal, borderline, or abnormal based on established age-specific cut-off points. The categorization used in this study adhered to norms validated for Indian school-aged children.13,14

To ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness, the SDQ was administered in both English and Telugu. The translation process followed WHO guidelines for linguistic validation, involving forward translation, back-translation, and expert review.15 This ensured that the tool retained its conceptual and psychometric integrity when used in a regional language setting.

The SDQ is recognized globally and in India for its brevity, ease of use, and effectiveness in screening for common emotional and behavioral problems in adolescents. It has been successfully used in both community-based and school-based studies and demonstrates good reliability and validity across diverse populations.10

Data Collection Procedure

A cluster sampling method was adopted for this study due to logistical feasibility and the school-based setting. Schools in and around Rajanagaram were treated as clusters. From the list of eligible schools, a predetermined number of schools were randomly selected. Within each selected school, entire classes (grades 5–10) were included, and all students who met the inclusion criteria and provided assent were invited to participate. This method was chosen to facilitate efficient data collection across multiple students at once, while also maintaining diversity in age and grade levels. On scheduled dates, the questionnaires were distributed to students during school hours under the supervision of trained field investigators. Instructions were explained verbally in the local language. Students completed the forms independently, without influence from teachers or peers.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 23.0. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequencies) were used for demographic data. Associations between junk food consumption levels and behavioral outcomes were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Pearson correlation was used to evaluate the relationship between total food scores and behavior scores. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In the Indian school context, the shift from traditional home-cooked meals to highly processed, convenience-based food has become increasingly apparent in recent years. With rapid urbanization and aggressive marketing by fast food brands, school-going children are now frequently exposed to and are consuming junk food such as packaged snacks, carbonated drinks, and street foods prepared with low nutritional value. This dietary transition has raised serious concerns, particularly regarding its potential influence on children's behavior, emotional well-being, and academic performance.3,7 This study is an attempt to highlight the importance of proper nutrition in shaping not only child's physical health but also their emotional and intellectual development. The paucity of such studies in the Indian scenario, despite the excessive consumption of street food, accentuates the relevance of the present study.

The study design included the use of SDQ for behavior assessment. The SDQ is a brief behavioral screening tool designed to assess emotional and behavioral problems in children and adolescents.13

The SDQ consists of 25 items grouped into five key domains: hyperactivity, conduct problems, peer relationship problems, emotional symptoms, and prosocial behavior. By evaluating these areas, the questionnaire provides a balanced view of a child's psychological well-being. Versions of the SDQ are available for completion by parents, teachers, and self-report (for older children), making it a versatile tool in multiple settings, including schools, clinics, and research studies, of which the self-reporting, adolescent version was used in the present study.13,14

SDQ is widely used by mental health professionals, educators, and researchers due to its simplicity, effectiveness, and strong psychometric properties.

Its growing global use reflects its adaptability across cultures and languages, contributing to better understanding and support for children's mental health worldwide. Food assessment, on the other hand, was done using a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire typically includes questions about meal frequency, portion sizes, food group consumption, snacking habits, and factors influencing food choices such as culture, availability, and personal preferences. By systematically gathering information about eating habits, the questionnaire serves as an important resource. Assessing eating habits as well as junk food consumption among the children, each question had possible options from which the children selected the suitable one.11,12

The findings of this study underline a significant concern regarding the dietary habits of school-going children and their potential implications for behavior. The data revealed that a majority of the participants consumed moderate to high levels of junk food, aligning with national surveys such as that of the CSE, which indicate that over 90% of children regularly consume packaged and sugar-sweetened foods.3

Notably, 71.3% of children fell into the moderate junk food consumption category, and 11.8% were identified with severe consumption patterns. Such dietary behaviors, characterized by frequent intake of instant noodles, fried snacks, sugary drinks, and processed foods, are likely influenced by the accessibility, taste preferences, and marketing strategies targeted at younger demographics.

In terms of behavioral outcomes, the SDQ showed that over 81% of children exhibited abnormal behavior, while 11.9% fell in the borderline category. The report by Child Guidance Center, NICPPD (2003–2007),16 observed similar findings about the rising incidence of behavioral issues such as aggression and depression among school-going children. The most affected domains according to our study included hyperactivity (54.78%), conduct disorders (67.02%), and peer relationship problems (35.54%). This result is consistent with the prevalence study by Datta et al.,17 which showed that conduct problems were the most common issue with a prevalence of 48.70%, followed by peer relationship problems (44.60%) among children living under varying parental care conditions in India. In addition, broader national and international literature confirms that the prevalence of mental health and behavioral disorders in school-aged children ranges from 30 to 43%, reflecting a growing public health concern.14,18 On the contrary, only 21.8% of children reported any kind of emotional problems, such as anger or impulsivity, and many (nearly 50% reported prosocial behavior).

Although the Pearson correlation test did not yield statistically significant results (p = 0.963), the pattern of behavioral problems was still more prevalent among children with higher junk food consumption. Specifically, 79.36% of children with severe junk food intake demonstrated abnormal behavior, compared to just 12.3% among those with minimal intake. This suggests a positive trend, if not a definitive causation, linking poor dietary habits to behavioral challenge and a higher likelihood of emotional symptoms in children. These findings are consistent with prior literature suggesting that high consumption of sugar and artificial additives is associated with behavioral disturbances, including impulsivity, inattention, and aggression.4,5,19

Nutritional deficiencies, particularly in key nutrients such as iron, omega-3 fatty acids, and B vitamins, have been linked to impairments in brain development and neurological functioning. These nutrients play vital roles in processes such as neurotransmitter synthesis, myelination, and energy metabolism within the brain. When these essential elements are lacking in the diet, it can negatively impact cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and behavior. As a result, inadequate intake of these nutrients may be one of the contributing factors behind certain behavioral differences observed in individuals.20

Our results are in agreement with the existing study, suggesting that children in the top 33% of the SDQ scale reflect the population with long-term nutritional imbalance due to a junk-food diet in early childhood.7 Existing studies have suggested an association between emotional symptoms and high-sugar food, and conversely, a lower likelihood of hyperactivity in a low-fat diet.21 This supports the results of the present study in which intake of chocolates high in sugar and fast food high in fat was found to be higher. High fat and sugar are thought to mediate behavioral neurochemicals through proinflammatory cytokines, which offers a possible explanation for the increased emotional symptoms; however, the evidence is not conclusive. In the present study, however, the majority of the children with emotional symptoms fell into the moderate junk food category as against the expected correlation between severe junk food consumption and behavioral problems. One potential contributing confounder could be the self-reporting nature of both questionnaires. Self-reported data, particularly among children, may introduce bias or inaccuracy in both dietary and behavioral assessments.22

Additionally, the lack of statistical significance may also stem from confounding factors such as socioeconomic status, parental influence, screen time, physical activity levels, or genetic predispositions to behavioral issues.

While this study helps in capturing the children's perspectives regarding diet and behavior, the self-reported method introduces bias that acts as a major limitation. Moreover, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Longitudinal studies would be more effective in establishing temporal relationships between junk food consumption and behavioral changes. The limited geographical area covered and the absence of an urban population limit the generalization of the study.

However, despite these limitations, the trends observed reinforce the argument that nutritional quality plays a pivotal role in children's emotional and cognitive well-being. Moreover, the reported higher incidence of behavioral concerns in schools with unregulated food habits supports the call for policy-level interventions in school meal planning and awareness initiatives.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study underscores the growing concern of junk food consumption and its possible behavioral consequences in school-going children aged 10–15 years. Although the statistical correlation between junk food intake and behavioral issues was not significant, a notable trend was observed wherein children with higher junk food intake exhibited greater behavioral disturbances, particularly hyperactivity, conduct disorders, and peer relationship issues.

These findings highlight the need for stronger school- and community-based nutritional awareness efforts. Behavioral health in children is a multifactorial issue, and dietary patterns play a crucial role in shaping cognitive, emotional, and social development. The high prevalence of abnormal SDQ scores among students with moderate to severe junk food intake suggests a potential modifiable risk factor that should not be ignored.
