Multi-locus genome-wide association mapping reveals loci underlying malt quality traits in heterogeneous Ethiopian Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) germplasm
Authors: Birhanu Babiye, Alemayehu Teressa Negawo, Wondimu Fekadu Ejerso, Gizachew Haile Gidamo, Sisay Kidane Alemu, Surafel Shibru Teklemariam, Adugna Abdi Woldesemayat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is widely cultivated for animal feed, human consumption, and most notably, for malting. Malt quality is determined by both genetic and environmental factors, and its improvement requires a clear understanding of the genomic regions controlling related traits. This study aimed to investigate the genetic basis of malt quality traits in Ethiopian barley genotypes using multi-locus genome-wide association studies (ML-GWAS). A barley panel of 260 were evaluated for malt quality traits across four sites in Ethiopia. ML- GWAS were performed on five traits related to malt quality using the information on Illumina 50 K iSelect single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Six different models were used for the ML-GWAS analysis. Significant variation in malt quality traits was observed across the studied genotypes. Extract Content (EC) ranged from 81.66% to 60.00%, while Protein Content (PC) varied between 17.63% and 9.03%. These traits exhibited moderate to high narrow-sense heritability, ranging from 72% for EC to 69% for GS, indicating strong genetic control and promising potential for selection in breeding programs. The coefficient of variation (CV) for all traits ranged from 5.37% to 32.63%. K-means clustering grouped the association panel into 5 distinct clusters. The principal component analysis (PCA) covers 76.1% variation. The genotypes B115.1, B248, B248 and B31.2 are the best overall performance across malt quality traits. The relative importance of the trait is BGC > EC > PC > GS. ML-GWAS identified 19 significant QTNs associated with malt quality traits. The Significant QTNs exhibited LOD scores ranging from 3.06 to 5.36 and r² values between 6.98% and 25.35%, and minor allele frequencies (MAF) from 0.054 to 0.465. The highest number of significant associations was detected on chromosomes 4 H, 6 H, and 7 H. The identified QTNs represent promising molecular markers for improving malt quality through marker-assisted selection, providing valuable tools for developing superior malting barley cultivars in Ethiopia. Further functional validation of these loci will contribute to a deeper understanding of the genetic architecture underlying malt quality traits in Ethiopian barley.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47506-6.

Introduction

Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is a major cereal crop primarily used for three purposes: food, feed, and malt1–3. Today, it is considered as a cash or industrial crop due to its importance in malt production4,5. Malting barley plays a crucial role in determining the phenotypic characteristics required for the malting and brewing industries6,7. Key phenotypic markers of malting barley include uniform and large grain size, active enzymes that break down the nutritional components of the grain (endosperm, protein, and carbohydrates), and a high protein content, which is essential for beer production8,9. Additionally, barley provides a vital food source for yeast during fermentation and contributes to the flavor and color of beer10,11.

Malt barley used for beer production undergoes three critical malting processes; steeping, germination, and kilning; that lead to compositional and enzymatic changes in the grain10,12. These biochemical and structural changes, collectively referred to as malt modification, are essential for producing high-quality malt13,14. Malted barley is characterized by uniform grain size, which is a key quality attribute15. The nutritional composition of malted barley grain significantly influences both the malting process and subsequent beer production, with the extent and method of modification playing a decisive role in determining the final product quality12; Ramirez and Hernandez16).

Several nutritional characteristics serve as indicators of malt quality, with the most important being protein content, malt extract, β-glucan, friability, and grain size5,17. The protein content is crucial for feeding the yeast during fermentation. Still, it must be balanced, as excessively high protein levels can reduce extract yield and impair beer clarity, as well as the availability of fermentable sugars, which directly affects the brewing efficiency5,18. β-glucan content affects wort viscosity and filtration; high β-glucan content can lead to processing difficulties and a cloudy beer8. Friability measures the ease with which malt grains can be crushed, which is essential for efficient access to starch and enzymes during mashing4,12. Grain size and uniformity are also important as they affect water absorption, uniformity of modification and the overall quality of malt processing (Garcia et al.19, Yin20. Together, these characteristics determine both the quantity and quality of beer produced during fermentation21; Garcia et al.19).

The protein of the barley grain influences the quantity and quality of the beer4,22. The grain protein and its amino acids and peptide are mainly used as a source of nitrogen for the yeast during fermentation5,23. Therefore, if the malt grain contains too little protein, it does not have enough nitrogen for the yeast, which leads to a premature termination of the fermentation process12,23. If fermentation is stopped prematurely, the alcohol content will be low4,12. On the other hand, a high protein content has a low proportion of fermentable sugars, and a low proportion of fermentable sugar leads to low alcohol production23. Proteins can interact with polyphenols to cause clouding of the beer22. Excess protein contributes to chill haze and reduces the shelf life of the beer9,24. Certain proteins (in particular the lipid transfer protein, LTP1) are beneficial as they stabilize the foam and improve the retention of the head (Garcia et al.19). A balanced protein content is required, not too low and not too high12. During kilning and later during brewing, proteins take part in Maillard reactions with sugars and thus contribute to the flavour and colour of the beer (Bonifácio et al.25,). The optimum protein content in malting barley is normally 9–12%26. Below this range, fermentation performance can suffer, while above this range, malt extract decreases, turbidity increases and beer quality decreases15.

The grain size of malting barley has an influence on the quality of its nutritional properties27,28. Large grains have a lower protein content because the endosperm covers the larger grain size, resulting in a lower protein content and dilution of the smaller protein29,30. Large grain sizes also have better friability because the larger grain size has a more uniform endosperm and undergoes a similar change during the malting process13. As the grain size increases, the β-glucan content decreases, which in turn leads to better modification during the malting process30,31. Large grain sizes contain more starch (endosperm) and fewer husks per unit weight. Starch is the main source of fermentable sugars32. Therefore, large grains provide a higher malt extract yield30,32,33. On the other hand, small grain sizes have a high protein content, which reduces the malt extract yield and lowers the brewing quality12,30. Small grain sizes also have low friability, which allows incomplete modification, resulting in low brewing quality8; Yin20. Small grains have a high content of β-glucan (polysaccharides in the cell wall that affect wort viscosity and filtration), which leads to filtration problems during brewing and poor beer quality18. As the grain size decreases, the malt extract content decreases as it has a low sugar content. This significantly reduces malt quality and brewing quality8,13; Yin20.

This conventional breeding is carried out in Ethiopia by focusing on the classical breeding method for malting barley34,35. This inefficient breeding method is carried out using modern molecular breeding genomics tools to identify the genetic basis of malt quality indicating nutritional properties of the malt grain and to accelerate breeding36,37. Various efforts have been made to improve the Ethiopian malted barley variety using both conventional and molecular breeding methods. Kefale and Abushu38, analyased the malt quality profiles of malt barley. On the other hand Girma et al.39, established the relationship between malt quality with germination time and temperature. Galano et al.40, investigated the association of malting barley with neck spot disease and malting quality. Dinsa41, evaluated the trait stability of Ethiopian malting barley cultivars in different environments. Asres42, investigated the malt quality and yield stability across environments for Ethiopian malt barley varieties. Daba et al.12, identified 81 genomic barley regions controlling malt quality traits using mixed linear Model (MLM) in GWAS on 42 Ethiopian, 2 Kenyan and 2 North Dakota state University barley lines.

Multi locus GWAS(ML-GWAS) offer several advantages over classical single-locus approaches in detecting genetic variants associated with quantitative complex malt quality indicative traits43,44. One major advantage of the ML-GWAS model is enhanced statistical power, which provides strong analytical power when detecting multiple loci with small effects on malt quality24,45,46. Additionally, ML-GWAS methods help reduce Type I errors. The classical Single-locus GWAS often require stringent multiple testing corrections, such as the Bonferroni method, to control false-positive rates47. These corrections can be overly conservative, potentially excluding true associations. ML-GWAS methods, by considering multiple variants simultaneously, mitigate this issue and allow for more accurate identification of significant loci48,49. Another advantage is the improved detection of pleiotropic effects, as complex malt quality traits are often influenced by pleiotropic genes that affect multiple malt quality traits at once50,51. ML-GWAS can capture these pleiotropic effects better, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic architecture underlying complex malt quality traits52,53. Furthermore, ML-GWAS models offer better control of population structure. Population stratification can confound GWAS results, leading to spurious associations54. ML-GWAS models incorporate sophisticated statistical methods to account for population structure, reducing potential biases and enhancing the reliability of the findings46,55. Lastly, multi-locus approaches facilitate multi-trait analysis, enabling the simultaneous exploration of multiple related traits. This allows researchers to examine genetic correlations and shared genetic bases between malt quality traits, uncovering novel insights into the genetic factors influencing complex malt quality traits56–58. Thus, the aim of this study was to identify SNP markers strongly associated with barley malt quality traits, and to evaluate the effectiveness of different ML-GWAS models in detecting significant malt quality trait-associated SNPs.

Materials and methods

Experimental association panel

The Ethiopian barley collection accessions used for this study were 260. The collections, including 21 barley breeding lines from the Barley Improvement Program of Holeta Agricultural Research Centre, and 239 landraces from the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute. The 260 barley collections were established to represent Ethiopia’s agro-ecological zones and geographical areas. In addition, the mapping panel was designed to contain 51 six-row, 193 two-row, and 16 irregular row barley Core Collections, and also the material utilized by59. The passport data for all the materials used in this study were provided as supplementary data (Supplementary file 1).

Description of study sites

Four experimental sites located within Ethiopia’s major barley-growing belt were selected for this study (Fig. 1), representing diverse agro-ecological zones. The first site, Holeta Agricultural Research Centre, is situated at 9°00’ N latitude and 38°30’ E longitude, with an altitude of 2,400 meters. It receives an average annual rainfall of 1,044 mm, with minimum and maximum temperatures of 13.8°C and 20.4°C, respectively. The soil is predominantly Nitosols and Regosols with a pH of 5.23. The second site, Kulumsa Agricultural Research Centre (Bekoji Sub-Centre), located at 7°35′N and 39°10′E at 2,810 meters altitude, has an annual average rainfall of 939 mm, temperatures ranging between 16.55°C and 27.55°C, and clay soil with a pH of 5.63. The third site, Welkite Agricultural Research Centre (Jenbero Sub-Centre), lies at 7°56’ N and 37°51’ E at an altitude of 2,219 meters, receiving 1,274.67 mm of rainfall annually, with temperatures ranging from 16.55°C to 27.55°C. The soil is clay with a pH of 5.63. The final site, Debre Markos Agricultural Research Centre, is located at 10°16’16.92’’ N and 37°46’49.968’’ E at an elevation of 2,467 m. It receives an average annual rainfall of 1,340 mm, with a temperature range of 9.9 °C to 19.5 °C and has clay soil with a pH of 5.72. These sites were chosen to capture the environmental variability within the barley belt of Ethiopia, ensuring robust evaluation of genotypic performance under diverse growing conditions for malt quality nutritional traits. Experimental design

An alpha lattice design with two replications was used for this experiment in the 2024/2025 cropping season. The full design includes a total of 13 blocks and 20 plots in each block, the plot size being 1 m in length and 0.8 m in width. Each plot has four rows, and each row in turn was drawn by a row marker material. A spacing of 1 m was maintained between blocks, 50 centimeters between plots, and 20 centimeters between rows within each plot. Each association panel was assigned to each experimental plot in a randomized manner. The randomization was done using a random allocation software for parallel group randomized trials60.

The experimental accessions were planted using the same design for all experimental sites. Before sowing, all experimental sites were prepared with the necessary land preparation. The experiment was sown by weighing a total of 10 g of seed from each association panel barley accessions for one plot and the weighed total seed for each row was 2.5 g of seed; equal amounts of seed were sown for the four rows in the plot. 45 kg of Di-ammonium phosphate (DAP) fertilizer per hectare was applied during sowing. Weeds were manually weeded once a month for 4 consecutive months in all four experimental sites. No pesticides were applied in all four experimental sites: only natural ones. Sentries were employed at all four experimental sites to prevent birds and other animals from damaging the experiment, ensuring that the necessary data were collected without any loss across all sites.

Grain size measurement by sieving

A 100 g grain sample was subjected to sieving using a nested set of sieves with aperture sizes of > 2.8 mm, > 2.5 mm, > 2.2 mm, and < 2.2 mm. Sieving was performed for five minutes, after which the grains were fractionated into four distinct size classes. The weight of each fraction retained on the respective sieve was recorded, and grains with a diameter of 2.5 mm were selected for this study.

Malt quality trait

Whole barley grain samples (100 g per sample) were cleaned and equilibrated at room temperature to standardize the moisture content across genotypes. The samples were scanned directly using a Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) instrument equipped with a rotating cup to ensure uniform presentation without compaction. Spectral data were collected as 32 co-added scans per replicate across the 1250 nm range, with reference scans performed prior to each batch, and the final dataset included protein content (%), β-glucan, friability (%), and malt extract (%) from each association panel genotype. The NIRS experiment was conducted according to the procedure61,62.

Genotyping data

Following Teklemariam et al.59, for genotyping, three seeds per accession of the 260 association panel were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions (20–22 °C day/17–19 °C night) in nutrient-rich Einheitserde ED73 soil. At the 2–3 leaf stage, approximately 300 mg of leaf tissue from a single plant was sampled for DNA extraction using a modified Cetyltrimethyle Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) protocol. Genotyping was conducted using the barley 50k iSelect SNP array (Illumina) at TraitGenetics GmbH, Germany. From an initial total of 40,387 SNPs, 10,644 high-quality markers were retained after removing monomorphic loci and applying thresholds for missing data (≤ 5%), minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05, and heterozygosity (≤ 12.5%). The resulting 10,644 high-density SNPs were used for further analysis in this study (Supplementary file 2).

Statistical analysis

All collected malt quality trait data were subjected to appropriate statistical analyses to identify meaningful patterns and relationships in the malt quality of Ethiopian barley germplasm. Both the trait and genotypic data sets were analyzed using methods suited to their characteristics to ensure reliable interpretation of the results. Details of the statistical analysis software used for each type of analysis are described below.

Malt quality data analysis

The collected malt quality data, before conducting advanced statistical analyses, all the collected data were first tested for normality. The distribution of the data was assessed using the Anderson- Darling test, supported by graphical approaches such as, histograms (Supplementary file 3) and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots (Supplementary file 4), performed in Minitab software (Version 12)63 and JMP (version 17) software64, separately. These procedures ensured that the data met the assumptions of normal distribution, which is a prerequisite for many parametric analyses. The results indicated that all datasets followed a normal distribution at a significant level of P ≤ 0.05 and were therefore considered appropriate for subsequent statistical analyses.

For the malt quality data, descriptive statistics were first applied to summarize the distribution and variation within traits, performed in Minitab software (Version 12)63. To classify accessions based on trait similarity, k-means clustering was performed, which allowed the identification of different phenotypic groups, using JMP (version 17) software64. All the data were analyzed using R (version 4.5) (Wnvenables65, and different packages. Biplot Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on standardized data (centered and scaled) to reduce dimensionality and identify the principal components explaining the largest proportion of variance among genotypes. This analysis facilitated visualization of multivariate relationships between malt quality traits and genotypes. The analysis was conducted using the ggplot2 package66. The correlation matrix analysis is used to quantify pairwise relationships among malt quality traits using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. The analysis was conducted using corrplot package (Taiyun et al.67,), pairwise scatter plots were generated to visualize relationships among malt quality traits and to assess linear trends between traits, providing enhanced plots with correlation coefficients and trait distributions. The analysis was performed using the GGally package68. The radar chart, which is used to compare and visualize the relative performance of malt quality traits across genotypes by displaying standardized trait values on a common radial scale. This approach facilitated multivariate comparison and the identification of top-performing genotypes for malt quality traits. The analysis was conducted using the ggplot2 package66. and the relative importance of malt quality traits was assessed to determine their contribution to overall malt quality variation within the association panel. This analysis was performed using a random forest model, which ranks traits based on their influence on prediction accuracy. The analysis was conducted using the ggplot2 package66. All the above listed analysis were performed to display the statistical significance among genotypes for malt quality traits and to examine the relationships between these traits. All statistical analyses were performed with a significant threshold of P ≤ 0.05 to ensure the robustness and validity of the results.

Genotype data analysis

Before conducting the ML-GWAS, SNP genotype data were subjected to quality-control analyses to ensure data reliability and suitability for downstream analyses. SNPs were filtered using a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 (5%) to exclude rare alleles with low statistical power, and a missing data rate of ≤ 0.05 (5%) to eliminate incomplete genotype information that could bias association results. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay analysis was performed to examine the pattern of LD across the genome using R software (version 4.5) (Wnvenables65, LDheatmap package69, which provides insights into the extent of recombination and the resolution power of association mapping. The decay pattern of LD was assessed by plotting the squared correlation coefficient (r²) against the physical distance between SNP pairs, thereby estimating the rate at which LD declines with increasing distance. Additionally, the distribution of r² values across all markers was examined to quantify the overall extent of genetic relatedness within the population. To further characterize marker quality, a histogram of r² values was constructed, reflecting the variation in marker correlations across the genome. Furthermore, the density of SNP markers was evaluated by plotting a histogram of SNP distribution across chromosomes, demonstrating that the marker density was sufficient to provide comprehensive genome-wide coverage for association mapping. These quality control steps ensured that the SNP dataset was robust and suitable for subsequent ML-GWAS analysis. All statistical analyses of the SNP data were conducted using a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05 to ensure the reliability and validity of the results.

The rationale for using six ML-GWAS models was to enhance detection strength, as they can identify SNPs with small effects that traditional models like GLM and MLM often miss. Combining multiple models reduces bias by minimizing false positives and false negatives, while cross-validation, where a locus is detected by several models, strengthens confidence in true SNP trait associations. Moreover, because most malt quality nutritional traits are polygenic and influenced by loci with varying effect sizes, the multi-model approach provides a more comprehensive genetic picture.

Statistically significant SNPs were then identified from the BLUP data using appropriate significance thresholds for each experimental site. SNPs showing significant and stable associations across experimental sites based on BLUP-derived malt quality trait data were considered robust and reliable and were selected as the final significant markers. The estimated narrow-sense heritability (h²) values for the investigated malt quality traits ranged from 68% to 88%, indicating that most of the observed malt quality trait variation within the panel was attributable to genetic factors. Conversely, the coefficients of variation (CV) ranged from 5.3% to 32.6%, demonstrating a reliable level of experimental precision and confirming that the observed differences among the association panel are primarily due to inherent genetic variation rather than environmental.

The statistical analyses of both malt quality data and genotypic datasets revealed that the 260 Ethiopian barley germplasms exhibited significant genetic variation for all malt quality related traits, including EC, F, PC, BGC, and GS. This wide malt quality trait diversity highlights the potential of germplasm as a valuable genetic resource for malt quality improvement in Ethiopia. Moreover, differences in genotype performance across traits emphasize the existence of superior alleles and highlight opportunities for marker-assisted selection in barley breeding programs targeting malt quality improvement in Ethiopia.

K-means clustering and PCA

The k-means clustering revealed clear population structure among the 260 Ethiopian barley germplasms based on malt quality traits. The cluster analysis grouped the studied barley genotypes into five distinct clusters with different number of genotypes (Fig. 2): Cluster I contained 2 genotypes, Cluster II contained 41 genotypes, Cluster III contained 101 genotypes, Cluster IV contained 2 genotypes, and Cluster V contained 114 genotypes (Supplementary file 5). Each cluster comprised genotypes with distinct trait profiles, highlighting the genetic and phenotypic diversity among the studied population. Clusters 3 and 5 (blue and light green), which contain the largest number of genotypes, are positioned close to the trait vectors (EC, FC, PC, BGC, and GS). This indicates that most of the variation in these traits is explained by the genotypes in these clusters, suggesting that they harbor higher malt quality diversity and potentially favorable alleles for malt quality improvement. Cluster 2 (green) is moderately separated along PCA1 and PCA2, representing a group of genotypes with intermediate performance across the traits studied. In contrast, clusters 1 and 4 (red and brown) are distinctly separated from the main groups, indicating that the few genotypes they contain have unique malt quality profiles, possibly representing rare allelic combinations or divergent selection histories.Fig. 2Clustering pattern of Ethiopian barley germplasm according to malt quality traits.

The correlation and pairwise scatter plots analysis of malt quality trait

The correlation matrix (Fig. 4) displays the interrelations among five malt quality traits: PC, EC, FC, BGC, and GS. PC has a strong negative correlation with EC (−0.87) and moderate negative correlations with FC and BGC. This means that when PC goes up, these traits tend to go down. EC is moderately positively correlated with FC (0.49) and BGC (0.39), which means that these traits tend to go up at the same time. FC and BGC have a moderate negative correlation (−0.42), and GS has weak correlations with all other traits, which means it is not very related to the rest. Linkage disequilibrium analysis

The integrity and distribution of SNP markers in barley were evaluated using several analyses. R values across markers and their frequency distribution indicate strong genetic relatedness and variation. Scatter plots of R and r² values against physical distance reveal patterns of linkage disequilibrium decay (Supplementary file 6). SNP distribution and inter-marker distances show uniform coverage across the genome, and histogram analysis confirms adequate marker density for effective genome-wide mapping (Fig. 8). In this study, LD decays rapidly at short inter-marker distances and subsequently stabilizes at lower levels with increasing distance (Fig. 8b). The decay pattern is clearly illustrated in the scatter plots of R (Fig. 8c) and r² (Fig. 8f) values against distance, where both measures are high at short distances and progressively decrease with increasing distance. The red curve in Fig. 8f represents a smoothing function rather than a simple regression line, thereby capturing the non-linear nature of LD decay, which is characterized by a sharp initial decline followed by a gradual reduction. LD decay size is conventionally defined as the physical distance at which r² falls below a critical threshold (0.1 or 0.2). Multi-locus GWAS analysis

Six complementary multi-locus GWAS (ML-GWAS) models were employed to identify significant QTNs across four different experimental sites. The number of significant QTNs varied among experimental locations: 86 at Bekoji, 77 at Holeta, 69 at Debre-Markos, and 55 at Welkite (Supplementary file 7). Across all experimental sites, a total of 19 stable QTNs consistently associated with barley quality traits were identified (Tables 2, 3). These QTNs represent reliable and robust associations, each explaining different proportions of phenotypic variation, as shown by their respective LOD scores and R² values. Taken together, they emphasize genetic architecture underlying malt quality. The identified QTNs were significantly associated with five traits indicative of malt quality and were distributed across all chromosomes at an LOD threshold of ≥ 3 (Table 2). Among the models, the mrMLM approach detected the highest number of QTNs (10), followed by ISIS EM-BLASSO (9), while the FASTmrMLM model detected the fewest. In terms of chromosomal distribution, the highest number of QTNs was identified on chromosomes 6 and 5, while the lowest number was observed on chromosome 3 (Table 3). Discussion

Multi-locus genome-wide association(ML-GWAS) studies provide a powerful framework for unraveling the complex genetic architecture of barley malt quality traits with greater resolution and accuracy than conventional single-locus GWAS models76,77. In barley, a crop of significant malting importance, ML-GWAS enables the simultaneous assessment of multiple loci, facilitating the detection of subtle genetic effects and epistatic interactions that are often overlooked by single-marker approaches78,79. This methodology is particularly advantageous for dissecting key malting quality traits, including extract content, friability, protein content, β-glucan concentration, and grain size12. By enhancing the precision of marker-trait association detection and reducing false positives, ML-GWAS provides a comprehensive understanding of the polygenic control underlying barely malt quality80. The identified SNPs represent robust candidates for marker-assisted selection and functional validation, offering a strategic foundation for accelerating the breeding of superior malt barley cultivars with optimized malting performance in Ethiopia81–83.

The analysis of SNP heterozygosity, Minor Allele Frequency (MAF), and R² values provides a comprehensive view of the genetic architecture underlying malt quality traits in Ethiopian barley germplasm (Elias et al., 202383). Variation in heterozygosity across chromosomes reflects the presence of diverse genetic pools within the barley panel, which is critical for improving key malt quality traits, such as extract content, friability, protein content, β-glucan concentration, and grain size, across diverse environmental conditions28,99. Regions exhibiting high heterozygosity may harbor alleles that positively influence these malt quality traits, underscoring the importance of genetic diversity in developing barley varieties with superior malting performance100. MAF analysis further aids in identifying alleles that are prevalent in the mapping population and potentially contribute to favorable malt quality characteristics76. For example, loci with higher MAF could carry alleles that enhance extract yield, reduce β-glucan levels, or improve friability traits directly relevant to malting efficiency and brewing quality12. These observations are consistent with previous studies highlighting the significance of identifying such alleles for targeted breeding101; U. Kumar32.

R² values, indicating the strength of association between SNP markers and malt quality traits, provide insights into genomic regions strongly linked to specific malt quality parameters29,102. These markers are valuable for guiding marker-assisted selection (MAS), enabling the precise selection of barley genotypes with superior malting attributes103,104. The combination of heterozygosity, MAF, and R² analyses thus offers a robust framework for identifying candidate SNPs and understanding the genetic basis of malt quality traits in Ethiopian barley105,106. Overall, these findings demonstrate that maintaining and exploiting genetic diversity, alongside identifying high-frequency favorable alleles and strongly associated markers, is essential for breeding barley varieties with optimized malting performance101,105. Such insights are particularly relevant for Ethiopia, where barley is cultivated across diverse agro-ecological zones and enhancing malting quality is critical for the brewing industry and local food security76,106.

Landscape of SNP distribution across the seven barley chromosomes

The identification of high SNP density regions on chromosomes 2 H, 6 H, and 7 H suggests that these genomic hotspots may reflect historical selective pressures shaping the genetic variation underlying malt quality in Ethiopian barley107. These SNP-rich regions not only indicate areas of elevated genetic diversity but also represent strategic targets for mapping malt quality traits, as they are likely to harbor alleles influencing key parameters such as extract content, friability, protein content, β-glucan levels, and grain size12,99. Our observations align with previous reports by Teklemariam et al.108, who documented similar SNP distribution patterns, confirming that certain genomic regions exhibit higher variability and enhanced potential for trait improvement. This is further supported by studies that identified specific SNP hotspots associated with agronomically important traits, mirroring our findings46,50,109.

Such SNP-dense regions are particularly valuable for barley breeding programs focused on malting quality, as they may contain alleles contributing to desirable brewing characteristics110. Additionally, as highlighted by Nice et al.111, high SNP density facilitates marker-assisted selection for complex polygenic traits, suggesting that these regions may underlie the intricate genetic architecture controlling multiple malt quality parameters simultaneously110. Conversely, traits with fewer marker-trait associations may be governed by simpler genetic mechanisms112. Overall, these findings emphasize the utility of SNP-rich regions as genomic landmarks for precision breeding, providing key insights for the development of Ethiopian barley varieties with superior malting performance and supporting the advancement of high-quality barley for the brewing industry110,113.

Impact of SNP variation on malt quality traits in barley

The observed variability in SNP effects and association strengths across malt quality traits highlights the importance of employing multiple analytical approaches to comprehensively dissect the genetic architecture of complex malt quality traits in barley114. Our findings are consistent with previous studies110, demonstrating that different statistical models can yield complementary insights into trait control. In particular, the ISIS EM-BLASSO and mrMLM models proved highly effective in identifying significant SNPs with strong LOD scores and substantial phenotypic variance explained (R²), whereas FASTmrMLM, FASTmrEMMA and pKWmEB detected comparatively fewer associations. This methodological variation underscores the influence of model selection on detection power and emphasizes that integrating multiple approaches provides a more robust and reliable understanding of the genetic basis of malt quality traits76,110,115,116.

As presented in Table 3, our ML-GWAS analysis identified significant SNPs associated with key malt quality traits, including extract content, friability, protein content, β-glucan levels, grain size, and related parameters, each characterized by chromosomal position, allelic effects, and LOD patterns. The use of diverse models (mrMLM, FASTmrMLM, FASTmrEMMA, pLARmEB, pKWmEB, and ISIS EMBLASSO) enhanced our capacity to detect both major and minor effect loci, particularly for polygenic traits influenced by complex interactions between genetics and environment46. These results not only deepen our understanding of the genetic architecture underlying barley malting quality but also provide actionable SNP targets for marker-assisted selection and precision breeding. Future studies could further refine these associations by integrating multi-omics data and validating SNP effects across diverse germplasm and environmental conditions46,50,117.

Comparison of the six ML - GWAS models

The comprehensive evaluation of six ML-GWAS methods (FASTmrEMMA, mrMLM, pLARmEB, FASTmrMLM, pKWmEB, and ISIS EM-BLASSO) revealed distinct performance patterns in detecting SNPs associated with key malt quality traits, including extract content, friability, protein content, β-glucan levels, and grain size. Notably, mrMLM emerged as the most effective, identifying 10 significant SNPs, followed by ISIS EM-BLASSO (9 SNPs) and pLARmEB (4 SNPs). These results are consistent with prior studies highlighting the superior detection power of ISIS EM-BLASSO and pLARmEB, which identified 1,653 and 913 SNPs, respectively, across diverse yield related agronomic traits not the malt quality trait46,50.

The observed variation in SNP detection efficiency among the six ML-GWAS methods likely reflects differences in trait complexity, population structure, and underlying statistical algorithms116. For instance, mrMLM and ISIS EM-BLASSO superior performance in our study, particularly for traits such as extract content and β-glucan levels, may be attributed to its enhanced ability to model polygenic architectures and capture epistatic interactions influencing malt quality46. These findings underscore the critical role of method selection in ML-GWAS and suggest that integrating complementary approaches leveraging the precision of mrMLM alongside the sensitivity of ISIS EM-BLASSO can optimize SNP discovery for complex malt quality traits80,116. Collectively, our findings highlight the power of ML-GWAS as a strategic tool for dissecting the genetic basis of barley malting quality, providing actionable targets for marker-assisted selection and accelerating the development of superior malt barley cultivars16,46,67,80,116,118–120.

Conclusion

In this study, six advanced ML-GWAS models were applied to dissect the genetic architecture of key malt quality traits in Ethiopian barley. A total of 19 significant QTNs were identified, with the most robust and consistent associations located on chromosomes 2 H, 5 H, and 6 H. Several SNPs overlapped or were proximal to previously reported loci, including FC-associated SNPs on 5 H (529–536 Mb) near qGL5H and a BGC locus on 7 H (70.7 Mb) near HvCslF6. Notably, potentially novel loci were discovered, including EC-associated SNPs on 3 H (166 Mb) and 6 H (373 Mb), FC-associated SNPs on 2 H (631 Mb) and 5 H (635.9 Mb), and a GS-associated SNP on 4 H (585.2 Mb), expanding the known genomic regions influencing malt quality. Many of these loci explained substantial proportions of phenotypic variation, highlighting their potential as reliable molecular markers for malting performance. These findings provide a valuable resource for marker-assisted and genomic selection, supporting the development of superior barley cultivars with enhanced malt quality traits. Future functional validation of the identified QTNs will further clarify their regulatory roles and guide precision breeding strategies, contributing to the sustainable improvement of barley for the malting and brewing industry in Ethiopia.
