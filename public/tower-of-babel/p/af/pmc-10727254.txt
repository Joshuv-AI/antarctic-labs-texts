Long‐Term Cumulative High‐Sensitivity C‐Reactive Protein and Mortality Among Patients With Acute Heart Failure
Authors: Lihua Zhang, Guangda He, Xiqian Huo, Aoxi Tian, Runqing Ji, Boxuan Pu, Yue Peng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElevated hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) level is associated with worse prognosis among patients hospitalized for heart failure. However, the prognostic value of the long‐term cumulative hsCRP remains unknown.Methods and ResultsWe consecutively enrolled patients hospitalized for heart failure and collected their hsCRP data at admission and 1 and 12 months after discharge. Long‐term cumulative hsCRP was evaluated using 2 approaches, cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and cumulative times of high hsCRP levels. Patients were classified into 4 groups by cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and cumulative times of high hsCRP levels (0‐ to 3‐times: number of times that hsCRP levels were higher than cutoff values at admission or 1 or 12 months), respectively. Multivariable Cox models were used to assess the association of mortality with cumulative hsCRP. A total of 1281 patients were included; the median age was 64 (interquartile range, 54–73) years, and 35.4% were women. Over a 4.8‐year (interquartile range, 4.2–5.1) follow‐up, 374 (29.2%) patients died. Elevated long‐term cumulative hsCRP level was related to higher mortality. Specifically, taking the quartile 1 as the reference, the hazard ratios (HRs) were 1.29 (95% CI, 0.92–1.81) for quartile 2, 1.62 (95% CI, 1.16–2.25) for quartile 3, and 2.38 (95% CI, 1.75–3.23) for quartile 4. Similarly, compared with the patients with 0‐times (hsCRP level lower than the cutoff values in all 3 time points) of high hsCRP level, the HRs were 1.36 for 1‐time (hsCRP level higher than the cutoff value in one of the 3 time points) (95% CI, 0.92–2.01), 1.95 for 2‐times (hsCRP levels higher than the cutoff values in 2 of the 3 time points) (95% CI, 1.34–2.82), and 2.80 for 3‐times (hsCRP levels higher than the cutoff values in the 3 time points) (95% CI, 1.97–4.00).ConclusionsIncreasing long‐term cumulative hsCRP level was associated with worse outcomes in patients hospitalized for acute heart failure. Repeated hsCRP measurements could assist physicians in identifying patients with a high risk of death.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT 02878811.Keywords: cumulative high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, heart failure, mortality, prognosisSubject Categories: Heart Failure

Heart failure (HF) is a life‐threatening syndrome causing substantial morbidity and mortality, with an estimated 64.3 million people affected worldwide. 1 In recent decades, it has been widely recognized that systemic inflammation plays an important role in the initiation and progression of HF via multiple mechanisms. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Among the known inflammatory biomarkers, hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) has been widely acknowledged to be a cost‐effective prognostic factor for the risk classification of cardiovascular disease. 7

Elevated hsCRP level at the time of risk assessment is associated with an increased risk of worse prognosis among patients with HF. 8 , 9 However, rather than being a static level that remains unchanged after a single assessment, the hsCRP level is a dynamic risk factor fluctuating over time. 10 , 11 Besides the temporal fluctuation in the severity of inflammation, the duration of inflammation also serves as a contributor to cardiovascular disease. 12 Recent evidence underscores that a high level of cumulative hsCRP is an independent risk factor for new‐onset HF, and has stronger risk predictive performance than baseline hsCRP level. 13 However, no previous study has reported the long‐term cumulative hsCRP and its prognostic value among patients with acute HF.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted an analysis on the basis of a nationally prospective cohort of patients hospitalized for HF, and aimed to examine the association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP and mortality in patients with acute HF.

Methods

Data Availability Statement

The data sets generated and analyzed during the current study are not publicly available; because of government policy stipulations, it is not permissible for the researchers to make the raw data publicly available at this time. And currently, it is not yet possible for other researchers to apply for access.

Study Design and Participants

The China PEACE 5p‐HF Study (China Patient‐Centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events Prospective Heart Failure Study) is a nationwide, prospective, multicenter observational cohort study. The detailed study design has been published. 14 Briefly, we conducted a nationally prospective study which recruited patients with acute HF from 52 hospitals throughout 20 provinces from all economic‐geographic regions in China from August 2016 to May 2018. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old, and hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of decompensated chronic HF or new‐onset HF. The diagnostic criteria for HF were based on the Chinese clinical guidelines for HF, 15 which was consistent with the international clinical guidelines. 16 Eligible patients were consecutively screened, and we obtained their consent within 48 hours of admission. We followed up the patients at 1, 6, and 12 months after discharge and annually thereafter. In the current analyses, we excluded the patients who died within 1‐year after discharge or did not have blood samples at admission or postdischarge follow‐up. The patient selection flowchart was presented in Figure S1.

The ethics committees of Fuwai Hospital and all collaborating hospitals had approved China PEACE 5p‐HF Study. The study was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT 02878811). The study conformed with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Data Collection and Definition

Age and sex data were obtained by physicians with a standardized questionnaire through face‐to‐face interviews during the index hospitalization. Clinical characteristics at admission, New York Heart Association class, comorbidities, and discharge medication data were collected from patients' medical charts. HF presentations were categorized as decompensated chronic HF and new‐onset HF. Decompensated chronic HF was defined as patients with previous HF at baseline, and new‐onset HF as patients without a medical history of previous HF. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured by trained physicians at the collaborating centers. And LVEF subtypes were categorized as HF with preserved ejection fraction (LVEF ≥50%), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (LVEF 40%–49%), and HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF <40%). 16 Patients' blood samples were collected within 48 hours at admission, and we uniformly assayed serum biomarkers (hsCRP, NT‐proBNP [N‐terminal pro‐B type natriuretic peptide], glycated hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], and creatinine) at the central laboratory. Albumin, hemoglobin, and leukocyte counts were measured at the local laboratories. Comorbidities (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, valvular heart disease, diabetes, anemia, reduced renal function, stroke, peripheral artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hyperuricemia, and gastritis) were defined based on discharge diagnosis, laboratory results, and medical history. Diabetes was defined as a history of diabetes or HbA1c ≥6.5%, and anemia was defined as hemoglobin <110 g/L in women or <120 g/L in men. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) level was calculated using a modified Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation developed based on Chinese patients with chronic kidney disease, and reduced renal function was defined as an eGFR <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2. 17 Self‐reported medications and data of rehospitalization were collected in the follow‐up interviews. Cardiovascular rehospitalization included rehospitalization for HF, implantation of implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, cardiac resynchronization therapy, cardiac arrest, angina pectoris, and stroke. And noncardiovascular rehospitalization included rehospitalization for other causes. We collected patients' quality of life at baseline, 1 month, and 12 months after discharge using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire‐12 (KCCQ‐12). KCCQ‐12 is a questionnaire for assessing the quality of life among patients with HF. The scores range from 0 to 100, and a higher score reflects possibly better quality of life. 18

HsCRP Assay

HsCRP was assayed by a clinical chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU680). The detection limit was 0.02 mg/L. The inter‐assay coefficient of variation was ≤8.28%, and the intra‐assay coefficient of variation was ≤6.21%.

The time points of hsCRP measurements were decided on the basis of a literature review. Nearly 30% of death events during 1‐year after discharge occurred in the first month, and ≈50% during 5‐year occurred in the first year. 19 , 20 , 21 Therefore, hsCRP data were measured at 3 time points: admission, and 1 month and 12 months after discharge.

Clinical Outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was all‐cause death after 12 months of discharge, and the secondary clinical outcome was cardiovascular death after 12 months of discharge. Cardiovascular death included death due to HF, acute coronary syndrome, cerebrovascular events, pulmonary hypertension, peripheral artery disease, aortic vascular disease, and sudden cardiac death.

Death event data were collected and adjudicated according to the practice used in the large‐scale international multi‐center clinical trials. 22 Death events were collected from the national database of causes of death, death certificates, or interviews of patients' relatives. The clinical outcome events were uniformly confirmed by clinic staff at the national coordinating center.

Statistical Analysis

Median (interquartile range [IQR]) was used to describe continuous variables, and numbers (frequency) for categorical variables. Patients' characteristics across groups were compared by the Kruskal‐Wallis test or Mantel–Haenszel test as appropriate. We also examined the Spearman correlation of hsCRP levels at different time points.

The long‐term cumulative hsCRP from baseline admission to 12 months after discharge was assessed by 2 statistical approaches, the cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and the cumulative times of high hsCRP levels. First, the cumulative hsCRP level was calculated using the area under the curve according to the same method in prior analyses, 23 , 24 , 25 and the detailed calculations of the area under the curve are presented in Figure S2. With the receiver operating characteristic curve approach, we analyzed the performance of cumulative hsCRP in predicting 4‐year all‐cause and cardiovascular death. And we classified the patients into 4 groups by cumulative hsCRP level quartiles: quartile 1, <13 mg/L*month (reference group); quartile 2, ≥13 and <26 mg/L*month; quartile 3, ≥26 and <56 mg/L*month; and quartile 4, ≥56 mg/L*month. Second, we sampled 70% patients to calculate the hsCRP cutoff values using receiver operating characteristic curve of 4‐year all‐cause death for 500 times and determined the value with the highest density as the cutoff value of hsCRP level in our analyses. The cutoff values of hsCRP at admission, 1, and 12 months were calculated, respectively. And we defined the elevated hsCRP level as an hsCRP level higher than the cutoff value. We classified the patients according to the times of elevated hsCRP level at admission, 1, and 12 months: 0‐times (hsCRP level lower than the cutoff values in all 3 time points, reference group), 1‐time (hsCRP level higher than the cutoff value in 1 of the 3 time points), 2‐times (hsCRP levels higher than the cutoff values in 2 of the 3 time points), and 3‐times (hsCRP levels higher than the cutoff values in all 3 time points).

We used Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests to illustrate and compare the cumulative clinical outcome incidences across patient groups. We used multivariable Cox proportional hazard models to examine the association of mortality with cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and cumulative times of high hsCRP levels. The Cox models were adjusted for sex, age, comorbidities (coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, valvular heart disease, diabetes, anemia, reduced renal function), LVEF subtypes (HF with preserved ejection fraction, HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction, or HFrEF), HF presentation (decompensated chronic HF or new‐onset HF), 12‐month creatinine, NT‐proBNP level, and medications at 12‐month follow‐up (angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, aldosterone antagonists, and β‐blockers). We selected these adjusted covariates based on literature review and clinical experience. We defined noncardiovascular death as a competing risk when analyzing cardiovascular death risk. 26 In addition, restricted cubic splines were plotted to evaluate the dose–response relationship between cumulative hsCRP level and risks of all‐cause and cardiovascular deaths. 23

We examined the interactions of sex, age, coronary heart disease, new‐onset/decompensated chronic HF, LVEF subtypes, and NT‐proBNP level at 12 months after discharge, and performed subgroup analyses. Moreover, using the C‐index, integrated discrimination improvement, and net reclassification index, we evaluated the predictive performance improvement of adding hsCRP level to the Get With The Guidelines‐Heart Failure (Get With The Guidelines‐HF) score or NT‐proBNP at baseline, which were validated. 27

Furthermore, we conducted sensitivity analyses after excluding patients admitted to hospital with pneumonia at baseline, which potentially led to hsCRP level elevation, and re‐examined the association.

The rates of missing data were <5.0% (HbA1c). We imputed the missing data at baseline using the multiple imputation approach. Tests for statistical significance were 2‐sided with a level of 0.05. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SAS software (version 9.4 SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Among patients who survived within 1 year of discharge, the characteristics of included and excluded patients were presented in Table S1. Association Between Long‐Term Cumulative hsCRP and Outcomes

Over a median follow‐up of 4.8 (IQR, 4.2–5.1) years, 374 (29.2%) all‐cause death events occurred after 12 months of discharge. The Kaplan–Meier curves revealed an increase in the all‐cause death incidence across cumulative hsCRP level quartiles (quartile 1 [19.0%], quartile 2 [25.0%], quartile 3 [29.1%], quartile 4 [43.8%], log‐rank P<0.001; Figures 2A and 3) and cumulative times of high hsCRP levels (0‐times [16.2%], 1‐time [22.8%], 2‐times [29.9%], 3‐times [42.2%], log‐rank P<0.001; Figures 2B and 3). The adjusted association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and clinical outcomes were shown in Figure 3. Taking the quartile 1 as the reference, the hazard ratio (HR) was 1.29 (95% CI, 0.92–1.81) for quartile 2, 1.62 (95% CI, 1.16–2.25) for quartile 3, and 2.38 (95% CI, 1.75–3.23) for quartile 4 (P for trend <0.001). The HR was 1.13 (95% CI, 1.06–1.20) for each SD increase of long‐term cumulative hsCRP level. Similarly, compared with the patients with 0‐times of high hsCRP level, the all‐cause death risk increased in the other groups: 1‐time (HR, 1.36; [95% CI, 0.92–2.01]), 2‐times (HR, 1.95; [95% CI, 1.34–2.82]), and 3‐times (HR, 2.80; [95% CI, 1.97–4.00], P for trend <0.001). Additionally, a nonlinear dose‐response association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP level and all‐cause death risk was shown in Figure 4A (P for nonlinearity <0.001).

During the follow‐up, 293 (22.9%) patients experienced cardiovascular death. Patients with elevated long‐term hsCRP had higher cardiovascular mortality (cumulative hsCRP level: quartile 1 [14.3%], quartile 2 [19.7%], quartile 3 [22.8%], quartile 4 [34.7%], log‐rank P<0.001; Figures 2C and 3; cumulative times of high hsCRP levels: 0‐times [12.1%], 1‐time [18.3%], 2‐times [23.3%], 3‐times [33.2%], log‐rank P<0.001; Figures 2D and 3). In the multivariable analyses (Figure 3), compared with quartile 1, cardiovascular mortality was increased for quartile 2 (HR, 1.35; [95% CI, 0.92–1.98]), quartile 3 (HR, 1.70; [95% CI, 1.16–2.47]), and quartile 4 (HR, 2.57; [95% CI, 1.81–3.66]; P for trend <0.001). The HR for each cumulative hsCRP level SD increase was 1.14 (95% CI, 1.07–1.23). Taking patients with 0‐times of high hsCRP level as the reference, the adjusted HR was 1.46 (95% CI, 0.93–2.28) for 1‐time, 2.04 (95% CI, 1.34–3.12) for 2‐times, and 3.08 (95% CI, 2.05–4.62) for 3‐times (Figure 3; P for trend <0.001). In addition, the dose‐respond association between cumulative hsCRP level and cardiovascular mortality was also nonlinear (Figure 4B; P for nonlinearity <0.001).

Generally, there was no significant interaction of long‐term cumulative hsCRP level with most subgroups for both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality (Table S5).

Incremental Prognostic Value

Additionally, compared with the Get With The Guidelines‐HF score and NT‐proBNP at baseline, the all‐cause death risk prognostic capabilities of long‐term cumulative hsCRP level improved as measured by the C‐index. However, the prognostic performance did not improve significantly when adding hsCRP at admission to the Get With The Guidelines‐HF score or NT‐proBNP at baseline (Table S6).

Sensitivity Analyses

The cutoff values of high hsCRP levels were presented in Table S7, and the characteristics of included and excluded patients in sensitivity analyses were presented in Table S8. The association of mortality with long‐term cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and cumulative times of high hsCRP levels was consistent (Figures S4 and S5).

Discussion

This large prospective cohort study is the first to demonstrate the association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP and mortality in patients hospitalized for acute HF. Patients with the highest quartile had a 2.4‐fold higher all‐cause death risk and a 2.6‐fold higher cardiovascular death risk than those in the lowest quartile. Similarly, patients with 3‐times of high hsCRP levels had a 2.8‐fold higher all‐cause death risk and a 3.1‐fold higher cardiovascular death risk than those with 0‐times of high hsCRP level. These results were independent of conventional risk factors among patients with acute HF, and the association was consistent in most subgroups. Our findings suggest that repeated hsCRP assays could assist physicians in identifying patients with high a risk of death.

Our study provided novel insights into the association between mortality and long‐term cumulative hsCRP from admission to 12 months of discharge among patients with acute HF. Most previous studies reported that elevated hsCRP level at a single time point was associated with worse outcomes. 10 , 28 , 29 For example, Minami et al found that elevated CRP levels at admission among patients with acute HF were related to excess cardiac and noncardiac death risks during a 2‐year follow‐up. 28 Nishimoto et al revealed that patients with a high CRP level (>10 mg/L) at discharge had excess mortality within 1‐year. 29 And Kalogeropoulos et al observed that elevated hsCRP level at 30 days after discharge was related to high 180‐day mortality. 10 However, the association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP level and mortality remains to be elucidated. In this study, with the cumulative approach, we combined the duration and intensity of inflammation, and found that elevated long‐term cumulative hsCRP level was associated with higher mortality. Nevertheless, up to now, the mechanism between systemic inflammation and HF progression is not quite clear, and it might be multifactorial. 30 The possible mechanism might be explained as follows. Persistently high systemic inflammatory level increases microvascular endothelial inflammation and promotes the expression of adhesion molecules, which attracts circulating leukocytes, and leads to myofibroblast formation and interstitial collagen deposition. Endothelial inflammation could also lead to cardiomyocyte stiffness and hypertrophy by increasing reactive oxygen species and reducing NO bioavailability. Finally, the persistently high inflammatory level could induce systolic and diastolic dysfunction and adverse cardiac remodeling and finally contributes to the progression of HF. 8 Therefore, long‐term cumulative hsCRP level could be an indicator in identifying vulnerable patients with HF.

Although routine hsCRP measurements have not been recommended by clinical guidelines of HF, 16 , 31 our findings suggest that hsCRP assays could be useful in routine evaluation of patients with acute HF. The hsCRP measurement is convenient and simple, which makes it accessible and feasible even in medical resource‐limited settings. In the current analyses, we firstly evaluated the prognostic value of long‐term cumulative hsCRP level in patients with acute HF, and found that elevated long‐term cumulative hsCRP level was associated with excess all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. Moreover, long‐term cumulative hsCRP level could also moderately improve the predictive performance in mortality beyond the traditional risk score (Get With The Guidelines‐HF score) or NT‐proBNP level. With our findings, physicians could routinely measure hsCRP levels and identify high‐risk patients with HF.

Our study is strengthened by a large‐scale nationally prospective cohort with detailed baseline data and well‐maintained longitudinal follow‐up. Thus, we could calculate the long‐term cumulative hsCRP level from admission to 12 months after discharge and explore its association with clinical outcomes with multivariable adjustment. However, our study is also subject to several limitations. First, the observational design could not rule out the unmeasured confounders, although we had collected comprehensive high‐quality data and adjusted the known confounding factors in the current analyses. Second, patients who died within 1‐year were excluded in the current analyses, and our results could only reflect the association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP and mortality among patients who survived over 1 year. Third, only patients with complete hsCRP data at admission, 1 and 12 months after discharge were included in the analyses, which accounted for ≈30% of patients who survived at 12 months after discharge. And the different characteristics of the included and excluded patients might introduce selection bias. However, we further evaluated the interactions with several important subgroups and observed that most interactions were not statistically significant. Finally, we only included Chinese patients in this study, the generalizability to other populations should be with caution.

Conclusions

In summary, an elevated long‐term cumulative hsCRP level from admission to 12 months after discharge was associated with excess all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. These findings suggest that repeated hsCRP assays could assist physicians in identifying HF patients with a high risk of death.
