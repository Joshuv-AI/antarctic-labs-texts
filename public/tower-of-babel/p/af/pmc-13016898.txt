Inducibility of cyclic electron transport is linked to the transition from C3–C4 to C4 photosynthesis in Flaveria
Authors: Ying Wang, Aidi Luo, Ming-Ju Amy Lyu, Yu-Meng Wang, Yuhui Huang, Xiaoxiang Ni, Jianzhao Yang, Yuan Wen, Xin-Guang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolutionary convergence of complex biological features offers valuable insights into the interplay among environmental factors, organismal traits, and evolutionary outcomes. C4 photosynthesis exemplifies an adaptive syndrome derived from ancestral C3 photosynthesis in response to environmental stresses. In this study, we investigated the inducibility of C4 photosynthetic characteristics in various Flaveria species, including one of the youngest C4 species on Earth, under low CO2 conditions (100 ppm). Species used include F. robusta (proto-Kranz type), F. sonorensis (type I C3–C4), F. linearis (clade B C3–C4), F. ramosissima (type II C3–C4), and F. trinervia (C4). After 4 weeks of low CO2 treatment, F. sonorensis exhibited the highest inducibility of C4-related traits, as evidenced by enhanced chloroplast content in bundle sheath cells, reduced CO2 compensation point of photosynthesis (Г), increased apparent maximum carboxylation rate of Rubisco, and elevated cyclic electron transport. Conversely, F. linearis and F. ramosissima, despite possessing more preexisting C4-related traits, demonstrated less induction of C4-related features, with no significant enhancement of cyclic electron transport observed. These results indicate that environmental stresses can induce C4-related characteristics in C3–C4 intermediate species. Furthermore, an inducible cyclic electron transport may represent a critical precondition for the evolutionary transition from C3–C4 photosynthetic metabolism to a C4 type.

Introduction

Approximately 35 million years ago, declining atmospheric CO2 concentrations, coupled with increasing aridity and temperature, exerted strong selective pressures on terrestrial plants. In response, certain angiosperm lineages independently evolved C4 photosynthesis from the ancestral C3 type, a remarkable example of convergent evolution that has arisen in approximately 66 independent lineages over the past 30 million years (Ehleringer et al. 1991; Sage et al. 2011; Sage 2017). C4 photosynthesis enhances photosynthetic efficiency by concentrating CO2 around Rubisco, thereby minimizing photorespiration. This biochemical innovation confers superior light, water, and nitrogen use efficiencies, enabling C4 plants to thrive in warm and high-light environments (Zhu et al. 2008; Sage and Zhu 2011).

Unlike C3 photosynthesis, where carbon fixation occurs entirely within a single cell type, C4 photosynthesis employs a 2-cell CO2 concentration mechanism. In mesophyll cells (MCs), CO2 is initially fixed into 4-carbon acids—oxaloacetate (OAA), malate, or aspartate—by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) (Hatch et al. 1967). These C4 acids are then transported to adjacent bundle sheath cells (BSCs), where they are decarboxylated, releasing CO2 in proximity to Rubisco for efficient carbon fixation. This spatial separation of initial fixation and Calvin–Benson cycle reactions is supported by Kranz anatomy (Hatch 1987; Sedelnikova et al. 2018), a specialized leaf structure characterized by enlarged BSCs with high chloroplast density (Slack et al. 1969), increased vein density for efficient metabolite transport (Christin et al. 2013; Lundgren et al. 2014, 2019), and enhanced cyclic electron transport (CET) (Nakamura et al. 2013; Ishikawa et al. 2016; Munekage and Taniguchi 2016).

The evolution of C4 photosynthesis from C4 ancestors is thought to proceed through intermediate stages (Heckmann et al. 2013; Williams et al. 2013; Mallmann et al. 2014). The first step is the development of “proto-Kranz” anatomy, marked by enlarged BSCs' cross-sectional area and an increased number of chloroplasts and mitochondria (McKown and Dengler 2007; Sage et al. 2013). Although these plants still perform C3 photosynthesis, they often exhibit a slightly reduced CO2 compensation point (Γ). The next key step is the establishment of a photorespiratory glycine shuttle, achieved by positioning glycine decarboxylase (GDC) in BSCs. This results in the decarboxylation of glycine in BSCs, elevating local CO2 concentration and giving rise to C2 photosynthesis, so named because of the intercellular shuttling of the 2-carbon compound glycine (Monson et al. 1984; Sage et al. 2012). This stage is considered a critical evolutionary precursor to C4 photosynthesis (Monson et al. 1986; Edwards and Ku, 1987; Mallmann et al. 2014). C2 species are further classified into type I C3–C4 (minimal C4 cycle activity) and type II C3–C4 (moderate C4 cycle activity) intermediates (Edwards and Ku, 1987). Within this evolutionary trajectory, the proto-Kranz stage is characterized by increased organelle volume in the BSCs while GDC remains primarily in the mesophyll (M) cells. In contrast, the C2 stage is defined by the confinement of GDC to the BSC, which is further divided into sub-C2 (incomplete GDC localization) and full-C2 (the vast majority of GDC localized to the BSC) (Adachi et al. 2023).

Despite the apparent evolutionary trajectory, species that perform C2 photosynthesis have an increased likelihood of evolving C4 photosynthesis (Heckmann et al. 2013; Williams et al. 2013), but not all C2 lineages progress to C4. While 15 of the 20 known C2 lineages have given rise to C4 species, 5 have not (Walsh et al. 2023). Metabolic and ecological studies suggest that some C3–C4 intermediates may represent evolutionary stabilized endpoints rather than transient stages (Walsh et al. 2023; Lyu et al. 2025). For instance, C3–C4 species often exhibit superior environmental flexibility compared to both C3 and C4 plants, particularly in regions with high climatic seasonality (Lundgren and Christin 2017; Mercado and Studer 2022). This raises an intriguing evolutionary question: What preconditions determine whether a C2 intermediate can transition to full C4 photosynthesis, and which traits are inducible under environmental stress?

Phenotypic plasticity, i.e., the ability of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in response to environmental cues, may play a crucial role in this transition (Sultan 2000). Plasticity allows rapid physiological adjustment to stress, potentially serving as a bridge to genetic adaptation (Sultan 2000). In fluctuating environments, such plastic responses can be selectively advantageous (Nicotra et al. 2010). Over time, if conditions remain stable, these traits may become genetically assimilated through canalization (Murren et al. 2015). Given that low CO2 is a major driver of C4 evolution (Ehleringer et al. 1991; Christin et al. 2011; Sage et al. 2011; Mallmann et al. 2014), the capacity to plastically induce C4-like traits under low CO2 stress could be a key facilitator of the C3–C4 transition.

To investigate this, we used species from the genus Flaveria, which includes a well-characterized series of C3, C3–C4 intermediate, C4-like, and C4 species (Ku et al. 1991). Flaveria is a premier model system for studying C4 evolution (Sage et al. 2012; Mercado and Studer 2022), having provided critical insights into the evolution of leaf anatomy (McKown and Dengler 2007), biochemistry (Ku et al. 1991), gene regulatory networks (Lyu et al. 2023, 2025), and the role of photorespiration (Mallmann et al. 2014). Notably, it contains some of the youngest known C4 species, offering a window into recent evolutionary innovation (Sage et al. 2012). In this study, we grew multiple Flaveria intermediate species under long-term low CO2 conditions to test the following: (i) whether low CO2 stress induces C4-like anatomical and biochemical traits in different photosynthetic intermediate species and (ii) what traits are inducible and may serve as preconditions for the evolutionary transition to C4 photosynthesis.

Results

Transcriptomic response to long-term low CO2 treatment

The genus Flaveria comprises 4 major evolutionary lineages (Fig. 1a). The basal group includes 3 C3 species, among which F. robusta exhibits proto-Kranz anatomy, a structural precursor to C4 photosynthesis. A derived lineage contains 2 C3–C4 intermediate species: F. angustifolia and F. sonorensis, the latter is the earliest-evolved C3–C4 species in the genus, which displays a weak C2 photosynthetic glycine shuttle (Adachi et al. 2023). Subsequently, the genus diverges into 2 major clades. Clade B includes C3–C4 and C4-like species, with F. linearis showing population-level variation; notably, the Yucatán population exhibits a CO2 compensation point (Γ) as low as that of C₄ plants, despite lacking a fully operational C4 cycle (Adachi et al. 2023). In contrast, Clade A contains C3–C4, C4-like, and multiple C4 species, including F. ramosissima, the only C3–C4 species in this clade, and F. trinervia, one of the most extensively studied C4 species in the genus (Borghi et al. 2022; Adachi et al. 2023; Lyu et al. 2025). Based on this well-resolved phylogenetic and functional work, we selected 5 representative species, i.e., F. robusta, F. sonorensis, F. linearis (Yucatán), F. ramosissima, and F. trinervia, to investigate the physiological and biochemical responses to low CO₂ conditions (LCO2).

The 5 Flaveria species were grown under low CO2 conditions with a CO2 concentration of 100 ppm for 4 weeks. Among these 5 Flaveria species, F. robusta represents the proto-Kranz type, F. sonorensis represents the type I C3–C4 (hereafter I C3–C4), F. linearis represents the C3–C4 species from clade B (hereafter B C3–C4), F. ramosissima represents the type II C3–C4 (hereafter II C3–C4), and F. trinervia represents the C4 type. Compared with plants grown under control CO2 conditions (Ctrl), those grown under LCO2 showed stunted growth across the C3 and 2 C3–C4 species, but not in F. linearis and F. trinervia (Fig. 1b).

Notably, F. sonorensis (I C3–C4) displayed the most pronounced transcriptional adjustments among the tested species under reduced CO2 levels, as revealed by whole-transcriptome analysis (Fig. 1c). F. robusta (C3) demonstrated the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 3,568 genes downregulated and 2,794 genes upregulated under LCO2, indicating a substantial transcriptomic shift. F. sonorensis followed, with 2,637 downregulated and 2,355 upregulated genes, respectively. Conversely, F. ramosissima (II C3–C4) exhibited the least responsive transcriptome, evidenced by 1,785 downregulated and 1,283 upregulated genes under the same CO2 deprivation (Fig. 1d).

To compare the intersection of differently expressed (DE) genes among the 5 species, the protein of F. robusta was used as a reference, and the genes from the other 4 species were assigned to the ortholog in F. robusta (Fig. 1e). A core set of 85 consistently upregulated and 128 consistently downregulated genes across all 5 Flaveria species was identified in response to low CO2 concentrations. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted that downregulated genes were primarily linked to translation processes and ribosomal biogenesis (Fig. 1f), indicative of potential metabolic realignment to conserve resources or redirect energy toward stress-responsive pathways under reduced CO2 availability. On the other hand, the upregulated genes were enriched in categories related to chloroplast, water stress-response mechanisms, including water transport, vacuole, and response to abscisic acid (ABA) (Fig. 1f). These observations align with anticipated physiological adjustments, suggesting increased stomatal density and aperture as potential adaptive strategies to low CO2 environments.

Measurements on stomatal density of the 5 species confirmed this hypothesis. The stomata density was increased under low CO2 conditions for all 5 species (Fig. S1a). The stomatal density was gradually decreased from C3 to C3–C4 intermediate and to C4 species under Ctrl (Fig. S1b), consistent with a previous report in Flaveria species (Zhao et al. 2022b). As the gene controlling stomatal development was expressed in the young leaves, and the RNA-seq data were from mature leaves, we only investigated the expression change of genes related to stomatal opening. Prominent genes among these included Plasma Membrane Intrinsic Protein 2 (PIP2), a pivotal channel facilitating water and CO2 permeability (Israel et al. 2021); hydrogen ion ATPase (H+-ATPase), a master enzyme that serves as a H+ pump to generate an electrochemical gradient (Palmgren 2001); vacuole sucrose invertase 2 (VIN2), the vacuolar membrane-localized sucrose invertase (Ni 2012); and Phototropin 2 (PHOT2), a key regulator of blue light-induced stomatal responses (Grunwald et al. 2022) (Fig. S1c).

We also examined vein density in response to LCO2 across the 5 Flaveria species. No significant changes were observed between control and low CO2 conditions (Fig. S2), consistent with earlier findings in Alloteropsis semialata (Lundgren et al. 2019), suggesting that venation patterning in these species is developmentally constrained and lacks phenotypic plasticity under short- to medium-term CO2 limitation.

Low CO2-grown plants showed increased chloroplast content of BSCs

Compared to normal CO2-grown plants, plants grown under LCO2 showed an increased percentage of chloroplast area in BSC across all 5 species except for F. linearis, which showed a slight increase but not significant (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05, BH adjusted) (Fig. 2a and b). Specifically, the average planar chloroplast coverage was 2.1% in F. robusta (C3), 8.0% in F. sonorensis (I C3–C4), 24.1% in F. linearis (B C3–C4), 18.5% in F. ramosissima (II C3–C4), and 32.6% in F. trinervia (C4) for plants growing under Ctrl, whereas those values were 4.2% in F. robusta, 14.1% in F. sonorensis, 27.1% in F. linearis, 22% in F. ramosissima, and 44.7% in F. trinervia grown under LCO2.

Consistently, genes related to chlorophyll biosynthesis and chloroplast development were largely upregulated under LCO2 in the 5 species (Fig. S3). Specifically, CGA1 (GATA nitrate-inducible carbon metabolism-involved like, or GNC-like) and GNL (GNC-like, or CGA1) (Bi et al. 2005; Bastakis et al. 2018), CLB6 (chloroplast biogenesis 6) (Gutiérrez-Nava et al. 2004; Hsieh et al. 2014), as well as ARC5 (accumulation and replication of chloroplast 5) (Zhou et al. 2015), which are reported to directly and positively regulate chloroplast development, were upregulated under LCO2 in 4 of the 5 Flaveria species (Fig. 2c). Additionally, MCD1 (multiple chloroplast division Site 1), which determines the site of chloroplast division (Chen et al. 2018), was upregulated in F. sonorensis (I C3–C4), F. linearis (B C3–C4), and F. trinervia (C4) under LCO2 (Fig. 2c).

The responses of different photosynthetic components to low CO2 conditions

We then investigated whether the transcript abundance of C4 genes and their counterparts in the C3 and C3–C4 species was increased under LCO2. Fifteen C4 genes, including 9 encoding C4 enzymes and 6 encoding transporters, were quantified based on RNA-seq data (Lyu et al. 2025) (Fig. 3a). Given the multiplicity of gene family members, we specifically analyzed the C4 paralogs with the highest expression in C4 species, which were identified in an earlier report (Lyu et al. 2025) and termed as functional copies of C4 genes. Our results showed that carbonic anhydrase 1 (CA1) was upregulated across all Flaveria species under LCO2 except for F. linearis, while NADP-dependent malate dehydrogenase (NADP-MDH) was upregulated in the C3–C4 and C4 species. F. robusta (C3) displayed downregulation of 4 C4 genes, i.e., PEPC1, aspartate aminotransferase 5 (AspAT5), alanine aminotransferase 1 (AlaAT1), and bile acid sodium symporter 2 (BASS2), whereas F. sonorensis (I C3–C4) showed significant upregulation of 5 C4 enzymes under LCO2, i.e., CA1, NADP-MDH, pyruvate/orthophosphate dikinase regulator protein (PPDK-RP), and dicarboxylate transporter 1 (DiT1); besides, 2 C4 enzymes were upregulated in F. ramosissima (II C3–C4) and F. linearis (B C3–C4), and 4 C4 enzymes were upregulated in F. trinervia (C4) (fold change > 1.5, P < 0.05, “BH” adjusted) (Fig. 3a).

We then examined how enzymes and transporters in the photorespiratory pathway responded to LCO2. Among the 15 enzymes and 2 transporters, 7 were significantly upregulated in F. robusta (C3), 8 in F. sonorensis (I C3–C4), 2 in F. ramosissima (II C3–C4), 4 in F. linearis (B C3–C4), and 7 in F. trinervia (C4) (P < 0.05, fold change > 1.5, “BH” adjusted) (Fig. 3b). Therefore, both the C4 and the photorespiratory pathway showed the most profound induction in the type I C3–C4 species F. sonorensis and the least change in the type II C3–C4 species F. ramosissima.

Considering the low photosynthetic CO2 compensation points (Γ) of C4 species due to their efficient CO2 concentrating mechanism (CCM) and reduced photorespiration (Hatch 1987), we assessed whether LCO2 could modulate Γ in the 5 species at growth PPFD of 200 µmol m−2 s−1. Consistent with previous reports (Ku et al. 1991; Adachi et al. 2023), Γ progressively declined from F. robusta (C3) to F. sonorensis (I C3–C4), F. ramosissima (II C3–C4), and F. linearis (B C3–C4), with values of 56, 39, 21, and 3.5 μbar for F. robusta, F. sonorensis, F. ramosissima, and F. linearis under Ctrl (Fig. 3c and d). Intriguingly, LCO2 treatment led to a significant decrease in Γ for F. robusta and F. sonorensis, reaching 45 and 25 μbar, respectively, but not in F. ramosissima or F. linearis (Fig. 3c). Additionally, the initial slope of the A-Ci response curve, reflecting an estimate of the maximum carboxylation rate of Rubisco, i.e., Vcmax (von Caemmerer 2000), increased by 117% in F. sonorensis (from 0.058 under Ctrl to 0.11 under LCO2) and by 54% in F. ramosissima (from 0.058 under Ctrl to 0.090 under LCO2) and 20% in F. linearis (0.05 under Ctrl and 0.06 under LCO2), whereas initial slope showed no obvious changes in F. robusta between Ctrl and LCO2 (Fig. 3c). The Γ of F. trinervia (C4) could not be calculated as the A-Ci response curve was not linear. The initial slope of F. trinervia showed no significant change between Ctrl and LCO2 (Fig. 3d).

The response of CET to low CO2 conditions

C4 species show higher CET activity than C3 and C3–C4 species, playing a crucial role in meeting the increased ATP/NADPH demand for C4 metabolism (Kanai and Edwards 1999; Nakamura et al. 2013). Two alternative CETs in higher plants have been documented (Shikanai 2007). One pathway is mediated by flavodoxin-like quinone reductase (FQR), involving 2 proteins, i.e., proton gradient regulation (PGR5) (Munekage et al. 2002) and PGR5-like (DalCorso et al. 2008), while the other is mediated by the NADH dehydrogenase (NDH) complex (Mi et al. 1995). As NDH-mediated CET was reported as the main functional pathway in Flaveria C4 species (Ogawa et al. 2023), we tested how the NDH-mediated CET pathways respond to low CO2 concentration treatment.

To assess the NDH-mediated CET, we exposed dark-adapted leaves to actinic light (AL) for 5 min, inducing a transient post-illumination rise (PIR) in chlorophyll fluorescence upon turning off the light (Fig. 4a). This PIR was attributed to the NDH-dependent reduction of the plastoquinone (PQ) pool upon the light being off (Mi et al. 1995). The C3 species F. robusta, type I C3–C4 species F. sonorensis, and C4 species F. trinervia showed a significant increase in PIR when grown under LCO2 conditions; however, the C3–C4 species F. ramosissima and F. linearis, which inherently exhibited a high PIR, displayed no notable change between plants grown under Ctrl and LCO2 (2-tailed t-test, P < 0.05, BH adjusted) (Fig. 4a and b).

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the expression levels of PGR5 and numerous NDH subunits were higher in both C3–C4 and C4 species compared to C3 species (Fig. 4c). Low CO2 concentration treatments resulted in increased transcript levels of CET-associated genes in F. robusta (C3) and F. sonorensis (I C3–C4). Specifically, in F. robusta (C3), PGR5 and 13 out of 22 annotated NDH complex genes showed increased expression levels under LCO2. In F. sonorensis, both PGR5 and PGR5-LIKE, along with 12 NDH complex genes, showed significant upregulation in transcript abundance under LCO2 (fold change > 1.5, P < 0.05, BH adjusted) (Fig. 4d). Notably, 8 NDH complex genes demonstrated upregulated expression in both F. robusta (C3) and F. sonorensis (I C3–C4) under LCO2, i.e., NdhN, NdhO, PnsB2, PnsL2, PnsL4, NdhU, Ndhv, and CRR6 (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the increased transcript abundances of CET-associated genes were not significant in F. ramosissima (II C3–C4), F. linearis (B C3–C4), or F. trinervia (C4). It is worth mentioning that more than 10 CET-associated genes showed increased transcript abundances in F. trinervia (C4) (Fig. 3d), whereas, as the expression level of CET genes is already high in F. trinervia, the statistic is not significant as the fold changes are less than 1.5.

To further investigate whether CET is inducible in C3, C3–C4, and C4 species, we subjected 3 additional Flaveria species to LCO2 treatment for 4 weeks: i.e., F. pringlei (C3), F. angustifolia (I C3–C4), and F. bidentis (C4). Phylogenetically, F. pringlei is the sister species to F. robusta (C3) and represents an early-diverging C3 lineage, while F. angustifolia is sister to F. sonorensis and belongs to the sub-C2 stage. F. bidentis is the closest relative of the C4 species F. trinervia (Fig. 1a). All 3 species experienced slight growth limitations under LCO2 conditions (Fig. 4e). Under Ctrl conditions, F. pringlei (C3) exhibited lower PIR than F. bidentis (C4), whereas F. angustifolia (I C3–C4) showed PIR levels comparable to those of the C4 species (Fig. 4f and g). Upon exposure to LCO2, both F. pringlei and F. bidentis significantly increased their PIR, indicating inducible CET under photorespiratory stress. In contrast, F. angustifolia showed no significant change in PIR under LCO2.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that LCO2 stimulates CET in C3 plants, the type I C3–C4 species F. sonorensis and C4 species, as evidenced by both physiological assessments and transcriptomic profiles. However, CET in F. ramosissima, F. linearis, and F. angustifolia, which inherently exhibit high CET levels (Nakamura et al. 2013), was not further augmented by LCO2.

CET genes show a greater degree of RNA level and less of a protein level in response to low CO2 conditions in F. sonorensis

F. sonorensis (I C3–C4) showed decreased Γ and increased CET under low CO2 conditions, which are the 2 major traits for the evolution of C4 photosynthesis. To uncover the regulatory mechanisms underlying its acclimation to low CO2, we performed an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis for F. sonorensis. Proteomes were profiled from F. sonorensis leaves grown under Ctrl and LCO2, with 3 biological replicates per treatment. A total of 10,561 proteins were quantified, of which 9,942 were assigned unique identifiers. Global comparisons of RNA-seq and proteomic data revealed a moderate positive correlation between transcript and protein abundance, with a Pearson r of 0.58 under Ctrl and of 0.61 under LCO2 (Fig. 5a and b), indicating a general concordance between transcript and protein levels across the genome.

To identify genes subject to distinct regulatory regimes, we calculated the protein-to-RNA ratio (PTR) for each gene and classified them based on global distribution: Genes with PTR higher than mean + SD were defined as high PTR and those with PTR less than mean - SD as low PTR. While photorespiratory genes were predominantly distributed within the median PTR range, CET-related genes tended toward lower PTR values, with 3—PGR5, PGR5-like, and PnsB5—falling into the low PTR category (Fig. 5a and b).

We further compared the responses of CET and photorespiratory pathway genes at RNA and protein levels (Fig. 5c and d). Both CET genes and photorespiratory genes displayed variable RNA and protein responsive patterns: Although many showed transcriptional upregulation under LCO2, corresponding increases in protein abundance were not observed, such as NdhM, NdhL, PnsL2, and NdhT, which showed an increase in RNA level higher than 1.5-fold, but almost no change in protein levels, e.g., PnsB5 was down regulated under LCO2 by 1.6-fold at protein level but no change in RNA level. Notably, even among genes that showed coordinated changes in both RNA and protein, the magnitude of change was consistently smaller at the protein level compared to RNA. For example, the CET protein NdhM showed a 2.5-fold increase in RNA level, but only a 1.3-fold increase in protein level, and for the photorespiratory genes Fd-GOGAT, showing a 1.4-fold increase at RNA level, but a 1.1-fold increase in protein level (Fig. 5c and d).

To systematically classify gene regulatory patterns, we identified DEGs (edgeR, “BH” corrected, P < 0.05, fold change ≥ 1.5) and proteins (DEPs, t-test, “BH”-corrected, P < 0.05, fold change ≥ 1.2) and grouped them into 6 groups based on their RNA-protein dynamics (Fig. 5e). These were consolidated into 4 broad regulatory types: Consistent: RNA and protein changed in the same direction (Groups 1: R_u_P_u and Group 2: R_d_P_d, here, R refers to RNA, P to protein, u to upregulated, d to downregulated, same below); Divergent: RNA and protein changed in opposite directions (Group 3 and Group 4: R_u_P_d, R_d_P_u); RNA-only: significant RNA change without significant protein change (Group 5: R_only); and Protein-only: significant protein change without significant RNA change (Group 6: P_only).

Across these groups, RNA-only genes exhibited higher baseline RNA and protein levels, a feature of most photosynthetic genes in leaves (Mergner et al. 2020; Lyu et al. 2025). Protein-only genes had significantly lower RNA and protein levels (Fig. 5f and g). Interestingly, RNA-only and R_u_P_d groups were enriched in photosynthesis-related terms (Fig. S4), implying that photosynthesis, including CET genes, showed more transcript changes than protein level, highlighting a multi-layered regulatory network for photosynthesis genes, including CET genes, in response to low CO2.

Functional characterization via GO enrichment analysis (Fisher's exact test, “BH”-corrected P < 0.05) revealed distinct biological roles for each regulatory group (Fig. S4). The R_u_P_u group was enriched in apoplast and jasmonic acid response, while R_d_P_d genes were associated with Golgi apparatus, ion transport, and vacuolar functions. Both R_u_P_d and RNA-only groups were enriched in photosynthesis-related terms, consistent with known post-transcriptional regulation of photosynthetic genes in Arabidopsis and Flaveria (Mergner et al. 2020; Lyu et al. 2025). Notably, RNA-only genes were linked to stomatal movement. In contrast, the protein-only group was enriched in catalytic activity and protein retention in the ER lumen. Together, these findings highlight a multi-layered regulatory network fine-tuning gene expression in response to LCO2.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the physiological and molecular responses of Flaveria species spanning a gradient of C4 evolutionary progression to long-term low CO2 conditions (LCO2). Our results reveal significant differences in the capacity of C3 and C3–C4 species to induce C4-associated traits under LCO2. Notably, we observed striking variation in the inducibility of CET, suggesting that the plasticity of CET in response to abiotic stresses such as LCO2, high light, or drought may represent a critical precondition for the transition from C3–C4 intermediate metabolism to full C4 photosynthesis.

Differential induction of C4-related traits across species

Our findings demonstrate that F. sonorensis, a type I C3–C4 species, exhibits the most pronounced induction of C4-related features under LCO2. This includes a significant increase in BSC chloroplast content (Fig. 2), upregulation of key C4 pathway genes (Fig. 3a), a marked reduction in the CO2 compensation point (Γ; Fig. 3c and d), and enhanced CET and expression of CET-related genes (Fig. 4). Similarly, the C3 species F. robusta showed moderate but significant induction of C4-like traits under LCO2, including increased BSCs' chloroplast density (Fig. 2), downregulated Γ (Fig. 3c), enhanced CET, and elevated transcript levels of CET genes (Fig. 4). The observed Γ reduction in F. robusta likely reflects the emergence of a partial C2 cycle, consistent with its increased BSC chloroplast content. In F. sonorensis, the lower Γ may indicate an enhanced C2 cycle, where the efficiency of glycine shuttling to the BSCs directly correlates with the degree of Γ suppression (Bellasio and Farquhar 2019). Although leaf day respiration (Ra) typically ranges from 0.5 to 2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (Atkin et al. 2015; Fan et al. 2024) and may be modulated under LCO2, such respiratory changes are unlikely to account for the magnitude of Γ decline observed. Thus, we conclude that long-term LCO2 exposure genuinely promotes functional enhancements in C2 metabolism in both F. robusta and F. sonorensis, with the latter exhibiting a more advanced intermediate phenotype.

The transcriptional and physiological responses observed in Flaveria under LCO2 align with stress acclimation mechanisms found in distantly related C3 plants. For instance, Arabidopsis thaliana, a species with no C4 preconditions, responds to low CO2 by increasing stomatal density (Li et al. 2014), accumulating NH4⁺ and ornithine (Blume et al. 2019; Miao et al. 2024), inducing genes involved in photorespiratory nitrogen recycling, and activating ABA signaling (You et al. 2020). These similar responses support the hypothesis that the evolution of C3–C4 and C4 photosynthesis co-opts preexisting stress-response pathways and gene regulatory networks present in ancestral C3 plants (Reyna-Llorens and Hibberd 2017; Swift et al. 2024; Dickinson et al. 2025) Thus, the emergence of C4 physiology may not require novel regulators but rather the rewiring and amplification of conserved modules to support new metabolic configurations.

Interestingly, despite possessing more constitutive C4-like anatomical and biochemical traits than F. sonorensis, including higher PEPC expression (Mallmann et al. 2014), reduced vein spacing (McKown and Dengler 2007), enlarged BSCs (Nakamura et al. 2013), and elevated BSCs' chloroplast content (Fig. 2), the C3–C4 species F. linearis and F. ramosissima exhibited lower inducibility of C4 features under LCO2, including CET and Γ. This suggests that the capacity to acclimate to low CO2 is not simply a function of existing C4 preconditions but is instead species-specific, likely governed by underlying regulatory or physiological constraints. This pattern echoes findings in other systems: for example, no Γ reduction was observed in the C3 grass A. semialata under LCO2, despite its close phylogenetic relationship to C4 lineages (Lundgren et al. 2019). Together, these results highlight that while anatomical traits such as vein density may be relatively fixed in Flaveria (Fig. S2) and A. semialata (Lundgren et al. 2019), metabolic and physiological plasticity varies across species, underscoring the importance of lineage-specific regulatory capacity in shaping the evolvability of C4 traits.

The above result suggests that the capacity to adjust C4-related traits in response to LCO2 is species-specific and likely depends on underlying physiological preconditions.

We propose that such differences may be linked to the inducibility of specific traits, such as CET, which is required to enable an effective CO2 concentration mechanism. Notably, Lundgren et al. (2014) argued that differential plasticity among plant lineages may explain why C4 photosynthesis has evolved repeatedly in some clades (e.g., Flaveria, Amaranthus) but not others (Lundgren et al. 2014). Thus, while anatomical plasticity (e.g., vein density) might be low across these lineages (Fig. S2) (Lundgren et al. 2019), metabolic and physiological responses vary, highlighting that the inducibility of C4-like traits is contingent on lineage-specific regulatory or functional capacities.

CET as a key enabler for the transition from C3–C4 to C4 metabolism

A key distinction between F. sonorensis and the other C3–C4 species (F. ramosissima, F. linearis) is the inducibility of CET under LCO2 (Fig. 4). C4 photosynthesis imposes a higher ATP/NADPH demand than C3 photosynthesis due to the ATP-dependent regeneration of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) via PEP carboxykinase or pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase (PPDK) (Kramer and Evans 2011; Munekage and Taniguchi 2016; Bellasio and Farquhar 2019). To meet this increased ATP demand, C4 species typically rely on enhanced CET around Photosystem I, which generates ATP without producing NADPH, thereby balancing the energy budget (Ogawa et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). Besides, CET plays a vital role in adjusting the ATP/NADPH ratio and maintaining cellular redox balance, especially under stress conditions (Allen 2003).

The selective induction of CET in F. sonorensis, but not in the other C3–C4 species, suggests that CET plasticity may be a critical functional trait enabling the transition from the C2 cycle to a functional C4 pathway. This idea is further supported by synthetic biology efforts: Engineering a complete C4 shuttle into C3 plants requires coordinated overexpression of multiple enzymes (CA, PEPC, PPDK, MDH, NADP-ME) in specific cell types (Ermakova et al. 2021). However, overexpression of NADP-malate dehydrogenase (NADP-ME) often leads to leaf bleaching and photo-oxidative damage due to excessive NADPH production (Tsuchida et al. 2001). Enhanced CET could mitigate this by consuming excess reducing power while simultaneously supplying the additional ATP needed for PEP regeneration (Zhao et al. 2022a; Zhang et al. 2024).

Post-transcriptional regulation and stability of CET components

We observed a moderate correlation between transcript and protein abundance in F. sonorensis (Pearson r = 0.58 to 0.61), indicating general concordance but also significant post-transcriptional regulation (Fig. 5a and b). Notably, changes in protein levels were often dampened compared to transcriptomic changes, particularly for proteins related to photosynthesis (Fig. 5c and d). This buffering effect likely reflects the long half-lives and high stability of many photosynthetic complexes, especially those containing subunits encoded by the chloroplast genome (Floris et al. 2013; Fankhauser and Aubry 2017). For example, subunits of the NDH complex have slow turnover rates, leading to delayed proteome dynamics under changing conditions (Bienvenut et al. 2011; Nelson and Millar 2015).

Specifically, we observed a tendency toward lower protein-to-RNA ratios (PTR) for CET-related proteins (Fig. 5a and b), which may reflect tight post-transcriptional control. Given that CET relies on multi-subunit complexes, e.g., the NDH complex comprises over 40 subunits (Shikanai 2020), the function of CET is likely regulated at multiple levels, including assembly, complex stability in addition to the commonly discussed transcription, post-transcription, translation, and post-translational controls, which are all needed to fine-tune CET capacity to achieve a dynamic adjustment of cyclic electron flow in response to fluctuating environmental cues.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Plants of F. robusta (C3, NA) and F. ramosissima (II C3–C4, NA) were from Prof. Peter Westhoff (Heinrich-Heine-University, Germany), and plants of F. pringlei (C3), F. sonorensis (I C3–C4, NA), F. linearis (B C3–C4, Yucatan), F. angustifolia (I C3–C4, UT-Grown), F. trinervia (C4, Plantation Key), F. bidentis (C4, G. Edwards) were from Prof. Rowan F. Sage (University of Toronto, Canada). The low CO2 treatment experiment was conducted as described in previous work (Lyu et al. 2023). Specifically, plants were grown from cuttings in a growth chamber under controlled CO2 conditions (380 ppm) for 2 weeks to establish roots. During this period, the light intensity was set at 200 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1, the temperature was maintained at 22 °C, the photoperiod was set to 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness, and the relative humidity was kept at 70%. During the experiment, a relatively cool temperature and moderate light intensity were used, as it was reported that high temperatures can induce stomatal closure, which would hinder gas exchange under low CO2 conditions and make long-term low CO2 experiments unfeasible (Vogan and Sage 2012). Additionally, there is evidence suggesting that the evolution of C4 photosynthesis occurred during periods of relatively cooler global temperatures (Sage 2003). Moreover, such conditions were tested to accommodate long-term exposure to low CO2 conditions for 1 month without being constrained by chamber size limitations (Li et al. 2014; Miao et al. 2024).

For the low CO2 treatment (low CO2 conditions, LCO2), plants were then transferred to an environmentally controlled growth chamber where the CO2 concentration was adjusted to 100 ppm. All other conditions, including light intensity, temperature, photoperiod, and relative humidity, remained the same as those mentioned above. Plants that continued to grow in the chamber with a CO2 concentration of 380 ppm served as the control group (control CO2 conditions, Ctrl). All RNA-seq data of F. robusta, F. sonorensis, F. ramosissima, F. linearis, and F. trinervia used in this study were from previous work (Lyu et al. 2023), and gene expression levels were calculated as described in an early study (Lyu et al. 2025). Specifically, RNA-seq raw reads were aligned to the genome sequence of the 5 species with star (Dobin et al. 2013), and gene expression levels were calculated by applying RSEM (Li and Dewey 2011). Orthologs of CET and photorespiratory genes were annotated based on Orthofinder (Emms and Kelly 2019). For this, with multiple gene copies in Flaveria species, the gene with the highest expression level was used for comparison.

Measurement of the BSC chloroplast content applying transmission electron microscopy

To assess chloroplast content in BSCs, leaf samples were harvested and cut into 2 mm × 5 mm cross-sectional segments, avoiding first- and second-order veins. Samples were immediately fixed in 0.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), subjected to vacuum infiltration for 30 min to ensure complete penetration, and stored overnight at 4 °C. Following primary fixation, samples were embedded in Eponate 12 resin (Ted Pella, Inc., USA). Ultrathin sections (∼50 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, collected on copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance membrane and organelle contrast. Sections were examined using a transmission electron microscope (H-7650, Hitachi) operated at 120 kV. Digital images were captured for analysis. The planar areas of BSCs and their chloroplasts were quantified from transmission electron microscopy images using ImageJ (Schneider et al. 2012). As most BSCs in F. robusta (C3) contain no chloroplasts, we only counted BSCs containing chloroplasts in the 5 species. Chloroplast content per BSC was calculated as the ratio of the total chloroplast area to the corresponding BSC area. Three biological replicates were used, and more than 15 BSCs were analyzed per replicate.

Light microscopy and anatomical analysis

Stomatal density was measured using epidermal peels taken from the abaxial side of the leaf and visualized under light microscopy. For each condition, 3 independent plants were analyzed, with 3 fields of view selected per plant for stomatal counting.

Vein patterns were examined in leaves grown under control (Ctrl) and low CO2 conditions (LCO2). Leaves were cleared by incubation in 100% ethanol at 60 °C, with ethanol refreshed periodically until chlorophyll was fully removed and veins were clearly visible under the microscope. Leaves were then put into 70% ethanol to avoid breaking the leaf. Leaves were then dyed with 0.1% safra-O for 1 min before vein counting. Vein density was quantified on transverse leaf sections, avoiding first- and second-order veins to ensure consistent sampling of the minor venation network. Sections were imaged using light microscopy, and vein density was calculated as the total length of minor veins (third-order and higher) per unit area. For each species and treatment, measurements were taken from at least 3 biological replicates.

Measurement of photosynthetic CO2 compensation point

The CO2 compensation point (Γ) was determined by measuring the photosynthetic CO2 net uptake rate (A) under a series of CO2 concentrations and then extrapolating A in the initial CO2 responsive curve to identify the intersection with the X-axis. The photosynthetic CO2 net uptake rate was measured with an infrared gas analyzer LiCor-6400 (LI-COR Biosciences, Nebraska, USA) at 4 CO2 concentrations, namely, 120 μmol mol−1, 90 μmol mol−1, 60 μmol mol−1, and 35 μmol mol−1. During measurement, plants were first kept at ambient CO2 concentration (380 μmol mol−1) until gs was stable, and then the A was measured soon at 4 CO2 concentrations, and photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) was set as 200 μmol m−2 s−1, and air flow rate was set as 250 μmol s−1. The block temperature was set as 25 °C, under which setting the reported leaf temperature is around 21 to 23 °C.

Measurement of PIR of Chl Fluorescence

Chl fluorescence was measured following the protocol developed by Schreiber et al. (Schreiber et al. 1986, 1988) with a pulse amplitude-modulated fluorimeter (Dual-PAM100; Walz, Effeltrich, Germany). Plants were dark-adapted overnight at room temperature. Fully expanded leaves, usually the second or the third pair of leaves counted from the top, were used for measurements. The modulated non-actinic measuring light beam, measure light (ML) (1.6 kHz), was switched on to obtain the dark fluorescence level or initial fluorescence (F0), where the PSII centers remain in an open state. The transient post-illumination increase in Chl fluorescence was recorded after the termination of the 5-min illumination under actinic light (AL) (170 μmol m−2 s−1), with slight modification as described in (Shikanai et al. 1998; Essemine et al. 2016). The PIR represents the reduction of the PQ pool in the dark by electrons from the NDH pathway. The PIR was assessed as the increase in the amplitude of fluorescence after turning off AL and in the presence of merely the ML.

Proteomics sample preparation

For proteomic analysis, leaves of Flaveria sonorensis were collected and followed by immediate flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen. For each of the 3 biological replicates, exactly 100 mg of frozen leaf tissue was weighed and pulverized into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen, with the addition of 20 mg of polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP).

Total proteins were extracted using a pre-cooled buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 100 mM EDTA [pH 8.0], 50 mM borax, 50 mM ascorbic acid, 30% sucrose [w/v], 1% Triton-X100 [v/v], 10 mM TCEP, 1 mM PMSF, and Roche protease/phosphatase inhibitors) at a 1:3 (w/v) ratio. After 60 min of sonication at 4 °C and centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min), the supernatant was recovered and mixed with an equal volume of Tris-saturated phenol (pH 8.0). The phenolic phase was isolated, washed twice with extraction buffer, and proteins were precipitated overnight at −20 °C using 0.1 M ammonium acetate in methanol (1:5 v/v). After 3 washes with chilled 90% acetone, the protein pellet was redissolved in 8 M urea to a concentration of 5 to 10 μg/μL, as determined by BCA assay. For digestion, 200 μg of protein was reduced with 10 mM TCEP and alkylated with 50 mM chloroacetamide at 37 °C for 30 min. After diluting the urea concentration to 0.8 M with NH4HCO3, trypsin was added at a 1:50 ratio for 2 h, followed by a second 1:50 addition for overnight digestion at 37 °C. The reaction was quenched with 1% TFA, and the resulting peptides were desalted via a C18 column.

LC-MS/MS analysis for proteomics

The proteomic profiles of Flaveria sonorensis were analyzed using a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled with a nanoElute system. For total proteome analysis, 50 μg of the desalted peptide sample was vacuum-dried and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid (FA). Peptides were separated on an IonOptics analytical column (25 cm × 75 μm, 1.7 μm) at a constant temperature of 50 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% FA in water (Phase A) and 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (Phase B). Separation was performed at a flow rate of 300 nL/min using a 60-min linear gradient: 2% to 22% B over 45 min, 22% to 37% B over 5 min, and 37% to 80% B over 5 min, followed by a 5-min wash at 80% B.

The mass spectrometer was operated in diaPASEF mode. The MS scan range was set from 319.5 to 1,159.5 Da, with an isolation window of 20 Da and an ion mobility (1/K0) range of 0.71 to 1.33 V·s/cm2. Collision energy was ramped linearly from 20 eV (at 1/K0 = 0.6 V·s/cm2) to 59 eV (at 1/K0 = 1.6 V·s/cm2) in accordance with the ion mobility.

All DIA raw data were processed using Spectronaut 18 with default BGS Factory Settings. A Flaveria sonorensis protein database was utilized for peptide identification. Trypsin was specified as the digestive enzyme. Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation (M) and phosphorylation (S, T, Y, only for phosphoproteomics) were included as variable modifications. Both precursor and protein identifications were filtered at a 1.0% false discovery rate (FDR). Decoy sequences were generated using a mutated strategy (shuffling amino acid sequences with a minimum length of 2 and maximum of half the peptide length). Data normalization was conducted using the local normalization strategy based on the average peak area of the top 3 peptides (FDR < 1.0%).
