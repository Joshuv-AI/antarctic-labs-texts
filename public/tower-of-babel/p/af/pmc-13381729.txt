Cutaneous Clues to a Silent Malignancy: Tripe Palm and Malignant Acanthosis Nigricans Unveiling Gastric Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Manisha Gartaula, Anupa Khadka, Laxman Chapagain, Ashu Sharma, Pooja Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe sudden onset of malignant acanthosis nigricans and tripe palm, even in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, should prompt immediate evaluation for internal malignancy, particularly gastric adenocarcinoma, as early dermatologic clues can be lifesaving.Keywords: adenocarcinoma, malignant acanthosis nigricans, paraneoplastic syndrome, tripe palm

Background

Cutaneous manifestations of internal malignancies can provide critical clues for early diagnosis, enabling timely treatment and potentially delaying or preventing disease progression.

Malignant acanthosis nigricans (MAN) is a well‐recognized paraneoplastic dermatosis, characterized by the sudden onset of hyperpigmented, hyperkeratotic, velvety plaques that primarily affect body folds and flexural areas [1]. While acanthosis nigricans is commonly associated with insulin resistance, its rapid development in adults should raise suspicion for an underlying malignancy. The most common malignancy associated with MAN includes adenocarcinomas, especially gastric adenocarcinoma, followed by pancreatic, ovarian, lung, esophageal, breast, renal cell, and bladder cancers [2].

Tripe palm (TP), also known as acanthosis palmaris, is a rare paraneoplastic dermatosis presenting with a wrinkled, velvety, and ridged appearance of the palms. It is considered a highly specific marker for malignancy, mostly associated with gastric and pulmonary cancers. The co‐occurrence of tripe palm and malignant acanthosis nigricans is strongly indicative of an internal malignancy and warrants thorough evaluation [1]. Tripe palm occurs before or concurrently with the cancer diagnosis of patients [3].

Here, we report a case of a 47‐year‐old man who presented with tripe palm and malignant acanthosis nigricans, leading to the diagnosis of locally aggressive gastric adenocarcinoma despite the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms. This case highlights the crucial role of dermatologic findings in the early detection of malignancy, particularly in asymptomatic patients.

Case Report

A 47‐year‐old man with a normal BMI presented to the Dermatology OPD with a six‐month history of progressive darkening of body folds and creases. Initially, the lesions appeared on the neck and axilla, but they rapidly spread to involve the groin and lower abdomen. He also reported progressive darkening and thickening of both palms, associated with itching. Additionally, he had unintentional weight loss of six kilograms over 6 months; however, he remained otherwise asymptomatic and was able to perform his daily activities without limitation.

There was no history of abdominal pain, generalized weakness, hematemesis, melena, anorexia, or vomiting. His past medical and family history was unremarkable.

On examination, his general condition was fair and vitals were stable. There were hyperkeratotic, hyperpigmented papules over the dorsum of both hands with accentuated dermatoglyphics (Figure 1A,B).

(A, B) Tripe palm, (C) Acanthosis nigricans, (D) Oral papillomatosis.

There was the presence of leathery hyperpigmentation over the nape of the neck, axilla, groin, and lower abdomen (Figure 1C).

Few skin‐colored papules were present over bilateral angles of mouth (Figure 1D).

A provisional diagnosis of malignant acanthosis nigricans with tripe palm and oral papillomatosis was made.

Baseline investigations were sent, which were within normal limits, and the patient was referred to an oncologist to rule out internal malignancy. Work up for malignancy was done by the oncology department, which revealed raised CA 19.9 (> 1200 U/mL), and whole‐body PET scan revealed a suspicious gastric mass with nodal metastases. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of gastric adenocarcinoma (Figure 2A,B).

(A, B) Histopathological examination from tumor resected from gastric mucosa Hematoxylin and eosin stain 400X revealing tumor cells arranged in diffuse sheets, nests arising from mucosa and invading the submucosa, muscularis propria.

Management and Outcome

The patient underwent perioperative chemotherapy, followed by D2 total gastrectomy.

At the 4‐month follow‐up after treatment, the patient demonstrated regression of the cutaneous manifestations of tripe palms and oral papillomatosis (Figure 3A–C). The regression of tripe palms and oral papillomatosis following treatment of the underlying malignancy further supports their paraneoplastic nature.

Discussion

Paraneoplastic dermatoses serve as early warning signs of underlying malignancies, often preceding systemic symptoms. Among these, malignant acanthosis nigricans (MAN) and tripe palm (TP) are well‐documented markers, most commonly associated with gastric (29%) and pulmonary (20%) malignancies [1]. Their presence should prompt an urgent search for an underlying cancer, even in asymptomatic individuals.

Sinha and Schwartz classified acanthosis nigricans into eight subtypes: obesity‐associated, benign, syndromic, malignant, unilateral, acral, drug‐induced, and mixed [4]. MAN is characterized by rapid‐onset, velvety hyperpigmentation of flexural areas, differing from the more common benign acanthosis nigricans associated with metabolic disorders [3]. Patients may also develop papillomatous hyperplasia involving the periorbital and perioral regions, along with brittle nails exhibiting longitudinal ridging [5]. The mechanism behind MAN is thought to involve tumor‐secreted growth factors, particularly transforming growth factor‐alpha (TGF‐α), which activate epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFRs), leading to keratinocyte proliferation [3]. Furthermore, hormones secreted by the tumor may stimulate the release of melanocyte‐stimulating hormone, thereby inducing the proliferation of melanocytes, fibroblasts, and keratinocytes [5].

In the majority of MAN cases (61.3%), malignancy is diagnosed simultaneously. In about 17.6% of cases, acanthosis nigricans (AN) appears before the cancer is identified, while in roughly 21.1% of cases, it develops after the diagnosis of cancer [6].

Tripe palm (TP), also known as acanthosis palmaris, is a rare paraneoplastic dermatosis characterized by thickened, velvety ridges on the palms, resembling the texture of tripe (cow stomach lining). It is highly specific for malignancy, particularly gastric adenocarcinoma, and is often seen in conjunction with MAN [3]. Tripe palms are most commonly associated with malignancies of the gastrointestinal tract, lungs, bladder, breast, female genital tract, ovaries, and kidneys. The mechanism is similar to MAN [7]. The onset of tripe palms precedes the diagnosis of the underlying malignancy in more than 40% of cases, occurs concurrently (within 1 month) in 37%, and follows the diagnosis in 19% of patients [4].

The course of the cutaneous lesions often parallels that of the underlying tumor, with successful treatment resulting in regression of both, while recurrence of the skin lesions may indicate tumor relapse [5].

Their recognition should therefore prompt a thorough evaluation for occult malignancy, as early diagnosis and timely treatment may improve outcomes. Although their prognostic significance remains uncertain, these lesions may serve as useful clinical markers of disease activity, warranting increased awareness and further research [8].

In our patient, the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms could have delayed the cancer diagnosis if not for the early recognition of these cutaneous signs. This highlights the critical role of dermatologists in detecting malignancy at an early stage, potentially improving outcomes [9].

The resolution of MAN and TP following surgical resection further supports their role as paraneoplastic markers, often regressing once the primary malignancy is treated [10].

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing paraneoplastic dermatoses as potential early indicators of malignancy. The recognition of these paraneoplastic changes should prompt a thorough evaluation for occult cancer, particularly in patients with rapidly progressive, extensive, or multiple cutaneous lesions accompanied by systemic features such as unexplained weight loss. Early identification of these manifestations facilitates timely diagnosis and treatment of the underlying malignancy, which may improve clinical outcomes. The presence of tripe palm and malignant acanthosis nigricans led to the early detection of locally advanced gastric adenocarcinoma, despite the absence of systemic symptoms. A high index of suspicion and timely investigation of such dermatologic findings can significantly impact patient prognosis.
