Autistic traits linked to anxiety and dichotomous thinking: sensory sensitivity and intolerance of uncertainty as mediators in non-clinical population
Authors: Haoqian Shi, Masahiro Hirai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA recent theoretical cognitive model posits that three cognitive constructs—intolerance of uncertainty, lack of predictive processing, and dichotomous thinking—may interact with anxiety in autistic individuals. However, the interrelationships among these constructs remain unclear. We investigated this relationship in a non-clinical population using five questionnaires (Autism Spectrum Quotient, Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire, Short Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale, Penn State Worry Questionnaire, and Dichotomous Thinking Inventory) and conducted a path analysis. Data were collected from 405 adults (aged 20–22 years) in Experiment 1 and 628 (aged 20–49 years) in Experiment 2. In both experiments, autistic traits were significantly associated with both anxiety and dichotomous thinking, mediated by intolerance of uncertainty. Autistic traits were linked to intolerance of uncertainty, mediated by atypical sensory processing. Furthermore, autistic traits were directly associated with anxiety. No direct association was observed between anxiety and dichotomous thinking. Our results confirm the validity of Stark’s cognitive model in a non-clinical population. These findings provide new insights into anxiety and dichotomous thinking and shed light on the cognitive styles of autistic individuals.Keywords: Anxiety, Sensory processing, Dichotomous thinking, Autistic trait, Intolerance of uncertainty, Path analysisSubject terms: Human behaviour, Autism spectrum disorders

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by clinical heterogeneity, diagnosed based on a combination of difficulties in social interaction, restricted interests, and atypical sensory processing1, with a prevalence of approximately 1 in every 36 individuals2. Studies have identified atypical perceptual-cognitive styles in autistic individuals, including hyper- or hypo-sensory sensitivity, dominant local processing3–5, and cognitive inflexibility6. Beyond these atypical features, anxiety is a prominent co-occurring mental health issue in autism, with a lifetime prevalence of 42%7and surpasses that observed in non-autistic populations8–10.

Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) has been implicated in the manifestation of anxiety in autism, serving as a mediator between ASD and anxiety11,12. IU is characterized by an overvaluation of predictability, a negative evaluation of unexpected or unknown events, and a tendency to seek sufficient information to predict unpredictable events13. Initially developed in the context of generalized anxiety disorder14,15, the IU model has been extended to autism, revealing that autistic individuals exhibit higher levels of IU compared to those with typical development11,12,16–18. Recent meta-analyses further support the associations between anxiety, IU, and autism19,20. Moreover, several studies have indicated that cognitive inflexibility could contribute to IU in autistic individuals10,12. IU is also linked to sensory atypicality in autistic individuals. Normansell-Mossa et al21. expanded on previous findings12,22 by proposing a neurodevelopmental model wherein sensory hypersensitivity in autism contributes to anxiety through uncertainty. This suggests a positive association between sensory atypicality, particularly sensory sensitivity, and IU.

A recent cognitive model presented by Stark et al23. posits that three cognitive components—attenuated top-down prediction, IU, and dichotomous thinking—may interact, causing anxiety in autistic individuals. Although this model serves as an initial step in delineating the cognitive features in autistic individuals, nearly half of the relationships among cognitive constructs are hypothetical.

Our previous study partially validated Stark et al.’s23 model by establishing a significant association between autistic traits, IU, and dichotomous thinking24. We utilized three questionnaires—the Adult Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ)25, the Japanese-translated version of the Short IU Scale (SIUS)26, originally developed by Carleton et al27., and the Dichotomous Thinking Inventory (DTI)28—involving adults aged 20–22 years. Structural equation modeling revealed that autistic traits were significantly associated with dichotomous thinking mediated by IU24.

However, the empirical relationship between IU, dichotomous thinking, and anxiety, outlined in the theoretical model23, remains unclear. Furthermore, considering the observed atypical sensory processing in autistic individuals29, its connection with cognitive patterns remains unknown.

This study aimed to validate the cognitive model in autistic individuals23by assessing five cognitive constructs: autistic traits, sensory profiles, IU, anxiety, and dichotomous thinking. By extending Stark et al.’s23model, we explored how sensory hyper-/hypo-sensitivity contribute to autistic traits and anxiety. In the first experiment, we explored the relationship between cognitive constructs using five questionnaires administered to Japanese non-clinical adults aged 20–22 years and applying path analyses, following the methodology of our previous study24. The five questionnaires were as follows: the Japanese-translated version of the AQ30originally developed by Baron-Cohen et al25., Japanese-translated version of the SIUS26, DTI28, Japanese-translated version of the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ)31, originally developed by Mayer et al32., and Japanese-translated version of the Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire (GSQ)33, originally developed by Robertson and Simmons34, assessing autistic traits, IU, dichotomous thinking, anxiety, and sensory profile, respectively. The second experiment validated whether the results obtained in Experiment 1 could be replicated in a wider age range, specifically 20–49 years.

We hypothesized that if the model proposed by Stark et al23. is plausible, the following would be observed: (1) a significant indirect effect of autistic traits on dichotomous thinking, mediated by IU; (2) an association between autistic traits and anxiety, mediated by IU; (3) a mutual association between dichotomous thinking and anxiety; and (4) an association between autistic traits and IU, partially mediated by an atypical sensory profile.

Consistent with the cognitive model proposed by Stark et al23. and our previous study24, we found a significant association between autistic traits and both dichotomous thinking and anxiety, mediated by IU. In addition, we found a significant association between autistic traits and IU, partly mediated by atypical sensory processing, which aligns with a previous study21. Furthermore, we found a significant association between autistic traits and anxiety. However, the association between dichotomous thinking and anxiety was not statistically significant. In addition, unlike our previous study, this study found no significant direct association between autistic traits and dichotomous thinking.

Autistic traits lead to anxiety, which is partially mediated by IU, whereas dichotomous thinking is fully mediated by IU. These findings establish that anxiety and dichotomous thinking are mutually independent, in contrast to Stark et al.’s23 model.

Akin to Experiment 1, Experiment 2 highlighted significant associations between autistic traits and dichotomous thinking and anxiety, mediated by IU, across a broader age range. Additionally, we found a significant association between autistic traits and IU, partially mediated by atypical sensory processing. Furthermore, we identified a significant association between autistic traits and anxiety. However, the relationship between dichotomous thinking and anxiety was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the interplay among the cognitive constructs observed in Experiment 1 is not confined to a specific age group but is consistent across a wide age range (20–49 years).

Discussion

In both experiments, we delved into the pathways through which autistic traits in non-clinical adults (aged 20–22 years in Experiment 1 and 20–49 years in Experiment 2) might contribute to anxiety, mediated by sensory processing, IU, and dichotomous thinking. Following the model proposed by Stark et al23., we employed five questionnaires and applied path analyses. Consistent results from Experiments 1 and 2 revealed that autistic traits are linked to anxiety and dichotomous thinking, mediated by IU, in non-clinical adults. Additionally, autistic traits were associated with IU, partially mediated by atypical sensory processes. Alongside these indirect associations, a direct association was observed between autistic traits and anxiety. However, in contrast to Stark et al.’s23 cognitive model, this study did not find a significant association between dichotomous thinking and anxiety. Additionally, the path analysis results were consistent in both experiments, indicating no influence of age in the relationships among cognitive components in non-clinical adults.

The outcomes of both experiments aligned with Stark et al.’s23model, indicating that IU serves as a mediator for anxiety and dichotomous thinking. This finding is consistent with prior research that highlighted the mediating role of IU in both dichotomous thinking and anxiety. Our previous study underscored the mediating effect of IU in autistic traits and dichotomous thinking24. The mediation by IU on anxiety profiles in autistic individuals was reported by Chamberlain et al11. and supported by subsequent research12.

Stark et al23. proposed that anxiety linked with IU may drive dichotomous thinking, suggesting that autistic individuals may employ dichotomous thinking as a coping mechanism to mitigate uncertainty and the associated anxiety. Conversely, our findings did not reveal a significant association between anxiety and dichotomous thinking, indicating that they are independent constructs. These results empirically support the notion that IU independently mediates anxiety and dichotomous thinking, representing distinct cognitive processes. Furthermore, we did not observe a significant path from anxiety to IU, indicating that IU also plays a partial mediating role in anxiety.

Given the suggested efficacy of IU in alleviating anxiety36,37, our current findings imply that intervention of IU may influence anxiety and dichotomous thinking. This could serve as initial evidence that anxiety and dichotomous thinking are mutually independent and grounded in shared psychological constructs such as IU.

The present findings highlight that autistic traits are linked to IU, with partial mediation by an atypical sensory profile, which aligns with a prior study21. However, in contrast to Boulter et al.’s12 study, our study revealed partial mediation of IU when sensory sensitivity and anxiety are associated. The nuances between partial and full mediation in these investigations warrant further exploration.

The potential association between sensory profiles and IU may be attributed to the atypical sensory prediction mechanisms in autistic individuals38, which may cause the incoming sensory information to be inadequately modulated owing to atypical priors updating formed by sensory inputs. The inflexibility in responding to environmental changes could compromise the accuracy of predicting input signals, elevating IU. The precise relationship between atypical sensory profiles and predictive mechanisms remains ambiguous and warrants verification in future studies.

The positive association between IU and dichotomous thinking implies that dichotomous thinking may act as a protective mechanism by diminishing cognitive uncertainty. Furthermore, given the positive correlation between IU and dichotomous thinking, an intolerance of unpredictable events could intensify anxiety. However, our study revealed that anxiety and dichotomous thinking are mutually independent, challenging the notion that dichotomous thinking serves as a protective strategy by alleviating anxiety23. The findings from our study highlight both direct and indirect associations between autistic traits and anxiety, suggesting that anxiety is, in part, mediated by IU.

Although this study empirically tested the cognitive model of autism proposed by Stark et al23., it has several limitations. First, it tested non-clinical adults, whereas Stark et al.’s23 model is designed for autistic individuals. Consequently, further investigations are necessary to determine the applicability of this cognitive model in autistic individuals. Second, the cognitive model was tested among adults aged 20–49; however, anxiety can manifest even during early childhood. Therefore, future research should explore whether the cognitive model applies to younger children. Third, the current model focused on specific cognitive/behavioral patterns in autistic individuals, excluding other manifestations such as repetitive behaviors. Future revisions should consider incorporating additional behavioral and cognitive properties of autistic individuals. Moreover, cognitive inflexibility is a contributor to IU10,12. Therefore, its role should be explored in further studies. Finally, this study employed subjective measures such as questionnaires; future studies should adopt objective measures to validate the proposed model.

In this study, we examined Stark et al.’s23 cognitive model in 1033 general adults by employing five questionnaires. Although our results partially supported the model, some inconsistencies emerged. First, we found no significant association between dichotomous thinking and anxiety or a bidirectional association between IU and anxiety, but only a significant unidirectional association between the two. Furthermore, we discovered a direct association between autistic traits and anxiety. These findings deepen the understanding of cognitive patterns of autistic individuals and pave the way for the construction of a nuanced cognitive model. This model, if replicated, can enhance our comprehension of the cognitive patterns exhibited by autistic individuals. Additionally, the insights gained from this research can inform the development of effective intervention programs aimed at helping individuals navigate these cognitive tendencies, improving their overall quality of life.

Methods

Experiment 1

Participants

A total of 405 Japanese adults (male = 204, female = 201, age range = 20–22 years, mean ± SD = 21.6 ± 0.91 years) were recruited through an online survey by Cross Marketing Inc., with whom they were registered. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before they responded to the questionnaires. This study was approved by the ethics committee at the Department of Cognitive and Psychological Sciences, Graduate School of Informatics, Nagoya University (NUPSY-230606-R-01) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Materials

The participants were assessed using five questionnaires. Autistic traits were assessed using the Adult AQ25, a 50-item questionnaire with five subscales (social skills, attention switching, attention-to-detail, communication, and imagination). Each item is appraised using a 4-point scale from “definitely disagree” to “definitely agree.” One point was assigned for every response indicating autistic traits, specifically “definitely agree” or “slightly agree” for regular items. However, for reversed items, one point was assigned for responses of “slightly disagree” or “definitely disagree.”

The Japanese-translated version of the GSQ33, originally developed by Robertson and Simmons34, was employed to assess sensory profiles. It is a 42-item questionnaire that detects hyper- and hypo-sensitivity in seven sensory domains: visual, auditory, gustatory, olfactory, tactile, vestibular, and proprioceptive. Three questions assessed hypersensitivity and another three assessed hypo-sensitivity across all seven sensory domains, resulting in 42 items. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The total scores ranged from 0 to 168.

The Japanese-translated version of the SIUS26, originally developed by Carleton et al27., was utilized to assess IU. This version consists of 12 questions derived by selecting 12 items from the original IU Scale, comprising 27 items15. Responses to the SIUS items were rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (not at all applicable) to 5 (very much applicable). The instrument encompasses two subscales: prospective anxiety, indicating fear and anxiety about future events, and inhibitory anxiety, denoting uncertainty-hindering actions or experiences.

For the anxiety profile, we adopted the Japanese-translated version of the PSWQ31, originally developed by Meyer et al32.. The PSWQ comprises 16 items, each rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all typical of me) to 5 (very typical of me). The total scores ranged from 16 to 80.

Dichotomous thinking was evaluated using the DTI developed by Oshio28. The inventory comprises three subscales: preference for dichotomy, dichotomous beliefs, and profit-and-loss thinking. Preference for dichotomy reflects an inclination to comprehend or experience things better in terms of binary opposition (e.g., “good or bad” or “all or nothing”). Dichotomous beliefs signify the conviction that complex events can be distinctly categorized into two main types: good or bad. Profit-and-loss thinking involves classifying things into two categories with an inclination to define each item as either a loss (disadvantage) or gain (advantage). The DTI comprises 15 items. Respondents used a 6-point scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The total scores ranged from 15 to 80.

The Qualtrics platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) was employed to randomize the order of the questionnaires across the five assessments.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlations were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 29 (2022), and R Studio39. Path analyses were conducted using Analysis of Moment Structures (AMOS), version 29 (2022). The model was evaluated using several indices, including the chi-square test (χ2 test), GFI, AGFI, CFI, normed fit index (NFI), RMSEA, SRMR, and AIC. For the overall model fit indices35, either a GFI ≥ 0.93 or an SRMR ≤ 0.08 indicates an acceptable fit when the sample size exceeds 100. For the estimation of path coefficients, variances and covariances were calculated by maximum likelihood estimation. Subsequently, the path coefficients were divided by their respective standard errors to compute the test statistics, and the corresponding p-values were derived. The standardized coefficients were reported in the analysis. Both direct and indirect effects were examined using a bootstrapping method with 5000 resamples, with significance indicated by a p-value below 0.05.

Experiment 2

Participants

A total of 628 Japanese adults, registered with Cross Marketing Group Inc., participated in an online survey conducted by Cross Marketing Inc. Those who participated in Experiment 1 were not allowed to participate in Experiment 2. The participants were divided into three age groups: 20–29 years (N = 207, [male = 102, female = 105], mean ± SD = 25.9 ± 2.70 years), 30–39 years (N = 206, [male = 102, female = 104], mean ± SD = 35.4 ± 2.74 years), and 40–49 years (N = 215, [male = 111, female = 104], mean ± SD = 46.1 ± 2.83 years). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before they responded to the questionnaires. This study was approved by the ethics committee at the Department of Cognitive and Psychological Sciences, Graduate School of Informatics, at Nagoya University (NUPSY-230606-R-01) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

All questionnaires and analysis procedures were identical to those of Experiment 1.
