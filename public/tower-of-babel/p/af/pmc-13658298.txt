Hydrophilicity, wettability, and elemental analysis of zirconia implant ceramic after surface conditioning methods as a function of storage time
Authors: Dario Philipp Luterbach, Luiza Freitas Brum Souza, Tan Fırat Eyüboğlu, Mutlu Özcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluated how distinct surface-modification protocols and storage time affect the wettability, surface free energy (SFE), and elemental composition of zirconia.Materials and methodsZirconia specimens were subjected to six surface conditioning methods: Control (CT), alumina airborne-particle abrasion (110 μm) (AL), tribochemical silica coating (30 μm) (SI), chemical etching (ET), alumina+etching (AL-ET), and laser treatment (LS). Measurements were taken at baseline and after 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Wettability was evaluated with water and diiodomethane contact angles to calculate work of adhesion (WoA) and surface free energy (SFE) via the Owens-Wendt-Rabel-Kaelble method. Elemental composition was assessed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. Data analysis involved one-way ANOVA with Tukey`s post-hoc tests for treatment comparison and two-way ANOVA for storage-time effects.ResultsSurface conditioning significantly affected contact angle, WoA, and SFE (p < 0.05), compared to storage. SI had the lowest water contact angles, highest WoA, and highest SFE, with a strong polar component. AL improved wettability and SFE, but less than SI. While ET showed the least hydrophilic properties at baseline but improved over time, LS was intermediate and stable. CT became more hydrophilic after 6–12 months and SI remained best. EDS confirmed these trends, with persistent silica, low aluminum after alumina treatment, and treatment-dependent elemental changes. The CT and LS spectra resembled those of the substrate.ConclusionsBoth the surface-modification strategy and storage time affected zirconia wettability, with surface modification playing a greater role. While tribochemical silica coating created a durable, hydrophilic surface, Al2O3 was less effective. Etching needed time to remove residues and laser treatment offered intermediate stability.Clinical relevanceTribochemical silica coating provided the best zirconia implant surface by maintaining a durable, hydrophilic, high surface-free energy state that may promote early protein interaction and predictable osseointegration.Keywords: Air-abrasion, Etching, Dental materials, Laser, Oral implants, Surface conditioning, Surface free energy, Ultrasonic cleaning, Wettability, Zirconia

Introduction

Metal-free materials are increasingly used in reconstructive and implant dentistry. In this context, zirconia ceramics have emerged as potential substitutes for titanium implants over the last decade [1]. Its application in dentistry began in the early 1990 s, with initial studies demonstrating favorable outcomes in mechanical stability, fracture resistance, and osseointegration [2, 3]. However, implant surface properties are significantly influenced by surface conditioning and preparation methods, which play a crucial role in the success of osseointegration [4].

The clinical success of implant therapy depends heavily on the quality of the bone-implant interface, which is governed by both mechanical and biological stability. Primary stability is the mechanical interlocking achieved at implant placement in bone [5], whereas secondary stability develops over time through new bone formation and remodeling around the implant, a process known as osseointegration [6, 7]. The physicochemical properties of the implant surface, including topography, hydrophilicity, and chemical composition, play a critical role in this biological integration [4, 8].

Immediately after placement, the implant contacts blood and plasma proteins, triggering biological responses. Hydrophilicity and wettability are key surface properties influencing this process. Hydrophilicity, indicated by water contact angles below 90°, promotes blood spreading and protein adsorption, vital for clotting and healing [9]. Wettability, also measured by contact angle, reflects how well a surface can be wetted by biological fluids. Low contact angles mean high wettability, supporting protein stability and function. Hydrophobic surfaces (contact angles > 90°) may denature proteins, hindering cellular attachment and tissue integration [8].

It has been demonstrated that hydrophilic surfaces not only enhance initial protein adsorption but also promote osteoblast differentiation and maturation, thereby expediting the onset of secondary stability, which is essential for long-term implant success [4, 10]. These surface-fluid interactions are further influenced by the surface free energy (SFE), which can be divided into polar and dispersive components. Surfaces with higher polar contributions and total SFE values above 50–60 mJ/m² tend to exhibit stronger protein binding and enhanced cellular responses [11]. Typically, contact angles are measured using both polar (ultrapure water) and non-polar (diiodomethane) probe liquids to calculate SFE and work of adhesion (WoA) using the Owens-Wendt-Rabel-Kaelble (OWRK) model.

Another critical parameter is surface roughness, or microtopography, which typically spans average height values ranging from the micrometer to the sub-millimeter scale, depending on the surface-conditioning method and measurement approach [4]. Roughened surfaces increase available surface area, enhance mechanical interlocking, and thereby influence osteoblast behavior. Parameters such as Ra (average profile roughness) and Sa (average area roughness) are commonly used to quantify topography. Moderately rough surfaces with average Sa values of approximately 1–2 μm have been shown to stimulate osteoblast proliferation, differentiation, and osteogenic marker expression, thereby promoting bone formation and implant integration [4, 12–14].

Various techniques were proposed, including alumina air-abrasion, tribochemical silica coating, chemical etching, and laser structuring. The SLA (Sandblasted, Large grit, Acid-etched) protocol, commonly applied to titanium, has also been adapted from titanium, increases roughness and surface energy, aiding protein adsorption [15]. Silica coatings prepared by sol-gel methods exhibit bioactivity through calcium and phosphate ion release, promoting apatite formation and osteoconduction [16–19]. While extensively studied in titanium, evidence on zirconia’s long-term surface stability remains limited and conflicting.

Beyond immediate effects, implant surfaces may change over time during storage due to environmental exposure, hydrocarbon adsorption, and surface hydroxyl reorganization [11, 15]. These aging processes can alter wettability, surface free energy, and elemental composition, affecting early biological interactions [4, 11]. Evaluating multiple storage intervals helps assess short- and long-term stability, with extended time points revealing durability under clinical storage conditions [15].

The objective of this study was to investigate the effects of different surface modification techniques on the surface roughness, wettability, surface free energy, work of adhesion, and elemental composition of zirconia specimens across multiple storage periods. The null hypotheses tested were that (a) the surface treatment protocol would not significantly affect any of the evaluated surface characteristics compared to the untreated control group; and (b) the storage time (baseline, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year) would have no significant effect on any of the surface characteristics within each treatment group.

Materials and methods

Materials and study design

The study used a factorial design with six surface conditioning protocols: Control (CT), alumina airborne-particle abrasion (AL; Al₂O₃, 110 μm), tribochemical silica coating (SI; silica-coated Al₂O₃, 30 μm), chemical etching (ET), combined alumina abrasion and etching (AL-ET), and laser treatment (LS), and five storage periods (baseline, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year), yielding 30 experimental groups. Each group comprised 9 zirconia specimens, for a total of 270 specimens. Specimen preparation

A total of 90 zirconia specimens (Emax ZirCAD, Ivoclar, Liechtenstein; 15 × 15 × 1.5 mm3) were prepared for this study. All specimens were polished using a rotary polishing unit (LaboPol-21, Struers, Copenhagen, Denmark) with constant water irrigation. The polishing sequence consisted of 20 s on P#1000 followed by 20 s on P#1200 waterproof silicon carbide abrasive papers (Struers). After polishing, the specimens were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath (Telsonic TPC-25, City, Country) containing distilled water for 5 min at room temperature. After drying with compressed air, the specimens were assigned to the surface treatments.

Surface conditioning methods

The specimens were randomly divided into six groups according to the surface conditioning protocol:

Control (CT): No additional surface modification beyond the standardized polishing and ultrasonic cleaning procedure previously described.

Al2O3 (AL): The surface was roughened with Al2O3 (3 M Rocatec Pre, 3 M ESPE, Minn, USA, 110 μm particles) at 2.5 bar from a distance of 10 mm for 20 s. After blasting, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 5 min and then air-dried.

Storage

Specimens from control and treated groups were randomly assigned to five storage periods before analysis: baseline, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year. Baseline specimens were analyzed immediately after surface conditioning, whereas the remaining specimens were stored at room temperature under dry conditions for their designated time intervals.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

After each surface treatment and storage period, the specimens were analyzed using a tabletop scanning electron microscope (TM4000Plus, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Surface morphology was assessed in secondary electron (SE) mode at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of approximately 10 mm. Elemental chemical composition was evaluated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using the integrated EDS system with a silicon drift detector (SDD). EDS analyses were performed in mapping mode at 15 kV to qualitatively assess the spatial distribution of elements on the specimen surfaces.

Surface roughness analysis

A micrometric roughness analysis was performed (n = 3) using a 3D digital microscope (VHX-7000, Keyence Co., Osaka, Japan) equipped with a 300× magnification objective. Three-dimensional surface images were acquired, and profile lines were traced on the reconstructed 3D topographies to determine the differences between the smallest and largest surface undulations, which were expressed as roughness values. For each specimen, three measurements were performed along each axis (x and y), using a cut-off length (λc) of 0.25 mm and a sampling length of 1.25 mm. The arithmetic mean of the measurements was calculated for each specimen in accordance with ISO 21920-2 (2021), considering the Ra (arithmetical mean surface roughness) parameter.

Statistical analysis

Since the data for surface roughness, contact angle, work of adhesion, and surface free energy met normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, verified by the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests (p > 0.05), parametric analyses were used (α = 0.05) with SPSS (version 21, IBM). Surface roughness effects from conditioning and storage time, and their interaction, were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s post hoc test for significant effects. Contact angle, work of adhesion, and surface free energy were assessed using one-way ANOVA because surface free energy and work of adhesion are derived parameters calculated from paired contact angle measurements according to the Owens-Wendt-Rabel-Kaelble method. Contact angle, work of adhesion, and surface free energy were assessed with one-way ANOVA at each storage time and surface conditioning, plus pairwise comparisons with Tukey’s test. This approach was applied to evaluate treatment-specific and storage-time-dependent changes in wettability. Differences among storage times within each group were also analyzed via one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc. Elemental data were reported descriptively. Effect sizes, as eta squared (η²), were calculated for all ANOVA models. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (η² ≈ 0.01), moderate (η² ≈ 0.06), or large (η² ≥ 0.14). Elemental composition data were analyzed descriptively and qualitatively.

Results

For water contact angle (θ), surface conditioning significantly influenced the results at all storage times with a large effect size (p < 0.05, η² = 0.41) (Table 2). SI consistently exhibited the lowest θ values, whereas ET and AL-ET showed the highest values. CT and LC groups demonstrated intermediate values. AL generally showed lower θ values than CT and ET and was statistically similar to SI at all storage times except at 1 month. The corresponding water-derived work of adhesion (WoA) followed the same ranking pattern among surface-conditioning protocols (p < 0.05, η² = 0.39). To facilitate simultaneous visualization of treatment-related effects and storage-time–dependent trends, grouped box plots were generated for water contact angle and surface free energy parameters, with all storage times displayed side by side within each surface-conditioning protocol (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5).

The polar component of surface free energy (σᵖ) was significantly influenced by surface conditioning with a large effect size (p < 0.05, η² = 0.49). SI showed the highest σᵖ values at all storage times, whereas ET and AL-ET exhibited consistently low σᵖ values. The dispersive component (σᵈ) was also significantly affected by surface conditioning, with a moderate effect size (p < 0.05, η² = 0.18), indicating smaller between-group variability than for σᵖ. Effect size analysis indicated that the surface-conditioning protocol accounted for a substantially larger proportion of variance in surface free energy parameters than storage time.

Storage time significantly affected water contact angle and total surface energy with moderate effect sizes (θ: p < 0.05, η² = 0.09; σ: p < 0.05, η² = 0.12) (Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9). In the CT group, θ values decreased significantly at 6 months and 1 year compared with Baseline. In the AL group, θ was significantly higher at 1 month than at all other storage times. In the SI group, Baseline θ values were significantly higher than those at 1 month and 1 year, and 3-month values were significantly higher than at 1 year. In the ET group, Baseline θ values were significantly higher than those at all subsequent storage times. In the AL-ET group, θ was significantly higher at 1 month than at Baseline and higher at 3 months than at 1 month. No significant differences in θ across storage times were observed for the LS group (p > 0.05, η² = 0.01).

Discussion

This study showed that surface-modification protocols and storage time jointly modulate wettability, surface free energy (SFE), work of adhesion (WoA), and near-surface chemistry of zirconia, thereby rejecting both null hypotheses. Tribochemical silica coating (SiO₂, 30 μm) consistently delivered the most hydrophilic profile, lower water contact angles, higher WoA, and higher total SFE with robust polar and dispersive contributions. Chemical etching, alone or after alumina abrasion, produced higher contact angles and lower polar SFE. LS and CT remained intermediate, and AL (110 μm) generally approached SI but showed a short-term inconsistency at one month. Effect-size analysis showed that surface conditioning was the dominant factor in wettability and SFE outcomes, accounting for substantially more variance than storage time, indicating that surface chemistry, rather than aging effects, governed wetting behavior. This distinction reflects the different physical nature of the investigated parameters: surface roughness is a directly measured topographical property, whereas surface free energy and work of adhesion are derived energetic parameters that depend on paired contact angle measurements.

The superiority of the SI protocol reflects a chemistry-dominated mechanism, inherent in tribochemical silica application with smaller particles [11, 13]. The process deposits a silica-rich layer with a higher density of surface silanol (–Si–OH) groups, increasing the polar component of SFE and lowering the contact angle, thereby enhancing aqueous and protein interactions at the interface [16, 19]. Concurrently, the 30 μm particles tend to produce a finer, more uniformly distributed microtexture (shallower, more frequent pits) that increases the real surface area without deep cratering, although effect-size analysis indicates that microroughness differences among non-laser groups contributed minimally to wetting behavior, thereby amplifying intrinsic hydrophilicity when surface chemistry is favorable [13, 20]. In contrast, 110 μm Al₂O₃ predominantly induces deeper cratering and localized plastic deformation with limited chemical activation (low embedded Al on EDS), which can raise roughness but does not increase the polar SFE to the same extent, explaining its generally intermediate wetting performance relative to SiO₂ [4, 13, 20].

Storage time produced treatment-specific trajectories consistent with time-dependent surface chemistry [11, 21]. CT specimens became progressively more hydrophilic, with increased total SFE, mainly polar, at 6 and 12 months, consistent with spontaneous hydroxylation “aging” in aqueous media that increases surface polarity [11, 21]. However, these changes that occur over time can also be affected by the unintentional buildup of airborne substances, such as hydrocarbons. These substances can gradually change how a surface behaves, including how it interacts with liquids. Nevertheless, effect-size estimates indicate that storage-time effects were secondary in magnitude compared with surface-conditioning effects. The SI group showed early hydrophilization at 1 month relative to baseline and maintained superior performance through 12 months, indicating durable silica-mediated surface activity despite modest fluctuations [4, 11, 21]. In contrast, the ET group began in a more hydrophobic/low-polar state at baseline and improved thereafter, mirroring elemental findings of initially detectable fluorine and nitrogen that declined with storage. Attenuation of etchant-related residues likely underpins the delayed wetting improvement [4, 11, 21]. The AL-ET condition, on the other hand, exhibited a similar early chemistry-driven shift followed by a stable plateau, whereas laser-treated specimens remained notably stable throughout the year, suggesting parameters that yielded a chemically stable surface with balanced polar/dispersive contributions [13, 20].

Elemental analysis helped contextualize these findings in terms of surface chemistry [4]. Specimens in the SI group retained a persistent silica signal at all time points, consistent with sustained hydrophilicity and elevated polar SFE [11, 21]. The AL group showed only low-level aluminum, with spectra dominated by Zr and O (minor Y/Hf), which explains why its wetting improvement was less uniformly maintained than that of the SI group and suggests that its apparent convergence in total SFE was driven primarily by dispersive rather than polar contributions [13, 20]. ET groups initially exhibited fluorine and nitrogen, consistent with etchant byproducts, that waned over time, paralleling the shift toward lower contact angles and higher SFE at later time points [4, 11, 21]. CT and LS specimens showed substrate-like spectra with qualitative stability, matching their intermediate and temporally robust wetting profiles [13, 20]. It should be emphasized that EDS is a semi-quantitative, near-surface technique with limited sensitivity to light elements and a sampling depth on the order of micrometers; small fluctuations in minor constituents (e.g., ± 0.5% in Hf) therefore fall within expected analytical variability and warrant cautious interpretation of absolute values, while relative trends across treatments and time remain informative.

The observation that the CT group exhibited slightly higher roughness values than some conditioned groups (ET, SI, AL, and AL-ET) can be attributed to the fact that polishing-induced surface features of untreated zirconia may persist as broad, low-frequency topographical variations. In contrast, air abrasion is characterized by the impact of aluminum oxide particles that promote erosion of the zirconia substrate [22, 23], modifying the surface topography by selectively eroding high surface peaks remaining after polishing procedures [24]. This process reduces deep grooves and leads to a more uniform and standardized distribution of surface defects, as previously described in the literature [24]. Consequently, certain conditioning protocols may partially smooth the surface at the microscale while simultaneously altering its chemical composition. This finding underscores that higher roughness values do not necessarily translate into superior wettability or biological performance in the absence of favorable surface chemistry.

In the broader literature, these findings reinforce that not only microtopography but also surface chemistry and polarity govern the earliest biological interactions at the implant interface [4, 12, 13]. The present results align with previous reports demonstrating that tribochemical silica coating enhances zirconia wettability and polar SFE more effectively than alumina abrasion or etching alone, while also extending these observations by showing the temporal stability of these effects over one year. The dominance of polar SFE effects observed here suggests that early protein adsorption kinetics, rather than purely mechanical interlocking, are likely the principal mediators of the initial biological response. Surfaces that combine high total SFE with substantial polar components, exemplified here by 30 μm tribochemical silica, are better aligned with the hydrophilic, protein-compatible state associated with a faster transition from primary mechanical stability to secondary biological stability [5, 6, 8, 10, 21, 22]. Airborne-particle abrasion with 110 μm alumina can move zirconia in the same direction but appears more susceptible to short-term reconditioning, while certain etching chemistries may initially depress polarity/hydrophilicity due to transient residues unless additional rinsing/neutralization or pre-aging is provided [4, 11, 13, 21]. Laser texturing, when tuned to avoid adverse chemical alterations, can provide a mid-range yet temporally robust energetic state with predictable behavior [13, 20].

Clinically, steering zirconia toward higher polar SFE appears advantageous for early blood/protein interactions and osteogenic responses at the bone-implant interface [8, 21, 22, 25]. In the current context, 30 μm tribochemical silica yielded the most consistently hydrophilic/high-SFE surface over one year, whereas 110 μm alumina abrasion was generally beneficial but showed a short-term dip that might be mitigated by immediate post-treatment cleaning or adjusted timing [11, 13, 21]. Etched surfaces may benefit from more aggressive rinsing/neutralization or pre-aging to minimize residue-related hydrophobicity, and laser protocols can deliver temporally stable energetics if parameters are optimized [13, 20]. Limitations include the in vitro nature of wettability/SFE assessments using two probe liquids and the OWRK model, the semi-quantitative and depth-limited character of EDS, and the absence of direct protein/cell or in vivo outcomes. Future work should integrate high-resolution surface chemistry (e.g., XPS/ToF-SIMS) with biological readouts to link specific chemical states (e.g., silanol density or residual fluoride species) to functional osseointegration endpoints.
