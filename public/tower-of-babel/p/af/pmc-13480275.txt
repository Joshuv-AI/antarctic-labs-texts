A Case of Cushing's Cardiomyopathy with a Convoluted Course of Treatment
Authors: Feng Hu, Cheng Yu, Yuansheng Wu, Tao Yi, Lianglong Chen, Shumei Li
Published: 2026

We report a case of a 55-year-old woman with secondary left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy due to adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-dependent Cushing's disease. The diagnosis was established through dexamethasone suppression testing, pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and inferior petrosal sinus sampling. Following surgical resection of a pituitary microadenoma and glucocorticoid replacement therapy, the patient demonstrated regression of LV hypertrophy, supporting a reversible, hormonally mediated process.

Background

Endogenous Cushing's syndrome, characterized by excessive cortisol secretion driven by ACTH hypersecretion, is associated with a spectrum of clinical manifestations, metabolic comorbidities, and elevated mortality rates.1 In patients with Cushing's syndrome, even the absence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus does not preclude the development of asymmetric septal hypertrophy and concentric remodelling of the left ventricle.2 Unlike essential hypertension or other forms of secondary hypertension, Cushing's syndrome is distinguished by a significantly thickened interventricular septum and a higher prevalence of asymmetric septal hypertrophy.3,4 Early diagnosis and intervention are crucial for preventing irreversible cardiac dysfunction, cardiovascular events, and heart failure.1,2,4

In this report, we describe a patient with secondary LV hypertrophy due to Cushing's syndrome. This case highlights the rare occurrence of apical and asymmetric LV hypertrophic manifestations and emphasizes the potential for reversibility. These observations suggest that excessive cortisol plays a central role in the pathogenesis of these cardiac alterations.

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old female patient with a history of hypertension and diabetes was admitted to the cardiology unit for tachypnea of 2 years' duration, on September 13, 2023. In May 2022, she had undergone tricuspid biological valve replacement and radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation due to severe tricuspid regurgitation and persistent atrial fibrillation (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Her cardiac function was classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III. The patient exhibited increased facial hair, bushy eyebrows, hyperpigmentation, and central obesity. Laboratory findings revealed an elevated level of N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) of 7604 pg/mL (reference range: < 350 pg/mL).

The electrocardiogram on admission revealed sinus tachycardia, a short P-R interval, increased LV voltage, ST-segment depression, and T-wave inversion across multiple leads (Supplemental Fig. S1B). A pulmonary computed tomography (CT) scan on hospital day 2 demonstrated scattered small nodules and interstitial pneumonia (Supplemental Fig. S1C). An abdominal ultrasound on hospital day 3 revealed hepatomegaly and dilated hepatic veins and inferior vena cava (Supplemental Fig. S1D).

On the fourth day of hospitalization, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed nonobstructive LV hypertrophy, both asymmetrical (interventricular septum thickness [IVS], 21 mm; LV posterior wall thickness (LVPW), 11 mm; IVS-to-LVPW ratio, 1.8) and concentric (LV mass index, 233 g/m2; relative wall thickness, 0.6) patterns (Fig. 1A; Videos 1-4 , view videos online). The LV end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters were 50 mm and 33 mm, respectively. The apical wall thickness was approximately 18 mm. No systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior mitral leaflet was observed. TTE also revealed overall diastolic dysfunction (LV ejection fraction , 63%; stroke volume, 73 mL; E/E′, 44; estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure, 65 mm Hg). The patient was diagnosed with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), based on symptoms of heart failure and elevated NT-proBNP level. To exclude pulmonary embolism, the patient underwent CT pulmonary angiography, which revealed no filling defects (Fig. 1B). Investigation

Given the presence of edema, hyperpigmentation, hepatomegaly, and endocrine abnormalities, we sequentially ruled out Fabry disease, cardiac amyloidosis, and polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, M-protein, skin changes syndrome. Bone marrow biopsy revealed active hematopoiesis with a normal granulocytic-to-erythroid ratio (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Additionally, Tc-99m-pyrophosphate (PYP) myocardial scintigraphy showed no radioactive tracer uptake in the heart (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Skeletal radiographs (Supplemental Fig. S2C) and electromyography yielded normal results.

Physicians had considered the possibility of Cushing's syndrome in February 2022. However, the diagnosis was not supported by a low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (Supplemental Table S2). Given the distinctive physical features (such as facial hair, bushy eyebrows, and hyperpigmentation), the concurrent metabolic syndrome, and the elevated fasting cortisol level during this hospitalization, a repeat low-dose dexamethasone suppression test was performed on hospital day 15 (Supplemental Table S3). Subsequently, a high-dose dexamethasone suppression test confirmed ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome (Supplemental Table S4).

Adrenal CT revealed no discernible nodules (Supplemental Fig. S3A). Cranial MRI demonstrated multiple ischemic lacunar infarcts (Supplemental Fig. S3B). Pituitary MRI identified a 4 × 3 × 4 mm adenoma with inhomogeneous enhancement in the left pituitary gland (Supplemental Fig. S3B; Videos 5-7 , view videos online). In addition, positron emission tomography–CT (PET-CT) did not reveal hypermetabolic activity at the pituitary gland (Supplemental Fig. S4). Inferior petrosal sinus sampling was performed on hospital day 38. The prolactin-corrected ACTH ratio (inferior petrosal sinus to superior vena cava) was greater than 0.8 (Supplemental Table S5). These findings confirmed that the source of ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome was the pituitary gland.

Endomyocardial biopsy was performed at the right ventricular septum (Videos 8 , view videos online). Histopathologic examination revealed myocardial interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 2, A and B). Congo red staining ruled out the possibility of myocardial amyloidosis (Fig. 2C). Cardiomyocytes displayed significant degeneration and injury, characterized by swollen intracellular organelles, cytoplasmic vacuolation, and disarrayed or fractured myofibrils (Fig. 2D). These biopsy findings are consistent with previously reported myocardial pathologic hypertrophy associated with Cushing's disease.1 However, the biopsy results were nonspecific and did not influence clinical decision-making. Treatment and follow-up

The patient's final diagnosis included ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome, pituitary microadenoma, and secondary LV hypertrophy. Three months after the initial hospitalization, the pituitary microadenoma in the sellar region was surgically resected, and glucocorticoid replacement therapy was initiated. Blood pressure remained well controlled perioperatively. Intraoperatively, the tumour (8 × 8 × 8 mm) was gray in color, soft in texture, with a moderate blood supply, and cystic changes. Postoperative pathology confirmed a pituitary adenoma. The diagnostic and therapeutic timeline is presented in Supplemental Figure S5. Follow-up TTE revealed near-normalization of cardiac structure, diastolic function, and pulmonary artery pressure (IVS, 12 mm; LVPW, 9 mm; IVS-to-LVPW ratio, 1.4; LV mass index, 171 g/m; relative wall thickness, 0.5; LV ejection fraction, 64%; stroke volume, 63 mL; E/E′: 37; estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure: 28 mm Hg; Supplemental Fig. S6; Videos 10-13 , view videos online). The LV end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters were 46 mm and 31 mm, respectively. Follow-up electrocardiogram showed normalization of LV voltage and P-R interval (Supplemental Fig. S7).

Discussion

This report describes a case of secondary LV hypertrophy attributed to hypercortisolism. This condition is distinct from primary hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, with important prognostic and genetic implications. The patient demonstrated combined pre- and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension, likely secondary to both HFpEF physiology and hypercortisolism. Following surgical resection of the pituitary adenoma and subsequent glucocorticoid replacement therapy, the patient demonstrated significant improvement in cardiac structure and biochemical markers, suggesting partial reversibility.

These findings have prognostic significance regarding the risk of future cardiovascular events and heart failure.1 The development of LV hypertrophy is influenced by both cortisol excess and concurrent metabolic comorbidities.2,3 Patients suspected of having Cushing's disease may require a comprehensive cardiac evaluation, including echocardiography, myocardial biopsy, CMR imaging, and inferior petrosal sinus sampling.1, 2, 3

In patients with Cushing's syndrome, restoration of impaired cardiac function can be achieved through biochemical remission, provided that the myocardium remains in the early stages of hypertrophic cardiomyocyte development and excessive lipid accumulation.1,4 Sugihara et al.5 reported 3 cases of Cushing's syndrome, each characterized by asymmetric septal hypertrophy and LV concentric remodelling. In 2 of these cases, right ventricular endomyocardial biopsies were performed. One patient, who achieved remission of hypercortisolism 8 months postoperatively, exhibited mild myocyte hypertrophy and recovery of cardiac function. In contrast, in the second patient, 3 years after adrenalectomy, a slight worsening of myocyte histology occurred, along with increased interstitial fibrosis, without complete recovery of cardiac function.5 Effective management of cortisol levels is essential to prevent the progression of myocardial fibrosis, improve cardiac function, reduce comorbidities, and ultimately enhance overall quality of life. The recommended approach for patients with unexplained asymmetric LV hypertrophy is to undergo routine endocrine evaluation.4

During the diagnostic and therapeutic process, several points merit reflection. The transition from negative to positive results on dexamethasone suppression testing presents a clinically noteworthy observation. Although a low-dose dexamethasone suppression test had been administered prior to this admission, the patient tested negative for Cushing's disease 19 months earlier, likely because the disease was at a premature stage. Consequently, physicians did not pursue further testing, given the equivocal results (refer to Supplemental Table S2). Additionally, LV hypertrophy and HFpEF were already present prior to tricuspid valve replacement, which had been performed approximately 1 year before the current presentation. TTE at that time revealed an apical wall thickness of approximately 15.30 mm and overall diastolic dysfunction. Functional tricuspid regurgitation may have been secondary to elevated left-sided filling pressures and pulmonary hypertension, rather than representing a primary pathology. Given that scenario, the valve intervention may have addressed a consequence, rather than the underlying cause. Additionally, follow-up invasive hemodynamic assessments, such as repeat right heart catheterization, were not performed, and improvements were evaluated solely by echocardiographic estimation. Finally, due to financial constraints, the patient declined CMR imaging and whole-exome sequencing, which could have identified potential pathogenic variants.

Conclusions

We report a case of a patient diagnosed with secondary LV hypertrophy attributed to hypercortisolism. Overt Cushing's syndrome is a debilitating disorder associated with numerous comorbidities and elevated mortality rates.
