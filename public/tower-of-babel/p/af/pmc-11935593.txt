Development and Validation of Prognostic Models for Bleeding and Ischemia in Elderly Patients With Comorbid Acute Coronary Syndrome and Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Honghong Zhang, Li Zheng, Zengao Yang, Haijing Zhao, Yue Zhu, Yuhan Ma, Zhengfeng Wu, Weize Qiu, Zhirui Zhou, Yuqi Liu, Yundai Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute coronary syndrome and atrial fibrillation are common cardiovascular diseases in elderly individuals. Patients with comorbidities face increased risks of bleeding and ischemia; however, there is a lack of prognostic models for quantifying these risks in this special population.Methods and ResultsIn this retrospective cohort study, 1851 patients (≥65 years old) with acute coronary syndrome and atrial fibrillation from 2 hospitals in China were included in the development cohort (1252 individuals) and 2 external validation cohorts (284 and 315 individuals). During 1‐year follow‐up, 96 Bleeding Academic Research Consortium type 3 or 5 bleeding events and 245 thromboembolic events were observed. In the development cohort, the concordance indexes for bleeding at 3, 6, and 12 mo ranged from 0.737 to 0.845 and for ischemia ranged from 0.723 to 0.777. The calibration curve and decision curve analysis indicated adequate calibration and clinical practicability. The concordance indexes varied from 0.679 to 0.809 in the validation cohorts. Subgroup analyses focusing on anticoagulant drugs and antithrombotic therapy were conducted, revealing similar discrimination and calibration. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated significant differences (log‐rank P<0.001). Additionally, the models outperformed conventional models in concordance indexes, integrated discrimination improvement, and net reclassification improvement.ConclusionsOur study provides 2 robust prognostic models with easily available clinical factors for predicting bleeding and ischemia in elderly patients with acute coronary syndrome and atrial fibrillation. Furthermore, we provide online calculators to facilitate individualized risk evaluation and clinical decision‐making.RegistrationURL: www.chictr.org.cn/. Unique Identifier: ChiCTR2200067185.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Previous studies have shown that 20% to 30% of patients with AF have comorbid coronary heart disease, 1 and 6% to 21% of patients with coronary heart disease have comorbid AF. 2 As populations age, the incidence and burden of AF and ACS among the elderly have increased significantly. 3 Balancing ischemic and hemorrhagic risks poses treatment challenges for patients requiring both anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy, especially considering the pathophysiological characteristics of the elderly. 4 , 5

Currently, risk scores such as the CHA2DS2‐VASc score 6 and HAS‐BLED score 7 are recommended for predicting ischemia and bleeding, respectively. However, their performance is limited in patients with ACS and AF. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Almost all elderly patients with ACS and AF have CHA2DS2‐VASc scores above the recommended anticoagulation threshold. 14 Our previous study 13 found that none of the conventional models displayed good discrimination in elderly patients with AF and ACS.

Moreover, evidence‐based medicine for the elderly population remains limited. 15 Clinicians tend to prioritize intuitive evaluation and conservative treatment, leading to a lack of standardized antithrombotic therapy in this population. 16 , 17 , 18 Thus, we developed and validated effective prognostic models for bleeding and ischemic risk in elderly patients with ACS and AF. We also implemented user‐friendly online calculators to promote the application of clinical practice.

Methods

Data used in this study are not publicly available due to patient privacy concerns even after deidentification.

Population and Data Sources

In this study, from January 2015 to December 2019, 1252 elderly patients with ACS and nonvalvular AF (≥65 years old) at the First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital were consecutively enrolled in the training cohort for model development. The spatial validation cohort included 284 participants from the Sixth Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital. Eligible participants met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 65 years or older; (2) diagnosed with AF upon discharge, including paroxysmal, persistent, or permanent AF or a history of AF upon admission; and (3) diagnosed with ACS upon discharge, including unstable angina, non‐ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction, and ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) valvular AF secondary to severe mitral stenosis or a history of artificial heart valve disease; (2) allergies or contraindications to aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, or warfarin; (3) psychosocial diseases, alcohol or drug abuse; (4) in‐hospital death or noncardiac death; (5) reversible causes of AF; (6) incomplete or missing records of key variables; and (7) loss to follow‐up after discharge.

We also conducted the temporal validation, enrolling 315 participants from the First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital between January 2020 and December 2022. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with the training cohort and spatial validation cohort, with an additional criterion: patients receiving novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs) combined with antiplatelet therapy at discharge (Figure S1).

All individuals underwent a 1‐year follow‐up, with data analysis conducted between September 2023 and May 2024. This study protocol complied with the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (No. S2020‐485‐02) under registration number ChiCTR2200067185. Considering the retrospective study design, the need to obtain informed consent was waived. This study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis reporting guideline. 19

Variables

Baseline characteristics, including demographic data, past medical history, physical examination, laboratory examination, ECG and echocardiogram data, and treatment regimens, were obtained from electronic medical record database. Potential covariates were selected based on clinical consensus, literature review, expert consultation, and clinical data availability. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula 20 was applied to compute the estimated glomerular filtration rate. The continuous variables were discretized according to clinical practice and prior research. The constructed database had a few missing values, ranging from 0% to 2.3% for all variables except NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide; 7.2%) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (12.3%).

Outcomes

Follow‐up was performed via outpatient visits, telephone calls, or medical record reviews by trained cardiovascular physicians at 12 months. Investigators verified the source documents when the suspected adverse event first occurred. The primary outcomes were (1) thromboembolic events, including myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, noncentral nervous system embolism, definite or probable stent thrombosis, and target vessel revascularization; and (2) bleeding events, defined as Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) type 3 or 5 bleeding events. 21 The independent Clinical Events Committee adjudicated all end point events and was blinded to the baseline information.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the means±SDs or medians (interquartile ranges) and were compared using Student's t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. P<0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The missing values were imputed using the random forest algorithm. 22 Following Harrell's guideline, 23 the number of events was at least 10 times the number of predictors.

Model Building

The procedure used to establish the predictive models in this study is shown in Figure 1. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to filter the variables. Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were applied to further screen prognostic factors significantly associated with bleeding and ischemia. By intersecting the results of multivariable Cox regression analysis with LASSO regression results and incorporating clinical expertise, essential factors were selected to construct bleeding and ischemia models. The total scores for these models were determined by summing up the scores for each prognostic factor obtained from the nomogram. Consequently, the probability of patients experiencing outcome events at 3, 6, and 12 months was acquired from the corresponding Cox regression equation. These models were visualized as nomograms, and corresponding online calculators were developed with the “shiny” package.

Model Validation

First, discrimination was assessed by time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves and Harrell's concordance index (C‐index) in the development cohort and 2 external validation cohorts. The calibration and net clinical practicability of the models were subsequently evaluated by calibration curve and decision curve analysis. Individual risk scores were computed based on the nomogram. X‐tile software was used to determine the optimal cutoff points for each model to stratify patients into low‐, medium‐, or high‐risk groups. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log‐rank test. The newly developed models were compared with the previous risk scores by the C‐index, integrated discrimination improvement, 24 net reclassification improvement, 25 and decision curve analysis to further evaluate the models' merits. In addition, to examine the potential impact of anticoagulant drugs, such as vitamin K antagonist and NOACs, and antithrombotic therapy, including dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT) and triple antithrombotic therapy (TAT), on the models' performance, we conducted subgroup analyses in the development cohort and two validation cohorts. The C‐index and Brier score were employed to evaluate the discrimination and calibration of distinct subgroups, respectively.

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 (R Foundation), X‐tile 3.6.1 (Yale University), and MedCalc 20.022 (MedCalc Software Ltd.).

Results

Population

From January 2015 to December 2019, a total of 1252 elderly patients with comorbid ACS and AF were enrolled in the development cohort (age, median 77 [range, 71–83] years; 790 were male [63.1%]), and 284 patients were enrolled in the spatial validation cohort (age, median 81 [range, 76–86] years; 172 were male [60.6%]). The detailed characteristics are summarized in Table. After 1‐year follow‐up, BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding events occurred in 79 patients (5.1%), and thromboembolic events occurred in 222 (14.5%). Patients with primary end points were elderly and had more comorbidities than those without endpoints (Table S1). Furthermore, 315 patients who received NOAC combined with antiplatelet therapy were enrolled in the temporal validation cohort from January 2020 to December 2022 (age, median 79 [range, 74–84] years; 205 were male [65.1%]). During 1‐year follow‐up, the incidence of BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding and thromboembolic events was 5.4% and 7.3%, respectively (Table S2).

Feature Selection

The LASSO algorithm was applied according to the shrinkage penalty by 10‐fold cross‐validation for stable variable selection. Table S3 presents the allocations of the potential covariates. Out of 37 factors, with the lambda of the minimum partial likelihood deviance, 8 significant factors with nonzero penalty coefficients served as potential predictors of bleeding. Similarly, 37 baseline clinical features were reduced to 21 potential predictors of ischemia (Figure 2).

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses demonstrated that body mass index <18.5 kg/m2 (hazard ratio [HR], 1.504 [95% CI, 1.286–1.888]), chronic renal failure (HR, 2.482 [95% CI, 1.390–4.433]), prior bleeding (HR, 2.923 [95% CI, 1.543–5.537]), and TAT (HR, 2.841 [95% CI, 1.383–5.838]) were associated with a higher probability of BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding after adjusting for other variables (Table S4). Similarly, on multivariable Cox regression analysis, age (HR, 1.033 [95% CI, 1.012–1.054]), heart failure (HR, 2.691 [95% CI, 1.724–4.201]), hyperlipidemia (HR, 1.753 [95% CI, 1.281–2.400]), diabetes (HR, 1.930 [95% CI, 1.436–2.595]), prior coronary artery bypass graft (HR, 2.585 [95% CI, 1.473–4.534]), previous myocardial infarction (HR, 2.112 [95% CI, 1.491–2.992]), and inpatient diagnosis of non‐ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (HR, 1.453 [95% CI, 1.284–1.722]) could independently predict ischemia in elderly patients with ACS and AF (Table S5).

Development and Validation of the Models

Based on LASSO and Cox regression analyses combined with clinical expertise, we incorporated age, body mass index, chronic renal failure, prior bleeding, and TAT into the nomogram for the bleeding model (Figure 3A). For BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding, the C‐indexes of the nomogram at 3, 6, and 12 months were 0.845 (95% CI, 0.753–0.937), 0.742 (95% CI, 0.655–0.828), and 0.737 (95% CI, 0.671–0.803), respectively (Figure 3C). In the 2 external validation cohorts, the C‐index ranged from 0.715 (95% CI, 0.570–0.860) to 0.809 (95% CI, 0.618–1.000), suggesting good predictive performance (Figure S2A and S2C). Additionally, we integrated age, heart failure, hyperlipidemia, prior coronary artery bypass graft, prior myocardial infarction, ACS type, and diabetes into the nomogram for the ischemia model (Figure 3B). Analogously, the nomogram for predicting the probability of ischemia also showed good prognostic accuracy, with the C‐index varying from 0.679 (95% CI, 0.574–0.785) to 0.777 (95% CI, 0.676–0.877) (Figure 3D, Figure S2B and S2D).

Time‐specific calibration plots of the nomogram for bleeding within 1 year after baseline displayed good agreement between the estimated probabilities and observed outcomes (Hosmer–Lemeshow P>0.05) (Figure S3). Subgroup analyses on discrimination and calibration showed similar results (Tables S6–S9). Decision curve analysis was used to quantify the net clinical practicability of the nomogram for bleeding incidence, demonstrating its benefits in supporting clinical decision‐making (Figure S4). Similarly, in the development and 2 external validation cohorts, the 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐month calibration curves (Figure S5) and decision curves (Figure S6) of the ischemia model revealed stable conformity and clinical utility.

Risk Stratification

For BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding events, patients in the development cohort were divided into low‐, medium‐, and high‐risk groups according to the optimal cutoff point of the predicted value through X‐tile software (Table S10, Figure S7A). As shown in Figure 4A, the risk of cumulative BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding in the low‐ and medium‐risk groups was significantly lower than that in the high‐risk group (P<0.001). Analogously, patients were stratified into low‐, medium‐, and high‐risk groups according to the probability of ischemia (6.98%, 17.35%, and 43.18%, respectively) (Table S11, Figure S7B). Kaplan–Meier curves of the different groups revealed significant differences (Figure 4B). Furthermore, similar results were obtained for patients in the 2 external validation cohorts (Figure S7C through S7F).

Model Comparison

Because patient status may change over time, we performed performance tests at various time points. We found that the performance of our newly developed nomogram was significantly superior to that of the HAS‐BLED, 7 PARIS‐MB (Patterns of Non‐Adherence to Anti‐Platelet Regimen in Stented Patients–Major Bleeding), 26 and PRECISE‐DAPT (Predicting Bleeding Complications in Patients Undergoing Stent Implantation and Subsequent Dual Antiplatelet Therapy)27 scores at each time point for BARC type 3 or 5 bleeding in elderly patients with ACS and AF (Table S12). When considering the ischemia end point, the reported C‐index values showed that the performance of all the models deteriorated over time (Table S13). Compared with those of the GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events), 28 PARIS‐CTE (PARIS–Coronary Thrombotic Events), 26 and CHA2DS2‐VASc 29 scores, the newly developed model had significantly greater C‐indexes. We additionally quantified the added value by using the net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement, illustrating the improved performance of the newly developed models over the existing models (all P values<0.001) (Tables S14 and S15). Regarding decision‐analytic measures, the newly developed models provided notably greater gains than the existing risk scores, indicating that their superiority led to the best decision (Figure S8).

Web‐Based Application

To visualize the prognostic models, we constructed free and easy‐to‐use online calculators for predicting the risk of bleeding and ischemia (https://elderlyacsaf.shinyapps.io/bleeding‐ischemia‐prediction/) in elderly patients with ACS and AF at future follow‐up time points.

Discussion

In this prognostic cohort study, based on routine demographic and clinical data availability, we first developed robust and well‐calibrated separate nomograms to predict the risk of bleeding and ischemia in elderly patients with ACS and AF at different time points. Although most predictive factors were distinct for each end point, age was a common predictor of these 2 events. Each nomogram had good discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility and effectively stratifying the risk of bleeding and ischemia in the development and two external validation cohorts. The similarity of the C‐indexes in the development and validation cohorts proved that the data were not overfitting. Moreover, the predictive performance of the newly constructed nomograms was superior to that of previous scores. To facilitate clinical practice, we developed web calculators corresponding to the nomograms that can automatically predict bleeding or ischemic risk by inputting the values of the corresponding prediction variables.

In this study, we used LASSO and Cox regression analyses to determine the main predictors of bleeding and ischemia, the results of which were consistent with the findings of previous studies. 4 , 12 , 30 , 31 , 32 Currently, the benefits of DAT over TAT have been observed in elderly patients. 31 , 33 However, the optimal timing of downgrading from TAT to DAT is still controversial. 34 , 35 , 36 Our study provides a risk assessment tool that helps predict the ischemic and bleeding risk of patients to formulate an individualized downgrading regimen of antithrombotic therapy, with early discontinuation of aspirin and substitution of the DAT for the TAT to reduce bleeding events. The AUGUSTUS (Open‐Label, 2×2 Factorial, Randomized, Controlled Clinical Trial to Evaluate the Safety of Apixaban vs Vitamin K Antagonist and Aspirin vs Aspirin Placebo in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Acute Coronary Syndrome and/or Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) trial 12 showed that renal insufficiency significantly increased the risk of bleeding. However, NOACs such as dabigatran and apixaban were found to be safe and effective for patients with impaired renal function. 12 , 33 A previous study 37 revealed a significant reduction in coronary artery embolism with cholesterol‐lowering therapy in patients aged ≥75 years (10.6% versus 12.8%, P=0.002). This finding suggested that we should dynamically update the model scores based on changes in risk factors. Therefore, we developed online calculators that can provide personalized risk estimates and be updated as new clinical data become accessible.

Guidelines 14 , 38 suggested that risk scores should be considered when choosing the optimal antithrombotic treatment strategy for patients with ACS and AF. The HAS‐BLED score has been widely used to predict the risk of bleeding. 39 , 40 However, vitamin K antagonists were mostly used in patients in the HAS‐BLED cohort, 7 and patients who could not take anticoagulants were excluded, which may have led to an underestimation of bleeding risk. Coincidentally, the development cohort of the PRECISE‐DAPT score 27 excluded all patients treated with NOACs. Unlike previous prognostic models, our study included elderly patients regardless of their antithrombotic regimen, thus presenting the real‐world scenario. Moreover, Savonitto 41 reported that 77% of elderly patients were at high risk of bleeding according to the PRECISE‐DAPT score, suggesting that the recommended cutoff points should be adjusted. The CHA2DS2‐VASc score is recommended by current guidelines 42 for assessing the ischemic risk of patients with AF. However, a previous study 43 revealed that the C‐index of the CHA2DS2‐VASc score for predicting 1‐year ischemic stroke in patients with AF treated with percutaneous coronary intervention was only 0.580 (95% CI, 0.439–0.721). According to the CHA2DS2‐VASc score, almost all elderly patients with ACS and AF are indicated to receive antithrombotic therapy unless there is a specific contraindication. Therefore, the CHA2DS2‐VASc score is not significant for risk stratification in this population. As previously reported, 8 , 44 our study also revealed the unsatisfactory predictive performance of the aforementioned traditional scoring systems in elderly patients with ACS and AF, and our newly constructed models could be more applicable in this special population.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several clinical strengths. First, we developed more effective assessment tools for estimating the risk of bleeding and ischemia in this population, the results of which showed excellent performance, clinical practicability, and risk stratification ability. Second, the treatment status of patients with ACS and AF has undergone significant changes with the widespread use of NOACs 45 and further advances in new coronary stents and left atrial appendage occlusion. Our model is more in line with the current state of diagnosis and treatment of this population and is convenient for clinical promotion. Third, we developed web calculators to quantify risks; these tools are relatively simple and easy to apply clinically.

This study has several limitations. First, the present study was retrospective. Therefore, it was not possible to exclude all potential biases, but our modeling sample size was relatively large, and external validation was carried out with 2 data sets. Second, the development cohort is based on real‐world study from 2015 to 2019. With the evolution of modern antithrombotic therapy regimens, the percentages of NOAC and DAT usage have changed. The models showed relatively stable performance in subgroup analyses and the temporal validation cohort, indicating that our models can maintain a certain level of accuracy and stability in different treatment strategies. However, as clinical prediction models ultimately aim to serve clinical applications, follow‐up studies with large sample sizes and prospective cohorts from different regions are needed to further validate the clinical utility of our models. Third, we addressed several common methodological limitations of predictive modeling studies, such as the lack of the inclusion of biological predictors (eg, genetic information and serum biomarkers) that are not routinely available. Thus, additional external data sets will be needed in the future to validate and update the nomogram.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed and validated 2 prognostic models for the risk of bleeding and ischemia in elderly patients with comorbid ACS and AF based on real‐world data and confirmed their robust predictive performance and clinical utility. These models could assist in identifying risk stratification, and the corresponding online calculators may help clinicians determine the optimal benefit–risk ratio of antithrombotic regimens for individual patients.
