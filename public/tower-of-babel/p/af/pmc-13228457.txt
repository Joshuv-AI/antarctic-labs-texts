Advances in the management of allergic rhinitis: clinical relevance of the mometasone furoate–olopatadine association and the challenge of patient self-diagnosis and self-treatment
Authors: Maria D’Amato, Gennaro D’Amato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Allergic rhinitis is a highly prevalent inflammatory disease that significantly impairs sleep, daily functioning and quality of life, with symptoms increasingly amplified by environmental changes such as prolonged pollen seasons and urban pollution. Despite effective therapies, many patients remain poorly controlled due to delayed diagnosis, inappropriate self-selection of over-the-counter medications, limited adherence and incorrect intranasal technique. The fixed-dose intranasal combination of mometasone furoate and olopatadine addresses these gaps by uniting rapid antihistaminic, mast-cell stabilizing effects with potent, sustained anti-inflammatory activity. Clinical trials demonstrate superior improvement of nasal and ocular symptoms, faster onset of action, and favourable long-term safety compared with monotherapies. Its single-device formulation may enhance adherence and reduce inappropriate polypharmacy, offering a practical, guideline-aligned option for optimizing management of allergic rhinitis and mitigating risks associated with patient self-treatment.

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a highly prevalent inflammatory condition affecting a substantial proportion of children and adults worldwide and is strongly associated with impaired sleep, reduced work and school performance, diminished quality of life and frequent coexistence with asthma.1,2 Beyond seasonal patterns, climate change, prolonged pollen seasons and urban pollution contribute to persistent symptoms throughout the year.1,2

Traditional AR management — primarily based on oral antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids (INS), leukotriene receptor antagonists (LTRAs) and immunotherapy — often provides incomplete symptom control due to delayed onset of action, adherence problems, poor spray technique and inappropriate self-selection of therapies.3–5 These limitations have supported the development of advanced topical therapies, including fixed-dose intranasal combinations engineered to provide broader anti-inflammatory coverage and more rapid relief.6

The combination of mometasone furoate (MF) and olopatadine hydrochloride (OLO) (MF/OLO) integrates a potent INS with low systemic bioavailability3,4,7 and an antihistamine with antihistaminic, mast-cell stabilizing and ocular efficacy.8–10 This dual mechanism provides complementary control of both early-phase and late-phase allergic inflammation.

On the other hand, self-diagnosis and self-medication represent a growing public health concern, contributing to treatment delays, inappropriate drug choices and poor disease control.5 Within this context, a fixed-dose MF/OLO therapy may offer a simplified and effective option. Across multiple clinical trials, the MF/OLO combination improved nasal symptoms and quality-of-life outcomes more effectively than single components or placebo, with sustained benefits in perennial AR and a favourable safety profile.6,11–14

This narrative review examines advances in AR management with a focus on the fixed-dose MF/OLO combination,15 contextualizing its pharmacological rationale, clinical evidence and potential role in addressing persistent treatment gaps and the growing issue of self-diagnosis and self-medication.

Methods

A narrative review was performed to evaluate evidence on MF/OLO combination therapy and patient self-treatment of AR. PubMed was searched for English-language, human studies published from 1990 to present using combinations of the following keywords: “allergic rhinitis”, “mometasone furoate”, “olopatadine”, “intranasal combination therapy” and “self-management”.

Articles were selected based on clinical relevance, study design (with priority given to randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews and key pharmacological studies), and applicability to real-world clinical practice. Emphasis was placed on studies evaluating MF/OLO combination therapy, INS, antihistamines and patient behaviour in AR management. Given the narrative nature of the review, no formal systematic selection process was applied; however, the authors aimed to include the most clinically relevant and widely cited evidence. The potential for selection bias inherent to narrative reviews is acknowledged.

Review

Allergic rhinitis

AR is an IgE-mediated inflammatory disorder of the nasal mucosa triggered by exposure to sensitizing allergens, clinically characterized by nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea, sneezing and itching, frequently accompanied by ocular symptoms such as redness, tearing and itching.10,16 AR is classified as intermittent or persistent depending on symptom duration (<4 days/week or <4 weeks/year versus ≥4 days/week or ≥4 weeks/year), and as mild or moderate/severe according to the degree of functional impairment and impact on daily activities.10,16

AR is amongst the most common chronic conditions globally, with prevalence in European adults ranging from 17% to 28%.16 Climatic and environmental changes contribute to prolonged pollen seasons and increased allergen exposure, helping to explain rising prevalence trends and greater symptom persistence.1,2

The disease imposes a significant socioeconomic and quality-of-life burden. Moderate-to-severe AR is associated with impaired work and school performance, limitations in sports and social activities, and sleep disturbances.16 Poorly controlled symptoms can contribute to complications, such as sinusitis or otitis media, and often coexist with asthma and other atopic conditions.1,16 AR also substantially affects patient-reported outcomes, with clinical tools such as the visual analogue scale facilitating assessment and monitoring of disease control.16

Despite the availability of effective therapies, including oral antihistamines, INS, LTRAs and immunotherapy, many patients continue to experience inadequate control. Persistent symptoms may be attributed to the delayed onset of action of certain therapies, suboptimal adherence, incorrect nasal spray technique and inappropriate self-selection of treatments, particularly over-the-counter (OTC) antihistamines.3,5 Surveys indicate that a large proportion of patients rely on multiple agents, often without professional guidance, yet still report insufficient relief.6

Pathophysiology of AR

AR results from an IgE-mediated inflammatory cascade initiated when sensitized individuals inhale specific allergens that cross-link IgE bound to mast cells in the nasal mucosa.10,16 This interaction triggers the early-phase response, characterized by immediate release of histamine and other preformed mediators, leading to sneezing, itching and rhinorrhoea.8,9 Histamine is the dominant mediator responsible for many acute nasal and ocular symptoms, explaining the rapid symptomatic relief achieved with antihistamines.8

The late-phase response develops hours after exposure and is driven by recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, including eosinophils, basophils and T lymphocytes. These cells release leukotrienes and a broad array of cytokines that contribute to sustained nasal congestion and mucosal oedema.3,7 MF, for example, reduces production of multiple cytokines (IL-1, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF) and inhibits leukotriene release, reflecting the central role of corticosteroids in controlling the chronic inflammatory component of AR7 (Figure 1).

AR pathophysiology therefore involves both an immediate histamine-driven phase and a delayed, cytokine-mediated inflammatory phase. This dual mechanism underpins the rationale for combining therapies that target complementary pathways. Antihistamines, such as OLO, provide rapid inhibition of histamine-mediated symptoms and exhibit mast-cell-stabilizing effects.8,9 INS, including MF, exerts broad anti-inflammatory actions with high topical potency and minimal systemic exposure.7

Addressing both the early and late phases is essential for optimal disease control, particularly in moderate-to-severe AR. The complementary pharmacological actions of antihistamines and INS justify the therapeutic development of fixed-dose combinations designed to act rapidly whilst providing sustained anti-inflammatory effects.

Current therapeutic landscape in AR

Current management of AR relies on a range of pharmacological options that act on different components of the allergic cascade. Despite this apparent therapeutic richness, many patients remain symptomatic or only partly controlled in routine practice, often using several medications at the same time.5,6 A clear understanding of the strengths and limitations of each class is essential to interpreting the rationale for fixed-dose topical combinations.

Oral H1-antihistamines are usually the first medication tried by patients and are widely available without prescription. They effectively reduce sneezing, rhinorrhoea and nasal itching by competitively blocking histamine H1 receptors, but their impact on nasal congestion is modest.16 Older molecules may cause sedation and anticholinergic effects, whereas second-generation agents have a better safety profile.16 However, population surveys and pharmacy-based studies show that many individuals self-diagnose AR and repeatedly use oral antihistamines as monotherapy, underestimating the chronic inflammatory nature of the disease and failing to achieve adequate control.5,6 This contributes to persistent symptoms and frequent switching between different oral products.

Intranasal antihistamines were developed to provide a rapid, locally acting alternative to oral agents. OLO combines selective H1 antagonism with mast-cell-stabilizing properties, reducing histamine release and other mediator-driven responses in the nasal mucosa.8,9 Clinical data show that intranasal OLO provides a fast onset of symptom relief and improves ocular manifestations, an important feature for patients with rhinoconjunctivitis.10 Nevertheless, intranasal antihistamines have limited effects on the broader network of cytokines and chemokines involved in late-phase inflammation, and they are generally less effective than INS for persistent nasal congestion.3

INS is considered the most effective monotherapy for moderate-to-severe AR. MF, in particular, demonstrates high topical potency, strong glucocorticoid receptor affinity and minimal systemic absorption.3,4,7 MF inhibits a broad spectrum of inflammatory mediators, including multiple interleukins and leukotrienes.3 It improves all nasal symptoms, including congestion, and can also have beneficial effects on ocular complaints in some patients.3 However, maximal therapeutic benefit may require several days to 2 weeks, and some patients perceive the onset as slow, contributing to early discontinuation or irregular use.3,16 Concerns about ‘steroids’, fear of long-term adverse effects and incorrect spray technique further limit real-world effectiveness.5,16

LLTRAs, such as montelukast, act by blocking cysteinyl leukotriene pathways involved in nasal congestion, mucosal oedema and bronchial inflammation. Recent meta-analyses confirm that LTRAs are more effective than placebo and that montelukast–antihistamine combinations improve daytime nasal symptoms and several individual symptom domains compared with monotherapy, although the magnitude of benefit is modest and varies according to the specific antihistamine used.17 As a result, LTRAs are usually reserved for patients with comorbid asthma or for those who cannot tolerate or do not accept INS, rather than being used as first-line therapy in isolated AR.

Allergen immunotherapy (AIT), delivered subcutaneously or sublingually, is the only disease-modifying intervention for AR and targets the underlying immune response rather than symptoms alone. AIT can reduce symptom burden and medication use in the long term but requires careful patient selection, specialist supervision and multi-year adherence.16 Consequently, many patients with AR never receive AIT and continue to depend exclusively on pharmacotherapy.

Across these options, real-world limitations are consistently observed. Patients often self-diagnose AR, treat it as a minor or transient condition and rely on OTC antihistamines, nasal decongestants or herbal products.5 Even when prescription therapies, such as INS, are initiated, adherence is frequently poor, devices are not used correctly and combinations of multiple agents are taken without a clear strategy.5,6 These patterns lead to polypharmacy, persistent symptoms and under-recognition of more severe disease and comorbidities. Within this therapeutic landscape, advanced topical strategies have been developed to simplify treatment and improve outcomes. Fixed-dose intranasal combinations aim to unify complementary mechanisms — rapid histamine blockade and broad anti-inflammatory activity — within a single spray. The MF/OLO combination (Rineffix) was designed to offer the rapid onset of an intranasal antihistamine together with the powerful and sustained anti-inflammatory effects of a modern INS.6,10 This approach is consistent with contemporary Allergic Rhinitis and Its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) recommendations, which favour integrated care pathways and escalation to combination therapy in patients whose symptoms are not adequately controlled with monotherapy.18,19

Amongst available intranasal combination therapies, the azelastine/fluticasone combination is widely used in patients with moderate-to-severe AR. Compared with this combination, MF/OLO may offer some distinct pharmacological and tolerability characteristics. MF is associated with very low systemic bioavailability,3,7 whilst OLO has demonstrated favourable local tolerability, which may influence patient preference and adherence.8,10

Although direct head-to-head comparisons between MF/OLO and other intranasal combinations are not currently available, the use of combination therapy is consistent with ARIA stepwise treatment approaches, in which treatment is intensified when symptom control is not achieved with monotherapy. In this framework, INS represents first-line therapy for many patients, and combination approaches may be considered in cases of persistent or more severe symptoms.6,18 In this context, MF/OLO may represent an alternative option within current therapeutic algorithms for patients requiring broader and more rapid symptom control.

Pharmacological rationale for combining MF and OLO

The rationale for combining MF and OLO arises directly from the dual-phase pathophysiology of AR. The early phase is dominated by histamine release following mast cell activation, causing sneezing, itching, rhinorrhoea and ocular symptoms. OLO is a selective H1-antihistamine with a rapid onset of action and additional mast-cell-stabilizing activity, which inhibits mediator release beyond histamine alone.8,9 These properties explain its demonstrated effects on both nasal and ocular manifestations and its utility for rapid relief.10

The late phase of AR involves the recruitment of eosinophils, basophils and T lymphocytes, accompanied by the production of cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-6 and IL-8) and leukotrienes, leading to nasal congestion and mucosal inflammation. MF exerts potent anti-inflammatory activity by binding the glucocorticoid receptor with high affinity and suppressing a wide spectrum of pro-inflammatory mediators.3,4,7 It offers high topical potency and minimal systemic bioavailability, supporting favourable safety during chronic use. MF is also effective in reducing nasal obstruction, the symptom least responsive to antihistamines.3

Because no single class addresses both phases sufficiently, many patients combine agents independently, often without medical guidance and with inconsistent adherence.5 However, using medications separately increases complexity, reduces adherence and is associated with inadequate control.6 A fixed-dose combination delivered through a single device can improve simplicity, convenience and consistent dosing whilst ensuring simultaneous coverage of both histamine-mediated and inflammatory components.

MF and OLO are pharmacologically complementary: OLO acts rapidly within minutes, whilst MF provides sustained anti-inflammatory effects that develop gradually but comprehensively. Their combination therefore addresses both immediate symptoms (via OLO) and persistent nasal congestion and inflammation (via MF). Lamb10 highlights this dual mechanism as central to achieving broad control of nasal and ocular symptoms.

Evidence from randomized controlled trials supports the clinical relevance of this complementary action, showing that the fixed-dose MF/OLO combination provides faster symptom relief and broader efficacy across nasal and ocular domains than INS monotherapy, without compromising tolerability.6,11,12 The ARIA guidelines also support escalation to combination therapy when monotherapy is insufficient and recognize the role of topical combinations within modern, personalized AR management pathways.18,19

Taken together, these factors provide a strong pharmacological basis for the MF/OLO fixed-dose combination: unified delivery, rapid onset, strong anti-inflammatory activity and simplified real-world use (Figure 2).

MF is a high-potency INS with strong affinity for the glucocorticoid receptor and extensive anti-inflammatory activity. It inhibits a wide range of cytokines and inflammatory mediators, including IL-1, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF, which contribute to oedema, mucosal inflammation and persistent nasal obstruction.3,4 MF displays very low systemic bioavailability due to its poor oral absorption and extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism.7,15 This pharmacokinetic profile minimizes systemic corticosteroid exposure and contributes to a favourable safety profile during long-term use. Systemic effects, such as adrenal suppression, are unlikely at recommended intranasal doses.15 MF is particularly effective in reducing nasal congestion, a symptom that responds poorly to antihistamines alone.3

The rationale for combining MF and OLO is based on the complementary targeting of the early and late phases of allergic inflammation. OLO provides rapid blockade of H1-mediated symptoms and reduces mast-cell activation, whilst MF suppresses recruitment of inflammatory cells and prevents cytokine-driven persistence of symptoms. This dual action mirrors the two-phase immunopathology of AR and offers broader coverage than either drug alone.3,8

Potential synergistic clinical effects arise from the complementary mechanisms: OLO provides immediate relief and may improve patient adherence by delivering perceptible early benefits, whilst MF ensures sustained control of congestion and mucosal inflammation. This combined approach reduces the need for multiple separate sprays, simplifying treatment and potentially improving consistency of use — an important advantage given the high rates of poor adherence and inappropriate polypharmacy seen in routine care.5

Compared with monotherapy or use of two separate formulations, the fixed-dose MF/OLO combination offers unified delivery, faster onset of action than corticosteroid alone and broader therapeutic coverage across nasal and ocular symptoms.10 Evidence from randomized controlled trials shows superior reductions in total nasal symptom scores and nasal congestion with the combination versus individual components, supporting its clinical relevance in patients with insufficient control on single-agent therapy.6,11,12

This pharmacological and clinical foundation supports the clinical development and real-world application of the combination in patients with insufficient control on single-agent therapy. In addition, the favourable safety profile of the combination is supported by the low systemic bioavailability of MF, which undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism, thereby minimizing systemic exposure and the risk of clinically relevant effects such as hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis suppression.3,7

Clinical evidence for MF/OLO fixed-dose combination

The clinical development programme for the fixed-dose MF/OLO combination includes several randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials conducted in patients with seasonal AR (SAR) and perennial AR (PAR). Collectively, these studies demonstrate significant improvements in nasal and ocular symptoms, rapid onset of action and a favourable safety profile.

Key randomized controlled trials

In two large phase III trials of SAR, Gross11 and Hampel12 showed that MF/OLO produced significantly greater reductions in total nasal symptom score (TNSS) compared with placebo and each individual single component. Improvements were observed across all four nasal symptoms — congestion, rhinorrhoea, sneezing and itching. In a further randomized study, Andrews13 evaluated once-daily versus twice-daily MF/OLO, confirming clinically meaningful reductions in reflective TNSS and demonstrating that both regimens were effective, with twice-daily dosing providing numerically greater relief in some symptom domains. Lim6 summarizes these controlled trials, emphasizing that MF/OLO consistently outperformed monotherapies in SAR.

Long-term efficacy in PAR was evaluated by Segall,14 who conducted a 52-week study demonstrating sustained TNSS improvement and stable symptom control throughout the treatment period. This trial also contributed key safety data over extended use.

Onset of action

Rapid onset is an important distinguishing feature of MF/OLO. Across SAR studies, symptom relief was detectable as early as 30 minutes after administration, as described in the analyses summarized by Lim6 and in the primary publications, within 15 minutes.11,12 The contribution of OLO to rapid action is highlighted by pharmacological studies demonstrating its fast antihistaminic effect and mast-cell stabilizing properties.8,9 The perception of rapid symptom relief may also be clinically relevant in supporting treatment persistence. Early lack of perceived efficacy is a known factor contributing to poor adherence with INS monotherapy, whereas faster onset of action may help reduce early treatment discontinuation and improve patient satisfaction.5,6

Impact on individual symptom domains

Nasal congestion, often the most troublesome and refractory symptom, improved significantly with MF/OLO compared with single components.11,12 The strong decongestant effect is consistent with the potent anti-inflammatory actions of MF.3 Ocular symptoms, including itching, redness and tearing, also improved significantly, in line with the known activity of OLO on ocular allergic pathways.8,10 Composite endpoints, such as the total symptom score (TSS) and reflective TNSS, demonstrated robust and sustained improvements.

Safety and tolerability

Across SAR and PAR trials, MF/OLO was well tolerated. The incidence of adverse events was low, mostly mild, and comparable to single components and placebo.11,14 No clinically meaningful systemic corticosteroid effects were observed, consistent with the low systemic bioavailability of MF.7 Nasal irritation and headache were the most common adverse events, occurring at similar rates across treatment arms.

Real-world effectiveness and patient-reported outcomes

Real-world prescribing data highlight that many patients require multiple therapies to achieve adequate symptom control and that adherence to INS alone is often suboptimal.5

In this context, a single-spray fixed combination offers a simplified therapeutic approach that may improve adherence and patient satisfaction. Lamb10 emphasizes that MF/OLO can reduce the need for separate sprays and deliver more consistent symptom control across both nasal and ocular domains. However, dedicated real-world comparative studies between fixed-dose combinations and free combinations remain limited, highlighting the need for further research in routine clinical settings.5,6

Relevance for moderate-to-severe or difficult-to-control AR

Evidence across SAR and PAR indicates that MF/OLO is particularly suitable for patients whose symptoms are not adequately controlled with monotherapy. Lim6 underscores the clinical need for rapid, comprehensive symptom relief in moderate-to-severe AR and positions MF/OLO as an effective option within this population. ARIA guidance supporting escalation to combination therapy further supports its role in the treatment of patients with more symptoms or with treatment-resistant AR.19 The main randomized studies supporting the efficacy and safety of the MF/OLO combination are summarized in Table 4.

Although the available randomized controlled trials consistently demonstrate the efficacy and safety of the MF/OLO combination, several limitations should be considered. Most studies were conducted in controlled clinical settings with selected patient populations, which may not fully reflect real-world heterogeneity.11,12 Study durations were relatively short in SAR trials, whereas long-term data are mainly derived from PAR populations.14 In addition, comparisons were primarily performed against placebo or individual components rather than against other active combination therapies. These aspects should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings to routine clinical practice.6

Patient behaviour in AR: the growing problem of self-diagnosis and self-treatment

Self-diagnosis and self-treatment are increasingly recognized as major contributors to inadequate management of AR. Population studies show that many individuals perceive AR as a ‘minor’ or self-limiting condition, leading them to rely on OTC medications without seeking medical assessment.5 This behaviour is reinforced by the chronic and fluctuating pattern of symptoms, which patients often attribute to transient irritation rather than an underlying inflammatory disease. As a result, diagnosis is frequently delayed, and treatment choices are guided more by convenience and marketing than by clinical effectiveness.

Epidemiology of self-diagnosis

Paoletti5 reports that a large proportion of patients with rhinitis symptoms manage their condition entirely through self-care, often without any formal diagnosis. OTC antihistamines are the most commonly used agents, driven by the widespread belief that AR is primarily histamine-mediated and easily controlled with oral products. However, this perception overlooks the complexity of AR pathophysiology and the need for anti-inflammatory therapy, particularly in persistent or moderate-to-severe disease.16

Risks of self-treatment

Misdiagnosis is amongst the most significant risks. Symptoms attributed to AR may instead reflect non-allergic rhinitis, infectious rhinosinusitis or chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps — conditions requiring different treatment strategies. Paoletti5 highlights that patients rarely distinguish between these entities, leading to inappropriate medication use and prolonged symptom burden.

Poor symptom control is common amongst self-treating individuals. Reliance on oral antihistamines alone often fails to address nasal congestion and late-phase inflammation, resulting in chronic symptoms and repeated seasonal exacerbations.16 Many patients escalate use by combining multiple antihistamines or adding topical decongestants, often without improvement.5 Delayed diagnosis of comorbidities is a further consequence. AR commonly coexists with asthma, allergic conjunctivitis and atopic dermatitis,2 yet patients who self-manage seldom undergo evaluation for these conditions. This increases the risk of undertreatment, especially in individuals with asthma–rhinitis overlap.

Overuse of sedating antihistamines remains a problem. Paoletti5 reports that first-generation antihistamines are still frequently available as OTC despite their sedative potential and unfavourable safety profile. Furthermore, incorrect use of intranasal sprays is widespread amongst those who self-treat. Insufficient instruction leads to poor delivery, reduced mucosal deposition and diminished clinical benefit.16 This contributes to the mistaken belief that INS are ineffective, reinforcing the reliance on oral agents.

Underutilization of effective medications, particularly INS, is a consistent finding in real-world analyses. Despite being the most effective monotherapy for moderate-to-severe AR, INS are used inconsistently or avoided due to misconceptions about ‘steroid’ safety.5

Opportunities for improving disease management

Improved patient guidance is essential. ARIA methodology documents emphasize the need for structured, evidence-based management pathways, including the use of validated symptom assessment tools and appropriate pharmacological escalation (ARIA–EAACI Methodology Paper). Pharmacists play a crucial role, as they are often the first and only healthcare professionals consulted by patients with rhinitis symptoms.5 Strengthening their role in triage, education and treatment optimization can significantly improve outcomes.

Public education on the chronic and inflammatory nature of AR is equally important. Encouraging patients to seek proper diagnosis and understanding the limitations of OTC-only treatment can reduce undertreatment and improve long-term control (Figure 3). Clinical positioning of MF/OLO in modern AR management

Modern AR management follows stepwise principles outlined in ARIA and EAACI frameworks, which emphasize symptom severity, patient-reported control and real-world feasibility of treatment strategies.19,20 Within this structure, the fixed-dose MF/OLO combination is positioned as an option for patients whose symptoms are not adequately controlled with monotherapy, particularly INS or antihistamines alone.

From a practical perspective, MF/OLO is best positioned as an escalation therapy in patients with moderate-to-severe AR who remain symptomatic despite INS or antihistamines alone. This positioning is consistent with stepwise approaches proposed in ARIA frameworks, which support treatment intensification when symptom control is insufficient.19

MF/OLO is especially suitable for moderate-to-severe AR, where single agents often fail to provide sufficient relief across nasal and ocular symptom domains. Evidence from SAR and PAR trials demonstrates superior reductions in TNSS, nasal congestion and ocular symptoms compared with single components.11,12,14 Its rapid onset of action, attributable to OLO, addresses the impatience or early discontinuation frequently observed with INS alone, whilst the potent anti-inflammatory activity of MF ensures sustained control.3,8

Suitable patient phenotypes include those with persistent nasal congestion, mixed nasal–ocular symptoms, or poor adherence to multiple separate therapies. MF/OLO can streamline treatment by replacing combinations of separate sprays often used inconsistently in self-management.5 Patients who habitually rely on OTC antihistamines or who underestimate the inflammatory nature of AR may also benefit from this simplified fixed-dose approach.

By offering rapid relief and broad inflammatory control, MF/OLO may help reduce risks associated with self-treatment, including inappropriate polypharmacy, delayed diagnosis and chronic undertreatment.5 Its formulation in a single device can improve adherence and facilitate guideline-concordant therapy, addressing barriers highlighted in real-world observational data.6

Future directions in AR treatment

Future AR management is moving toward increased personalization, improved adherence technologies and multimodal approaches to disease control. Novel intranasal combinations are under investigation, continuing the trend initiated by MF/OLO by pairing fast-acting antihistamines with potent anti-inflammatory agents to further enhance onset and coverage.10 Advances in formulation technology may also improve mucosal deposition and patient experience.

Digital adherence monitoring represents a promising frontier. Emerging ‘smart’ inhalers and nasal spray devices, incorporating sensors and app connectivity, can track use patterns and symptom fluctuations. Although current evidence is more developed in asthma, similar principles are expected to benefit AR by improving technique, adherence and data-driven treatment adjustments (ARIA–EAACI Methodology Paper).

Personalized medicine approaches are likely to become increasingly relevant, with biomarker-driven selection aimed at differentiating allergic from non-allergic rhinitis, identifying polysensitized patients and predicting response to specific therapies. Integration of pharmacological treatment with AIT remains central to long-term management. As highlighted by Lipiec,16 combining symptom control therapies with disease-modifying strategies may improve overall outcomes and adherence to AIT programmes.

Future AR care will therefore combine effective topical combinations like MF/OLO with digital tools, personalized pathways and immunomodulatory strategies.

Emerging digital tools, including smart inhalers and mobile health (mHealth) applications, may further improve adherence and enable real-time monitoring of treatment use and symptom control. Digital health approaches in allergic diseases have shown potential to enhance adherence, support patient self-management and provide clinically relevant feedback on symptoms and treatment patterns.21,22 In addition, early studies suggest that wearable and mobile technologies may improve medication adherence and symptom control in patients with allergic conditions, supporting their integration into routine care.23 In the context of fixed-dose intranasal therapies, such as the MF/OLO combination, these technologies may support more consistent use of single-device regimens and facilitate personalized treatment adjustments in routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

AR continues to be a highly prevalent and burdensome condition, profoundly affecting daily functioning, sleep and quality of life. Its persistence is increasingly influenced by environmental changes and the high prevalence of comorbidities.2 Although several therapies are available, treatment gaps remain due to delayed diagnosis, insufficient symptom control and widespread reliance on self-diagnosis, which often leads patients to use oral antihistamines or other OTC products with limited effectiveness.5,16

The fixed-dose MF/OLO combination demonstrates enhanced efficacy and effectiveness in managing AR. Clinical evidence shows that this combination provides superior symptom relief —both nasal and ocular — compared with monotherapies, with a rapid onset of action and sustained anti-inflammatory control.11,12,14 The single-device formulation further improves treatment adherence and reduces inappropriate polypharmacy often seen in self-managed cases.

Addressing issues of self-diagnosis and suboptimal self-treatment is critical to maximizing the benefits of MF/OLO. By promoting patient education, increasing pharmacist and primary care engagement, and encouraging guideline-based therapy, healthcare providers can ensure the combination therapy is used appropriately. These strategies can lead to more consistent disease control and a substantial reduction in the long-term burden of AR.
