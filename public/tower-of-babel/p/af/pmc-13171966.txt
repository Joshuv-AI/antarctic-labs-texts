Pentose phosphate pathway fuels cGAS-STING signalling to boost function of intratumoral conventional dendritic cells
Authors: Ben Liu, Zhonglei Geng, Yujie Huang, Yaqing Yuan, Tongchang Xu, Yi Zhu, Jingwen Zhang, Zhekai Luo, Sijia Zhang, Rui Ding, Ranran Tang, Yun Li, Zhilin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumors employ diverse mechanisms to impair conventional dendritic cell (cDC) function within the tumor microenvironment, yet the underlying processes remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) reduction in late-stage intratumoral cDCs compromises their function. Both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of the PPP attenuate cDC-mediated antitumor responses. Conversely, PPP augmentation restores late-stage intratumoral cDC antitumor capacity. PPP deficiency in cDCs enhances the immune checkpoint PD-L1 expression level, and combining cDC-specific PPP potentiation with immune checkpoint blockade synergistically enhances immunotherapy efficacy. Mechanistically, PPP activation fuels purine metabolism, thereby increasing ATP and GTP levels required for 2’3’-cGAMP synthesis, ultimately promoting cGAS-STING signaling and STING-dependent cDC antitumor responses. The PPP is associated with STING-dependent cDC activities in tumor tissues from female patients with breast cancer. Collectively, our findings establish PPP as an intrinsic metabolic checkpoint in STING-mediated cDC antitumor immunity, and suggest targeting PPP in cDCs as a promising cancer immunotherapy strategy.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has revolutionized clinical cancer therapy1,2. However, only a subset of patients responds well to ICB3. Emerging evidence suggests that dysfunctional dendritic cells (DCs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME) limit ICB efficacy4,5. DCs, particularly conventional DCs (cDCs), play a critical role in antitumor immunity by presenting tumor antigens to prime and activate T cells4,6,7. Nevertheless, tumor immunoediting has evolved multiple strategies to suppress DC function within the TME7–9. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying DC functional modulation in the TME and developing strategies to enhance DC activity are essential for improving cancer immunotherapy.

Tumor immunoediting is a dynamic process that shapes immune cell function to dictate tumor evolution10–14. Naive and activated CD8+ T cells present in early tumors are replaced by exhausted and precursor-exhausted populations in late tumors. Late-stage tumors exhibit suppression of antitumor immunity genes, resulting in diminished immune cell infiltration and impaired tumor-immune communication. Notably, innate immune genes, particularly interferon-stimulated genes are downregulated in late tumors13. Latest finding has revealed that decreased cGMP levels in intratumoral DCs during the late stage compromise their interstitial motility and antitumor activity14. However, the underlying mechanism of tumor immunoediting mediating the DC phenotype remains elusive.

Metabolic reprogramming plays a central role in the development and maturation of DCs, providing essential energy for their functions9,15–18. Synergizing metabolic interventions with immunotherapy significantly enhances antigen-presentation capabilities and T cell activation by DCs, thereby improving the efficacy of current antitumor therapies7,19,20. Despite these advancements, the intrinsic metabolic disruptions within tumor-infiltrating DCs remain poorly understood, presenting a barrier to the development of innovative immunotherapy strategies. The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) stands as a critical branch of glucose metabolism essential for producing ribose 5-phosphate (R5P) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). R5P acts as a foundational component for nucleotide synthesis, and NADPH plays a pivotal role in maintaining redox balance and supporting various biosynthetic processes21,22. Recent studies emphasize the PPP’s impact on regulating T cell function, underscoring its pivotal role in boosting CD8+ effector T cell activity23. Furthermore, the PPP enhances antioxidant capacity and mitigates exhaustion in CAR T cells24. Notably, Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2) deficiency results in increased PPP flux, fostering the generation of TCF1+ progenitor CD8+ T cells25. Despite the growing evidence showcasing the PPP as an immune effector, how and whether PPP regulates DC function, especially in the TME is largely unknown.

In this work, we perform single-cell analyses on early- and late-stage tumor-infiltrating cDCs isolated from MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Our results reveal that late-stage intratumoral cDCs exhibited significant dysfunction and markedly impaired PPP activity. Notably, enhancing PPP activity in late-stage intratumoral cDCs restores their functionality and augmentes ICB efficacy. Mechanistically, the PPP fuels cGAS-STING signaling and sustains STING-dependent cDC-mediated antitumor immunity. Importantly, the PPP supports STING-dependent cDC activities in tumor tissue samples from patients with breast cancers. Our findings elucidate how tumor immunoediting compromises cDC function in the TME and highlight the PPP as a critical metabolic regulator of STING-driven cDC antitumor responses. Our study suggests that targeting the PPP in cDCs could be a promising strategy for improving cancer immunotherapy.

Results

Tumor-infiltrating cDCs from late tumors show signs of dysfunction

The TME modulates DC function and influences ICB efficacy. To investigate tumor-infiltrating DC dynamics during tumor progression, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on DCs (live CD45+CD24−F4/80−Ly6C−CD11c+MHC-II+) isolated from MC38 tumor-bearing mice at early (8-10 days) and late (20-30 days) stages (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 1a)13. For the identification of DC populations in the single-cell dataset, we referred to established DC-specific signatures and markers from published studies26–28. First, cell clusters expressing high levels of lineage markers (Cd3d, Cd19, Ncr1, Adgre1) were excluded. Tumor-infiltrating DCs were divided into four major clusters, including monocyte-derived DCs (mo-DCs), plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s) and conventional type 2 DCs (cDC2s) (Fig. 1b). Dirichlet multinomial regression analysis revealed temporal changes in DC composition, with pDCs and cDCs decreasing while mo-DCs became predominant in late-stage tumors (Fig. 1c). Flow cytometry analysis revealed lower levels of typical DC maturation markers, including major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), CD80 and CD86 in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 1d, e). qPCR analysis confirmed significant downregulation of genes associated with DC maturation, including co-stimulatory factors, antigen processing and presentation molecules and adhesion molecules in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Supplementary Fig. 2a-c). Furthermore, key DC functional genes were suppressed in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 1f, and Supplementary Fig. 2d). CCR7 is necessary for the migration of tumor-infiltrating DCs into tumor-draining lymph nodes29,30, and CXC-chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) and CXCL10, predominantly secreted by tumor-infiltrating DCs, promote the recruitment of CD8+ T cells into the TME6. Our data further confirmed that Ccr7, Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 were all downregulated in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 1g). IRF4 and IRF8 are both necessary for the development of cDCs31,32. Late-stage intratumoral cDCs displayed lower expression levels of both Irf4 and Irf8 (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Given the pivotal role of cDCs in T cell priming and antitumor immunity, we further distinguished cDC subpopulations from total DCs in our scRNA-seq data, which were defined based on canonical markers: Itgax, Flt3, and Zbtb46. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed impaired antigen processing and presentation capacity in late-stage intratumoral cDCs, characterized by negative regulation of both adaptive immune responses and T cell-mediated immunity (Supplementary Fig. 2f and Fig. 1h). To validate these findings in vitro, we isolated early- and late-stage intratumoral cDCs and co-cultured them with OT-I T cells in the presence of SIINFEKL peptides. Late-stage intratumoral cDCs demonstrated significantly reduced capacity to stimulate OT-I T cell proliferation (Fig. 1i, j) and effector cytokine production (Fig. 1k) compared to their early-stage counterparts. These results collectively indicate that late-stage tumor-infiltrating cDCs exhibit markedly impaired T cell priming functionality.

As supported by the latest studies, cDCs in the TME can be classified into three main subsets: cDC1, cDC2, and mature DCs (also referred to as mregDCs)33–36. Among these, mature DCs represent the dominant subset responsible for mediating antitumor immunity and are thought to originate from cDC1 and cDC2 following tumor antigen uptake33,34,36. Further analysis of our scRNA-seq data revealed a notable subset of mature DCs (defined by expression of Ccl22, Ccl17, and Ccr7) in late-stage tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). Flow cytometry analysis revealed reduced proportions of mature DCs and cDC1 in late-stage tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2i). Furthermore, the absolute numbers of infiltrating mature DCs, cDC1, and cDC2 per gram of tumor tissue were significantly lower in late-stage compared to early-stage tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2j). GSEA analysis indicated that mature DCs from late-stage tumors displayed impaired antigen-presentation capacity (Supplementary Fig. 2k). Next, we isolated mature DCs from early- and late-stage tumors and observed decreased expression of maturation markers and functional cytokines in the late-stage mature DCs (Supplementary Fig. 2l, m). To further assess the functional capacity of cDC subsets, equal numbers of early- and late-stage tumor-infiltrating mature DCs, cDC1, and cDC2 were isolated. Mature DCs and cDC1 were co-cultured with OT-I T cells, while cDC2 were co-cultured with OT-II T cells, all in the presence of SIINFEKL peptides. Compared to early-stage cells, late-stage mature DCs showed significantly impaired ability to stimulate OT-I T cell proliferation (Fig. 1l, m) and effector cytokine production (Fig. 1n). Similarly, late-stage cDC1 and cDC2 also exhibited reduced T cell priming capacity (Fig. 1o–t). Together, these results demonstrate that all major cDC subsets in late-stage tumors are functionally compromised relative to their early-stage counterparts.

Late-stage tumor-infiltrating cDCs exhibit the PPP activity impairment

To explore what’s the dominant different events between late- and early-stage intratumoral cDCs, we next performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis using scRNA-seq data. The results displayed significant alterations in key biological processes within intratumoral cDCs during tumor progression. These changes affected pathways involved in antigen processing and presentation, T cell receptor signaling and critical inflammatory cascades, as well as the TNF, JAK-STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways, suggesting the notable differences between late- and early-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 2a). Given the pivotal role of metabolic rewiring in modulating intratumoral DC function and antitumor immunity, we focused our investigation on central carbon metabolism (Fig. 2a). Central carbon metabolism-including glycolysis, the PPP and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle-contributes a fundamental metabolic framework essential for sustaining cellular function37. To validate these observations, we performed central carbon metabolomics on early- and late-stage intratumoral cDCs. Our analysis revealed obviously reduced levels of PPP-associated metabolites in late-stage intratumoral cDCs, whereas glycolytic metabolites remained no unified changed (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The PPP, a critical branch of glucose metabolism, generates nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and ribose 5-phosphate (R5P) to support cellular functions22,38. Consistent with the metabolic profiling, late-stage intratumoral cDCs exhibited markedly decreased NADPH and R5P production (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Given the established role of R5P in nucleotide biosynthesis21,25, we further observed significantly reduced nucleotide abundance in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 2c). The initial step of the PPP is catalyzed by glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), which converts glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to 6-phosphogluconolactone (6PGL). We found that 6PGL level was reduced in late-stage intratumoral cDCs, whereas glucose and G6P remained comparable between early- and late-stage cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Moreover, protein expression of G6PDX (the murine ortholog of human G6PD) and mRNA levels of key PPP enzymes were significantly lower in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). Together, these findings demonstrate that late-stage intratumoral cDCs exhibit impaired PPP activity. To further validate our observation, we performed KEGG pathway analysis on mature DCs using scRNA-seq data. The analysis identified the PPP as one of the most significantly altered pathways in late-stage compared to early-stage intratumoral mature DCs (Supplementary Fig. 3d). We then isolated early- and late-stage intratumoral mature DCs and observed reduced levels of the key PPP metabolite NADPH in late-stage mature DCs (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Additionally, qPCR analysis showed decreased mRNA expression of major PPP enzymes in late-stage mature DCs (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Moreover, early-stage intratumoral mature DCs were treated in vitro with the PPP inhibitor G6PDi-1. Flow cytometry showed that PPP inhibition reduced the frequency of mature DCs (Supplementary Fig. 3g) and downregulated expression of key maturation markers, including MHC-I, CD80 and CD86 within this subset (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Conversely, treatment of late-stage intratumoral mature DCs with the PPP agonist AG139 significantly increased both the proportion of mature DCs and the expression of maturation markers (Supplementary Fig. 3i, j). To validate our results in vivo, we treated early-stage intratumoral cDCs with PPP inhibitors G6PDi-1 and 6-Aminonicotinamide (6-AN). PPP inhibition markedly downregulated DC maturation markers, including MHC-I, CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 2d, e), as well as DC functional genes, including Tnfa, Il12 and Ccr7 (Fig. 2f) without affecting their viability (Supplementary Fig. 3k). We then adoptively transferred PPP inhibitor-treated early-stage intratumoral cDCs into tumor-bearing mice. Compared to non-treated DCs, G6PDi-1- or 6-AN-treated DCs displayed reduced efficacy in suppressing tumor growth (Fig. 2g). Additionally, G6PDi-1- or 6-AN-treated DCs exhibited decreased proportions of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the TME (Fig. 2h, i). Most significantly, when we augmented the PPP activity using AG1 in late-stage intratumoral cDCs, we observed upregulated expression of both maturation markers and functional genes in vitro (Fig. 2j–m) without affecting their viability (Supplementary Fig. 3l), along with restored antitumor function in vivo (Fig. 2n–p). In summary, our results demonstrate that the PPP activity impairment contributes to late-stage intratumoral cDC dysfunction, and modulating PPP activity in cDCs effectively regulates their antitumor responses.

PPP deficiency in cDCs restricts antitumor function

The first step of PPP branch is G6PD catalyzing the conversion of G6P to 6PGL (Fig. 3a). Additionally, G6PD is the sole rate-limiting enzyme in the PPP branch22. Therefore, we crossed G6pdx-floxed mice with Zbtb46-Cre mice to obtain G6pdxfl/flZbtb46-Cre (KO) mice (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), representing PPP deficiency in cDCs. First, we observed that PPP deficiency did not affect DC viability (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in the populations of DCs in the spleens (Supplementary Fig. 4d–f), as well as the frequencies of T cells in the thymus (Supplementary Fig. 4g), lymph nodes and spleens (Supplementary Fig. 4h) between 6 and 12-week-old gender-matched G6pdx+/+Zbtb46-Cre (WT) and KO mice, indicating PPP abolition was dispensable for DC and T cell development. To delve deeper into the impact of the PPP on DC function, we primed OT-I T cells using WT and KO bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) (Supplementary Fig. 5a) loaded with the SIINFEKL peptides. We observed that PPP-depleted DCs induced decreased proliferation (Fig. 3b, c) and effector cytokine expression (Fig. 3d) of OT-I T cells compared with WT DCs. To explore the effect of PPP on DC antitumor immunity in vivo, WT and KO mice were inoculated with MC38 tumor cells. Notably, our findings revealed that PPP deficiency within DCs expedited tumor growth (Fig. 3e) and led to premature lethality (Fig. 3f). Moreover, the frequencies of mature DCs and cDC1 were reduced in KO mice, whereas cDC2 proportions remained comparable between WT and KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5b). The absolute numbers of infiltrating mature DCs, cDC1, and cDC2 per gram of tumor tissue were significantly lower in KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5c). The absence of PPP activity led to decreased expression of key functional genes such as Tnfa, Il12 and Ccr7 in tumor-infiltrating cDCs (Fig. 3g). Notably, PPP deletion correlated with diminished proportions of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the TME (Fig. 3h, i). We extended our studies in mice challenged with B16-F10 and LLC tumor cells. To explore the PPP effect on DC-mediated tumor immunotherapy, we employed the BMDC-based therapeutic tumor model8,17,18,40,41. We injected WT and KO BMDCs into the wild-type MC38-OVA tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 5i). Compared to WT DCs, KO DCs promoted tumor growth (Supplementary Fig. 5j) and decreased T cell effector cytokine expression in the TME (Supplementary Fig. 5k, l). PPP ablation attenuates STING signaling in cDCs

To delve into the foundational mechanism by which the PPP supports cDC function, we isolated intratumoral cDCs from MC38 tumor-bearing WT and KO mice for smart RNA sequencing. Notably, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) unveiled distinct transcriptomic differences between WT and KO intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 4a). Intriguingly, our KEGG analysis highlighted an enrichment in the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway and PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 4b). Previous research had underscored the pivotal role of the cGAS-STING signalling pathway, an essential player in cytosolic DNA sensing, in orchestrating crucial DC-mediated antitumor functions within the TME42–44. And STING signaling activation stimulates the downstream activation of the JAK-STAT pathway45. Our prior investigation also proved glycolysis improved STING-dependent DC antitumor responses18. Yet, the precise metabolic regulations governing the STING signature within intratumoral DCs remain largely enigmatic. To investigate these observations, we isolated early- and late-stage intratumoral cDCs from MC38 tumor-bearing wild-type mice. Western blot analysis revealed significantly reduced phosphorylation of STING and STAT1 in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 4c), indicating impaired activation of both the cGAS-STING and JAK-STAT signaling pathways. In contrast, PI3K-AKT signaling remained unchanged, as evidenced by comparable AKT phosphorylation levels between early- and late-stage cDCs (Fig. 4c). To further validate these findings, we isolated intratumoral cDCs from both WT and KO MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Our results confirmed that G6PDX deficiency obviously impaired STING and STAT1 phosphorylation, but had no effect on AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 4d). Moreover, the expression level of type I interferons was notably decreased in KO DCs (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, we cultured WT and KO BMDCs in vitro and stimulated them with the STING signaling agonist Interferon Stimulatory DNA (ISD, a synthetic double-stranded DNA that is sensed by cGAS to activate the cGAS-STING pathway). To confirm the intricate crosstalk between the PPP and STING signaling, we exposed BMDCs to the PPP inhibitors G6PDi-1 and 6-AN following stimulation of the STING signaling. Notably, our findings revealed a clear impairment in STING phosphorylation, as well as the expression of type I interferons upon the PPP inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 6c–h). Tumor-infiltrating DCs recognize tumor-derived DNA to engage STING signaling, thereby amplifying DC antitumor responses46. Consequently, we freshly isolated intratumoral cDCs from MC38 tumor-bearing mice and subjected them to treatment with the PPP inhibitors. Encouragingly, the results validated that PPP deficiency hindered the STING signaling activation (Supplementary Fig. 6i–l). Expanding on our investigations, ectopic expression of G6PDX in murine DC cell lines (DC2.4) to heighten the PPP flux resulted in elevated activation of the STING signaling (Supplementary Fig. 6m, n). Likewise, treatment with the PPP agonist AG1 facilitated STING phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 6o) and type I interferons expression (Supplementary Fig. 6p). In summary, our data indicate that the PPP plays a supportive role in STING signaling within cDCs. To further explore the interplay between the PPP and STING signaling, we collected STING signaling stimulated and non-stimulated cDCs for metabolomics. Interestingly, STING stimulation elicited an upregulation of metabolites associated with the PPP (Supplementary Fig. 6q), suggesting a positive correlation between the PPP flux and STING signaling. Collectively, our results demonstrate that PPP deficiency in cDCs impairs STING signaling activation.

PPP supports STING-dependent cDC antitumor immunity

Given the supportive role of the PPP in STING signaling within cDCs, we proceeded to employ the BMDC-based therapeutic tumor model. Relative to WT DCs, STING deficient (Tmem173−/−) DCs resulted in accelerated tumor growth (Fig. 4f), decreased frequencies of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the TME (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 6r). In contrast, when comparing STING deficient DCs to double- knockout (DKO) DCs lacking both G6PDX and STING, no further alterations were discerned in terms of tumor growth or the proportions of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4f, g, and Supplementary Fig. 6r). To confirm the phenotype, we treated WT and STING-deficient DCs with AG1. In WT DCs, AG1 stimulation obviously decelerated tumor growth (Fig. 4h). Conversely, in STING-deficient DCs, AG1 stimulation had no effect on tumor progression and antitumor immunity (Fig. 4h, I, and Supplementary Fig. 6s). In conclusion, our results indicate that the PPP supports STING-dependent DC antitumor immunity.

To further test whether STING agonists retain their ability to activate DCs in PPP-deficient conditions, we treated BMDCs with the PPP inhibitor 6-AN, either alone or in combination with the STING agonist DMXAA (a direct small-molecule binder of murine STING). We found that 6-AN treatment suppressed STING signaling activation and downstream type I interferons expression in BMDCs, whereas co-treatment with 6-AN and DMXAA effectively restored STING signaling and downstream type I interferons expression (Supplementary Fig. 6t, u). In the BMDC-based tumor therapy model, DCs stimulated with 6-AN alone accelerated tumor growth (Fig. 4j) and reduced the proportions of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4k and Supplementary Fig. 6v) in the TME relative to untreated DCs. In contrast, DCs treated with both 6-AN and DMXAA exhibited delayed tumor growth and restored T cell cytokine production, achieving outcomes comparable to those of untreated DCs (Fig. 4j, k, and Supplementary Fig. 6v). Furthermore, we performed similar BMDC-based tumor therapy experiments using WT and KO BMDCs. While PPP deficiency impaired STING signaling and downstream type I interferons expression, DMXAA stimulation in KO DCs successfully restored STING pathway activation and downstream type I interferons expression in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 6w, x), as well as DC-mediated antitumor function in vivo (Fig. 4l, m, and Supplementary Fig. 6y). These results collectively demonstrate that PPP deficiency in cDCs compromises STING-dependent antitumor function, and that restoration of STING activity in PPP-deficient cDCs rescues their antitumor response.

Next, we isolated early- and late-stage tumor-infiltrating cDCs from MC38 tumor-bearing mice and treated late-stage cDCs with or without the DMXAA in vitro. These cells were then subcutaneously injected adjacent to the tumors to wild-type MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Compared with untreated late-stage intratumoral cDCs, those receiving DMXAA treatment exhibited delayed tumor growth and enhanced T cell cytokine production, achieving functional levels comparable to early-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 4n, o, and Supplementary Fig. 6z). These results demonstrate that restoring STING signaling in late-stage intratumoral cDCs rescues their antitumor immunity. In summary, the findings confirm that impaired STING activity contributes to the dysfunctional state of late-stage intratumoral cDCs.

PPP-driving purine metabolism boosts STING signaling

Subsequently, we delved deeper into the intricacies of how the PPP governed the regulation of STING signaling. PPP supplies NADPH and R5P (Supplementary Fig. 7a). NADPH actively contributes to maintaining redox homeostasis. Measurement of both cellular and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) revealed significantly higher levels in late-stage intratumoral cDCs compared to early-stage counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Therefore, we treated WT and KO BMDCs with a ROS scavenger, MITO-TEMPO. Remarkably, PPP deficiency in cDCs led to heightened ROS accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Subsequent combined treatment with MITO-TEMPO successfully mitigated ROS levels in both WT and KO DCs (Supplementary Fig. 7d). However, despite the reduction in ROS levels upon MITO-TEMPO administration, the phosphorylation of STING (Supplementary Fig. 7e) and the expression of type I interferons (Supplementary Fig. 7f) were not restored, even decreased in KO DCs. This suggested that the compromised STING signature resulting from the PPP deficiency was not attributable to ROS accumulation. R5P plays a pivotal role in nucleotide synthesis, encompassing purines and pyrimidines21,25. Next, we treated WT and KO BMDCs with R5P. We observed that PPP deficiency markedly suppressed STING signaling. Supplementation with R5P restored both STING activation and downstream type I interferons production (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h). These results confirm that impaired PPP activity curtails R5P production, thereby inhibiting STING signaling in DCs. Furthermore, we treated DCs with the purine metabolism inhibitor 6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP). Remarkably, stimulation with 6-MP significantly hampered STING phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 7i) and the expression of type I interferons (Supplementary Fig. 7j). Conversely, inhibition of pyrimidine metabolism showed no discernible effect on STING signaling (Supplementary Fig. 7k, l). Building upon these findings, we then isolated intratumoral cDCs from MC38 tumor-bearing mice and subjected them to treatment with 6-MP. Encouragingly, the results validated that perturbation of purine synthesis impaired STING signaling (Supplementary Fig. 7m, n). In conclusion, our data demonstrate the pivotal role of PPP-derived purine metabolism in augmenting STING signaling. Since PPP deficiency reduces purine synthesis, we asked whether it induces DNA damage. Using etoposide as a positive control, we observed no significant difference in the DNA damage marker γH2AX between WT and KO DCs (Supplementary Fig. 7o). To further validate this finding, we compared early- and late-stage intratumoral cDCs and similarly detected no increase in DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 7p). In summary, our data indicate that PPP deficiency and the consequent purine depletion in cDCs do not lead to elevated DNA damage.

PPP deficiency impairs 2’ 3’-cGAMP production to restrict STING signaling

PPP deficiency undermines STING signaling. We delved deeper into elucidating the specific stage at which the PPP regulated STING signaling. By activating STING signaling using the STING direct agonists, cGAMP (the natural enzymatic product of cGAS that directly binds and activates STING) and DMXAA, we observed that PPP deficiency did not impact STING signaling upon stimulation with these agonists (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d). This suggests that the impairment of STING signaling mediated by the PPP deficiency occurs upstream of the STING molecule.

Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) senses double-stranded DNA, catalyzing ATP and GTP to generate 2’3’-cGAMP as a second messenger, which serves as the initiator for activating STING signaling47,48. Initially, we evaluated the production of 2’3’-cGAMP in both intratumoral cDCs and BMDCs. Interestingly, late-stage intratumoral cDCs exhibited significantly reduced levels of 2’3’-cGAMP compared to early-stage populations (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, we observed that PPP deficiency markedly hampered 2’3’-cGAMP production in cDCs (Fig. 5b, d). Drawing from our aforementioned findings revealing PPP-driven purine metabolism supports STING signaling, we were spurred by existing studies demonstrating that deficiencies in purine synthesis diminished the nucleotide pool, including ATP and GTP49,50. This led us to postulate that PPP deficiency might restrict ATP and GTP production, thereby hindering 2’3’-cGAMP generation and impeding STING signaling. To validate this hypothesis, we examined the levels of ATP and GTP in both intratumoral cDCs and BMDCs. Strikingly, our data unveiled a notable reduction in ATP and GTP levels due to PPP ablation in cDCs (Fig. 5c, e). Our metabolomic analysis also confirmed significantly reduced ATP and GTP levels in late-stage intratumoral cDCs (Fig. 2c). To determine whether the decline in ATP and GTP levels caused by PPP deficiency impairs DC function, we treated WT and KO BMDCs in vitro with varying concentrations of ATP and GTP. Supplementation with 100 µM ATP and GTP restored the expression of maturation markers in KO DCs to levels comparable to WT DCs, without affecting WT DCs (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). Higher concentrations (300 and 500 µM) enhanced maturation in both genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). Based on these results, 100 µM was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments. Correspondingly, qPCR analysis confirmed that 100 µM ATP and GTP supplementation also rescued the expression of functional cytokines in KO DCs, while leaving WT DCs unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 8g). Furthermore, 100 µM ATP and GTP supplementation restored intracellular ATP and GTP levels in KO DCs to WT levels (Fig. 5e) and reversed the decrease in 2’3’-cGAMP observed in KO DCs (Fig. 5f). To further confirm this phenotype, we isolated late-stage tumor-infiltrating cDCs and treated them with ATP and GTP. ATP and GTP supplementation restored the expression of maturation markers in late-stage cDCs to levels seen in early-stage cDCs (Supplementary Fig. 8h) and also rescued the expression of functional cytokines in late-stage cDCs (Supplementary Fig. 8i). Notably, the restoration of ATP and GTP levels facilitated the recovery of STING phosphorylation (Fig. 5g) and the expression of type I interferons (Fig. 5h) in KO DCs. Interestingly, individual supplementation of ATP or GTP did not suffice in rescuing STING signaling in the KO DCs (Supplementary Fig. 8j, k). Subsequent validation through 6-MP treatment confirmed our observations, showcasing that inhibition of purine metabolism impaired 2’3’-cGAMP production following STING activation, unlike pyrimidine metabolism inhibition (Fig. 5i). Crucially, the impairment in purine metabolism also led to restricted ATP and GTP levels within the DCs (Fig. 5j). Intriguingly, the supplementation of ATP and GTP successfully restored 2’3’-cGAMP production and STING signaling upon 6-MP administration (Fig. 5j–n). Also, individual administration of either ATP or GTP failed to resuscitate STING signaling (Supplementary Fig. 8l, m). Recognizing that compromised 2’3’-cGAMP production serves as a linchpin in the inhibition of STING signaling by PPP deficiency, we directly provided KO DCs with 2’3’-cGAMP to normalize its levels to those of WT DCs (Fig. 5o). Notably, the reinstatement of 2’3’-cGAMP showcased a recovery of STING signaling (Fig. 5p, q). PPP improves the efficacy of DC-mediated tumor immunotherapy

Given the vital role of the PPP in sustaining STING signaling, which is crucial for DC-mediated antitumor function. We delved into exploring whether PPP could bolster DC-based immunotherapy. Employing the BMDC-based therapeutic tumor model, we administered ATP and GTP to KO DCs and noted a significant reduction in tumor size compared to untreated KO DCs (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, this treatment led to the restoration of the number of tumor-infiltrating CD11c+ lymphocytes, CD4+ T, CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6b), as well as enhanced frequencies of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6c, d). Moreover, the activation of PPP by the agonist AG1 augmented the effectiveness of DC-mediated tumor therapy (Fig. 6e). Notably, in KO DCs, treatment with AG1 did not result in any significant alterations in tumor size (Fig. 6e), the populations of tumor-infiltrating CD11c+ lymphocytes, CD4+ T, CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6f), or the frequencies of IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6g, h). In KO tumor-bearing mice, we observed elevated PD-L1 expression in intratumoral DCs compared to WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c). To validate our discoveries, we freshly isolated DCs from breast cancer (BC) tissues. In line with our observations in mice, inhibiting the PPP through G6PDi-1 and 6-AN treatment impaired STING phosphorylation (Fig. 7b) and type I interferons expression (Fig. 7c). Furthermore, we treated human BC DCs separately with purine and pyrimidine metabolism inhibitors. Consistent with our murine findings, inhibition of purine but not pyrimidine metabolism suppressed STING signaling in human BC DCs (Fig. 7d–f, and Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). We noted that inhibition of PPP and purine metabolism both led to reduced ATP and GTP levels (Fig. 7g, l), along with impaired synthesis of 2’3’-cGAMP (Fig. 7h, m), ultimately resulting in diminished STING phosphorylation (Fig. 7i, n) and type I interferons expression (Fig. 7j, k, o). Notably, we found that supplementing ATP and GTP restored depleted pools in DCs exposed to G6PDi-1, 6-AN and 6-MP treatments, consequently reviving STING signaling (Fig. 7i, j, k, n, o). Moreover, direct supplementation of 2’3’-cGAMP was able to rescue STING signaling in the absence of PPP and purine metabolism (Fig. 7p–s). Collectively, our results demonstrate that the PPP maintains STING signaling in DCs from human BC patient samples.

Discussion

Tumor immunoediting shapes DC function in the TME, but the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrated that impaired PPP activity was a metabolic hallmark of late-stage intratumoral cDCs. Restoration of PPP activity rescued late-stage intratumoral cDC antitumor function. Furthermore, potentiating PPP in cDCs enhanced anti-PD-L1 efficacy. Both pharmacological and genetic PPP inhibition attenuated cDC-mediated antitumor responses. Mechanistically, PPP-driven purine metabolism sustained ATP and GTP levels to support 2’3’-cGAMP production, thereby activating cGAS-STING signaling and STING-dependent cDC antitumor immunity. Our findings establish PPP-maintained purine metabolism as a critical regulator of STING activation and cDC functionality in the TME.

Tumor immunoediting represents a dynamic process through which the immune system exerts dual effects on tumor development-both suppressing malignant growth and facilitating tumor progression. During this process, tumors undergo immunogenic modulation while simultaneously developing heterogeneous immunosuppressive mechanisms that foster disease advancement10,12. DCs play a critical role in antitumor immunity. Nevertheless, tumor immunoediting has evolved multiple strategies to suppress DC function within the TME7–9. Here, we discovered that late-stage intratumoral cDCs showed signs of dysfunction, characterized by significantly reduced expression of maturation markers and functional genes. Integrated RNA-sequencing and metabolomics analyses further showed that late-stage intratumoral cDCs displayed downregulated PPP activity. Notably, restoration of PPP flux in late-stage intratumoral cDCs rescued their antitumor capacity. Additionally, both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of the PPP compromised cDC-mediated antitumor responses. Our study additionally revealed that tumor immunoediting suppresses TCA cycle activity in late-stage intratumoral cDCs. These findings suggest a potential role for TCA cycle metabolism in regulating cDC function during tumor evolution within the TME, a hypothesis we plan to investigate in our future studies. While the mechanisms underlying PPP activity differences between early- and late-stage intratumoral cDCs remain to be fully elucidated, our findings establish the PPP as a critical intracellular metabolic checkpoint governing tumor-infiltrating cDC function.

PPP is a crucial branch of glucose metabolism that operates alongside the upper glycolysis pathway to produce R5P and NADPH. R5P serves as a fundamental component for nucleotide synthesis, encompassing purine and pyrimidine metabolism. NADPH plays pivotal roles in maintaining redox balance and facilitating various biosynthetic processes. Our findings indicated that purine metabolism, not pyrimidine metabolism impairment destroyed STING signaling. Mechanistically, deficiency in PPP within cDCs constrained purine metabolism, leading to diminished production of ATP and GTP. This deficiency cascaded into restricted synthesis of the second messenger 2’3’-cGAMP, ultimately resulting in impaired STING signaling. Our previous research showcased that mild ROS accumulation in DCs bolstered STING signaling17. In contrast, another study validated that deficiency in glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) triggered cellular lipid peroxidation, thereby restricting STING signaling in macrophages45. These findings indicated that ROS exhibited dual roles in modulating STING signaling, primarily contingent upon their levels within the cellular environment. NADPH plays a pivotal role in maintaining redox balance. Notably, deficiency in the PPP within cDCs indeed led to heightened accumulation of ROS. Treatment with the ROS scavenger, MITO-TEMPO, reduced STING signaling in WT cDCs, aligning with our previous research. Curiously, in PPP-deficient cDCs, stimulation with MITO-TEMPO did not restore STING signaling, even diminished its activity. These results hinted that the impairment of STING signaling in PPP-deficient cDCs might be partially unrelated to ROS levels. Another crucial function of NADPH involves facilitating various biosynthetic processes, such as deoxyribonucleotides, fatty acids and cholesterol synthesis22. Recent studies have suggested that metabolites governing STING pathway represent an essential component of the cellular intrinsic metabolic network associated with STING signaling52–54. In this study, we do not explore whether NADPH-mediated biosynthetic processes regulate STING signaling, an aspect we plan to address in our future research efforts.

Enhancing the PPP in immune cells represents an emerging strategy to improve the efficacy of ICB in cancer therapy. Recent studies highlight its potential through improved T cell functionality and persistence in the TME. For instance, PPP activation has been shown to promote the generation and maintenance of TCF1⁺ progenitor-like exhausted CD8⁺ T cells, thereby enhancing tumor control when combined with PD-1 blockade. Another study demonstrates that the PPP metabolite Xu5P sustains the expansion of effector SLC35E2⁺CD8⁺ progenitor-like exhausted T cells, and Xu5P supplementation synergizes with PD-1 blockade in tumor therapy. In our study, we found that PPP activation in cDCs improved cDC-mediated antitumor immunity. Moreover, PPP activation in cDCs enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy. In conclusion, both previous reports and our current work suggest that activating the PPP in T cells or DCs constitutes a promising strategy for improving immunotherapy outcomes. Thus, selectively activating the PPP in T cells or DCs, in combination with ICB, represents a promising tumor therapy strategy worthy of investigation.

PD-L1 expression on DCs is critical for their survival, functionality, and response to ICB55–57. Our findings indicated that PPP deficiency upregulates PD-L1 expression in intratumoral cDCs. This observation appears to contrast with a recent report demonstrating that STING activation robustly increases PD-L1 expression in monocytes via the STING-IRF3-type I interferon axis58. Such a discrepancy suggests that the relationship between STING signaling and PD-L1 expression may be cell-type-specific. PPP deficiency impairs NADPH production, leading to ROS accumulation. Elevated ROS levels are known to activate signaling pathways that induce PD-L1 expression in non-small cell lung cancer59. We observed that PPP deficiency in cDCs induced ROS accumulation, which may contribute to increased PD-L1 levels. While our study establishes a link between PPP deficiency and PD-L1 upregulation in intratumoral cDCs, the precise molecular mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Taken together, our study demonstrates that PPP activity is critical for tumor-infiltrating cDC function and establishes a mechanistic link between the PPP and cGAS-STING signaling. These findings identify the PPP as a key metabolic checkpoint in tumor-infiltrating cDCs, suggesting that therapeutic targeting of this pathway may represent a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Study approval

BC tissue samples were gathered from female patients with breast cancer at the Women’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection, including consent to publish potentially identifying clinical information. The Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Women’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University granted the study protocol, ensuring adherence to ethical standards. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Medical University, which sets a maximum allowable tumor size of 225 mm². Mice that exceeded this limit were humanely euthanized and used for subsequent in vitro analyses.

Human samples

BC tissues were obtained from 30 randomly selected female patients (See Supplementary Table 1) in Women’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, with informed consent achieved from all patients. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were isolated and concentrated via density-gradient centrifugation with Lymphoprep (78071, Stem cell). DCs were enriched via the Human Pan-DC Pre-Enrichment Kit (19521, Stem cell). The enriched DCs were treated with the indicated drugs and subsequently lysed for protein and RNA extraction for downstream analyses. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital (Approval No. 2024KY-137-01). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment and did not receive financial compensation. All samples were collected and used exclusively for scientific research purposes in accordance with the approved ethical guidelines.

Mice

All mice strains were maintained with a C57BL/6 background. G6pdx-floxed mice (Cyagen) were crossed with Zbtb46-Cre mice (Shanghai Model Organisms) to generate G6pdx+/+Zbtb46-Cre mice and G6pdx fl/flZbtb46-Cre mice. Tmem173−/− mice (STING deficient) (Shanghai Model Organisms) crossed with G6pdxfl/flZbtb46-Cre mice to obtain G6pdxfl/flZbtb46-Cre Tmem173−/− mice. All mice used in our experiments were 6–12-week-old gender-matched mice. Both male and female mice were included to avoid sex bias. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Animal Core Facility of Nanjing Medical University under a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ambient temperature maintained at 22 ± 1 °C and relative humidity at 50 ± 10%.

Cell lines and cell culture

DC2.4 (murine DCs, SCC142) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. MC38 (murine colon cancer, ENH204-FP) was obtained from Kerafast. LLC (murine Lewis lung carcinoma, CRL-1642), B16-F10 (murine melanoma, CRL-6475), and HEK293T (human embryonic kidney cells, CRL-3216) were obtained from ATCC. LLC, MC38, and HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. DC2.4 and B16-F10 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

BMDC generation and culture

Bone marrow cells were extracted from tibiae and femurs of mice and subjected to treatment with red blood cell lysis buffer. The cells were then cultured for 7 days in RPMI 1640 medium containing 200 ng/mL FLT-3L (BE0098, Bio X Cell), 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin to generate BMDCs. Live CD11c+MHC-II+ cells were isolated by flow cytometry for further experiments.

Cell isolation

Single-cell suspensions of the spleen were prepared for cell isolation. Splenic DCs (Spl-DC) were isolated utilizing the Mouse CD11c Positive Selection Kit (18780, Stem Cell). For tumor samples, tissues were enzymatically digested, followed by density-gradient centrifugation with Percoll (Cytiva) to enrich TILs. Tumor-infiltrating DCs (Tu-DC) were isolated via flow cytometry by gating on CD45+CD24−F4/80−Ly6C−CD11c+MHC-II+ cells.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared for flow cytometry analysis. For surface marker analysis, cells were incubated with specific fluorochrome-labeled antibodies in MACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA) for 30 minutes following by flow cytometry analysis. For intracellular staining, cells were labeled with surface marker antibodies and then fixed with fixation/permeabilization buffer at 4 °C for 1 hour. Subsequently, the cells were labeled with the relevant fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in permeabilization buffer. To evaluate intracellular cytokine production, the cells underwent stimulation with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), monensin and ionomycin for 4 hours, then harvested and incubated with appropriate fluorochrome-labeled antibodies for flow cytometry analysis.

Co-culture assay of OT-I T cells with DCs

OT-I T cells were isolated from the spleens of OT-I mice using the Mouse CD8α Positive Selection Kit (18953, Stem Cell). OT-II T cells were isolated from the spleens of OT-II mice using the Mouse CD4 T cell Isolation Kit (480033, Biolegend). For the detection of T cell expansion, 1 × 105 OT-I or OT-II T cells were labeled with CFSE (C34570, Thermo Fisher Scientific), then co-cultured with 3 × 104 DCs and supplemented with SIINFEKL peptides (A5503, Sigma) for 72 hours. The proliferation of T cells was analyzed via CFSE dilution using flow cytometry. To assess cell proliferation and effector cytokine expression of T cells, 1 × 105 OT-I or OT-II T cells were incubated with 3 × 104 DCs in the presence of SIINFEKL peptide for 72 hours, and the proportions of T cells producing Ki-67, GZMB, IFN-γ and TNF-α were determined by flow cytometry.

Western blot

2 × 106 Cell pellets were gathered and then resuspended in the lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM EDTA, supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails) at 4°C for 20 minutes. The cell extracts were then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 minutes. After centrifugation, the supernatants resulting from the separation were collected for subsequent western blot analysis.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted utilizing TRIzol Reagent (AG21102, Accurate Biology). Subsequently, cDNA was generated utilizing the one-step RT-gDNA digestion SuperMix for qPCR (11142ES60, YEASEN). The qRT-PCR was performed with specific primers as detailed in Supplementary Table 2, coupled with qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11202ES60, YEASEN).

RNA sequencing

For smart-RNA sequencing, tumor-infiltrating cDCs were isolated and purified via flow cytometry. Total RNA was enriched utilizing TRIzol Reagent and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform to acquire 150 bp paired-end reads. Subsequent mapping to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm10) was achieved using HISAT2. GSEA software facilitated GSEA analysis, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was utilized by clusterProfiler. The RNA sequencing data have been uploaded to the Genome Sequence Archive of the National Genomics Data Center, available at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/search?searchTerm=CRA020726 (accession numbers: CRA020726).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

For scRNA-seq, tumor-infiltrating DCs were harvested from MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Transcriptomic profiling was conducted using the BD Rhapsody system. scRNA-seq libraries were constructed following the BD Rhapsody single-cell whole-transcriptome amplification workflow and sequenced utilizing DNBSEQ-T7 for 150 bp paired-end reads. Data analysis was proceeded in R (v4.4.0) via a Seurat (v5.1.0) pipeline. Cells displaying > 20% mitochondrial gene expression and > 5000 detected genes were filtered out. Clustering was done using the FindClusters() function with a resolution of 0.5. High lineage marker-expressing cell clusters (Cd3d, Cd19, Ncr1, Adgre1) were excluded. To investigate the potential function of cDCs, we further distinguished cDCs subpopulations (identified by Itgax, Flt3 and Zbtb46) from total DCs. Differential gene expression within specific clusters was explored with the FindMarkers() function, employing a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test by default. The RNA sequencing data have been uploaded to the Genome Sequence Archive of the National Genomics Data Center, available at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/search?searchTerm=CRA017022 (accession numbers: CRA017022).

Metabolite extraction and mass spectrometry

2 × 106 late- and early-stage intratumoral cDCs (live CD45+CD24−F4/80−Ly6C−CD11c+MHC-II+) were isolated by flow cytometry from MC38 inoculated wild-type mice. Cell pellet was collected by centrifuging at 100 × g and 4 °C for 5 minutes. Samples were mixed with 500 µL extraction buffer (methanol: water = 3:1, containing 0.5×internal standard). After placement on dry ice for pre-cooling, the samples underwent three freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen, followed by vortexing for 30 s to mix well. Subsequent sonication was performed in an ice-water bath for 15 minutes, and samples were then incubated at −40 °C for 1 hours. Following centrifugation at 13,800 × g and 4 °C for 15 minutes, 200 µL of supernatant was collected into a 2.0 mL tube and dried under vacuum. The dried residue was then reconstituted in 160 µL of methanol: acetonitrile: water (1:1:2, v/v/v). The solution was filtered and placed into an autosampler vial for liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-MS). For reversed-phase system, an Agilent 1290 UPLC system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 150 mm) was used. For hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) system, the same UPLC system was employed with an Atlantis Premier BEH Z-HILIC column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 150 mm). An AB Sciex Triple Quad 6500+ mass spectrometer was used for metabolite detection. Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using SCIEX Analyst Workstation Software (v1.7.3), and metabolite quantification was performed with the automated analysis software BIOTREE Bio Bud (v2.0.3).

DC stimulation and drug treatment

DC2.4, BMDCs and tumor-infiltrating DCs were quantified and then seeded into designated cell culture plates. To trigger STING signaling in DC2.4 and BMDCs, the cells were subjected to treatment with ISD, cGAMP or DMXAA for 4 hours. ISD and cGAMP were transfected utilizing PEI (40815ES03, YEASEN) transfection reagent. DCs were treated with MITO-TEMPO (20 μM), R5P (200 μM), AG1 (3 μM), G6PDi-1 (100 μM), 6-AN (50 μM), 6-MP (50 μM), 5-FU (10 μM), LEF (50 μM) or BRQ (1 μM) for 12 hours and then harvested for further experiments. The ISD used in this study was prepared with equimolar amounts of oligonucleotides: F: 5′-TACAGATCTACTAGTGATCTATGACTGATCTGTACATGATCTACATACAGATCTACTAGTGATCTATGACTGATCTGTACATGATCTACA-3′; R: 5′-TGTAGATCATGTACAGATCAGTCATAGATCACTAGTAGATCTGTATGTAGATCATGTACAGATCAGTCATAGATCACTAGTAGATCTGTA-3′. The oligonucleotides were annealed in PBS at 70°C for 30 minutes before cooling to room temperature.

Nucleotides supplement assay

BMDCs were pre-treated with ATP (100 μM) and GTP (100 μM) for 4 hours, followed by treatment with the corresponding drugs for 12 hours. Subsequently, the quantified cells were plated into specified cell culture plates and stimulated with ISD for 4 hours before being harvested for subsequent experiments. Tumor-infiltrating DCs were counted and then seeded into designated cell culture plates. After pre-treatment with ATP and GTP for 12 hours, the cells were collected for further investigations.

ATP, GTP and cGAMP measurement

To measure ATP levels, 3 × 105 cells were plated in the 12-well cell culture plate, and the intracellular ATP levels were detected by the Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (G7570, Promega) in accordance with the instructions of manufacturer. For GTP and cGAMP measurements, 1 × 106 cell pellets were lysed in Protein Extraction Reagent (78503, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, the cell lysates were utilized for assessing intracellular GTP levels and cGAMP levels using the GTP ELISA Kit (66393 A, MEIBIAO BIOLOGY) and the 2’3’-cGAMP ELISA Kit (501700, Cayman Chemical) respectively, according to the instructions of manufacturer.

Stable cell lines generation

Mouse G6pdx was cloned into the MSCV2.2 plasmids. HEK293T cells were transfected with MSCV2.2-G6PDX plasmid in conjunction with pge plasmid to generate lentivirus. After 48 hours, the supernatants containing the lentivirus were collected. DC2.4 cells were then exposed to the lentivirus-containing supernatants with polybrene (10 μg/mL) for 12 hours. Subsequently, the medium was refreshed for another 12 hours, repeating this cycle. The transduced G6PDX-overexpressing cells (GFP+) were isolated using flow cytometry for subsequent experiments.

Tumor model

Tumor size, calculated as length × width (mm2), served as an indicator of tumor growth. Tumor-induced lethality was defined as a tumor reaching 225 mm2. For the subcutaneous tumor model, MC38 (5 × 105 cells per mouse), B16-F10 (1 × 106 cells per mouse) and LLC (1 × 106 cells per mouse) were injected subcutaneously into G6pdx+/+Zbtb46-Cre and G6pdxfl/flZbtb46-Cre mice. For the BMDC-based therapeutic tumor model, wild-type mice were injected subcutaneously of MC38-OVA (5 × 105 cells per mouse) tumor cells. On day 3 and day 6 post tumor cell inoculation, the mice were subcutaneously injected adjacent to the tumors with BMDCs (3 × 106 cells per mouse) that had been pre-treated with SIINFEKL peptides (A5503, Sigma) overnight. For another DC-based therapy tumor model, MC38 (5 × 105 cells per mouse) tumor-bearing wild-type mice (on day 6 post tumor cell inoculation) were subcutaneously injected adjacent to the tumors with 3 × 106 intratumoral cDCs. Early- and late-stage intratumoral cDCs were isolated from wild-type MC38 tumor-bearing mice (5 × 105 cells per mouse) at day 10 and day 20 post-inoculation, respectively. Intratumoral cDCs (live CD45+CD24−F4/80−Ly6C−CD11c+MHC-II+) cells were isolated by flow cytometry. For in vitro treatment, early-stage intratumoral cDCs were treated with PPP inhibitors (G6PDi-1 or 6-AN) or untreated (Ctrl) for 12 hours, and late-stage intratumoral cDCs were treated with the PPP agonist AG1 or untreated (Ctrl) for 12 hours prior to subcutaneous injection. For the combined anti-PD-L1 therapeutic tumor model, wild-type mice received an injection of MC38-OVA (5 × 105 cells per mouse) tumor cells and treated with anti-PD-L1 antibodies or IgG2b isotype control (100 µg per mouse) every 3 days for a total of three doses. Additionally, AG1-treated or non-treated BMDCs (3 × 106 cells per mouse), pre-incubated with SIINFEKL peptides overnight, were subcutaneously injected adjacent to the tumors on days 3 and 6 post-tumor inoculation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out utilizing GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, with results presented as mean ± SEM. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests were utilized to assess parameters between two distinct groups. One-way ANOVA was employed to analyze the comparisons involving more than two groups with a single variable. Two-way ANOVA analyses were conducted to compare multiple experimental groups or continuous outcomes. Survival curves underwent assessment via the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Gene expression levels from scRNA-seq data were examined using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test.
