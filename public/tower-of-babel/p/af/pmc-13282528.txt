Sex Differences in the Effects of High Temperature on Cardiovascular Disease Hospitalizations in Montreal
Authors: Yusheng Zhou, Sasha Ramkhelawan, Bulemba Katende, Hassan Behlouli, Louise Pilote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change has increased temperatures in Canada. Although heat-related mortality is well documented, less is known about its impact on cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity, particularly hospitalization patterns. This study examines the association between ambient temperature and CVD hospitalizations in Montreal, focusing on sex-stratified vulnerability.MethodsA time-series analysis was conducted in Montreal, Quebec, examining CVD hospitalizations during the warm season (May 1-September 30) from 2018-2022. Ambient temperature was represented by daily mean temperature. Analyses were stratified by sex and heat vulnerability index (HVI) levels. The HVI integrates multiple vulnerability dimensions (exposure index, sensitivity index, and coping capacity index); higher HVI scores indicate greater vulnerability to extreme heat impacts. Daily mean temperature (lag, 0-7 days) and CVD hospitalizations were analyzed using distributed lag nonlinear models, adjusting for possible confounders.ResultsAmong 262,740 CVD patient hospitalizations (mean age 76.4 years; 49.6% female), the relative risk (RR) for CVD hospitalization at the 99th percentile of temperature (28.1°C), as compared to the identified minimum morbidity temperature (MMT; 23°C) for the total population was 1.08 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05-1.11). Although overall heat-related risk was similar between sexes, vulnerability components revealed differences. Female participants exhibited significant cardiovascular risk in areas with poor adaptive resources based on coping capacity index (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04-1.23), whereas male patients did not (RR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.96-1.12), suggesting greater susceptibility when community-level resources are limited.ConclusionsFemale participants demonstrated greater vulnerability in areas lacking adaptive resources, indicating that sex-specific heat-protection strategies should prioritize enhancement of community-level cooling infrastructure and support systems.

Average ambient temperatures in Canada have risen by 1.7°C since 1948, with projections indicating a further increase of 1.8°C by 2050.1 This warming trend is accompanied by more frequent and extreme heat events, resulting in prolonged population exposure to extreme temperatures.2 Such heat exposure can trigger various health conditions, including stroke and cardiovascular disease (CVD).3, 4, 5

Extensive epidemiologic evidence links heat exposure to mortality,6, 7, 8, 9 yet research on heat-related morbidity, particularly cardiovascular hospitalizations, is relatively limited.10 The significance of this gap is underscored by the substantial burden of CVD in Canada, where it ranks among the top 5 causes of hospitalizations and accounts for Can$21 billion of healthcare costs, according to 2019 reports on heart, stroke, and vascular cognitive impairment.11 Understanding heat impacts on cardiovascular morbidity is particularly critical given that at least 1 in 10 Canadian adults lives with a chronic condition, with hypertension being the most prevalent of these.12 During heat exposure, physiological responses include peripheral vasodilation and increased cardiac output to facilitate heat dissipation. These changes place additional strain on the cardiovascular system, potentially triggering acute events requiring hospitalization in vulnerable individuals.

Heat vulnerability is determined by a complex interplay of socioeconomic and environmental factors.3,13 Heat vulnerability indexes have been developed to identify populations at greatest risk, combining variables such as income, education, housing quality, vegetation cover, and urban heat-island intensity.14 These indices highlight how social determinants of health and built-environment characteristics can significantly modify heat-health relationships. In addition, notable sex differences in heat-CVD association have been observed, with some studies indicating that women, particularly older women, may experience greater heat-related health impacts.5

As climate change accelerates, the burden of heat on health is likely to increase. This study aims to examine the association between daily mean temperatures and cardiovascular hospitalizations during warm periods, with particular attention to identifying vulnerable subpopulations and sex differences in the effect of heat on CVD.

Methods

Study design and population

A time-series ecological study design was implemented, employing distributed lag nonlinear modelling (DLNM) to examine temporal associations between daily meteorological exposures and cardiovascular morbidity outcomes. Analyses were conducted using data from Montreal, in the province of Quebec, Canada (Fig. 1). Montreal (45.5°N, 73.6°W) represents Canada's second-largest metropolitan area, encompassing 1.7 million residents within the urban core and 4.3 million across the greater metropolitan region, constituting approximately 10.5% of Canada's total population.15 Given its diversity in socioeconomic, urbanization, and climate conditions, Montreal is a good candidate for such a study. Hospitalization data encompassed all patients receiving care within the designated healthcare service area comprising 4 integrated administrative regions (Laval 13, Montréal 06, Laurentides 15, Lanaudière 14) that constitute the central and northern sectors of metropolitan Montreal. These regions serve integrated urban and peri-urban populations through coordinated healthcare delivery networks, facilitating comprehensive regional health outcome assessment. Patient demographic characteristics and socioeconomic indicators were determined using forward sortation area (FSA) geographic boundaries, corresponding to the first 3 digits of Canadian postal codes. Health and meteorological data

Health data were made available by the Institut National de Santé Publique du Quebec (INSPQ; Quebec National Institute of Public Health), based on the 2021 administrative boundaries. We received daily all-cause hospitalization data from 2018 to 2022. We considered the CVD-related morbidity according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) revision 10 codes (I00-I99). As the research focused on heat exposure, associations between daily CVD admissions and ambient temperature during the period from May 1 to September 30 were evaluated.

Daily maximum, minimum, and mean temperature data were extracted at the reference weather stations located within the Montreal area from Historical Climate Data of Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) (https://climate.weather.gc.ca/). A reference station is a station considered representative of a given region by Environment Canada,16 which obtains daily concentrations of air pollutants, including particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm (PM 2.5 - Fine particulate matter) and O3 from the Réseau de surveillance de la qualité de l'air (RSQA: Air Quality Monitoring Network).17 Daily humidity was computed from vapor pressure from the nearest 1 km × 1 km grid in the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Daymet database, incorporated using MetPy library (version 1.5) in Python (version 3.11, Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE). Relative humidity was included as a covariate in all main models to account for the physiological effects of moisture on heat stress.

Heat vulnerability index

To assess population vulnerability to extreme heat events, we used a heat vulnerability index (HVI) adapted from the methodology developed by the geography department at Université Laval in Quebec City.18 The indices were constructed at the dissemination-area (DA) level using 2021 Canadian census data. DAs represent smaller geographic units than FSAs, with approximately 400-700 persons per area, allowing for more granular vulnerability assessment. Given that health data were available at only the FSA level due to privacy requirements, DA-level HVI scores were aggregated to the FSA level using population-weighted averages.

The HVI comprises the following 3 constituent domains: (i) exposure index—built-environment characteristics, including urban heat-island intensity and green-space accessibility; (ii) sensitivity index—demographic and socioeconomic factors including age distribution, income levels, education, immigration status, household composition, and housing conditions; and (iii) coping capacity index—adaptive resource accessibility, including proximity to cooling centres, healthcare facilities, community centres, and recreational facilities. All indices were scored such that higher values indicate greater vulnerability. Following principal component analysis to reduce dimensionality, these components were normalized and combined to generate a composite vulnerability score, with higher scores indicating greater vulnerability to heat-related health impacts.18 HVI scores were categorized into tertiles representing low (0-33rd percentile), medium (34th-66th percentile), and high (67th-100th percentile) vulnerability levels. To further explore the association, quantile groups also were assessed to validate the non-linear vulnerability patterns observed across different levels of heat risk.

Statistical analysis

We investigated the association between cardiovascular morbidity and temperature using a DLNM approach during the warm season (May 1 to September 30) of 2018–2022, as described by Gasparrini & Leone.19 The DLNM approach relies on the definition of a “cross-basis” function, allowing for nonlinear and delayed effects of temperature.20 The distributed lag structure (lag 0-7) simultaneously estimates both immediate and delayed effects of temperature exposure. This approach inherently accounts for cumulative heat effects during periods of sustained high temperatures, as each day’s cardiovascular hospitalizations are modelled as a function of the complete temperature profile over the preceding 8 days (current day plus 7 lag days). This approach captures the compounding physiological burden from consecutive hot days or clustered heat events.21 To characterize the temperature–hospitalization relationship, we used natural cubic spline for temperature (with 3 degrees of freedom including 2 internal knots), and natural cubic for lags (degrees of freedom = 3). A quasi-Poisson distribution was used, and the models further adjusted for long-term trend (day of study), seasonality (day of year), day of week, public holidays, humidity, and air pollutants (O3 and PM 2.5). For air-pollution adjustment, 4-day average concentrations were selected by minimizing the Akaike information criterion (AIC).

The relative risk (RR) estimates were calculated comparing the 99th percentile temperature (28.1°C) to the minimum morbidity temperature (MMT). We identified the MMT empirically as the temperature associated with the lowest relative risk in the exposure-response curve generated by the DLNM model. This temperature represents the optimal thermal zone for cardiovascular function and serves as the reference point for all risk estimates. We prespecified the 99th-percentile temperature as our primary comparison, with the 95th percentile being examined in sensitivity analyses. This approach follows established epidemiologic frameworks that use empirically derived optimal temperatures as reference points for heat risk assessment. Quasi AIC and adjusted R2 were checked for model performance. The RR at the 95th percentile was also reported for reference. For air-pollution adjustment, 4-day average concentrations were selected by minimizing the AIC. To further investigate the hospitalization patterns, we conducted stratified analyses by sex (females, males) and HVI categories (low, medium, high) through separate models.

Apart from the main analysis, sensitivity analyses were included as follows: (i) changing the definition of summer season to the 3 hottest months only (ie, June to August) instead of May to September; (ii) considering the impact of longer lags: lag 0–14 days; (iii) changing the temperature exposure variable to minimum temperature, maximum temperature22; (iv) examining results with and without air-pollution adjustment to assess potential confounding; (v) alternative HVI quartile stratification to confirm nonlinear vulnerability patterns. The statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted with the statistical software R (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using the “dlnm”23 and “mgcv”24 packages.

Results

The DLNM exposure-response curve revealed a nonlinear J-shaped relationship between temperature and CVD hospitalizations, with the empirically derived MMT identified at around the 75th percentile, which refers to 23°C. This temperature represents the optimal thermal zone in which cardiovascular risk is minimized. Temperatures below, and particularly above, the MMT were associated with increased hospitalization risk, with the steepest risk increase observed at extreme heat thresholds.

The association between ambient temperature and CVD hospitalizations demonstrated a nonlinear exposure-response pattern, with statistically significant increases in hospitalization rates observed at extreme temperature thresholds (Fig. 2). At the 99th percentile temperature (28.1°C) compared to the MMT, the overall relative risk for CVD hospitalization was 1.08 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05-1.11), representing a statistically significant 7.5% increase compared to moderate-temperature conditions (Fig. 3). Female patients demonstrated a similar heat-related cardiovascular risk (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05-1.13) compared to male patients (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02-1.11). HVI stratification revealed nonlinear vulnerability patterns. High-HVI populations demonstrated the greatest heat-related risk (RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06-1.15), followed by low-HVI populations (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02-1.14). Analysis of distributed lag effects revealed progressive cardiovascular risk amplification extending beyond immediate heat-exposure periods (Supplemental Table S1). Minimal associations were observed at immediate exposure (lag day 0: RR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.03), with risk progressively intensifying through intermediate lag periods. By lag day 4, sex differences began to emerge, with female patients demonstrating RR = 1.04 (95% CI: 1.01-1.07), and male patients a RR = 1.02 (95% CI: 0.99-1.05). The temporal progression culminated at lag day 7, on which maximum risk amplification was observed: overall RR = 1.08 (95% CI: 1.05-1.11), with female patients (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05-1.13) maintaining consistently higher risk profiles than male patients (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02-1.10).

The intersection of sex and HVI tertiles revealed complex vulnerability patterns diverging from anticipated additive models. Among female patients, significant associations were observed in low-HVI (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01-1.18) and high-HVI areas (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03-1.16), whereas mid-HVI female patients showed nonsignificant associations (RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.98-1.12). Male patients exhibited different vulnerability profiles, with significant heat-related risk in only high-HVI areas (RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.04-1.17). Male patients in low-HVI (RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.97-1.14) and mid-HVI areas (RR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.95-1.08) demonstrated nonsignificant associations, suggesting sex-specific pathways in structural vulnerability indicators.

To investigate the potential pathways underlying observed sex-HVI interaction patterns, we further conducted disaggregated analyses examining temperature-cardiovascular associations across individual HVI component dimensions of exposure index, sensitivity index, and coping capacity index (Fig. 4). The exposure index revealed expected dose-response relationships, with areas of high environmental vulnerability showing significant heat-related cardiovascular risk and minimal sex differences (male patients: RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03-1.17; female patients: RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03-1.16). The sensitivity index, encompassing demographic and socioeconomic factors, demonstrated contrasting patterns across sex groups: in areas with a low socioeconomic level, female patients showed significant heat-related cardiovascular risk (RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01-1.20) whereas male patients did not (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.92-1.09). This pattern was reversed in areas with a high socioeconomic level (male patients: RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03-1.16; female patients:RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.99-1.12). The coping capacity analysis revealed sex-specific differences in vulnerability related to adaptive resources. Female patients demonstrated elevated cardiovascular risk in areas with poor adaptive capacity (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04-1.23), compared to that of male patients (RR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.96-1.12), indicating that women may be more susceptible to cardiovascular impacts when community-level adaptive resources are limited.Figure 4Relative risk (RR) for cardiovascular disease hospitalization at the 99th percentile of mean temperature over a lag of 0-7 days by different groups (age, sex, and Health Vulnerability Index [HVI] levels). Several sensitivity analyses were conducted. In analysis examining specific CVD subtypes (Supplemental Table S2), we observed significant heat-related risks for congestive heart failure (RR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.03-1.40) and hypertension (RR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03-1.12). Alternative HVI quartile stratification confirmed nonlinear vulnerability patterns, with significant associations in higher HVI score quantiles (Supplemental Table S3). Analysis at the 95th percentile temperature threshold (26.6°C) demonstrated consistent patterns with attenuated magnitudes: overall RR = 1.05 (95% CI: 1.03-1.07), with preserved sex differentials and HVI stratification patterns (Supplemental Table S3). Sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of the warm season (June-August instead of May-September) and different temperature metrics (maximum and minimum temperature) showed results consistent with the main analysis. Temperature and air pollutants (O3 and PM 2.5) showed moderate positive correlations during the warm season (Pearson r = 0.45 and 0.38, respectively), indicating potential confounding. Models adjusted for 4-day moving average pollutant concentrations yielded relative risk estimates within 5% of unadjusted models, suggesting that although air pollution co-occurs with high temperatures, the observed heat-CVD associations are not primarily driven by pollution confounding. In addition, consistent heat-CVD associations between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods were observed (RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01-1.09 for 2018-2019; RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02-1.08 for 2020-2022), indicating that COVID-19-related healthcare utilization changes did not affect our findings.

Discussion

Our analysis of 262,740 CVD hospitalizations across Montreal's warm seasons (2018-2022) demonstrates significant associations between extreme temperature exposure and cardiovascular morbidity. Using the empirically derived minimum morbidity temperature as our reference point, we observed a 5% increase in hospitalization risk at the 95th percentile temperature threshold (26.6°C), and a 7.5% increase at the 99th percentile (28.1°C). These findings establish a clear dose-response relationship between extreme heat and cardiovascular events. Sex-stratified analysis revealed that female patients demonstrate a heat-related cardiovascular risk similar to that of male patients, with disparity between the 2 being more pronounced at extreme temperature thresholds. An important finding is that examination of vulnerability components revealed that female patients exhibited significantly greater cardiovascular risk in areas with poor adaptive resources (coping capacity: RR = 1.13 vs 1.03 for male patients), indicating sex-specific susceptibility related to access to community-level cooling infrastructure, healthcare facilities, and social support networks. These findings establish evidence for sex-specific pathways in heat vulnerability, providing foundations for targeted risk stratification and differentiated public health intervention strategies in urban Canadian populations.

Our findings demonstrate consistency with recent Canadian research across multiple regions and health outcome domains. The Ontario study by Bai et al. offers the comparable Canadian evidence for cardiovascular heat-health relationships.25 Their comprehensive analysis of 1.4 million cardiovascular hospitalizations (1996-2013) documented significant associations between ambient temperatures and coronary heart disease and stroke hospitalizations, with findings of higher heat effects in female patients for certain cardiovascular conditions, thereby providing direct external validation for our sex-stratified results. The comprehensive burden analysis by Boudreault et al. across Quebec provides particularly relevant comparative context, albeit for broader health outcomes than our cardiovascular-specific investigation.26 Although our study design focused on characterizing vulnerability patterns rather than quantifying attributable burden, our observed relative risk of 1.08 at extreme heat is consistent with their effect estimates and suggests a meaningful heat-associated burden in Montreal's cardiovascular population.

Albeit important, a focus on mortality outcomes obfuscates the importance of investigation of cardiovascular morbidity patterns. Although epidemiologic evidence establishes associations between elevated ambient temperatures and cardiovascular mortality, morbidity research presents more heterogeneous findings.4,5 Critical distinctions emerge between these research domains: mortality studies typically demonstrate consistent positive associations with extreme heat, whereas morbidity investigations often reveal variable patterns, including protective effects for certain conditions during moderate heat exposure. This divergence likely reflects mechanistic differences, with mortality representing end-stage pathophysiological processes, and morbidity encompassing broader healthcare utilization patterns influenced by behavioural and healthcare access factors.25,27,28 The Swiss study by Schulte et al. provides some insights bridging mortality and morbidity research paradigms.29 Their demonstration that heat-related mortality increases for hypertension and heart failure is likely to reflect antihypertensive drug use rather than direct cardiovascular impacts. Their work provides a potential explanation for our findings, which is that heat-related hospitalizations may reflect complex interactions with multiple factors rather than solely direct thermal stress. Recent investigations examining individual cardiovascular conditions in isolation29,30 have provided valuable insights but limited understanding of differential vulnerability patterns across population groups. Our analysis across multiple sex and HVI groups addresses this knowledge gap by revealing previously uncharacterized response gradients.

The observed sex differences in heat-cardiovascular relationships extend existing literature while revealing unprecedented interaction patterns with structural vulnerability indicators. Our findings align with those of previous reports30,31 documenting elevated cardiovascular risk in women during heat exposure. Notably, acute myocardial infarction has been identified as the first clinical manifestation of ischemic heart disease in approximately 50% of patients in prior cross-sectional research assessing extreme heat-event exposure.32 Physiologically, women tend to exhibit higher core body temperature, skin temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure than men during heat exposure, potentially reducing heat tolerance; however, studies controlling for body size and adiposity show minimal sex differences in thermoregulation, suggesting that physical attributes may drive variations in heat susceptibility.33

Our findings indicate that female patients demonstrated significantly greater heat-related cardiovascular risk in areas with poor adaptive capacity, whereas male patients did not. This finding suggested that female patients may have differential access to, utilization of, or benefit from community-level heat-protection resources.34,35 Some studies addressing social determinants discuss the effect of “gender” on top of “sex.” For example, during a heat wave in Paris, heat risk increased for unmarried men but not for unmarried women.36 In a systematic review on heat-related illness in men and women, the authors found that women are at a lower risk of heat illness than are men across all ages, severity of heat illness, and occupations; however, the reason for these differences was not clear, and they were postulated to be due to sex-related behavioural differences.4 These observations align with our findings that structural composite vulnerability indices, such as HVI, may inadequately capture behavioural and social determinants that differentially protect or expose male and female individuals to health risks.37 To our knowledge, our analysis is one of very few epidemiologic analyses examining sex-stratified heat vulnerability index components in cardiovascular morbidity assessment. Previous heat vulnerability research has predominantly employed population-aggregate methodologies without evaluating sex-specific vulnerability gradients, representing a critical gap in thermal health risk stratification frameworks.

Some limitations should be addressed. First, the inherent spatial aggregation in FSA-level analysis introduces potential modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) bias,38 —statistical relationships may vary systematically depending on the geographic scale and configurational boundaries of analytical units. This methodological consideration could affect the robustness of vulnerability assessments,39 as postal geography boundaries represent administrative rather than ecologically meaningful divisions that may not align with actual exposure gradients, social network structures, or environmental risk distributions that influence heat-health relationships. Second, our analysis focused on the aggregate CVD hospitalizations (ICD-10 codes I00-I99) rather than specific cardiovascular conditions. Heat may differentially affect distinct cardiovascular pathways—for example, acute myocardial infarction via thrombotic mechanisms, heart failure through volume overload, or arrhythmias through electrolyte disturbances. However, many patients present with multiple concurrent cardiovascular diagnoses during heat-related hospitalizations, and administrative coding reflects the principal diagnosis for reimbursement purposes rather than necessarily identifying the primary heat-triggered pathophysiological mechanism. Stratifying by specific CVD subtypes presents several analytical challenges beyond simple categorization, as follows: (i) competing risks and cascade effects; (ii) diagnostic overlap and comorbidity; and (iii) heterogeneity within diagnostic categories. Future CVD-type specific analyses require sophisticated competing risk frameworks and careful consideration of the limitations inherent in administrative diagnostic data for climate-specific research. Third, the 5 observation periods (2018-2022) constrain assessment of long-term climate-health relationships and potential population adaptation effects that may modify heat vulnerability over extended timescales. Our stratified analysis approach facilitates visualization and interpretation of vulnerability patterns but does not provide formal statistical testing of multiplicative interactions. The observed patterns should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of effect modification. In addition, some meteorological indicators were not addressed in our model, such as barometric pressure, which has been suggested as a potential contributor to cardiovascular events in some studies.40,41 Future research with comprehensive meteorological monitoring could explore this association.

Conclusion

Sex differences were observed in the association between ambient temperature and cardiovascular risk. Although overall heat-related risk was comparable between sexes, female individuals demonstrated significantly greater vulnerability in areas lacking adaptive resources such as cooling centres and healthcare facilities. As climate change intensifies extreme heat frequency, our findings establish evidence for sex-stratified heat-protection strategies that prioritize enhancing community-level cooling infrastructure and support systems, particularly in areas with limited adaptive capacity, to safeguard vulnerable populations from escalating thermal cardiovascular risks.
