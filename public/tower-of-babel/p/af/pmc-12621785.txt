Single-port versus multiport robotic surgery in head and neck procedures: the emergence of a new platform
Authors: June Young Choi
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Robotic surgery has rapidly matured across many surgical fields, and the head and neck (H&N) domain is no exception. In this editorial, we focus on the region from the skull base to the neck, explicitly excluding intracranial neurosurgical procedures. This anatomy is defined by narrow corridors, minimal working tolerances, proximity to critical neurovascular structures, and high cosmetic expectations [12]. In H&N surgery, the choice of platform influences both technical strategy and clinical outcomes. Here, we compare single-port (SP) and multiport (MP) robotic approaches in the context of H&N indications, draw reasonable inferences from adjacent specialties, and propose a research and consensus agenda centered on safety, effectiveness, and value [2].

WHY THE PLATFORM DEBATE MATTERS IN HEAD AND NECK

Procedures such as thyroidectomy, central and lateral neck dissection, and transoral resection of the oropharynx or supraglottis are performed alongside nerves responsible for voice, swallowing, and shoulder function. Accordingly, a platform must provide (1) stable three-dimensional visualization; (2) tremor filtration with precise, wristed articulation; (3) ergonomics and docking that do not exacerbate crowding; and (4) instrument reach tailored to the selected corridor—bilateral axillo-breast approach (BABA), transaxillary, retroauricular, transoral, or submental/subplatysmal tunnels [34].

WHAT WE MEAN BY A “PLATFORM”

Consider a familiar analogy. When drafting a document on a computer, you can choose among many apps. If the chosen app does not support printing, you would have to reopen the file in another program to complete the task. For a writing app to function as a true platform, it should offer end-to-end capabilities—authoring, editing, spell-check, printing, signing, security, and more—covering everything the user needs.

Similarly, in the H&N domain, a surgical approach that aspires to be a platform should be capable of both thyroid lobectomy and total thyroidectomy; it should permit central and, when indicated, lateral neck dissection; and it should achieve low recurrence rates with low rates of nerve injury and postoperative hypoparathyroidism.

Beyond capabilities, we must also guard against novelty bias. When a new modality appears, the impulse to be “first” or to gain visibility can nudge surgeons and institutions to portray a technique as a platform before it truly functions as one. Such enthusiasm is understandable, but premature endorsement risks normalizing workflows that are not yet mature or widely reproducible. The non-negotiables remain clear: patient safety, superior or at least noninferior surgical outcomes, and meaningful improvements in quality of life. Any candidate “platform” should therefore be adopted within appropriate safeguards—clear indications, structured training and proctoring, prospective data capture, and transparent benchmarking—until its performance is validated across teams and indications [45].

WHAT THE CURRENT EVIDENCE SUGGESTS

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis comparing SP and MP platforms in H&N surgery by Dr. Mahfoz [6] found that SP consistently shortens docking/setup time, with overall operative time modestly reduced in a subset of studies. Console time was similar between platforms, suggesting that once the operation is underway, technical execution is largely platform-agnostic. Safety profiles were comparable—major complications were rare in both groups, and oncologic adequacy (e.g., negative margins) was noninferior for SP across the included indications. Length of stay and blood loss showed small, directionally favorable signals for SP but were not uniformly consistent.

Importantly, the certainty of evidence is tempered by study design: most comparisons were retrospective, with procedure heterogeneity (transoral robotic surgery, transaxillary, BABA with or without central neck and lateral neck dissection) and potential learning-curve confounding. Across different operations, the overall message is the same: single-port tends to save setup time while keeping safety and cancer control on par with multiport, so it’s a solid option for selected H&N cases [7].

This paper merits an editorial spotlight for its methodological clarity and transparency. It adheres to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) with a clearly documented screening flow and explicit inclusion criteria, and—crucially for operative research—distinguishes among docking time, console time, and total operative time. That operationally meaningful separation is often blurred in surgical meta-analyses. The authors also apply a random-effects framework, report heterogeneity metrics (I2), and grade certainty with GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation), which collectively anchor the conclusions in balanced interpretation rather than overreach.

Equally important, the study focuses on outcomes that clinicians can use in practice. The chosen measures—time elements that affect operating room throughput, major complications, margin status, and the need for conversion—feed directly into how H&N teams choose a platform, plan personnel, and manage room turnover. By demonstrating setup efficiencies for SP systems without a safety penalty, the analysis informs practice in ways that are immediately translatable to scheduling and resource allocation.

The manuscript is also admirably even-handed in its treatment of limitations. It is candid about the predominance of retrospective designs, the potential for learning-curve effects, and the heterogeneity introduced by pooling across procedures. Rather than asserting superiority where the evidence supports noninferiority or context-dependence, the authors carefully delimit their claims and pinpoint where prospective, indication-specific trials are most needed—such as lateral neck dissection and standardized functional outcomes.

Finally, the work balances generalizability with appropriate guardrails. Including diverse H&N procedures and multiple geographies, it suggests relevance beyond a single center or operation. It also marks the conditions under which its conclusions hold, reminding readers that transferability depends on case composition and surgical experience. Overall, it’s a careful, practice-focused summary with sensible limits—the kind of evidence we need to guide the ongoing debate over platforms in H&N robotics.

CONCLUSION

SP and MP systems both meet core needs in H&N robotics but with different strengths—SP offers reproducible setup efficiency and less external crowding, while MP provides arm independence, broader instruments, and wide triangulation for multicompartment work. Current evidence supports SP as a credible alternative to MP with noninferior safety and oncologic adequacy and a consistent advantage in docking time. Platform enthusiasm, however, should not outpace validation: selection ought to be procedure-specific and outcomes-driven, coupled with structured training, prospective data capture, and transparent benchmarking, with future indication-focused trials and cost-effectiveness analyses to refine these choices.
