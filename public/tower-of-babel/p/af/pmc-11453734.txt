12607 Female Mice Exhibit Sex-specific Steatosis Patterns With Aging

Pessin: None.Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) affects 38% of the global population and is becoming a leading cause of liver transplant and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). MASLD affects adult men at higher rates than adult women, but during aging, women lose their protection from MASLD. Declines in serum estrogen during menopause are the suspected cause of this aging-associated loss of protection, but the molecular effects of estrogen loss on the liver have not been fully characterized. Here, we modeled the effect of menopause on MASLD using young and aged female mice on high fat diet and characterized the liver steatosis profile associated with menopause. Male and female C57BL/6J mice were placed on high fat diet (HFD) or low fat chow diet (NCD) for 15 weeks from 6-21 wks/old (young cohort) or 64-79 wks/old (aged cohort). Body weight and fat composition were tracked throughout the experiment. Extracted livers were used to perform hepatic triglyceride (TG) analysis, liver H&E staining, whole liver RT-qPCR, proteomics, and lipidomics. Liver TG analysis showed young female mice had almost no HFD-induced liver steatosis after 15wks while aged female mice develop extensive steatosis. Additionally, aged female mice on HFD gained weight at significantly faster rate than young female mice. Liver H&E staining demonstrated that the steatosis type in aged female mice was exclusively macro-vesicular and located in zone II (mid-zone) of the hepatic lobule compared to combined micro-vesicular and macro-vesicular patterns originating from zone III (pericentral) in male mice. In humans, micro-vesicular steatosis is more severe and associated with disruptions in mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation while macro-vesicular steatosis is more benign1. Proteomic and lipidomic analysis clustered samples according to their steatosis type (macro, micro, or combined) and identified distinct lipid and protein signatures of each steatosis type. These findings suggest there may be two distinct, sex-specific mechanisms by which mammals accumulate fat in the liver: an estrogen-mediated mechanism controlling macro-steatosis and an alternative mechanism regulating micro-vesicular steatosis possibly through impairments in pericentral fatty acid oxidation. This model would have broad implications for MASLD therapeutic strategy. (1) Fromenty, B. & Roden, M. Mitochondrial alterations in fatty liver diseases.
