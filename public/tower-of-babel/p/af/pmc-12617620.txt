Early Oxygenation and Mortality in Infants Treated with Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO) for Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN)
Authors: Marina S Oren, Kikelomo Babata, June Hu, Venkatakrishna Kakkilaya, Patti J Burchfield, Michelle Hagans, L Steven Brown, Satyan Lakshminrusimha, Vishal Kapadia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether oxygenation levels and exposure to hyperoxemia in the first 12 postnatal hours are associated with death in neonates treated with inhaled nitric oxide for persistent pulmonary hypertension of newborn (PPHN).Study designThis was a retrospective study of neonates ≥35 weeks with PPHN treated with inhaled nitric oxide. The cohort was divided into 4 groups on the basis of maximum Pao2 within the first 12 hours: hypoxemia (<60 mm Hg), normoxemia (60-99 mm Hg), mild-moderate (100-199 mm Hg), and severe hyperoxemia (≥200 mm Hg). The primary outcome was death before discharge.ResultsIn total, 118 neonates were included. Hypoxemia, normoxemia, and mild-to-moderate and severe hyperoxemia were present in 14%, 36%, 32%, and 17% of infants, respectively. Mortality followed a U-shaped relationship, with significantly greater rates in hypoxemia (23%) and the severe hyperoxemia (30%) groups and no deaths in the normoxemia group (P < .05). In multivariable Firth logistic regression controlling for confounders, both hypoxemia (OR 29.0; 95% CI 2.8-3937.2, P < .01) and severe hyperoxemia (OR 39.0; 95% CI 4.2-5204.3, P < .01) remained significantly associated with increased odds of death compared with normoxemia.ConclusionsEarly hypoxemia and severe hyperoxemia were associated with mortality in infants with PPHN. These findings underscore the importance of cautious oxygen titration. Prospective studies are needed to establish optimal oxygen management strategies in this population.Key words: persistent pulmonary hypertension of newborn, hyperoxia, oxygen, outcomes, mortality, Spo2, oxygen saturation

Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) is a severe condition, often associated with parenchymal lung disease, that occurs when pulmonary vascular resistance remains abnormally elevated after birth, leading to respiratory distress and hypoxemic respiratory failure. Despite treatment advances, in a large Californian epidemiologic study of infants ≥34 weeks, the authors observed mortality of 7.6% in infants with PPHN, increasing to 10.7% when neonates required positive pressure ventilation.1 In other countries, mortality rates are reported as high as 16%-37%.2, 3, 4

Respiratory support with supplemental oxygen is the mainstay of treatment for PPHN with hypoxemic respiratory failure and improves oxygen delivery. However, data from animal models suggest that hyperoxemia can be associated with negative effects. Although oxygen causes pulmonary vasodilation, hyperoxia can cause pulmonary vasoconstriction by increasing reactive oxygen species, platelet activating factor, and activation of phosphodiesterase 5 in lamb models of PPHN.5, 6, 7 In these models, increasing the inspired fraction of oxygen (Fio2) to 1.0 during resuscitation at delivery impaired response to iNO, the only treatment approved by the Food and Drug Administration for newborns with PPHN, without benefit of additional decrease in pulmonary vascular resistance.7

Despite the deleterious effects, infants with PPHN frequently are exposed to hyperoxemia inadvertently. A recent study reported 82% of neonates treated with iNO experienced hyperoxemia and 25% severe hyperoxemia.8

In the neonatal population, Kapadia et al demonstrated and association between admission hyperoxemia and greater incidence of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) and abnormal brain magnetic resonance findings.9 Subsequently, Badurdeen et al demonstrated that early hyperoxemia was associated with increased risk of death or major disability in infants with HIE participating in the Infant Cooling Evaluation trial, highlighting a U-shaped relationship between partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (Pao2) and mortality or major disability.10 This pattern of increased risk of death with hyperoxemia is also seen in adults and children.11,12 Clinical impact of early hyperoxemia in newborns with PPHN is not well known. The aim of this study is to evaluate whether exposure in the first 12 hours after birth to hyperoxemia is associated with death in infants born at term and near term who were treated with iNO for PPHN.

Methods

Setting

This is a retrospective cohort study of infants born ≥35 weeks of gestational age from January 2016 to December 2022 in 3 level III neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) affiliated with University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas, Texas. The study included Parkland Health and Hospital System, Texas Health Presbyterian Hospital Dallas, and Clements University Hospital. Individual consent was waived. This project was approved by the Office of Research Administration at Parkland Health, the Human Research Protection Program for Texas Health Resources, and by the institutional review board of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center.

PPHN Management Guideline for All 3 Hospitals

From 2016 to 2018, there was no standardized algorithm for the management of infants with PPHN. In 2019, a guideline document was created in which infants with Fio2 requirements >0.4 and labile hypoxemia underwent monitoring of arterial blood gas and pre- and postductal oxygen saturation. The guideline suggested initiating iNO when the oxygen index (OI) was >20, and echocardiographic findings of PPHN such as septal flattening, bidirectional or right-to-left shunting, right ventricular hypertrophy, or estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure ≥50 systemic arterial pressure. Titration of Fio2 to keep preductal Pao2 between 60 and 80 mm Hg and preductal Spo2 >95% was advised. No guidance was given regarding postductal Pao2, weaning iNO or Fio2. There was no mention of how frequently or by what magnitude the Fio2 should be titrated. The guidelines did not state criteria or contraindications for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) referral. Generally, infants with worsening respiratory failure and OI ≥30 were discussed with pediatric intensivists at Children's Medical Center Dallas. ECMO eligibility was determined in a case-by-case basis. Infants referred to ECMO were then transferred from inborn NICU. It remains unclear whether the guidelines were followed during the study period.

Study Population

Infants born at ≥35 weeks gestational age treated with iNO in the first postnatal week were included if they had clinical documentation of PPHN by a physician and echocardiographic evidence of PPHN. Neonates with severe congenital malformations affecting pulmonary and cardiovascular physiology, such as pulmonary hypoplasia secondary to congenital diaphragmatic hernia or fetal renal disease, critical congenital heart disease, severe lymphatic malformations and/or hydrops, and surgical abdominal pathologies, were excluded.

Data Collection

Patients were identified through each center's database search. Patients were selected if gestational age was ≥ 35 weeks and exposed to iNO. Individual electronic medical records were then reviewed to confirm timing of iNO use in the first week of age. Data were obtained from electronic medical records and from databases with standard definitions. Maternal and neonatal variables collected included birth hospital, birth weight, gestational age, sex, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes, and if resuscitation was performed in the delivery room. Etiology of PPHN such as meconium aspiration syndrome, pneumonia, HIE, and respiratory distress syndrome were reviewed. Pulmonary comorbidities such as pulmonary hemorrhage and pneumothorax were also included. Clinical and laboratorial data such as respiratory severity score (mean airway pressure × inspired fraction of oxygen [Fio2]) in the first hour were recorded when available. However, some infants were admitted from the newborn nursery at later time points. Ventilatory mode and Fio2 at NICU admission, age at start of iNO therapy, OI (mean airway pressure × Fio2/Pao2) before and after iNO initiation, Fio2 levels in the first 12 hours of life, surfactant use, use of vasopressors, hydrocortisone treatment, ECMO cannulation, and death during initial hospitalization were collected. Arterial line position also was documented.

Outcome and Exposure

Arterial Pao2 levels from all blood gases obtained in the first 12 postnatal hours were analyzed. On the basis of maximum Pao2 during this period, the cohort was divided into 4 groups: hypoxemia (Pao2 <60 mm Hg), normoxemia (Pao2 60-99 mm Hg), mild-to-moderate hyperoxemia (Pao2 100-199 mm Hg), and severe hyperoxemia (Pao2 ≥200 mm Hg). The definition of hypoxemia was determined by established clinical practice guidelines that recommend Pao2 values >60 mm Hg in the management of PPHN.13,14 Definitions of hyperoxemia and severe hyperoxemia were determined on the basis of previous studies demonstrating increased risk of mortality and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes among newborns exposed to these oxygen levels.9,10

To assess the combined effect of duration and level of hyperoxemia, average time-weighted Pao2 was calculated for each patient using the formula (T1xPaO21)+(T2xPaO22)+(TaxPaO2a)T−total, in which T a represents the time interval in hours between 2 consecutive blood gases, when PaO2 of the second blood gas was assumed to be present.15 For example: if a neonate had Pao2 values of 60 mm Hg at 1 hour, 100 mm Hg at 3 hours, and 200 mm Hg at 5 hours, the calculation would be: (1x60)+(2x100)+(2x200)5 = 132 mm Hg. Primary outcome was death during initial NICU hospitalization.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29.0.2.0, with R extension. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or the Fisher exact test. Medians were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for 2 groups, and with the Kruskal-Wallis test if more than 2 groups. Means were compared with independent t test for 2 groups, and ANOVA if more than 2. To control for known confounders, Firth logistic regression was performed. This model allows for logistic regression in small sample sizes, when there is a high chance of zero events in one of the groups leading to an infinite estimate in the standard logistic regression.16

A directed acyclic graph was created to identify confounding variables associated with Pao2 values and mortality (Figure 1). HIE and gestational age were selected as the most important confounders, because hyperoxemia has been associated with mortality in patients with HIE. Degree of hypoxemia, HIE, and lower gestational age are also predictors for nonresponse to iNO and death in prognostic studies of infants with PPHN. Both logistic regression models were then controlled for those variables.1,3,17,18 In addition, we explored maximum Pao2 and Pao2/Fio2 levels on upper extremity vs lower extremity/umbilical arterial samples to assess for differences between sampling sizes.

Directed acyclic graph (DAG) using arrows to demonstrate relationship between exposure in green arrows (hyperoxemia and hypoxemia) and outcome (death). The pink arrows demonstrate biasing pathways with confounders in pink. Mediator is in light blue (High Fio2). Ancestor of outcome is in blue. Adjusted variables are in white. HIE and low gestational age were selected as most influential and clinically significant.

Last, we used R extension (rms package) to perform a logistic regression model with restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots to evaluate for the presence of a nonlinear relationship between maximum Pao2 as a continuous variable and death. Knots were automatically selected on the basis of percentiles of distribution of Pao2 values. A Wald χ2 test was used to assess the significance of the test. Similarly, RCS was used to evaluate nonlinear relationship between other continuous oxygenation variables such as time-weighted Pao2 and Pao2/Fio2 ratio (Pao2/Fio2), and death.19

Further, the cohort was divided into survivors and nonsurvivors to analyze multiple clinical characteristics and oxygenation parameters and assess the impact of prolonged and/or repeated exposure to hyperoxemia. To account for the nonlinear relationship between oxygenation and outcomes, we stratified the analyses of time-weighted Pao2, mean Fio2, and Pao2/Fio2 ratio by maximum Pao2 group. Normoxemia and mild hyperoxemia were combined because of the absence of deaths in one of the groups. A P value < .05 was considered significant for all analysis.

Results

Between January 2016 and December 2022, 170 infants born ≥35 weeks were treated with iNO for PPHN in the 3 centers included in this study. Of those, 38 infants were excluded for the presence of congenital malformations that interfered significantly with pulmonary function and 14 were excluded for lack of arterial levels of Pao2 in the first 12 postnatal hours, leaving 118 infants included in the final analysis (Figure 2). A total of 523 arterial blood gases from the first 12 hours were analyzed, with an average of 4.4 blood gases per patient.

Baseline characteristics were similar among the 4 groups (Tables I and II), with the exception of proportion of infants with 5-minute Apgar ≤5, which was lower in the normoxemia group. Gestational age, sex, delivery room resuscitation, and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes were similar between the 4 groups. Only 2 infants had right upper extremity arterial access, whereas the remaining had umbilical arterial access or other extremity peripheral arterial line. There was no difference in Pao2 levels in exploratory analysis between sampling sites (Supplementary Table I; available at www.jpeds.com). Admission respiratory support and respiratory severity score at 1 hour were similar.

Hypoxemia was present in 17 (14.4%), and 43 (36.4%) were in the normoxemic range. Mild-moderate and severe hyperoxemia were present in 38 (32.2%) and 20 (16.9%) of infants, respectively.

Regarding clinical course and outcomes, although newborns in hyperoxemia group seem to have iNO started earlier, there was no statistical difference between the 4 groups. Mean OI before starting iNO and duration and response to iNO (measured by OI pre- and post-iNO) were similar. The mean OI in the first 12 hours was greater in the hypoxemia group and lower in the severe hyperoxemia group. Interestingly, the mean Fio2 over the first 12 postnatal hours was lower in the hypoxemia group and greater in the severe hyperoxemia group compared with the normoxemia group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Cannulation for ECMO was performed in 29%, 21%, 18%, and 0% of the hypoxemia group, normoxemia group, mild-moderate hyperoxemia group, and severe hyperoxemia, respectively.

There was a statistically significant difference for in-hospital mortality between the 4 groups. Mortality rate in the hypoxemia (23%) and severe hyperoxemia group (30%) were greater compared with the normoxemia group (0% mortality). Bonferroni adjustment showed normoxemia was significantly different than hypoxemia and hyperoxemia but not mild-moderate hyperoxemia.

Figure 3 demonstrates a U-shaped curve mortality curve, greater in infants with hypoxemia and severe hyperoxemia compared with the infants in the normoxemia group. The association of increased odds of death in the hypoxemia (OR 29.00, 95% CI 2.8-3937.28, P < .01) and severe hyperoxemia group (OR 39.00, 95% CI 4.19-5204.35, P < .01) compared with the normoxemic group remained statistically significant even after controlling for confounders using Firth logistic regression. HIE was also significantly associated with in hospital mortality in this model (Table III).

In the evaluation of direct cause of death: in the hypoxemia group, all 4 infants died from cardiopulmonary failure. Two infants died before the arrival of transport team for ECMO evaluation. Two cases were complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), in one of them, parents opted for partial do not resuscitate orders; in the mild-moderate hyperoxemia group, mortality was attributed to cardiopulmonary failure in 2 infants who died before the arrival of transport team, and one case of withdrawal of support for severe HIE. In the severe hyperoxemia group, 3 infants died from cardiopulmonary failure, with one of those infants undergoing withdrawal of care for nonimprovement of clinical status and severe HIE, one with cardiopulmonary arrest during transfer, and one developed DIC and pulmonary hemorrhage, 2 died from cardiogenic shock and acute kidney injury, with one of those cases involving withdrawal of care for severe acute kidney injury requiring dialysis and severe HIE, the other complicated by DIC when parents opted for partial do not resuscitate orders, and one infant where care was withdrawn for severe HIE.

Discussion

Our multicenter retrospective cohort study showed that both early hypoxemia and hyperoxemia in infants with PPHN were associated with increased risk of death. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in neonates evaluating the association between maximum Pao2 levels in the first 12 postnatal hours and mortality in PPHN.

PPHN is a clinical condition characterized by labile hypoxemia. Interestingly, 49% of newborns in our cohort experienced hyperoxemia in the first 12 hours, with 17% of newborns exposed to severe hyperoxemia. Our study confirms high incidence of hyperoxemia in newborns with PPHN, an observation also noted in a study from the Netherlands that reported 82% of 181 infants with PPHN experienced hyperoxemia during treatment with iNO.8 Another study from a pediatric population in Pennsylvania with 6250 critical care encounters showed 27% of patients had at least one Pao2 level ≥300 mm Hg.12

The unexpected finding of high incidence of hyperoxemia in PPHN newborns may be iatrogenic. Severe hyperoxemia observed in our study was associated with a greater mean Fio2 and lower OI. It can be speculated that this could be due to provider hesitancy and lack of consistency in weaning Fio2 when oxygenation goals have been met or exceeded. Additionally, although the guidelines recommended use of pre-ductal Pao2 to guide oxygenation, only two infants had right upper extremity arterial lines. Although no difference was seen in max Pao2 levels between different sampling sites, it is possible that greater degree of hyperoxemia was present, potentially leading to more toxic effects. Infants enrolled in the therapeutic hypothermia trials within the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Neonatal Research Network with PPHN were also exposed to high mean Fio2 (∼0.8) with high resultant Pao2 (∼150 mm Hg), suggesting that systemic hyperoxemia due to inappropriately high Fio2 administration is a common occurrence in PPHN in infants born near term and term. There is a possibility that the exposure to hyperoxia and not the hyperoxemia itself, leads to the increased risk of death. However, we did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in mean Fio2 between survivors and nonsurvivors (Table IV).20 Other unmeasured factors, such as illness severity, should also be considered as contributors to elevated FiO2 as shown in our directed acyclic graph (Figure 1). In this context, there was a trend to earlier initiation of iNO in the hyperoxemia group compared with the normoxemia (4 h vs 14 h, P = .08), which could suggest more severe clinical illness.

We observed a U-shaped distribution of mortality associated both with hypoxemia and severe hyperoxemia, and lowest mortality in the normoxemic range, even when adjusted for major perinatal variables. This is similar to what was reported by Numa et al, in another large cohort where high admission Pao2 was associated with death.21 A previous pediatric critical care cohort of 4469 patients treated with invasive mechanical ventilation demonstrated a “U-shaped” relationship with lower mortality until a maximum Pao2 of 384 mm Hg, above which mortality rises significantly, similar to our findings.22

It is well known that hypoxic respiratory failure is a common cause of mortality in newborns with PPHN. The precise mechanisms underlying severe hyperoxemia and mortality in PPHN are not clear, and most likely multifactorial. In neonates receiving iNO, greater Fio2 is associated with increased levels of inflammatory markers.23 Similarly, newborn rats exposed to hyperoxia had increased pulmonary inflammatory markers through multiple different pathways including increasing neutrophil chemokines and increased production of reactive oxygen species, which resulted in mitochondrial damage and apoptosis.24,25 In PPHN neonatal lamb models, hyperoxia resulted in decreased response to iNO, with decreased cGMP, increased phosphodiesterase-5 expression, and worsened oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.5,7,26

Presence of HIE as a comorbidity increased the odds of mortality in our study. We have previously shown an association between early hyperoxemia, HIE, and abnormal results on magnetic resonance imaging of the brain in infants with perinatal distress.9 A post-hoc analysis from the Infant Cooling Evaluation trial including 116 infants randomized to hypothermia or normothermia demonstrated that infants with PaO2 ≥ 100 mmHg in the first 2 hours were at greater risk of death or major disability (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.21-2.00).17 Perinatal asphyxia frequently co-exists with PPHN. HIE was diagnosed in 39% of our cohort, with no difference in incidence between different oxygen exposure groups. However, in multivariate logistic regression, HIE was strongly associated with 6.4 times greater odds of mortality. This is in accordance with the study by Zhao et al that showed asphyxia was associated with 4 times greater odds of death in infants with PPHN.18 Similarly, data from Lakshminrusimha et al. demonstrated that in infants with HIE treated with therapeutic hypothermia in 2 clinical trials, PPHN was also associated with increased mortality (27% vs 16%, P < .05).20

The mechanism for this association is unclear. Giesinger et al demonstrated that in infants with HIE, the presence of RV dysfunction was strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including death and abnormal findings on magnetic resonance imaging. Those newborns also had lower tissue oxygen extraction and higher cerebral saturations, which could be a physiological reason for hyperoxemia and poor outcomes, although there was no difference in somatic oxygen saturation. In addition, Fio2 requirements were higher in infants with poor outcomes, making it challenging to establish causality.27

Another mechanism contributing to the increased mortality could be greater levels of oxidative and nitrosative stress (with formation of peroxynitrite) in infants exposed to iNO and hyperoxia.28 It is possible that the interaction of PPHN, HIE, and hyperoxemia is detrimental and culminates in a higher risk of death, as higher Pao2 levels remained significantly associated with increased mortality even when controlled for HIE and other confounders.

Strengths and Limitations

This is a multicenter study of 3 level III NICUs in Dallas, Texas. Study collected all available arterial blood gases and morbidities by doing extensive chart review to perform comprehensive analysis of association between max Pao2 and in-hospital mortality in this patient population.

However, as a retrospective observational study limited to the first 12 postnatal hours it is difficult to establish causation. Although we found an association between hyperoxemia and death, infants in the early hyperoxemia group were treated with iNO earlier than in the normoxemia group, which may reflect severity of illness in this group and be responsible for the difference in outcomes. Our exploratory analysis of survivors and non-survivors diverge from the logistic regression results, likely due to the low frequency of death, large variability in the nonsurvivor group, and non-linear association between exposure and outcome. Logistic regression is a more sensitive approach to explore the association, using all 118 datapoints, and allows for adjusting of confounding variables. The gasometric studies analyzed reflect individual timepoints and not the continuum of exposure to hyperoxemia an infant was experiencing, and it is possible that more blood gases were obtained from sicker infants. The Firth logistic regression was used to address zero events of death in the normoxemia group, but we recognize this may overestimate the OR, especially with small sample sizes.

In conclusion, hyperoxemia in the first 12 postnatal hours was associated with increased mortality in infants treated with iNO for PPHN in a multicenter cohort. A U-shaped curve was noted with greater mortality associated with severe hypoxemia and hyperoxemia. Our findings call for prospective studies evaluating optimal oxygenation values and Fio2 weaning strategies in infants with PPHN, especially with associated HIE. In the meantime, it may be prudent to carefully wean Fio2 to avoid severe hyperoxemia in neonates born at term and near term with PPHN.
