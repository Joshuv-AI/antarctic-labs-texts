Colorimetric correction of electrocatalytic urea quantification
Authors: Tianshang Shan, Hongpan Rong, Zechao Zhuang, Jiarui Yang, Shenghua Li, Jiatao Zhang, Siping Pang, Dingsheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a feasible path for the green and sustainable synthesis of urea, electrochemical C-N coupling reactions have attracted considerable attention. To achieve high catalytic coupling performances, efficient electrocatalysts are indispensable. Notably, precise quantification of urea serves as the cornerstone for assessing catalyst performance. Urease can catalyze the decomposition of urea into ammonium (NH4+), and this process is widely used in the quantification of electrocatalytic urea synthesis via calculating the increase of NH4+ concentration. Unexpectedly, NH4+ impurities are present in all four commercial ureases, posing a substantial risk of false-positive of urea concentration. Even when using the urease with the lowest content of NH4+ impurity, the false-positive production rate of urea can exceed most reported performance thus far in electrocatalytic urea synthesis. Therefore, we recommend a simple procedure that adds NH4+ impurity of commercial ureases as a subtractive term into the equation of conventional urease method for reliable urea quantification. The accuracy of the refined urease method is validated in previously reported CO2/NO3− system, thereby ensuring reliable development in urea electrosynthesis.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Analytical chemistry

Introduction

Urea (CO(NH2)2), accounting for ~40% of global food production, has become the most widely used fertilizer with its high nitrogen content (46%) and easy conversion into plant-available ammonium (NH4+)-N by the urease in soil1–3. The global urea production now exceeds 100 Mt per year, mainly relying on the energy-intensive Bosch−Meiser process (150–200 °C; 150–250 bar)4, which consumes ~80% of industrial NH3 synthesized via the Haber-Bosch process (300–500 °C; 150–200 bar), contributing to ~2% of global energy consumption5. The high-energy-demanding processes highlight an urgent imperative to develop green and sustainable synthesis of urea. Currently, the emerging field of green electrocatalytic C-N coupling, as an expansive and critical research frontier, represents a paradigm shift in sustainable urea production. It has established a transformative technological pathway for simultaneously closing both the carbon and nitrogen cycles by coupling CO/CO2 with diverse nitrogen species (e.g., NH36, N27–9, NO2−10,11, and NO3−12–16) (Fig. 1a). Among them, the electrocatalytic transformation of CO2 and NO3− is highlighted over the past 5 years, attributed to the imperative for negative carbon emissions and the comparatively lower dissociation energy of the N = O bond (204 kJ·mol−1) in NO3− against N217–21.

However, the urea yield (at the level of mmol·gcat.−1·h−1) of most reported electrocatalysts remains relatively low, posing considerable challenges for precise urea quantification. Hence, several methods have been developed rapidly over the past 5 years (Fig. 1b), including colorimetric methods (urease-indophenol blue (Urease-IB) method and diacetyl monoxime (DAMO) method), 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) method, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method. To date, the Urease-IB method has become the main approach due to its simplicity, low experimental quantification minimum (EQM, > 0.1 ppm), and reliable accuracy, offering a robust alternative to the DAMO method, which is prone to false positives by NO2− interference (a widely existing byproduct generated during CO2 + NO3− electrocatalysis)22–25. In the Urease-IB method, urease can catalyze one molecule of urea into one molecule of CO2 and two molecules of NH4+ (Supplementary Fig. 1a)26. Thus, the urea concentration can be calculated based on the increase in NH4+ released by urea decomposition. It is worth noting that, in the protocol reported by Zhang et al., strict control of hydrolysis time, reaction temperature, and urease dosage was emphasized, along with minimizing system contamination from other C/N-containing impurities (e.g., in catalysts, the reactor, and reactants). However, the possibility that the urease itself may contain impurities seems not to have been fully considered25. At present, nearly all commercially available ureases are crystallized from Jack beans, during which ammonium sulfate ((NH4)2SO4) is probably used as a salting-out agent (Supplementary Fig. 1b)27. Therefore, although prior studies have proved the reliability of the Urease-IB method for urea quantification, the presence of (NH4)2SO4 residues in commercial ureases and their potential impact on urea quantification have not received adequate attention.

To address this gap, we selected four commercial ureases (Urease-S10095 (Lot#1: KS342564), Urease-R002401 (Lot#1: RH674004), Urease-U835597 (Lot#1: C17397396), and Urease-U1500 (Lot#1: 0000398390)) from various chemical suppliers for comparative urea quantification (Supplementary Fig. 2), based on previously reported literature (Supplementary Table 1). Unexpectedly, UV-vis and 1H NMR analyses revealed that all four commercial ureases contain NH4+ impurity (NH4+[Urease]). The NH4+-N content in Urease-S10095 (Lot#1: KS342564), Urease-R002401 (Lot#1: RH674004), Urease-U835597 (Lot#1: C17397396), and Urease-U1500 (Lot#1: 0000398390) were 1865.78 ± 28.98, 1633.80 ± 57.68, 379.82 ± 23.66, and 114.10 ± 13.44 ppm, respectively (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figs. 3–5). Given the substantial batch-to-batch variation in NH4+ levels in ureases, Urease-S10095 (Lot#2: LS432922), Urease-R002401 (Lot#2: RH857140), Urease-U835597 (Lot#2: C18419883), and Urease-U1500 (Lot#2: 0000497227) with different batches are also quantified (Supplementary Fig. 6). Interestingly, although some ureases, such as Urease-R002401 (from 1633.80 ± 57.68 to 258.24 ± 7.80 ppm) and Urease-U835597 (from 379.82 ± 23.66 to 1161.05 ± 27.02 ppm), exhibit larger fluctuations, averaging the NH4+ contents across different batches still yield a consistent trend: Urease-S10095 > Urease-R002401 > Urease-U835597 > Urease-U1500 (Supplementary Table 2). This result seemingly reflects inherent differences in crystallization and purification processes among vendors. Accordingly, we used the previous batches of these four ureases, denoted as Urease-S10095, Urease-R002401, Urease-U835597, and Urease-U1500, for subsequent experiments. Notably, under a specified assumptions (electroreduction time: 300 s, catalyst mass: 0.025 mg, urease concentration (add after electroreduction): 5.0 mg·mL−1) referred to reported researches of urea electrosynthesis (Supplementary Table 1), the NH4+ of all four commercial ureases caused a substantial false-positive production rate of urea reaching from 29.22 ± 5.58 to 513.17 ± 20.47 mmol·gcat.−1·h−1 in standard 0.1 M KHCO3/KNO3 systems (Supplementary Table 3). Even the lowest value caused by Urease-U1500 surpassed most of currently reported production rates of urea electrosynthesis (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, in the actual CO2/NO3− electrocatalytic system, the rapid reduction of NO3− to NH4+ at elevated potentials generates high NH4+ concentrations, necessitating dilution prior to urease addition. Given the impact of dilution, false-positive urea yield values will be further amplified by the dilution factor.

Therefore, we recommend introducing a subtractive term (NH4+[Urease]) into the conventional Urease-IB method to offset the impact of NH4+ residues in commercial ureases. Encouragingly, the accuracy and feasibility of the refined Urease-IB method were further validated in CO2/NO3− systems. By revealing the presence of NH4+ residues in commercial ureases, our findings aim to positively serve the scientific community by providing a more reliable protocol for future electrocatalytic urea quantification studies.

Results

Revealing unexpected NH4+ impurity in commercial ureases

NH4+ is abundant in soils and serves as a key inorganic nitrogen source for plant growth28,29. Unexpectedly, NH4+ impurity has been detected in all four commercial ureases by IB method, and the UV-vis absorbance of each urease showed a good linear relationship (R2 ≈ 0.999) with its concentration (Fig. 2 and Fig. 3a–h). To enhance measurement accuracy and minimize relative errors associated with low enzyme concentrations, a standardized aqueous urease solution of 5.0 mg·mL−1 was adopted for NH4+ quantification (Supplementary Table 1). Subsequent analysis determined the NH4+-N contents to be 1865.78 ± 70.98 ppm for Urease-S10095, 1633.80 ± 57.68 ppm for Urease-R002401, 379.82 ± 23.66 ppm for Urease-U835597, and 114.10 ± 13.44 ppm for Urease-U1500. A striking disparity was observed among these commercial ureases, but no clear correlation with specific activity (U·mg−1) was evident (Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably, Urease-S10095 exhibited the highest level of NH4+ impurity, exceeding the concentration found in Urease-U1500 by more than 16-fold. Therefore, this notable finding serves as a clear warning that the issue of NH4+ impurity in commercial ureases cannot be overlooked and must be given serious consideration.

To provide further validation of the results from UV-vis based IB method, 1H NMR (500 MHz) method was employed to quantify NH4+ impurity in the same four commercial ureases. Two representative concentrations of each urease (2.5 and 5.0 mg·mL−1; Fig. 3i–p and Supplementary Fig. 5) were chosen to compare consistency between the IB and 1H NMR methods. For Urease-S10095 and Urease-R002401, the concentrations of NH4+ impurity obtained by the IB and 1H NMR methods were in close agreement, with a minimal discrepancy of approximately 6%. This strong concordance between the two methods significantly reinforces the reliability of our finding for the existence of NH4+ impurity hidden in commercial ureases. For Urease-U835597 and Urease-U1500, no significant 1H NMR signals of NH4+ were detected at the two concentration levels of 2.5 and 5.0 mg·mL−1. This lack of detection is likely because the trace NH4+ levels are near the limit of detection (LOD, ~1.57 ppm) of the 500 MHz 1H NMR instrument used here. In addition, instrumental stability may further hinder the LOD.

Quantification of urea in standard solution

To study the relative errors induced by NH4+ impurity for urea quantification, it is necessary to select a specific urea concentration. By analyzing reported studies on urea electrocatalysis (CO2/NO3−) over the past 5 years (Supplementary Table 1), we found that part of the researches provided UV-vis spectra before and after the addition of urease. Based on these data, the corresponding urea concentrations mostly fall within the range of 0–5.0 ppm calculated by Urease-IB method. Herein, we selected the intermediate value (2.5 ppm) as the urea concentration to investigate the impact of NH4+ impurity on the relative errors of urea quantification. Besides, 5.0 mg·mL−1 has been the most used concentration of urease for urea quantification as illustrated in Supplementary Table 1. Therefore, the concentrations of urea and urease solutions are 2.5 ppm and 5.0 mg·mL−1, respectively. We also prepared a 2.5 ppm NH4+-N solution and a mixed solution of 2.5 ppm urea/2.5 ppm NH4+-N as control samples to study the interference of NH4+. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 8–11 (Solvent: 0.1 M KHCO3/KNO3), the relative deviation between the measured NH4+ concentration in the Urease/urea/NH4+ mixture and the sum of the NH4+ concentrations from the individual components (urease, urea, and NH4+) were less than 1%. This demonstrates the good interference resistance of the Urease-IB method in electrolyzed electrolyte.

As shown in Fig. 4a, when quantifying a standard 2.5 ppm urea solution (0.1 M KHCO3/KNO3), the false urea induced by NH4+ impurity from Urease-S10095, Urease-R002401, Urease-U835597, and Urease-U1500 constituted substantial portions of the total urea quantitation, which accounted for 46.6% ± 1.8%, 41.5% ± 2.4%, 14.6% ± 1.6%, and 3.9% ± 1.2%, respectively. Since the urea yield (Supplementary Table 1) is a key evaluation indicator for electrocatalytic urea synthesis, with a unit of mmol·gcat.−1·h−1, it is necessary to specify the catalyst mass and electroreduction time to calculate this value. Based on the reported studies (Supplementary Table 1), we selected four catalyst masses and three electroreduction times for the calculation (Supplementary Table 3). When selecting catalyst mass of 0.025 mg and electroreduction time of 300 s, the false-positive urea production rates induced by NH4+ in Urease-S10095, Urease-R002401, Urease-U835597, and Urease-U1500 were calculated to be 513.17 ± 20.47, 438.72 ± 24.30, 107.40 ± 13.03, and 29.22 ± 5.58 mmol·gcat.−1·h−1, respectively. Even the lowest value caused by Urease-U1500 has exceeded most of currently reported production rates of urea electrosynthesis over the past 5 years (Supplementary Table 1). This result is striking, as it was unexpected that the NH4+ impurity in commercial ureases from chemical reagent vendors could cause such a significant false-urea yield. Therefore, we have developed a refined Urease-IB method to minimize the interference of NH4+ impurity from four commercial ureases. In detail, we modified the conventional formula of Urease-IB method by incorporating a corrective term, NH4+[Urease]. After this colorimetric correction, the relative errors of Urease-S10095, Urease-R002401, Urease-U835597, and Urease-U1500 decreased markedly from 87.2% ± 3.3%, 70.8% ± 4.1%, 17.1% ± 1.8%, and 4.0% ± 1.3% to an absolute relative error (≤ 2.2%) in urea quantification, confirming the accuracy and reliability of the refined Urease-IB method (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 4).

Quantification of urea in CO2/NO3− electrocatalytic system

In practical electrocatalytic reaction systems, the precise quantification of urea may be interfered with by the generated intermediates and byproducts. To date, the effects of byproducts such as NO2− and NH2OH on urea quantification by the Urease-IB method have been extensively investigated. For example, Wang et al. systematically demonstrated NO2− and NH2OH are not urease substrates and do not undergo enzymatic decomposition, and that 0.5–50 mM NO2− and 5 ppm NH2OH do not interfere with the quantification of 2 ppm urea using the Urease-IB method22. For lower urea concentrations (0.5 ppm), Dong et al. examined that 0–100 ppm NO2− induces relative errors within 3%, whereas NH2OH causes measurable relative errors (~20%) when present at concentrations more than 10-fold higher than that of urea23. In our electrocatalytic system (Fig. 5a, b), the concentration of urea is 2.5 ppm, while the concentrations of NO2−-N (< 4.0 ppm) and NH2OH (< 4.5 ppm) are far below the previously reported interference thresholds. Therefore, additional interference evaluation was not considered.

To further demonstrate the feasibility and reliability of the refined Urease-IB method for precise urea quantification in a real electrocatalytic system, we consulted reported studies on electrocatalytic urea synthesis over the past 5 years to determine the conditions. Specifically, we used pristine carbon paper, which is essentially incapable of producing urea, as the working electrode, and 0.1 M KHCO3/KNO3 as the electrolyte in a H-cell at room temperature (25 °C). The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) and i-t curves of pristine carbon paper was illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Figs. 14–24, pristine carbon paper generated various products at the potential range of −0.6 V to −1.3 V versus (vs.) RHE, including liquid-phase products (e.g., NH4+, NH2OH, and NO2−) and gaseous products (e.g., H2), without urea detected by 500 MHz 1H NMR (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). This finding directly provides negative validation that pristine carbon paper hardly generates urea within the experimental potential range (−0.6 V to −1.3 V vs. RHE). Furthermore, we evaluated an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) method for urea quantification, as it typically affords higher separation efficiency and faster analysis than conventional HPLC method. However, UHPLC was ultimately not adopted because the strong UV absorption of NO3− in the 200–220 nm region overlaps with the urea absorption at 195 nm, causing severe interference that impedes accurate quantification (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28)30,31.

After the electroreduction reaction, urea was added to the catholytes (which showed no urea signal by 500 MHz 1H NMR) to a theoretical concentration of 2.5 ppm. Then the urea quantification was investigated using 5.0 mg·mL−1 urease (Supplementary Table l) via Urease-IB method. The capability of urease (5.0 mg·mL−1) to fully decompose 2.5 ppm urea to NH4+ in the electrolyte was further validated by control experiments at −1.3 V vs. RHE, where the concentrations and varieties of byproducts (NH4+, NH2OH, and NO2−) are almost the highest. In detail, urea was then added to this electrolyte to reach a theoretical concentration of 25 ppm (10‑fold higher than 2.5 ppm, to assess the decomposition capacity of urease), followed by the addition of urease (5.0 mg·mL−1) at the same electrolyte‑to‑urease ratio (9:1) and reaction conditions (37 °C, 1 h) as used in the refined Urease‑IB method. Subsequently, 1H NMR analysis (Supplementary Figs. 29–31) confirmed that the 25 ppm urea was completely decomposed by the four commercial ureases, demonstrating the strong decomposition capability of ureases toward urea. In addition, the measured urea concentrations for all four commercial ureases (Urease‑S10095, Urease‑R002401, Urease‑U835597, and Urease‑U1500) by the refined Urease-IB method remained close to the expected 2.5 ppm over the potential range −0.6 V to −1.3 V vs. RHE (Supplementary Fig. 32). This result indirectly proved that pristine carbon paper hardly produces urea under our experimental conditions.

For the quantification of liquid products, all samples obtained at various potentials were diluted to fall within the linear range of the UV-vis calibration curve established for the corresponding analyte (e.g., NH4+ and NH2OH). Notably, for the measurement of NH4+ concentration, a specific dilution strategy was employed. The absorbance increases corresponding to NH4+ from the conversion of 2.5 ppm urea are between 0.3 and 0.4 arb. units. In addition, among these four commercial ureases, the maximum absorbance increase (~0.3 arb. units) was observed when Urease-S10095 was used. Therefore, in the potential range of −0.8 V to −1.3 V vs. RHE, all post-electrolysis electrolytes were diluted until their absorbance dropped below 1.0. This step guaranteed that the final UV-vis absorbance—after adding urea and urease solution—would remain within the linear range of the NH4+ calibration curve. For the gaseous products, no CO and C2+ products (e.g., C2H2, C2H4, and C2H6) were detected by gas chromatography (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). Only CH4 reached a maximum Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 0.13% at −1.3 V vs. RHE.

Then, all four commercial ureases at a concentration of 5.0 mg·mL−1 were applied for the urea quantification by the Urease-IB method (Supplementary Figs. 33–41). However, the necessary dilution step for NH4+ leads to an alarming fold increase in the false-positive urea production rate in CO2/NO3− electrocatalytic system. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, without subtracting NH4+[Urease], the relative error of urea obtained with Urease-S10095 (diluted × 5) reached as high as 433.4% ± 17.4% at −1.3 V vs. RHE. While even Urease-U1500, containing the least NH4+ impurity, still exhibited a relative error of 33.0% ± 6.6% at the same potential. This overestimation introduced by NH4+ impurity was attributed to the amplified relative errors from dilution (Supplementary Table 5). Encouragingly, after correcting for the influence of residual NH4+ in each commercial urease, the relative errors of urea quantification at all potentials (from −0.6 V to −1.3 V vs. RHE) sharply decreased to an acceptable level (≤ 5.1%), thereby validating the feasibility and reliability of the refined Urease-IB method.

In summary, it is essential to screen all commercial ureases bought from chemical reagent vendors for inherent NH4+ impurity via a simple UV-vis protocol. Our findings establish that integrating NH4+[Urease], as a subtraction term, into the conventional Urease-IB method successfully rectifies the systematic overestimation of urea yield. This procedural refinement is crucial for deriving trustworthy FEs of urea, ultimately providing more rigorous and reliable data for assessing the true performance of catalysts in the electrochemical synthesis of urea.

Discussion

Unexpectedly, NH4+ impurity was detected in all four commercial ureases (Urease-S10095, Urease-R002401, Urease-U835597, and Urease-U1500), probably originating from the use of (NH4)2SO4 as a salting-out agent during the industrial isolation of crystalline urease from Jack bean. If the influence of residual ammonium ions is not considered, it can lead to significant errors, resulting in false positives in the evaluation of catalysts for electrocatalytic urea synthesis. To be specific, when quantifying 2.5 ppm urea in an undiluted standard system (0.1 M KHCO3/KNO3) using Urease-S10095 solutions (5.0 mg·mL−1), the maximum relative error could reach a striking 87.2% ± 3.3%. Moreover, even Urease-U1500, which has lowest NH4+ impurity, can yield a shocking false urea production rate exceeding 29 mmol·gcat.−1·h−1 under the specified conditions (sampling time: 300 s, catalyst loading: 0.025 mg·cm−2, urease concentration: 5.0 mg·mL−1), surpassing most reported electrocatalysts for urea synthesis. Besides, it is crucial to note that this consequence is based on undiluted conditions. For practical electrocatalytic reaction systems, a dilution factor is introduced due to the generated NH4+, making the situation far more problematic. Herein, we proposed a refined Urease-IB method which added a subtractive item (NH4+[Urease]) for the accurate quantification of urea. After this colorimetric correction, the relative errors in urea quantification decreased markedly and fell within an acceptable range in CO2/NO3− electrocatalytic systems. To better serve the scientific community, we therefore propose the adoption of this refined Urease-IB method as a standardized protocol for urea quantification. Its implementation will enable a more accurate evaluation of the structure-activity relationships between electrocatalysts and urea production, thereby promoting the sustainable development of this field.

Determination of residual NH4+ in commercial ureases

The NH4+ contents in commercial ureases were quantified via the modified Indophenol-Blue (IB) method16 (all “ppm” concentrations are mass ratios, μg·g−1, numerically equivalent to mg·L−1 for the dilute aqueous solutions used in this study). In details, 0.9 mL pure water was mixed with 0.1 mL urease solution (e.g., 0.5 mg·mL−1, 1.0 mg·mL−1, 2.5 mg·mL−1, and 5.0 mg·mL−1), 1 mL NaOH solution (1 M, containing 5 wt.% salicylic acid and 5 wt.% sodium citrate), 0.5 mL NaClO (1% (w/w)) and 0.1 mL C5FeN6Na2O·2H2O aqueous solution (1 wt.%). After reaction in darkness for 1 h, the absorption spectra were measured using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu) and the absorption intensity at 660 nm was applied to calculate the NH4+ concentration of electrolytes at different potentials. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, the concentration-absorbance curves were calibrated using standard solutions with a series concentration of NH4Cl.

Furthermore, the concentrations of NH4+ impurity in commercial ureases were determined using the modified 1H NMR method32. For analysis, 0.5 mL of an aqueous urease solution (2.5 mg·mL−1 and 5.0 mg·mL−1) was mixed with 25 μL of HCl and 100 μL of DMSO-d6, followed by acquisition of spectra on the 1H NMR spectrometer (500 MHz) with 32 scans. A linear correlation was observed between NH4+ concentration and the integrated area of the characteristic resonance signals (chemical shift: 6.8–7.1 ppm). Electrocatalytic measurements

Electrochemical urea synthesis was evaluated in a H-cell, where the cathode and anode compartments were separated by a Nafion 117 membrane (DuPont) with a thickness of 183 μm and an effective area of ≈ 1.77 cm2 (diameter: 1.5 cm). Prior to use, the Nafion 117 membrane underwent sequential pretreatment by immersion in 5% H2O2 solution and pure water at 80 °C for 1 h, followed by 0.05 M H2SO4 solution and pure water under identical conditions. Pristine TGP‑H‑060 carbon paper (1 × 2 cm, active area: 1 × 1 cm) served as the working electrode without pretreatment. Electrochemical measurements were carried out on a CHI 760E workstation using a three-electrode system, with an Ag/AgCl electrode (saturated KCl electrolyte) as the reference electrode and a platinum (Pt) sheet electrode as the counter electrode. The Ag/AgCl reference electrode was calibrated in H2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4, using a Pt sheet as the working electrode and a graphite rod as the counter electrode. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed at a scan rate of 1 mV·s−1, and the thermodynamic potential of the hydrogen redox reaction, corresponding to 0 V vs. RHE, was determined from the average of the two potentials at which the current equals 0. All potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using the Nernst relation (ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.059 × pH + 0.197). The solution resistance was measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and all the voltages in our work were reported without IR compensation (non-IR corrected). EIS of the catalysts was carried out at an amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range from 1 MHz to 0.01 Hz. The measurements of resistance were repeated three times, and the value is reported as mean ± standard deviation. The electrochemical data were collected using the CHI software and exported for further processing. Experiments were conducted at room temperature (25 °C) in 0.1 M KHCO3/KNO3 electrolyte, which was used as both catholyte and anolyte, with a measured pH of 8.30 ± 0.04 using a PHS-25 pH meter. Freshly prepared electrolyte was stored in a volumetric flask in the dark and used immediately. Before electrolysis, CO2 (99.999%) was bubbled through the catholyte for 30 min to displace dissolved air. Following each electrochemical reduction, the catholyte was collected for subsequent analysis of quantification.

Products identification and quantification

Determination of Urea in standard/practical electrocatalytic system

Conventional Urease-IB method35

Briefly, 0.1 mL of urease solution (5.0 mg·mL−1) was added into 0.9 mL of sample solution (urea was added to achieve a final concentration of 2.5 ppm), and then heated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 mole of urea (CO(NH2)2) can be decomposed to 1 mole of CO2 and 2 moles of NH4+ in aqueous solution. Next, NH4+ concentrations of catholyte before and after urease decomposition were detected via the IB method. The NH4+ of test solution after urease decomposition (nNH4+, after enzymolysis) consist of NH4+ before urease decomposition (nNH4+, before enzymolysis) and NH4+ decomposed by urea (nurea). Therefore, the moles of urea (nurea) can be calculated by (nNH4+, after enzymolysis − nNH4+, before enzymolysis)/2.

Refined Urease-IB method

The quantification of urea in test solution was performed using the same procedure as described above (Conventional Urease-IB method), except that adding NH4+ in commercial urease (nNH4+[Urease]) as a subtracting item into the conventional formular of urea quantification. Hence, the moles of urea (nurea) can be calculated by (nNH4+, after enzymolysis − nNH4+, before enzymolysis − nNH4+[Urease])/2.

1H NMR method

A total of 0.5 mL of sample solution was mixed with 100 μL of DMSO-d6, followed by acquisition of spectra (32 scans) on the 1H NMR spectrometer (500 MHz) with water suppression. A linear correlation was observed between concentration and the integrated area of the characteristic resonance signals (chemical shift: 5.6 ppm). Thus, the urea content could be determined by applying the calibration equations (Supplementary Fig. 25).

UHPLC method

For analysis, UHPLC was performed using a C18 column with the corresponding mobile phase (acetonitrile/water: 5:95), column temperature (30 °C), flow rate (0.1 mL·min−1), and wavelength (λ = 195 nm). A linear correlation was observed between concentration and the integrated area of the characteristic absorbance signals (76 s). Thus, the urea content could be determined by applying the calibration equations (Supplementary Fig. 27).

The NO2− contents in catholyte were determined by a modified Griess assay36. Subsequently, 3 mL of the diluted catholyte (within the calibration curve range) was sequentially mixed with solutions A and B.
