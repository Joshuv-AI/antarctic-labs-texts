Long-term outcomes of two surgical protocols for the autotransplantation of mandibular third molars: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nadim Sleman, Abdulqader Alomar, Ali Khalil, Mounzer Assad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This randomized clinical trial aimed to compare the mid-term (5-year) prognosis of mandibular third molar autotransplantation between two surgical protocols: conventional autotransplantation and immediate pulpectomy autotransplantation.Materials and Methods:Forty patients with unrestorable molars and suitable impacted third molars were randomized into two equal groups. Group I (n = 20) underwent conventional autotransplantation, while Group II (n = 20) received extra-alveolar pulpectomy (performed on the extracted donor tooth before transplantation). Primary outcomes were 5-year tooth survival, tooth mobility, root resorption, and periapical pathology.Results:The overall 5-year survival rate was 92.5%. Survival was 95% in the conventional autotransplantation group and 90% in the immediate pulpectomy autotransplantation group, with no statistically significant difference (log-rank test, P > 0.05). All cases of periapical pathology (10%) occurred in the conventional autotransplantation group, while root resorption (15%) was equally distributed. No significant differences were found for any secondary outcomes.Conclusion:Autotransplantation of mandibular third molars demonstrates excellent 5-year survival. Selecting between conventional autotransplantation and immediate pulpectomy autotransplantation. involves a clinical balance. Immediate autotransplantation preserves periodontal ligament vitality but may necessitate later endodontic treatment, whereas prior pulpectomy ensures endodontic stability. Both approaches are clinically sound, and the decision can be individualized according to surgical conditions without affecting mid-term survival.

Introduction

Dental reimplantation, also known as tooth autotransplantation, is a surgical procedure that involves the repositioning of an erupted, partially erupted, or non-erupted autologous tooth from one site to another within the same individual. This technique has been utilized as a therapeutic alternative for replacing missing or non-restorable teeth since it was first reported in 1950[1,2].

While autotransplantation is not widely utilized in general dental practice, it represents a clinically effective and economically feasible alternative to more complex prosthetic and dental implant-based treatments[3]. This is particularly relevant in cases of significant alveolar bone loss, where implant placement often necessitates extensive, multi-staged bone augmentation procedures such as vertical and horizontal ridge augmentation with guided bone regeneration (GBR) prior to rehabilitation[4,5].

In contrast, successful autotransplantation can simultaneously address tooth loss and induce alveolar bone regeneration, potentially obviating the need for such complex preparatory surgeries and avoiding the multi-stage approaches often required to manage bone complications following other therapies[6].

Despite its biological advantages such as the preservation of a functional periodontal ligament, proprioception, and potential for alveolar bone induction, clinical adoption remains limited. A recent survey of dentists revealed that 71.7% did not consider dental autotransplantation (DAT) in their treatment plans, and 59.3% either did not view it as a viable option or were uncertain of its viability[7]. This underscores a significant gap between emerging evidence and real-world clinical practice, highlighting the need for further high-quality clinical studies to validate and promote this underutilized procedure.

The scope of tooth autotransplantation extends far beyond the simple replacement of a non-restorable tooth. A growing body of evidence supports its use in a wide range of complex clinical scenarios. These indications include the management of developmental dental anomalies (such as regional odontodysplasia), congenital tooth absence (hypodontia), closure of oroantral communications, reconstruction of alveolar clefts, rehabilitation of deficient alveolar ridges, surgical repositioning of ectopic or impacted teeth, and as part of the reconstructive approach following maxillofacial injuries[8]. This breadth of application highlights its versatility as a biologically driven reconstructive modality.

In the specific context of replacing missing teeth in the growing adolescent patients, particularly in situations where permanent molars are lost prematurely due to extensive caries, trauma, or other pathological processes, lower third molars frequently emerge as a valuable source for autotransplantation[9,10].

Incorporating autotransplantation of teeth into the orthodontic treatment repertoire, a novel therapeutic option emerges for specific clinical scenarios, enabling the relocation of a tooth to distant or opposing segments of the same dental arch or even to the contralateral jaw. Beyond its role in tooth replacement, autotransplantation offers additional advantages, most notably the potential for bone induction and the restoration of a functional alveolar process. Even in cases where the transplant ultimately fails, the recipient site remains intact and can subsequently be utilized for dental implant placement[11].

The success of autotransplantation is thought to be determined by a number of factors, which include patient age, developmental stage of the transplanted tooth, type of tooth transplanted, surgical technique employed, and extra-alveolar time span before the tooth is transplanted[12–14]. However, the presence of healthy and viable periodontal ligament (PDL) cells on the root surface is a critical factor for healing and mid-term success[15].

The viability of the PDL is the most significant determinant of successful tooth transplantation. The PDL is highly sensitivite to pH fluctuations and its susceptibility to degradation during prolonged extraoral dry periods necessitate careful handling and rapid reimplantation procedures[16–18].

Studies show that extraoral times exceeding 18 minutes significantly reduce PDL cell survival and thus transplantation success. Immediate autotransplantation into a fresh socket maximizes the chances of PDL healing[19–21].

The PDL contains cells that can differentiate into cementoblasts, osteoblasts, and fibroblasts, which are essential for tissue regeneration and the re-establishment of a normal periodontium at the recipient site[22].

Atraumatic extraction of the donor tooth is critical to preserve the PDL and minimize damage. The probability of successful periodontal ligament regeneration is substantially increased when a donor tooth is immediately implanted into a freshly prepared extraction site. The use of atraumatic surgical techniques is crucial for maintaining existing bone and periodontal structures[23].

Trauma to the donor tooth’s root surface during extraction impairs PDL regeneration, hindering transplant integration and potentially causing compromised root growth, ankylosis, or root resorption. Adequate PDL regeneration is therefore essential for successful transplantation[24,25].

This study aims to assess the 5-year prognosis of mandibular third molar autotransplantation, comparing outcomes following conventional autotransplantation with those following immediate pulpectomy autotransplantation.

Materials and methods

To address the research purpose, the investigators designed and implemented a randomized clinical trial in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with the Consolidated Standard of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement writing guidelines for clinical trials[26].

Study sample

The study included 40 patients with impacted lower third molars and indicated molars to be extracted. Randomization was performed using a manual card-based method: each patient selected one card from a sealed, opaque envelope containing cards numbered either “1” or “2.” Number 1 indicated pulpectomy preceding molar replacement, while number 2 indicated conventional autotransplantation without pulpectomy. The envelopes were prepared and shuffled by a research assistant not involved in the clinical procedures to minimize selection bias.

Preparation and assessment

A comprehensive preoperative assessment was conducted for all patients. This included clinical examination, periapical radiographs, and panoramic radiography (orthopantomogram) to evaluate the recipient site for adequate bone height, width, keratinized tissue, and proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve. The donor tooth’s root development, crown mesiodistal width, and length were also assessed from these radiographs.

For cases where two-dimensional radiographs suggested close proximity of the donor tooth to the inferior alveolar nerve (n = 12), or when complex root morphology was suspected, an additional preoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was obtained. The CBCT data were used to precisely visualize the relationship between the donor tooth roots and the mandibular canal, as well as to assess the three-dimensional anatomy of the recipient site.

Surgical protocol

Recipient site preparation

Prior to surgery, patients rinsed with 0.02% chlorhexidine for 1 minute, followed by local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine and 1:100 000 epinephrine.

Recipient site preparation involved full-thickness flap elevation and extraction of the compromised molar. The site was then prepared using surgical and implant burs with saline irrigation; the socket was prepared by removing the interradicular septum, taking the donor tooth’s measurements into account.

While flapless extraction is less invasive, it was deemed unsuitable for this study as it limits visibility and controlled socket modification, increasing the risk of alveolar plate fracture or inadequate preparation. Socket Preparation (Osteotomy):

Instrumentation: The recipient socket was precisely prepared using sequential round and tapered carbide surgical burs and, in cases requiring significant enlargement, implant drills (specifically, parallel-walled osteotomy drills). Implant drills were selected for their precision, ability to create parallel walls, and efficient bone removal with minimal heat generation.

Standardization: All drilling was performed with a surgical micromotor at a standardized low speed of 800–1200 rpm under continuous, copious external saline irrigation to prevent thermal osteonecrosis.

Donor Tooth Extraction

Atraumatic Technique: Atraumatic extraction of the impacted third molar was paramount. A mucoperiosteal flap was reflected, and bone removal was performed with a surgical bur under saline irrigation to expose the cementoenamel junction fully. The tooth was luxated using straight and Cryer elevators placed at the mesiobuccal and distobuccal line angles, applying controlled rotational forces rather than levering. The tooth was then delivered with forceps, gripping only the crown. The goal was to sever the periodontal ligament fibers while avoiding any crushing force or contact with the root surface.

Donor tooth handling: Group I – Without Prior Pulpectomy (conventional autotransplantation protocol): Immediately upon extraction, the donor tooth was placed in a sterile container with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) to maintain PDL cell viability. The tooth was handled only by the crown. No endodontic procedure was performed at this stage. The extra-alveolar time was recorded and kept to an absolute minimum (<2 minutes).Group II – With Prior Pulpectomy (Delayed Protocol): Upon extraction, the donor tooth was transferred to a sterile field. An extra-alveolar pulpectomy was immediately performed. The root surface was continuously irrigated with sterile saline-soaked gauze throughout the procedure to prevent desiccation. The tooth was handled exclusively by the crown using cotton pliers or by gripping the enamel surfaces with a sterile gauze pad. Direct contact with the root surface was strictly avoided. The pulp chamber was accessed from the occlusal surface using a high-speed handpiece. Pulp tissue was extirpated, and the canal was instrumented to a size #40 K-file. The canal was then irrigated with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite, dried with paper points, and obturated with a non-setting calcium hydroxide paste (Metapex®) as an interim medicament. The access cavity was sealed with a temporary restorative material (Cavit™). The total extra-alveolar time for this group, including pulpectomy, was recorded and maintained at ≤20 minutes. During this period, the root surface was kept moist with sterile saline.

Placement and stabilization

The donor tooth was placed in the recipient site and stabilized in an infraocclusion position to prevent occlusal forces from interfering with the healing process. Stabilization was achieved using flexible splinting which is a stabilizing device that secures the transplanted tooth to adjacent teeth while permitting controlled physiological micromotion

Prescribed medications included amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (Amoxam® 875/125 mg) twice daily for 7 days and 600 mg ibuprofen twice daily for 5 days. Oral hygiene was managed with 0.12% chlorhexidine solution applied three times daily until suture removal. Follow-up and Outcome Assessment:

Regular clinical and radiographic evaluations were performed at intervals such as 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months to monitor healing and detect complications. The primary outcome analysis for this study was based on a minimum follow-up period of 5 years (60 months). While some patients have been followed beyond this period (up to 6 years), the 5-year time point was selected as the definitive endpoint for comparing the two surgical protocols. No patients were lost to follow-up during this 5-year period. All radiographic assessments (including evaluation of root resorption, periapical pathology, and periodontal healing) were performed by an independent, blinded radiologist who was not involved in the surgical procedures and had no access to patient group allocation. Clinical assessments of tooth mobility were conducted by a second blinded examiner. This approach minimized potential bias in outcome interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis, with a supplementary per-protocol analysis conducted to verify robustness. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) depending on normality, which was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline comparisons between groups were performed using the independent-sample t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell frequencies were <5.

The primary outcome was 5-year tooth survival. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed, and inter-group differences were evaluated with the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for potential confounders (age, sex, extra-alveolar time, and root development stage). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

Secondary outcomes (tooth mobility, root resorption, and periapical pathology) were analyzed as binary or ordinal variables. Between-group comparisons at each time point employed the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression models provided adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. For outcomes assessed longitudinally at multiple time intervals, generalized estimating equations (GEE) and mixed-effects models were applied to account for within-subject correlations and time-by-treatment interactions. Model fit and assumptions were verified prior to interpretation.

Missing data were minimal (<5%) and handled by complete-case analysis. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) for all tests. Effect sizes (HRs, ORs, and mean differences) were reported with corresponding 95% CIs. Graphical survival and outcome trends were generated using SPSS and GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Study population

Forty patients (22 male, 18 female) with autotransplanted lower third molars were included and completed follow-up to 60 months. The mean age was 23.57 ± 4.64 years. Patients were randomized into two equal groups: conventional autotransplantation group (n = 20) and immediate pulpectomy group (n = 20).

Survival

Over the 60-month follow-up, 37 of 40 teeth (92.5%) survived. Group-specific 5-year survival rates were 95% (19/20) in the conventional autotransplantation group and 90% (18/20) in the Pulpectomy group. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for each group; comparison by the log-rank test indicated no statistically significant difference in survival between the two treatment arms (log-rank test, χ2 = 0.36, P = 0.55) (Fig. 13). Kaplan–Meier survival curves for autotransplanted teeth over a 5-year period.

Early postoperative mobility

Tooth mobility within the first 4 weeks postoperatively was observed in 3 of 40 cases (7.5%). Of these three cases, two occurred in teeth that had undergone pulpectomy before autotransplantation and one occurred in the conventional autotransplantation group. Mobility resolved in the majority of cases with conservative management; no mobility event led directly to tooth loss during the study period. Between-group comparison of early mobility showed no statistically significant difference (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.61).

External root resorption

External root resorption was identified radiographically in 6 of 40 teeth (15%). The distribution between groups was equal: three cases in the conventional autotransplantation group and three cases in the Pulpectomy group. None of these resorption cases progressed to clinical failure during the 5-year follow-up. Comparison of resorption frequency between groups found no significant difference (Fisher’s exact test, P = 1.00).

Periapical pathology

Periapical radiolucency suggestive of pulpal or periapical infection was observed in 4 of 40 teeth (10%) at some point during follow-up; all four cases occurred in the conventional autotransplantation group (4/20, 20%), while none were recorded in the Pulpectomy group (0/20, 0%). This difference produced a higher absolute rate of radiographic periapical pathology in the conventional autotransplantation arm; comparison using Fisher’s exact test indicated this difference did not reach statistical significance at the α = 0.05 level (P = 0.11 > 0.05). All affected cases were managed with appropriate endodontic intervention or monitoring; no systemic complications were reported. Complications and adverse events

Minor postoperative complications (transient swelling, discomfort) were infrequent and similarly distributed between groups. There were no reports of systemic adverse events.

Discussion

The principal findings of this 5-year follow-up study indicate that both conventional autotransplantation and pulpectomy prior to autotransplantation are effective protocols for the autotransplantation of mandibular third molars. While the conventional autotransplantation group showed a marginally higher survival rate (95% vs. 90%), this difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding key secondary outcomes, including tooth mobility, external root resorption, and the development of periapical pathology.

The overall 5-year survival rate of 92.5% (37/40) observed in this study strongly reinforces the existing body of literature that positions autotransplantation as a clinically reliable and biologically sound treatment modality. This high success rate aligns with findings from Yang et al and others, confirming that when performed under controlled conditions, autotransplantation can achieve predictable long-term outcomes. It substantiates the claim, that this technique is a clinically effective and economically feasible alternative to prosthetic and implant-based solutions, particularly in young patients where it offers the unique advantage of preserving the alveolar process and potential for bone induction[18].

A central finding of this study is that the ultimate success was largely independent of the pulpal management protocol. This underscores a critical principle reiterated throughout the literature: the viability of the PDL is the most crucial determinant of success[27].

By strictly limiting the extra-alveolar time to less than 20 minutes and employing atraumatic extraction techniques, PDL damage was minimized successfully. This is reflected in the low and comparable rates of root resorption and ankylosis (of which there were no cases) across both groups. The excellent survival rates, therefore, can be primarily attributed to this meticulous surgical handling, which preserved the capacity for PDL healing and regeneration[28].

The conventional autotransplantation protocol, by minimizing extra-oral time, favored PDL survival – yielding a numerically higher survival rate – but at the cost of a predictable risk for pulp necrosis, as evidenced by all periapical radiolucencies occurring in this group. Conversely, the pulpectomy approach proactively eliminated this pulpal risk but required longer extra-oral time, which was associated with a marginally higher incidence of root resorption, reflecting the inherent trade-off between securing endodontic health and optimizing conditions for PDL healing[29].

Ultimately, the two protocols present a balanced trade-off. Conventional autotransplantation optimizes periodontal outcomes by minimizing extra-oral time but accepts the likelihood of subsequent pulp necrosis and endodontic therapy. Conversely, pre-reimplantation pulpectomy eliminates pulpal risk but introduces a slightly higher periodontal challenge due to extended operative time. Given their statistically equivalent survival, the decision can be individualized based on surgical complexity and clinical judgment.

Despite its robust design, this study has several limitations. The modest sample size may have limited the power to detect small but clinically significant differences between the treatment protocols.

Furthermore, the external validity of the findings is constrained by the strict inclusion criteria. The study population was limited to healthy, non-smoking individuals with excellent oral hygiene (oral hygiene index >80%). As such, the high success rates observed (92.5% survival) may not be generalizable to patients with compromised systemic health (e.g., diabetes), tobacco use, or suboptimal oral hygiene which are factors known to impair periodontal healing and increase the risk of postoperative complications.

Additionally, the manual card-based randomization method, while practical, may not provide the same level of allocation concealment as computer-generated randomization systems, introducing a potential for selection bias.

The exceptional outcomes are also likely indicative of a high level of surgical expertise, highlighting the procedure’s operator-dependent nature. Finally, while the 5-year follow-up is substantial, even longer-term monitoring would be valuable to assess the incidence of very late complications such as progressive root resorption or ankylosis.

Conclusion

This 5-year randomized clinical trial demonstrates that autotransplantation of mandibular third molars is a highly predictable and successful procedure for tooth replacement, with an overall survival rate of 92.5%. The study found no statistically significant difference in mid-term survival or health between teeth transplanted immediately and those that underwent pulpectomy prior to autotransplantation. This indicates that the critical factor for success is not the pulpal management protocol per se, but the meticulous surgical technique that preserves PDL viability through atraumatic extraction and minimized extra-alveolar time.

The two protocols present a strategic trade-off: immediate autotransplantation prioritizes PDL health but carries a foreseeable risk of pulp necrosis requiring endodontic therapy, while performing pulpectomy prior to autotransplantation eliminates pulpal risk at the cost of slightly longer operative time. Therefore, the choice of technique can be individualized based on clinical circumstances, surgical complexity, and operator experience. Autotransplantation, performed with careful attention to PDL preservation, stands as a robust biological alternative to dental implants, particularly in young patients.
