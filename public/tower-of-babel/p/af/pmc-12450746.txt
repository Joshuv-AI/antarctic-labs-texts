Ultrasonic-assisted separation and extraction of submicron and nano-sized straw particles: Reconstruction of physicochemical characteristics and a promising breakthrough in overcoming technical bottlenecks of straw return to the field
Authors: Xuanming Wang, Xu Su, Yanqi Li, Yu Guan, Bingchun Yan, Yuqi Liu, Jiping Gao, Qi Wu, Wenzhong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Ultrasound treatment, Straw nanoization, Microstructure, Functional nano-fertilizer, Straw humification

Introduction

Crop straw, as one of the largest renewable biomass resources globally, holds strategic significance for sustainable agricultural development [1]. As the world's largest rice producer, China generates over 220 million tons of rice straw annually [2]. However, traditional disposal methods face dual challenges: despite utilization approaches such as incineration for power generation [3,4], solid fuel production, and anaerobic digestion for biogas [5], approximately 50 % of the straw is still disposed of in a rudimentary manner, causing severe environmental pollution. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop efficient and environmentally friendly straw recycling techniques for returning to field.

Current conventional technologies for the resource utilization of straw via field return primarily include composting, biochar production, and straw crushing for direct returning [6]. Biochar (achieved through thermochemical conversion) and composting (achieved through biological pretreatment) provide valuable alternative pathways for straw treatment, yet their principles and products are fundamentally diverse: Biochar emphasizes chemical modification to achieve long-term carbon sequestration, yet faces challenges such as high costs and limited comprehensive improvement effects [[7], [8], [9]]; composting relies on the external environment for biological predigestion, and its efficiency is readily constrained by factors like the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio [10,11]. While straw crushing returning to the field boasts advantages of operational simplicity, low cost, and effective supplementation of soil organic matter, incomplete physical fragmentation induces soil carbon-to-nitrogen ratio imbalance [12,13] and nutrient competition effects [14]. The root of this contradiction lies in the fact that traditional straw shredding technology has long been trapped in an empirically optimized technological loop, failing to break through the physical barriers of the lignocellulosic complex [15], thus impeding the decomposition process of straw.

Current straw utilization technologies are confronted with three key scientific bottlenecks: (I) The physical limits of mechanical grinding processes: Although smaller particle sizes accelerate the initial decomposition stage, they have no significant impact on the mid-term decomposition process, confirming that traditional particle size control methods are fundamentally constrained, indicating inherent limitations in traditional particle size control [16,17]; (II) The absence of structure–function correlation mechanisms: the quantitative relationship between fragmentation scale and nutrient release has not been established, especially the regulatory mechanisms of lignocellulose degradation at the micro- and nano-scales remain unexplored; (III) Insufficient innovation in technological pathways: over a century of agricultural machinery development, straw fragmentation technology has lacked interdisciplinary breakthroughs and has failed to effectively integrate advanced concepts from fields such as materials science. To address the aforementioned bottlenecks, it is imperative to pursue breakthroughs in interdisciplinary technologies. In recent years, ultrasonic cavitation technology has achieved significant advancements in material fragmentation, successfully preparing micro/nano materials with specific surface areas increased by 2–3 orders of magnitude. These materials possess abundant active sites, capable of significantly altering their physicochemical properties, thereby endowing them with entirely new performance characteristics [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Concurrently, existing research indicates that ultrasonic technology can convert agricultural waste streams into value-added products through pretreatment and valorization processes, yielding numerous beneficial effects [22]. Based on this, this study proposes an innovative approach—ultrasound-assisted centrifugal separation technology. Compared to the thermochemical conversion of biochar and biological pretreatment of composting technologies, its core lies in directly deconstructing the physical resistance barriers of lignocellulosic complexes through ultrasonic cavitation physics. This leads to a disruptive hypothesis: achieving submicron/nanoscale engineered fragmentation of straw via ultrasound technology holds promise for breaking through the performance bottlenecks of traditional crushing techniques.

Recognizing the critical knowledge gap concerning the graded fragmentation of crop straw, this study innovatively employs ultrasound-assisted centrifugal separation technology to establish an investigative scope encompassing micron, submicron, and nano straw particles, aiming to reveal the size-dependent properties of crop residues under ultrasonic treatment. The core approach leverages ultrasonic cavitation for precise fragmentation: We first optimized key ultrasonic cavitation parameters (e.g., power, time, frequency) using Response Surface Methodology (central composite design), successfully preparing submicron and nano straw particles. These particles were then systematically characterized and quantitatively analyzed, with a focus on elucidating the relationships between particle size and changes in hydrophilicity, functional group abundance, as well as nutrient element speciation. Furthermore, to validate field application potential, soil column leaching experiments were designed to quantify the water and fertilizer retention capacities of straw particles across different size fractions. Concurrently, pot experiments revealed the unique nutrient release behavior—within the soil–plant system—of the submicron and nano particles prepared via ultrasonic cavitation, along with their significant promotion of rice growth. This research not only provides a novel theoretical paradigm for straw return technology but also pioneers a micro-nano technology approach for the high-value utilization of agricultural waste by deeply exploring the potential of ultrasonic cavitation technology.

Methods

Preparation of micron/submicron/nano-particle straw

The raw materials used in this experiment came from the stalks remaining after the rice harvest. Since larger particles require higher ultrasonic energy and longer processing times for effective fragmentation [23], the collected straw was pulverized and sieved through a 120-mesh sieve to enhance nano straw preparation efficiency. This process yielded uniform and controllable micron straw particles with particle sizes below 125 μ m for subsequent analysis and experiments. The particle size obtained via 120-mesh sieving guarantees that ultrasonic energy acts efficiently on particle fragmentation [24]. Initially, these particles were transferred into 500-mL beakers containing deionized (DI) water, followed by stirring for 1 min to ensure even distribution and suspension within the liquid. To obtain more nano straw, we conducted ultrasonic crushing of the straw and optimized the ultrasonic parameters, selecting the best preparation conditions. Details can be found in the Design of experiments section. Afterward, the straw solution was sonicated at 90.0 % power for 3 h. This technology facilitated the breakdown of larger particles by generating high-frequency sound waves, causing intense agitation and collision between the particles, effectively fracturing them into finer pieces. After ultrasonic treatment, the suspensions were allowed to settle quiescently for 24 h for particle size < 1 μm according to Strokes Law, then the solid and liquid were separated by siphonage, and the liquid suspension was named submicron particle straw (SMP) suspensions as Fig. 2E [25]. A portion of the SMP suspension was centrifuged at 3500 × g for 30 min to obtain a supernatant with particle sizes less than 100 nm [26], which was designated as nano particle straw (NP) suspensions (Fig. 2E). All SMP and NP suspensions were freezing-dried to obtain SMP straw and NP straw powders and then stored in a desiccator (Fig. 2G). The detailed preparation processes of micron particle straw (MP), SMP, and NP straw are shown in Figs. S1–S3.

Design of experiments

Fig. 1A illustrates the factors that may affect the size of the straw through ultrasonic crushing. Among these factors, the machine setup, including the type and capacity of the probe and beaker, were fixed, and all the experiments were performed at room temperature. Ultrasonic crushing was carried out using an ultrasonic dispersing crusher (GZ-2000F; Ningbo Xinzhi) with a 500 mL beaker and a special probe designed for the ultrasonic dispersing crusher. The rated power of ultrasound is 2000 W. The time intervals were set to 3 s ON and 3 s OFF; these values were obtained in preliminary experiments to prevent overheating of the straw and probe [27]. To optimize the milling conditions to reduce the size of the straw particles to the lowest possible value, the remaining parameters, including ultrasonic time, sample weight, and ultrasonic power, were manipulated in different experiments.

MP, SMP, and NP straw characterization

The obtained submicron-, and nano-scale rice straw was freeze-dried. The submicron- and nano-scale straw samples prepared using the optimal process parameters (3 h, 30 g, 90 %) exhibited the highest dry weight yield. Subsequently, these samples underwent a series of morphological observations and physicochemical property characterizations. Specific characterization methods are provided in the Supplementary Material (Note S1).

Soil column leaching experiment

Soil samples were collected from the 0–20 cm surface layer of an experimental field at Shenyang Agricultural University, Shenyang, Liaoning Province, China (41°82′63″ N, 123°57′18″ E), using a five-point sampling method. After air-drying, the samples were cleared of crop residues and debris, sieved through a 2 mm mesh, and stored for subsequent analysis. The soil was classified as a silty loam, with the following basic physicochemical properties: NH4+-N content of 37.22 mg/kg, NO3−-N content of 2.32 mg/kg, total nitrogen content of 1.38 g/kg, total carbon content of 16.90 g/kg, and available potassium content of 24.7 mg/kg.

This study systematically evaluated the effects of micron, submicron, and nano straw particles on the retention and slow-release characteristics of soil nutrients such as carbon, nitrogen, and potassium through soil column leaching experiments. Soil permeability was measured using a one-dimensional vertical water infiltration method, with the infiltration apparatus consisting of a Markov bottle and a cylindrical polyethylene tube (water head height of 2 cm). During the experiment, the water level in the Markov bottle and the vertical wetting depth were recorded by dynamically adjusting the observation frequency (frequent in the initial stage and sparse in the later stage) until the wetting front reached the bottom of the soil column, at which point water supply was stopped.

The leaching apparatus comprised a cylindrical polyethylene tube with an inner diameter of 10 cm and a height of 30 cm, equipped with a 2 mm diameter outlet at the bottom. The soil column was filled from bottom to top as follows: a 100-mesh nylon net, 3 cm of quartz sand (pretreated with 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid and neutralized with deionized water), 15 cm of subsoil (without straw addition), 5 cm of topsoil (with straw addition), and 3 cm of quartz sand. The quartz sand layers ensured uniform water distribution. To prevent disturbance of the topsoil during leaching, the top of the soil column was covered with filter paper [29].

This experiment employed a completely randomized single-factor design with four treatments: micron straw particles, submicron straw particles, nano straw particles, and a no-straw control. Each treatment was replicated three times, resulting in a total of twelve soil columns. The straw addition rate was 1 kg/m2 (equivalent to 7.85 g per column), a level commonly used in studies on straw return to fields, providing practical guidance for agricultural practices [[30], [31], [32]].

To quantitatively characterize the influence mechanisms of straw particles of different sizes (micron, submicron, and nano) on soil water infiltration processes, this study employed the Philip and Kostiakov models to simulate the infiltration dynamics (Note S2). The infiltration kinetic parameters for each treatment were systematically analyzed [33].

After filling the soil columns, pre-leaching was conducted to promote the integration of the straw-soil system. This involved the daily application of 78.5 mL of distilled water (equivalent to 10 mm of rainfall) until a stable outflow was observed at the bottom of column [34]. Following this, the formal experiment commenced, with leachate samples collected at 24-hour intervals over 12 complete leaching events. Post-experiment, the following parameters were systematically measured: (1) leachate volume and concentrations of NH4+-N, NO3−-N, and total potassium; and (2) NH4+-N, NO3−-N, total carbon, total nitrogen, and available potassium contents in the top and bottom soils of the columns. Detailed measurement methods are provided in Note S3.

Pot experiment on rice

To systematically evaluate the impact mechanisms of nano straw on rice growth characteristics and soil carbon, nitrogen, and potassium dynamics, this study employed a completely randomized single-factor design. Four treatments were established: micron straw particles, submicron straw particles, nano straw particles, and a no-straw-amendment control. Each treatment was replicated three times, requiring a total of twelve pots. All pots were arranged in a completely randomized manner within the experimental space to eliminate interference from environmental gradients on the results. The rice variety “Shennong 9816″ was used, and the straw application rate was uniformly set at 1 kg/m2. The basic physicochemical properties of the experimental soil were as follows: NH4+-N content of 28.62 mg/kg, NO3−-N content of 2.25 mg/kg, total nitrogen content of 0.70 g/kg, total carbon content of 7.16 g/kg, and available potassium content of 7.20 mg/kg. The fertilization scheme is detailed in Note S4. During the experiment, the following parameters were monitored: (1) rice growth parameters: tillering dynamics and final yield; and (2) changes in NH4+-N, NO3−-N, total carbon, total nitrogen, and available potassium contents at different growth stages. All measurements were conducted using standardized methods, with detailed procedures provided in Note S3.

Statistical analyses

Minitab statistical software was used for the design of the ultrasonic experiments and statistical analysis. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the significance of differences in the average straw particle size among different treatments, as well as the significance of soil nutrient content and rice yield in both the pot and column experiments. When the ANOVA results indicated significant differences (p < 0.05), Tukey’s HSD test was further used for multiple comparisons (significance level fixed at 0.05).

Results

Ultrasonic-assisted separation and extraction of SMP and NP straw and their yields

The yields of both submicron straw and nano straw were calculated based on the dry weight after freeze-drying. The yield of nano straw demonstrates that ultrasonic crushing is an effective method for producing fine straw particles. Under different ultrasonic conditions, the yield of submicron straw is 10.0 % to 21.0 %, and the yield of nano straw is 4.7 % to 8.0 %. Since experiment 8 (3 h, 30 g, and 90 %) yielded the highest nano straw yield (Table 2), the straw samples obtained from experiment 8 were selected for analysis and characterization in this study.

The effects of the independent variables (period of time, weight of straw, and power of ultrasonic) were analyzed according to the polynomial model. The standardized impacts of these variables are illustrated in Fig. 1B. Accordingly, the period of time, weight of straw, and power of ultrasonic significantly influence the yield of nano straw. The linear effect estimate of ultrasonic power was positive (P = 0.000004), and its quadratic effect estimate also was positive (P = 0.000769). The two-dimensional response surfaces, based on the regression model provided in Eq. (1), are plotted in Fig. 1C–E. In general, when the ultrasonic intensity of a liquid increases, the cavitation intensity increases, thus leading to a greater sonochemical effect in the presence of more bursting bubbles with faster pulsation and collapse [35]. This can increase straw shredding, which is beneficial for the production of more submicron and nano straw. Additionally, the interaction effect between ultrasonic power and straw weight was found to be significant (P = 0.012327). Therefore, the influence of straw weight on the yield of nano straw is likely modulated by the level of ultrasonic power.

Extracted SMP and NP straw suspensions and their yield are shown in Figs. 1F and S4, respectively. The SMP suspensions looked dark-brown, while the NP suspensions had light-brown colors. Overall, the ultrasound treatment produces more submicron particles, accounting for 17 % of the total, hence the darker color. In contrast, the yield of nano straw particles is lower, at 8 % of the total, resulting in a lighter color.

The size distribution indicates that SMP and NP straw particles were successfully separated from MP straw. The hydrodynamic diameter of MP straw particles is primarily below 10000 nm, ranging from 775 to 10000 nm. SMP straw particles are generally less than 1000 nm, ranging from 77.4 to 1000 nm. The size distribution of SMP straw particles is uneven, with a main peak diameter of 100 to 147 nm and a secondary peak diameter of 167 to 278 nm. This irregularity in size distribution suggests the presence of competing fragmentation pathways—direct cellulose microfibril exfoliation and the formation of lignin-carbohydrate complex clusters. The NP straw particles are mainly less than 100 nm (Fig. 2G). Both nano and micro straw particles exhibit an unimodal distribution with relatively broad symmetry.

Morphology observation

Fig. 2A–C presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of straw samples under different treatment conditions. Fig. 2A1 and A2 depict the original microstructure of rice straw, revealing its fully porous framework structure. However, in Fig. 2B1 and B2, a noticeable reduction in the porous structure of the rice straw can be observed, along with the emergence of a smooth-surfaced, lamellar morphology. This morphological characteristic suggests that lignin may have lost its structural supporting function for the cellular framework. This observation is consistent with the lignin content measurement data obtained in this study, further confirming the effective removal of lignin. When zooming in on Fig. 2B1, it is observed that many small particles are gathered on the straw's surface, indicating the aggregation phenomenon of submicron straw particles. In Fig. 2C1 and C2, this study found that the surface of nano straw became smoother and had fewer pores than submicron straw. In Fig. 2C2, the aggregation phenomenon of nano straw particles is also confirmed.

Characterization by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2D1 and 2D2) reveals that nano straw particles primarily exhibit a spherical morphology when observed at scales of 200 nm and 100 nm. This structural change may originate from ultrasonication-induced reorganization, where intense cavitation energy overcomes the elastic barrier of the natural fiber structure. Notably, these nanoparticles displayed a pronounced tendency to agglomerate. This observation suggests that as particle size decreases, the surface properties and aggregation behavior of straw particles undergo significant changes [36], likely attributable to increased surface energy and enhanced interparticle interactions. Shrestha et al. [37] indicate that as the size decreases, surface free energy becomes significant, and particles reduce the system's total energy by decreasing the external surface area, increasing the interparticle attraction, which causes agglomeration. Additionally, interparticle interactions such as Van der Waals forces may further promote the aggregation of straw particles [38]. These findings provide important theoretical support for understanding the aggregation behavior of nano straw particles.

Characterization of the physical properties of straw

In this study, EDS analysis was employed to reveal the disruptive elemental reconstruction patterns triggered by straw nanosizing (Fig. 2E-G). Innovatively, it was found that when the straw particle size is reduced to the nanoscale (<100 nm), the elemental existence forms undergo fundamental changes: (1) The nitrogen content of nano straw was significantly increased compared with that of micron and submicron straw, this phenomenon can be attributed to the ultrasonic cavitation process, which facilitates the detachment of non-nitrogenous components such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin from the straw matrix. Furthermore, the disruption of cellular structures releases intracellular proteins and amino acids, thereby augmenting the overall nitrogen content; (2) XPS depth analysis (Fig. 3D-F) confirms that nitrogen exists in the form of plant-directly available amino groups (−NH2, 399.9 eV) and protonated amino groups (−NH3+, 401.7 eV); (3) Potassium simultaneously achieves surface enrichment at the nanoscale, with the potassium content in submicron/nano straw increasing by 719.35 % and 750.53 % compared to microscale (p < 0.001), respectively, indicating that straw particles smaller than 1000 nm can directly participate in plant enzyme activity regulation and water balance [39,40]. This finding overturns the traditional straw incorporation nutrient release pattern and first confirms that nano straw can be directly used as a fast-acting fertilizer. By controlling the milling scale, a paradigm shift from “slow organic matter mineralization” to “direct nutrient supply by nanoparticles” has been achieved, opening up a new pathway for straw resource utilization.

Elemental analysis results show that using ultrasonic cavitation technology to reduce straw particle size to below 1,000 nm can significantly enrich carbon and nitrogen nutrients, with carbon content increasing by 3.48 %-5.30 % and nitrogen content increasing by 136.52 %-193.60 %. Meanwhile, the O/C and C/N ratios are optimized, with average reductions of 10.71 % and 60.15 %, respectively (Fig. 2H). This indicates that straw particles smaller than 1,000 nm can rapidly decompose when applied to the soil, thereby enhancing soil carbon sequestration capacity and nitrogen availability. More importantly, the nanosizing process significantly enhances the hydrophilicity of straw, with its water-holding capacity increasing by 18.52 % compared to micron straw (Fig. 2I). This phenomenon is attributed to the surface chemical restructuring induced by ultrasonic cavitation: high-intensity ultrasonic cavitation can break down lignin-carbohydrate complexes, exposing a large number of hydroxyl (−OH) and carboxyl (−COOH) groups (Fig. 3K), thereby significantly enhancing the water-affinity capacity of the particle surface [41,42]. Furthermore, BET analysis (Fig. 2J) further reveals the unique structural characteristics of nano straw. Its specific surface area decreased due to particle aggregation (from 3.13 m2/g to 2.58 m2/g), while the average adsorption pore size decreased from 8.73 nm to 5.73 nm. The smaller pore size can generate stronger capillary forces, further enhancing its water-holding capacity. This synergistic effect of structure and function provides a revolutionary agricultural water and fertilizer management solution. The enhanced water-holding capacity can effectively improve soil moisture conditions, thereby reducing irrigation requirements, and holds significant application prospects and practical value.

In this study, ultrasonic cavitation fragmentation technology was employed to elucidate the differential response mechanisms of lignocellulosic components during biomass fractionation. Fig. 2K–M illustrates the determination of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose content in micron, submicron, and nano straw particles. The contents of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose progressively decreased as the particle size of the rice straw was reduced. This reduction can be attributed to ultrasound-induced facilitation of cellulose and lignin breakage and dispersion, leading to decreased fiber diameters and degradation through physical effects such as shear forces, microbubble collapse, and chemical effects like free radical generation [43]. For instance, Qian et al. [44] reported that Chinese fir treated with ultrasound at 240 W and 28 kHz exhibited a 9.55 % reduction in cellulose content compared to the control group. Additionally, the precipitation and centrifugation processes may have contributed to removing fragmented lignin and cellulose deposits. Consequently, the lignin and cellulose contents in submicron straw and nano straw particles did not reach significant levels.

It was found that hemicellulose, due to its unique physicochemical properties, exhibits distinct behaviors from cellulose and lignin in the ultrasonic treatment system: (1) Hemicellulose has a relatively low molecular weight (degree of polymerization 100–200), and its amorphous structure results in significantly weaker thermodynamic stability compared to crystalline cellulose [45]; (2) The smaller molecular weight endows it with a unique colloidal behavior tendency, making it more likely to form a stable dispersion system under ultrasonic cavitation. By comparing the hemicellulose content in micron-sized and submicron-sized straw, it can be inferred that ultrasonic milling of straw has a relatively good effect on the milling of hemicellulose. Notably, the synergistic action of ultrasonic cavitation fragmentation and gradient precipitation demonstrates specific removal effects on submicron/nano lignocellulosic components: the removal efficiency of lignin reached 77.46 % and 76.91 %, respectively, while the degradation rate of cellulose reached 86.27 % and 93.43 %, respectively.

Characterization of the chemical properties of straw

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis reveals the key chemical bond transformations during the nanosizing process of straw (Table S1). For the C1s peak (Fig. 3A–C), the characteristic peak intensity of the C–N/C–O bond at 286.5 eV significantly increases with decreasing particle size, with its relative proportion rising from 20.3 % in microscale straw to 41.4 % in nano straw [46]. This change indicates that ultrasonic cavitation induces a fertilization trend in straw by breaking C–C bonds in lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose while simultaneously promoting the formation of C–O and C–N bonds. This chemical bond reconstruction not only enhances the degradability of straw, increasing the number of degradation sites recognizable by microorganisms but also improves its hydrophilicity and ion exchange capacity, enhancing its water retention and fertilization value as a soil conditioner or natural fertilizer [47].

The N1s spectrum shows (Fig. 3D-F) that within the range of 399.90–400.07 eV, the N1s peak can be divided into two peaks, corresponding to free amino–NH2 at around 402.0 eV and protonated amino–NH3+. Comparing straw of different particle sizes, it is found that the binding energies of these two peaks are stable, and the proportions of –NH2 and –NH3+ are almost identical. The amino groups on the straw surface mainly exist in the form of free amino–NH2 [48], accounting for 80.9 %, 77.4 %, and 80.6 %, respectively, while protonated amino–NH3+ accounts for 19.1 %, 22.6 %, and 19.4 %. This indicates that the proportion of amino groups on the straw surface is chemically stable and unaffected by changes in particle size.

The carbonyl functional groups enriched on the surface of nanoparticles play a key role in straw returning to the field: their strong polarity promotes the formation of hydrogen-bond networks with soil water molecules, enhancing water retention capacity; they adsorb organic matter and mineral nutrients through van der Waals forces, slowing nutrient loss; and they act as active sites to accelerate oxidation reactions, promoting the decomposition and transformation of straw [50,51]. The XPS full-scan spectrum further indicates (Fig. 3J) that the N1s peak of submicron and nano straw is significantly stronger than that of micro straw, indicating a higher content of N–H groups as a whole. Meanwhile, the increase in oxygen-containing functional groups with decreasing particle size is consistent with the analysis results of the C1s, N1s, and O1s spectra.

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis (Fig. 3K) revealed that submicron/nano straw surfaces exhibit more diverse functional group characteristics compared to micron-scale counterparts (Tables S2 and S3). Notably, the absorption peak intensities at 3352–3378 cm−1 and 2918–2920 cm−1 were significantly enhanced in submicron/nano samples. These peaks correspond to O–H stretching vibrations and C–H stretching vibrations, respectively [52,53]. The study demonstrates that ultrasonic treatment generates hydroxyl radicals (O–H) through the cleavage of water molecules, inducing mild oxidation of organic components. This process amplifies existing functional group signals and introduces hydrophilic groups such as hydroxyl (O–H) and carboxyl (−COOH) moieties onto the material surface. This surface modification substantially improves the material's water retention capacity, rendering it highly suitable for agricultural applications where enhanced soil moisture retention is critical [54].

In micron straw, the characteristic shoulder peak at 1732 cm−1, which is associated with the ester groups of lignin, disappears at the submicron and nanoscale [55]. This finding reveals for the first time that a physical threshold for lignin degradation exists at the submicron scale. This indicates that when straw particle size decreases to sub-micrometer levels, the conjugated system of lignin macromolecules undergoes deconstruction, and the cleavage of ester bonds may arise from the molecular chain breakage caused by mechanochemical effects.

This indicates that nano straw possesses a stronger capacity for adsorbing water, which may contribute to improving soil water retention [56]. As the particle size decreases from the micron to the nano, the vibration peaks of C–H bonds (1407–1416 cm−1) in submicron and nano straw are enhanced, and new bending vibration peaks appear (781–779 cm−1 and 617 cm−1). This suggests that the surface chemical reactivity is significantly enhanced with the reduction in particle size [57]. Such increased reactivity may facilitate the decomposition of straw in soil and the release of nutrients. New vibration peaks (such as 1323 cm−1 and 617 cm−1) emerge in submicron and nano straw, indicating that the reduction in particle size leads to the diversification and functionalization of the straw's surface chemical structure. This structural diversity may offer new possibilities for applying straw in agriculture and the environment.

Moreover, with the decrease in particle size, the peaks of O–H and C–O bonds shift to higher wavenumbers due to the weakening of conjugation effects. For instance, the peak value of the O–H bond in micron-scale straw is 3352 cm−1, 3378 cm−1 in submicron straw, and 3370 cm−1 in nano straw. Meanwhile, the peak of the C–O bond in micron-scale straw, originally at 1043 cm−1, shifts to 1078 cm−1 in submicron-scale straw. These observations imply that the number of conjugated phenyl rings within submicron and nano straw decreases [50,51]. The reduction in conjugated phenyl rings may indicate the depolymerization of macromolecules, such as lignin, leading to changes in the conjugated system. The alteration of the conjugated system directly determines the emergence of new chemical properties in straw.

The mineral composition of a micron, submicron, and nano straw was characterized using the XRD method (Fig. 3L). Cellulose consists of crystalline and non-crystalline regions, whereas hemicellulose and lignin are amorphous [45]. The crystallinity of cellulose is a key factor in determining the physical properties of straw. The original straw showed two diffraction peaks at 2θ = 15.1° and 2θ = 22.0°, corresponding to the overlapping (1–10)/(110) lattice planes and (200) lattice planes of cellulose I [44,58]. It can be observed that after ultrasonication, precipitation, and centrifugation, the characteristic peaks of cellulose I at (1–10)/(1 1 0) lattice planes and (200) lattice planes in submicron and micron straw became significantly flat. These results indicate a reduction in the cellulose content of submicron and micron straw. Simultaneously, the crystallinity index (CrI) of micron-sized straw is 32.3 %, while that of submicron and nano straw is 30.4 % and 26.8 %, respectively. This significant decrease in crystallinity indicates that, as the particle size decreases, the crystalline structure of cellulose is disrupted, leading to an amorphous nature of the material. This structural change may enhance the biodegradability and environmental reactivity of straw. The ultrasonic cavitation effect disrupts plant cell walls and membranes, facilitating the release of inorganic salts (such as KCl) from the cells. This process explains the enrichment of minerals in submicron and nano straw and provides theoretical support for the physical pretreatment of straw in resource utilization.

The mineral composition of straw is primarily comprised of SiO2. Several new sharp peaks were observed in submicron and nano straw samples, attributed to the enrichment of inherent minerals [59]. The peak signals of KCl in submicron and nano straw are enhanced, which is consistent with the results of the EDS analysis. Meanwhile, the aggregation of a large number of KCl particles can also be observed in SEM (Fig. S6). This suggests that most minerals are concentrated in submicron and nano straw during ultrasonication, precipitation, and centrifugation processes [51]. The physical effects of ultrasonic cavitation can disrupt plant cell walls and membranes, facilitating the release of inorganic salts from the cells [27]. This suggests that potassium availability is significantly limited during straw decomposition in particles larger than one micron, whereas it is markedly enhanced in particles smaller than 1000 nm. These findings corroborate the trend observed in EDS analysis, indicating a pronounced tendency for submicron and nano straw particles to undergo fertilizer conversion.

The differences in thermal stability among micron, submicron, and nano straw were analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis (Fig. 3M, N). These materials demonstrated varied thermal transitions, dependent on their chemical compositions. Between 60 °C and 160 °C, the weight of the straw decreased due to the loss of bound water [60]. In micron straw, hemicellulose and cellulose composed of various randomly arranged amorphous structures of sugars, glucose, and glycosidic bonds, along with a small fraction of lignin, initially decompose at a temperature range from 160 °C to 330 °C. Above 330 °C, lignin and other phenolic compounds gradually decompose, with no significant change in mass loss [61,62]. In contrast, submicron and nano straw began to decompose rapidly around 160 °C (Fig. 3N), suggesting that only some cellulose, hemicellulose, and a minor amount of lignin remain within these materials, leading to poorer thermal stability. In terms of thermal stability, the study found that submicron straw particles exhibited an accelerated decomposition rate at 330 °C, indicating that their lignin content is slightly higher than that of nano straw but significantly lower than that of micron straw. This finding reveals that straw particles smaller than 1,000 nm, with lower lignin content, are more easily decomposed in the soil, providing a scientific basis for the application of nano straw as a fast-acting fertilizer.

The molecular weight distribution profiles of micron, submicron, and nano straw particles were characterized using gel permeation chromatography (GPC), with representative chromatograms presented in Fig. 3O and Fig. S5. Analysis revealed distinct molecular weight distributions within the 100–3000 Da range, with micron-sized particles predominantly distributed around 460 Da, submicron particles centered at 390 Da, and nanoparticles primarily distributed at 330 Da. This progressive shift toward lower molecular weights with decreasing particle size suggests substantial macromolecular degradation processes. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the number-average molecular weight (MN) ranged from 391 to 482 Da, while the weight-average molecular weight (MW) varied between 629 and 677 Da across different particle sizes. Notably, micron-sized straw particles exhibited the highest molecular weight (MW = 677 Da), indicating greater structural stability compared to their submicron and nano counterparts. In contrast, nanoparticles showed significantly reduced molecular weight (MW = 629 Da) and stability, a finding corroborated by thermogravimetric analysis. This reduction in molecular weight is considered to enhance the bioavailability of the nano straw particles during microbial utilization and biomass conversion processes [63]. Integrating the TGA and GPC results, the reduction in particle size simultaneously decreased the thermal stability and molecular weight of the straw, and the decline in both is commonly recognized as a key prerequisite for accelerating biodegradation [[64], [65], [66]]. Due to their small spatial hindrance and high transmembrane transport efficiency, the low-molecular-weight components exhibit a higher microbial assimilation rate in composting and soil environments[67]. Therefore, the characteristic low molecular weight of the nano straw particles revealed in this study, combined with their reduced thermal stability, provides a solid scientific explanation from both the molecular level and the material stability perspective for their faster decomposition in soil, and strongly supports their application potential as a fast-acting fertilizer.

In this study, 3D-EEM fluorescence spectroscopy was used to unveil the humification evolution mechanism of straw particles during their ultrasonic nanosization (Fig. 3P-R). Generally, the (EX > 250, EM > 380) region in fluorescence spectra signifies fulvic and humic acids [68,69]. As straw particle size was reduced from the micrometer to the nanoscale, the fluorescence intensity in this region significantly increased, likely due to the high humification of submicron and nano straw [68]. The evolution process of functional characteristics of straw

Ultrasonic cavitation releases energy through the instantaneous collapse of high-energy microbubbles, disrupting the complex structures of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose (Fig. 4A-B). Fig. 4C illustrates the fragmentation mechanisms of these biopolymers alongside the physicochemical properties and elemental composition of straw at nano, submicron, and micron scales. Studies have shown that at the submicron to the nano level, the energy impact leads to the internal bond rupture and structural framework collapse of these biopolymers, decreasing their content. The breakdown of lignin exposes C–C and C–O functional groups, enhancing their degradability [70]; the destruction of cellulose and hemicellulose generates O–H and C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> O groups, increasing their hydrophilicity and cation exchange capacity, which promotes microbial growth and decomposition. Additionally, the degradation of proteins exposes N–H and C–N functional groups, increasing nitrogen content. Thermogravimetric analysis indicates that for straw particles smaller than 1000 nm, the thermal stability, molecular weight, and cellulose crystallinity are reduced, and the structural integrity is weakened. The cleavage of chemical bonds and rupture of cell walls also increase carbon, nitrogen, and potassium ratios, thereby enhancing nutritional value.

Notably, the physicochemical properties of straw remain largely consistent until reaching the submicron scale, underscoring the submicron threshold as a critical transition point. Below this threshold, straw particles exhibit three key trends: enhanced biodegradability, increased hydrophilicity, and a pronounced tendency toward fertilizer conversion. These transformative changes alter the physicochemical properties of straw and advance the technological potential for sustainable straw return practices in agriculture.

Soil column leaching experiment

Soil permeability was measured using a one-dimensional vertical water infiltration method, with the infiltration apparatus consisting of a Markov bottle and a cylindrical polyethylene tube (water head height of 2 cm) (Fig. 5A). Fig. 5B shows the changes in leachate volume across the 12 leaching events. Compared to the no-straw treatment, the addition of micron-, submicron-, and nano-straw reduced leachate volume by 15.24 %, 28.27 %, and 35.89 %, respectively. This indicates that submicron and nano straw exhibit superior water retention capacity compared to micron straw. This effect primarily arises from the interaction between nano/submicron straw particles and soil particles: Due to their extremely small size, nano straw and submicron straw particles effectively fill or modify the pores between soil particles (specifically increasing micropores and capillary pores). This alteration in pore structure significantly enhances the soil's capillary forces, thereby better retaining water within the pore spaces. Concurrently, the surface of the straw particles exhibits an increased number of hydrophilic functional groups (such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups). This enhances interactions between the particles and soil mineral surfaces (e.g., hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction) [71], and also strengthens the binding force between the particles and water molecules. These mechanisms enable the straw particles and soil matrix to synergistically enhance water retention capacity.

The one-dimensional vertical movement of the wetting front increased progressively with infiltration time (Fig. 5C). Compared to treatments without straw and with micron-sized straw, both submicron and nano straw treatments significantly extended the infiltration time required for the wetting front to reach any given depth. The wetting front movement curve slope for submicron and nano straw treatments was markedly lower than that for micron-sized straw treatments. Notably, nano straw increased the infiltration time by 150 % at an application rate of 1 kg/m2 compared to micron-sized straw.

Soil water cumulative infiltration, commonly used to characterize soil infiltration capacity before reaching steady-state infiltration (Fig. 5D), was lower in submicron and nano straw treatments at any given time than micron-sized straw treatments. However, at an application rate of 1 kg/m2, the cumulative infiltration volumes for micron, submicron, and nano straw treatments were 67.44 %, 65.12 %, and 70.93 % higher, respectively, than the treatment without straw. The coefficient of determination (R2) values for the Philip and Kostiakov models were > 0.99 and > 0.97, respectively (Fig. 5D, E, Table S4), indicating that these models effectively simulated the changes in soil infiltration rates under different straw application rates and gravel contents. In the Philip model, submicron and nano straw treatments increased the soil adsorption rate (“S”) compared to the no-straw treatment, while the steady infiltration rate (“A”) decreased relative to both no-straw and micron-sized straw treatments. In the Kostiakov model, the initial infiltration rate (“a”) and steady infiltration rate (“b”) were lower in nano straw treatments compared to the no-straw treatment.

The dynamic changes in total potassium, ammonium nitrogen, and nitrate nitrogen in the 12 leaching solutions are shown in Fig. 5F-H. The total potassium content in the nano straw treatment reached a peak of 14.90 mg/L during the fourth leaching event, significantly higher than other treatments (p < 0.05), and then rapidly decreased. The conventional and micro-straw treatments showed a relatively stable fluctuation trend. The initial ammonium nitrogen concentration in the leachate was highest (0.89 mg/L) for the treatment without straw application, indicating faster nitrogen loss when straw was not applied. In contrast, the initial ammonium nitrogen concentrations for the submicron straw and nano straw treatments were lower, and they continued to rise after the 4th leaching event, reaching their peaks at the 6th leaching event at 0.28 mg/L and 0.24 mg/L, respectively. Overall, the average ammonium nitrogen concentrations in the leachate for the submicron straw and nano straw treatments were significantly reduced by approximately 69.6 % and 76.2 %, respectively, compared to the treatment without straw application. For nitrate nitrogen, both treatments also exhibited a decreasing trend, although the reductions were smaller, at approximately 4.9 % and 3.5 %, respectively. These results collectively demonstrate that the submicron straw and nano straw treatments possess a stronger nitrogen retention capacity. This nitrogen retention effect may stem not only from the physical adsorption by straw particles (specifically, chemical adsorption involving functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) [72]. Microbial assimilation is equally crucial: small particle size materials like nano straw may provide an abundant carbon source for soil microorganisms [73]. While utilizing this carbon source, microorganisms concomitantly absorb nitrogen sources to build their own biomass, thereby competitively assimilating inorganic nitrogen from the soil solution and temporarily immobilizing it within their cells. This process significantly reduces the risk of nitrogen leaching. The observed increase in ammonium nitrogen concentration during later leaching events may also correspond to nitrogen released from the turnover of microbial biomass.

After 12 leaching events, straw particle size significantly influenced soil nitrogen forms and nutrient content (Fig. 5I-P, Fig. S7). The nano straw treatment significantly increased the nitrate and ammonium nitrogen content in the topsoil, indicating an enhancement in soil nitrogen mineralization processes, which is beneficial for plant uptake and utilization. Total nitrogen and carbon content were highest in nano straw treatments in both topsoil and subsoil, with submicron and micron treatments also significantly higher than the no-straw treatment in the topsoil. Available potassium content was optimal in nano straw treatments, with topsoil levels 34.85 %, 296.29 %, and 439.42 % higher than submicron, micron, and no-straw treatments, respectively; in the subsoil, levels were 58.82 %, 94.18 %, and 133.22 % higher, respectively. These results demonstrate that nano straw treatments exhibit superior nutrient release characteristics, particularly in available potassium release. Simultaneously, this treatment may optimize soil structure by improving microbial activities (such as promoting aggregate formation), thus more effectively enhancing the retention of nutrients like carbon, nitrogen, and potassium [74].

The results of the pot experiment are presented in Fig. 6 and Table 3. The impact of straw of different particle sizes on soil nutrients is shown in Fig. 6A-B. Straw returning to the field directly affects the carbon input into the soil [75]. In this study, we systematically monitored the dynamic changes in total carbon content in soil during the entire growth period of rice. The results showed that soil total carbon content exhibited an overall downward trend throughout the growth period (Fig. 6A). Notably, the soil total carbon content in the nano and submicron straw treatments was significantly higher than that in other treatments during the jointing and panicle development stage to the heading and grain-filling stage. However, during the yellow-ripening stage, the carbon content in the nano treatment declined rapidly, indicating a fast carbon release rate and greater availability for crop uptake. This rapid pattern of carbon consumption and release strongly indicates active microbial respiration and decomposition activities.

Simultaneously, this study systematically analyzed the soil nitrogen transformation processes. The total nitrogen analysis results (Fig. 6B) revealed that the total nitrogen content under all straw treatments was higher than that of the control during the tillering stage. Among these, the total nitrogen content in the nano straw treatment exhibited a continuous increasing trend from the jointing-grain filling stage to the maturity stage, averaging 2.98 %-15.36 % higher than the other treatment groups. Further research demonstrated (Fig. S8) that during the entire growth period, the ammonium nitrogen content in the nano straw treatment increased by 6.53 %-21.05 % compared to the control group. Its nitrate nitrogen content was also significantly higher than other treatment groups from the tillering stage to the heading-grain filling stage (averaging an increase of 2.21 %-5.73 %), with the differences gradually diminishing thereafter. This series of phenomena may be primarily attributed to the continuous mineralization of the abundant nitrogen-containing functional groups (such as amide groups, amino groups, etc.) within the nano straw. The rich nitrogen-containing functional groups in the nano straw provide more readily utilizable substrates for microorganisms (e.g., ammonifying bacteria, nitrifying bacteria), thereby effectively stimulating the activity of relevant microorganisms and accelerating the mineralization rate of organic nitrogen into inorganic nitrogen (ammonium nitrogen, nitrate nitrogen). However, from the maturity stage to the yellow-ripening stage, the total nitrogen content in the nano straw treatment decreased by 8.86 %, while that in other treatments increased, which is speculated to be due to the efficient absorption of nitrogen by crops to meet the needs of grain filling. In addition, this study found an accumulation of available potassium in the soil (Fig. S9), with the available potassium content in the nano straw treatment being 17.34 % to 67.68 % higher than that in other treatments, consistent with the results of EDS analysis, XRD analysis, and leaching experiments.

Tillering, as a key growth indicator of rice (Fig. 6C), showed that the tiller number in the nano straw treatment was significantly higher than that in other treatment groups, which was in line with the photograph in Fig. 6. From the yield data (Fig. 6D), the average yield of the nano straw treatment (77.53 g) and the submicron straw treatment (70.96 g) was significantly higher than that of the micron straw treatment (62.04 g) and the no straw treatment (61.89 g), confirming the promoting effect of straw refinement on yield increase.

Conclusion

This study successfully achieved controllable preparation of straw particles from micron to nano scale through ultrasonically-assisted centrifugal separation technology. Multiscale analysis revealed that the intense physical effects induced by ultrasonic cavitation not only reduced particle sizes but also effectively fractured molecular chains and ester bonds in straw, resulting in significant decreases in lignin and cellulose content. More importantly, this cavitation-driven nanoization fundamentally transformed the speciation and release patterns of straw nutrients: it substantially enriched nutritional elements including carbon, nitrogen (which surged by 136.52 %-193.60 %), and potassium (with surface enrichment rate reaching 750.53 %), while simultaneously enhancing water retention capacity by 18.52 % through increased surface hydrophilic functional groups (hydroxyl and carboxyl groups), and reducing cellulose crystallinity by 17.02 %. Soil column leaching and pot experiments confirmed its superior performance: nano straw dramatically reduced ammonium nitrogen loss (by 76.16 %), significantly increased soil available potassium (17.34 %-67.68 %) and ammonium nitrogen (6.53 %-21.05 %), and achieved sustained slow-release via surface functional groups (maintaining 439.42 % higher available potassium even after 12 leaching cycles), ultimately increasing rice tiller number by 19.48 % and yield by 25.27 %. This conclusively demonstrates that ultrasonic cavitation technology achieves a fundamental paradigm shift in converting straw into highly efficient, hydrophilic, and fast-acting nano-fertilizers through the “physical nanoization-chemical functionalization” cascade effect. This principle exhibits universal applicability and can be extended to the efficient fertilizer conversion of cellulose-rich agricultural wastes such as wheat straw and corn cobs, thereby opening a sustainable new pathway for the resource utilization of agricultural waste biomass. Future research will focus on validating the long-term field effects of nano straw and its coupling and integration with traditional biological treatment processes (e.g., composting), aiming to establish a more comprehensive resource utilization technology system.
