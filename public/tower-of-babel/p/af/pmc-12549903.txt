Water adlayers bridging metal spillover to boost catalytic activity
Authors: Yamei Fan, Rongtan Li, Xiangze Du, Fei Wang, Xiaohui Feng, Youyuanhe Yang, Conghui Liu, Jiaxin Li, Cui Dong, Jianyang Wang, Na Ta, Wenhao Cui, Yanxiao Ning, Rentao Mu, Qiang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen spillover has been extensively studied in heterogeneous catalysis, whereas the analogous migration of metal species remains largely underexplored. Here, we report a spillover phenomenon for metal species, exemplified by copper, which spontaneously migrates across physically contacted hydrophilic supports under humid ambient conditions. This process is facilitated by water adlayers on support surfaces, which act as molecular bridges to enable surface and interfacial migration of cooper species via hydroxylated intermediates. The phenomenon is universal across diverse supports, including oxides, carbides, and sulfides, and extends to metals such as ruthenium, cobalt, and nickel. Remarkably, catalysts prepared via this spillover approach exhibit substantially enhanced low-temperature activity in reactions including carbon monoxide oxidation, reverse water-gas shift, selective catalyst reduction with ammonia, and hydrogen cyanide oxidation, outperforming counterparts prepared by conventional impregnation. This work redefines the spillover phenomenon by extending it to metal species through water adlayer-mediated migration, opening new avenues for the design of dynamic catalysts.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Energy, Catalytic mechanisms, Catalyst synthesis

Introduction

Spillover refers to the migration of atomic or molecular species from one solid surface (donor) to another (acceptor) where these species cannot form or adsorb under identical conditions1. Initially, this phenomenon was extensively studied in the context of hydrogen (H) spillover, which is fundamental to hydrogenation reactions and the hydrogen storage process2–4. Over time, the study of spillover effects has extended to various non-metal species, including oxygen (O)5, hydroxyl (OH)6,7, and others8–10. These spilled-over species provide new reaction sites by serving as active agents or altering the structure of the acceptor surface2,11,12. Moreover, the dynamic migration of spilled-over species enables remote interaction between the donor and acceptor surfaces, offering new opportunities for precise catalytic regulation.

Although the spillover phenomenon has been predominantly studied in the context of non-metal species, metal species also exhibit significant migration under specific conditions, paving the way for more efficient catalytic processes13–17. For instance, metal adatoms or metal-adsorbate complexes such as (CHO)Cu1*18, Pd-CO13, and Au-Cl19 can diffuse across support surfaces and physically contact interfaces via surface diffusion or gas-phase migration at elevated temperatures. The migration of metal species has inspired emerging concepts, such as dynamic single-atom catalysis20,21, dynamic confinement catalysis22–25, and mobile catalysis15,19,26, generating new active sites and enhancing catalytic performance. However, the high-temperature conditions required for metal migration often present challenges, such as unpredictable metal sintering, which diminishes the catalytic efficiency and long-term stability of many reactions. Therefore, it is essential to explore whether metal migration across interfaces can occur under milder conditions, referred to as metal spillover. Understanding how to manipulate metal spillover under ambient or near-room-temperature (RT) conditions is the key to the precise control of active centers.

Previous studies have shown that H2O molecules from the environment can facilitate the migration of metal species under near-RT conditions. For example, model studies reveal that Rh adatoms are mobile in the presence of H2O adlayers on α-Fe2O3 (11¯02) surface27. Our works demonstrate that H2O-generated surface OH groups greatly enhance the diffusion of metal atoms on oxide surfaces, promoting the redispersion or aggregation of supported Ag28 and Cu29 nanostructures at RT. The indispensable role of H2O in metal structural evolution under mild conditions is similar to its critical role in H spillover as highlighted by Boudart et al. in 196630. They explained the spillover mechanism as a “bucket brigade”1,30, wherein H spillover cannot proceed under dry conditions. Instead, surface OH groups31–34 from dissociative H2O adsorption and H2O clusters or adlayers through molecular H2O adsorption32,33,35–37 serve as proton acceptors, effectively driving the process. The crucial role of H2O in H spillover suggests the potential for achieving metal spillover across interfaces with the assistance of H2O.

This work provides compelling evidence that Cu species can spontaneously migrate from one support to another under ambient humid conditions, a phenomenon resembling H spillover and herein referred to as Cu spillover. Spectroscopic analyses reveal that H2O adlayers on hydrophilic surfaces are essential for this process, which cannot occur on hydrophobic surfaces or in dry environments. These H2O adlayers not only promote the diffusion of hydroxylated Cu intermediates along the support surface but also serve as molecular bridges that facilitate interfacial migration. Furthermore, the concept of metal spillover can be extended to other transition metals, such as Ru, Co, and Ni. Importantly, the spilled-over Cu species on CeO2 exhibit nearly double the CO production rate of conventional Cu catalysts in the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction at 400 °C. Beyond RWGS, spillover-derived catalysts also demonstrate superior performance in CO oxidation, selective catalyst reduction (SCR) with NH3 (NH3-SCR,) and HCN oxidation, with pronounced advantages over their impregnated counterpart at low temperatures. These findings uncover the metal spillover phenomenon under ambient humid conditions, highlight the critical role of H2O adlayers in enabling controllable interfacial migration, and offer valuable insights for the rational design of highly active catalysts.

Results

Spontaneous Cu spillover across support interfaces induced by ambient O2/H2O

Supported Cu catalysts were prepared by impregnating the support with aqueous Cu(NO3)2·3H2O at a weight loading of 6 wt%, followed by calcination at 500 °C for 4 h. The sample is labeled as Cu/S-C (S represents SiO2, CeO2 and NC support; C for calcination). After reduction in H2 at 300 °C for 4 h, the obtained sample is referred to as Cu/S. Cu/S powders were then physically mixed with another pure support powder at a specific mass ratio by mechanical grinding, denoted as Cu/S1-S2 (details seen in Supplementary Methods). For the Cu/SiO2-CeO2 sample, a magnified view at 40–50° in XRD patterns (Fig. 1a) indicates that Cu nanoparticles (NPs) (2θ ≈ 43.3°) exist in Cu/SiO2-CeO2 sample, whose average size was measured to be approximately 5.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1) by high-angle annular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM). The same distribution of Cu element with Si rather than with Ce element in energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping (EDS-mapping) images (Fig. 1b and c) suggests that Cu NPs are mainly located on the SiO2 surface in the as-prepared Cu/SiO2-CeO2 sample.

The Cu/SiO2-CeO2 and Cu/SiO2 samples were treated in a flowing O2/H2O atmosphere at 50 °C for 48 h (all O2/H2O treatments in this work follow the same procedure unless specified), denoted as Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O and Cu-OH/SiO2, respectively, as schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Table 4. Following ambient O2/H2O treatment, the characteristic diffraction peaks of metallic Cu (2θ ≈ 43.3 and 50.4°) completely disappeared in the Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O sample, and no additional Cu-related phases (e.g., CuO and Cu2O) were detected (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 3). Meanwhile, the diffraction peaks corresponding to CeO2 and amorphous SiO2 remained essentially unchanged. In contrast, metallic Cu peaks were still observed in the Cu-OH/SiO2 sample, indicating that Cu NPs cannot be fully redispersed on SiO2 surface under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4). EDS-mapping images (Fig. 1d) reveal a highly consistent distribution of Cu and Ce elements in the Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O sample, suggesting that highly dispersed Cu species as single atoms or clusters are predominantly located onto CeO2 surface (Fig. 1e). The results suggest that O2/H2O treatment promotes the redispersion of Cu NPs from SiO2 onto CeO2 surface, which otherwise remain undispersed on the SiO2 surface under the same conditions.

Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) has been employed to analyze surface composition and uncover coordination structures through fragment ions at specific mass-to-charge ratios (m/z)38. In the fresh Cu/SiO2-CeO2 sample, a strong Cu-O-Si⁺ signal (m/z = 106.9) was observed, whereas the Cu-O-Ce⁺ signal (m/z = 218.8) was negligible. In contrast, the intensity ratio I(Cu-O-Ce⁺)/I(Cu-O-Ce⁺) increased by more than an order of magnitude after O2/H2O treatment (Fig. 1f). The results, together with the EDS-mapping images (Fig. 1e), clearly verify the migration of Cu species from the SiO2 surface to the CeO2 surface under ambient O2/H2O conditions.

The spontaneous Cu migration process from SiO2 to CeO2 surface under mild conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5) may involve the following steps: (i) detachment of Cu species from Cu NPs, (ii) migration of Cu species across the Cu-SiO2 interface to the SiO2 surface (donor support), (iii) diffusion on the SiO2 surface, (iv) interfacial diffusion between SiO2 and CeO2, and (v) subsequent capture by the CeO2 support (acceptor support). This process shares mechanistic similarities with classical H spillover11, where active H species generated on a metal surface, e.g., Pt NP, migrate across the metal-support e.g., Pt-Al2O3 interface, diffuse on the support surface (Al2O3), cross the support-support (such as Al2O3-WO3) interface, and ultimately interact with the accepting surface (WO3). These similarities underscore that, like H species, Cu atoms can undergo interfacial migration, referred to as Cu spillover.

Universality of H2O-assisted metal spillover

To date, H spillover has been observed on both oxide and non-oxide materials, including metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)35 and carbon-based materials39, raising the question of whether Cu spillover can occur on non-oxide supports. To explore this, nitrogen-doped carbon (NC) was utilized as both a Cu acceptor (Cu/SiO2-NC) and a Cu donor (Cu/NC-CeO2) to study Cu spillover behavior under ambient O2/H2O conditions.

For Cu/SiO2-NC, distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic Cu were initially observed (Fig. 2a), and EDS-mapping images showed that Cu distribution exclusively overlaps with Si, with no detectable Cu signals on NC domains (Fig. 2b). After ambient O2/H2O treatment, the Cu peak intensity decreased (Fig. 2a) and obvious Cu signals emerged on NC surface (Fig. 2c). Correspondingly, ToF-SIMS results showed a pronounced increase in the relative intensity I(Cu-O-N⁺)/I(Cu-O-Si⁺) after O2/H2O treatment (Fig. 2d). A similar migration behavior was observed in Cu/NC-CeO2 samples after O2/H2O treatment, as evidenced by comparative XRD patterns, EDS-mapping images and ToF-SIMS analysis, which clearly show the migration of Cu species from NC onto CeO2 surface (Fig. 2e–h). These results demonstrate that Cu spillover can be induced by O2/H2O even onto non-oxide supports such as NC.

To evaluate the generality of Cu spillover, we examined a broader range of donor-acceptor pairs. Besides SiO2-based donors, other supports such as NC, CeO2, and γ-Al2O3 also enabled Cu migration (Supplementary Figs. 6–9). Acceptors included both reducible (CeO2, TiO2, γ-Al2O3) and non-reducible supports such as NC, Si3N4, Mo2C, and MoS2 (Supplementary Figs. 10–14). These results indicate that Cu spillover is not limited to specific interfaces but can occur broadly under ambient O2/H2O conditions. We attribute this spillover phenomenon to a concentration gradient between donor-acceptor pairs that drives Cu transport. However, Supplementary Figs. 7, 8 reveal limited Cu spillover from CeO2 to NC or SiO2, attributable to the strong metal-support interaction between Cu and CeO2. Importantly, this behavior is not unique to Cu. Similar interfacial migration was observed for other metals, including Ru, Ni, and Co (see Supplementary Figs. 15–17 for details), underscoring the broader applicability of this metal spillover process.

Mechanistic insights into Cu spillover across support interfaces

Understanding the migration intermediates is crucial for elucidating the metal spillover mechanism. In our study, we observe that Cu species initially located on the SiO2 surface are unable to migrate onto CeO2 under pure O2, as confirmed by XRD and EDS-mapping images in Supplementary Figs. 18, 19. However, O2/H2O atmosphere enables this migration process, suggesting that H2O plays a critical role in facilitating Cu transport across the support interface. Quasi in-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis reveals the formation of Cu-OH29,40 species in Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O sample, whereas these species are absent in the sample treated under pure O2 (Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2) (Supplementary Fig. 20). Accordingly, Cu-OH intermediates are proposed to be the active migrating species for Cu spillover from SiO2 to CeO2 surface. This hypothesis is further supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations, which show that Cu-OH species possess higher mobility and lower diffusion energy barriers compared to non-hydroxylated Cu41. Building on these insights, we extended our investigation to other metals, including Ru, Co and Ni, under similar O2/H2O atmospheres. These metals were also found to form analogous metal-OH (M-OH) species that serve as key intermediates in their respective spillover processes (Supplementary Figs. 21–23). The universality of M-OH intermediates across different metals and support interfaces highlights their critical role in enabling metal spillover under ambient humid conditions.

To probe the migration behavior of Cu-OH species, we first generated Cu-OH on the SiO2 surface via O2/H2O treatment, as confirmed by the Cu 2p XPS peak at 935.6 eV (Supplementary Fig. 24). To ensure that the migration process was not influenced by surface-adsorbed H2O, the CeO2 acceptor was pre-dehydrated. 1H solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (1H MAS NMR) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 25) confirmed the absence of physisorbed H2O on the CeO2 surface. XRD and elemental mapping by scanning electron microscopy (SEM-mapping) results confirm that pre-formed Cu-OH species supported on the SiO2 surface do not migrate to the pre-dehydrated CeO2 surface in ambient dry O2, but they do migrate in an Ar/H2O atmosphere (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27). These findings demonstrate that Cu-OH species serve as the crucial migration intermediates for the Cu spillover process, and H2O plays an indispensable role in both the formation and subsequent migration of these Cu-OH species. Interestingly, despite little surface OH (as evidenced by H-D exchange, Supplementary Figs. 28, 29), NC and Si3N4 still enable efficient Cu spillover. These findings indicate that, beyond surface OH, other H2O-related effects in humid environments contribute to interfacial Cu migration, though the exact mechanism remains to be clarified.

Solid-state 1H MAS NMR spectroscopy has been employed to characterize surface H2O-related species after ambient O2/H2O treatment. All samples were exposed to ambient O2/H2O for 48 h, followed by Ar purging for 1 h. A sharp peak near 4.6 ppm was observed in the SiO2-O2/H2O and CeO2-O2/H2O samples, attributed to H2O adlayer42,43. For the NC-O2/H2O sample, the chemical shift of the H2O adlayer shifts to 3.6 ppm44, likely due to differences in molecular mobility and changes in electronic structure on the various support surfaces (Fig. 3a). The presence of H2O adlayer was further confirmed by a peak at 532.8 eV in in-situ near-ambient pressure XPS O 1 s spectra, obtained under 0.5 mbar O2/H2O at RT, corresponding to adsorbed H2O species45 (Supplementary Figs. 30, 31). Additionally, H2O amounts on these supports were quantified through cyclic H2O adsorption-desorption thermogravimetric (TG) analysis, yielding values of 0.038, 0.011, and 0.165 gH2O/gcat for SiO2, CeO2, and NC, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 32–34, Supplementary Tables 1–3). The corresponding thickness of H2O adlayers was calculated to be 1.45, 1.31, and 0.99 monolayers, respectively. These results confirm the formation of continuous H2O adlayers with monolayer thickness on the supports under O2/H2O conditions.

To further elucidate the role of the H2O adlayer, the above-mentioned hydrophilic SiO2, CeO2, and NC supports (Supplementary Fig. 35) are rendered hydrophobic through chlorotrimethylsilane (TMCS) modification without alteration of morphology, phase composition, and pore structure (Supplementary Figs. 36–40)46, labeled as S(T), where S represents SiO2, CeO2 and NC. Water contact angle measurements confirm that the modified S(T) surfaces exhibit a markedly hydrophobic character (Supplementary Fig. 41). Solid-state 1H MAS NMR results show that all S(T) samples exhibit a signal at 0.83 ppm, corresponding to protons in the terminal methyl group (-CH3) introduced by the TMCS modification47. Upon treatment with O2/H2O, S(T) samples exhibit a nearly negligible adsorbed H2O signal (Fig. 3a), indicating the absence of H2O adlayers on the hydrophobic surfaces. Cu spillover onto hydrophobic accepting surfaces has been investigated in Cu/SiO2-CeO2(T), Cu/SiO2-NC(T), and Cu/NC-SiO2(T) catalysts under ambient O2/H2O treatment. ToF-SIMS analysis of Cu/SiO2-CeO2(T)-O2/H2O shows that the I(Cu-O-Ce+)/(Cu-O-Si+) intensity ratio remains negligible, indicating that Cu species located on the SiO2 surface do not migrate onto the hydrophobic CeO2(T) surface after O2/H2O treatment. Similarly, no appreciable increase in the I(Cu-N-C+)/I(Cu-O-Si+) ratio was observed in Cu/SiO2-NC(T) and Cu/NC-SiO2(T) after O2/H2O treatment (Fig. 3b). These results suggest that Cu cannot spill over onto hydrophobic accepting surfaces under ambient O2/H2O conditions.

The results highlight the critical role of the H2O adlayer in facilitating Cu spillover. First, O2/H2O induces the formation of mobile Cu-OH species, which then detach from Cu aggregates (Fig. 3c, (i)). Second, a continuous H2O adlayer provides a rapid diffusion channel27,30,48 for Cu-OH surface diffusion (Fig. 3c, (ii)). Finally, H2O adlayer facilitates stronger interaction between adjacent physically-contacted particles, enhancing Cu migration across the interface (Fig. 3c, (iii)). In the absence of an H2O adlayer, as observed under dry conditions or on hydrophobic surfaces, Cu spillover is significantly blocked (Fig. 3c). These findings further suggest that modulating surface hydrophilicity enables control over the H2O adlayer, thereby regulating the Cu spillover process.

Strategies for tuning Cu spillover behavior

From a mechanistic perspective, we propose that Cu spillover is governed by both thermodynamic factors, including concentration gradients and variations in Cu-support interactions, and kinetic factors, i.e., the promoting role of H2O. This framework establishes that comprehensive regulation of Cu spillover requires multiparameter optimization, involving: tailored support properties (including defect density and hydrophilicity), proximity between the donor and the acceptor, engineered atmospheres, and other interfacial or environmental parameters influencing spillover thermodynamics and kinetics.

O2 and surface H2O synergistically facilitate Cu spillover by lowering the migration barrier through the formation of mobile Cu-OH intermediates and reducing diffusion resistance via the surface H2O adlayer. The absence of this H2O adlayer on hydrophobic supports, e.g., TMCS-treated CeO2 suppresses Cu spillover. Consistently, a kinetic isotope effect was observed when comparing O2/H2O and O2/D2O treatments, with an apparent KIE ≈ 2 (details are shown in Supplementary Fig. 42), corroborating that H2O adlayer-assisted Cu-OH transfer plays a decisive role in Cu spillover49,50. Complementary ToF-SIMS analysis further reveals a direct correlation between H2O content and spillover efficiency, with 6.5% H2O yielding double the I(Cu-O-Ce+)/I(Cu-O-Si+) compared to 3.1% H2O (Fig. 4a), underscoring the effectiveness of tuning humidity and support hydrophilicity to modulate Cu spillover.

Beyond surface hydrophilicity, thermodynamic control is dominated by metal-support interactions. Spillover preferentially occurs from weakly interacting donors such as SiO2 and N-doped carbon (NC) to strongly interacting acceptors like CeO2. The reverse process is incomplete, with residual Cu persisting on CeO2 even after O2/H2O treatment (Supplementary Figs. 7, 8), consistent with previously reported high-temperature migration behavior51. This suggests that engineering interaction strength differences (referred to as asymmetric donor-acceptor pairs) provides a rational strategy for directional control of Cu spillover (Supplementary Fig. 43a). To further probe this directionality, a physical mixture of Cu/SiO2, CeO2, and NC was prepared. EDS-mapping shows Cu distributed on both CeO2 and SiO2 surfaces, with the strongest signal observed on CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 44), reinforcing the role of strong metal–support interactions in driving spillover and providing direct evidence for the preferential migration of Cu toward thermodynamically favorable acceptor sites. Although all tested hydrophilic supports enable spillover, migration may be suppressed on surfaces lacking anchoring sites such as hydroxyl groups or vacancy defects, e.g., oxygen vacancies (OV), sulfur vacancies (SV), and nitrogen vacancies (NV), where Cu species are less stably anchored and may remain mobile or migrate back to the donor support (Supplementary Fig. 43b).

Considering the strong dependence of H spillover on proximity52, we prepared a series of Cu/SiO2-CeO2 catalysts with varying degrees of proximity to probe their effect on Cu spillover. Close contact between Cu/SiO2 and CeO2 during O2/H2O treatment (labeled as Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O) results in a CO2 conversion as high as 20% at 400 °C (Fig. 4b). By contrast, loosening this contact (Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2Oa) significantly lowers CO2 conversion to 8.2%. The activity further declines to 4.2% when Cu/SiO2 and CeO2 are physically mixed but separately granulated before O2/H2O treatment. Increasing the spatial separation by physically isolating Cu/SiO2 and CeO2 with quartz wool (Cu/SiO2||CeO2-O2/H2O) causes a drastic drop in conversion to 1.6%, comparable to that of Cu/SiO2-C. These findings highlight that RWGS performance strongly depends on the physical proximity between Cu/SiO2 and CeO2, with closer contact markedly enhancing catalytic activity. This progressive decline correlates well with ToF-SIMS analysis, where the I(Cu-O-Ce+)/I(Cu-O-Si+) signal diminishes and eventually disappears as the distance increases (Cu/SiO2||CeO2-O2/H2O) (Fig. 4b). These findings highlight that modulating donor-acceptor proximity is an effective method for precisely controlling the efficiency of Cu spillover and catalytic activity.

Overall, these findings establish a clear framework for regulating Cu spillover by tuning interfacial hydration, proximity, surface properties, and reaction atmosphere. In addition, temperature can further modulate the balance between concentration gradient-driven and interaction-driven migration. Specifically, Cu spillover occurs from CeO2 to SiO2 under near-RT conditions, while elevated temperatures can reverse the direction due to stronger Cu-CeO2 interaction51. The thermodynamic preference of Cu for one support over another, together with kinetic factors such as H2O-assisted surface diffusion, enables precise and reversible control over the direction of Cu spillover.

Enhanced reaction activity of spilled-over Cu species

Cu-based catalysts are widely recognized as efficient non-precious metals for RWGS reactions due to their high activity and selectivity53. Figure 5a compares the CO2 conversion of various Cu-based catalysts at 400 °C. The impregnated Cu/SiO2-C and Cu/CeO2-C catalysts achieved CO2 conversion of 1.7% and 12.8%, respectively (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 45), while their pure supports exhibit negligible activity (Supplementary Fig. 46). A co-impregnated Cu/SiO2CeO2-C catalyst reached 11.2% conversion. Notably, the Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O catalyst, prepared via the spillover strategy, exhibited a markedly higher CO2 conversion of 20.0%, which is ~56% higher than that of Cu/CeO2-C under identical conditions. A 12-hour continuous test revealed gradual deactivation for all Cu-based catalysts. Remarkably, their catalytic activity was almost completely recovered after ambient O2/H2O treatment, demonstrating excellent regenerability (Fig. 5a).

Post-reaction XRD patterns show slight Cu sintering on Cu/SiO2-C, and no detectable Cu diffraction peaks for Cu/CeO2-C, Cu/SiO2CeO2-C and Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O catalysts, suggesting limited Cu sintering (Supplementary Fig. 47). To better understand the chemical state transformation and structural changes of Cu under RWGS conditions, in-situ and quasi in-situ characterizations were performed on the Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O catalyst. In-situ UV-Vis spectroscopy revealed a gradual increase in the ~ 570 nm absorption peak during RWGS, indicating sintering of highly dispersed Cu species into Cu NPs (Supplementary Fig. 48). Upon cooling and brief air exposure (2–3 min), this peak vanished, suggesting rapid oxidation and re-dispersion of the sintered Cu NPs (Supplementary Fig. 48). Quasi in-situ XPS further confirms the reduction of Cu to its metallic state after reaction, followed by re-oxidation and the reformation of Cu-OH species upon ambient O2/H2O treatment (Supplementary Fig. 49). EDS-mapping images of the post-reaction catalyst reveal that Cu remains uniformly distributed and co-localized with Ce, without obvious aggregation (Supplementary Fig. 50). These findings emphasize the structural advantage of the spillover strategy, where Cu species remain anchored without reverse migration.

Figure 5b illustrates the apparent activation energy (Ea) of various Cu-based catalysts. Cu/CeO2-C and Cu/SiO2CeO2-C exhibit similar Ea values of 71.2 ± 1.5 and 71.8 ± 1.4 kJ/mol, respectively. In contrast, Cu/SiO2 treated with O2/H2O exhibits a significantly lower Ea of 55.2 kJ/mol, indicating more favorable reaction kinetics. This improvement is likely due to the formation of highly dispersed and reactive spilled-over Cu species. Notably, this kinetic advantage is not limited to the CeO2 acceptor. Cu spillover from SiO2 to other acceptor oxides, including SiO2, CeO2, TiO2, and γ-Al2O3, consistently improves RWGS performance, even under low Cu dilution ratios (Supplementary Fig. 51). Collectively, these results underscore the pivotal role of Cu spillover in enhancing catalytic efficiency and establish Cu spillover as a broadly applicable strategy for optimizing RWGS catalysts.

Importantly, the promotional effect of Cu spillover extends beyond the RWGS reaction. We further evaluated the spillover-prepared Cu/SiO2-CeO2-O2/H2O catalyst in reactions of NH3-SCR, HCN oxidation, and CO oxidation. In NH3-SCR, the spillover catalyst achieves complete NOx conversion at 275 °C, outperforming the impregnated Cu/CeO2-C catalyst, which only reaches 66% conversion at 250 °C and suffers activity loss at higher temperatures. Across the entire 100–300 °C range, the spillover-prepared catalyst consistently outperforms its impregnated counterpart (Fig. 5c). In HCN oxidation, full conversion is attained at 175 °C, compared to ~76% for Cu/CeO2-C under the same conditions (Fig. 5d, e). Moreover, the spillover catalyst exhibits superior selectivity by minimizing NOx byproducts (Fig. 5e). A similar trend is observed in CO oxidation, where complete CO conversion occurs at 175 °C for the spillover catalyst, outperforming Cu/CeO2-C (200 °C) and Cu/SiO2CeO2-C (225 °C) (Supplementary Fig. 52).

Together, these findings demonstrate that the spillover strategy not only enhances the intrinsic reactivity and regenerability of Cu species for RWGS, but also broadens their applicability across multiple low-temperature oxidation reactions. The generality of this approach highlights its potential as a versatile platform for the design of efficient and regenerable Cu-based catalysts.

Discussion

Since the discovery of H spillover in 196411, the spillover phenomenon e.g., O5, OH6,7, CO8,54, NCO1,9 and NOx10 has been extensively studied because of its profound effect on catalysis2. Recent research has increasingly focused on the dynamic migration of active metal species13,27–29,55, which can enhance catalytic activity and stability by mitigating adsorbate26 or coke56 accumulation. However, these migration processes typically require high temperatures and complex reaction environments. In contrast, metal migration under milder conditions reduces the reliance on extreme conditions, allowing for more precise control over catalyst structure and interface properties.

Our findings reveal that some metal atoms, e.g., Cu, Ru, Co and Ni, can migrate not only across the metal-support interface but also between support-support interfaces under ambient conditions. This expands the spillover concept from small atomic or molecular species to metal atoms. Similar to H spillover, where promoting agents or molecular “cocatalysts”, such as water, alcohols, carbon, perylene, N-Apiezon grease, and oxygenates57,58, effectively reduce the diffusion barrier of H atoms and facilitate H spillover at RT, we observe a similar mechanism in the case of Cu spillover. Specifically, H2O adlayers and/or H2O-derived OH groups enable the continuous hopping of H or Cu atoms from one OH group to the next, analogous to the long-distance H spillover and surface diffusion of Rh atoms mediated by H2O27. We propose that surface OH groups and H2O adlayers together form a hydration lubrication layer on the solid support surface, which reduces the diffusion barrier for active components between physically contacted supports, thereby facilitating efficient migration.

Building on the fundamental understanding of metal spillover, Cu spillover can be actively tuned by thermodynamic factors such as interaction strength and concentration gradients, as well as kinetic parameters like H2O vapor content. Precise control over this dynamic process is achievable through the rational design of donor-acceptor pairs with tailored support properties, including hydrophilicity, surface functional groups, defect sites, and spatial configurations. This strategy leverages the enhanced reactivity of spilled-over Cu species across various reactions and highlights the potential of dynamic metal species to improve catalytic performance, consistent with recent advances in single-atom catalysis59.

Methods

Sample preparation

Commercial nano SiO2 (99.9% purity, ≤100 nm, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemistry technology Co., Ltd.), nano CeO2 (99.9% purity, 50 nm, Shanghai Macklin biochemical Co., Ltd.) and nano TiO2 (≥99.8% metals basis, 60 nm, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemistry technology Co., Ltd.) were purified by calcination at 500 °C for 5 h and then used for catalyst preparation. Nano Si3N4 (≥99.9% metals basis, 20 nm, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemistry technology Co., Ltd.) and utilized without further purification. γ-Al2O329, N-doped carbon support (NC)60, Mo2C61 and MoS262 supports were prepared according to the published literature.

Cu/S-C (S represents SiO2, CeO2, and NC; C for calcination) samples were synthesized using conventional impregnation techniques. In a typical procedure, 1 g of S1(SiO2, CeO2, and NC) support was suspended in 20 mL of deionized water and agitated for 30 min. Following this, 2493 µL of copper nitrate solution (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, 0.4 mol L−1) was introduced dropwise into the mixture. The resultant mixture was continuously stirred at 50 °C until all moisture evaporated. The residue obtained was then transferred to a vacuum-drying oven and dried at 80 °C overnight. After drying, the sample was placed in a quartz boat and then positioned in a tube furnace. The furnace was programmed to ramp up at 2 °C min−1 to 550 °C under Air or Ar (for the Cu/NC-C sample only) for 4 h, and the resulting sample was named Cu/S1-C.

Cu/SiO2CeO2-C was prepared using a co-impregnation method. Specifically, 1 g SiO2 and 3 g CeO2 were suspended in 80 mL of deionized water and stirred for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Subsequently, 9.375 mL of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O solution (0.4 mol L−1) was introduced dropwise under continuous stirring. The resulting suspension was stirred until the solvent fully evaporated at 50 °C, leaving a solid residue. This solid was transferred to a crucible and calcined in a tube furnace under air, with the temperature ramped at 2 °C min−1 to 550 °C for 4 h.

Cu/S1 samples were prepared by reducing the Cu/S1-C samples in pure H2 at 300 °C for 4 h.

Cu/S1-S2 samples were physically mixed by the mechanical grinding method. A specified amount of pure support S2, in a mass ratio of SiO2: NC: CeO2 = 6:1:18, was incorporated into the pre-prepared Cu/S1 catalyst and then ground in an agate mortar at RT for 15 min, yielding Cu/S1-S2. For instance, Cu/SiO2-CeO2 sample was prepared by grinding 1 g Cu/SiO2 with 3 g CeO2 pure support in a mortar for 15 min. Cu/SiO2-NC sample was prepared by grinding 360 mg Cu/SiO2 with 60 mg NC support in a mortar for 15 min. Cu/CeO2-NC sample was prepared by grinding 360 mg Cu/CeO2 with 20 mg NC support in a mortar for 15 min.

Cu/S1-S2-O2/H2O was obtained by treating Cu/S1-S2 in a tube furnace by bubbling O2 through a water bottle, resulting in a water content of approximately 12.1 vol% at 50 °C for two days.

Cu/S1-S2-O2/H2Oa was prepared by loading Cu/SiO2 powder and CeO2 powder into a reaction tube, with Cu/SiO2 positioned above and CeO2 below. The reaction tube was then treated with O2/H2O at 50 °C for two days.

Cu/SiO2|CeO2-O2/H2O was synthesized by mechanical mixture. Cu/SiO2 granules of 635–1270 μm were well mixed with CeO2 granules of 635–1270 μm with a mass ratio of 1:3. The mixture was transferred to a quartz boat and placed in a tube furnace at 50 °C for an O2/H2O treatment for two days.

Cu/SiO2||CeO2-O2/H2O sample was prepared by loading Cu/SiO2 powder and CeO2 powder into a reaction tube, separated by quartz wool, with Cu/SiO2 positioned above and CeO2 below. The reaction tube was then treated with O2/H2O at 50 °C for two days.

Saturated H2O vapor was introduced into O2 through a bubbler held at 60 °C, and the resulting gas was denoted as 19.7% H2O/O2, where 19.7% refers to the fraction corresponding to P(H2O) ≈ 19.7 kPa in the gas stream at ~1 atm. H2O content of the gas stream was then adjusted by mixing dry O2 and moist O2 (from the bubbler) in defined proportions at a total flow of 100 mL min−1. The resulting mixtures were denoted as 12.1%, 6.5%, 3.1%, and 0.6% H2O/O2 (vol%) and correspond to wet/dry O2 flow rates of 61.4/38.6, 33.0/67.0, 15.7/84.3, 3.1/96.9 mL min−1, respectively. To prevent water-vapor condensation, the sample (reactor inlet and downstream lines) was maintained at 60 °C.

The hydrophobic support (ST) was synthesized according to a reported method47. Briefly, the fresh support was preheated in a vacuum oven at 150 °C overnight. After cooling to 30 °C in the vacuum oven, n-hexane was added, followed by the addition of chlorotrimethylsilane (TMCS, 10 mL g−1support). The mixture was then ultrasonically treated at room temperature for 6 h. The resulting solid was washed thoroughly with n-hexane and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C overnight and the final material was denoted as ST.

Instrument and characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using the Empyrean-100 diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5418 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA. The step size was 0.03° over the range of 10°–90°. The Cu-based catalyst was encapsulated in reaction tubes sealed at both ends and subjected to rapid XRD analysis.

The textural properties of the samples were determined by N2 adsorption/desorption experiments at −196 °C on a Micromeritics Quadrasorb-EVO (Quadrasorb-EVO, Quantachrome Corporation, America) gas adsorption apparatus. The specific surface area was measured according to the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method, and the BJH model was used to calculate the pore size distribution.

The thermogravimetric curves (TG) of the samples were acquired utilizing a Diamond TG/DTA instrument. A specified quantity of the sample was placed in an alumina crucible and heated from RT to 500 °C in N2 with a flow rate of 10 mL min−1. After cooling to RT, the sample was subjected to in-situ N2/H2O treatment for 4 h before undergoing a second thermogravimetric analysis. H2O was introduced into the sample through N2 gas bubbling, with an approximate H2O content of 3 vol.% at RT.

Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) analysis of measurements was carried out with a M6 Plus instrument (IONTOF GmbH), equipped with a 30 kV Bi cluster primary-ion gun for analysis and a dual-source column for Cs+ depth profiling.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a TIR Vertex 80 v infrared spectrometer manufactured by Bruker, coupled with a Hyperion 2000 FTIR microscope and a liquid nitrogen-cooled narrow-band mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. The coal sample was scanned 32 times in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 8 cm−1.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and element mapping images were recorded on JEOL JSM-7900F instrument with an EDS detector operated at 15 kV. High-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping images were recorded on a JEM-F200 microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

Quasi in-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a PHI VersaProbe 4 XPS instrument equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα x-ray source, which was operated at a power of 200 W. In a typical procedure, samples were transferred from a sealed reaction tube to a glovebox, then moved through a transfer chamber to the XPS analysis chamber. Throughout the transfer process, the samples were maintained in an inert atmosphere, ensuring no exposure to air. In-situ near-ambient pressure XPS (NAP-XPS) spectra were acquired on a laboratory-based SPECS NAP-XPS system.

H-D exchange experiments were performed on a microreactor connected to a Pfeiffer OmniStar mass spectrometer. 100 mg samples were packed into a quartz tube and then pretreated with Ar at a flow rate of 30 mL min−1 at 200 °C for 1 h. Then, the H-D exchange experiment was performed, and the HD mass signal (m/z = 3) was recorded.

The 1H solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (1H MAS NMR) was performed on a Bruker Avance III HD spectrometer operating at 400 MHz Larmor frequency, utilizing a 4 mm dual-channel MAS probe for high-sensitivity detection. Solid-state 1H MAS NMR measurements were carried out at a rotor spinning frequency of 8 kHz, employing a single-pulse sequence with π/2 pulse durations. All proton chemical shifts were calibrated against the methylene resonance of adamantane (δ = 1.74 ppm) as an external reference.

In situ UV-Vis spectra were collected using a Lambda 950 spectrometer (Perkin Elmer) operated in diffuse reflectance mode (DRS). The Kubelka-Munk function (KM) is used in DRS to linearize the diffuse reflectance signal. Catalyst powders (30–50 mg) were pressed into self-supporting wafers and placed within a temperature-controlled stainless-steel cell equipped with CaF2 windows and connected to a gas flow system. The sample was heated from RT to 500 °C with a rate of 10 °C min−1 under a CO2/H2 (3:1) atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 mL min−1. The cell temperature was controlled using resistive heating cartridges, and the temperature was measured by a K-type thermocouple (Omega) in contact with the sample. For the kinetic isotope experiments using in-situ UV-Vis, the sample temperature was heated from RT to 500 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under H2 atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 mL min−1 for 1 h, followed by cooling to RT in Ar. Subsequently, the atmosphere was switched to O2/H2O or O2/D2O to investigate the structure evolution of Cu species.

Catalyst performance evaluation

CO2 hydrogenation reaction and CO oxidation reaction tests were carried out on a homemade three-channel reactor. 50 mg pelleting catalysts (20~40 mesh) were loaded in a quartz tube with an inner diameter of 8 mm. The reactant gas consists of 24% CO2, 24% H2 (volume ratio), balanced with Ar.
