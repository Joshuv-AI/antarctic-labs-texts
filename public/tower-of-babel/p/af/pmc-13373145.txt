Prevalence of Enterobacterales Producing Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamases According to One Health Approach in Burkina Faso: A Systematic Review with Meta-Analysis
Authors: William Alix L. Tiendrebeogo, Sibidou Yougbaré, Palpouguini Lompo, Serge Ouoba, Daniel Valia, M. Christian Tahita, Halidou Tinto, G. B. Leon Savadogo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents an important public health challenge worldwide. Given the significant and interdependent human, animal and environmental dimensions of this resistance, a ‘One Health’ approach is necessary to combat it. However, its extent remains poorly known and understood in resource-limited settings. This study aims to describe the current prevalence of antibiotic resistance and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales species according to One Health approach in Burkina Faso.MethodsPubMed, Hinari, African Journal Online, Scopus and Google Scholar were the bibliographic databases used for screening of the relevant articles according to the reporting elements for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA). The meta-analysis was performed using the statistical analysis R software version 4.2.3 (2023).ResultsA total of 24 research articles were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. E. coli, Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp. and Salmonella spp. were the most frequently reported bacteria from at least two sources. The environment had the highest rate of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales (60.01%). The highest proportion of ESBL production was recorded for E. coli in all sources (64.92%).ConclusionOur study showed a significant cluster prevalence of ESBL Enterobacterales species from all sources, with the highest prevalence found in the environment. Strong resistance to commonly used antibiotics was found in these species. Studies in humans were far more numerous than those in animals and the environment. This highlights the need to strengthen integrated interventions according to One Health approach to fill the gaps in the fight against the emergence and spread of AMR in Burkina Faso.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00571-6.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global public health problem that threatens the health system ability to successfully treat bacterial infections [1, 2]. This leads to high mortality and morbidity, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to appropriate diagnostic tools is limited and where the prescription and use of antimicrobials are not sufficiently regulated [1]. Antimicrobial-resistant micro-organisms are present in humans, animals and the environment and can spread worldwide [1]. It is an ecological problem involving complex interactions between various microbial populations and affecting human, animal and environmental health [3].

Enterobacterales are bacteria that are usually part of the digestive flora and released in stools [4]. These bacteria produce enzymes against beta-lactam antibiotics and have developed a high level of resistance to antibiotics, making treatment of infections very complex [5]. The production of enzymes called extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBL) which inactivate penicillins, first to third generation cephalosporins and monobactams is one of the most widespread mechanisms of AMR. Recent literatures fully described ESBL-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-E), and their numbers are increasing in both low-, middle- and high-income countries [6–13]. However, the situation is alarming in limited-resource settings, where infectious diseases, poverty and malnutrition are constantly co-existents. In addition, hygiene conditions are still precarious and/or the use of antibiotics is often abused and poorly controlled [14]. In West African countries, particularly in Burkina Faso, the endemicity of respiratory infections, bacterial meningitis, diarrhea, other infectious diseases and livestock farming have increased the consumption of antibiotics for both symptomatic treatment and prophylaxis [15]. In addition, shortcomings in healthcare, particularly in terms of human resources and/or diagnostic capabilities, combined with unregulated access to antibiotics, contribute to exacerbate bacterial resistance [15].

Numerous individual studies have been carried out on the prevalence of AMR particularly ESBL-E in Burkina Faso [16–19]. However, to our knowledge, no systematic review of this type has been published to provide a comprehensive description of antibiotic resistance in Burkina Faso using One Health approach. This study aims to describe the antibiotic resistance in Burkina Faso, the status of ESBL-E according to the One Health approach and describe the gaps in surveillance and methodology.

Methods

Review Protocol and Registration

A systematic review protocol was developed according to PRISMA guidelines, and registered in PROSPERO under the reference CRD CRD42023429700 (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/recorddashboard#) [20].

Search Strategy

The literature search was based on inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria encompassed all articles published in French or English up to March 2023, and no earlier start date was defined. The additional inclusion criteria were research studies conducted in Burkina Faso (cross-sectional, cohort or surveillance), addressing bacterial resistance with antibiotic susceptibility testing and screening for ESBLs, cross-sectional surveys in healthcare facilities on any priority bacterial pathogen listed by the WHO [21], and bacteria isolated from human, animal and environmental samples.

The exclusion criteria were primarily editorials, reviews, experimental and veterinary studies, as well as articles on resistance that had not carried out BLSE testing.

The databases consulted were PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, African Journals Online and Hinari. The following search terms were used: "Antibiotics OR Antimicrobial " AND "Resistance OR Susceptibility OR ESBL OR Multiresistance" AND "Burkina Faso". Moreover, additional relevant publications were searched in a snowballing fashion, using reference lists of selected articles. The literature search was completed by scanning other libraries such as grey literature databases, including master's thesis repositories [22]. Two independent investigators checked the eligibility of retrieved articles based on title and abstract using Rayyan systematic review selection software (WALT and SY) [23]. Any difference was solved by consensus between both.

Data Extraction

The relevant data from the included studies were extracted in an Excel file (Microsoft Excel, version 16.105 (26011018) and comprise (i) general information such as title, first author, year of publication, and location, (ii) the design of the study (study type, sample type, sample size, hospital acquired or in the community, number of samples collected, type and number of isolated bacteria), (iii) the methods of bacterial identification, (iv) the details on antimicrobial susceptibility testing (methods, number of antibiotics, interpretation and standards used), and (v) data on antibacterial resistance (resistance to antibiotics, research of ESBL, presence of ESBL and number of ESBL). These data were extracted by WALT and checked by SY.

Risk of Bias Assessment

We assessed the risk of bias in the articles included in our study using Egger's test and the funnel plot. Microbiology Investigation Criteria for Reporting Objectively (MICRO) guidelines were used to appraise the quality of the individual study and the risk of bias [24]. The appraisal focused on MICRO checklist criteria to evaluate the methods of cumulative microbiology and antimicrobial resistance data. Two investigators (WALT and SY) independently completed the appraisal and presented the data in the traffic light model using green, yellow, red and grey color.

Data Analysis

A meta-analysis of proportions was performed to estimate the pooled prevalence of AMR for selected bacterial isolates. Analyses were stratified by isolate source including clinical (symptomatic or asymptomatic), environmental, and animal.

For selected bacterial species, additional analyses were conducted to calculate the pooled prevalence of resistance to specific antibiotics.

All meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models, with Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test (p < 0.05) and quantified by the I2 statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 with the ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’ packages.

General Characteristics of Included Studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis included a total of 24 studies, including 19 human, 03 environmental and 2 animal studies (Fig. 2). The included studies were published between 2009 and 2023, and all studies followed a cross-sectional study design. The samples were mainly taken from localities in the centre, west-centre, west, north-west and north of the country. The study areas represented were predominantly urban areas (Ouagadougou, Bobo Dioulasso, Gourcy, Boromo, Nouna and Koudougou) and a single rural area (Nanoro, a rural commune of Koudougou) (Fig. 1). According to the study area, 58.33% (14/24) of the studies were carried out in the capital city of Ouagadougou, followed by Bobo-Dioulasso 16.66% (4/24), Gourcy, Boromo and Nanoro with 12.5% (3/24) each, Nouna and Koudougou 4.16% (1/24). Of the 19 human studies included, 18 used various clinical samples for the detection of ESBL-E in patients suffering from multiple infections including gastroenteritis, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, surgical site infections, skin infections, etc. A study on humans investigated the carriage of ESBL-E in stools.Fig. 2Flow chart of literature search for systematic review

Frequency of Selected Bacterial Isolates

In this review, a total of 13 genera of Enterobacterales were extracted from the 24 included studies. However, the meta-analysis included six genera of Enterobacterales from human, environmental and animal studies. E. coli (24/24), Klebsiella species (18/24), Enterobacter species (11/24) and Salmonella species (8/24) were the most frequently reported species from the 3 samples sources.

Prevalence of ESBL-Producing Enterobacterales

In this study, the rate of ESBL-E was calculated according to the source of samples. The environment had the highest rate of ESBL-E (Fig. 3). The rate of ESBL-E was also calculated for each bacterial species. The overall prevalence in the selected bacteria was 27.69%. The highest proportion of ESBL production was recorded for E. coli (64.92%) in all sources. Salmonella species were ranked second among all clinical strains (50.57%). Among isolates from environmental studies, the highest proportion of ESBL production, after E. coli, was observed in Klebsiella species. The Pooled Prevalence of AMR for Selected Bacterial Isolates by Selected Antibiotics

The antibiotics selected were the most tested in the selected studies. They represent those commonly used in practice in Burkina Faso. Thus, E. coli was reported to have a high proportion of pooled resistance to tetracycline 81% (95% CI: 75%; 86%), ampicillin 78% (95% CI: 55%; 95%), amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 78% (95% CI: 56%; 95%) and trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 67% (95% CI: 35%; 93%). Discussion

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis showed that resistant Enterobacterales producing extended-spectrum beta-lactamases were present in Burkina Faso, in humans, animals, and the environment. However, the majority of studies was carried out in urban areas and much less in rural areas. This could be explained by the fact that like many other countries in Africa, rural areas in Burkina Faso lack infrastructures and equipment needed for microbiology studies [48, 49]. Published articles focused more on humans (19/24), while only a few dealt with the environment (03/24) and animals (2/24). Earlier reports have documented the same trends in South America and Ethiopia [50, 51]. This illustrates the lack of research studies in the field of antimicrobial resistance from animal and environmental sources, compared to that in humans in resource-limited settings. This means that the One Health concept is not yet well implemented and calls for advocacy for implementing multi-sectoral action plans as recommended by WHO [3]. However, there is a general awareness of the issues and growth in research in these areas in these countries.

The most commonly recorded Enterobacterales were E. coli, Salmonella spp., Klebsiella spp. and Enterobacter spp. Our results confirm the findings of other reviews conducted in Cameroon, Ghana and Ethiopia [51–53]. This can be explained by the fact that in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, these bacteria are most often incriminated in bacterial infections [26, 54–57]. In the assessed articles, ESBL-E were most frequently detected in environmental samples, followed by human and animal samples. These results are consistent with those reported from a study carried out in Bangladesh [58]. In opposite, reviews from Ethiopia and South America reported different results [50, 51]. Indeed, in Ethiopia, ESBL-E was most frequently detected in human samples, whereas in South America it was mainly found in animal samples. This difference could be explained by the type of environmental samples collected in our study, which were mainly wastewater, and also by the differences in study design and population compared to that of Ethiopia and South America, where environmental samples were mainly swabs taken from hospital and mobile phone contact surfaces, indoor air and wastewater/river water. The presence of ESBL-E in humans, animals and environment represents a threatening health risk, as these isolates can be transferred from one component to another. Infections due to ESBL-E are usually difficult to treat and impair with high morbidity and mortality in patients [59]. Among the selected bacteria, E. coli was the most prevalent in all sources. Salmonella species were ranked second among symptomatic clinical strains. In the environment, Klebsiella species ranked second after E. coli. In animals, only ESBL-E. coli was recorded. Our results are in line with previous reports from sub-Saharan Africa in general, and particularly from East Africa (Ethiopia), Central Africa (Cameroon) and West Africa (Ghana) [51, 53, 60, 61].

Our study recorded high AMR prevalences in all sources, E. coli being the most represented, followed by Salmonella spp. and Klebsiella spp. Among the selected antibiotics, those associated with very high bacterial resistance rates were ampicillin, amoxicillin + clavulanic acid, trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole and tetracycline (rates of over 80%). Ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin showed moderate levels of resistance and gentamycin was the most active antibiotic (on average 3% and 15%). Our results confirm the findings from other studies reporting high resistance to ampicillin, amoxicillin + clavulanic acid and trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole. In Cameroon, for example, 65.6% of E. coli were resistant to ampicillin, 63.3% to amoxicillin + clavulanic acid and 85.2% to trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole, while Klebsiella species showed a resistance rate of 76.1% for ampicillin and 78.3% for trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole. Similarly, a study conducted in Ethiopia showed 89% resistance rate of E. coli species to ampicillin, 77% to amoxicillin + clavulanic acid and 83% to trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole. Resistance rates to these antibiotics were 66%, 61% and 63% respectively for Salmonella species and 71%, 80% and 82% respectively for Klebsiella species. A study carried out in Africa in general showed resistance rates of 86.7%, 43.5% and 80.7% respectively to the same antibiotics for E. coli species. Salmonella and Klebsiella species showed resistance rates of 57.8%, 6.1%, 52.8% respectively and 100%, 55.4%, 67.8%, respectively to the same antibiotics, while another study in sub-Saharan Africa showed resistance rates to ampicillin and trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole for E. coli (87% and 80% respectively), Salmonella spp. (91% for both) and Klebsiella spp. (88% and 75%, respectively) [51, 52, 62, 63]. However, there were reports with higher resistance rates than those reported in our study for ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone and gentamycin in East Africa (E. coli (72%, 77%, 81%, respectively), Klebsiella spp. (66%, 92%, 92%, respectively)) and Ghana (E. coli (89%, 78%, 62%, respectively), Salmonella spp. (67%, 22%, 67%, respectively), Klebsiella spp. (76%, 91%, 83%, respectively)) [53, 60]. In addition, WHO review of the African region reported resistance rates lower than those reported in our study [64]. This variation of resistance rates from one study to another can be attributed to the difference in methodologies, study population, the extent of antibiotic use and the study periods. Overall, our study revealed that the level of resistance of bacteria isolated from humans, animals and the environment is high for antibiotics commonly used in Burkina Faso. The causes of the increase in AMR include the misuse of antibiotics, such as inappropriate prescribing, self-medication, the lack of hygiene in health care facilities and in community, and the use of antibiotics in livestock farming and agriculture [8]. The close interaction between humans, animals and their environment open the door to the transfer of resistant strains, and thus to the spread of AMR. Therefore, the One Health approach, which integrates all these fields, is relevant and essential in the fight against AMR. This approach has been recommended by WHO and adopted by many countries although it remains scarcely implemented mostly in sub-Saharan Africa. In such context, the national stakeholders (human, environment and animal health policy makers) are encouraged to work together in One Health approach for an effective containment of AMR.

According to the WHO recommendations and the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance, Burkina Faso developed and validated its multisectoral national action plan in 2017. All key sectors, namely human health, agriculture, environment, and animal health, contributed to the development of this plan and defined areas of action to enable the implementation of AMR surveillance and the improvement of antimicrobial use through five strategic objectives [65]. Strategic objective 1 focuses on strengthening surveillance and research on AMR and antimicrobial use, and efforts are being made to achieve this objective. However, this action plan is poorly implemented as only 22 sentinel laboratories have been set up for passive surveillance of AMR throughout the country (National symposium on resistance 2024) and published data show a worrying increase in resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics in the country [66, 67]. Moreover, not all sectors are yet covered by surveillance as only one veterinary laboratory is involved in this surveillance system while for the environment and agriculture sectors, monitoring is not yet implemented. In addition, the existing data are poorly documented as only hospital data are considered and then, many data generated by university hospitals, research centers and universities are not taken into account in this national surveillance system. Another bottleneck is the lack of link between different sectors as each sector is financed individually and works independently according to the objectives of the moment. A multi-sectoral structure for the fight against AMR with a single funding source could be an efficient approach for the coordination of the AMR surveillance. Nevertheless, the system should carefully consider the issue regarding the accuracy and completeness of the data as this may be biased due to incorrectly filled prescriptions and self-medication [68]. Considering the latter, the use of sales data may be underestimated due to the presence of informal vendors, which is not negligible, especially in rural areas [69]. Finally, there is the failure to comply with regulations on the sale of antibiotics, which are classified as class 1 drugs and must not be sold without a prescription [70].

WHO has sounded the alarm on AMR and considers it a crisis that must be urgently managed. The global action plan highlighted the need for a One Health approach, involving coordination between several sectors and players, including human and animal health, agriculture, finance, environment and well-informed prescribers and consumers. However, setting up an adequate surveillance system requires close collaboration between all these sectors and players, capacity building and strong political will.

Our study had some limitations. The results of animal and environmental studies were recent data with a small number of articles (n = 02 and n = 03, respectively). A higher number of articles and earlier studies could yield different results. We were also unable to analyse resistance trends by year due to the heterogeneity of sample origins and the number of articles included. Finally, the studies conducted and included did not cover all regions of the country.

Individual Study Appraisal

The bias assessment using color coding for relevant elements of the reports included in this review provides an overview of the articles evaluated according to the checklist proposed by MICRO. Overall, the titles and abstracts, objectives, details of data collection, sample size, and microorganisms investigated were considered as good. Microbiological methods such as identification, antibiogram, and quality control were acceptable. Most reports did not provide a definition of resistance and then they were scored poor for this item. The definition of multidrug-resistant bacteria and their detection, as well as the presentation and discussion of study limitations, were generally not provided.

Conclusion

This study shows a high prevalence of ESBL-E and important resistance to antibiotics commonly used in humans, animals, and environment, particularly in E. coli, Klebsiella spp., Salmonella spp., and Enterobacter spp. The presence of ESBL-producing bacteria in the various components is a major public health issue, given their interactions and their implications for population health. This review highlights the need for comprehensive AMR surveillance in Burkina Faso in order to identify the exact action of ESBL-E in all components of the One Health approach. This will enable the effective achievement of objective 1 of the national action plan. The information provided by this meta-analysis can serve as a basis for future AMR surveillance initiatives, including human, animal and environmental health in the country.
