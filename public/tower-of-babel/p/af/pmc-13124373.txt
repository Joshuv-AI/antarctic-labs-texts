Quality and reliability of Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili: A cross-sectional content analysis study
Authors: Xingzhan Zhang, Yunjing Mai, Chao Li, Zhangheng Li, Ling Zhao, Jie Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Social media platforms have become important channels for public access to health information. Recent studies have evaluated stroke-related videos on TikTok, Bilibili, and other platforms; however, evidence focusing specifically on Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the content, quality, and reliability of Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili. In this cross-sectional study, the quality and reliability of Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili were evaluated on October 2, 2025. Video duration, engagement metrics, and uploader identity were collected. The Global Quality Scale (GQS) and modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) tools were used to assess video quality and reliability. Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for group comparisons, and Spearman rank correlation was used for correlation analysis. A total of 186 videos were included. The videos primarily focused on clinical manifestations (21.33%) and treatment (18.86%), with limited content on prognosis (9.35%). The median GQS score was 3.0 (interquartile range: 2.0–3.0), and the median mDISCERN score was 2.0 (interquartile range: 2.0–3.0). There were no significant differences in GQS and mDISCERN scores between platforms (P > .05). Videos uploaded by specialized healthcare professionals had the highest GQS and mDISCERN scores (P < .05). There was no correlation between engagement metrics (likes, comments, shares) and video quality (P > .05). The overall quality of ischemic stroke-related short videos on social media platforms is suboptimal, and professional background significantly influences video quality and reliability. Future efforts should strengthen content supervision on platforms and optimize health information dissemination strategies to enhance the accuracy of ischemic stroke-related video content.

1. Introduction

Ischemic stroke, caused by arterial occlusion resulting in cerebral ischemia and infarction, accounts for approximately 70% of all stroke cases and remains a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide.[1] In 2021, the global prevalence of ischemic stroke reached an estimated 69.9 million, with 7.8 million new cases and 3.6 million deaths, contributing to more than 70 million disability-adjusted life years. The disease burden continues to rise, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, imposing substantial pressure on healthcare systems and society.[2] Early recognition, timely intervention, and standardized management are key to improving outcomes.[3] However, public awareness of stroke symptoms, emergency response, and rehabilitation strategies remains limited,[4] underscoring the need for effective health education and dissemination of reliable information.

In the past, newspapers and news outlets were the primary channels for disseminating health information. The advent of digital technologies has transformed this landscape, leading to a growing reliance on online platforms by patients seeking health-related knowledge.[5] With the rapid evolution of digital media and mobile internet technologies, social media platforms have become crucial tools for health communication.[6] Among them, TikTok (known as Douyin in mainland China) and Bilibili have emerged as leading short-video platforms that enable rapid and large-scale dissemination of medical content through algorithmic recommendation systems and extensive user engagement.[7,8] Despite these advantages, the lack of standardized verification and medical content review raises concerns regarding information accuracy, reliability, and balance.[9] A study assessing the quality of hypertension-related videos on a video-sharing platform revealed that, although the majority were produced by healthcare professionals, their overall quality remained at a moderate level, primarily due to short video length, insufficient source citation, and a lack of content rigor.[10] Studies on cataract and prostate cancer-related videos have found that these videos often have high engagement, but their quality and reliability remain suboptimal.[11,12] Recent studies have increasingly examined stroke-related videos on social media platforms. Nie et al evaluated stroke-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili in the Chinese context and found substantial variation in content quality across platforms.[13] Shu et al further compared stroke-relevant videos on YouTube, Bilibili, and TikTok, highlighting platform-related differences in dissemination characteristics.[14] Wang et al also reported marked variability in the quality of stroke-related TikTok videos and showed that user engagement did not necessarily correspond to information quality.[15] However, these studies mainly focused on stroke broadly rather than ischemic stroke specifically, and their analytic frameworks varied considerably in terms of platform selection, language inclusion, and disease scope. Some were limited to a single platform and English-language videos, whereas others combined multiple platforms, stroke subtypes, and languages within the same analysis. Therefore, evidence specifically addressing Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos on major Chinese video platforms remains limited. Given that Mandarin is the national common language in China and has broad population coverage, focusing on Mandarin-language content may improve linguistic homogeneity and enhance the practical relevance of findings for public health communication in mainland China.[16]

Accordingly, this study analyzed Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili to assess their thematic distribution, quality, and reliability; compare differences across uploader groups; and explore correlations between engagement metrics and information quality.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design, data sources, and retrieval strategy

This study was designed as a cross-sectional content analysis of Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos on TikTok (https://www.douyin.com) and Bilibili (https://www.bilibili.com). A systematic search was conducted on October 2, 2025, using the keyword “ischemic stroke.” To minimize potential bias introduced by personalized recommendation algorithms, newly registered accounts were used, and browser history and cache were cleared prior to the search. The first 100 videos returned by each platform in the default search order were collected. The video selection process is summarized in Figure 1.

Flowchart of video retrieval and selection. Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos were searched on TikTok and Bilibili using the keywords “缺血性脑卒中” and “脑梗死.” The top 100 videos from each platform were screened. On TikTok, 3 irrelevant videos and 3 advertisements were excluded, leaving 94 videos. On Bilibili, 6 irrelevant videos and 2 duplicate videos were excluded, leaving 92 videos. A total of 186 videos were included in the final analysis.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Videos were included if they met the following criteria: related to ischemic stroke, presented in Mandarin Chinese, and original and publicly accessible. Videos were excluded if they were irrelevant to ischemic stroke, contained commercial or promotional content, or were duplicates.

2.3. Data extraction and variable definition

This study collected the following information for each video: title, uploader identity, platform, upload date, video duration, engagement metrics, and video content. Uploader identity was categorized into specialized healthcare professionals (SHPs), nonspecialized healthcare professionals (non-SHPs), and general users. SHPs were defined as neurology-related healthcare professionals with relevant specialty training or clinical expertise in cerebrovascular disease, such as neurologists, stroke specialists, or neurology nurses. Non-SHPs were defined as healthcare professionals without a neurology specialty, including but not limited to cardiologists, rehabilitation therapists, radiologists, and emergency physicians. General users were defined as uploaders without professional healthcare credentials, including patients, caregivers, and other lay content creators. Uploader categories such as news agencies or nonprofit organizations were not analyzed separately because they were infrequently encountered in the retrieved dataset and therefore were not retained as independent uploader categories in the final analysis. Uploader identity was determined based on publicly available profile information on each platform, including account name, profile description, and verification status (if available). When uploader identity was unclear, classification was determined through consensus between 2 reviewers. Engagement metrics were extracted for each video, including the number of likes, collections, comments, and shares, as provided by the platform at the time of data collection. These variables were recorded as absolute values without normalization. Engagement metrics were treated as continuous variables and analyzed using the Spearman rank correlation to assess their associations with video quality and reliability scores. Video content was coded into 6 predefined thematic domains: epidemiology, etiology, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. A multiple-coding approach was used for thematic analysis; therefore, a single video could be assigned to more than 1 thematic domain when it contained relevant information across multiple content areas. Personal experience-based content shared by patients or caregivers was not treated as a separate thematic category. When such videos contained disease-related information, all applicable thematic domains were coded. Videos consisting primarily of personal narratives without substantive disease-related informational content were not assigned to an independent thematic domain. The thematic coding framework was developed with reference to the 2024 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association Guideline for the Primary Prevention of Stroke and the 2019 update to the 2018 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association Guidelines for the Early Management of Acute Ischemic Stroke,[17,18] which were used to guide the classification of clinically relevant stroke-related information domains.

2.4. Quality and reliability assessment

Video quality was assessed using the Global Quality Scale (GQS),[19] a 5-point ordinal scale used to evaluate the overall quality, logical flow, and usefulness of online health content, particularly in the context of patient education. Scores ranged from 1 (very poor) to 5 (excellent), with higher scores indicating better quality and greater educational value. Video reliability was assessed using the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), an adapted version of the original Decision-making Information Support Criteria for Evaluating the Reliability of Non-randomized Studies tool for written health information,[20,21] The mDISCERN consists of 5 items assessing clarity of objectives, citation of credible sources, objectivity, balanced presentation of risks and benefits, and provision of supplementary information. In this survey, each positive response was assigned a score of 1 and each negative response a score of 0, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater reliability of the video content. Both tools have been widely applied in research assessing the quality and reliability of health-related information disseminated through social media and video-sharing platforms.[22,23] GQS and mDISCERN were selected because they provide complementary assessment of 2 key dimensions relevant to short-video health information: overall educational quality and informational reliability. GQS has been widely used to evaluate the overall quality, flow, and usefulness of patient-oriented health videos, whereas mDISCERN is a concise and commonly used tool for assessing reliability. By contrast, the Journal of the American Medical Association benchmark criteria primarily assess source-related transparency, including authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency, which may be more applicable to webpage-based or text-based health information than to short-form video content. Therefore, GQS and mDISCERN were considered more suitable for the primary objectives of the present study.[24] The full scoring criteria for GQS and mDISCERN are provided in Tables 1 and 2. Before the formal evaluation, both reviewers (XZ and JP) received training and calibration based on the predefined scoring criteria and relevant stroke guidelines. Each video was independently assessed for GQS, mDISCERN, and thematic content classification, and disagreements were resolved by discussion with adjudication by a third senior reviewer (LZ) when necessary. Inter-rater reliability, indicating the consistency between reviewers, was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) before consensus scoring.[25]

2.5. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the included videos. For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between platforms were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons of parameters between different uploader groups were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Associations between engagement metrics and quality or reliability scores were assessed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ). All P values were two-tailed, with P < .05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and plots were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

2.6. Ethics statement

This study analyzed publicly accessible videos on TikTok and Bilibili and did not involve interaction with human participants or access to personally identifiable information. Therefore, formal ethics committee or institutional review board approval was not required. Informed consent was also not required because all analyzed materials were publicly available and no individual-level private data were collected.

3. Results

3.1. General characteristics of ischemic stroke-related videos

A total of 186 short videos related to ischemic stroke were included in this study, of which 49.46% were retrieved from Bilibili and 50.54% from TikTok (Fig. 2). The median video duration was 192.00 seconds (IQR: 75.00–1502.50). In terms of engagement metrics, the median numbers of likes, collections, comments, and shares were 82.50 (IQR: 27.25–322.00), 61.50 (IQR: 18.25–269.75), 4.00 (IQR: 1.00–16.50), and 28.00 (IQR: 10.00–136.75), respectively. Regarding video quality and reliability, the median GQS score was 3.00 (IQR: 2.00–3.00), and the mDISCERN score was 2.00 (IQR: 2.00–3.00). Detailed descriptive statistics are presented in Table 3. When compared between platforms, Bilibili videos were significantly longer in duration and received fewer likes and comments than TikTok videos (all P < .01), whereas no significant differences were observed in GQS or mDISCERN scores between platforms (both P > .05; Table 4). The included TikTok videos were uploaded between June 2023 and October 2025, whereas the included Bilibili videos were uploaded between September 2023 and October 2025.

3.2. Inter-rater reliability

Inter-rater reliability was assessed across all 186 included videos. Agreement between the 2 reviewers was good to excellent for GQS (ICC = 0.79, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.763–0.814), mDISCERN (ICC = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.801–0.832), and thematic content classification (ICC = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.882–0.920).

3.3. Content distribution of ischemic stroke-related videos

Thematic distribution analysis showed that etiology (18.86%), clinical manifestations (21.33%), diagnosis (18.35%), and treatment (18.86%) were the most frequently addressed topics in ischemic stroke-related videos. In contrast, content concerning epidemiology (13.25%) and prognosis (9.35%) was relatively underrepresented (Table 3). The distribution of video content across the 2 platforms is shown in Figure 3.

3.4. Uploader characteristics

Differences in the distribution of uploader types were observed across platforms. On TikTok, SHPs constituted the largest proportion (55%), followed by non-SHPs (23%) and general users (21%). In contrast, on Bilibili, SHPs accounted for 43%, while non-SHPs and general users represented 33% and 24%, respectively (Fig. 4).

3.5. Comparison of video quality and reliability across different uploaders

Overall differences in video characteristics, quality, and reliability among uploader groups are summarized in Table 5. Figure 5 shows the distribution of GQS (Fig. 5A) and mDISCERN (Fig. 5B) scores among different uploader groups. Significant differences in video quality and reliability were observed across uploader groups. Videos produced by SHPs achieved significantly higher GQS and mDISCERN scores compared with those created by non-SHPs and general users (both P < .05). Specifically, the median GQS score for SHPs’ videos was 3.0 (IQR: 3.0–4.0), compared with 2.0 (IQR: 2.0–3.0) for non-SHPs and 2.0 (IQR: 2.0–3.0) for general users (P < .001). Similarly, the median mDISCERN scores were 3.0 (IQR: 2.0–3.0) for SHPs, 2.5 (IQR: 2.0–3.0) for non-SHPs, and 2.0 (IQR: 2.0–3.0) for general users (P < .001). Figures 6A and 6B further present the comparative results of GQS and mDISCERN scores among different groups, respectively. No statistically significant differences were found between non-SHPs and general users in either GQS or mDISCERN scores.

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis evaluated the content, engagement characteristics, and informational quality of ischemic stroke-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili. The content of the videos showed insufficient coverage of epidemiology and prognosis, with overall quality and reliability being suboptimal. Videos uploaded by SHPs exhibited the highest quality and reliability. Engagement metrics were not correlated with video quality. Several alternative explanations may account for these findings. For example, the underrepresentation of epidemiology-and prognosis-related content may reflect the short-video format, which tends to favor brief, symptom- or treatment-oriented messages over more comprehensive educational topics. The higher quality observed in SHP-uploaded videos may be attributable to their formal medical training and greater familiarity with evidence-based clinical information. In addition, engagement metrics may be influenced by platform algorithms, audiovisual presentation style, and audience entertainment preferences rather than by informational quality alone. These findings highlight the deficiencies in ischemic stroke-related videos and provide a preliminary reference for optimizing health information strategies.

Several recent studies have evaluated stroke-related videos on social media platforms.[13–15] The present study extends this literature by focusing specifically on Mandarin-language ischemic stroke-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili. This narrower scope may be important because ischemic stroke has distinct educational priorities, including symptom recognition, timely treatment, secondary prevention, and prognosis communication. In addition, restricting the analysis to Mandarin-language content may improve linguistic homogeneity and enhance the relevance of the findings to public health communication in mainland China.

In this study, the most frequently coded thematic domains were etiology (18.86%), clinical manifestations (21.33%), diagnosis (18.35%), and treatment (18.86%), while insufficient attention was given to epidemiology (13.25%) and prognosis (9.35%). The lack of comprehensive dissemination of diagnostic and prognostic information may lead to the public having an incomplete understanding of ischemic stroke prevention and treatment, thereby affecting the adoption of healthy behaviors.[26–28] Moreover, although ischemic stroke can reduce symptoms with timely treatment, failure to diagnose it promptly can lead to severe sequelae, such as long-term neurological impairment, mobility issues, and cognitive decline.[29] Therefore, future short-video content should place greater emphasis on the early diagnosis, epidemiology, and prognosis of ischemic stroke to ensure that the public receives more comprehensive and practical health information.[30]

No significant difference in video quality or reliability was identified between TikTok and Bilibili in this study, suggesting increasing similarity in content across the 2 platforms. Bilibili generally attracts younger, academically oriented users and promotes educationally driven content,[8] whereas TikTok’s algorithm emphasizes rapid dissemination and visually engaging presentation.[10] The observed convergence likely reflects improved public health awareness and increasing platform-level content moderation.[31] Collaboration between social media companies and health authorities to establish verified medical content certification systems may further enhance the visibility of reliable sources and foster an evidence-based information ecosystem.[32]

Significant differences in video quality and reliability were observed among the different uploader groups in this study. Videos created by SHPs received significantly higher GQS and mDISCERN scores compared with those produced by non-SHPs or general users, reinforcing the notion that professional medical training is a key determinant of informational accuracy.[33] In a cross-sectional study analyzing TikTok and Bilibili videos on gastroesophageal reflux disease, Cai et al found that videos produced by healthcare professionals demonstrated significantly higher completeness, informational credibility, and educational value than those uploaded by nonprofessional users.[34] Similarly, Gong et al reported that physician-generated videos on coronary heart disease exhibited greater reliability and adherence to evidence-based information compared with videos produced by lay users.[35] Both studies highlighted the decisive role of medical expertise and professional training in determining the scientific quality and reliability of health-related short videos. However, despite outperforming other uploader groups, SHP-generated videos still achieved only moderate ratings (median GQS = 3.0; median mDISCERN = 3.0). This finding suggests that even professionally produced short videos often lack sufficient depth, proper citation, and an evidence-based structure. The inherent brevity of short-video formats may limit the ability to comprehensively address clinical topics, leading to informational compression and reduced educational value. Introducing a tiered review and recommendation system, along with greater involvement of healthcare professionals, could enhance both the scientific rigor and visibility of high-quality content.[36]

This study found no significant correlation between engagement metrics and GQS or mDISCERN scores, which is consistent with previous reports.[8,23] This may lead to the rapid spread of erroneous, false, or sensationalist information.[37] Although such content may attract more views, likes, or shares, it may mislead the public, highlighting the need for algorithms that prioritize content quality and reliability. This discrepancy between engagement and content quality poses a challenge for digital health communication, emphasizing the importance of strengthening content verification mechanisms and promoting evidence-based medical information. A weak correlation was observed between video length and video quality, which may suggest that appropriately extending video duration could help improve video quality.[38] While the brief nature of videos facilitates rapid dissemination, it may also limit the depth and structure of information, leading to content compression and reduced educational value.[22] Future content creators and platforms should consider balancing video length and information quality to ensure that even within short-video formats, the scientific accuracy and practical value of the information are fully conveyed.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to TikTok and Bilibili, excluding other platforms such as YouTube, Kuaishou, and Xiaohongshu, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, as a cross-sectional study, it was unable to assess the dynamic changes in content quality. Third, despite using standardized tools (GQS and mDISCERN), the rating process inevitably introduced subjectivity. Finally, the small sample size of this study may limit the representativeness of the conclusions. Future research should include larger and more diverse video samples, evaluate changes in content quality over time, and examine how platform features, uploader characteristics, and audience preferences influence the dissemination and impact of ischemic stroke-related health information.

5. Conclusion

This study found that ischemic stroke-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili were of suboptimal overall quality and reliability, with insufficient coverage of epidemiology and prognosis. Videos produced by SHPs exhibited the highest quality and reliability. Engagement metrics were not correlated with video quality. These findings highlight the need for improved verification mechanisms, algorithmic prioritization of evidence-based content, and enhanced digital health literacy. Future efforts should strengthen content supervision of ischemic stroke-related videos and encouragegreater participation of SHPs in public education on ischemic stroke.
