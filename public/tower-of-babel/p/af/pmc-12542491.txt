Genome loci with selection signatures revealed in a domesticated mandarin fish population
Authors: Chengfei Sun, Yunyun Yan, Yuan Zhang, Junjian Dong, Xiao Chen, Fengying Gao, Hetong Zhang, Ziyang Wang, Chengbin Wu, Xing Ye, Fubao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial selection enables the targeted alteration of allele frequencies within a population, leading to the consolidation and enhancement of beneficial variations and the stable genetic development of new varieties. Mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) is a commercially important freshwater fish species in China. Through four generations of artificial selection targeting growth rate and survival rate as key traits, our team developed the new variety “Guangqing No. 1”. To identify the genomic loci subjected to selection during the artificial selection process, we conducted whole-genome resequencing on 150 individuals, comprising two wild populations of S. chuatsi (HN and AH) as the base populations and the selectively bred population “Guangqing No. 1”.ResultsA total of 3.90 million high-quality SNPs were identified across the three groups. Analysis of population structure and genetic diversity revealed that the individuals were divided into three groups, consistent with their geographical origins. Moreover, the selectively bred population was subjected to more intensive selection and exhibited reduced genetic diversity. Using Fst, Pi ratio, and XP-EHH, this study identified 71 significantly selected regions in S. chuatsi populations, along with 424 candidate genes potentially under selection. Among the candidate genes identified through selection loci analysis, some are closely associated with key economic traits such as growth, immunity, reproduction, and feeding (e.g., pfkp, igf1, Ig, il4i1, npy2r, omp, lrat, c2orf71, sept12).ConclusionsIn this study, we identified valuable candidate genes and selection markers for the artificial selection of S. chuatsi, offering valuable insights and guidance for future aquatic breeding efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12145-0.Keywords: Artificial selection, Genetic structure, Genome-wide SNPs, Selection signatures, Siniperca chuatsi

Background

The process by which humans utilise and modify nature involves the artificial selection and domestication of animals and plants, leading to significant improvements in economic traits such as growth, disease resistance, and stress tolerance. Through targeted changes in the genetic frequency of populations, artificial selection alters the types of organisms, disrupting the randomness of natural reproduction and rapidly forming various breeds. By accumulating changes in allele frequencies across generations, beneficial variations can be consolidated and strengthened, thus stabilising their inheritance [1]. Artificial selection is extensively applied in aquaculture, helping improve aquatic species, increase the yield and quality of breeds, and enhance the efficiency of cultivation [2]. In China, the Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), after six generations of artificial selection, achieved significant genetic improvements in growth compared to the base population imported from Vietnam, with an increase of 60%–90% in body weight at harvest [3]. Similarly, after five generations of artificial selection, the Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) exhibited better growth rates, feed conversion ratios, and protein and energy retention rates than the wild populations [4]. These reports demonstrate that artificial selection has achieved notable genetic improvement in fish growth traits.

Altering an organism’s type through artificial selection fundamentally involves directed and purposeful changes in allele frequencies. Conducting artificial selection necessitates understanding the population’s genetic background and diversity. With the rapid advancement of next-generation sequencing technology, genome resequencing of different individuals or populations and comparing these results can reveal extensive variation information [5]. By comparing genome sequences and structures both between and within species, researchers can understand the functions of genes, their expression mechanisms, and the relationships in species evolution while discovering new genes and genetic markers and analysing the phenotypic variations of species [6]. Using whole-genome resequencing technology, Zhang et al. (2023) [7] examined genetic variations in wild grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) populations from the Yangtze and Pearl Rivers, as well as in its gynogenetic populations; using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers to provide genomic evidence of genetic diversity and selection loci, they found that compared to the wild populations, the gynogenetic populations exhibit higher genetic diversity and a higher degree of linkage disequilibrium (LD). Farmed populations of red snapper (Lutjanus campechanus) grow faster than their wild counterparts, and genome sequencing of three red snapper populations from different fish farms compared to that of the wild populations revealed the impact of artificial selection on their genetic diversity [8].

These genes and loci, serving as candidate markers for artificial selection, facilitate the selection of individuals with superior traits, enhancing the efficiency of selection for target traits [9]. In the Atlantic salmon, selection loci were identified by analysing three types of complementary statistical data across four Atlantic salmon populations with a common genetic origin, and this method identified several gene-containing regions associated with growth and immunity [10]. Whole-genome resequencing in three farmed Nile tilapia lines identified the latest selection loci, with candidate genes associated with growth, early development, and immunity detected in each population [11]. Thus, population genetics provides basic information for artificial selection, and identification of selection loci offers candidate markers for the screening of target traits. The application of these methods can efficiently facilitate artificial selection in aquaculture.

Mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) is an important species in China’s freshwater aquaculture, with artificial selection enhancing its economic yield. Through artificial selection, several new varieties of S. chuatsi have been developed. Current research on the artificial selection of S. chuatsi primarily focuses on analysing the genetic diversity of selectively bred populations [12] and identifying genes and loci associated with growth traits [13–16]. However, the research on genomic regions of candidate genes and their relationship with economic traits is still nascent due to a lack of analysis on selection loci in S. chuatsi, leaving the markers and genes available for breeding S. chuatsi quite limited. In prior research, our team developed the new variety “Guangqing No. 1” (GQ) through four generations of artificial selection of wild populations from Anhui and Hunan provinces as the base breeding populations, achieving significant improvements in growth rate and survival rate [17]. Focusing on the GQ population, this study compared it with the initial base breeding populations from Anhui and Hunan using whole-genome resequencing to identify selected loci at the genome level, providing basic data on the markers and candidate selection loci for S. chuatsi breeding and offering valuable insights for aquaculture breeding.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 150 matured S. chuatsi were sampled. The wild population of Anhui (AH, 50 individuals) comes from Qiupu Special Aquatic Development Co., Ltd. (Chizhou, Anhui, China). The wild population of Hunan (HN, 50 individuals) comes from the Hunan Provincial Aquatic Seed Farm (Changsha, Hunan, China). The artificially bred population “Guangqing 1” (GQ, 50 individuals) comes from Yushun Agriculture, Animal Husbandry and Fisheries Technology Service Co., Ltd. in Qingxin District, Qingyuan City (Qingyuan, Guangdong, China). In this study, “Guangqing 1” was a new variety obtained through four consecutive generations of breeding based on wild populations from Anhui and Hunan provinces by family selection. A small portion of the caudal fin tissue from each individual was sheared under 2-phenoxyethanol anesthesia, and stored in ethanol absolute for DNA extraction.

Genome resequencing and variants calling

All 150 samples were re-sequenced with a sequencing depth of 30× by DNBSEQ from BGI genomics. Raw reads were filtered by using SOAPnuke (v2.1.0) [18] with the following criteria: (i) adaptor sequences were removed; (ii) low quality bases and reads containing more than 10% N bases were discarded. The obtained clean reads should be Q20 >90% and Q30 >80%. The clean reads were then mapped to S.chuatsi reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_020085105.1) [19] by using BWA-mem (v0.7.17) function with default parameters [20]. The mapped results were sorted with SAMtools (v1.9) [21]. GATK (v4.0, HaplotypeCaller function) [22] was employed for variant calling of SNPs and InDels by following the best-practice pipeline. In order to obtain high quality variants, the SNPs were filtered with the following parameters: (i) QD < 2.0, FS >60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < − 12.5 and ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0; (ii) missing rate < 0.2, and coverage depth >4 and < 100; (iii) minor allele frequency (MAF) >0.01, performed using PLINK (v1.9.0) with a window size of 10 kb, step size of one SNP, and r2 threshold of 0.5 [23].

Population genetics analysis

The kinship coefficients between the 50 individuals sampled from each population was calculated using GCTA (v1.94.0) to avoid full- or half-siblings [24]. Principle component analysis (PCA) was conducted with PLINK (v1.90) using whole-genome SNPs. Model-based population structure was analyzed by using ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) [25], where the number of ancestry populations of the individuals was determined by using a fivefold cross-validation strategy. The likelyhood of ancestral kinship (K) from 2 to 8 was tested using all SNPs. A Neighbour-joining tree (NJ tree) was constructed with 100 boostraps using MEGA (v11.0.13) [26]. To exploit the population difference, we evaluated the nucleotide diversity (π) of the individuals from different populations inferred by the population structure analysis mentioned above in a 10-kb nonoverlapping window using VCFtools (v0.1.16) [27], and by calculating Fst between populations with a 10-kb nonoverlapping window as well. The LD (calculated as r2) of each population was calculated using all SNPs by PopLDdecay with the default parameters [28].

Selective sweeps during modern breeding

A XP-EHH statistics (detection of selective sweeps based on extended haplotype blocks) was used to scan for the genome-wide selective sweeps during modern breeding. We used all wild fish populations (AH and HN) as a reference and the GQ popilation as a query to identify potential selective sweeps that occurred during modern breeding, using Selscan (v1.3.0) [29]. To further confirm the results, we also respectively used the AH population and HN population as a reference and the GQ as a query for detecting selective sweeps. We normalized the raw XP-EHH values into z-score and calculated the p-values of the normalized XP-EHH values as they have followed normal distribution. As XP-EHH evaluates selective sweeps by exploiting selective centers by identifying selective SNPs, selective SNPs within 200 kb were merged into one selective sweep. To improve the confidence of the selective sweeps, we also calculated Fst and π-ratio between reference and query populations by slide window approach with a window size of 10 kb and a step of 5 kb. The π-ratio, Fst and XP-EHH values were sorted from large to small, with the top 5% of the significant score SNPs (p-value < 0.05) selected sweeps disignated as candidate regions under positive selection. Genes directly located on the selective sweeps were identified the candidate selected genes.

Functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes

For functional enrichment analysis, we re-annotated the function of all annotated genes in the reference genome with eggNOG-mapper (v2) with default settings [30]. All genes were then used as the background and candidate selected genes were used as the foreground for performing Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis with the OmicShare tool, an online platform for data analysis (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/Home/Soft/enrich_circle) [31].

Results

Variants filtering and annotation

The whole-genome resequencing performed on 150 S. chuatsi (two wild population and one breeding populaions, n = 50 for each population) generated a total of 51.72 billion 150 bp paired-end clean reads (Table S1). We totally obtained initial 7.02 M variants (5,264,947 SNPs and 1,758,237 InDels) after basic filtering. SNPs were further filtered, with 3,897,498 high-quality SNPs retained for the following analysis. The 50 individuals sampled from each population were unrelated individuals. The SNP density plot was shown in Fig. 1.

Density of SNPs in all chromosomes

Base on the annotation of SNPs, we observed that most of the SNPs (60.459%) were annotated to intron regions, 7.437% were localized in intergenic regions (Table S2). The ratio of nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitutions is 0.71 (Table S3).

Population genetics analysis

Based on the PCA plot, we observed that the individuals were grouped based on their origin locations: AH, HN and GQ (Fig. 2A). In addition, Neighbour-joining tree (NJ tree) constructed by all variants showed that all individuals were clustered into three populations as well (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, model-based method implemented in the ADMIXTURE was additionally employed for inferring population structure and genome components (Fig. 2C and D). We observed that the cross-validation error for determining the number of ancestry populations has lowest value at K = 3, suggesting these individuals potentially originated from three ancestry populations. At K = 3, in according to the PCA results, the individuals were grouped based on their origin locations: AH, HN and GQ, where we observed that each of the individuals could be assigned to one of the three groups and the group generally contributed more than 70% of the genome. In sum, our PCA analysis, population structure inference and NJ-tree simultaneously supported that the individuals were grouped into three populations which subject to their origin locations. Base on the population structure, the HN population is more diverse than the AH and GQ populations, and GQ population potentially shared part of the genome background with HN population.

Population genetics analysis. A PCA plot of all sequenced individuals. B Neighbour-joining tree of all sequenced individuals constructed from all genome variants. (A&B share the legend. Red represents AH, green represents GQ, while blue for HN population.) C and D Population structure analysis (C cross-validation analysis showed potential ancestry populations. D population structure of all sequenced individuals)

In agree with the results of population structure analysis, we observed that the average diversity (π) of the genome-wide 10-kb windows of GQ population is 0.00051, of HN population is 0.00070 and of AH population is 0.00057, suggesting genetic diversity had been reduced during domestication. The 10 kb-window averaged Fst between AH and GQ is 0.123, between AH and HN is 0.054 and between HN and GQ is 0.073, which is according to their polygenetic relationship inferred by NJ-tree and the breeding history of GQ which had employed individuals from HN population for genetic improvement (Fig. 3A).

Genetic divergence among the three populations. A Fst between polpulations. B LD decay of the three populations

The distance of LD decay was represented as the physical distance over which r2 drops to half of the highest value. The LD decay in AH population is 20.50Kb, in HN population is 27.80Kb and in GQ population is 38.2Kb, suggesting GQ may integrate multiple genetic resources during breeding, which have decreased the genetic recombination within the population (Fig. 3B).

Selective signals produced by selective breeding

To explore potential selected sweeps, we applied Fst and and nucleotide diversity ratio (π-ratio) to identify candidate genes between GQ and wild populations. By combining Fst and π-ratio, genomic loci with significantly high π-ratio and Fst values (top 5%) were identified as highly divergent (Fig. 4). A total of 41 putatively selected regions containing 272 genes were selected during breeding (Table S4).

Selection signal detection between GQ and wild populations by Fst andπ-ratio. Note: The blue and green areas are selected sites

To improve the confidence of the selective sweeps, we used XP-EHH statistics (detection of selective sweeps based on extended haplotype blocks) to detect putative selective sweeps during modern breeding of GQ: GQ vs. all wild fish populations (AH and HN) (Fig. 5A). The top 5% of selected regions were retained to screen candidate selective genes (Supplementary Table S5).

Putative selective sweeps in GQ vs. wild populations. A Selective regions detected by XP-EHH. B GO enrichment of genes in the selected regions. C KEGG enrichment of putative domestication relative genes

We totally obtained 590 selected regions which span 2.99% of genome in the comparisons of GQ vs. wild fish population. The top 5% (30) regions encompassed 168 genes (Table S5). Gene ontology analysis of the 168 genes encompassed in the selective sweeps revealed enrichment of genes in 767 GO categories (p-vale < 0.05, Table S6), showing regulation of complement activation, interleukin binding, IgA binding pathways were under selection during modern breeding of S. chuatsi (Fig. 5B).

KEGG functional enrichment of the genes in selected regions comprised 31 pathways (p-vale < 0.05, Table S7), including complement and coagulation cascades, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, olfactory transduction pathway, which were functionally related to immunity and sensory system (Fig. 5C). Notably, we found that many important genes (a2ml1, il4i1, npy2r, npbwr1) involved in immunity and sensory system were selected during breeding.

Discussion

Population genetic analyses

Genetic diversity and structure form the foundation and core of biodiversity, serving as the crucial underpinning for a species’ evolutionary potential. A population with higher genetic diversity possesses greater adaptability to its environment, while a reduction in genetic diversity leads to reduced environmental adaptability [32]. This study revealed that intense selection reduced genetic diversity in the “Guangqing No. 1” strain, consistent with the findings in the “Shuike No. 1” rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) [33]. In this study, PCA, population structure analysis, and NJ-tree indicated that all individuals were divided into three populations, aligning with the actual geographical distribution. Genetic structure is associated with the geographical distribution of water systems, as shared water systems facilitate frequent gene flow, resulting in greater genetic similarity. PCA and NJ tree analyses revealed the presence of outlier individuals within the HN population, which may potentially be attributed to sampling bias. Furthermore, the genetic diversity and degree of differentiation among the three populations were assessed based on nucleotide diversity (π), the coefficient of genetic differentiation (Fst), and the decay of LD.

Nucleotide diversity is one of the indicators for evaluating genetic diversity, reflecting the degree of genetic variation, which decreases in populations during the selection process [34]. The results of this study showed that the average nucleotide diversity of the HN (0.00070) and AH (0.00057) populations was higher than that of the GQ (0.00051) population. The HN and AH populations were wild populations, indicating that the genetic diversity of GQ had declined after four generations of artificial selection compared with the original base populations but remained at a relatively high level. Our research team previously analysed and compared the genetic diversity among different breeding generations of the selectively bred S. chuatsi variety GQ using microsatellite markers. The results indicated that the genetic diversity of the five generations (F0–F4) declined after four generations of artificial selection but still remained relatively high [35]. In large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea), studies on nucleotide diversity and genetic structure using restriction site-associated DNA sequencing showed that, compared to the wild populations, the farmed populations exhibited lower genetic diversity [36].

Fst is a crucial metric for evaluating genetic differentiation within or between populations, with higher Fst values indicating greater population differentiation and a higher degree of selection. This study observed Fst values of 0.123 between GQ and AH, 0.073 between GQ and HN, and 0.054 between AH and HN. This indicates that the coefficients of genetic differentiation between the selectively bred population and the two base populations were higher than that between the two base populations themselves, suggesting that the selectively bred populations underwent more intense selection. LD decay, the reduction in LD with increasing physical distance between two alleles in the genome, can indicate the degree of selection a population has experienced. Typically, wild populations exhibit faster LD decay rates than domesticated or improved populations. LD decay rates are typically represented by LD decay distances. The results showed that the LD decay distances for the AH (20.50 Kb) and HN (27.80 Kb) populations were shorter than that for the GQ (38.2Kb) population, indicating a faster LD decay in the two base populations compared to the selectively bred population (GQ), i.e., a higher selection intensity in the GQ population. Similar results were also found in a China-based study of largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides), where the LD decay rate of the native population introduced from the United States was higher than that of four populations improved through domestic breeding, indicating that the bred populations underwent a higher degree of selection [37].

Identification of selection loci

We selected growth rate and survival rate as target traits, applied family selection, and developed a new S. chuatsi strain (Guangqing No. 1) with superior growth performance through four generations of selective breeding. In this study, we employed three complementary approaches (Fst, Pi ratio, and XP-EHH) to identify putative selection signatures in the selectively bred Guangqing No. 1 strain through genome-wide comparison with two wild populations (HN and AH). The final screening identified 71 putative selected regions and 424 candidate genes potentially under selection, some of which were associated with growth, immunity, reproduction, feeding, and other functions.

As one of the most significant traits in aquaculture, the growth performance of fish is considered a principal trait for artificial selection, influenced by a set of physiological processes that regulate energy metabolism and muscle growth. Under the same breeding conditions, compared to its basic breeding population “Huakang No. 1”, the growth rate of GQ increased by an average of 16.3%. This suggests that there may be growth-related genes in the selected genomic regions. Among them, phosphofructokinase (pfkp) is located in the 4,457,001–4,736,000 bp region on chromosome 13 of S. chuatsi, where 576 SNP loci were detected and 3 genes were annotated. pfkp is a key enzyme involved in the glycolysis process, promoting glucose breakdown and energy production [38]. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (igf1) is located in the 5,452,001–5,759,000 bp region on chromosome 23 of S. chuatsi, where 645 SNP loci were detected and 8 genes were annotated. The igf1 gene plays crucial roles in myoblast survival, proliferation, differentiation, and muscle damage repair [39–41]. High-resolution melting analysis was used to explore potential associations between igf1 gene polymorphisms and growth traits in a hybrid S. chuatsi population (S. chuatsi ♀× Siniperca scherzeri ♂), which led to the identification of two SNP loci on the igf1 gene that are significantly associated with body weight, body length, and body width, and thus may serve as genetic markers for selecting certain growth traits [42]. The diversity of immune genes is a crucial mechanism for aquatic animals to adapt to environmental changes, and it can enhance their survival capability. Research on the fish immune system can help understand the evolution of the adaptive immune system in vertebrates. Compared to the base breeding population “Huakang No. 1”, the survival rate of GQ increased by an average of 12.6%, indicating that immune-related genes were also under selection. We found that the immune-related genes immunoglobulin (Ig) and interleukin-4-induced gene 1 (il4i1) are both located on chromosome 22 of S. chuatsi. Ig, a globulin produced by B lymphocytes in response to pathogen infection in vertebrates, plays an immune role by specifically recognising and binding to corresponding antigens and is an essential molecule for the humoral immune response [43]. The il4i1 gene is expressed in the immune and central nervous systems, regulating the differentiation and activation of immune cells and promoting the development of the central nervous system (CNS) [44, 45]. In summary, selection of genes related to growth and immunity can improve growth rate and survival rate.

The regulation of food intake is one of the most complex phenomena in mammals and fish, closely linked to genetic differences and dietary habits. The neuropeptide Y receptor Y2 (npy2r) gene is located within the 21,773,001–22,836,000 bp region of chromosome 22 in S. chuatsi. A total of 1,512 SNP loci were detected in this region, with 56 genes annotated. This candidate gene npy2r is one of neuropeptide Y (NPY) receptors. A genome-wide association study on growth traits in orange-spotted grouper (Epinephelus coioides) using RAD-seq genotyping reveals that npy2r plays a critical role in its growth [46]. Wang et al. reported that npy2r was mainly expressed in the heart, liver, ovary and brain, and demonstrated that it played a major role in regulating food intake as well as took part in metabolism in ovary and liver of orange-spotted grouper [47]. Additional studies indicate that deficiency of npy2r can increase food intake in fish [48]. Vision and olfaction are the most important sensory modalities, which are essential for the recognition of food in many fish species [49]. Previous studies have shown that the olfactory function of S. chuatsi plays almost no role in the feeding process, and they primarily rely on vision and touch [50, 51]. The olfactory marker protein gene (omp) is located in the 27,875,700–28,075,637 bp region on chromosome 7 and exhibits a unique association with olfactory receptor neurons in many vertebrates [52]. The reinforcement of olfactory and visual systems could be adaptive for the distinctive feeding behavior of S. chuatsi. Lecithin-retinol acyltransferase (lrat), an enzyme predominantly expressed in retinal pigmented epithelial cells and hepatocytes, catalyzes the conversion of all-trans-retinol to all-trans-retinyl esters. The lrat gene is located within chromosomal region 21,773,001–22,836,000 bp on chromosome 22. In the retinal pigment epithelium, lrat serves as a crucial component of the visual cycle (retinoid cycle) [53]. Chromosome 2 Open Reading Frame 71 (c2orf71) plays an important role in the development of normal vision [54]. Studies show that lack of c2orf71 causes a retinal phenotype in zebrafish and indicate that the function of the c2orf71 gene is conserved across species [55]. Furthermore, researching genes associated with the reproductive process in aquatic animals is crucial for breeding offspring and ensuring the continuity of species. Septin 12 (sept12) is a testis-specific gene involved in the terminal differentiation of male germ cells and plays critical roles in sperm morphology and function [56, 57]. In conclusion, this study, based on resequencing and the analysis of selective loci, identified several candidate genes associated with growth, immunity, reproduction, and feeding. The detection of selective loci may reveal genes and mutations linked to important phenotypic traits, providing a reference for accelerating selective breeding of superior strains.

Conclusions

This study conducted population genetics analyses on the selectively bred population GQ and two wild populations (AH and HN) as the base populations, revealing that the genetic diversity in the selectively bred population is lower than in the wild populations. Through the identification of selection loci associated with growth traits, several genomic regions subjected to selection were identified. From these regions, candidate genes closely associated with economically important traits were identified, such as pfkp, igf1, Ig, il4i1, npy2r, omp, lrat, c2orf71, sept12. This research provides candidate genes and selection markers for the artificial selection of S. chuatsi, offering valuable insights and guidance for aquaculture breeding.
