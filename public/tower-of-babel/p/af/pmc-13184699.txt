Artificial intelligence for pleural effusion and pneumothorax detection on thoracic ultrasound: an educational viewpoint on the promise, pitfalls and path forward
Authors: Guido Marchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracic ultrasound (TUS) is a key bedside tool for detecting pleural effusion and pneumothorax, offering high sensitivity, portability and radiation-free assessment. However, its reliability is limited by operator dependency and variable training, posing challenges in emergency, intensive care and resource-limited settings. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a potential adjunct to support TUS interpretation, with deep learning algorithms showing promising accuracy in research studies. Evidence suggests AI may perform well for straightforward cases, yet performance declines significantly during external validation and for complex or low-quality images, precisely where clinical decision support is most needed. Five potential scenarios for AI application are identified: emergency triage, intensive care unit monitoring, post-procedural safety checks, deployment in resource-limited environments, and educational feedback for trainees. Despite these opportunities, current AI systems remain immature: methodological limitations, operator-dependence and lack of real-world outcome data constrain safe clinical adoption. Rigorous prospective trials, multisite validation, standardised reporting, and integration of quality assurance are essential before routine use. At present, AI-assisted TUS should be regarded as a research and educational tool rather than a substitute for clinical judgment. Thoughtful development and cautious implementation are required to transform AI from an experimental promise into a reliable, patient-centred clinical resource.

Introduction

Thoracic ultrasound (TUS) has become an essential tool for diagnosing pleural effusion and pneumothorax, offering superior sensitivity to chest radiography while being radiation-free, portable and well suited for bedside assessment. Beyond procedural guidance, it has transformed the diagnostic evaluation of pleural disorders and is increasingly recognised as an area of active clinical and academic research [1–7]. However, TUS remains highly operator-dependent, requiring substantial training and experience for reliable interpretation. This limitation is particularly challenging in emergency departments with limited specialist coverage, in intensive care units where frequent reassessment is necessary, and in resource-limited settings where trained sonographers are scarce.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is playing an expanding role in respiratory medicine, supporting data-driven clinical decision-making and predictive modelling across domains such as interventional pulmonology, interstitial lung diseases and lung cancer screening, among others [8–14]. In the context of TUS, AI has emerged as a potential tool to democratise access to expert-level image interpretation [15–19]. Algorithms using deep learning have demonstrated promising accuracy in research settings, generating considerable enthusiasm about automating pleural effusion and pneumothorax detection [20, 21]. But can these systems truly deliver on their promise? This educational viewpoint examines what AI can realistically achieve today, identifies critical limitations that must be addressed, and outlines the path towards safe clinical implementation.

Understanding AI in TUS: the basics

For clinicians unfamiliar with AI terminology, these systems learn patterns from thousands of labelled ultrasound images to distinguish normal from pathological findings [22]. Through exposure to large datasets, algorithms develop the ability to recognise ultrasound features associated with pleural pathology, although the specific sonographic patterns they identify may be complex and may not always correspond directly to individual diagnostic criteria that human interpreters use.

Current AI models vary in their clinical tasks: some classify images as normal or abnormal, others attempt to localise pathology within the image, and some provide real-time scanning guidance. The key distinction from traditional computer-aided detection is that modern deep learning systems develop their own internal representations of disease patterns rather than following pre-programmed rules. This means the AI may identify subtle combinations of features that humans might not consciously recognise, but it also means the reasoning process can be opaque – a “black box” that produces outputs without a transparent explanation of how the conclusions were reached [23].

Importantly, ultrasound interpretation for pleural pathologies involves multiple sonographic features evaluated in the clinical context, not single findings in isolation. AI systems trained on ultrasound data may learn these complex patterns but obviously can not capture the full clinical nuance that experienced operators apply.

For pneumothorax detection, results are notably variable. The best-performing algorithms achieved sensitivities of 92–94% with specificities of 80–87% in controlled settings. However, other studies reported substantially lower performance, with sensitivities dropping to 79–86% [20]. To contextualise these findings, human operators performing TUS achieve pneumothorax sensitivities of 78–97% for trained clinicians (57–72% for minimally trained operators), with specificities consistently exceeding 98%. Critically, these variations in AI performance raise questions about reliability across different clinical environments and equipment.

For pleural effusion detection, AI demonstrates generally superior performance. Several studies achieved sensitivities exceeding 95% with specificities above 90% in internal testing [21]. For comparison, pleural effusion detection by human operators demonstrates sensitivities of 85–100% and specificities of 77–100%, with more consistent performance across training levels. Crucially, AI performance consistently declined during external validation at different hospitals. One multi-institutional study showed sensitivity dropping from 82% internally to just 71% at an external site, illustrating how algorithms trained at one institution may struggle elsewhere [24].

Five clinical scenarios where AI could genuinely help

Rather than replacing expert interpretation, the realistic potential of AI in TUS may be best realised in specific, well-defined situations. 1) In emergency triage, rapid automated screening during FAST (focused assessment with sonography for trauma) examinations could flag suspected pneumothorax for immediate review by a senior clinician, potentially reducing time-to-diagnosis when minutes matter.2) For intensive care monitoring, AI-integrated static ultrasound devices could enable continuous bedside monitoring for early identification of evolving pleural effusion or pneumothorax in critically ill patients. Unlike repetitive handheld examinations, which would represent an unjustified burden given the low incidence rate of spontaneous complications, wearable or fixed ultrasound sensors with AI interpretation could provide automated surveillance of high-risk intensive care unit patients without requiring repeated operator intervention or continuous intensivist availability.3) Post-procedural safety checks represent another promising application. Following thoracentesis or chest tube placement, automated detection of suspected pneumothorax could standardise post-procedural safety assessments performed at the bedside, enabling early detection of suspected pneumothorax and potentially preventing progression to tension physiology.4) In resource-limited settings, where regions face critical shortages of trained sonographers, AI could enable community health workers to perform basic screening examinations, although this requires systems that function offline without reliable internet connectivity.5) Finally, in training and education, AI could provide real-time feedback to learners, helping them recognise pathological findings and improving their acquisition technique through immediate quality assessment.

The reality check: four critical problems

Despite promising research results, current AI systems face substantial barriers to clinical implementation.

Problem 1: performance collapses outside research settings

Studies often report high accuracy for AI models in controlled settings, but performance declines in real-world clinical practice. This discrepancy arises because research datasets systematically exclude challenging cases that define everyday care, such as suboptimal image quality, mixed pathologies, atypical presentations and emergency situations where ideal scanning conditions cannot be achieved.

Problem 2: AI fails where help is needed most

Algorithms perform very well on straightforward cases, such as large pleural effusions, but show substantially lower performance on more challenging presentations, including complex or trace effusions that require expert interpretation [21]. This creates a paradox: AI provides confident diagnoses in cases that clinicians would identify reliably, while failing in scenarios where decision support is most needed. Current systems thus risk functioning primarily in situations of limited clinical value.

Problem 3: image quality determines everything

Unlike computed tomography (CT) where quality is standardised, ultrasound is fundamentally operator-dependent. Probe positioning, depth settings and scanning technique dramatically affect images. Most current AI systems assume adequate acquisition but provide no feedback on scanning quality. This means a poorly positioned probe yields a confidently incorrect AI interpretation – a dangerous combination. Without integrated quality assessment, these systems work only when experts operate them, defeating their purpose of democratisation.

Problem 4: training on the wrong standard

Most studies used physician ultrasound interpretation rather than CT confirmation as their reference standard [20]. This creates fundamental circularity: AI learns to mimic physician interpretation patterns, including systematic errors and subjective variability. For pneumothorax, multiple conditions produce absent lung sliding beyond true pneumothorax, including apnoea, endobronchial intubation and extensive consolidation, among others. Training AI on physician interpretations means algorithms learn these ambiguities rather than resolving them.

The research-to-practice gap

Current publications systematically exclude scenarios that define real clinical practice. Patients with subcutaneous emphysema or prior thoracic surgery are routinely excluded, as are images with suboptimal quality from agitation or emergency situations. Mixed pathologies, such as concurrent pleural effusion and pneumothorax, are similarly omitted, along with the natural equipment variability across manufacturers and probe types that exists in real clinical environments. These exclusions create evidence that looks impressive but does not reflect clinical reality. Published accuracy represents the best-case scenarios, not typical performance.

Furthermore, no studies evaluate what actually matters: clinical outcomes. We lack evidence addressing whether AI reduces time-to-diagnosis, decreases procedural complications, enables safe task-shifting, or improves patient outcomes. Diagnostic accuracy alone does not guarantee clinical utility.

The path forward: what must change

For researchers, the priority must be conducting prospective trials that include challenging cases rather than excluding them. Rigorous external validation across multiple sites and equipment platforms should become standard practice. Studies must measure clinical outcomes, not just diagnostic accuracy, and report honestly about failure modes and limitations. Following standardised AI reporting guidelines, such as TRIPOD+AI, will ensure transparency and reproducibility [25].

For clinicians considering AI adoption, a healthy scepticism toward impressive accuracy claims from single-institution studies is warranted. Demand evidence of external validation across diverse settings and insist on transparency about which cases were excluded and what failure modes exist. Recognise that diagnostic accuracy does not guarantee clinical utility, and maintain physician oversight for all AI-assisted interpretations regardless of the system's reported performance.

For safe implementation if and when systems mature sufficiently, institutions should start in controlled environments under expert supervision. Establish clear governance structures defining physician oversight and liability frameworks before deployment. Continuous performance monitoring stratified by patient subgroups is essential, as is creating explicit protocols for handling low-confidence outputs. Integrating real-time scan quality feedback will help address the operator-dependency problem. Only after demonstrating consistent multisite performance should gradual expansion to more challenging environments be considered.

Regulatory and economic realities

AI diagnostic tools must navigate complex approval pathways as “Software as a Medical Device”, with regulatory requirements that vary by risk classification. Systems making autonomous diagnostic claims face substantially higher scrutiny than decision-support tools, further limiting translation into practice. At the same time, cost-effectiveness remains largely unexplored. This gap is particularly relevant in resource-limited settings, where the potential clinical impact may be greatest, yet upfront investment and implementation costs are most prohibitive.

Conclusions: realistic expectations

AI-assisted TUS for pleural effusion and pneumothorax detection represents a promising but immature technology. Current evidence does not support widespread clinical deployment. Performance remains inconsistent across sites and equipment, with concerning degradation in challenging cases precisely where support is most needed.

The realistic path forward requires acknowledging AI as decision support tool rather than an autonomous diagnosis tool, with initial implementation in controlled environments. The ultimate goal should be augmentation rather than replacement of clinical judgment. For the promise to become a reality, we need methodological rigor, transparent limitation acknowledgement and outcomes-focused research. Until these elements align with consistently validated performance, AI remains a research tool rather than a clinical solution. Success depends not on algorithm performance alone, but on comprehensive approaches addressing training, quality assurance, regulatory frameworks, workflow integration and economic feasibility.

The technology shows genuine potential, but realising this promise requires collaborative effort between clinicians, researchers and technology developers, grounded in rigorous validation and an unwavering commitment to patient safety. Thoughtful development today will determine whether AI becomes a transformative tool or an unfulfilled promise tomorrow.
