A Review of Analgesic and Anaesthetic Approaches in Local Anaesthetic (Medical) Thoracoscopy
Authors: Jasandra Sutanto, Duneesha DeFonseka, Bernard Duysinx, Valentina Pinelli, Elizabeth Turnbull, Avinash Aujayeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pleural disease is a subspecialist area of respiratory medicine, requiring diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. The most invasive pleural procedure is local anaesthetic thoracoscopy (LAT) used in the context of unexplained exudative pleural effusions. LAT can be exquisitely painful because of instrumentation of the parietal pleura which carries all nociceptive nerve endings. The optimal anaesthetic and analgesic requirement for LAT is not known, and recommendations are based on expert opinions and small usually single-centre studies. We provide a narrative description of existing literature, show the potential application of regional nerve blocks for LAT and provide practice points.

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Pleural procedures are common, with approximately over 30,000 procedures performed yearly in the UK alone [1]. They include therapeutic aspirations, small and large bore chest drains, indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) insertion (or removal), pleural biopsies and local anaesthetic thoracoscopy (LAT), otherwise known as medical thoracoscopy or pleuroscopy. LAT is the investigation of choice in unexplained exudative pleural effusions [2], as it provides a multimodality approach: first, by providing symptom relief from fluid drainage; second, by allowing diagnosis from biopsies of directly visualised abnormal areas of parietal pleura, and third, by treating and preventing fluid recurrence by pleurodesis of or inserting an IPC [2]. LAT has a strong evidence base, a high diagnostic yield with experienced operators and a low complication rate [3, 4].

All such procedures require a breach of the pleural space initially with 21-gauge hypodermic ‘green’ needle or larger 6, 8, 12, 24 French gauge (Fr) catheters or drains, with most of them requiring scalpel incisions, tract dilatation, port insertion and manipulation of the parietal pleura. The exact descriptors of each procedure are beyond the scope of this article [1]. We will concentrate on LAT.

This is a narrative review that relied on the expert opinions of the authors, and no specific search strategy was employed. No ethical approval was required, given use of existing literature. Why are Pleural Procedures Painful?

The initial pain from pleural procedures is from the irritation of subcutaneous pain fibres and the periosteal surface of the ribs. The parietal pleura, of somatic origin, lines the thoracic wall and has rich innervation rendering it extremely sensitive to pain. The parietal pleura and peripheral portion of the diaphragmatic pleura are innervated by the intercostal nerves (T1–T11) – they run parallel to the ribs. Pain from the costal pleura is associated with the corresponding part of the thoracic or abdominal wall, whereas the mediastinal pleura and central diaphragmatic pleura are innervated by the phrenic nerve (C3–C5) and pain from this is referred to the tip of the shoulder or to the neck [5].

The visceral pleura, derived from splanchnic mesoderm, closely adheres to the lung parenchyma and is innervated by autonomic vagal and sympathetic fibres – they are not pain sensitive, as shown in Figs. 1 and 2. Thus, any manipulation (positioning of the patient, instrument manipulations, the pressure of the endoscope acting as a lever on the ribs, dissection of adhesions or coagulation, biopsy) of the parietal pleura including intrapleural fibrinolysis and talc pleurodesis can cause pain. The pleura is more sensitive in younger patients and in patients with normal parietal pleura compared with those, for instance, with advanced tumour spread on the pleura [9]. Thus, pain control in LAT has been the focus of several studies, with pretty much all proceduralists using sedation, opiates and, increasingly, regional nerve blocks [10].

Why is Pain Control Important?

Patients with pleural disease often undergo multiple procedures [11]. Whilst one of the tenets of pleural disease is to minimise the number of procedures to be performed, often this cannot be helped – for example, in a patient with failed pleurodesis who needs an IPC or a patient who needs further tissue for molecular analysis, so thoracoscopic biopsies are required [12]. Previous painful experiences are known to have a negative impact on patients’ sensitivity to a procedure, making them reluctant to undergo further ones [13]. Pain can cause unwanted immobility and increased length of stay, potentially leading to complications such as thromboembolic disease and hypostatic pneumonias [14].

What is the Role of LAT in Pleural Disease?

LAT is a procedure that has been performed for over a century by physicians, and the techniques such as Boutin needle introduction have been developed over time [4]. Traditionally, such patients would undergo video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS). For VATS, a patient would be under general anaesthesia, always in a theatre environment and the procedure will require support from one or two thoracic surgeons, an anaesthetist and theatre staff. LAT, however, can be performed under conscious sedation, by a respiratory physician with minimal assistance. There is widespread variation in LAT practice [15].

LAT can be a one-stop-shop in the investigation and management of pleural disease. LAT offers the opportunity to drain the effusion, visualise and biopsy the pleura and consider definitive management of pleural effusion. It thus avoids the need for future multiple invasive procedures, as talc poudrage or indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) insertion can be performed at the end of the LAT procedure. A ‘direct to LAT’ approach, abolishing the initial diagnostic aspiration, is increasingly performed as often cytological diagnosis alone is often insufficient for personalised oncological management [16, 17].

LAT biopsies must be deep and contain underlying adipose tissue to demonstrate invasion, (or lack thereof) in certain conditions, for example, pleural mesothelioma. Biopsies must also be performed at various different sites on the pleura [1]. Adequate analgesic cover of the parietal pleura is thus essential to ensure good full thickness biopsies.

More recently, LAT is offered as a day-case procedure with IPC insertion, and this is another reason for increasing its popularity [18, 19].

What Does the Current Guidance Say Around Sedation/Anaesthesia for LAT?

LAT is performed with local anaesthesia followed by ‘conscious sedation’ and intravenous analgesia, with the patient spontaneously breathing.

Sedation for LAT

No consensus exists regarding the choice of sedative and analgesic agents for LAT. Literature on analgesic strategies LAT includes pharmacological measures (sedatives, chest wall blocks, local and intrapleural anaesthesia, analgesia including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioids) and non-pharmacological measures [transcutaneous electric nerve stimulation (TENS), cold application and changes to the intervention or technique] [10].

Pain tolerance varies from person to person, and as part of the assessment of a patient’s suitability for LAT, their full history including surgical and anaesthetic ones should be taken into consideration.

The procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) guidelines in adults suggests that patients with comorbidities including end organ failure (chronic renal or hepatic disease), severe cardiovascular issues, obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome, morbid obesity (body mass index greater than 40 kg/m2) and patients with an American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of 3 and 4 should ideally be managed by anaesthetists [20].

The optimal sedative and analgesic for LAT should have a predictable pharmacokinetic profile, a rapid onset of action, a short recovery time and overall minimal risks.

Respiratory changes in arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide and intra-operative drop in the ratio of the arterial oxygen tension to the fraction of inspired oxygen and hemodynamic ones such as hypotension or atrial fibrillation are primarily related to the type of sedation although the patient’s lateral decubitus position, fluid shift and pneumothorax which is induced can also be contributory. Monitoring is thus suggested during LAT – this can be just with pulse oximetry or, if resources allow, via cutaneous capnography, alongside haemodynamic measurements to allow the prompt recognition of cardio-respiratory compromise and to provide safe sedation [21, 22].

Some practitioners offer pre-medication with 0.4–0.8 mg of atropine to prevent vasovagal reactions, hydrocodone to suppress coughing or even inhaled methoxyflurane. However, these recommendations come from single-centre studies and do not form part of the practice of any of the authors of this review [23].

Midazolam is the most commonly used sedative drug for LAT. Its co-administration combined with fentanyl represents a relative standard approach and is described by the British Thoracic Society guideline on pleural disease [2]. Koulelidis et al. showed that midazolam associated with local anaesthetic infiltration does not necessarily add to respiratory side effects for LAT and improves patient-related outcomes by comparison to local lidocaine alone [24].

An alternative to midazolam is propofol, which has a faster onset of action and a more rapid recovery. The use of propofol does require an anaesthetist, which is not the case for the vast majority of LAT [15]. Propofol has a narrow therapeutic window and the risk for over-sedation is high. It also has very potent vasodilatory effects and can cause severe respiratory depression, especially with the co-administration of opiates [25]. A retrospective cohort of 199 patients published by Vakil et al. [26] reported significant lower mean oxygen saturations and hypoxia in patients receiving the combination of propofol with co-administration of opiates, narcotics and sedatives. This study also showed the safety of LAT using propofol with capnography even without monitoring. Grendelmeier et al. [27] also compared midazolam and propofol for sedation during LAT, with pethidine given for analgesia in all. Oxygen saturations levels were lower in the propofol group, and patients were more hypotensive, but pethidine requirement was lower. Vorster et al. [28] showed similar findings.

The rapid recovery from propofol compared with midazolam and the ease of propofol up-titration are also factors in choosing it for LAT [29].

Other drugs have been studied for LAT. For example, dexmedetomidine can reduce the dose of narcotics needed and keep the patient’s respiratory drive intact. It works as an agonist on the pre- and post-synaptic alpha 2 receptors producing analgesia, sedation and anxiolysis. When compared with midazolam and fentanyl co-administration, dexmedetomidine sedation significantly improved pulmonologist-rated overall procedure satisfaction, pulmonologist-rated cough on a visual analogue scale (VAS), patient-rated faces pain scale scores and reduced total additional fentanyl dose without hemodynamic adverse events [30–32].

Analgesia for LAT

Step-by-step local anaesthesia lidocaine infiltration reduces pain induced by skin incision, subcutaneous and intercostal muscle dissection and finally parietal pleura penetration. Buffering lidocaine is recommended [33]. There is some emerging evidence that higher doses of local anaesthetic might be safe and that guidance suggesting the accepted 3-mg/kg doses for 1% lignocaine should be reconsidered [34].

Adequate intraoperative and postoperative pain management is well established for thoracoscopic surgeries and can also be extrapolated LAT [35].

In addition to local anaesthesia, analgesia can be provided with a traditional combination medications like acetaminophen, tramadol, NSAIDs and opioids.

However, local anaesthesia at the port insertion for LAT does not anaesthetize the parietal pleura from where biopsies are taken. Thoracic epidural anaesthesia seems to be excessive for LAT [35]. Current guidance suggests that opioids such as fentanyl administration can be helpful [1], but our view is that, often, despite increasing intravenous doses, parietal pleural biopsies can be exquisitely painful. There is also the risk of administering too much opioids which might then require reversal.

What is the Emerging Evidence Around Regional Nerve Blocks for LAT?

Chest-wall blocks are well established in thoracic surgery [35] – they provide excellent regional anaesthesia and analgesia, complement local anaesthetic infiltration, have negligible side effects and reduce intra-operative opioid use [36].

The various blocks are ultrasound-guided multiple-injection thoracic paravertebral blocks (TPVB), intercostal nerve blocks (ICNB) and single-injection erector spinae plane blocks (ESPB). Chen et al. showing that ICNB and single-injection ESPB were as effective as each other in reducing pain after surgical thoracoscopic surgery [37].

Blocks for Thoracoscopy

TPVBs are performed by injecting local anaesthetic into the paravertebral space under direct vision with ultrasound [38]. The approach to the space can be either sagittal or transverse, and the block can be done at a single level with a high volume of local anaesthetic or multi-level with lower volumes. For LAT, TPVBs would be at the thoracic level that corresponds to the rib space used for port insertion.

The paravertebral space sits adjacent to the vertebral column bilaterally and is bordered by the ribs superiorly and inferiorly, the posterolateral area of the vertebral body and intervertebral foramen medially, the parietal pleura anterolaterally and the superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL) posteriorly (Fig. 3). The SCTL is an important landmark on ultrasound and needs to be pierced. The paravertebral space contains the spinal nerves (anterior and posterior rami), fatty tissue, rami communicantes, the sympathetic chain and the intercostal vessels. The space is also contiguous with the epidural space medially and the intercostal space laterally. The TPVB, therefore, produces ipsilateral somatic and sympathetic blockade by anaesthetising the spinal nerve as it emerges from the intervertebral foramen, along with the rami communicantes and sympathetic chain within the paravertebral space. This results in multi-dermatomal sensory blockade of the chest wall and attenuation of sympathetic-mediated visceral pain. Analgesia of the diaphragmatic pleura is often incomplete, as the phrenic nerve (C3–5) is not affected by the TPVB; therefore, procedures involving close proximity to the diaphragm may result in ipsilateral shoulder pain.Fig. 3The paravertebral space – images reproduced from NYSORA.com under their educational image use policy [39]

The most common local anaesthetics used to perform a TPVB are levobupivacaine (2.5–5 mg/ml) or ropivacaine (3.75–7.5 mg/ml). Dosing is weight based and the type of local anaesthetic, volume and number of injections should be selected on the basis of the coverage required. Typically, a single injection of 20–25 ml of local anaesthetic provides coverage of four to five dermatomes. Complications include pneumothorax, spread of local anaesthetic into the epidural space (which can cause hypotension), local anaesthetic toxicity, bleeding and direct nerve damage.

ESPBs are newer alternatives to the TPVB. They have a lower complication rate owing to the greater distance from vulnerable structures and is easier to perform when visualisation of the required structures with ultrasound is difficult [40, 41]. The erector spinae plane sits between the transverse process and the base of the erector spinae muscle (Fig. 4). The block is performed by injecting local anaesthetic into this fascial plane (normally using an in-plane approach) which spreads anteriorly through small holes in the SCTL into the paravertebral space to cover the posterior rami of the spinal nerves. This is a fascial plane block and it therefore relies on volume of local anaesthetic to work (normally 20–30 ml of levobupivacaine/ropivacaine). Coverage by this block is similar to the TPVB but generally considered to be less dense owing to reliance on spread of local anaesthetic into the paravertebral space rather than direct deposition of local anaesthetic here.Fig. 4The erector spinae plane block – images reproduced from NYSORA.com under their educational image use policy [39]. The final block to mention is the intercostal nerve block (ICNB) which directly anaesthetises the intercostal nerve as it sits in the subcostal groove [42]. The intercostal nerves run from T1 to T12, originating from the ventral ramus of each corresponding spinal nerve. The intercostal nerve can be blocked anywhere along its course in the subcostal groove until the mid-axillary line, where it gives off the lateral cutaneous branches. The nerve runs as part of the neurovascular bundle along with the intercostal artery and vein, so there is the risk of vascular injury and of rapid absorption of the local anaesthetic causing local anaesthetic toxicity. The ICNB acts directly on the single level it is administered at and does not spread between levels owing to the ribs acting as a physical barrier. There is only partial coverage of the costal pleura from this block.

In summary, the TPVB provides the most comprehensive unilateral thoracic analgesia but with greater technical demands, the ESPB offers a simpler and potentially safer alternative with predominantly somatic coverage and the ICNB provides highly targeted analgesia but requires multilevel injection.

Agnoletti et al. [43] looked at multi-level paravertebral blocks (PVBs) at thoracic levels three to seven with 1% ropivacaine and 2% lidocaine, showing again reduced pain scores and overall reduced analgesic requirements. PVBs have also been studied by Abo-Zeid et al. [44], where there were three groups of patients – three-level PVB, two-level PVB and a local infiltration one. Using sensory, visual analogue scores and pulmonary function (after thoracoscopy), they found that the three-level PVB provided improved pain scores and pulmonary function. However, this was a single-centre study that has not been reproduced for external validity and applicability.

ESPBs at thoracic levels five, six and seven using 20–35 mL 0.25% bupivacaine were trialled by McPherson et al. [45] in nine patients. They showed the feasibility of their approach and an analgesic effect lasting several hours, allowing same-day discharge after LAT and IPC insertion. Opiate use post-procedurally was minimal. Wijayaratne et al. [46] also showed reduced pain scores and reduced analgesic and sedation requirements in patients with ESPBs.

It is worth noting that Ajmal et al. [47] also showed the ESPBs decreased pain severity during LAT and decreased analgesic and sedation requirements during the procedure when compared with patients not receiving ESPBs. The ESPB was done by a physician and anecdotally added about 30 min to the overall procedure [46] and required the physicians to keep their skills up by doing the ESBPs regularly. Ajmal et al. [47] from the same centre also studied ICNBs and had a comparator group. They inserted levobupivacaine/xylocaine at the angle of the rib immediately before LAT in up to five rib spaces, and this was physician led. The signal in this small group of patients (ten in each group), was that ICNBs reduced pain during and after LAT and perhaps even reduced length of stay. Salguero et al. [48] conducted the Placebo versus Erector Spinae Plane Block for Medical Thoracoscopy Study (PAINLESS) study, where they randomised 17 patients to each group. The trial stopped early owing to perceived non-effect, but the limitations of this study – small numbers, mostly benign diagnoses, intraoperative removal of chest tube and lack of control of intraoperative analgesia – suggest the study findings cannot be generalised.

Other Analgesic Techniques and Recovery

Intra-cavitary anaesthetic therapy has been studied before by Reda et al. [49] in a study which included 20 patients, of which 90% had malignant pleural disease. The anaesthetic was provided via a saline drip into the pleural space, and again patient outcome measures were favourable [50]. Lee et al. [51] looked at giving atomised lignocaine prior to talc poudrage for thoracoscopy in patients with pneumothorax [51]. Rudimentary pain scores improved, but again this has not been replicated and published. In a similar fashion to the aforementioned study by Reda et al. [49], Abdelhady et al. [52] inserted a large bore chest drain approximately 1 h before LAT, drained the pleura to dryness and then inserted 3 mg/kg of lidocaine intra-pleurally, along with air so that the pleural layers separated. There was no difference in pain scores between the two groups. Thus, the evidence for intra-cavity anaesthesia is poor [53].

Post-procedure monitoring will depend on available resources. We would recommend post-procedure monitoring with routine observations in a recovery area and, after a period of stability, typically 1 h in our institutions, the patients can be stepped down to the normal ward areas.

Conclusions

Local anaesthesia thoracoscopy (LAT) is an integral part of respiratory medicine. We would argue that it is the most invasive procedure that respiratory physicians can do, and we have to get the analgesic aspect right for our patients. There are multiple examples of good practice as described above but, to date, a dearth of randomised controlled evidence. Published guidance would suggest sedation with midazolam or propofol with concurrent opioid administration, typically fentanyl. This would be the usual practice of all the authors of this review, and we would also suggest a regional nerve block [an erector spinae plane block (ESPB)] prior to the LAT. Until formal randomised trial evidence is available, practice will remain variable and even thereafter, will be resource and cost dependent.
