Long-Term Health Care Utilization Among Mothers of Children with Epilepsy – Including Psychiatric and Surgical Services: A 20-Year National Cohort Study
Authors: Malene Risager Lykke, Katalin Veres, Erzsébet Horváth-Puhó
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMothers of children with epilepsy (MCE) face elevated risks of psychological stress and psychiatric disorders, yet their overall health care utilization post-diagnosis remains underexplored. Understanding these patterns may inform more integrated, evidence-based care for affected families.Patients and methodsA population-based cohort study using national health registry data in Denmark. From 1990 to 2017, 18,775 MCE were matched on maternal age, gestational age, child’s sex and age at epilepsy diagnosis with up to 10 mothers without a child diagnosed with epilepsy (n = 188,630) and followed until 2018. Period prevalence was calculated as the proportion of MCE health care utilization (HCU). We compared period prevalence of health care utilization (HCU)—including primary care, inpatient, outpatient, psychiatric, and surgical contacts—between MCE and matched comparators using modified Poisson regression to estimate risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Mean annual HCU was visualized, and rate ratios were derived via negative binomial regression. Analyses were stratified by follow-up periods: 0–5, 6–10, and 11–20 years post-index.ResultsOver 20-years’ follow-up, MCE showed higher HCU than comparators across all services except primary care, which remained comparable. The RR of outpatient visits and surgical care contacts were slightly increased over the follow-up period. The risk of using psychiatric services was higher for MCE compared to the comparison cohort and increased over time, being 35% higher during the period of 11–20 years of follow-up (RR11-20 years = 1.35 [95% CI 1.18–1.39]). The rate of HCU was increased for MCE compared to the comparison cohort across services, except for psychiatric services, which was comparable for MCE and the reference cohort.ConclusionMothers of children with epilepsy had more health care use across hospital services compared with matched mothers of children without epilepsy, regardless of the child’s age at diagnosis. These findings underscore the need for proactive mental health support and integrated care planning for mothers to mitigate long-term psychological burden.Keywords: epilepsy, health care utilization, maternal health, epidemiology

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common chronic neurologic conditions, globally affecting 0.3%–0.6% of all children1 and imposes complex caregiving demands, often borne by mothers. Epilepsy is characterized by seizures with unpredictable frequency and might be presented as a transient condition with complete remission after one or a few episodes or persist as a lifelong condition.2–4 Caregiver responsibilities are linked to elevated risks of stress, anxiety, and depression. Poor caregiver health can increase health care use and costs, highlighting the need for integrated care models that support maternal mental health and inform health system planning. This has a large impact on family functioning, restrictions on family activities and increasing financial burden.5 Caregivers face increased risk of elevated psychological stress, depression, and social isolation.6–9 Caregivers’ distress and psychological symptoms are strongly associated with their resources and the child’s behavioural difficulties rather than with epilepsy-related factors.5,6,10–12 Despite this, the health care utilization patterns of mothers caring for children with epilepsy remain underexplored. Existing studies have primarily focused on the child’s need or treatment options, overlooking the broader family context and caregivers’ health care trajectories. Recent studies reported increased risk of psychiatric disorders in parents caring for a child with cancer,13 in mothers who gave birth to a child with a congenital abnormality14 and in mothers giving birth to a child with invasive Group B Streptococcus disease.15 Consequently, the use of health care services could increase among caregivers, but comprehensive data on the association of health care use among mothers raising a child with epilepsy are lacking. Evidence suggests that caregiving burden can be mitigated through targeted support and integrated care models, such as family-centered empowerment interventions, which have shown promise in improving maternal knowledge, coping strategies, and reducing psychological strain.16 Understanding how mothers engage with health care services following their child’s epilepsy diagnosis is critical for designing responsive, family-centered care systems. Such insights can inform policies and interventions that not only support the child’s medical needs but also promote the well-being of the caregiver, ultimately enhancing outcomes for the entire family unit.

The objectives of this study were to examine health care utilization (HCU) among mothers of children with epilepsy (MCE) to that of mothers of unaffected children. We hypothesized that MCE would have greater HCU across all categories of services, regardless of the child’s age at diagnosis.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Data Sources

This was a population-based matched cohort study. The Danish population is covered by a tax-funded health care system in which every individual has a unique civil personal registration number.17 This registration number enables exact individual-level linkage of all records in the different registries, as well as lifelong follow-up.18 Registration in healthcare is mandatory for health care employees, enabling prospective, routinely, and complete data collection. As part of its universal health care system, Denmark provides primary care, including consultation visits with general practitioners for all family members.19 We conducted a population-based matched cohort study using some of the Danish registries.

The following registers were used for data linkage: The Danish Civil Registration System (CRS) is an administrative register containing individual-level information on all persons residing in Denmark. The Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR) has recorded all admissions to Danish non-psychiatric hospitals since 1977, as well as outpatient clinic and emergency room visits since 1995. The Danish Medical Birth Registry (DMBR) contains information on all live births and stillbirths in Denmark since 1973 and allows linkage to both mother’s and father’s civil personal registration number and to information regarding pregnancy and birth.20 The Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register (PCRR) contains information on inpatient, outpatient, and emergency psychiatric hospital encounters.21 To retrieve information from primary health care use (ie, general practitioners, physiotherapists, dentists, medical specialists in medical practices and psychologists) we used the Danish National Health Service Register.22,23 Statistics Denmark is the central authority on Danish statistics, and from there we included information on maternal education and income, using the Education Registries24 and Income Statistics Register.25 The CRS, DNPR and DMBR were used to identify the study population. The DNPR, PCRR, and the Danish National Health Service Register were linked to the cohorts and provided outcome data (see stable 1).

Study Cohorts

The source population consisted of all mothers with livebirths between 1 January 1990 and 31 December 2017 (study period) in Denmark. The exposed cohort consisted of mothers whose child was diagnosed with epilepsy before the age of 18 years and was born in the study period. The date of a first inpatient admission or outpatient clinic visit for epilepsy was defined as the diagnosis date/index date. If more than one child was diagnosed with epilepsy in a family, the index date was defined as the diagnosis date of the child with the earliest diagnosis. We used the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes for mental, behavioral, and nervous system disorders recoded in the DNPR and PCRR to identify children diagnosed with epilepsy (stable 2).26 In Denmark, the diagnostic criterion for epilepsy follows the guidelines and consensus definitions of the International League Against Epilepsy and the International Bureau for Epilepsy for epileptic seizure and epilepsy. We included primary and secondary inpatient and outpatient clinic diagnoses of epilepsy. Emergency diagnoses were excluded from the analysis, since diagnostic evaluation is performed in an in- or outpatient neurological setting.

The comparison cohort consisted of randomly selected women from the source population. The unexposed mothers were matched 10:1 to the exposed mothers by age of the mother in years and age of the child in years at diagnosis, child’s sex, and gestational age group, using matching with replacement. The index date of comparison cohort members was defined as the diagnosis date of the affected child of the mother to whom they were matched. Comparison cohort members were required to be alive and unexposed as of their index date. When fitting regression models, matching was dissolved, and the comparison was performed between the two cohorts.

Study Outcomes

The outcome of interest was HCU, expressed as a period prevalence, and mothers’ quantity of HCU (primary, inpatient, outpatient, psychiatric, and surgical care).

Primary care services included consultations with general practitioners, physiotherapists, dentists and psychologists (including routine visits, telephone contacts, email, and after-hours visits), as well as contacts with medical specialists in private practice, almost all of whom have contracts with the National Health Service (see stable 2 and 3 for additional information). Maternal inpatient care included all hospital admissions registered in the DNPR after the index date. Maternal outpatient clinic visits included all hospital-based ambulatory clinic visits, excluding emergency room visits. Maternal psychiatric service contacts (inpatient care and outpatient visits) were captured using data from PCRR and DNPR (stable 2). Surgical care included all surgical procedures performed in hospitals after the diagnosis/index date. When multiple surgical procedures were reported on the same day, we counted them separately.

Covariates

The following baseline covariates were defined for each child: subtype of epilepsy (focal, generalized or unspecified), age at epilepsy diagnosis/index date (categorized as 0–6, 7–13 and 14–18 years), sex, gestational age at birth (20–27 weeks, 28–36 weeks and ≥37 weeks), year of birth, and pregnancy type (single or multiple). Maternal covariates included birth year (<1970, 1970–1980, 1981–1990 and >1990), birth order of the exposed/comparator child (first, second, third +), maternal epilepsy, education (missing, low, medium, or high), income level (missing, low, medium, medium high or high), smoking habits, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score at baseline (0, 1, 2+) (stable 4).

Maternal income was defined at index date using the Income Statistics Register, divided into low (<25th percentile), medium (25th–<50th percentile), medium high (50th-75th percentile), and high income (≥75th percentile). Maternal educational status was defined using the Education Registries,24 which records information on highest education achieved. Education level was measured at index date and divided into low (primary or lower), medium (upper secondary or academic professional degree), and high (University education at the bachelor’s degree or higher level).

Potential confounding was accounted for via matching or by adjusting for covariates in the multivariable regression models. We used a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify causal relationships and select variables for adjustment (parity, birth year, maternal age) and excluded non-confounders (eg, socioeconomic position) (sfigure 1). Matching at baseline and selective adjustment minimized confounding while ensuring model stability.

Statistical Analysis

Mothers were followed from index date until December 31, 2018, emigration, loss to follow-up, or death of the mother or the child, whichever occurred first. If a child from the comparison cohort was diagnosed with epilepsy during follow-up, follow-up of the mother was stopped in the comparison cohort and the mother switched to the MCE cohort starting on the date of the epilepsy diagnosis (n = 2,405). Cohort members with missing values for any covariates used in the respective analysis were excluded (approx. 1.5% of the data for the unadjusted analyses and 13–24% for the adjusted analyses depending on the follow-up period).

Period Prevalence of Health Care Utilization

Period prevalence is the percentage of the mothers who used health care services at least once during the period analyzed. It is calculated directly from data, separately for MCEs and comparator mothers. This crude measure can be interpreted as risk during the period. The ratio of the period prevalences of the two cohorts is a crude risk ratio for HCU. To estimate a confounder-adjusted risk ratio, regression models were fitted to the data of individuals with complete follow-up time during the period analyzed. Crude incidence rates are defined as the number of HCU divided by the length of period. The ratio of the incidence rates of the two cohorts is a crude (incidence) rate ratio for HCU.

A modified Poisson regression models with a robust variance estimator was used to estimate unadjusted and adjusted risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), accounting for overdispersion of the data27 and for repeated measures in the same individuals. Unadjusted analyses accounted for the matching factors by design, while adjusted analyses accounted for the matching factors, pregnancy type, birth order, calendar period of epilepsy diagnosis, highest education achieved, income, marital status, smoking, and epilepsy status of the mother. Subgroup analyses by child’s age group were performed, where the prevalence proportion and risk ratios (RRs) were calculated for the first 5 years of follow-up (stable 5 and 6).

Annual Mean Health Care Utilization and Health Care Utilization Rates

The annual winsorized mean numbers of contacts were calculated for each health care service and presented overall and by child’s age categories as of the index date, ie, 0–6 years, 7–13 years, or 14–18 years (sfigure 2–6).

Crude incidence rates are defined as the number of HCU divided by the length of period. The ratio of the incidence rates of the two cohorts is a crude (incidence) rate ratio for HCU. To estimate adjusted (incidence) rate ratios, regression models were fitted to the data of individuals with complete follow-up time during the period analyzed. For primary care, unadjusted and adjusted rate ratios (RAs) were estimated using a negative binomial regression model to account for overdispersion. For all other health care categories, we specified a zero-inflated binomial regression model. Unadjusted analyses accounted for the matching factors, while adjusted analyses for the matching factors, pregnancy type, birth order, calendar period of epilepsy diagnosis, highest education achieved, income, marital status, smoking, and maternal epilepsy status. In a sensitivity analysis a negative binomial model with and without offset and a negative binomial zero-inflated model with and without offset were fitted to the data. Subgroup analyses by child’s age group were performed for the first 5 years of follow-up (stable 7).

Ethical and Regulatory Considerations

We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. All data were analyzed at Statistics Denmark using encrypted identification numbers with no contact with individuals. Statistical analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). RStudio version 4.4.1 (2024–06-14) was used for figure generation. According to Danish law, analyses of anonymous data do not require ethical review board approval. The study was reported to the Danish data protection agency (record no. 2015–57-0002).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The source population consisted of 958,024 mothers with livebirths from 1990 to 2017 in Denmark. In this period, 20,385 mothers were identified having a child with incident epilepsy. Among these, 18,898 mothers could be matched with a comparison cohort member (Figure 1). Maternal- and child characteristics are shown in Table 1. Matching resulted in equal distribution of the mother’s characteristics, and the MCE and comparison cohorts were comparable concerning pregnancy type (singleton or twin/higher order pregnancy), year of birth and birth order of the exposed/comparison children. The mothers in the MCE cohort had slightly more comorbidities, slightly lower educational and income levels, a greater likelihood of smoking, and a greater likelihood of being divorced, single, or living alone compared to mothers of unaffected children. The median age of children diagnosed with epilepsy was 5 years [interquartile range (IQR): 2–10]. Maternal median age at the time of a child’s epilepsy diagnosis was 36 years (IQR: 31–41 years).

Period Prevalence of Health Care Utilization

The period prevalence of mothers’ utilization of inpatient care, outpatient visits, psychiatric services, and surgeries was greater in the MCE cohort than in the comparison cohort (Figure 2). The period prevalence of primary care did not differ between the two cohorts. After five years of follow-up, the period prevalence of maternal utilization of inpatient care was 48% in the MCE cohort and 45% in the comparison cohort. Corresponding figures for outpatient visits were 79% and 77%, for psychiatric services 6% and 4%, and for surgical procedures 50% and 46%, respectively.

Association between MCE and period prevalence of health care utilization.

Adjusted RRs for primary care and outpatient visits were close to 1 in all follow-up periods (Figure 2). An increased period prevalence ratio of hospital admissions was observed 6–10 years after epilepsy diagnosis (RR6-10 years = 1.14 [95% CI 1.11–1.17]), decreasing slightly in the subsequent follow-up period (RR11-20 years = 1.10 [95% CI 1.05–1.14]). The RR for psychiatric service contacts was increased during the entire follow-up period, particularly from 11 to 20 years after the child’s diagnosis (RR11-20 years = 1.35 [95% CI 1.18–1.39]). The RR estimate for surgeries was highest during 6 to 10 years following a child’s epilepsy diagnosis (RR6-10 = 1.09 [95% CI 1.07–1.12]), but the magnitude of the RR differed only slightly during the entire follow-up period. Subgroup analyses by child’s age group showed a similar pattern (stable 5).

Annual Mean Health Care Utilization and Health Care Utilization Rates

Mean yearly utilization of each health care service is presented in Figures 3–7. The mean yearly number of all contacts with health care services was higher in the MCE cohort than in the comparison cohort during the years following a child’s epilepsy diagnosis. The mean number of health care contacts was higher in the MCE cohort than in the comparison cohort by approximately 2.0 for primary care contacts and 0.4 for outpatient contacts over the 20-year follow-up period.

Stratified by the child’s age at index date, the mean yearly number of primary care use, inpatient admissions, and outpatient visits remained higher in the MCE cohort than in the comparison cohort regardless of child’s age at index date. The mean number of contacts with psychiatric services was higher following the index date for a mother of a child diagnosed between the ages of 0–6 years, than for mothers of unaffected children of the same age. The difference was not as pronounced in the case of a child diagnosed at age 7 years or older. The mean number of surgeries followed the same pattern as the age-stratified mean number of contacts with psychiatric services (sfigures 1–5).

MCE had a higher rate of primary care use, inpatient care, outpatient visits, and surgical service use than the comparison cohort (Figure 8). The RA of primary care use steadily increased during follow-up with highest rate 11–20 years after diagnosis (RA11-20 = 1.11 [95% CI 1.08–1.14]). The number of inpatient admissions was higher among MCE than in the comparison cohort in the years following the child’s epilepsy diagnosis and the highest RA reached 1.38 (RA11-20 = 1.38 [95% CI 1.29–1.48]) in the period of 11–20 years after the epilepsy diagnosis. The rate of outpatient visits was higher for MCEs by approximately 15% during the 6–10-year follow-up period (RA6-10 = 1.15 [95% CI 1.12–1.19]). The RA of surgical care was greatest 11–20 years after epilepsy diagnosis (RA11-20 = 1.21 [95% CI 1.13–1.29]). Rates of psychiatric service contacts in the MCE cohort were comparable to the rates in the comparison cohort during follow-up. In subgroup analyses by child’s age, the RAs of psychiatric service contacts increased (RA14-18 1.48 ([95% CI 0.95–2.31]) (stable 6). Results for all model types in the sensitivity analyses were similar across outcomes, except for psychiatric service contacts, where negative binomial models showed increased rate ratio, while zero inflated models suggested that the rates in the two groups were comparable (stable 7).

Discussion

Over a 20-year period, HCU among MCE was consistently greater across health care services compared to a matched comparison cohort. Although HCU varied by health care setting, HCU remained elevated for MCE regardless of the length of follow-up time. Risk ratios for psychiatric service contacts were 3%-points higher for MCE compared to the comparison cohort, while rates for psychiatric use were comparable. Both risk- and rate ratios for inpatient care, outpatient visits and surgical contacts were increased for MCE. Six to ten years after epilepsy diagnosis resulted in a 6%-point increase in inpatient care for MCE compared to comparisons cohort members. Primary care use rates increased, but risk ratios were similar for MCE and the comparison cohort. Despite a decline in health care contacts as the child aged, HCU rates for MCE remained elevated.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the use of health care services for MCE compared to a matched general population comparison cohort. Health care use in mothers might reflect the immediate distress following their child’s epilepsy diagnosis6,12 as well as the subsequent consequences of a possible disregard and postponement of their own health needs, even after the child has turned 18 years. Our study adds quantitative evidence and describes the patterns of health care service use. In general, HCU is slightly lower as the age at epilepsy diagnosis gets higher. The use of inpatient care and primary care was highest when the child was below 10 years old, which might implicate an increasing burden from the time of epilepsy diagnosis. Although we found an increased relative risk of psychiatric service contacts, the rates remained comparable to the comparison cohort. This might be due to the natural cause of childhood onset as a transient condition resulting in complete remission, improved treatment options and improved seizure control among patients. The increase in surgical contacts over time could be explained by the postponement of minor and non-acute interventions for the mother. The subgroup analysis by child’s age allowed for the identification of variations in HCU following the time of diagnosis. It was hypothesized that the child’s age and the initial 5-year follow-up period would exhibit the most significant differences in HCU.

To date, studies have tried to evaluate HCU among mothers with a severely ill child, requiring specialist evaluation and care.14,28–31 Our results are consistent with a study evaluating HCU in mothers giving birth to a child with a major congenital malformation, in which the mothers had greater HCU across services.14 Our findings also align with those of a study examining parents of children diagnosed with cancer, where mothers of children with cancer had a higher risk of mental health disorders in the first year after diagnosis.13 Studies of quality of life among patients with childhood-onset epilepsy showed substantial quality of life deficits in both later childhood and adulthood.32–36 It is well known that these patients have poorer physical fitness,37 higher levels of comorbidity, in particular psychiatric comorbidity,33 poorer educational level,38 are less likely to be employed2,36 and hence with lower socioeconomic status.32 The burden for caregivers of a child with epilepsy might show a corresponding increase in psychiatric contacts as a result of experiencing depressive periods, and emotional stress,6,10–12,39,40 but the mean annual number of HCU contacts for psychiatric care services was comparable between MCE and non-affected mothers in our study. Psychiatric contacts in the study might only reflect the most severe episodes of psychiatric disease, since mild and moderate psychiatric symptoms are most often managed in the primary care setting.

The main strength of this study is the use of routinely collected registry data with high validity and completeness, enabling long-term follow up of our cohorts. The coding of epilepsy diagnosis has been validated in the DNPR and PCRR, yielding a positive predictive value of 81% (95% CI: 75–87%).41 This study was conducted in a longitudinal setting among mothers in Denmark, where health care is primarily covered through government taxes. This allowed us to examine the long-term effects of caregiver burden while eliminating the effects of access to care or the ability to pay for services.

The study also has some limitations. The slightly higher HCU pattern observed in the MCE cohort compared to unaffected mothers could be due to their baseline characteristics, eg the exposed cohort had a slightly larger proportion of women in the low-income and low-education group compared to the unexposed cohort, and a larger proportion of MCEs had a higher CCI score than unaffected mothers. Unmeasured variables such as paternal factors, duration of the child’s hospital stay, or social determinants not captured at baseline could introduce residual confounding. Additionally, the inability to stratify analyses by epilepsy severity or subtype could obscure differential impacts. Exposed mothers indicated a greater prevalence of comorbidities at index date, which may partially account for their increased HCU, particularly over extended follow-up periods. Approximately 60% of the children in this study were diagnosed before the age of six years and 17% within the first year of life. This might have produced a time lag before diagnosis and a period of uncertainty and worry for the caregiver. This period could have led to increased use of health care and hence bias our results. Another concern is that we did not have the opportunity to stratify our analyses for severity of epilepsy. One might theorize that milder forms of epilepsy present with non-specific symptoms for a time before triggering an epilepsy diagnosis, since 2/3 of the exposed children had idiopathic epilepsy, which includes the different types of absence epilepsy.42 Furthermore, misclassification might have occurred, since education, income levels, and comorbid conditions were measured only at index date and are prone to change throughout a woman’s lifetime. Unmeasured confounding factors, such as paternal characteristics, neonatal infections, other brain injuries or congenital conditions, genetics, or drug or alcohol abuse, were not included in this analysis, thereby causing residual confounding and a bias away from the null. Finally, the observed association between MCE and HCU may be influenced by the distinctive features of the Danish national health care system, which offers comprehensive services and an extensive range of social benefits, and we cannot rule out the influence on this is the associations observed in this study. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted with caution when applying them to countries with different health care systems. Caring for a child with epilepsy does not impact all mothers equally, and the condition varies widely in severity. Nevertheless, our results highlight the need to provide integrated care for mothers of children with epilepsy to promote prevention, detection, and adequate mental health support. These findings also have practical implications for health care policy and caregiver support programs, emphasizing the importance of proactive mental health screening and tailored interventions to reduce long-term burden and optimize resource allocation.

Conclusion

Mothers of children with epilepsy had more health care contacts than unexposed mothers, depending on the type of service, up to 20 years after a child’s diagnosis, regardless of the age at epilepsy diagnosis. Increased health care contact might be a marker of the physical and psychological challenges mothers of children with epilepsy face and imply an increased risk of poor health. These findings underscore the need for proactive mental health support and integrated care planning for mothers to mitigate long-term psychological burden.
