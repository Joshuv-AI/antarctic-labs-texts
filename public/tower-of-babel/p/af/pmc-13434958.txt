Associations between albumin–bilirubin score and all-cause mortality among individuals with acute myocardial infarction: A cohort study based upon MIMIC-IV
Authors: Xianan Xu, Jinyun Shen, Yanan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) score is a validated prognostic tool in liver diseases. Nevertheless, its role in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains underexplored. This research sought to explore associations between ALBI score and all-cause mortality (ACM) among individuals with AMI.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used MIMIC-IV v3.1 data. Patients were classified into four groups by ALBI score quartiles. The primary outcomes encompassed ACM within 30 and 365 days. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used to compare differences in survival outcomes among the four groups. Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate associations between ALBI scores and ACM. Moreover, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to investigate dose–response relationships between ALBI score and ACM at different time points among individuals with AMI. We calculated the area under the ROC curve (AUC), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and net reclassification improvement (NRI) to evaluate the incremental predictive value of adding ALBI to the SOFA, SAPSII, and APSIII scoring systems.ResultsIn total, 2,105 patients were included, 1,294(61.47%) were men. KM curves showed lower survival in highest vs lowest ALBI quartile((log-rank P<0.05) Cox proportional hazards regression analysis indicated that in the extensively adjusted model, when ALBI was analyzed as a continuous variable, each one-unit elevation correlated with a 47.7% rise in ACM within 30 days(adjusted HR=1.477, 95%CI:1.249–1.746, P<0.001) and a 52.2% rise in ACM within 365 days (adjusted HR=1.522, 95%CI:1.331–1.741, P<0.001).When ALBI was examined as a categorical variable, compared to the Q1 group, the risks of ACM in the Q4 group were 1.873 times higher within 30 days and 1.784 times higher within 365 days. RCS analyses indicated a positive linear association between ALBI score and ACM within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days. In addition, the inclusion of ALBI in the SOFA, SAPSII, and APSIII scoring systems enhanced the predictive accuracy of the models.ConclusionsHigh ALBI scores may be associated with an increased risk of ACM in critically ill patients with AMI admitted to ICUs, and ALBI may provide incremental prognostic information independent of conventional critical illness scoring systems.Keywords: acute myocardial infarction, ALBI, all-cause mortality, MIMIC-IV database, critically ill populations

1. Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) encompasses all instances of myocardial necrosis attributable to acute coronary artery occlusion. 1 The American Heart Association estimates that the overall prevalence of AMI is 3%, 2 with approximately 7 million deaths occurring globally each year. 3 Although the age- and sex-adjusted incidence of AMI has declined substantially over recent decades owing to advances in primary prevention, 2 AMI remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide. 4 Nonetheless, research regarding prognostic indicators for severe AMI remains relatively scarce. Identification and management of risk indicators are crucial for reducing mortality in these populations.

Emerging evidence suggests that serum albumin (ALB) and total bilirubin (TBIL) correlate with prognosis and mortality among critically ill populations with AMI.5–8 However, the predictive utility of single indices may be limited. The albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) score serves as an emerging tool for estimating hepatic function. It has been demonstrated to reflect hepatic synthetic function and the metabolic status of bilirubin, 9 exhibiting favorable accuracy and reliability in forecasting outcomes among individuals with liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and those undergoing liver transplantation.10–12 With the increasing application of the ALBI score in non-hepatic diseases, studies have revealed its excellent prognostic performance in conditions like acute kidney injury, heart failure, esophageal cancer, and pancreatic cancer.10,13 Nevertheless, associations between ALBI scores and outcomes among populations with AMI remain inadequately investigated.

Therefore, this research sought to explore associations between ALBI score and all-cause mortality (ACM) among critically ill populations with AMI. Our findings might provide novel insights for early detecting high-risk populations and formulating prognostic improvement strategies.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source

This retrospective study was conducted by using data from Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV; v 3.1). MIMIC-IV is a relational database that comprises authentic hospitalization data for individuals admitted to a tertiary academic medical facility in Boston, MA, USA. It encompassed clinical information on all individuals admitted to the ICU between 2008 and 2022, encompassing demographic characteristics, hospital length of stay (LOS), ICU admission and discharge records, vital signs, laboratory data, administration of medication, and nursing records.14,15 To access the database, the author (Jinyun Shen) completed the National Institutes of Health course on the protection of human research participants and passed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative examination (ID: 14648417). Given the database did not encompass protected health information and all data on patients were de-identified, the requirement for informed consent was unnecessary. All patient records in this database were fully de-identified (with no protected health information). This study was conducted in compliance with the 2024 revised Declaration of Helsinki and was granted institutional review board exemption for retrospective data analysis with a waiver of written informed consent. The reporting of this study complies with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 16

2.2. Study design and population

This research analyzed 9,086 individuals (≥18 years) with AMI who were admitted to the ICU for the first time. Exclusion criteria were outlined below: (i) individuals discharged or deceased within twenty-four hours after ICU admission; (ii) individuals with missing or implausible data on survival time; (iii) individuals lacking measurements of ALB or TBIL within the first twenty-four hours after ICU admission. The exact number of patients excluded due to missing ALB/TBIL was 5,422, and baseline comparison between included and excluded cohorts is displayed in Table S7 to assess potential selection bias induced by non-random missing laboratory data. Finally, 2,105 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the analysis. They were stratified into four groups based upon ALBI quartiles (Figure 1). All included patients were critically ill patients with AMI presenting with hemodynamic instability, respiratory failure, severe arrhythmia, cardiogenic shock, or multi-organ dysfunction and requiring continuous ICU monitoring.

Flowchart of the selection of the study population from the MIMIC-IV database.

2.3. Data extraction

Data were extracted using PostgreSQL 17. The extracted variables encompassed the following categories: (i) Demographics covered age, sex, and race; (ii) Comorbidities encompassed hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), atrial fibrillation, and sepsis; (iii) Vital signs encompassed temperature (T), respiratory rate (RR), heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2); (iv) Laboratory parameters encompassed white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), TBIL, and ALB; (v) Pharmacologic and procedural interventions included aspirin, β-blockers, invasive mechanical ventilation (MV), and non-invasive MV (NMV); (vi) LOS and outcomes included ICU LOS, and ACM within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days; (vii) Disease severity scores included Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) (Table S1).

All laboratory parameters and vital signs were measured within twenty-four hours of ICU admission and before any treatments. Variables with missing data surpassing 20% were excluded from the analysis. For variables with < 20% missing values, multiple imputation was implemented utilizing the ‘mice’ package (with 5 imputations) (Table S2).

2.4. Outcomes

The primary outcomes were ACM within 30 and 365 days after ICU admission. Secondary outcomes included ACM within 14 and 90 days after ICU admission.

2.5. Calculation of ALBI score

The ALBI score was attained using the first recorded levels of ALB and TBIL within twenty-four hours after ICU admission. The ALBI score was defined as:

ALBI score = (log10 TBIL [μmol/L] × 0.66) + (ALB [g/L] × −0.085). 17

2.6. Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed for continuous variables. As all continuous variables in this research were non-normally distributed, they were presented as median and interquartile range. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were shown as absolute numbers and percentages. Intergroup differences were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used to evaluate the incidence of the primary and secondary outcomes. Patients were subsequently stratified based on ALBI score. This research examined multicollinearity for all included variables, thereby ensuring that all variance inflation factors were < 5(Table S3). Cox proportional hazards models were used to explore associations between ALBI scores and ACM. The Schoenfeld residual test was used to validate proportional hazards assumption for all variables, and all P > 0.05 satisfied proportional hazards condition. Model1 included solely the ALBI score; Model2 was adjusted for sex, race, and age; and Model3 was adjusted for sex, race, age, heart rate, RR, SBP, DBP, SpO2, WBC, PLT, HGB, DM, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, sepsis, aspirin, β-blockers, NMV, MV, and GCS score. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to investigate dose-response relationships between ALBI scores and ACM. Subgroup analyses were performed by sex (men, women), age (< 70 years or ≥70 years), and the status of DM, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and sepsis. We plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) to evaluate the discrimination of models and assess the predictive performance of ALBI. The DeLong test was used to compare the AUC differences between models with and without ALBI. Additionally, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) were calculated to quantify the incremental predictive value of adding ALBI as a continuous variable to the SOFA, SAPSII, and APSIII scores for clinical outcomes. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v 4.5.1). P < 0.05 signified statistical significance.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline features of study population

In total, 9,086 patients were enrolled, of whom 2,105 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. The median age of the subjects was 70.91 (61.43, 80.91) years, and 1,294 (61.47%) were men. Overall, 454 patients died within 30 days following ICU admission. The mortality rate was 21.57%. Meanwhile, 772 patients died within 365 days following ICU admission. The mortality rate was 36.67%.

Participants were stratified into four groups based on ALBI scores: Q1 (−3.6349 ≤ ALBI < −2.4515), Q2 (−2.4515 ≤ ALBI < −2.0839), Q3 (−2.0839 ≤ ALBI < −1.6589), and Q4 (−1.6589 ≤ ALBI < 0.1227). Table 1 provides the baseline features of these groups. Relative to the lowest ALBI group (Q1), individuals in the highest ALBI group (Q4) demonstrated significantly older age, prolonged ICU LOS, higher incidences of atrial fibrillation and sepsis, increased proportion of patients receiving MV, elevated SOFA, SAPS II, and APS III scores, and higher levels of TBIL, WBC, heart rate, and RR. Nevertheless, they exhibited lower ALB, SBP, DBP, PLT, and HGB. Notably, mortality rates were significantly higher in Q4 across all observed time points.

3.2. KM curves

KM curves demonstrated significant differences in ACM across varied ALBI groups. Compared to the lower ALBI groups (Q1, Q2, Q3), individuals in the highest ALBI group (Q4) exhibited significantly lower survival rates within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days (log-rank P < 0.001) (Figure 2).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for all-cause mortality stratified by ALBI index. Kaplan–Meier curves for all-cause mortality at 14 days (a), 30 days (b), 90 days (c), and 1 year (d).

3.3. Cox proportional hazards model

Schoenfeld residuals were applied to verify the proportional hazards assumption of all Cox models (Figure 3). Cox proportional hazards models were employed to explore associations between ALBI scores and ACM. In the extensively adjusted model, when ALBI was treated as a continuous variable, every one-unit elevation in ALBI correlated with a 47.7% rise in ACM within 30 days (HR = 1.477, 95% CI = 1.249, 1.746, P < 0.001). When ALBI was determined as a categorical variable, the risks of ACM in the Q4 group were 1.873 times higher compared to the Q1 group (HR = 1.873, 95% CI: 1.375–2.552, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Similarly, when ALBI was treated as a continuous variable, every one-unit elevation in ALBI correlated with a 52.2% rise in ACM within 365 days (HR = 1.522, 95% CI = 1.331, 1.741, P < 0.001). When ALBI was determined as a categorical variable, the risks of ACM in the Q4 group were 1.784 times higher relative to the Q1 group (HR = 1.784, 95% CI = 1.415, 2.249, P < 0.001) (Table S4). Comparable trends were detected for ACM within 14 and 90 days (Tables S5-S6).

The Schoenfeld residual test is used to rigorously test the proportional hazards (PH) assumption for all Cox models.

3.4. RCS

RCS was applied to evaluate dose–response relationships between ALBI and ACM at different time points among patients with AMI. RCS analysis indicated a positive linear association between ALBI and ACM within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days (P for overall <0.05; P for nonlinear >0.05 for all time points) (Figure 4).

Restricted cubic splines for the association between ALBI index and all-cause mortality. RCS of ALBI index with all-cause mortality at 14 days (a), 30 days (b), 90 days (c), and 1 year (d).

3.5. Subgroup analyses

To further assess whether the associations between ALBI scores and ACM within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days persisted under different conditions, subgroup analyses were performed by sex, age, DM, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and sepsis. The number of death events for each subgroup was added to the legend of Figure 5 to improve the transparency of the results. The results indicated that the hazard ratios (HRs) for ACM within 14 days remained significant in all subgroups except for women, individuals with DM, those without hypertension, and those without sepsis (P < 0.05). For ACM within 30 days, the HRs were significant in all subgroups except for women (P < 0.05). Regarding ACM within 90 days, significant HRs were detected in all subgroups except for women, individuals without hypertension, and those without sepsis (P < 0.05). Finally, the HRs for ACM within 365 days were significant across all subgroups (P < 0.05). The non-significant association in female subgroups may be attributed to limited sample size and heterogeneous baseline systemic stress among female patients, both of which reduced statistical power.

Association with all-cause mortality. Forest plots showing subgroup analyses of the ALBI index for 14-day (a), 30-day (b), 90-day (c), and 1-year (d) all-cause mortality.

Interaction analyses indicated no significant differences in ACM within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days regarding age (<70 or ≥70 years), presence of DM, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, or sepsis (P for interaction >0.05) (Figure 5). No significant interaction was observed for sex regarding ACM within 14, 30, and 90 days (P for interaction >0.05) (Figure 5). Our findings suggested that the positive association between ALBI scores and ACM among individuals with AMI was consistent across different subpopulations and might be generalizable to varied clinical settings.

3.6. Incremental value of adding ALBI to disease severity scores for predicting clinical outcomes

To evaluate the predictive performance of ALBI for ACM, we calculated the AUC values. The AUCs of SOFA, SAPS II, and APS III scores for predicting ACM within 30 days were 0.698, 0.753, and 0.750, respectively (Figure 6(a)–(c)). After we added ALBI as a continuous variable to each of these three models, the AUCs increased (SOFA + ALBI: 0.709; SAPS II + ALBI: 0.760; APS III + ALBI: 0.753). The DeLong test indicated that the differences in AUC between the models were statistically significant (Table 3). To further quantify the incremental predictive value of ALBI beyond the baseline critical illness scores, we calculated the IDI and NRI. The results showed that adding ALBI significantly improved the performance of models. For the prediction of ACM within 30 days, the IDI values for the SOFA + ALBI, SAPS II + ALBI, and APS III + ALBI models were 0.010, 0.010, and 0.006 (P < 0.001, P < 0.001, and P < 0.005, respectively), with corresponding NRI values of 0.193, 0.208, and 0.144 (all P < 0.001) (Table 3). These findings were consistently observed in the analysis of ACM within 365 days (Figure 6(d)–(f)). ALBI demonstrated robust incremental predictive value across most time points and models.

4. Discussion

This research included 2,105 participants to explore associations between the ALBI score and ACM among individuals with AMI. These findings indicate a significant positive linear association between ALBI scores and ACM within 14, 30, 90, and 365 days among individuals with AMI. Further subgroup analyses and interaction tests substantiated the reliability of this association. However, this retrospective clinical cohort study can only demonstrate a statistical association. It cannot establish causality or elucidate the underlying molecular pathways.

The association between ALBI scores and unfavorable prognosis among individuals with AMI might be attributable to several interrelated mechanisms. Hypoalbuminemia leads to a reduction in plasma oncotic pressure and insufficient effective circulating blood volume. This process further decreases coronary perfusion pressure, enlarges the area of ischemic infarction, and increases the risk of cardiogenic shock. 18 Given that ALB serves as a carrier for nutritional substrates, its deficiency results in insufficient supply of energy substrates for myocardial metabolism, thereby exacerbating energy depletion and necrosis of cardiomyocytes. Additionally, ALB acts as a carrier for medications such as antiplatelet agents and statins. Reduced ALB levels decrease the bioavailability of these drugs and impair therapeutic efficacy.5,19 Moreover, the onset of AMI disrupts the physiological balance between oxidative stress and inflammation. Bilirubin exerts dual biological effects. Mild hyperbilirubinemia confers anti-inflammatory and antioxidant protective properties, whereas persistent and markedly elevated bilirubin levels indicate severe dysfunction of multiple systemic organs and predict poor long-term prognosis. Hypoalbuminemia combined with hyperbilirubinemia promotes massive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS damage cardiomyocyte and endothelial lipid membranes and activate mitochondrial apoptotic pathways, thereby accelerating myocardial necrosis and promoting platelet aggregation and thrombosis.20–22 Oxidative stress further activates the NF-κB pathway and stimulates the secretion of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, which indirectly induce secondary hepatocyte injury after AMI.23–25 Impaired liver synthetic function reduces the production of coagulation factors and the metabolic clearance of drugs, further increasing the risks of bleeding, thrombosis and cardiorenal damage.25,26 In addition, low albumin limits the transport of repair-related growth factors to ischemic myocardium, inhibits angiogenesis and cardiac stem cell proliferation, aggravates long-term ventricular remodeling, and finally increases the incidence of heart failure and malignant arrhythmia. 5

A large number of existing AMI cohort studies have proven that isolated albumin or total bilirubin can independently predict long-term mortality.27–31 A Chinese AMI cohort has reported that each 1 g/dL decrease in albumin is associated with a 66% increase in ACM and 47% increase in cardiovascular death. 29 Other studies have also verified that elevated bilirubin is an adverse prognostic marker for patients with AMI.20,31 However, single laboratory indicators only reflect a single pathological dimension. Our head-to-head ROC comparison analysis based on patients with AMI confirmed that ALBI possessed significantly superior predictive AUC to albumin alone or bilirubin alone, thereby demonstrating that ALBI could provide unique prognostic information beyond simple hypoalbuminemia. Previous studies on heart failure have also found ALBI could independently predict in-hospital mortality.10,32 A recent cohort study on moderate- to high-risk pulmonary embolism has confirmed that ALBI is a systemic marker reflecting systemic haemodynamic load and multi-organ dysfunction. Instead, it should not be viewed as a liver-specific indicator associated solely with coronary ischaemia. This finding further confirms the universal prognostic value of ALBI across various types of acute cardiovascular disease. 33

Although ALBI is closely associated with overall critical illness burden indicators, including SOFA, SAPS II and APS III, supplementary incremental prognostic analyses (C-index, NRI and IDI) indicate that, after full adjustment, ALBI provides additional predictive information independent of traditional critical illness scoring systems. This finding suggests that ALBI is not merely a surrogate marker reflecting overall disease severity. The ALBI score relies solely on albumin and bilirubin levels routinely measured on admission, which are readily available in clinical practice. This study suggests that ALBI may serve as an adjunctive tool for early risk stratification in patients with AMI in the ICU. However, its precise clinical utility needs to be validated in further external prospective studies.

This research has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single-center study conducted in a US ICU, this cohort cannot support causal inference. Its findings may not generalize to non-ICU settings, international populations, or broader AMI groups. Second, due to limitations in the MIMIC-IV dataset structure, it is not possible to obtain data on subtypes of AMI, reperfusion therapy, left ventricular ejection fraction and troponin levels. This omission remains a significant source of residual confounding. Third, a large proportion of participants were excluded due to missing albumin and bilirubin test results. Baseline comparison between included and excluded patients (Table S8) revealed that patients with complete ALBI laboratory data carried a heavier critical illness burden, which introduced potential selection bias and may compromise the validity of study conclusions. Fourth, all multi-timepoint and subgroup analyses were post-hoc exploratory analyses with an increased risk of false positives. Therefore, all subgroup results should be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, inflammatory markers such as CRP, procalcitonin and IL-6 had high missing rates and were excluded from analysis, restricting in-depth exploration of underlying pathological pathways. Therefore, large-scale, multicentre, prospective external validation cohort studies are required in the future to validate the conclusions of this study.

5. Conclusion

This study suggests that elevated ALBI score may be associated with an increased risk of ACM among critically ill patients with AMI in the ICU As a readily available laboratory composite index, ALBI can serve as an adjunctive tool for early risk stratification in patients with AMI admitted to ICUs. However, its clinical utility remains to be validated externally.
