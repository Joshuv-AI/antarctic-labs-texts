Anti-Thyroid Peroxidase Antibodies and Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone for Risk Stratification of Subclinical Thyroid Dysfunction in Patients With Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Walaa Mohammedsaeed, Samah Alfahl, Reham Aljohani, Amjad Alamri, Shahad Almutairi, Mai Almutairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThyroid dysfunction and autoimmune thyroid disease frequently coexist with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), yet optimal screening strategies remain uncertain. Anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (anti-TPO) may help identify patients at increased risk of thyroid dysfunction before overt biochemical abnormalities occur. This study evaluated the clinical decision-support utility of anti-TPO and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) for risk stratification of subclinical thyroid dysfunction in T2DM.MethodsIn this matched case–control study, 200 adults with T2DM and 100 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were recruited. Clinical, glycemic, lipid, and thyroid parameters were assessed. Subclinical hypothyroidism was defined as elevated TSH with normal free thyroxine. Multivariable logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic analysis, net reclassification improvement, and decision curve analysis were used to evaluate discriminative performance and clinical utility.ResultsSubclinical hypothyroidism was more prevalent in patients with T2DM than controls (27.5% vs 3.0%, P < 0.001). Anti-TPO positivity was higher among diabetics and independently associated with subclinical hypothyroidism. The combined anti-TPO and TSH model demonstrated superior discrimination (area under the curve 0.88) and improved clinical utility compared with TSH alone.ConclusionAnti-TPO and TSH are independently associated with thyroid dysfunction (subclinical hypothyroidism) in T2DM. Their combined use significantly improves risk discrimination and clinical utility. Screening for thyroid autoimmunity in diabetics, especially women and those with poor glycemic control, may enhance early detection and improve long-term outcomes. These findings support a targeted, risk-stratified screening approach, rather than universal thyroid autoimmunity testing in all patients with T2DM.

Clinical Relevance

This article highlights that subclinical hypothyroidism is relatively common among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and that autoimmune thyroid markers, particularly anti-TPO antibodies, are more prevalent in this group compared to non-diabetic controls. Clinically, it emphasizes that combining anti-TPO status with TSH levels enhances risk stratification for thyroid dysfunction beyond using TSH alone, leading to better diagnostic accuracy and potential clinical decision-making benefits. The findings suggest that incorporating autoimmune markers is especially valuable in high-risk diabetic subgroups, supporting a more targeted and individualized approach to screening and managing thyroid disease in patients with diabetes.

Introduction

Despite growing recognition of the coexistence of thyroid dysfunction and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), current screening practices remain largely uniform and rely predominantly on thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) measurement alone, with limited consideration of autoimmune risk.1, 2, 3 Although anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (anti-TPO) are established markers of autoimmune thyroid disease and have been reported at higher prevalence in patients with T2DM, existing studies are primarily descriptive or cross-sectional and do not address their potential role in clinical risk stratification.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Moreover, few investigations have evaluated whether combining anti-TPO with conventional thyroid markers meaningfully improves identification of individuals at increased risk of subclinical thyroid dysfunction, particularly in high-prevalence populations.9

Although subclinical thyroid dysfunction is often perceived as a benign or incidental finding, accumulating evidence suggests that even mild abnormalities in TSH may have clinically relevant consequences, particularly in patients with T2DM. Subclinical hypothyroidism has been associated with impaired glycemic control, increased insulin resistance, adverse lipid profiles, endothelial dysfunction, and elevated cardiovascular risk. In individuals with T2DM, who already carry a high burden of metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidity, these effects may compound existing risks. Importantly, identification of subclinical thyroid dysfunction does not necessarily mandate immediate treatment, but rather enables targeted monitoring, risk stratification, and individualized clinical decision-making. Recognizing this condition is therefore relevant to optimizing long-term metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes in diabetic populations. Subclinical hypothyroidism is conventionally defined by elevated TSH levels in the presence of normal circulating free thyroxine (free T4). However, thyroid dysfunction exists along a biological continuum, and autoimmune thyroid activity, most commonly reflected by anti-TPO antibody positivity, may be present before biochemical abnormalities in TSH become apparent. In this context, anti-TPO positivity represents a state of subclinical thyroid autoimmunity or susceptibility, rather than established subclinical hypothyroidism. While TSH is essential for diagnosis, it may not fully capture early autoimmune processes that precede or accompany mild thyroid dysfunction, particularly in metabolically vulnerable populations such as patients with T2DM.

Importantly, regions such as Saudi Arabia experience a disproportionate burden of metabolic disease, where genetic susceptibility, obesity, and chronic glycemic stress may accelerate autoimmune and endocrine comorbidities.10, 11, 12, 13 In this context, reliance on TSH alone may fail to detect early autoimmune activity in biochemically euthyroid patients, delaying recognition of those most likely to progress to thyroid dysfunction. To date, no study in the Middle Eastern population has systematically evaluated the combined clinical utility of anti-TPO and TSH using risk-classification and decision-analytic approaches.

Importantly, the objective of this study is not to identify new biological mechanisms or novel biomarkers, but to evaluate the incremental clinical decision-support value of an established autoimmune marker when combined with routine thyroid testing. By applying discrimination, risk reclassification, and decision-analytic methods, this work focuses on whether anti-TPO antibodies improve risk stratification and targeted screening decisions in patients with T2DM rather than on biological discovery.

Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the value of anti-TPO antibodies and TSH, individually and in combination, for risk stratification of subclinical thyroid dysfunction in patients with T2DM. By incorporating multivariable modeling alongside receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and decision curve analysis (DCA), this study moves beyond association to evaluate whether autoimmune markers provide incremental clinical benefit for identifying high-risk diabetic subgroups who may benefit from targeted thyroid screening.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A matched case–control study was conducted between January 2024 and May 2025 at outpatient endocrine and primary care clinics in Madinah, Saudi Arabia. The study was designed to evaluate the clinical utility of anti-TPO antibodies and TSH, individually and in combination, for risk stratification of subclinical thyroid dysfunction among patients with T2DM.

Study Population

Adults aged 30–50 years with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for at least 2 years were recruited as cases. Age- (±2 years) and sex-matched healthy individuals without a history of diabetes, thyroid disease, autoimmune disorders, or endocrine conditions were enrolled as controls at a 2:1 ratio. Participants were excluded if they had known thyroid disease, prior thyroid surgery, pregnancy or lactation, renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), hepatic dysfunction, malignancy, or use of medications known to affect thyroid function, including corticosteroids, amiodarone, biotin, or thyroid hormone preparations within the preceding 3 months.

Sample Size and Matching

Sample size estimation was performed using G∗Power version 3.1, indicating that a minimum of 276 participants was required to detect a moderate effect size with 80% power at a two-sided significance level of 0.05 for multivariable logistic regression analyses. To account for potential exclusions and to enable subgroup analyses, a total of 300 participants were enrolled. Frequency matching by age and sex was applied to minimize confounding and enhance comparability between groups.

Data Collection and Clinical Assessment

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), duration of diabetes, medication use, and comorbid conditions, were obtained through structured interviews and medical record review. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized according to World Health Organization criteria.8

Laboratory Measurements

Following an overnight fast of 10–12 h, venous blood samples were collected and processed within 2 hours in a CAP-accredited laboratory. Fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and lipid profiles were measured using standardized enzymatic and chromatographic methods. TSH and free T4 concentrations were determined by chemiluminescent immunoassay, while anti-TPO antibodies were quantified using a validated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Anti-TPO levels >35 IU/mL were considered positive in accordance with manufacturer-defined cutoffs. Subclinical hypothyroidism was defined as elevated TSH (>4.5 μIU/mL) in the presence of normal free T4 levels.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and MedCalc version 20.1. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests or nonparametric equivalents for continuous variables and McNemar’s test for categorical variables. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate factors associated with subclinical thyroid dysfunction, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed, adjusting for clinically relevant covariates including age, sex, BMI, glycemic control, lipid parameters, and diabetes duration. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. These analyses were designed to assess clinical discrimination, risk classification, and net clinical benefit, rather than to infer causality or underlying biological mechanisms.

Risk Stratification and Clinical Utility Analyses

The discriminative performance of anti-TPO and TSH, individually and in combination, was assessed using ROC curve analysis, with areas under the curve compared using DeLong’s test. Optimal thresholds were identified using the Youden index. To evaluate whether the addition of anti-TPO provided incremental clinical value beyond TSH, NRI was calculated after adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes duration. DCA was performed to estimate the net clinical benefit of each model across a range of threshold probabilities, thereby assessing their potential utility for targeted screening in clinical practice.

Subgroup Analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the consistency of risk stratification performance across clinically relevant groups, including sex, glycemic control (HbA1c <7% vs ≥7%), and diabetes duration. ROC analyses and regression models were repeated within each subgroup to identify populations in which anti-TPO provided the greatest incremental value.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 300 participants were included in the final analysis, comprising 200 patients with T2DM and 100 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Baseline demographic characteristics were comparable between groups, with no significant differences in age or sex distribution, confirming successful matching. Patients with T2DM exhibited significantly higher BMI and poorer glycemic control compared with controls. The prevalence of thyroid autoimmunity, reflected by anti-TPO antibody positivity, was also significantly higher in the T2DM group. Prevalence of Subclinical Thyroid Dysfunction and Anti-TPO Positivity

Subclinical hypothyroidism, defined as elevated TSH with normal free T4 levels, was observed in 27.5% of patients with T2DM compared with 3.0% of control participants (P < 0.001). Similarly, anti-TPO positivity was markedly more prevalent among individuals with T2DM (32.0%) than among controls (9.0%) (P < 0.001). These findings indicate a substantially higher burden of both biochemical thyroid dysfunction and underlying thyroid autoimmunity in the diabetic population (Supplementary Table S1).

Factors Associated with Subclinical Hypothyroidism in T2DM

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with subclinical hypothyroidism among patients with T2DM. After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, glycemic indices, lipid parameters, and diabetes duration, anti-TPO positivity remained strongly associated with subclinical hypothyroidism (odds ratio 4.21, 95% confidence interval 2.33–7.98). Higher TSH levels, poorer glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%), and longer diabetes duration were also independently associated with the presence of subclinical hypothyroidism. Anti-TPO alone demonstrated good discrimination, while TSH alone showed moderate discrimination. Incremental Risk Classification with Anti-TPO

To evaluate whether anti-TPO provided incremental value beyond TSH, NRI analysis was performed. Addition of anti-TPO to a base model including TSH resulted in a significant improvement in risk classification, with an NRI of +19% (P = 0.003). This indicates that incorporating autoimmune status meaningfully enhanced identification of individuals at higher risk for subclinical thyroid dysfunction (Supplementary Table S2).

Clinical Utility Assessment

DCA was conducted to assess the potential clinical benefit of each model across a range of threshold probabilities. The combined anti-TPO and TSH model consistently demonstrated greater net benefit compared with either marker alone, supporting its potential utility for targeted thyroid screening in patients with T2DM. Subgroup Analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses revealed variability in the risk-stratification performance of anti-TPO across clinically relevant populations. Anti-TPO demonstrated stronger discriminative ability in female patients, in those with poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%), and in individuals with longer diabetes duration. These findings suggest that thyroid autoimmunity assessment may be particularly informative in specific high-risk subgroups within the T2DM population (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

Although the biological association between anti-TPO antibodies and thyroid dysfunction is well established, the present study extends existing knowledge by quantifying the incremental discriminative and clinical utility of anti-TPO when added to TSH using NRI and DCA. Therefore, in this matched case–control study, we demonstrated a high prevalence of subclinical thyroid dysfunction and thyroid autoimmunity among patients with T2DM compared with age- and sex-matched controls. Nearly one-third of diabetic participants exhibited subclinical hypothyroidism, and anti-TPO antibody positivity was more than threefold higher than in nondiabetic individuals. Beyond describing prevalence, this study provides evidence that anti-TPO positivity, elevated TSH, poor glycemic control, and longer diabetes duration are independently associated with subclinical thyroid dysfunction. Importantly, combining anti-TPO with TSH improved discrimination, risk classification, and clinical net benefit, supporting a risk-stratified approach to thyroid screening in T2DM. While the association between anti-TPO antibodies and thyroid dysfunction is well established, the present study extends existing knowledge by demonstrating their incremental value for clinical risk stratification and screening decision-making, rather than by proposing new biological pathways.

Thyroid Dysfunction and Autoimmunity in T2DM

The observed prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism in patients with T2DM is consistent with earlier reports demonstrating increased thyroid dysfunction among diabetic populations.2,3 Thyroid hormones play a critical role in glucose homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolism, while chronic hyperglycemia may disrupt the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis and impair peripheral thyroid hormone conversion.14,15 This bidirectional interaction may partly explain the high coexistence of these conditions and underscores the importance of integrated endocrine assessment in T2DM.

Anti-TPO antibodies are well-established markers of autoimmune thyroid disease and often precede biochemical or clinical thyroid dysfunction.4,7 In line with previous regional and international studies, we found a significantly higher prevalence of anti-TPO positivity among patients with T2DM compared with controls.5, 6, 7,9 These findings support the concept that autoimmune thyroid activity may be under-recognized in diabetic patients who are biochemically euthyroid and asymptomatic.

Based on the present findings, a pragmatic, risk-stratified screening algorithm can be proposed for clinical practice. In patients with T2DM, serum TSH should remain the initial screening test as part of routine metabolic evaluation. Measurement of anti-TPO antibodies should be selectively considered in individuals with normal or borderline TSH who belong to higher-risk subgroups, including women, those with poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%), and patients with long-standing diabetes. Anti-TPO positivity in euthyroid individuals should prompt closer biochemical surveillance rather than immediate treatment. This stepwise approach supports targeted screening and avoids unnecessary universal testing. Although elevated TSH is integral to the definition of subclinical hypothyroidism, reliance on TSH alone may fail to identify individuals with underlying autoimmune thyroid susceptibility who remain biochemically euthyroid. In the present study, anti-TPO antibodies were not evaluated as a substitute for TSH, but as a complementary marker that captures autoimmune activity associated with thyroid dysfunction. The improved discriminative performance and risk stratification observed when anti-TPO was combined with TSH reflect the ability of this dual-marker approach to distinguish between established subclinical hypothyroidism and earlier stages of thyroid involvement. This distinction is clinically relevant, as anti-TPO positivity in euthyroid individuals may justify closer surveillance rather than immediate therapeutic intervention.

Metabolic–Immune Interactions

Our results also revealed significant associations between anti-TPO positivity and markers of glycemic burden, including fasting glucose and HbA1c. Chronic hyperglycemia has been shown to promote oxidative stress, low-grade inflammation, and immune dysregulation, potentially accelerating autoimmune responses against thyroid antigens.15,16 This metabolic–immune interaction is further supported by the independent association between longer diabetes duration and subclinical hypothyroidism observed in our multivariable analyses. Together, these findings suggest that cumulative metabolic stress may amplify autoimmune susceptibility over time, increasing the risk of thyroid dysfunction in long-standing or poorly controlled T2DM.

Clinical Value of Combined Biomarker Assessment

A key strength of this study is the evaluation of anti-TPO and TSH not only as individual markers but also as a combined risk-stratification tool. While TSH remains the cornerstone of thyroid screening, it reflects a relatively late biochemical change and may fail to identify patients with underlying autoimmune activity.2,7 Our ROC analysis demonstrated that the combined anti-TPO and TSH model provided superior discrimination compared with either marker alone, an effect that was further supported by significant NRI and favorable DCA results. These findings indicate that autoimmune markers offer incremental clinical value beyond conventional thyroid function testing.

Importantly, DCA showed that the combined model yielded greater net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities, suggesting potential usefulness in real-world screening decisions.17 Rather than advocating universal thyroid antibody testing, our results support a targeted approach in which anti-TPO assessment may be most informative in patients at higher metabolic or autoimmune risk.

Subgroup Findings and Implications for Targeted Screening

Subgroup analyses revealed that the discriminative performance of anti-TPO was particularly strong among women, individuals with poor glycemic control, and those with longer diabetes duration. Female predominance in autoimmune thyroid disease is well documented and likely reflects hormonal, genetic, and immunologic factors.3,18 Similarly, poor glycemic control and chronic disease duration may amplify inflammatory and autoimmune pathways, increasing vulnerability to thyroid dysfunction.9,15,16,19

These findings have important clinical implications. Current diabetes and thyroid guidelines do not uniformly recommend routine thyroid antibody testing in T2DM.7 However, our data suggests that selective anti-TPO testing in high-risk subgroups may improve early identification of patients at risk for progression to overt thyroid disease, allowing closer monitoring and potentially earlier intervention.

Regional Context and Public Health Relevance

Saudi Arabia faces a rapidly growing burden of T2DM, obesity, and associated endocrine comorbidities driven by genetic susceptibility, lifestyle changes, and urbanization.12,13,20 In such high-prevalence settings, undiagnosed thyroid dysfunction may further worsen glycemic control, lipid abnormalities, cardiovascular risk, and quality of life.14,17 By demonstrating the incremental value of readily available biomarkers, this study provides evidence that may inform more efficient and cost-conscious screening strategies within resource-limited or primary care settings. Although this study was conducted in Saudi Arabia, the underlying mechanisms linking T2DM, thyroid dysfunction, and thyroid autoimmunity are not region-specific. Chronic insulin resistance, metabolic inflammation, and autoimmune susceptibility have been consistently reported across diverse populations worldwide. Moreover, TSH and anti-TPO antibodies are standardized, widely available biomarkers routinely used in clinical practice in many health care systems. As such, the analytical framework and risk-stratified screening approach proposed in this study may be relevant to other populations with a high burden of T2DM. Nonetheless, regional differences in genetic background, iodine intake, environmental exposures, and health care access may influence prevalence estimates and screening yield. Therefore, validation of this approach in non–Middle Eastern populations and in prospective, population-based cohorts is warranted before broader implementation.

Clinical Implications for Practice

Who to Test

Patients with T2DM at higher risk for thyroid dysfunction, particularly women, individuals with poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%), and those with long-standing diabetes.

When to Test

Following initial TSH assessment, especially in patients with normal or borderline TSH values where autoimmune susceptibility may not be captured by TSH alone.

Why to Test

Anti-TPO antibody positivity identifies individuals with increased autoimmune susceptibility who may benefit from closer biochemical surveillance and individualized follow-up, rather than immediate treatment. This targeted approach improves risk stratification and clinical decision-making while avoiding unnecessary universal testing.

Clinical Implementation

Importantly, these findings support a targeted screening strategy, in which anti-TPO antibody testing is selectively applied to higher-risk subgroups, rather than universal testing of all patients with T2DM.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings support a risk-stratified approach to thyroid screening in patients with T2DM rather than universal intensive testing. While serum TSH remains the standard first-line assessment, our results demonstrate that the addition of anti-TPO antibodies provides meaningful incremental discriminative and clinical utility, particularly in high-risk subgroups such as women, individuals with poor glycemic control, and those with long-standing diabetes. Importantly, anti-TPO positivity in euthyroid patients should not be interpreted as an indication for treatment, but rather as a marker of increased autoimmune susceptibility that may justify closer biochemical surveillance. The improved risk classification observed through NRI and the greater net benefit demonstrated by DCA suggest that combining anti-TPO with TSH can inform more individualized monitoring strategies within routine diabetes care. This stepwise, targeted screening model is especially relevant in high-prevalence and resource-constrained settings, where optimizing diagnostic yield while avoiding unnecessary testing is a clinical priority. This targeted strategy is particularly relevant in resource-constrained settings, as it balances clinical benefit with cost considerations and minimizes unnecessary testing.

Current diabetes and thyroid guidelines generally recommend TSH- based screening for thyroid dysfunction and do not endorse routine thyroid autoantibody testing in patients with T2DM. Our findings do not challenge these recommendations but rather complement them by providing evidence in support of a selective, risk-stratified approach to thyroid autoimmunity assessment. Specifically, the incremental discriminative and clinical utility observed with the addition of anti-TPO antibodies suggests that antibody testing may be most informative in predefined high-risk subgroups, rather than as a universal screening strategy. This approach aligns with existing guideline principles by preserving TSH as the primary screening tool while offering a data-driven framework to refine surveillance strategies in patients at increased risk.7

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited from outpatient endocrine and primary care clinics, which may introduce referral bias by over-representing individuals with greater health care utilization or metabolic complexity. In addition, the study was conducted in a single geographic region (Madinah, Saudi Arabia), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different genetic backgrounds, environmental exposures, iodine intake, or health care systems. Nevertheless, the observed prevalence rates and associations are consistent with reports from other Middle Eastern and South Asian populations, supporting relevance within similar high-risk settings.

Second, the cross-sectional, case–control design precludes inference of causality or temporal relationships between anti-TPO antibodies, TSH, and thyroid dysfunction. As such, the study cannot assess longitudinal outcomes, including progression from anti-TPO positivity to overt thyroid disease, nor determine whether autoimmune markers precede biochemical dysfunction. Prospective cohort studies are required to validate temporal associations and disease trajectories.

Third, although anti-TPO was selected due to its established clinical relevance and availability, other markers of thyroid autoimmunity, such as anti-thyroglobulin antibodies, were not assessed. In addition, thyroid ultrasound findings, iodine status, inflammatory markers, and indices of thyroid hormone resistance were not evaluated and may have provided further mechanistic insight.

Fourth, the analysis relied on single-point biochemical measurements, which may be subject to biological and analytical variability, particularly for individuals with borderline TSH values. Repeated measurements over time could improve diagnostic precision and reduce potential misclassification.

Finally, while advanced analytical methods including ROC analysis, NRI, and DCA were used to evaluate discriminative performance and clinical utility, these approaches do not replace prospective validation or economic evaluation. Formal cost-effectiveness analyses were beyond the scope of this study; however, the proposed risk-stratified screening strategy was intentionally designed to avoid universal testing and to enhance economic feasibility in routine clinical practice. Despite these limitations, the study provides robust evidence supporting the incremental clinical utility of combining anti-TPO with TSH for risk stratification of thyroid dysfunction in patients with T2DM, particularly within high-risk subgroups.

Conclusion

In summary, this study shows that anti-TPO antibodies and TSH provide complementary information for identifying patients with T2DM at increased risk of subclinical thyroid dysfunction. Their combined assessment improves discrimination, risk classification, and clinical utility beyond TSH alone. These findings support a targeted, risk-based approach to thyroid screening, particularly among women, individuals with poor glycemic control, and those with long-standing diabetes—and position anti-TPO antibodies as a clinical decision-support tool to refine risk stratification and guide selective thyroid surveillance, rather than as a marker of novel pathophysiology.
