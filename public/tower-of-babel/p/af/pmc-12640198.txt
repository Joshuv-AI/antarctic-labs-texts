Digital cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia compared with digital patient education about insomnia in people with multiple sclerosis in Norway: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Simen Berg Saksvik, Liv Marie Rønhovde, Kristine Ytrehus-Lynum, Knut Langsrud, Alexander Olsen, Roshan das Nair, Stian Lydersen, Julie Grini Holthe, Joar Øveraas Halvorsen, Gunnar Morken, Lee M Ritterband, Lars Bø, Håvard Kallestad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsomnia is prevalent in multiple sclerosis (MS) and may negatively affect quality of life and brain health. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as the first-line treatment, but access is limited due to a shortage of trained therapists. Fully automated digital CBT-I (dCBT-I) has been developed to expand availability. Its effectiveness in MS, and whether improvements in insomnia severity lead to benefits in fatigue, mental health, and cognitive function, remains unknown. This randomized controlled trial will examine the effectiveness of dCBT-I compared with patient education about insomnia (PE) in people with MS. We plan to invite all individuals in the Norwegian MS registry (>90% of people diagnosed with MS in Norway) to the trial with the aim of including 550 participants. Participants will be randomly allocated 1:1 to dCBT-I or PE. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline, 9 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months, with additional health registry data collected 5 years after randomization. The between-group difference in self-reported insomnia severity at 9-weeks is the primary endpoint (intention to treat). Secondary outcomes include between-group differences in fatigue, mental health, cognitive function, sleep and daytime activity with actigraphy, medication use, pain, alcohol use, quality of life, social and work functioning, and resource utilization and whether changes in fatigue, mental health and cognitive function are mediated by changes in insomnia severity. The study protocol is approved by the Norwegian Ethics Committees for Clinical Trials on Medicinal Products and Medical Devices (Ref: 623308) and is preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ref: NCT06113666).A Digital Therapeutic to Improve Insomnia in Multiple Sclerosis: A Randomized Controlled Trial. (NorseMS) Sponsor: St.Olavs Hospital, Postboks 3250 Torgården, 7006 Trondheim. Statement of SignificanceInsomnia is common in multiple sclerosis (MS), and has a significant impact on daytime functioning. Fully automated digital Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia offers a scalable option that could be deployed in routine MS care. This pragmatic randomized trial is designed to detect effects on insomnia severity in a heterogeneous sample of people with MS and self-reported insomnia, and to test whether improving insomnia leads to benefits in fatigue, mental health, and cognitive function. Data on pain, alcohol use, quality of life, social and work functioning, national registry data, and actigraphy recordings for 90 days during the intervention phase will also be collected. Findings will directly inform clinical implementation pathways and provide new knowledge about the role of insomnia in MS.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a common neurological disorder with onset in early adulthood.1 The prevalence of MS is increasing, affecting >2 million people worldwide. Around 13 000 people live with MS in Norway, with incidence rates ranging from 211 to 336 per 100 000 inhabitants1-3 compared with 111 to 300 per 100 000 inhabitants in the WHO European Union and the American regions.4 MS is associated with long-term functional impairment and disability.5,6

Insomnia is defined as difficulties with falling asleep, and/or staying asleep, or non-restorative sleep leading to significant daytime impairment or excessive worry about sleep. Insomnia symptoms are frequently reported by people with MS with a prevalence rate of 20%-50%.7-12 People with MS often develop fatigue, mental health problems, and cognitive dysfunction, and such problems have been closely linked to insomnia in samples without neurological disorders.13-15 It is assumed that sleep disturbances can worsen MS symptoms, whereas normal sleep is associated with a lower symptom burden and may support beneficial anti-inflammatory processes.16,17 Thus, insomnia could be an important treatment target in MS.

The etiology of insomnia symptoms in people with MS is likely multifactorial. Although insomnia disorder is frequent, symptoms may also arise from MS-related pathology and associated complaints, including muscle spasms, nocturia due to neurogenic bladder dysfunction, neuropathic pain, lesions affecting the sleep–wake regulatory systems, or adverse effects of medications used to manage MS symptoms.12 The standard medical approach involves careful differential diagnosis followed by treatment targeting the underlying cause. However, this approach is challenged by scientific findings and real-world limitations. First, although nocturnal awakenings may be triggered by MS-related symptoms (e.g. nocturia), it may be insomnia-related mechanisms, such as conditioning, worry and rumination, or maladaptive beliefs and safety behaviors that hinder the ability to return to sleep.18,19 Second, and related, treatment of the underlying medical condition has been found to have limited effectiveness in improving insomnia.20 Third, comprehensive differential diagnostics can be resource-intensive and may not be feasible in all clinical settings. Fourth, long-term pharmacological treatment of insomnia remains problematic due to concerns about polypharmacy in MS, potential side effects, and limited evidence for sustained efficacy.21 As a result, insomnia symptoms may persist even in patients receiving the current optimal treatment for MS and its associated symptoms.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first-line treatment for insomnia.22 A meta-analysis aggregating data from 87 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found that CBT-I is effective in reducing insomnia severity in various patient populations with a number needed to treat of 1.95 within 8 weeks.23 However, despite the high prevalence and assumed relevance for brain health, there have been few studies of CBT-I in neurological disorders in general, and MS in particular. Two pilot RCTs,24,25 two case reports26,27 and one case series28 have explored the effectiveness of CBT-I in people with MS.29 Results from these trials indicate that people with MS can complete the course of CBT-I with no severe adverse events. However, sample sizes in existing trials have been low (n = 1 to n = 41), limiting the statistical power to detect differences in insomnia severity. Moreover, previous studies have excluded individuals that likely could have benefited from CBT-I (e.g. symptoms of depression or suicidal ideation24,25). CBT-I has been studied in conditions where insomnia is comorbid to a mental disorder, cancer, traumatic brain injury, or organic sleep disorders.30-32 These studies have shown that CBT-I is effective regardless of these conditions, and most attempts at finding clinically meaningful moderators of the effectiveness of CBT-I have so far failed.30-32

Given its favorable safety profile, tentative effectiveness in small studies in MS, and general effectiveness across other comorbid conditions, and the lack of consistent moderators of treatment response, a pragmatic approach may be to offer CBT-I as a low-threshold intervention to all people with MS experiencing insomnia symptoms, regardless of the underlying etiology. However, the effectiveness and safety of this broad approach remain uncertain, and several methodological and logistical challenges must be addressed.

As pragmatic trials introduce larger variability in outcomes than efficacy trials, there is a need to adequately power such trials. Adequately powered trials will also enable researchers to address moderators of outcomes which can have important clinical and scientific implications. However, few clinicians are trained in CBT-I, and any in-person psychological intervention has considerable costs, making a trial with an adequate sample size difficult to conduct. Clinical implementation could therefore also be difficult, due to a lack of trained staff.33 Self-guided digital interventions based upon CBT-I (dCBT-I) have been developed to manage this challenge.

Previous research has shown that fully automated dCBT-I with a simple online screening is highly efficacious in reducing insomnia severity in the general population,34-37 and that it can also improve symptoms of fatigue and mental health.13,34 Evidence for the effect of dCBT-I on improvement of performance based cognitive function is mixed, possibly because the samples recruited have had low levels of impairment, and due to a variability in the cognitive assessments used.38,39 The etiology of fatigue and mental health in MS is likely to be multifaceted, but the contribution of insomnia to fatigue is unknown, and there is a lack of non-pharmacological treatments to address these issues. The small-scale trials that have been conducted on CBT-I in MS have shown promising results also on these secondary measures.25,40 Improving insomnia severity could therefore be a potential avenue for improving other symptoms related to both insomnia and MS.

To advance both clinical practice and scientific understanding of insomnia in MS, there is a need for a pragmatic, adequately powered randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effectiveness of dCBT-I on insomnia severity as well as key secondary outcomes such as fatigue, mental health, and cognitive function, in a representative MS population. Moreover, performing mediation analyses in RCTs is a gold standard method of assessing the role of insomnia in the maintenance of fatigue, mental health, and cognitive function in MS.41

Current study

This protocol outlines a pragmatic RCT. The research question of this trial is to investigate the effectiveness of dCBT-I on insomnia and other common, comorbid symptoms and MS specific symptoms in a sample of people with MS. The experimental intervention (dCBT-I) will be compared with a control intervention of digital patient education about insomnia (PE). This RCT will recruit individuals with a clinical diagnosis of MS, identified through the Norwegian MS Registry (Norsk MS-Register og biobank). This MS registry includes more than 90% of all people with MS in Norway.

The primary endpoint of the trial is level of insomnia severity nine weeks after randomization. Nine weeks allows sufficient time for the participants to complete the intervention. We will perform additional follow-up assessments 33 weeks after randomization (6 months after the primary outcome, i.e. 6-months follow-up) and 61 weeks after randomization (12-months follow-up). Additionally, we will access public health registers 61 weeks and 5 years after randomization.

We hypothesize that the dCBT-I intervention compared with PE will, at all assessment points (9-weeks, 6- and 12-months follow-up), be more effective in terms of:

Primary aim: To investigate the effectiveness of dCBT-I compared with PE in reducing insomnia severity in people with MS at 9-weeks.

Secondary aims: To investigate between-group differences in insomnia severity at 6 and 12 months. We will also investigate between-group differences in symptoms of fatigue, mental health, cognitive function (self-report and performance-based), alcohol use, pain, quality of life, side effects and safety, objective markers of sleep–wake cycle and daytime activity patterns (with actigraphy) from randomization to 9-weeks, and if treatment gains are maintained at 6-months and 12-months follow-up.

Further secondary outcomes include changes in and long-term effects on resource utilization (e.g. contacts in the health care system and length of sick leave or disability benefits), medication use (hypnotic, sedative/anxiolytic, and antidepressant, other psychotropic medications, and pharmacological treatment of MS) and information about the MS disease (e.g. previous and current treatments for MS, previous attack phases, and first diagnosis of MS). These data will be collected from official health registries at 12 months and 5 years.

Finally, to test if improving insomnia severity causes improvement in fatigue, analyses are planned to test whether changes in insomnia severity during the 9-week intervention phase mediate improvements in fatigue, mental health and cognitive function at 6 and 12 months. We will also test if secondary outcome variables appear as possible moderators of the association between the digital sleep intervention and outcomes.

Material and Methods

This protocol for the RCT follows the Standard Protocol Items for Randomized Trials (SPIRIT) statement guidelines.42 The trial is preregistered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT06113666). A completed SPIRIT checklist and a completed WHO Trial Registration Data Set can be found in the supplementary material.

Key measures and timing of assessment

Participants, infrastructure, and patient flow (Figure 1): Participants are recruited via invitations from the Norwegian MS registry. All people diagnosed with MS in Norway are asked if they want to be registered, and all consenting patients are registered by a neurologist. There are currently about 12 000 people with MS in this registry who have all consented to be contacted about future studies relevant to MS. Invitations are sent to all individuals in the MS-registry, 240 at a time, via the Norwegian Directorate for Health official website www.helsenorge.no with a link to the trial website. Interested individuals sign up at the trial website. First, interested individuals will receive a phone call from qualified study personnel. This is to inform the participants about the study and to answer questions. After the phone call, the interested participants will sign into a website, sign the consent form and take a test to establish eligibility for inclusion in the trial. If eligible, the individuals will complete baseline assessments online. After completion, there will be an automated randomization procedure, and participants will be allocated to one of the two digital conditions. The participants will receive an email with a link to the study website and be unaware of the allocation. The entire setting of the study, from inclusion and screening to follow-up is online. The first participant signed the informed consent form on November 16, 2023, and participants are currently being enrolled (by August 1, 2025). We expect that enrollment of participants will be completed in January 2026, and study completion (final 5 year follow-up) is expected in April 2032.

Safety

There have been no reported side effects of dCBT-I other than increased sleepiness in the early stages of treatment. No severe side effects or other serious adverse events have been identified in our previous or ongoing trials using the same design and intervention. Previous trials investigating CBT-I in people with MS25,40 and other neurological disorders44,45 have not reported side effects or any worsening or MS relapse.

Interventions

Digital CBT-I (dCBT-I): CBT-I19 is a multi-component psychotherapy that targets insomnia and consists of the following interventions: Psychoeducation about sleep and sleep hygiene, sleep restriction therapy, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, and relapse prevention. dCBT-I is a digital adaptation of the traditional CBT-I protocol and various digital versions exist. The digital intervention that will be utilized in this study is named Sleep Healthy Using The Internet (SHUTi) which is based on the same theoretical model of insomnia and involves the same interventions as ordinary CBT-I.19 SHUTi is a fully automated, web-based program, requiring no interaction with healthcare professionals. Users are presented with weekly psycho-educative “cores” and the interactive interface individualizes the content based on daily registration of digital sleep diaries The participants are reminded to complete their daily sleep diaries via automated email prompts. SHUTi is developed by the research team at the University of Virginia in collaboration with the originator of CBT-I, Charles Morin.46 SHUTi has been translated into Norwegian for use in Norway.

Patient education about sleep (PE)

The control condition will be a static online webpage with general sleep hygiene advice, such as reducing caffeine intake at night, not exercising too close to bedtime, not napping, etc., but also suggesting specific behavioral changes. The content partly overlaps with the PE presented in dCBT-I, but the full content is available to users from the first entry, and no interactive features are included. PE presents a common control condition used in previous RCTs of dCBT-I (SHUTi).34,47

Randomization

The RCT employs the same randomization process as previous studies.47,48 Eligible participants will be randomly allocated 1:1 to the experimental condition or the control condition using an automated randomization procedure which cannot be affected by the researchers (i.e. allocation concealment is maintained). The procedure involves block randomization with variable block sizes. Participants are blinded to which group they are allocated. However, it is impossible to completely blind participants as it is evident whether one receives an active, interactive intervention, or is allocated to the control group with PE.

Withdrawal: Participants will be informed that they may withdraw from the study at any time, without being required to state a reason for withdrawal. Withdrawing from the study has no consequences for clinical care as usual. The research team will assist in deleting collected data if withdrawing participants demand deletion of their collected data.

Crisis management procedure: If a participant should experience worsening of his or her condition, this will be reported by the participant to the neurological department where the patient receives treatment for MS and be handled by the department. It is also possible to reach out to the research team by telephone or e-mail. This contact information is visible on the webpage and in the treatment program. Neurological departments across Norway are informed about the study and the procedure for reporting potential adverse events or serious adverse events to the research team. Participants will report potential negative effects of treatment using standard assessments.

Outcome assessments

Self-reported outcome assessments are collected at baseline, post-intervention (9-weeks post baseline), 6 months post-intervention and 12 months post-intervention unless otherwise specified in Table 1. Additionally, outcomes from registries are collected at baseline and 5 years post-intervention. Objective assessment with actigraphy is collected for 90 days from baseline until the post-intervention assessment.

Sleep assessments

Primary outcome measure

The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) 49 is a 7-item questionnaire assessing insomnia severity over the previous 14 days. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all”, 4 = “extremely”). Scores range from 0 to 28 with higher scores indicating greater insomnia severity. Adequate psychometric properties have been reported for ISI.49 A reduction of 8 points from pre- to post-intervention is regarded as a clinically relevant treatment response. A total score < 8 is considered to indicate remission after the intervention.

Other assessments of sleep and sleepiness

The Bergen Insomnia Scale (BIS) 50 is a 6-item assessment of insomnia symptom severity based on inclusion criteria for insomnia in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV-TR (DSM-IV-TR).51 The total score ranges from 0 to 42 and items are rated on an 8-point scale based on frequency represented by days per week (0 to 7). The questionnaire is validated in the general population and population with primary insomnia.52

The Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) 53 is an 8-item questionnaire that assesses daytime sleepiness or the inclination to fall asleep in eight distinct situations. Each statement is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “Would never doze”, 3 = “High chance of dosing”). Respondents obtain a total score of 0-24 in which a common convention is to use a cut-off of ≥11 as an indication of excessive daytime sleepiness.54 The test–retest variability of ESS is excellent in clinical populations.55

The Consensus Sleep Diary 56 allows individuals to longitudinally report prospective sleep–wake patterns, including bedtime and rise time, sleep latency, total sleep time, number and length of nocturnal awakenings, sleep quality, and daytime sleep. Participants report 10 out of 14 consecutive days of sleep diaries at baseline and each follow-up assessment. Reported items include bedtime, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, number of nocturnal awakenings, final waking time in the morning, rise time, and a 5-point rating of experienced sleep quality (higher scores indicate increased sleep quality), dose and timing of alcohol use, dose and timing of sleep medication.

MS disability status

The Patient Determined Disease Steps (PDDS) 57 is a single-item patient-reported assessment of MS disability status with nine levels spanning from 0 (normal) to 8 (bedridden). A recent systematic review supported criterion and construct validity and test–retest reliability of the PDDS in people with MS with mild to moderate disabilities.58

Levels of psychopathology

The Outcome-Questionnaire-45.2 (OQ 45.2) 59 is a 45-item self-report assessment tool that examines the efficacy of clinical interventions in therapy through patient progress. Items are assessed on a 5-point Likert scale (4 = “never”, 0 = “always”) where higher scores indicate greater symptom burden and lower functioning. It includes a total score and three subscales: symptom distress, interpersonal relations, and social role functioning. The concurrent validity and reliability of OQ 45.2 are satisfactory in Norwegian and international clinical and non-clinical samples.60

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) 61 is a 14-item assessment that measures generalized anxiety and anhedonia over the last 7 days on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not present”, 3 = “highly present”). Scores range from 0-42 with higher total sum scores indicating increased mental health. A digital version of HADS is reliable and valid with high internal consistency and good convergent validity among people with MS.62

Fatigue

The Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFQ) 63 is an 11-item questionnaire to measure fatigue severity within clinical and non-clinical populations. It consists of two separate subscales of seven and four items representing physical and mental fatigue, respectively. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “less than usual”, 3 = “much more than usual”). Responders are asked to rate items according to how they have felt “lately”, compared to when they were last well. Higher scores represent higher fatigue, with a maximum total score of 33. The CFQ is reliable and valid for measuring fatigue severity in people with MS.64

The Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) 65 is a 9-item measure that assesses the severity of fatigue and its influence on daily activities and lifestyle. Each item is scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree”, 7 = “strongly agree”) and scores range from 9-63 or 1-7 if the mean is used to calculate the score. Mean scores between 4 and 5 are suggested cut-off scores for “severe fatigue” across clinical populations. Studies have documented good test–retest reliability and construct validity across clinical samples, while there is an indication that the measure might have lower construct validity in the general population and risk of overestimating prevalence based on the original cut-off score of 4.66

Quality of life

The Short Form 12 item Health Survey (SF-12) 67 measures self-assessed psychological (“mental composite summary”) and physical (“physical composite summary”) components of health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Response options vary from e.g. 2-point scales (“yes or no”) to 6-point Likert scale (“all of the time”, “none of the time”). Each composite summary yields a score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate better HRQoL. There is not a total HRQoL sum score. The psychometric properties of the SF-12 have been validated in general and clinical populations,68 including in people with MS.69

The 5-level EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D-5L) 70 is a 5-item assessment of general health state and the five dimensions include mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Items are rated on a 0 to 5 Likert scale representing increasing severity. The EQ-5D-5L has in recent years been used to assess the HRQoL of patients from different clinical populations in evaluations of health resource utilization.71

Cognition

The Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function – Adult Version (BRIEF-A) 72 is a self-report assessment of self-perceived executive function and self-regulation in daily life. The 75-item questionnaire covers nine non-overlapping clinical scales, namely inhibition, self-monitoring, planning/organizing, shifting, initiating, task monitoring, emotional control, working memory, and organization of materials.72 Items are scored on a 3-point Likert Scale (“Never”, “Often”). Scores are compared to normative data to determine the extent of executive difficulties, indicated by higher scores.

The Cognitive Complaints in Bipolar Disorder Rating Assessment (COBRA) 73 is a 16-item instrument. It assesses cognitive complaints operationalized as subjective cognitive dysfunctions including executive function, processing speed, working memory, verbal learning and memory, attention/concentration, and mental tracking. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “never”, 3 = “always”). A summation of all scores yields a total score in which a higher score represents increased subjective complaints. A cut-off score of 10 indicates the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. The COBRA is found to have satisfactory convergent validity and high internal consistency in patients with bipolar disorder.73 COBRA was originally developed for assessments in patients with Bipolar disorder, but can also be used in other clinical populations.

Pain

A pain map with a numeric rating scale measures perceived continuous pain in muscles and joints over the last year.74-76 The participants are instructed to indicate the location of their pain on a body map, and to rate their pain with a numeric rating scale from 0, indicating no pain, to 10 indicating serious pain.

Work performance

The Work Productivity and Impairment Scale – General Health (WPAI-GH) 77 is a 6-item self-report instrument that assesses paid and unpaid work performance and daily living impairments over the last 7 days. The questionnaire generates four main outcomes which are absenteeism (the percentage of work time missed due to health), presenteeism (experience of impairment experienced while at work), overall work productivity loss (the combination of absenteeism and presenteeism), and activity impairment (the percentage of impairment in daily activities).

Alcohol use

The Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test - Consumption (AUDIT-C) 78 is a 3-item adaptation of the original WHO 10-item AUDIT instrument and assesses the frequency and magnitude of alcohol consumption. It is reliably found to identify individuals with hazardous drinking behaviors. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “never” or “1-2”, 4= “4 or more times per week”, “10 or more” or “daily or almost daily”) with scores ranging from 0-12. A higher score indicates an increased risk of dependence or hazardous drinking. AUDIT-C is found to have appropriate psychometric properties, including high sensitivity and specificity in screening for alcohol use disorders and risk drinking.79,80

Intervention-related assessments

Treatment expectations are evaluated with a single-item questionnaire. The item measures to which degree the individual believes the digital sleep intervention can work for them (1 = “not at all”, 9 = “very well”).

The Use of Sleep Strategies is a 6-item questionnaire in which respondents report how frequently they apply six different therapeutic techniques recognized from CBT-I and psychoeducation about sleep, as well as the perceived utility of each technique.

The Negative Effects Questionnaire (NEQ) 81 is a 20-item self-report measure that assesses the perceived negative treatment effects of digital psychological treatment. Individuals consider the presence of 20 different conditions (“Yes” or “No”). Positively endorsed statements are rated by their negative effect on a 5-point Likert scale (0-4) in which a higher score indicates a greater negative impact, followed by a question of whether the negative effects were caused by the treatment or by other circumstances.

Objective and performance-based measures

Actigraphy

The GENEActiv (GENEActiv Original, Activinsights Ltd., Kimbolton, United Kingdom) is a wrist-worn motion detector (accelerometer). The actigraph82 has sensors for measuring acceleration, light, and temperature. Raw data are extracted in the GENEActiv Software, version 4.0.9. that derives patterns of sleep-and-wake cycles and adherence to the intervention. Raw data will be analyzed with established accelerometer analyses and corrected with sleep diary data where appropriate. Participants will receive actigraphs by mail and carry them for 90 consecutive days (2x 45 days), from baseline throughout the intervention period and post-assessment.

Cognitive testing

Cognitive test performance: Participants will complete the online computerized and home-based cognitive test battery Memoro.57 Memoro represents a performance-based measure of cognitive function. Internet-based cognitive tests have been included in a prior dCBT-I trial.83 This trial demonstrated that home-based internet testing is a feasible outcome measure in dCBT-I trials. In the present trial, a comprehensive Memoro cognitive test battery will be completed at baseline, and a shortened version will be completed at 9-weeks, 6-months, and 12-months follow-up. The baseline battery includes adapted home-administered internet versions of Objects in Grid immediate and delayed recall (spatial memory), Simple Reaction Time, Digit Span forward and backward (working memory), the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (processing speed), the Continuous Performance Test (CPT) (visual attention and reaction time), and Matrices (abstract reasoning). The first test at follow-ups is the adapted version of CPT.63 CPT seems well-suited to assess the relationship between sleep and cognitive function.64 The other test is an adapted version of the Symbol Digit Modalities Test, which is a recommended continuous performance test commonly used in clinical trials in people with MS.84

Before starting, each participant will provide demographic data (year of birth, sex, education level, hand preference, comfort with the use of computers) and answer questions about fatigue and pain levels and how they think they will perform. Participants will evaluate the same questions after completion, as well as a question of how much they exerted themselves during the test.

National registries and resource use

Objective data accessible from Norwegian national registries will be collected at 12-months and 5 years follow-up. The inclusion of health register data will help address whether improvements in insomnia also result in favorable long-term outcomes related to symptom severity, overall health, and utilization of specialist services (such as the number of hospital sessions required and admissions).

The Norwegian Patient Registry provides data on the number of appointments at mental health care clinics, the type and timing of treatment and admissions, and the date of the first appointment for each patient during the study period (data on 1 and 2 are collected from the NPR).

The Norwegian Prescription Database will be used to describe the dose, timing, type of, and changes in prescribed hypnotic, sedative/anxiolytic, antidepressant, and other psychotropic medications and medications used in the treatment of MS (according to the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System) and changes recorded during the trial.

The cost of treatment offered by the public services (“Kommunalt pasient og brukerregister”) is a database from which information on treatment costs provided by the public services will be collected.

Sick leave and disability benefits (“Forløpsdatabasen”) is an administrative database from which information about sick leave and received disability benefits will be collected.

The MS Registry will yield data on the state of the MS disease, including the Extended Disability Status Scale, previous and current treatments for MS, previous attack phases, number of and results from MRI scans (including lesions), and first diagnosis of MS.

Sample size: The primary outcome is between-group change in total ISI score from baseline to 9-week follow-up. We will explore if treatment gains are maintained at 6- and 12-month follow-ups. Treatment response is categorized as a reduction of 8 points on the ISI from baseline, and an ISI score ≤ 8 is categorized as remission.19 To date, most RCTs of dCBT-I (i.e. trials of SHUTi vs PE) report effect sizes (ES, Cohen’s d) between 1.2 and 2.0 on the ISI.34,36,85 However, dCBT-I is likely to be less effective in a pragmatic trial of people with MS. As such, we have estimated the effectiveness of dCBT-I in this study based on a Cohen’s d = 0.5. In addition, we want the sample size estimate to provide statistical power to detect small to moderate ES (Cohen’s d = 0.3 - 0.5) on the secondary outcomes (fatigue, mental health, cognitive function etc). We have identified small to moderate effect sizes of dCBT-I on fatigue and mental health in previous trials on non-neurological samples.34 Lastly, other RCTs of dCBT-I have had between 20% and 40% attrition rates.36,37 Based on the long-term follow up in our previous RCTs of dCBT-I, we have predicted that the study dropout rate is likely to reach about 50% at the 12-month follow-up. Therefore, we aim to recruit 550 participants, to enable us to retain 275 participants (137 in each treatment arm) at the end of the RCT. For a two-sample t-test with alpha = 0.05, this sample size gives a power of 98% of detecting a difference of Cohen’s d = 0.50 and 90% of detecting a difference of Cohen’s d = 0.40.

Statistical analytic plan

Clinical characteristics and demographic summaries will be presented for each group (dCBT-I and PE). Continuous variables will be presented as means and standard deviations or as medians and interquartile range as appropriate, binary and categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages.

We will use linear mixed models to examine the difference between the two randomized groups on the primary and secondary outcomes with continuous variables. The model will include time as categorical covariate and the time*group interaction, and patient as random effect. Since outcomes at baseline are recorded before the participant can know the treatment group, any systematic effect at baseline is excluded from the model, as recommended by Coffman et al., 2016 and Twisk et al., 2018.86,87 Baseline covariates which plausibly affect the outcome will be included in the analyses.88 These baseline predictors are age, sex, sleep medication (yes/no), HADS total score and FSS total score. An equivalent logistic mixed model will be used for binary secondary outcomes. Linear mixed models and logistic mixed models provide approximately unbiased results when the data are missing at random (MAR), while complete case analyses would provide unbiased results only under the more restrictive missing completely at random assumption. Model assumptions will be reviewed and if the assumptions are not met, this will be handled using bootstrapping, robust variance estimators, logarithmic transformations, or non-parametric methods as appropriate.

We will use intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses for all outcomes. In addition, we will carry out a complier average causal effect analysis for the primary outcome, to estimate the effect of the number of completed cores (1,2, 3, 4, 5, or 6) in the intervention group, following the recommendations in Dunn et al (2005).89

To investigate the influence of possible deviations from the MAR assumption, we will use pattern-mixture models to simulate a range of situations where the data for both primary and selected secondary outcome variables are missing not at random. This might apply to situations where the distribution for each outcome differs between those with complete data and those with missing data. Secondary mediator and moderator analyses will also be performed. As far as practically possible, statistical analyses will be undertaken by a biostatistician who will be masked for group allocation.

No access to any self-report data or other data is permitted until recruitment and 12-month follow-up are completed for the set sample size.

Patient and public involvement

We will collaborate with representatives from the MS association in Norway. They will be involved in the implementation of the project. The project will be presented and discussed with the MS association 1-2 times annually.

The user group meetings will work to (1) be a link between the users and the research group, (2) contribute with knowledge and experience to the research, (3) represent the users at various events, (4) work for political attention for the work of the research group and increase funding related to relevant research, and (5) contribute towards good principles for user involvement.

Ethics and dissemination.

The project is approved by the Norwegian Directorate for Medical Products (CIV-NO-23-03-042653), the Norwegian Ethics Committees for Clinical Trials on Medicinal Products and Medical Devices (Ref: 623308) and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ref: NCT06113666). Version 1 of the protocol was approved by the ethics committee in June 2023 and the current version (v 2.5) was approved in July 2024. The RCT will follow the guidance and principles outlined in the Revised Declaration of Geneva.90 Invited individuals who show interest through a web-based form will receive information about the study via phone before obtaining access to the consent form. All eligible participants must give online written informed consent before study entry. Participants will be provided with printable documents about the RCT and all interested patients will be contacted by telephone by an investigator who will give oral information about the RCT. Both the written and oral information emphasize that participation is voluntary, that individuals will receive the same standard of care and treatment at the public health services regardless of their participation, that they may withdraw from the trial at any time without any consequences for their clinical care and that they do not need to give a reason for withdrawal.

Self-report data will be de-identified and stored in encrypted and password-protected databases that are compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation. Data from national registries will be downloaded and incorporated into the files and anonymized. Trusted researchers involved in data analysis will be granted access to de-identified data from the trial.

Any substantial amendments to the protocol will be reported to the Norwegian Directorate for Medical Products and the Norwegian Ethics committee. Non-substantial amendments and substantial amendments will be reported to the Ethics committee and registered at clinicaltrials.gov.

The results will be published in peer-review journals, presented at scientific conferences, for clinicians and user groups and communicated popularized results in media and social media outlets. Exploratory outcomes will be reported separately from the main outcomes. Investigators will adhere to the Vancouver Recommendations in reporting and publishing results from the trial.

Study monitoring

A monitor will visit the investigator regularly according to the monitor plan. A monitoring plan specific to this study is held separately from the protocol. The monitoring plan is set up by the sponsor (St.Olavs Hospital) and outlines the level of monitoring to be performed for the trial and how this will be carried out. The level of monitoring is based on a trial-specific risk assessment of the approved protocol. Guidelines for follow-up and management of any deviations from the protocol will be defined and described in the clinical monitoring plan.

Discussion

This randomized trial will deliver definitive evidence on whether a fully automated dCBT-I improves insomnia severity in people with MS and whether benefits extend to fatigue, mental health, and cognitive function that are highly salient in MS care. Given the scale, sampling from a national MS registry, and the use of both subjective and objective measures, results will be directly informative for clinical services that seek to integrate behavioral sleep care.

Fully automated digital therapeutics is inherently scalable and cost-efficient. Our design couples a simple self-guided screening with an automated intervention to approximate real-world implementation. In this context, people with MS and insomnia can self-refer to dCBT-I, regardless of cause of the insomnia symptoms and without relying on a provider. This approach has trade-offs, and the brief screening may miss patients better served by alternative pathways and increase heterogeneity. The minimal human contact can increase attrition and missing data. We will address this issue with predefined sensitivity analyses and missing-data approaches described in the Statistical Analysis Plan, and with continuous actigraphy to provide objective markers during the active phase.

Finally, while the present trial tests an unmodified dCBT-I, MS specific adaptations may further optimize outcomes. Trial data, including actigraphy features and cognitive profiles, will help identify for whom and under what conditions dCBT-I may be more effective and whether targeted modifications are warranted for future iterations.
