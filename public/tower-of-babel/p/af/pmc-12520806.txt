Spread of Injectate After Combined Deep Serratus Anterior and Superficial Parasternal Intercostal Plane Injections: A Cadaver Study to Evaluate Their Utility in Breast Surgery
Authors: Abhijit Biswas, Vivienne P. Hang, William Newmarch, Kody M. Wolfstadt, Christopher Hansebout, Anne Agur, Timothy D. Wilson, Vishal Uppal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnalgesia for breast surgery by a single peripheral nerve block is challenging as breast tissue receives innervation from multiple sources. In this cadaveric study, we evaluated the injectate spread of combined superficial parasternal intercostal plane (PIP) and deep serratus anterior plane (SAP) injections in the tissues relevant for breast surgery analgesia.MethodsWe performed ultrasound‐guided dye injections in 18 hemithoraces. 20 mL of methylene blue solution was injected into the superficial PIP at the fourth rib. Following that, 20 mL of black India Ink solution was injected in the SAP at the fourth rib at the midaxillary line. We dissected the specimens, 15 min post‐injection to visualize dye dispersion. Staining of the intercostal nerves from each injection was measured by counting the number of intercostal spaces and nerves stained.ResultsFor PIP injections, the cranial spread of injectate was observed up to the second intercostal nerve involving the anterior cutaneous branch in 14/16 (87.5%) with caudal spread to the fifth intercostal nerve in 15/16 (94%). In addition, the staining of the medial and lateral pectoral nerves was noted in 14/16 (88%) of the specimens. For the SAP dye injections, the cranial spread of injectate was observed up to the lateral cutaneous branch of the third intercostal nerve level in 9/16 (56%) of specimens and caudal spread up to the lateral cutaneous branch of the sixth intercostal nerve in 14/16 (88%) specimens.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated extensive spread of dye injectate with combined PIP and SAP injections involving anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves and lateral and medial pectoral nerves. This study demonstrates an alternative analgesic approach to provide perioperative analgesia for breast surgery.Editorial CommentThis cadaver study demonstrates that combining deep serratus anterior block with parasternal intercostal plane block provides reliable injectate spread to the cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves. In a clinical setting, this could provide comprehensive analgesia for the breast. Compared with paravertebral blocks, the combination of two fascial plane blocks may offer a technically less challenging alternative with a lower risk of complications.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer experienced by women, with a lifetime incidence of 13% [1]. The mainstay of treatment is surgery, which is associated with a high incidence of persistent post‐surgical pain [2]. Up to 47% of patients report chronic post‐surgical pain following major breast surgery [3, 4]. Uncontrolled acute post‐surgical pain is associated with chronic post‐surgical pain [4, 5]. Effective management of acute post‐surgical pain could improve a patient's perioperative experience and may reduce the risk of patients developing persistent post‐surgical pain [6].

Sensory innervations of breast tissue are derived from anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of T2–T6 thoracic intercostal nerves [7]. The contribution of the supraclavicular nerve for breast analgesia is uncertain. Of the seven studies included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis of anatomical studies to delineate innervations of female breast tissue, three studies documented contributions from cutaneous branches of the supraclavicular nerve to the upper medial quadrant of the breast, in addition to the cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves [7]. Due to the complex innervation of the breast tissue from multiple anatomical origins, a single‐injection technique may not provide comprehensive analgesia. Paravertebral blocks are routinely practiced at several institutions and may be considered the gold standard. For trainees or faculty who have infrequent practice of regional anesthesia, paravertebral blocks may be challenging to perform compared to the fascial plane blocks, with a possible risk of complications [8]. Several fascial plane blocks are in practice, including combinations of serratus anterior plane (SAP) and PECS block or parasternal intercostal plane (PIP) and PECS block to provide comprehensive coverage of the breast tissue [9, 10]. Though several of these blocks are in practice, none are considered the gold standard to date and have yet to demonstrate complete coverage of the surgical area in anatomical studies [11].

The deep serratus anterior plane block is commonly used in practice and targets the lateral cutaneous branches of the T2–T6 intercostal nerves within the fascial plane deep to the serratus anterior, as demonstrated by anatomical studies [11]. Spread could be limited or minimal to the anterior chest wall with a single‐level serratus block [12]. The superficial parasternal intercostal plane block is reported to target the anterior cutaneous branches of the T2–T6 intercostal nerves within the fascial plane deep to the pectoralis major [13, 14]. Some case reports have demonstrated its utility for pain management [14, 15], and anatomical studies have confirmed their spread to block those specified nerves [16]. The analgesic efficacy of combined SAP with PIP block is already established in clinical studies [17].

In this cadaver study, we combined injections at the superficial parasternal intercostal plane and deep serratus anterior plane to study the spread of injectate to the nerves innervating breast tissue. The aim of our study was to evaluate the spread of dye to the anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of the second to sixth intercostal nerves, which provide comprehensive analgesic coverage of the breast tissue.

In a cadaver model, we hypothesized that deep serratus anterior plane injections would spread to the lateral cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves and superficial parasternal intercostal plane injections would spread to anterior cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves, providing more extensive sensory coverage of the breast tissue.

Methods

After approval from the Western University research ethics board (REB #118799), we evaluated the spread of ultrasound‐guided dye injections in the SAP and PIP fascial planes. We included 18 hemithoraces (fresh frozen [n = 8], partially embalmed [n = 10]) donated to the Body Bequeathal Program at Western University, London, Ontario, with no history of surgery or trauma to the upper extremities or thorax. We chose to use both fresh frozen and partially embalmed cadavers to increase the sample size as the availability of one particular type of cadaveric donor was not sufficient. Injections and dissections were performed in the laboratory space at the University of Western Ontario. The study took place between March and December of 2022. We are reporting this study as per anatomical quality assurance (AQUA) guidelines for reporting original anatomical studies.

Ultrasound Guided Dye Injections

Two 20 mL diluted solutions of injectate were mixed and drawn into separate syringes prior to the injections, one using 0.5 mL of methylene blue ink per 19.5 mL of water for the superficial parasternal intercostal plane injection and the other using 1.0 mL of black India ink with 19.0 mL of water for the injection at the deep serratus anterior plane. Ultrasound guidance was used to identify the correct fascial plane for the dye injection. A high‐frequency (6–13 MHz) 38 cm linear transducer (M‐turbo; Fujifilm SonoSite, Bothell, WA, USA) with a sterile cover was used by a single regional anesthesiologist with more than 10 years of experience in ultrasound‐guided regional techniques. For both fascial plane injections, the ultrasound probes were positioned in the sagittal orientation, and the injections were performed using an in‐plane technique.

Superficial Parasternal Intercostal Plane Injection

With the cadaver in a supine position, the parasternal intercostal fascial plane was identified under ultrasound guidance at the parasternal area between the pectoralis major muscle and the ribs and intercostal muscles within 1–2 cm lateral to the sternal border. The plane was further established by injecting 1–2 mL of saline on the fourth rib and confirming the spread within the correct fascial plane. Once confirmed, 20 mL of diluted methylene blue solution was injected above the fourth rib in a caudal to cranial direction using a 22‐gauge (G) Stimuplex (Braun, Bethlehem, PA, USA) 80‐mm echogenic needle (Figure 1A).

Deep Serratus Anterior Plane Injection

With the cadaver moved into the lateral decubitus position, the deep serratus anterior plane was identified under ultrasound guidance at the midaxillary line between the serratus anterior muscle and the ribs and intercostal muscles. We chose the lateral decubitus position due to the ease of ultrasound‐guided injections, based on our routine clinical practice, where the dorsal decubitus position can be challenging for hand‐eye coordination and needle control during the SAB block. The plane was further established by injecting 1–2 mL of saline on the fourth rib and confirming the spread within the fascial plane. Once confirmed, 20 mL of diluted black India ink solution was injected above the fourth rib in a caudal to cranial direction using a 22‐G Stimuplex (Braun, Bethlehem, PA, USA) 80‐mm echogenic needle (Figure 1B). The combined injections were performed bilaterally, and specific injection pressures were not recorded.

Dissection, Photography, and Data Analysis

All the dissections were performed by a final year master student of the Department of Anatomy and Cell Biology (VPH), Western University, under the guidance of a supervising professor of the Department of Anatomy and Cell Biology (TW; AA). Dissection of the anterior and lateral chest occurred 15 min after the initial injection to outline the spread of dye, simulating clinical practice. Skin and subcutaneous fat were reflected with cuts made along the length of the clavicle, down the mid‐sternum, and along the costal margin to the midaxillary line, allowing lateral reflection of the skin while maintaining the integrity of the axilla. Careful consideration was made in the areas of dye spread, ensuring superficial dissection was performed to retain the original dye dispersion within the serratus anterior and parasternal intercostal fascial planes. Spread of dye towards the nerves of interest was noted and photographed with a measurement scale within the field of view within each plane after each step of muscle reflection.

First, the sternal and clavicular insertions of the pectoralis major were cut, enabling complete lateral reflection of the muscle. Blunt dissection was performed to lift the muscle fibers, while retaining the underlying neurovascular structures to observe the spread of the injectate at the parasternal intercostal plane. Next, the origins of pectoralis minor along ribs 3–5 were cut, enabling complete superior reflection to expose fibers of the serratus anterior muscle. Lastly, the visible origins of the serratus anterior muscle along ribs T‐8/9 were cut, enabling complete lateral reflection to view the spread of the injectate at the serratus anterior plane.

The spread of dye along the chest wall was quantified by the number of intercostal nerves stained in the supero‐inferior plane at the parasternal area and at the midaxillary line in the mediolateral plane. Qualitative observations were recorded on data collection sheets to accompany the photography immediately after dissection.

Results

Dye injections at deep serratus anterior and superficial parasternal intercostal planes were performed on bilateral hemithoraces in four unembalmed cadavers (eight hemithoraces) and five partially embalmed cadavers (10 hemithoraces). The injectate for serratus anterior plane blocks one and two (partially embalmed) was not visible within the fascial planes when dissected, as green dye, instead of black India Ink, was used as part of a pilot study and did not stain the tissues well. Thus, results of the serratus anterior plane injections are reported out of 16 (partially embalmed n = 8, unembalmed n = 8). The injectate for two parasternal intercostal plane blocks was injected superficial to the pectoralis major due to poor ultrasound visibility of the structures. Thus, results of the parasternal intercostal plane injections are reported out of 16 (partially embalmed n = 8, unembalmed n = 8). We found the volume of injectate had the most extensive spread at the parasternal intercostal plane. With a readily recognizable tissue plane above the ribs, these injections were simpler to execute. We could easily recognize a continuous fascial plane beneath pectoralis major and minor muscles. The lateral pectoral nerve and medial pectoral nerve could be consistently stained by dye distribution in this plane. The dye stained the anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves and stayed concentrated along the parasternal line in the area between the midsternal and midclavicular lines in 50% of the dissections.

Spread of Dye in Superficial Parasternal Intercostal Plane

The spread of injectate after Superficial PIP injection in cadavers is shown in Table 1. Following injections in all specimens, the cranial spread of injectate was observed up to T2 level in 14/16 (88%) specimens. Caudal dye spread to T5 was found in 14/16 (88%) specimens. Dye stained the medial and lateral pectoral nerves in 14/16 (88%) of the specimens. Injection 13 resulted in intramuscular staining of the pectoralis major muscle; however, there was no visible staining of the terminal branches of either medial or lateral pectoral nerves. A 20 mL injection made at the level of the fourth rib in a cranial direction was enough to create a spread that would consistently stain anterior cutaneous branches of T2–T5 as well as the lateral and medial pectoral nerves.

Following parasternal intercostal plane injections in eight partially embalmed hemithoraces, 88% of the dissections demonstrated cranial dye spread to T2. Consistent distal spread to T5 was noted in 63% and T6 in 25% of injections, respectively. Spread to the medial and lateral pectoral nerves was noted in 63% of specimens. Out of the three specimens that did not demonstrate dye spread to the medial and lateral pectoral nerves, two had previous thoracic dissections, which may have been a reason for inadequate spread.

Following injections in eight unembalmed hemithoraces, dye spread was noted cranially to T2 and caudally to T5 in 7/8 (88%) of specimens.

Spread of Dye in Serratus Anterior Plane

The spread of injectate after SAP injection in cadavers is shown in Table 2. Following injections in the serratus anterior plane, the cranial spread of injectate was observed in lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves up to T3 level in 9/16 (56%) of specimens and caudal spread to T6 in 14/16 (88%) specimens. The dye extended laterally into the area between the anterior axillary line and the midclavicular line at T4 level in 50% (8/16) of the injections.

For serratus anterior plane injections in eight partially embalmed specimens, the cranial injectate spread was observed in 5/8 (63%) to T3 level while caudal spread to T6 and beyond was noted in 7/8 (88%) specimens. Two of the specimens had previous thoracic surgery and demonstrated dye spread limited between T6 and T9. It is notable that the majority of spread in the SAP plane remained below T3, despite directing the injections cranially at the level of the fourth rib. In all serratus anterior plane injections, dye spread remained concentrated along the midaxillary line, staining the lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves without spread in a mediolateral direction beyond the anterior axillary line.

In eight unembalmed hemithoraces, cranial dye spread was noted as high as T2 in 3/8 (38%) of specimens; in another 3/8 (38%) of specimens, dye spread was limited to T4. Consistent distal dye spread was noted from T6 and beyond in 7/8 (88%) of specimens.

Discussion

Our results suggest that an extensive spread of injectate in chest wall fascial planes is possible by combining the PIP and SAP injections. As expected, we were able to target both the anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves with these two fascial plane injections. Injection at T4 in the parasternal intercostal plane demonstrated constant staining of T2–T5 anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerve, but without coverage at the T6 level. Following injection at the deep serratus anterior plane at the fourth rib, most spread was at level T3 and below, as low as T9 lateral cutaneous branch of the intercostal nerve.

Sensory innervation of the breast is complex, with its cutaneous and subcutaneous structures primarily innervated by the anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves T2–T6 [7]. The intercostal nerves give rise to the lateral cutaneous branches at the midaxillary line before traveling anteriorly to emerge more superficially as the anterior cutaneous branches at the parasternal line [7]. Although the lateral and medial pectoral nerves are primarily motor nerves, peripheral nerve blocks targeting these nerves have demonstrated significant analgesic benefits in clinical studies. Patients receiving inter‐pectoral (PECS‐1) blocks (that target lateral and medial pectoral nerve) for breast surgery have shown reduced opioid consumption and lower pain scores compared to those receiving only systemic analgesia [18, 19].

This study demonstrated that injections at the superficial parasternal intercostal plane may also target the medial and lateral pectoral nerves, as evidenced by the spread of dye along the fascial plane between the pectoralis major and minor muscles. This would make this fascial plane block unique in its ability to cover both the anterior cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves and the pectoral nerves, as opposed to interpectoral blocks, which mainly target the pectoral nerves and the lateral cutaneous branches [20]. Our results are consistent with other recent studies evaluating injectate spread in the intercostal plane, further reinforcing the utility of this block [21, 22]. These studies noted that injections in the deep intercostal plane provided superior parasternal spread but did not target the pectoral nerves. Furthermore, the deep parasternal intercostal plane block is technically more challenging due to the proximity of the internal mammary artery, pleura, and pericardium [21]. Earlier studies demonstrated that the combined approach of superficial PIP with PECS I and SAP was superior in providing perioperative analgesia compared to the combination of interpectoral block (PECS I) and SAP [23]. This study is very meaningful anatomically, as a combined approach of these three blocks will definitely provide comprehensive analgesia. But this approach has its limitations as it involves three injections with increased risk of LAST.

Our study demonstrated an existing continuous fascial plane at the superficial PIP level. Dye spread demonstrated staining of the anterior cutaneous branches of intercostal nerves as well as the lateral and medial pectoral nerves in more than 70% of the donors. The combination of SAP block and superficial PIP block only involves two injections and likely reduces the risk of LAST with the possibility of complete analgesic coverage of the surgical area compared to Song et al. [23]. Multiple injections may sound daunting to some patients, as low patient satisfaction is most commonly related to discomfort during block placement and transient post‐operative pain when the effects of local anesthesia initially subside [24]. However, both routine clinical practice and several studies have overwhelmingly found that the use of multiple injections results in a greater success rate when compared to single‐injection techniques while also having a quicker onset and longer duration [25, 26].

A comprehensive fascial plane block could be of value in improving perioperative pain management after breast surgery as a part of multimodal analgesic techniques [3]. This may help reduce the risk of both acute and chronic post‐surgical pain, thus improving overall quality of recovery [3, 5]. Our results are consistent with existing research demonstrating significantly lower pain scores and higher patient satisfaction after breast surgery with this combined technique [16, 17, 23].

Paravertebral blocks are commonly used for breast surgery and have been shown to provide similar analgesia to the serratus anterior plane (SAP) block. Despite their effectiveness, paravertebral blocks are associated with potential complications, including epidural spread (2.1%), hypotension, and pneumothorax [27, 28]. Given the technical complexity and potential risks of the paravertebral approach, a dual fascial plane block combined with multimodal analgesia may offer a safer and equally effective alternative for perioperative pain management in breast surgery.

Our study had several limitations, including a limited sample size, suboptimal patient positioning during dye injection at the SAB, and the inherent constraints of any cadaveric model. We were required to use both unembalmed and partially embalmed cadavers to achieve a meaningful sample size as there was limited availability of cadavers donated through a body bequeathal program and shared within an academic teaching institution. Though we had mixed specimen types, dye dispersion was similar and consistent in most of the specimens. One possible factor for the more inferior spread at the serratus anterior site was difficulty locating the fourth rib due to suboptimal positioning of the cadavers. This could have resulted in more limited dye spread as compared to previous studies.

There are inherent limitations to any cadaveric study. The spread of dye can vary depending on the biomechanical properties of the cadaveric tissue, such as whether the specimen is embalmed or fresh‐frozen. Additionally, the distribution may differ from that seen in living patients due to factors such as tissue compartment pressures, characteristics of the fascia, respiratory movement, and the muscle tone present in vivo. Therefore, findings from cadaveric dissection studies should be interpreted as indicative or suggestive rather than definitive, serving primarily as a signal for potential clinical outcomes.

A more extensive spread of injectate could be obtained by increasing the injectate volumes. Injection at serratus anterior plane in a caudal‐to‐cranial direction at the fourth rib led to spread that favored the inferior T3–T6 intercostal spaces, whereas the injection at the parasternal intercostal plane at the same level led to spread to the superior T2–T5 intercostal spaces. Future studies could explore serratus anterior plane injections at a higher rib (such as the third) and injection of the PIP at a lower rib (such as the fifth) to determine if the level of injection affects the distribution of spread.

Conclusion

This dual‐injection technique, targeting the deep serratus anterior plane and superficial parasternal intercostal plane, is unique in its ability to achieve extensive spread, effectively covering both the lateral and anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves, as well as the medial and lateral pectoral nerves. The promising findings from this study provide clinicians with anatomical evidence to support this technique for comprehensive fascial plane block in breast surgery.
