Current and future use of donor human milk in hospitals: data from polish human milk banks
Authors: Małgorzata Witkowska-Zimny, Melania Majewska, Aleksandra Mołas, Gabriela Olędzka, Aleksandra Wesołowska, representatives of the network of human milk banks in Poland “MILKNET”
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDonor human milk (DHM) is the recommended alternative when a mother’s own milk is unavailable or insufficient, especially for preterm and ill infants. Poland has established a national network of non-profit, hospital-based human milk banks (HMBs) to ensure equitable access to DHM. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the development and activity of Polish HMBs between 2012 and 2024 and identifies future directions for the national human milk banking system. It evaluates trends in the number of HMBs, cooperation between HMBs and hospitals, donor numbers, milk volume, and recipient profiles to support equitable service planning and adaptation to new EU regulations recognising human milk as a Substance of Human Origin (SoHO).MethodsThe study analysed retrospective data collected by the Human Milk Bank Foundation (HMBF) from all Polish HMBs. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the number of milk banks, HMB–hospital cooperation, donor numbers, milk volume, and the number and types of recipients.ResultsIn 2024, Poland’s HMB network included 16 non-profit, hospital-based banks supplying DHM to 83 hospitals. The average number of donors per HMB decreased over time, from 37 donors in the first bank in 2012 to an average of 20 donors per bank in 2023. This decline partly reflects the expansion of the HMB network rather than a reduction in donor engagement. From 2017 to 2024, over 30,000 L of milk were donated, and over 26,000 newborns received DHM. In 2023 and 2024, approximately 25% of preterm infants were DHM recipients in Poland—however, variability in hospital eligibility criteria and a decreasing donor-per-bank ratio represent systemic challenges.ConclusionPoland’s HMB model demonstrates how coordinated policy, NGO involvement, and public funding can ensure equitable access to DHM. To meet growing demand and align with EU SoHO regulations, national guidelines should be expanded, eligibility criteria standardised, and the milk bank system further professionalised.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of life and exclusively breastfeeding infants for the first six months. Only human milk provides essential nutrients and bioactive components that are crucial for the health and development of newborns, particularly those born prematurely. WHO further recommends that low birth weight (LBW) infants who cannot be fed with their own mother’s milk should receive donor human milk (DHM) as the preferred alternative, provided that safe and affordable milk banking services are available or can be established [1]. In addition, when a mother’s own milk is unavailable or insufficient, DHM is considered the optimal alternative for hospitalised newborns until lactation has been established in the biological mother.

The psychological stress associated with preterm birth may impair lactation [2]. The availability of DHM can reduce pressure on mothers and support both maternal lactation and psychological well-being [3]. DHM plays a vital role in neonatal care, especially for preterm infants and newborns with serious health problems, by reducing the risk of serious complications and acting as nutritional therapy [4]. Many studies have shown that it helps prevent conditions such as necrotising enterocolitis (NEC), a complication more frequently observed in preterm infants fed cow’s-milk-based formula, and reduces the length of stay in critical care [5, 6]. However, recent systematic reviews consistently indicate that DHM reduces the risk of NEC in very preterm or very low birth weight infants, while showing little or no effect on late-onset invasive infection or all-cause mortality before hospital discharge compared with infant formula [4, 7, 8].

The recent American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) position reinforces the need for DHM for very-low-birth-weight preterm infants, while the British Association of Perinatal Medicine (BAPM) advocates broader access to DHM [9, 10]. When a mother’s own milk is unavailable or contraindicated, DHM from a milk bank may be used as a temporary substitute, supporting an equal start in life and bridging until the maternal milk is available. Recognising the medical importance of DHM and the need to ensure its safety, quality, and equitable access, the new EU legislation has recognised DHM as SoHO (Substances of Human Origin) since 2024. Therefore, a new regulatory framework is required for the Member States [11]. This study aims to assess and summarise the past and project the future use of DHM in hospitals, based on data collected from all non-profit Polish milk banks. It focuses on the number of milk banks, HMB–hospital cooperation, donor numbers, milk volume, and the number and types of recipients. The study aimed to: (i) evaluate and understand current DHM usage; (ii) identify variability in eligibility criteria and guidelines; and (iii) predict future demand and support planning for equitable HMB service provision. It also provides a comprehensive overview on the development of the HMB network in Poland and summarises the previous DHM distribution practices.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a national retrospective observational analysis of the development, activity, and service coverage of non-profit HMBs operating in Poland between 2012 and 2024. The analysis focused on trends in the number of HMBs, voluntary donor recruitment, milk volume collection and distribution to hospitals, and recipients’ coverage.

Data collection

Aggregated annual data were collected by the HMBF from all operational Polish HMBs. The basis for data collection was a partnership agreement between the hospitals and the HMBF. The study included information obtained through an annual survey monitoring the number of donors per year, the volume of donated milk, the number of hospitals to which each HMB provided DHM, and the number of recipients. Data collected between 2012 and 2024 were compiled. Since 2023, the survey has also included questions on indications for DHM and the priority recipient group fed with DHM. Ethical approval was not required because the study used aggregated, anonymised statistical data.

National open-access data—including the number of advanced neonatal care units and total live births, as well as the number of infants born with a birth weight < 2,500 g—were obtained from the National Health Fund and the Polish Central Statistical Office (GUS) databases [12, 13].

Statistical analysis methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. Data are presented as numbers and annual totals. Temporal changes were evaluated descriptively by comparing the number of HMBs, year-to-year variations in donor numbers, milk volume, and recipient counts. Each HMB was assigned a unique alphabetic identifier (e.g., A, B, C…) for analysis. Due to the aggregated nature of the dataset and the service-monitoring design, inferential statistical testing was not performed. To assess coverage of potential demand for DHM in the analysed region, a demand coverage indicator was calculated. The reference population consisted of all newborns born in the region during the analysed calendar year with a birth weight < 2,500 g, based on official regional statistical data. The demand coverage indicator was calculated as the percentage of infants fed DHM among all newborns with a birth weight < 2,500 g in the administrative region.

Results

Development of HMB network in Poland

There are currently 16 non-profit HMBs operating within hospitals in Poland, of which 15 are under the patronage of the HMBF (Fig. 1). In 2012, there were 2 HMBs; in 2015–4; in 2016–6; in 2017–7; and in 2018- 11 (Table 1). A dynamic increase in the number of HMBs occurred after 2018, when state budget financing was launched to support the establishment and operation of milk banks, as well as reimbursement for enteral nutrition for preterm infants up to 4 weeks of corrected age and for ill, low birth weight infants up to 4 weeks of age [14]. HMBs were established within hospital structures, most often in facilities providing highly specialised services for preterm infants. Initially, each HMB supplied milk only to newborns from its parent hospital. Over time, HMBs expanded the number of facilities they supplied with DHM, which was treated as a therapeutic product. As a result, the number of hospitals in Poland receiving milk from HMBs increased steadily. In 2024, this comprised 83 hospital facilities (64,8%) out of 128 hospitals with advanced level II and III neonatal care units [15]. A summary of aggregated data from HMBs in Poland is presented in Table 1.

Donors

In the first year of operation, the first Polish HMB, coded as A, cooperated with 37 donors. The highest total number of donors, 532, was recorded in 2019 (Tables 1 and 2). The average number of donors per HMB decreased over time, from 37 donors per HMB in 2012 to 20 donors per bank in 2023. This trend reflected the increase in the number of milk banks, with 15 operational in 2023 compared with just one in 2012. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic led to a gradual decline in the number of donors, reaching its lowest point in 2023, with only 305 registered donors. It was not until 2024 that the number of women donating milk to the banks increased again, reaching 438 (an average of 27 per unit). Across all years, HMB coded as G consistently had the highest number of donors, the largest volume of donated milk, and the highest number of human milk recipients.

Meanwhile, smaller milk banks, such as HMBs coded as M and J, consistently had the fewest donors. For example, in 2023, HMB coded as M had just 5 donors, the lowest number among all milk banks. The data show a trend towards an increasing number of milk banks, which has led to a lower average number of donors per bank.

Volume

The amount of donated milk has been tracked since 2017. That year, seven active HMBs collected a total of 1,753 L of donor milk. In 2018, with 11 banks operating, the volume rose to 4,916 L (Table 1). Over the last six years, a total of 30,993 L of donor milk were collected, averaging 5,165.5 L per year (Table 2).

In 2020, a total of 4,802 L of donor milk were delivered across 16 milk banks. This represented a slight decrease compared with 2019, when 5,271 L were delivered, and it occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. A similar decline in the volume of donated milk was recorded in 2023 (4,939 L compared with 5,215 L in 2022), when one of the milk banks in Warsaw (coded as A) was closed. According to 2024 data, Polish HMBs collected 5,590 L of milk. Among the milk banks, HMB coded as G, consistently collected the largest volumes of donor milk, delivering more than 700 L per year from 2019, with a peak donated milk volume of 1,001 L in 2021. Other notable milk banks included HMB coded as H, which delivered 962 L in 2022, and HMB coded as B, which provided 992 L in 2020. In contrast, smaller banks such as HMB coded as M and J had lower deliveries, with HMB coded as M, contributing only 54 L in 2023.

Recipients

This part of the study aimed to understand the current use of DHM in Polish advanced neonatal care units. Data on the number of patients who received milk from HMBs have been collected since 2017, and the number of milk recipients has been growing steadily. In 2017, there were 939 recipients; in 2018–2,313; and by 2023, the number had exceeded 5,000. The number of recipients reached 5,071 in 2023 and 5,389 in 2024.

The number of hospitalised patients who received DHM varied significantly between facilities. The highest number of recipients was recorded at HMB coded as G (1,033 and 1,007 recipients in 2022 and 2023, respectively) and HMB coded as D (1,022 in total, including 69 patients who received milk from the HMB parent hospital, and 953 patients in other facilities supplied by this milk bank in 2023). High numbers of recipients were also observed at HMB coded as L (490 in 2022, 558 in 2023 and 569 in 2024). Some facilities, such as HMB coded as K (77 and 81 patients in 2023 and 2024, respectively) and HMB coded as H (40 patients in 2023), had lower numbers of milk recipients. Over the past six years, 26,536 newborns received donor milk from Polish HMBs.

Figure 2 presents coverage of DHM demand, calculated based on the number of infants born with a birth weight < 2,500 g in a given administrative unit in 2024. Coverage ranged from below 10% to nearly 80%, indicating marked disparities in access to donor milk relative to the number of infants born with a birth weight < 2,500 g. The highest levels of DHM demand coverage were observed in selected western and northern administrative units, where coverage exceeded 70%, with a maximum value of 78%. In contrast, several regions demonstrated low coverage, below 20%, including one administrative unit where coverage was estimated at less than 10%. In central regions, coverage levels were generally moderate, most commonly ranging between 30% and 40%. There were no HMB in two administrative units; therefore, data were unavailable.

Priority for receiving donated milk was given to hospitalised preterm infants up to 4 weeks of corrected age and to sick neonates with low birth weight up to 4 weeks of age; however, patient demand for milk from HMB was much broader. In 2023 and 2024, a survey was conducted among milk banks to gather information about recipients of donated milk who received milk while undergoing therapy in hospitals where milk banks operated (Table 3). According to the survey, preterm infants up to 4 weeks of corrected age, regardless of the method of milk administration, and sick newborns requiring enteral nutrition who, due to unforeseen circumstances, could not be fed with their own mother’s milk were eligible to receive DHM from all HMBs in Poland, subject to parental informed consent. Only two milk banks (coded as I and J) did not provide DHM for sick newborns without formal indications for enteral nutrition when they could not be fed with their own mother’s milk due to unforeseen circumstances. Seven milk banks provided DHM for newborns born at term, who required surgical intervention, and whose mothers experienced lactation difficulties: HMBs B, C, E, G, L, M, and P. Three HMBs coded as B, G, and H provided donor milk to all children whose mothers experienced lactation problems during their hospital stay. In exceptional cases, such as maternal death, newborns received donor milk in three milk banks: E, G, and L. In the case of infants whose mothers had undergone mastectomy, donor milk was provided by HMBs coded as L, M, and N. When the mother suffered from a chronic illness preventing breastfeeding, full-term newborns—regardless of mode of delivery or clinical condition—received donor milk from HMBs coded as B, L, M, and N.

Discussion

Development of the polish HMB network

This study presents a comprehensive national analysis of the development and activities of HMBs in Poland over a decade of operation. The development of the Polish network of HMBs has been significantly shaped by the activities of the non-governmental organisation, the HMBF, which was established in 2009 and registered with the Patient Ombudsman, and represents the interests and needs of children and their caregivers in terms of equal access to human milk. Thanks to this background initiative, the first HMB in Poland was opened in 2012 in cooperation with the HMBF. The first HMB was funded by local municipal authorities (coded as A), while subsequent HMBs were developed within local government programmes (coded as C), leading to expanded coverage across entire regions. Paradoxically, the first Polish HMB, established in 2012, was closed in 2023 following the closure of the maternity and neonatology department in that hospital.

The operating principles of milk banks were defined through HMBF expert recommendations prepared in accordance with the principles of the European Milk Bank Association (EMBA) [16, 17]. To date, Polish national guidelines have not had the status of national law [18]. With regard to quality monitoring of milk bank services in Poland, the HACCP system (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point) was piloted within a research project; however, it is not, and has never been, a mandatory tool [19].

The collected longitudinal data show that while the total volume of donor milk delivered to hospitals in Poland increased over the years, the average volume per milk bank decreased as more banks were established. This trend reflected the ongoing expansion of milk bank services across the country, ensuring broader access to donor milk, but it also suggests that individual milk banks handled smaller amounts of milk.

The results demonstrate a dynamic expansion of the Polish milk banking network, alongside increasing HMB-hospital collaboration, but also highlight systemic challenges that must be addressed to ensure equitable and sustainable access to DHM for vulnerable neonates and other potential recipients groups.

Policy, financing and the role of the HMBF

The development of the Polish HMB network indicates that policymakers have significant opportunities at the national level to strengthen HMB development and the use of DHM feeding to ensure access for all patients who need it.

A breakthrough moment in the development of the HMB initiative in Poland was the inclusion of human-milk-based enteral nutrition in the catalogue of guaranteed healthcare services—as reimbursement of nutritional therapy either with the mother’s own expressed milk or with DHM when the mother’s own milk is unavailable —for preterm and ill newborns up to 4 weeks of corrected age [14]. A second important factor was the allocation of funds from the state budget for equipping HMB laboratories under the governmental programme “For Life” to establish an interregional HMB network. Together, these measures led to an increase in the number of HMBs to 16 units by 2019 and to an expansion of the scope of their activities. The results of this study illustrate a robust response from hospitals to the therapeutic needs of preterm and ill newborns as well as progress towards greater equity in DHM access for patients. State budget financing for establishing and equipping HMBs, as well as for supplying DHM from milk banks in Poland, demonstrates how appropriately shaped healthcare policy can increase the availability of services. The HMBF has played a pivotal role in the development of the national network. Its advocacy contributed to the inclusion of human milk in national reimbursement schemes and continues to influence public policy. Its efforts should be acknowledged as instrumental in establishing milk banking as an integral component of perinatal care in Poland.

HMB-hospital collaboration and equity in regional DHM access

The results demonstrate the dynamic expansion of the Polish milk banking network and increasing HMB-hospitals collaboration, while also highlighting systemic challenges that must be addressed to ensure equitable and sustainable access to DHM for vulnerable neonates and other potential beneficiary patients.

This study revealed significant heterogeneity in hospital practice concerning the eligibility criteria for DHM use—an issue also noted in international contexts [2]. Variability in clinical practices and criteria results from internal or local decisions about covering DHM costs for beneficiaries outside the scope of reimbursement. However, this may lead to unequal treatment and resource distribution [20]. According to EDQM, access to DHM, considered as SoHO should extend beyond preterm infants to a broader group of patients, given its various potential benefits, for example, in managing necrotising enterocolitis [21].

While overall milk volume and beneficiary numbers have increased, the average volume per HMB and the number of donors per bank have decreased due to system expansion and contextual factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic. Periods of reduced milk donation, such as in 2020 and 2023, underscore the importance of planning for service continuity and strengthening the integrity and capacity of the HMB network, particularly as demand for DHM is projected to rise in line with global recommendations [22, 23].

The increased demand for DHM also reflects greater clinical awareness among healthcare providers regarding the therapeutic function of human milk, parental preferences, and global health authority recommendations. The collected data indicate that 28,867 newborns have received milk from HMBs in Poland, with the annual number exceeding 5,000 since 2023. In the context of a continuously declining birth rate— 273,369 children were born in Poland in 2023, including 19,635 preterm births, while 5,071 infants were recipients of donor human milk. This means that approximately one in four preterm infants received support from Polish HMBs. Thus, the proportion of children receiving DHM is increasing.

Our results highlight the essential role of regional milk banks, not only in supporting infants within their own hospitals but also in their regional coverage in supplying milk to neonatal units in medical centres without HMBs. Overall, the spatial distribution revealed a heterogeneous pattern of DHM availability, suggesting uneven capacity of regional systems to meet the nutritional needs of preterm and low-birth-weight infants.

Future DHM system development and implications of the EU SoHO regulation

Under the new EU regulation, HMBs are SoHO establishments, while neonatal units using milk from HMBs are classified as SoHO entities [11]. Following the national implementation of the SoHO Regulation, many new obligations will apply to both SoHO establishments and SoHO entities. One such obligation is detailed reporting on the use of DHM from HMBs, including recipient follow-up.

Our study revealed substantial gaps in data reporting, particularly from hospitals receiving DHM from regional HMBs. In a few cases (HMBs coded as E and H), no data were available on patients receiving milk outside the facility. In practice, this lack of data prevents reliable assessment of clinical outcomes and identification of adverse events associated with DHM feeding. The SoHO regulation mandates the validation of protocols and practices applied in SoHO entities, along with periodic risk analyses and audits.

The WHO recommendations on the care of preterm and low birth weight infants indicate that DHM should be available to all preterm infants when a mother’s own milk is insufficient [1]. However, demand for DHM from neonatal units can fluctuate significantly, making it difficult, particularly for small local milk banks to assess and predict demand for their services. Therefore, models integrating HMBs with broader cell and tissue banking systems are being considered. Such integration is already taking place in many countries, and EU legislation appears to support and further strengthen this approach.

Strengths and limitations

The present study, based on aggregated national data collected from Polish HMBs between 2012 and 2024, has several important limitations. First, the analysis was based on retrospective, aggregated annual data collected through surveys. Second, the quality and completeness of reporting varied between milk banks and hospitals. Substantial gaps in HMB data collection and reporting were identified. No data were available on recipients outside the parent institution, which limits the reliability of coverage estimates and prevents assessment of clinical outcomes. Third, the study relied on self-reported institutional data provided within the framework of partnership agreements, which may be subject to reporting bias or inconsistencies in definitions (e.g., eligibility criteria, recipient classification). Fourth, the demand coverage indicator was based on the number of infants with a birth weight < 2,500 g in a given administrative region. However, fluctuations in local demand, cross-regional milk distribution, and variability in eligibility criteria may affect the accuracy of regional comparisons.

The conclusions are limited to system-level trends in supply, distribution, and service coverage rather than patient-level effectiveness. An important limitation of this study is that it did not collect data on the number of women who were recruited and qualified as potential donors but ultimately did not provide milk to the bank. Therefore, it was not possible to assess donor attrition rates or identify barriers between recruitment and actual milk donation. Furthermore, the dataset did not include information on the volume of donated milk that was discarded (e.g., due to microbiological screening failure, expiry or logistical reasons). As a result, the analysis reflects only the volume of milk collected and distributed, without insight into losses within the system. Finally, no data were collected on potential recipients who were eligible for donor human milk but did not receive it due to insufficient supply at a given time. Consequently, the study does not allow estimation of unmet clinical demand or the extent of shortages in specific regions or periods.

Despite these limitations, the study provides the first comprehensive national overview of the development and activity of the Polish HMB network. The findings indicate substantial geographic inequalities in the ability to meet DHM demand among preterm infants with a birth weight < 2,500 g, underscoring the need for coordinated, regionally tailored strategies to ensure more equitable availability of DHM nationwide. In light of the SoHO regulation, a key priority should be not only reducing of regional disparities in access to DHM within the country, but also unifying access to donor milk across borders.

Conclusions

This summary of HMBs activity in Poland enables an initial analysis of milk supply trends in response to growing demand, which is particularly important during adaptation to the new SoHO regulations. While compliance with EU Regulation 2024/1938 poses organisational and financial challenges for HMBs, it also offers an opportunity to formalise services, strengthen quality and safety standards, and expand the scope of milk bank activities.

Existing evidence highlights the growing role of regional milk banks supplying multiple hospitals, suggesting a foundation for further development of coordinated HMB networks and broader service provision. At the same time, increasing demand—driven by expanded eligibility and greater acceptance of DHM as SoHO, underscores the need for systematic collaboration between neonatal units, HMBs, donors and recipients, as well as further research on demand forecasting, distribution models and clinical effectiveness of DHM.
