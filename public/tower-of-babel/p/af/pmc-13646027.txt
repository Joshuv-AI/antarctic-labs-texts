Early discharge after paediatric liver biopsy: A prospective observational study
Authors: Fie Brantbjerg Tinning, Jakob Stensballe, Birthe Henriksen, Marianne Hørby Jørgensen, Bente Utoft Andreassen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the safety of early discharge after percutaneous liver biopsy (PLB) in children using prospective bleeding risk stratification.MethodsIn this prospective observational cohort study, paediatric patients scheduled for PLB were stratified into low‐ and high‐risk groups based on bleeding history and laboratory values. Low‐risk patients were discharged after 6 h, while high‐risk patients were observed for 24 h. Outcomes included bleeding complications, transfusion requirements and readmissions.ResultsA total of 167 patients underwent PLB; 119 (71%) were classified as low‐risk and 48 (29%) as high‐risk. Two minor bleeding events occurred, both in the high‐risk group. Only high‐risk patients received blood products prior to PLB.ConclusionPre‐procedural risk stratification effectively identifies paediatric patients bleeding risk and eligibility for early discharge after PLB. This approach appears safe and can reduce hospital admission time for low‐risk patients.Keywords: bleeding risk, hepatology, safety, trombelastography, ultrasound

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous liver biopsy (PLB) provides essential diagnostic information for liver disorders when non‐invasive methods are inconclusive. PLB enables assessment of liver damage, inflammation, cell morphology and fibrosis stage to aid diagnosis and monitor disease progression in conditions like biliary atresia, liver transplant rejection and autoimmune liver disease. 1 , 2 , 3

PLB is an invasive procedure carrying potential risks of bleeding, infection and perforation of other organs. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 The risk of minor bleeding after PLB in children is reported to be low to moderate, 1.5%–15% depending on the comorbidities especially oncological disease, focal lesions, coagulopathy or use of anticoagulation. The incidence of severe complications with bleeding combined with hemodynamic instability or necessitating blood transfusion ranges from 1:2500 to 10.000 (2.9–12). Although the total risk of complications is low, without proper treatment, the complications in PLB can potentially be severe.

The European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) published in 2015 a position paper recommending preprocedural coagulation testingto determine the risk of bleeding after PLB. 1 The following is a list of risk factors for complications: focal lesions, 1 , 4 , 6 , 9 , 10 acute liver failure, 1 , 4 , 6 , 9 , 10 infants age <3 month, 1 , 4 , 6 , 9 , 10 ascites or cirrhosis, 1 , 7 thrombocytopenia, 1 , 4 malignancy or bone marrow transplantation, 1 , 7 chronic renal failure 1 and biliary tract dilation or cholestasis. 1 , 7 Despite this, to date, there is still no strong consensus or official guideline to assess these factors and comorbidities before PLB.

The recommended admission time after a PLB is not well defined. It can be as short as 2–3 h in adult populations. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Many paediatric hospitals have traditionally chosen a 24‐h observation period that requires overnight admission. Few studies have investigated the duration from PLB to bleeding episodes in paediatric patients. Two studies have shown that reducing admission time for PLB in an outpatient setting is safe: A retrospective study conducted in 2020 involving 100 outpatient paediatric PLB per cohort demonstrated that reducing the post‐procedural observation period from 4 to 2 h did not result in an increased rate of complications. 17 Similarly, a retrospective cohort study of 112 paediatric patients undergoing PLB found that shortening the admission time to 8 h was not associated with a higher incidence of adverse events. 18 Furthermore, one study including 184 paediatric patients who underwent PLB supported the shortened admission time by showing that all clinically significant bleeding symptoms manifested within 4 h of the procedure. 19 One of these studies included patients with abnormalities of coagulation that were corrected before PLB. 19 One study included patients that received prophylactic fresh frozen plasma prior to PLB. 18 None of the studies included patients admitted to a hospital ward.

This study aimed to assess whether implementing a pre‐procedural bleeding risk stratification tool in a mixed‐risk paediatric hospital setting can safely support early discharge of low‐risk patients following PLB, thereby offering a clinically applicable model for a potential broader implementation.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was authorised by the Chief Physician of the Department of Paediatrics and Adolescent Medicine, Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark, and classified as a prospective observational quality improvement study conducted outside the scope of the Danish Act on Research Ethics Review of Health Research Projects (Act No. 1338 of 1 September 2020). Under Danish legislation, formal written informed consent was not required. Data handling was conducted in accordance with institutional data protection regulations. The study was approved by the institutional review board and categorised as a quality improvement project.

Use of artificial intelligence (AI)

AI‐based tools (PaperPal and ChatGPT) were used solely for language editing related to spelling and punctuation. No patient data, scientific content, or interpretation was generated, modified or analysed using AI.

Patient population

All children and adolescents (age 0–19 years) who underwent ultrasound‐guided PLB between October 2018 and October 2023 at our department for paediatric and adolescent medicine were included in this study. The standard procedure for admitting patients undergoing PLB was altered from a 24‐h observation period in the hospital to a new policy where low‐risk patients were monitored for 6 h in the outpatient clinic and high‐risk patients were admitted to the hospital ward for 24 h of observation.

Definition of low‐ and high‐risk group

To classify the patients into low‐ and high‐risk categories, we identified several variables to be considered in our risk evaluation. These factors were chosen based on existing research 1 , 4 , 6 , 7 , 9 , 10 , 20 and clinical experience and are listed in Table 1. All the patients were registered in the patient record system (EPIC). Data were collected from patient medical charts, such as age, sex, laboratory results, and indication for PLB, and the patients were stratified into low‐risk and high‐risk groups. We registered the use of blood products, k‐vitamins, fibrinogen substitution, or tranexamic acid prior to the procedure. Non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetylsalicylic acid were discontinued 4 days before the procedure. Low‐risk patients were observed in the outpatient clinic for 6 h for signs of complications. If there were no signs of bleeding on clinical examination and circulatory observation, the patient was discharged, provided that the journey back home was no longer than 30 min. Otherwise, patients were admitted to a hospital hotel. Patients with a high risk were admitted to the ward. They were discharged after 24 h if no bleeding or complications occurred. Upon discharge, families were provided with a direct telephone number to the paediatric emergency department open 24 h with skilled nurses answering the phone. They were instructed to call the nurses if pain did not resolve with paracetamol. The families were advised that the child must be supervised by a caregiver for the first 24 h and must not be left alone. Contact sports should be avoided the following week. A roadmap for patient flow can be seen in Figure 1. Outcomes were registered as complications defined as minor or major bleeding, readmission, surgery or death. Major bleeding events were defined as requiring clinical intervention, including surgery or administration of blood products, whereas minor bleeding events were defined by being managed conservatively with observation only and without further diagnostic evaluation. Ultrasound examinations were recorded for descriptive purposes to document the number of patients evaluated for suspected bleeding; ultrasound referral alone was not considered a complication. Rather than being recorded as a separate complication, severe pain was indirectly captured through hospital readmission.

Trombelastography (TEG)

TEG is a viscoelastic whole‐blood assay used to assess global haemostatic function. TEG was introduced in 2021 during the study period (2018–2023) and was thereafter routinely performed exclusively in high‐risk patients. Decisions regarding pre‐biopsy interventions were based on TEG results following consultation with the on‐call haemostasis specialist.

Liver biopsy

Liver biopsies were performed under ultrasound guidance using an 18‐G Tru‐Cut needle, under anaesthesiologist‐administered deep sedation with spontaneous ventilation. Local anaesthetic is applied. Samples are obtained from the right lobe using a subcostal or intercostal approach, depending on anatomical conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into SPSS (version 29.0.1.0 [171]) for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were assessed for normality by visual inspection of distribution plots, and all were found to be non‐normally distributed. Accordingly, continuous baseline characteristics are reported as median (interquartile range) and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages and were compared using χ 2 or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The distribution of PLB indications (diagnostic, liver transplantation control, other) between high‐ and low‐risk groups was compared using a χ 2 test based on a contingency table, and two‐sided p‐values are reported.

Associations between risk group and the outcome were assessed using Fisher's exact test due to sparse data and the presence of zero cells. Because of the very low number of events, odds ratios with confidence intervals and adjusted regression analyses, or multivariable analyses, could not be reliably estimated. There were no missing data for baseline characteristics, or the primary outcomes included in the analyses. TEG data were missing for some patients, but were not included in the statistical analyses and were used descriptively only.

RESULTS

We identified 167 patients who required PLB from 2018 to 2023. If the child had more than one biopsy during the study period, only the first biopsy was included in the analysis. The age was from 0 to 17 years, the ratio of male to female was 1:1.17, further characteristics are shown in Table 2. Of all the PLB, 48 (28.7%) were in the high‐risk group, and 119 (71.3%) were in the low‐risk group, respectively. One patient was excluded due to missing data, as he is a foreign national and not properly registered in our patient record system (EPIC). The indications for PLB were diagnostic evaluation in 116 PLB with 42 high‐ and 74 low‐risk (36.2% vs. 63.8%), 43 PLB were routinely protocolled PLB of LTX and only four of these were high risk (9.3%). Seven were suspicion of flair or disease control of autoimmune liver disease and one of those was high risk (14.3%), and one was on the suspicion of biliary atresia. Complications

The general outcome data are shown in Table 3. No patients died. Of the 167 PLB, two patients from the high‐risk group experienced minor bleedings (4.2%); none were major bleedings, and treatment was conservative non‐operative management. This difference did not reach statistical significance (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.081). One of the patients with a minor bleeding was classified as high risk due to impaired coagulation with an INR at 2.1 and had received FFP for the INR reduction, fibrinogen after TEG and k‐vitamin before PLB, and the other because of varices and had received fibrinogen substitution and platelets according to TEG results before PLB. Both bleeding events were discovered within 6 h of admission due to pain and were confirmed on ultrasound by visualising free liquid. Both patients resolved on conservative treatment and with good outcome.

One patient from the low‐risk group had readmission primarily due to pain, ultrasound was performed, and no bleeding was discovered. The only treatment was paracetamol as pain relief and observation. Prevention of bleeding risk pre‐biopsies

Vitamin K was given to 12 patients before PLB prophylactic, only based on known or suspected liver disease and not because of blood test results.

Blood products were not used in the low‐risk group. TEG was introduced in 2021 and then routinely offered to only the high‐risk group. In the high‐risk group 18 patients had TEG test performed pre procedure. In addition, 13 patients had TEG‐functional fibrinogen maximum amplitude available. Twenty‐one patients altogether received blood products, all from the high‐risk group. Seven patients had platelets before procedure, three of these with low platelet numbers, and three because of affected TEG results. The last patient had an acute presentation of autoimmune hepatitis, suspicion of cirrhosis (spider naevi) platelets at 95 × 109/l and INR at 1.4 and was biopsied before 2021 and was not offered a TEG test. Two received cryoprecipitate (Cryo) based on TEG. Tranexamic acid was given to eight patients, all after TEG test and in consultation with the coagulation expert, one based on INR alone. Four received FFP, three of whom were due to an INR over 1.4, and one based on the TEG results.

Fourteen patients had an INR > 1.4, 12 of these received a TEG prior to PLB, one patient was before 2021 before we started offering TEG and one was admitted to the neonatal ward and was not offered a TEG test. Only three of the patients received FFP after TEG and consultation with the coagulation expert prior to PLB.

DISCUSSION

In this single‐centre cohort study stratifying patients into low and high bleeding risk groups before PLB, we observed no major complications, such as death, infection or major bleeding in need of surgery in none of the two groups. Using this risk stratification procedure allowed for us to safely perform short‐term observation.

We observed two bleeding episodes that required only observation in the high‐risk group, and no one in the low‐risk group had any bleeding episodes. Importantly, no low‐risk patients experienced bleeding or required pre‐biopsy blood products, whereas a substantial proportion of high‐risk patients underwent pre‐biopsy coagulation assessment and prophylactic intervention, which likely influenced bleeding outcomes. The risk of minor bleeding complications in this study is 1.2% for all PLB and 4.2% for the high‐risk group. Reported incidences of minor complications range from 2.8% to 15%, depending on comorbidities. 1 , 9 , 21 Although overall complication rates in comparable studies are similar, several of these studies classify the administration of analgesics as either minor or major complications. In contrast, in the present study analgesic use and pain were not recorded as independent complications but were documented only in relation to readmission or as part of the clinical evaluation leading to the detection of minor bleeding events. It is feasible to assume that our complication rate would be higher if we were able to include use of analgesia as a measure of minor complications. Despite this limitation, our study still found a low overall bleeding rate compared to similar studies and confirms the existing data that all complications after PLB in children are rare. 1 , 9 , 21 These findings could also be interpreted in the context of the systematic risk stratification and targeted pre‐biopsy optimisation, particularly in the high‐risk group, rather than as evidence of uniformly low bleeding risk irrespective of patient characteristics.

Both minor bleeding events were discovered within 6 h of admission due to pain and were confirmed on ultrasound by visualising free liquid. This support similar studies that the majority of bleedings in a low risk population is discovered within 4–8 h of PLB in paediatric patients. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 18 The two bleeding events we discovered were in the high‐risk population and none in the low‐risk population, supporting the theory that 24 h admission post PLB can safely be shortened to 6 h for a subgroup of patients, if deemed low risk by specific criteria. Based on the strength of our findings, we have since implemented a 4‐h bedrest period and extended the transport limit to 60 min for low‐risk patients. This was supported by the clear safety signal observed in our results.

Because the complication rate was very low, it was not possible to demonstrate statistical significance of the stratification. Consensus on the criteria to choose for stratification are not fully agreed upon but many factors and comorbidities have been suggested. 1 , 4 , 6 , 9 , 10 This study chose a wide range of suggested risk factors. No major or minor complications occurred in the low‐risk group, supporting the theory that stratifying patients is a safe method for reducing the admission time. Because bleeding events were rare, we were unable to adjust for pre‐biopsy optimisation, laboratory parameters, or transplant status in multivariable models, and the study was not powered to exclude rare complications. This limitation should be considered when interpreting comparisons between risk groups. Another limitation of our study is the requirement for patients to remain close to the hospital after discharge following PLB, which may limit the applicability of our findings in countries with larger catchment areas. With further research, it may be possible to reduce the number of criteria for the high‐risk group, thereby reducing the admission time for a larger number of patients and extend the transportation time limit even further. A further limitation to consider is regarding the use of TEG. TEG was introduced during the study period and subsequently incorporated into pre‐biopsy optimisation strategies. Both observed bleeding events occurred after its implementation; however, the study was not designed to evaluate the independent effect of TEG on bleeding risk, and no conclusions can therefore be drawn regarding its preventive impact.

While the overall complication rate was low, this should be interpreted in light of systematic risk stratification. No bleeding events or pre‐biopsy blood product use occurred in the low‐risk group, whereas the heterogeneous high‐risk group more frequently received prophylactic interventions, which likely influenced bleeding outcomes. Compared with reported bleeding risks in the literature, this underscores the novelty of our study in demonstrating how risk stratification can guide both early discharge and targeted pre‐biopsy prophylaxis.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, PLB in paediatric patients was associated with a low complication rate. No complications occurred among any patients after 6 h, supporting the safety of early discharge in low‐risk cases. Pre‐procedural risk stratification appears to be a useful tool for identifying patients at higher risk of complications and to individualise interventions to reduce the risk of complication, although further studies are warranted.
