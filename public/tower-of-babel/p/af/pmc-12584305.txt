Obesity and health-related quality of life among immigrant Meskhetian Turks in Turkey: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Deniz Karabalık, Esma Aksoy Kendilci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a major determinant of health-related quality of life, affecting both physical and mental well-being. Meskhetian Turks, due to their migration history, face specific socioeconomic (low income, limited healthcare and education access) and cultural challenges (acculturation stress, dietary changes, social exclusion) that may increase obesity risk and adversely affect health-related quality of life.MethodsThis cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of obesity and health-related quality of life among immigrant Meskhetian Turks residing in Ahlat District, Bitlis Province, Turkey. A total of 120 adults (62 men, 58 women) participated voluntarily between October 2021 and May 2022. Data collection included socio-demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements (height, weight, waist, hip, and neck circumferences), and the SF-36 Health Survey.ResultsThe mean age was 41.32 ± 15.47 years. Overall, 60.8% were overweight and 14.2% had obesity. The mean SF-36 scores were below the norm values of Turkish society, indicating reduced health-related quality of life. Daily meal frequency was significantly associated with several SF-36 domains, with participants consuming ≥ 4 meals per day reporting lower physical and mental health scores. In multivariate regression analyses, age emerged as the only independent predictor of both PCS and MCS scores, with older adults reporting lower quality of life, whereas BMI and other sociodemographic and lifestyle factors were not independently associated.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the dual challenges of obesity and reduced quality of life among Meskhetian Turks. Interventions should particularly address the needs of older adults while also promoting healthy dietary behaviors. Future research should explore longitudinal and context-specific factors, including acculturation, chronic disease burden, and preventive health service access, to better understand how migration-related stressors influence health-related quality of life over time.Keywords: Meskhetian turks, Immigrant health, Obesity, Health-Related quality of life, SF-36

Introduction

Migration refers to the displacement of an individual from one place to another, usually voluntarily. However, mass migration can also be caused by disasters such as wars, conflicts, and diseases [1]. Meskhetian Turks have been forced to emigrate either voluntarily or forcibly due to war and political circumstances [2]. When the regions where Meskhetian Turks lived in Ukraine were destroyed by the conflicts between Russia and Crimea in 2014, 677 families who lived in risky regions were allowed to be admitted to the Republic of Turkiye under the status of settled immigrants upon the request of the Ukrainian Association of Meskhetian Turks [3]. Upon negotiations with the Emigration Bureau, 72 families of Meskhetian Turks from Ukraine were settled in Ahlat [4]. Such migration-related challenges can also influence lifestyle behaviors and health outcomes, including the risk of obesity and reduced quality of life among displaced populations.

Obesity is defined as a chronic disease characterized by an abnormal or excessive accumulation of body fat that presents a risk to health, typically assessed by Body Mass Index (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) [5]. According to the World Health Organization, more than 1 billion people worldwide are living with obesity, including 650 million adults. In Turkiye, the obesity rate among adults was reported as 20.2% in 2022 [5, 6]. In immigrant groups, acculturation stress, limited access to healthy food, and socioeconomic barriers may further exacerbate obesity risk.

Meskhetian Turks have experienced forced migration, which often results in socioeconomic disadvantages such as low income, limited access to healthcare, and unstable employment. These challenges, combined with lifestyle shifts—like altered dietary habits and reduced physical activity—commonly seen among immigrants, may increase obesity risk and negatively impact health-related quality of life. Immigrant populations worldwide tend to experience higher rates of overweight and obesity compared to host populations [7, 8], and studies show that within 10–15 years post-migration, overweight prevalence in migrant groups may approach or exceed that of the host country [9]. These observations suggest that obesity may represent a potential health risk for the Meskhetian Turk community in Ahlat.

Obesity can lead to high mortality diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases and its incidence is gradually increasing [10]. Although obesity appears to be the consequence of a disturbed balance between energy intake and consumption, different factors are involved in its etiology. Some of the underlying factors include age, eating habits, genetic factors, inactivity, psychological factors, and socio-economic and cultural factors [11].

A systematic review reported that higher adherence to a healthy diet, including Mediterranean-style eating, is positively associated with both physical and mental dimensions of quality of life [12]. Identifying the factors that contribute to quality of life provides an insight into the mechanisms for maintaining optimal levels of quality of life [13]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has been working to assess and measure quality of life since 1980. To this end, the comprehensive World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale (WHOQOL-BREF), which allows for the assessment of the well-being of an individual and cross-cultural comparisons, has been developed. Although the WHOQOL-BREF is a comprehensive tool developed by WHO, in this study, the SF-36 Health Survey was preferred due to its widespread use and well-established reliability and validity in the Turkish population [14]. Therefore, examining how obesity interacts with migration-related challenges to influence quality of life is of particular importance in vulnerable groups such as the Meskhetian Turks.

Immigrants often face not only physical health issues but also significant barriers in accessing healthcare services. These include language differences, limited knowledge of the system, and cultural mismatches [15, 16]. Communities with a history of forced migration, such as Meskhetian Turks, may experience these challenges more severely [17]. These difficulties can negatively affect their overall quality of life.

The eating habits of societies have turned into a tradition with the influence of the geography where they live and these habits have continued by passing on from the past. As science advances and conditions change, these eating habits can be observed to negatively affect people’s quality of life and lead to health problems. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of obesity and quality of life in immigrant Meskhetian Turks settled in the Ahlat district of Bitlis.

Methods

Study design and study participants

This is a cross-sectional study. The study participants were recruited from the community of Meskhetian Turks who were officially settled in the Ahlat district of Bitlis, Turkey, under the framework of Law No. 3835 on the Acceptance and Settlement of Ahiska Turks, published in the Official Gazette on May 17, 2015. The sample was drawn from households registered in this settlement program and currently residing in Ahlat. Eligible participants were contacted through local community networks and household visits.

This study employed a census approach. All adults from households of Meskhetian Turks officially settled in the Ahlat district (Bitlis, Turkey) under Law No. 3835 were approached for participation. Therefore, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The researchers aimed to include all individuals aged 18 years and older; of the 180 eligible individuals, 120 participated and provided complete data, yielding a response rate of 66%. A total of 60 individuals were excluded from the study due to various reasons such as being under 18 years old, being pregnant, serving in the military, being out of province, or declining to participate in the study. The data were collected between October 2021 and May 2022.

Participants did not receive any financial compensation for taking part in the study. However, they were briefly informed about their anthropometric measurements, such as body weight, BMI, and waist circumference, after data collection.

The participants were informed about the study and granted their written informed consent. Voluntary participants who were not pregnant or lactating, had no physical, mental, or other disabilities, and agreed to participate were included in the study. Pregnant and lactating women were excluded because physiological changes during this period could influence both body composition and quality of life outcomes. Similarly, individuals with disabilities were excluded, as such conditions could independently and substantially affect health-related quality of life, introducing potential confounding factors unrelated to obesity.

Data collection methods

The questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews. The researcher read the questions aloud and recorded participants’ responses directly, ensuring consistency in data collection and minimizing misinterpretation.

The researchers prepared the questionnaire upon a literature review, which included socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, occupation, educational background, and marital status), anthropometric measures, and the SF-36 Quality of Life Scale [18–20].

Occupational status was self-reported by participants. ‘Housewife’ referred to women engaged in domestic responsibilities without paid employment, whereas ‘Unemployed’ referred to individuals without current employment who were available for or seeking work. Employment statistics were categorized separately for clarity.

In addition to sociodemographic and anthropometric data, participants were asked about their dietary habits using a structured questionnaire developed by the research team. Items included the number of daily meals, meal-skipping behavior, eating speed, food choice criteria (e.g., low-fat, low-salt, vitamin-rich, taste preference), salt preference, fast-food consumption and frequency, eating out frequency, and regularity of meal timing.

The participants’ body weight, height, waist circumference, hip circumference, and neck circumference were measured and assessed according to the technique [21, 22]. Their waist/hip and waist/height ratios were calculated [23]. Their body weight was measured with a Tanita BC 601 body analyzer, their height was measured with an Ade MZ10020 portable height meter, and their circumferences were measured using a non-flexible tape measure. Their Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared and categorized according to the World Health Organization criteria: underweight (< 18.5), normal range (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), and obese (≥ 30.0) [24].

Data collection measures

The SF-36 Quality of Life Scale (SF-36) developed by Ware (1992) was used. Koçyiğit adapted the SF-36 to the Turkish population in 1999 by conducting its validity study and in 2002, Dündar et al., showed that the scale was highly valid and reliable (with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient ranging 0.74–0.92 for the subscales). The scale includes items on the perception of change in health in the last four weeks and the perception of change in health in the last week [25, 26]. Norm values for the Turkish population were defined by Demiral et al., [27]. The SF-36 Health Survey comprises eight domains: physical functioning (limitations in physical activities), role-physical (limitations in usual role activities due to physical problems), bodily pain (intensity of pain and its interference with daily life), general health (perceived overall health status), vitality (energy and fatigue), social functioning (impact of health on social activities), role-emotional (limitations in usual role activities due to emotional problems), and mental health (general mental well-being including anxiety and depression). The subscale scores range from 0 to 100. There is no total score and the questions have positive statements. Higher scores indicate a better quality of life. The SF-36 Health Survey provides two composite summary scores: the Physical Component Summary (PCS), derived from physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, and general health subscales; and the Mental Component Summary (MCS), derived from vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health subscales. These summary scores were used as dependent variables in the regression analyses [28].

Statistical analyses

SPSS 22 statistical software was used to evaluate the data. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, number, and percentile) were given for categorical and continuous variables in the study. Moreover, homogeneity of variances was determined by “Levene”’s test. “Shapiro-Wilk” test was run for normality assumption. Since normal distribution was not provided, the differences between two groups were analyzed by Mann Whitney-U test and the comparisons of three or more groups were analyzed by Kruskal Wallis test. Bonferroni-Dunn test was used for multiple comparisons. The correlation between two continuous variables was tested with Spearman correlation coefficient and power relationship. The correlation coefficient was interpreted as 0.00-0.10 (very weak), 0.10–0.39 (weak), 0.40–0.69 (moderate), 0.70–0.89 (strong), and 0.90-1.00 (very strong) [29]. To identify independent predictors of health-related quality of life, multivariate linear regression analyses were performed with the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores of the SF-36 as dependent variables. Independent variables included age, gender, marital status, education level, body mass index (BMI), waist-to-hip ratio risk status, number of daily meals, and meal skipping behavior. All independent variables were entered simultaneously using the enter method. Standardized β coefficients, standard errors, p-values, and model fit indices (R², F-statistic) were reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance level was taken as p < 0.05.

Ethics

Written permission was obtained from Ahlat District Governorate to conduct this study. Approval was obtained from Bitlis Eren University Ethics Committee (decision dated 28.09.2021 and numbered 21/9–10). The participants were informed about the study and granted their written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Findings

Baseline characteristics of participants

Table 1 shows the distribution of the participants according to their sociodemographic characteristics. 51.7% of the participants were male and 48.3% were female. The mean age of the immigrants was 41.32 ± 15.47 years, 46.7% were over 40 years old, and 61.7% were married. 72.5% of them were primary school graduates. Before migration, 26.7% of the participants were engaged in animal husbandry and farming, and 30.8% were housewives. After migration, 24.2% were unemployed, the proportion of housewives remained the same (30.8%), while 15.0% were construction workers and 13.3% were security guards.

The mean scores were significantly higher in married people (80.60 ± 21.98) than single people (63.26 ± 37.39) on the physical functioning subscale of SF-36 according to marital status (p = 0.043). The mean scores were significantly higher in single people (56.52 ± 46.97) than in married people (81.41 ± 35.37) on the role physical subscale (p = 0.001). The mean scores were significantly higher in single people (57.17 ± 8.54) than in married people (53.64 ± 7.82) on the vitality subscale (p = 0.024).

The mean scores of the participants who graduated from high school and above (97.50 ± 4.18, 75.00 ± 8.36, 72.91 ± 14.61, respectively) were significantly higher than those who graduated from primary school (70.80 ± 30.40, 54.36 ± 19.15, 54.02 ± 17.72, respectively) on the subscales of Physical Functioning, Bodily Pain, and Social Functioning (p = 0.023, p = 0.009, p = 0.049, respectively).

The mean scores of the participants in the at-risk group for the waist/hip ratio on the vitality and mental health subscales (54.51 ± 8.11 and 59.07 ± 11.05, respectively) were significantly lower than those in the no-risk group (61.87 ± 7.53 and 69.00 ± 13.31, respectively) (p = 0.027, p = 0.032).

The number of daily meals was significantly associated with physical functioning, role-physical, vitality, and mental health subscales (p = 0.016, p = 0.020, p = 0.004, and p = 0.003, respectively). Participants consuming ≥ 4 meals per day had lower mean scores in these domains compared to those consuming fewer meals. No significant differences were observed for bodily pain, general health, social functioning, or role-emotional subscales (p > 0.05).

Meal-skipping behavior was not significantly associated with any of the SF-36 subscales (all p > 0.05).

Overall, higher SF-36 scores were observed among male, married, and more educated participants, as well as those without waist/hip ratio risk. In contrast, consuming four or more meals per day was associated with lower quality of life scores. These findings highlight the sociodemographic and lifestyle factors that may contribute to better physical and mental health outcomes.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide insight into the post-migration obesity status and health-related quality of life of Meskhetian Turks. Access to healthcare is a fundamental determinant in improving the quality of life of immigrants and preventing adverse health outcomes [30, 31].

In this study, a significant portion of participants reported that their lives in Turkey had improved compared to previous conditions; the most frequently cited reasons for this improvement were access to social security (44.0%) and healthcare services (25.2%). This highlights the importance of healthcare access in shaping the quality of life among immigrant populations, consistent with the literature emphasizing health service accessibility in migrant adaptation [15, 32].

This study showed that 60.8% of Meskhetian Turks who immigrated to Ahlat were overweight and 14.2% had obesity. According to national data, the prevalence of obesity among adults in Turkey was 20.2% in 2022 [6]. In a study of Meskhetian Turks in Erzincan, 34.8% were overweight and 22% had obesity [33], while among Meskhetian Turks in the United States the prevalence of obesity was even higher [34]. The incidence of obesity among Meskhetians living in Turkiye is lower than the rate in the United States. This difference may be associated with the poorly structured nature of social strata in developing countries as compared to developed countries, and therefore the failure to demonstrate the correlation between social strata and health. Secondly, social variables based on culture or behavior might play a more dominant role in developing countries than in developed countries. Thirdly, higher social strata in developing countries are wealthy enough to afford characteristic lifestyles typical of developed countries, such as high-fat diets and sedentary lifestyles. Relatively close obesity rates among Meskhetian Turks in Erzincan and Meskhetians living in Ahlat were found in this study.

Studies of other immigrant groups also report high prevalence of overweight and obesity, ranging from 25 to 42% [35–37]. Our findings that overweight was more common in women, whereas obesity was more prevalent in men, are similar to the results reported by Çolak et al. [33]. Likewise, this study revealed that the rate of overweight women was higher than that of men, and the rate of men with obesity was higher than that of women.

This study revealed that the prevalence of underweight and obesity was high among Meskhetian Turks settled in Ahlat. This makes this society vulnerable to potential emergencies and disasters. Medically fragile communities may be among the groups most affected by secondary disasters, in addition to their vulnerability to disasters. Because the incidence risk of many chronic diseases, primarily hypertension and heart disease, increases along with obesity [38]. Differences in cultural values may affect an individual’s behaviors, eating habits, and physical activity in the culture that hosts them, depending on which cultural values they adhere to. It could be asserted that the rate of overweight and people with obesity increased since in the region where Meskhetian Turks previously had lived, they had commonly engaged in agriculture and animal husbandry, but they could not engage in these activities in Ahlat. Also, it could be asserted that while the Meskhetian Turks were mostly fed meat in the Caucasus and led an active life in the countryside, they adopted a carbohydrate-based eating habit with a more monotonous life after migrating. This lifestyle leads to the development of overweight and obesity.

This study discussed the quality of life of Meskhetian Turks and the contributions to the understanding of the affecting factors by using the SF-36 Quality of Life Scale. Regarding quality of life, the highest mean score was observed in the physical functioning subscale (73.9 ± 29.9), while the lowest was in general health (52.5 ± 18.7). These scores were generally below the Turkish normative values reported by Demiral et al. [27], suggesting that migration and acculturation processes may negatively affect both physical and mental health [32, 39].

The mean scores of the Meskhetian Turks living in Ahlat on the quality-of-life scale appear to fall below the norm values of Turkish society. This difference may be due to the difficulties brought about by migration and acculturation, as mentioned in the literature.

Gender-based differences were also evident, with men reporting higher scores than women on several subscales. This finding is consistent with Turkish normative data [27] and highlights the multiple stressors faced by women, including biological factors, caregiving roles, and exposure to violence or marginalization, which may reduce their quality of life [40, 41].

Our study also showed that single participants had lower physical functioning scores compared to married ones, although this contrasts with some studies reporting higher scores among single women [42].

Importantly, meal frequency was associated with quality of life: participants consuming ≥ 4 meals per day had significantly lower scores in physical functioning, role-physical, vitality, and mental health. This aligns with studies indicating that irregular or excessive meal patterns can negatively affect HRQoL [43–45]. While the SF-36 is widely used, some authors note its limitations in capturing mental and social domains [46]. Future research could include obesity-specific instruments for a more nuanced assessment.

Additionally, although the SF-36 is widely used to assess quality of life in obesity, some authors note its limitations in specifically capturing mental and social domains [46]. Future research could evaluate whether incorporating obesity-specific instruments might reveal more nuanced differences. Altogether, these findings suggest that higher meal frequency (≥ 4 meals/day) may be a marker of disordered eating patterns that negatively affect both physical and mental dimensions of quality of life.

Age and BMI were also correlated with SF-36 outcomes. Older age was associated with lower scores in physical functioning, role-physical, role-emotional, and mental health, but higher scores in bodily pain and social functioning. These results are in line with trends reported in Turkish normative data [27]. This supports the results of this study except for the Mental Health, Bodily Pain, and Social Functioning subscales.

The participants’ BMI had a highly weak negative correlation with their scores on the Physical functioning and Mental health subscales and a highly weak positive correlation with their scores on the Vitality subscale. Accordingly, as the average BMI of Meskhetian Turks rises, their physical functioning and mental health levels lowered and their vitality level elevated.

In our model, the association between BMI and quality of life did not remain significant after adjustment, suggesting that age may be a stronger determinant of quality of life than BMI in this sample. The finding that age was the only independent predictor of PCS and MCS scores is consistent with previous research demonstrating that quality of life declines with advancing age due to both physical limitations and psychosocial stressors [27, 47].

In their study, Kolotkin et al., reported that the mean quality of life (especially in physical dimensions) dropped significantly as the average BMI increased [48]. Another study showed that as the average BMI increased, the mean score dropped in all subscales of the quality-of-life scale [49]. A study conducted in China reported that obesity weakened physical functioning and weakness negatively affected mental health [50]. An increase in body weight causes negative attitudes towards these individuals in society and reduces self-esteem [51]. The findings are compatible with the literature. Obesity one of the major health disorders related to diet impairs the quality of life. In particular, it can be asserted that it adversely affects physical and mental health.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the few that have investigated both obesity and quality of life in Meskhetian Turks residing in Turkey, utilizing a validated and culturally adapted instrument—the SF-36 Quality of Life Scale. This contributes meaningful data to the limited literature on health outcomes in this marginalized immigrant group. However, the study has several limitations that should be considered. Key factors known to influence obesity and quality of life—such as physical activity, dietary habits, chronic disease status, regular medication use, and sleep patterns—were not included in the analysis due to the lack of standardized and measurable data. The study was limited to Meskhetian Turks aged 18 and older who were settled in the Ahlat District of Bitlis Province, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader Meskhetian community or other immigrant populations. Moreover, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Another limitation of this study is that participants were recruited on a voluntary basis, which may have introduced selection bias, as those who chose to participate could differ systematically from those who did not.

Despite these limitations, the findings of this study provide valuable insights into the health status and quality of life of a vulnerable immigrant population. They also underscore the need for future research involving larger and more diverse samples, standardized assessment tools for lifestyle and health factors, and longitudinal designs to better understand how cultural transitions impact immigrants’ anthropometric characteristics and overall well-being over time.

Conclusion

Most of the Meskhetian Turks who participated in this study were overweight, and one out of ten individuals had obesity.The SF-36 mean scores fell below the norm values of Turkish society. Obesity and low quality of life among Meskhetian Turks in the Ahlat district make this society vulnerable to potential emergencies and disasters. Medically fragile communities may be among the groups most affected by secondary disasters, in addition to their vulnerability to disasters. Immigrants should be made aware of matters such as healthy eating and diet in case of disasters so that they can build a healthy society. Information about the risk factors and treatment of obesity should be provided. Training and programs should be organized to enhance the quality of life and risk factors affecting the quality of life.

Additionally, the number of daily meals was significantly associated with several domains of health-related quality of life, with participants consuming ≥ 4 meals per day reporting lower physical and mental health scores. These findings suggest that meal frequency may play a role in shaping both physical and psychological well-being. Future studies should incorporate more detailed dietary analyses to further elucidate these associations.

Furthermore, multivariate regression analyses revealed that age was the only independent predictor of both PCS and MCS scores, with older participants reporting lower physical and mental health-related quality of life. Other sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, including gender, education, BMI, and dietary habits, were not independently associated with PCS or MCS in this sample.

In addition to health education and awareness about obesity and nutrition, ensuring continuous and equitable access to healthcare services is essential for supporting the physical and mental well-being of immigrant populations.

Access to healthcare emerged as a notable factor contributing to improved life conditions among the participants. A considerable number of individuals stated that healthcare services were one of the main reasons for their positive perception of life in Turkey. This highlights the importance of strengthening healthcare access as part of broader efforts to improve the well-being of immigrant communities.

These results suggest that interventions to improve health-related quality of life among Meskhetian Turks should particularly focus on addressing the needs of older adults. Future research should also explore longitudinal and context-specific factors, such as acculturation, chronic disease burden, dietary habits, physical activity levels, mental health, and access to preventive health services, to better understand how migration-related stressors and structural barriers impact health outcomes over time.
