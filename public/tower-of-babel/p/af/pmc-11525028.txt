Targeting NAT10 inhibits osteosarcoma progression via ATF4/ASNS-mediated asparagine biosynthesis
Authors: Yutong Zou, Siyao Guo, Lili Wen, Dongming Lv, Jian Tu, Yan Liao, Weidong Chen, Ziyun Chen, Hongbo Li, Junkai Chen, Jingnan Shen, Xianbiao Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryDespite advances in treatment, the prognosis of patients with osteosarcoma remains unsatisfactory, and searching for potential targets is imperative. Here, we identify N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) as a candidate therapeutic target in osteosarcoma through functional screening. NAT10 overexpression is correlated with a poor prognosis, and NAT10 knockout inhibits osteosarcoma progression. Mechanistically, NAT10 enhances mRNA stability of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) through ac4C modification. ATF4 induces the transcription of asparagine synthetase (ASNS), which catalyzes asparagine (Asn) biosynthesis, facilitating osteosarcoma progression. Utilizing virtual screening, we identify paliperidone and AG-401 as potential NAT10 inhibitors, and both inhibitors are found to bind to NAT10 proteins. Inhibiting NAT10 suppresses osteosarcoma progression in vivo. Combined treatment using paliperidone and AG-401 produces synergistic inhibition for osteosarcoma in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Our findings demonstrate that NAT10 facilitates osteosarcoma progression through the ATF4/ASNS/Asn axis, and pharmacological inhibition of NAT10 may be a feasible therapeutic approach for osteosarcoma.Keywords: osteosarcoma, N4-acetylcytidine, NAT10, ATF4, ASNS, asparagine

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents and is characterized by high disability and mortality rates.1 The application of neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with surgical treatment significantly improves the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of patients with osteosarcoma to 60%–70%.2,3 However, the therapeutic effect for osteosarcoma has remained stagnant over the past 30 years. Approximately 35% of patients develop recurrence and metastasis during treatment, leading to an unfavorable prognosis.4,5,6,7,8,9 Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the molecular mechanisms driving osteosarcoma progression, identify therapeutic targets, and develop effective drugs.

Emerging evidence implies that multiple post-transcriptional modifications of RNA play a crucial role in cancer. RNA modifications include N1-methyladenosine, N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), 7-methylguanosine (m7G), N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), pseudouridine (ψ), etc. These RNA modifications are dynamically regulated by writers, erasers, and readers, influencing gene expression.10,11,12 It has been reported that dysregulation of RNA modifications and their corresponding regulators plays a crucial role in multiple cancers, including leukemia, gastric cancer, bladder cancer, lung cancers, etc. Dysregulation of m6A, m5C, and m7G modifications impact the self-renewal of tumor stem cells,13 proliferation,14 metabolic reprogramming,15 metastasis,16 and tumor angiogenesis,17 thereby influencing tumor progression. Clinically, RNA modification and its regulators have also been reported to be associated with clinical outcomes and could be potential targets in cancer therapy.18,19

In osteosarcoma, previous studies have demonstrated the essential role of RNA modification. The accumulation of m6A modification regulated by METTL14 plays a critical role in osteosarcoma, including resistance to therapy.20 Deposition of m5C modification mediated by NSUN2 promotes osteosarcoma progression through metabolic reprogramming.21 Dysregulation of m7G mediated by METTL1/WDR4 has been implicated in extracellular matrix organization and chemoresistance.22 These studies reveal that aberrant RNA modifications can influence osteosarcoma progression. However, systematic screening and validation of vital molecules involved in RNA modification have not been reported in osteosarcoma. Therefore, uncovering critical epigenomic molecules involved in osteosarcoma progression may serve as a basis for exploring potential therapeutic strategies for osteosarcoma treatment.

In this study, we combined RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and functional screening to search for a potential therapeutic target involved in RNA modification for osteosarcoma and identified NAT10 as a candidate target. NAT10 was upregulation in osteosarcoma samples, and this phenotype was associated with a poor prognosis. NAT10 promoted osteosarcoma cell growth in vitro and metastasis in cell line-derived xenograft models. By combining acetylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (acRIP-seq) with RNA-seq, we found that NAT10 regulated the ac4C modification of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) mRNA, enhancing its mRNA stability. Thus, ATF4 promoted the transcription of asparagine synthetase (ASNS), which catalyzes asparagine (Asn) biosynthesis. Upregulated ASNS provided Asn for protein and nucleotide synthesis, facilitating the proliferation and metastasis of osteosarcoma cells. In addition, the suppression of proliferation and metastasis induced by NAT10 knockout (NAT10-KO) could be attenuated by overexpression of ATF4 and ASNS or supplementation with Asn. Through structure-based virtual screening of Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs and small-molecule libraries, paliperidone and AG-401 were identified as potential inhibitors of NAT10. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) demonstrated that paliperidone and AG-401 could bind to the NAT10 protein. Both inhibitors effectively reduced ac4C modification levels of mRNA and decreased ATF4 and ASNS expression, leading to diminished Asn levels. Treatment with both inhibitors resulted in impaired cell proliferation and metastasis of osteosarcoma in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, both inhibitors and combined treatment suppressed osteosarcoma in patient-derived xenograft mouse model (PDX) mouse models and organoids. Our findings reveal that NAT10 facilitates osteosarcoma progression through ATF4/ASNS-mediated Asn biosynthesis, and targeting NAT10 with paliperidone and AG-401 holds promise as a potential therapeutic strategy for patients with osteosarcoma.

Results

NAT10 is a candidate therapeutic target for osteosarcoma and is associated with a poor prognosis in patients with osteosarcoma

To screen a potential therapeutic target for osteosarcoma among RNA modification regulators, we combined RNA-seq of tumor samples with RNA interference-mediated functional screening in osteosarcoma cells (Figure 1A). We collected osteosarcoma samples (N = 20) and adjacent normal tissues (N = 10) for RNA-seq to explore the role of RNA modification in osteosarcoma. We initially profiled the differentially expressed genes (Table S1), and well-documented oncogenes and tumor suppressors in osteosarcoma23 were shown in the plot (Figures 1B and 1C). By gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we found that the RNA modification (Gene Ontology, GO:0009451) gene set was significantly upregulated in osteosarcoma tissues compared with normal tissues (nominal p = 0.045, permutation test) (Figure 1D), and the top 40 ranked core enriched genes were listed (Figure 1E). By intersecting the core enriched genes in the RNA modification pathway with the up-regulated genes (log2 fold change >1 and adjusted p value <0.05), we identified a set of 28 genes (Figure 1F) (Table S2). Then, we performed in vitro RNAi functional screening using a cell proliferation and migration model with customized small interfering RNA (siRNA) (2 siRNAs for each gene and 4 nonspecific control siRNAs) (Figures 1G, 1H, 1I, and 1J). After knockdown of each candidate gene, we found that interfering NAT10 achieved the best inhibitory effect on cell proliferation and migration (Figure 1K) (Table S3).

We further analyzed the mRNA and protein levels of NAT10 in tumor samples by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) and immunoblotting. The results showed that the mRNA and protein levels of NAT10 were significantly increased in tumor samples and osteosarcoma cell lines (Figures 1L and 1M). The ac4C modification level of mRNA in tumor samples was detected through dot blot using monoclonal antibody against ac4C. Dot blot assay revealed a substantial increase in the level of ac4C modification in mRNA, which may be induced by NAT10 expression in tumor samples (Figure 1N). To reveal the association between NAT10 expression and clinical outcomes in patients with osteosarcoma, we examined NAT10 expression in osteosarcoma tissues by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of paraffin sections. IHC results demonstrated that 53% (n = 39) of patients exhibited strong or moderate staining for NAT10, while 47% (n = 35) showed weak or negative staining for NAT10 (Figure 1O). Kaplan-Meier analysis suggested patients with high NAT10 expression had worse OS (p < 0.001, log-rank test) and lung metastasis-free survival (LMFS) (p < 0.001, log-rank test) (Figure 1P). The NAT10 expression level was identified as an independent prognostic factor in patients with osteosarcoma by Cox univariate (hazard ratio [HR] 2.656 [1.490–4.736], p = 0.001, Wald test) and multivariate (HR 3.130 [1.673–5.857], p < 0.001, Wald test) regression analyses (Figure 1Q). These results indicated that NAT10 may be a promising therapeutic target for osteosarcoma and could serve as a prognostic marker in osteosarcoma.

NAT10 promotes osteosarcoma proliferation and metastasis in vitro and in vivo

We next generated NAT10-KO 143B and HOS cell lines by CRISPR-Cas9 using two single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting NAT10 and a nontargeted control sgRNA (Figure 2A). Dot blot assays revealed that NAT10-KO significantly reduced the level of ac4C modification in mRNA (Figure 2B). NAT10-KO impaired the proliferation (Figure 2C) and colony formation of osteosarcoma (Figure 2D). Moreover, depletion of NAT10 suppressed the migration and invasion of osteosarcoma cells (Figures 2E–2G). To illustrate the role of NAT10 in osteosarcoma growth and metastasis in vivo, we built tibial orthotopic xenograft osteosarcoma models in BALB/c nude mice and observed the tumor volume and lung metastasis. Depletion of NAT10 significantly decreased tumor volume and resulted in extended survival compared to the control group (Figures 2H and 2I). Furthermore, knockout of NAT10 decreased the number of lung metastatic nodules (Figures 2J and 2K). IHC assay indicated that tumors derived from the NAT10-KO cell line exhibited lower expression of NAT10 and Ki-67 (Figure 2L). Taken together, these results indicate that NAT10 plays a critical role in osteosarcoma growth and metastasis.

ATF4 is regulated by NAT10 through ac4C modification

We further performed RNA-seq and acRIP-seq with NAT10-KO and control 143B and HOS cells, respectively. RNA-seq revealed the differentially expressed genes in NAT10-KO compared to control cell lines (Figure 3A). acRIP-seq revealed that the NAT10-KO cell line exhibited a decrease in global mRNA ac4C levels (Figures 3B and 3C), and a cytidine (C)-rich sequence motif was characterized by four cytidines separated by two or three nucleotides (CXX or CXXX) (Figure 3D). We observed 9 genes with decreased ac4C modification and mRNA levels upon NAT10-KO (Figure 3E). Among these genes, ATF4 exhibited the highest correlation (r = 0.633, Pearson correlation test) with NAT10 in the RNA-seq analysis of patient osteosarcoma samples (Figure 3F; Table S4). Indeed, acRIP-seq analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the ac4C modification level of ATF4 mRNA (Figure 3G). As ATF4 was reported to be a transcription factor,24,25,26 we interrogated the TRRUST database27 to find the candidate downstream transcriptional targets of ATF4 (Table S5). GSEA demonstrated that the target gene of ATF4 was significantly downregulated in NAT10-KO cells (Figure 3H). Moreover, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) qPCR using NAT10 antibody demonstrated that NAT10 could bind to ATF4 transcripts (Figure S1A), and acetylated RNA immunoprecipitation qPCR showed a reduced ac4C modification level in ATF4 mRNA (Figure 3I). ATF4 mRNA and protein levels were decreased in the NAT10-KO cells (Figures 3J and 3K). A recent study demonstrated that ac4C modification of mRNA plays a regulatory role in modulating mRNA stability.28 The mRNA stability assays using actinomycin D demonstrated that NAT10-KO significantly decreased the half-life of ATF4 mRNA (Figure 3L), promoting its degradation. To further validate the cytidine acetylation in ATF4 mRNA, we used the chemical reduction method using sodium cyanoborohydride (NaCNBH3),29 which causes the incorporation of non-cognate deoxynucleotide triphosphates in acetylated cytidine sites upon reverse transcription, and the ac4C site can be detected via Sanger sequencing at single-nucleotide resolution (Figure 3M).

We found there were two distinct ac4C sites in ATF4 transcript, and misincorporation in ac4C site was exclusively observed in NaCNBH3-treated control group but not in mock-treated or NAT10-KO samples (Figure 3N), with a higher misincorporation rate (Figure 3O). To further validate the essential role of ac4C modification, we constructed dual-luciferase reporter using the wild-type (WT) and ac4C site mutant (MUT) ATF4 transcript sequence. We found that NAT10-KO significantly decreased the luciferase activity of the WT reporters but had no effect on the MUT reporter (Figure 3P).

To validate the physiological function of ac4C sites in ATF4 mRNA, we conducted point mutations using base editor in the ac4C site of ATF4.30 Sanger sequencing demonstrated the mutation (Figure 3Q). We found that the ac4C modification of ATF4 mRNA and ATF4 expression significantly decreased in MUT compared with WT control (Figures 3R and S1B). Further mRNA stability assays demonstrated that ac4C site mutation significantly decreased the half-life of ATF4 mRNA (Figure 3S). The ac4C site mutation in ATF4 impaired the proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion of osteosarcoma cells (Figures 3T–3V, S1C, and S1D). Results showed that ATF4 may mediate the malignant progression of osteosarcoma induced by NAT10. Then, we overexpressed ATF4 (ATF4-OE) in NAT10-KO cell lines (Figure S1E) and found a significant recovery of proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion in NAT10-KO cell lines (Figures S1F–S1I). These results demonstrated that NAT10 regulated ATF4 through ac4C modification and overexpression of ATF4 rescued proliferation and metastasis, which were impaired by NAT10-KO.

NAT10 regulates ASNS expression and Asn biosynthesis via ATF4 transcriptional regulation

Among the candidate target genes of ATF4, ASNS was found to be downregulated in both cell lines (Figure 4A). ASNS is the enzyme responsible for Asn biosynthesis (Figure 4B).31 RT-qPCR and immunoblotting showed that the mRNA and protein levels of ASNS were decreased in NAT10-KO group (Figures 4C and S2A). Furthermore, we performed ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) for targeted analysis of amino acids to identify the changes in Asn and aspartic acid (Asp). NAT10-KO induced a decrease in Asn levels, while the levels of its precursor Asp were elevated, also confirmed by ELISA (Figures 4D–4F).

To confirm the transcriptional regulation of ASNS by ATF4, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) with ATF4 antibodies in genomic DNA, combining RT-qPCR and PCR. The results showed that ATF4 could bind to the promoter of ASNS (Figures 4G and 4H). ASNS promoter sequences contained a putative C/EBP-ATF response element (CARE; counterparts of which in other genes are known to be bound by ATF432). In the dual-luciferase reporter assay, the full-length ASNS promoter and a CARE-deleted sequence were utilized to validate whether ATF4 binding to the ASNS promoter leads to transcriptional induction (Figure 4I). Knockdown of ATF4 (ATF4-KD) reduced the transcriptional activity of ASNS, and CARE deletion abolished ASNS promoter induction (Figure 4J). RT-qPCR and immunoblotting showed that ATF4-KD significantly decreased ASNS mRNA and protein levels (Figures 4K and S2B). The Asn level also decreased in ATF4-KD osteosarcoma cell lines (Figure 4L).

Furthermore, we found that knockout of NAT10 significantly diminished ASNS transcription (Figure S2C), which was abrogated by ATF4 overexpression (Figure 4M). Overexpression of ATF4 rescued the decreased protein levels of ASNS induced by NAT10-KO (Figure 4N). In addition, the decrease in Asn level induced by NAT10-KO was attenuated by overexpression of ATF4 (Figure 4O). Collectively, our findings indicate that NAT10 regulates ATF4-mediated ASNS transcription and Asn biosynthesis in osteosarcoma.

ATF4/ASNS promotes osteosarcoma progression through Asn-mediated protein and nucleic acid synthesis

We next explored the role of ATF4 and ASNS in osteosarcoma. ATF4 knockdown impaired the proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion of osteosarcoma cells (Figures 4P–4R, S2D, and S2E). Then, we knocked out ASNS using CRISPR-Cas9 system (Figure 4S). We further conducted a flux assay across ASNS using N15-labeled Asp (Figure 4T). ASNS knockout induced a significant decrease of N15-Asn derived from N15-Asp in ASNS-knockout cell lines (Figure 4U). Knockout of ASNS impaired the proliferation, colony formation, and migration (Figures S2F–S2H) ability of osteosarcoma cells. ASNS and its metabolic product Asn play crucial roles in protein and nucleotide synthesis.33 Global protein synthesis levels were reduced following ASNS knockout, as demonstrated by puromycin assay (Figure S2I). Phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) and adenosine, essential substrates for nucleotide synthesis, were both reduced (Figures S2J and S2K). Supplementation with additional Asn (0.1 mM) rescued osteosarcoma proliferation, colony formation, and migration (Figures S2L–S2N). Protein synthesis, PRPP, and adenosine levels were also rescued by supplementation with Asn (Figures S2O–S2Q). To explore the role of ASNS and Asn in mediating the function of ATF4 in osteosarcoma, we performed functional rescue assays. Overexpression of ASNS (ASNS-OE) (Figure S3A) or supplementation with Asn attenuated the impairment of proliferation, colony formation, and migration, in ATF4-KD cells (Figures S3B–S3D). Additionally, protein synthesis, PRPP, and adenosine levels were also restored by ASNS-OE or supplementation with Asn in ATF4-KD cells (Figures S3E–S3G). Collectively, these results demonstrated that ATF4/ASNS supports osteosarcoma progression through Asn biosynthesis, which participates in protein and nucleic acid synthesis.

NAT10 promotes osteosarcoma progression via ATF4/ASNS/Asn in vivo and in vitro

We further overexpressed exogenous ATF4 and ASNS in the NAT10-KO cell line to clarify whether NAT10 regulates osteosarcoma progression in an ATF4/ASNS/Asn-dependent manner (Figure 5A). Overexpression of ATF4 and ASNS or supplementation with Asn (0.1 mM) rescued the impaired proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion ability in NAT10-KO cell lines (Figures 5B–5E and S4A). The impaired global protein synthesis level was restored (Figure 5F). Moreover, Asn, PRPP, and adenosine levels were alleviated after overexpression of ATF4 or ASNS or supplementation with Asn (Figures S4B–S4D). Furthermore, we built tibial orthotopic osteosarcoma models with those treatments and observed the volumes and metastasis of tumors. Overexpression of ATF4, ASNS, or Asn supplementation34 significantly attenuated the impairment of tumor growth (Figure 5G) and resulted in shorter survival (Figure 5H). The number of lung metastatic nodules increased with those treatments (Figures 5I and 5J). IHC analysis indicated that overexpression of ATF4 or ASNS and Asn supplementation restored the expression of Ki-67 (Figures 5K and S4E). Overall, these results indicate that NAT10 promotes osteosarcoma growth and metastasis via the ATF4/ASNS/Asn axis.

Clinical correlation between NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS in patients with osteosarcoma

Based on the regulatory mechanism, we examined the mRNA and protein levels of NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS and found a correlation between NAT10 and ATF4 (mRNA level: R2 = 0.361, p = 0.005, Pearson correlation; protein level: R2 = 0.504, p < 0.001, Pearson correlation) as well as ASNS (mRNA level: R2 = 0.261, p = 0.022, Pearson correlation; protein level: R2 = 0.476, p < 0.001, Pearson correlation) in fresh osteosarcoma tissues (Figures S4F and S4G). Next, IHC staining was performed in paraffin osteosarcoma samples, and we found that NAT10 expression significantly correlated with both ATF4 and ASNS in patients with osteosarcoma (Figure S4H). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis suggested that the patients with triple-positive markers had shorter OS (p < 0.001, log-rank test) and LMFS (p < 0.001, log-rank test) times than the triple-positive patients (Figure 5L).

Moreover, in the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, the combined index of three markers (NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS) exhibited a higher area under the curve (AUC) value for OS (p = 0.031, Venkatraman method test) and LMFS (p = 0.039, Venkatraman method tests) than NAT10 as a single marker (Figure 5M). Collectively, these results indicated that there is a close correlation between NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS expression in osteosarcoma patient specimens, and an IHC panel combining NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS can be used to predict patient prognosis.

Structure-based virtual screen identified paliperidone and AG-401 as potential inhibitors of NAT10

To identify small molecules or approved drugs with the potential to disrupt NAT10 function, we conducted a structure-based virtual screen of the Specs chemical library and FDA-approved drugs in DrugBank35 (Figure 6A). The hit compounds were chosen according to binding energy and were included for further analysis (Figure 6B; Table S6). The top-ranked 20 compounds in the two libraries were screened via the ac4C dot blot assay for their potential inhibition of NAT10, a consequence of the ac4C modification level in 143B cells. Paliperidone and AG-401 showed the best inhibitory ability for NAT10 (Figures 6C and S5A). To evaluate the engagement of paliperidone and AG-401 in a cellular context, we performed standard cellular thermal shift assays36 in the 143B cell line. Both compounds exhibited the ability to decrease NAT10 degradation (Figure S5B).

To further validate whether paliperidone and AG-401 could bind to NAT10, we conducted the ITC assay. We conducted the protein expression and purification of NAT10 (Figure S5C). Then, we assessed whether paliperidone and AG-401 could inhibit osteosarcoma cell growth. Cell proliferation assays showed that both paliperidone (143B IC50 = 10.81 μM, HOS IC50 = 7.73 μM) and AG-401 (143B IC50 = 26.80 μM, HOS IC50 = 22.03 μM) suppressed tumor cell growth (Figure S5D). In addition, we found that the documented NAT10 inhibitor Remodelin37 could also inhibit osteosarcoma cell growth. However, the IC50 of Remodelin (143B IC50 = 50.17 μM, HOS IC50 = 45.61 μM) was higher than that of paliperidone and AG-401 (Figure S5D). Furthermore, dot blot assays showed that all compounds suppressed ac4C modification in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6E). Functional assays showed that paliperidone and AG-401 suppressed cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion (Figures 6F–6I and S5E–S5H).

Based on the NAT10/ac4C/ATF4/ASNS regulatory mechanism, we further examined ATF4 and ASNS expression by immunoblotting and found a decrease in ATF4 and ASNS protein levels, but the expression of NAT10 was not changed after treatment (Figure 6J). Moreover, the Asn level was also reduced after paliperidone and AG-401 treatments (Figure 6K). We next explored whether paliperidone and AG-401 treatment could inhibit osteosarcoma progression in vivo. Paliperidone and AG-401 treatment significantly impaired tumor growth (Figure 6L), prolonged the survival rate (Figure 6M), and reduced lung metastasis (Figures 6N and 6O). IHC staining showed that both drug treatments decreased the expression of ATF4, ASNS, and Ki-67 (Figure 6P; Figure S5I). These results indicated that paliperidone and AG-401 from the virtual screen could suppress osteosarcoma progression via the NAT10/ac4C/ATF4/ASNS/Asn axis.

Paliperidone and AG-401 inhibited organoid initiation and osteosarcoma progression in PDX

To further explore the therapeutic capacity of paliperidone and AG-401 and assess the efficacy of the combination of the two inhibitors in osteosarcoma, we performed the drug synergism assay and established the organoid and PDX model (Figure 7A). We assess the drug synergism in osteosarcoma cell lines to explore the proper concentration for combination of the two inhibitors. Combination treatment induced cytotoxic responses at a lower concentration for each single agent (Figure 7B). The Bliss scores were used to assess the synergistic effects of inhibitors. Paliperidone and AG-401 elicited strong synergistic effects on osteosarcoma cell growth across a range of concentrations, and the combination of 10 μM AG-401 and 5 μM paliperidone demonstrated the highest Bliss score (Figure 7C). Then, we conduct the combined treatment in our organoid and PDX models. Combined treatment of paliperidone and AG-401 demonstrated a significant inhibitory activity on organoid growth and formation (Figures 7D–7F and S6A–S6C). Furthermore, the PDX models were used to assess the efficacy of the combined treatment. Combination of paliperidone and AG-401 offered a favorable antitumor effect with slower tumor growth than single-agent treatment (Figures 7G and S6D), which resulted in extended survival of mice (Figures 7H and S6E), and there were no significant changes in body weight of combined treatment group (Figures 7I and S6F). Consistent with the regulatory mechanism, drug treatment inhibited the expression of ATF4, ASNS, and Ki-67 (Figures 7J and S6G). Collectively, these results demonstrated that paliperidone and AG-401 could suppress osteosarcoma progression in PDX models, and combination treatment of both inhibitors held therapeutic potential for osteosarcoma.

Discussion

Accumulating evidence indicates that RNA modification is dysregulated in multiple cancer, enabling tumor progression. The ac4C modification is the acetylation of exocyclic amino hydrogens in cytidine and is considered a highly conserved RNA modification.38,39 ac4C modification is catalyzed by the NAT10 enzyme. NAT10 is an essential ATP-dependent RNA acetyltransferase containing N-acetyltransferase, ATPase, and helicase domains and is currently considered the writer of ac4C modification.37 Recently, it has been reported that dysregulation of ac4C modification and NAT10 plays a crucial role in multiple cancers, including myeloma, bladder cancer, colon cancer, etc. Dysregulation of ac4C modification and NAT10 impacts the proliferation and apoptosis of myeloma cells, thereby influencing tumor progression.40 Xie et al. reported that NAT10 regulated the chemoresistance of bladder cancer through ac4C modification.41 In colon cancer, NAT10 and ac4C modification were reported to regulate the proliferation and metastasis of tumor cells, facilitating malignant progression.42 In osteosarcoma, many studies focusing on RNA modification have demonstrated the dysregulation of m6A, m5C, and m7G,21,22,43 while systematic screening and validation of vital molecules in RNA modifications are lacking. In this study, we combined RNA-seq with functional screening to uncover the critical epigenomic molecules involved in osteosarcoma progression. We identified NAT10 as a candidate therapeutic target for osteosarcoma and revealed that NAT10 plays a critical role in osteosarcoma growth and metastasis.

RNA modification has been reported to influence mRNA capping, splicing, export, stability, translation, and many other processes, which play an essential role in tumors.44,45 Recently, ac4C modification was observed on mRNA and was found to enhance mRNA stability.28,46 In colorectal cancer, NAT10 promotes the ac4C modification of KIF23 mRNA, which enhances mRNA stability and inhibits the apoptosis of tumor cells.42 In bladder cancer, upregulated NAT10 increases the ac4C modification level on AHNAK mRNA, which suppresses its mRNA decay and further results in chemoresistance.41 Zong et al. reported that NAT10 facilitates the ac4C modification of AXL mRNA, enhancing AXL mRNA stability and promoting the proliferation and metastasis of tumor cells.47 In gastric cancer, NAT10 was reported to regulate the stability of SEPT9 mRNA via ac4C modification.48 In the present study, we detected the presence of ac4C modifications in mRNA using acRIP-seq, and ac4C modification was found to be located in the coding sequence of ATF4 mRNA. We revealed that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification increased ATF4 mRNA stability.

ATF4 is one of the key regulators in the cellular stress response that is frequently upregulated in cancers, promoting cellular adaptation to limited nutrient circumstances.49 Studies have demonstrated that ASNS is a vital transcriptional downstream target gene of ATF4.50 ASNS serves as the key enzyme in Asn biosynthesis, converting aspartate to Asn in an ATP-dependent reaction.51 Asn has been reported to impact protein and nucleotide synthesis, regulating proliferation, apoptosis, ammonia metabolism, and many other vital biological processes.33,52,53 ATF4/ASNS-mediated Asn biosynthesis regulatory mechanisms have been reported to play important roles across multiple cancers.24,50,51,54 In melanoma and pancreatic cancer, ATF4-mediated ASNS transcription impedes growth suppression in response to Asn limitation, and inhibition of ATF4, which attenuates ASNS transcription, sensitizes tumor cells to Asn restriction.50 Gwinn et al. reported that through ATF4-mediated ASNS transcription, KRAS alters amino acid metabolism. ATF4/ASNS-mediated Asn biosynthesis contributes to apoptotic suppression, protein biosynthesis, and cell proliferation in non-small cell lung cancer.26 In our study, we found that ATF4 facilitated the transcription of ASNS. ASNS promoted Asn biosynthesis, providing abundant amino acids for protein and nucleotide synthesis in osteosarcoma cells, and facilitated tumor proliferation and metastasis. Here, we proposed that NAT10 facilitated osteosarcoma malignant progression via the ATF4/ASNS/Asn regulatory axis, and our research connected NAT10 and ac4C modification with amino acid metabolism. In addition, according to the NAT10/ATF4/ASNS regulatory axis, we confirmed the clinical correlation between NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS. We found that the combination of NAT10/ATF4/ASNS was an independent prognostic factor in patients with osteosarcoma and that triple-positive status was associated with a poorer prognosis in patients with osteosarcoma. Moreover, ROC analysis demonstrated that the combined index of NAT10, ATF4, and ASNS exhibited a higher predictive value for OS and LMFS than the individual marker NAT10.

Given that NAT10 may be a potential therapeutic target for osteosarcoma treatment, further exploration of inhibitors would be of great significance for the clinic. In our study, we predicted the NAT10 protein structure using AlphaFold55,56 and selected the ATP binding pocket as the targeting site for virtual screening by molecular docking. We demonstrated that FDA-approved paliperidone and the small molecule AG-401 could effectively suppress the progression of osteosarcoma in vitro and in vivo. Early reports have identified Remodelin as a NAT10 inhibitor.37,57 Our findings demonstrated that osteosarcoma cells were more sensitive to paliperidone and AG-401 than to Remodelin, as the IC50 values of paliperidone and AG-401 were much lower than that of Remodelin. Paliperidone is a second-generation antipsychotic featuring D2 antagonism and serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism.58,59 AG-401 is a compound from the Specs chemical library with the full ID: AG-401/43237643. Paliperidone and AG-401 treatment inhibited the NAT10/ATF4/ASNS regulatory axis. Furthermore, using organoid and PDX models derived from patients with osteosarcoma, both inhibitors showed favorable therapeutic activity for osteosarcoma. Our findings in this study offer translational significance for paliperidone and AG-401 in patients with osteosarcoma. Clinically, there is a consensus that a drug dose of 100 mg/kg daily in mice is equal to a dose of 8.13 mg/kg daily in humans.60 Accordingly, the doses of paliperidone used in this study were within the clinical drug treatment window. The combined therapy may improve treatment response rates and durability while reducing toxicity.61,62 Therefore, the combination of different drugs focusing on the same target may have additional therapeutic potential. However, the clinical efficacy of paliperidone and AG-401 in osteosarcoma requires further evaluation in future clinical trials. Collectively, we demonstrated that NAT10 supports osteosarcoma progression both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, NAT10 stabilizes ATF4 mRNA and inhibits ATF4 mRNA degradation by ac4C modification. ATF4, in turn, further promotes the transcription of ASNS, facilitating Asn biosynthesis. Adequate Asn supports protein and nucleotide synthesis in tumor cells and facilitates osteosarcoma progression. These findings provide insights into the role of NAT10 and ac4C modification in osteosarcoma, implying that amino acid metabolism is closely linked to the dysregulation of epigenetic modifications. In addition, we identified paliperidone and AG-401 as potential inhibitors of NAT10 and proposed that pharmacological inhibition of NAT10 may be a feasible therapeutic approach for patients with osteosarcoma.

Limitations of the study

This study uncovered the essential roles of NAT10 and ac4C modification in osteosarcoma and identified NAT10 inhibitors through virtual screening. We reported the upregulation of NAT10 in osteosarcoma; however, the underlying mechanisms of NAT10 overexpression remain to be fully elucidated. Paliperidone and AG-401 have demonstrated promising inhibitory efficiency against NAT10 and osteosarcoma progression. However, further exploration is required to elucidate the detailed molecular and structural mechanisms of both inhibitors in targeting NAT10. In addition, the clinical efficacy and safety of NAT10 inhibitors should be evaluated in further research and clinical trials.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human samples

Osteosarcoma and adjacent normal tissues used in this study were collected from the Department of Musculoskeletal Oncology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. All tissue samples were obtained with informed or broad consent. Study was approved by the Research Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The clinical information and application of patients and samples was listed in Table S7.

Cell lines

The cell lines used in this study, including hFOB 1.19, G-292, U2OS, Zos, Zos-M, SaOS, HOS, 143B, SJSA-1 and HEK293T, were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The hFOB 1.19 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 300 mg/mL neomycin (G418, Gibco). in an incubator at 34°C and 5% CO2. Other cells were cultured with high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. The hFOB 1.19, G-292, U2OS, Zos, Zos-M, SaOS, and SJSA-1 were used for immunoblotting. HOS, 143B and HEK293T were used for stable cell lines construction and functional experiments. All these cells were certified and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Organoid culture

Osteosarcoma organoids were established as described.64 Part of tumor samples from patients were isolated and was minced into small pieces (0.3–0.5 mm3). The minced tissues were digested for 0.5 h using digestion medium containing Collagenase II (500 units/mL) Collagenase IV (200 μg/mL) and Hyaluronidase (0.1 mg/mL) at 37°C with continuous shaking. After digestion, the cells were treated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer on ice for 3 min. Cells were then filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. After centrifugation, cell pellet was embedded in Matrigel (Corning, 356231) in a 24- or 48-well plate, allowed to solidify for 30 min, and then overlaid with the medium organoid full culture medium [DMEM/F12 supplemented with HEPES (1 mM, Invitrogen), Glutamax (1x, Invitrogen), Nicotinamide (10 mM, Sigma), N-Acetylcysteine (1 mM, Sigma), A83-01 (0.5 μM, Tocris), B-27 without vitamin A (1x, Invitrogen), EGF (50 ng/mL, Peprotech), Gastrin (10 nM, Sigma), Noggin (100 ng/mL, Peprotech), RSPO1 (500 ng/mL, Peprotech), SB-202190 (10 μM, Sigma)]. After 24h culturing, the organoids were treated with Paliperidone and AG-401 as indicated. Organoids viability was measured using CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega, #G7572).

Animal studies

BALB/c nude mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University and housed in specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facilities. Orthotopic xenograft osteosarcoma models were established by injecting 1×106 indicated cells suspended in 20 μL PBS into the proximal tibia through the anterior tubercle cortex. For supplement of Asn, mice were administered with Asn (0.25 mmol/kg every two days, i.p.).34 For inhibitor studies, mice were randomly assigned into different treatment groups and were administered with paliperidone (3 mg/kg daily, i.p.)65 and AG-401 (5 mg/kg daily),66,67 respectively. The length and width of tumor were monitored every 3 days. Following sacrifice, tumors samples were harvested, weighed and fixed in formalin for further experiment. Tumor size was measured and calculated using the formula: width2 × length × π/6, as described.68 The researchers were unaware of the group allocation during the experiments and outcome assessment. PDX models were established as described.69 Fresh osteosarcoma samples were subcutaneously transplanted into the upper back of 4-week-old NCG (NOD-Prkdcem26Il2rgem26/Nju) mice with anesthesia. Xenografts appeared at the graft site in 1 month after transplantation. Then the tumor-bearing NCG mice were sacrificed and the tumor were subsequently transplanted to nude mouse. After 1 week, mice without tumor growth were excluded, and the remaining mice were randomly divided into control and treatment groups, ensuring equal initial tumor size distribution. Tumor size was measured every 3 days. Following sacrifice, tumors were removed and fixed in formalin for further experiments. Animal studies were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Sun Yat-Sen University and were conducted according to established guidelines for the Use and Care of Laboratory Animals.

Method details

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and was next reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, R111). Real-time reverse transcription fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using the Real-time Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Kit (Vazyme, Q321) and Fast Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). ACTB was used as endogenous control. The comparative Ct(2−ΔΔCT) method was applied to calculate the relative expression. The primers used are listed in Table S8.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from the indicated samples and was detected using Agilent 2100. mRNA enrichment was carried out using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The RNA-seq library was prepared by fragmenting the RNAs, synthesizing cDNA fragments, repairing double-stranded cDNA ends, and ligating adaptors. The final library was obtained through PCR amplification. Following quality control, the library was sequenced using the BGISEQ-500 platform. GSEA was performed using GSEA desktop application.70 Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2.63 Gene expression data of patient samples were listed in Table S9.

Functional screening

Osteosarcoma cell 143B stably expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) were generated and validated for functional screening. For each targeted gene, two independent target sequences were employed, listed in Table S10. Transfection of GFP+ 143B cells with siRNA was conducted following established methods at a final concentration of 20 nM for transcriptional.71 Following 48h of cell culture, the plates were photographed for proliferation assay and the Transwell chambers were photographed after removing upper remaining cells for migration assay. The GFP+ area of each siRNA was calculated and further compared with the control group.

Total protein extraction and western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA reagent (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, USA). Total proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE using a 10% polyacrylamide gel (EpiZyme, China) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was then blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After that, the membrane was incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The visualization of target protein bands was achieved using the High-sig ECL substrate (Tanon, China). The antibodies for western blot assay were provided in Table S11.

Histologic evaluation and immunohistochemical staining

For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the tissues were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5μm. The section was deparaffinized and stained using the H&E staining kit (Solarbio, G1120-3). For IHC staining, sections were deparaffinized and treated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min. Antigen retrieval procedure was conducted with 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Next, tissue sections were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody. Slides were then incubated with secondary antibody and visualized using3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) detection. The levels of IHC staining were evaluated by two independent pathologists and immunostaining degree was quantified according to immunoreactive score (IRS) systems. The cut-off value for defining high or low expression of the indicated protein in IHC staining was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, where the sum of sensitivity and specificity was maximized, as previously described.68 Samples with scores above the obtained cut-off value were considered to have high expression. The antibodies for IHC were provided in Table S11.

Dot blot

Total RNA was extracted from cells, and then mRNA was enriched with Poly(A)Purist MAG Kit (Invitrogen, AM1922) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, equal amounts of mRNAs were denatured at 75°C for 5 min and immediately placed on ice for 1 min. The mRNA samples were loaded onto an Amersham Hybond-N+ membrane (GE Healthcare, RPN203B) using Bio-Dot (Bio-Rad). The membranes were further UV cross-linked twice with 2000 mJ for 25-50s. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk, and incubated with an anti-ac4C antibody (ab252215, Abcam) overnight at 4°C. The HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was then added to the blots at room temperature for 1 h. Membrane was detected with Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon 5200 SF, China). The same mRNA samples were loaded on the membranes, which were then stained with 0.2% methylene blue in 0.3M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) for 30 min and washed with nuclease-free water for an hour.

Chemical reduction assay of ac4C sites

The chemical reduction of ac4C was conducted as described.29 In short, the enriched ploy(A) RNA of indicated group was treated with either NaCNBH3 or mock (buffer) for 20 min at 20°C. Then reactions were stopped and treated-RNA was precipitated using ethanol, following RNA extraction. The RNA was used for reverse transcription and PCR. PCR products was separated with agarose gel and desired bands was extracted for Sanger sequencing. Percentage misincorporation was calculated.29

Plasmid construction and lentiviral transduction

The sgRNA were inserted into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector (#52961, Addgene) to generate the knockout plasmids. For knockout, indicated knockout plasmids was co-transfected with the 3rd generation packaging plasmids (#12251, #12253 and #12259, Addgene) into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Supernatants were collected 48 h after transfection and were used to infect osteosarcoma cells. Following puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) for 7 days, the NAT10 protein level was assessed using western blot analysis. For knockdown of ATF4, small hairpin RNAs targeting ATF4 annealed into the pLKO.1-puro vector (#8453, Addgene) to construct the knockdown plasmids. For overexpression of ATF4 and ASNS, the wild-type coding sequence of ATF4 and ASNS were obtained from the NCBI and cloned into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1 vector plasmids obtained from Sun Yat-sen University. Base editing was performed as described,72,73 cells were transfected with 1 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) using base-editor plasmid (#112099, #139998, Addgene) and guide RNA expression plasmids. Cell clones were obtained by limited dilution and Sanger sequencing was used to detect the expected mutation. The sgRNA and shRNA target sequences are listed in Table S10.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

Osteosarcoma cells were seeded at a density of 1500 cells per well in 96-well microplates for the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CK04, DOJINDO) assay. Cell viability was measured daily for four days following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the colony formation assay, cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in 6-well plates. After a 2-week incubation, the cell colonies were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The cell colonies were then counted.

Migration and invasion assay

For migration assays, uncoated upper cell culture insert chambers (Corning Falcon, USA) were used, while for invasion assays, chambers were precoated with Matrigel (BioCoat, USA). Cells were seeded in the upper chambers with 200 μL FBS-free medium. The lower chambers were filled with 600 μL of medium containing 10% FBS. After a 24-h incubation, the cells that invaded the lower chambers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. The cells in the upper chambers were removed, and the cells in the lower chambers were observed under a microscope.

3D spheroid cell invasion assay

The 3D Spheroid cell invasion assay was performed as described by the manufacturer’s instructions (3500-096-K, R&D systems). Briefly, Cells were harvested and resuspended in 1× spheroid formation ECM. Each well of a 3D culture qualified 96-well spheroid-forming plate received 1×103 cells in 50 μL of suspension. The Plate was centrifuged at 200×g for 3 min at room temperature, followed by incubation at 37°C for 24 h to promote spheroid formation. Then, 50 μL invasion matrix was added into each well and plate was centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at 4°C to remove air bubbles and position the spheroids within the invasion matrix at the center of the wells. After incubating the plate at 37°C for 1 h to promote gel formation, 100 μL of cell culture medium was added to each well. The plate was then incubated at 37°C in the for 7 days, and then the spheres and invasion were photographed for each well.

acRIP-seq and RIP-qPCR

For acRIP-seq, RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) as mentioned above and mRNA was enriched with Poly(A)Purist MAG Kit (Invitrogen, AM1922) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. acRIP was conducted following a previously published protocol.28 Briefly, mRNA was fragmented using NEBNext Magnesium RNA Fragmentation buffer (E6150S, NEB) for 5 min at 94°C. Next, fragment mRNA was incubated at 4°C for 6 h with 5μg anti-ac4C antibody (ab252215, Abcam) coupled Protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen). Then, RNA was extracted using phenol/chloroform extraction method. The library was constructed using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. For RIP-qPCR, RNA obtained from RIPs was subjected to reverse transcription and qPCR analysis, as mentioned above, to detect mRNAs of target genes, and primers were listed in Table S8.

mRNA stability assay

Cells were treated with actinomycin D (MCE) for 0 h, 3 h, 6 h and 12 h, followed by RNA extraction as described above. The half-life of ATF4 mRNA was analyzed by RT-qPCR as previously described.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

ASNS promoter sequences was obtained from JASPAR74 and Cistrome75 database, which was amplified from human genomic DNA with PCR. A putative ATF4 binding site was deleted from the full length ASNS promoter using PCR-based mutagenesis. Primers were listed in Table S8. The full ASNS and mutant ASNS promoter (ΔCARE) was further inserted into dual-luciferase vector pEZX-FR01 (GeneCopoeia). For ac4C mutation dual-luciferase assay, indicated sequence was cloned into pGL3 basic vector (Promega). Osteosarcoma cells were transfected with the reporter plasmids. Reporter activity was assayed 24 h after transfection using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ChIP analysis

The ChIP assays were performed using Pierce Magnetic ChIP kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti-ATF4 antibody (#11815, Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were cross-linking with formaldehyde and digested with micrococcal nuclease. Chromatin (4×106 cells) and 2.5 μL of ATF4 antibody were used for ChIP assays. Isotype IgG was used as a negative control. Immunoprecipitated DNA was amplified by PCR and separated by DNA gel electrophoresis. qPCR analysis of immunoprecipitated DNA was mentioned above.

UHPLC-MS/MS and flux analysis

A total of 1×107 cells were used for UHPLC-MS/MS assay. Each cell sample that was mixed with 1mL extract solvent (acetonitrile-methanol-water, 2:2:1, containing isotopically-labelled internal standard mixture, precooled at −20°C). Then, samples were pre-cooled in dry ice, freeze-thawed three times in liquid nitrogen, vortexed for 30 s and sonicated in an ice-water bath for 15 min, then incubated for 1 h at −40°C and centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm, 4°C. Supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis. The UHPLC separation was carried out using 1290 Infinity II series UHPLC System (Agilent) and tandem mass spectrometry carried out using 6460 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (Agilent). Agilent MassHunter Work Station Software (B.08.00, Agilent Technologies) was employed for MRM data acquisition and processing. For flux assay, indicated cells were cultured in medium containing N15 labeled Asp (4mM) for 48h. The metabolites were then extracted from cells using acetonitrile for further detected.

Puromycin assay

For puromycin assay, cells were treated with puromycin at a final concentration of 1 μM for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were harvested for protein extraction. 15 μg total protein were loaded for SDS-PAGE. The following procedure was performed as Western blot analysis mentioned above, and the anti-Puromycin antibody (MABE342, Merck Millipore) was employed to detect the signal.

Virtual screening of potential inhibitors

A structure-based VS docking study was carried out using Autodock Vina program.76 The 3D structure of the protein NAT10 was downloaded from the AlphaFold protein structure database. Docking is performed to obtain a population of possible conformations and orientations of the ligand at the binding site. All bonds of ligands were set as rotatable. All calculations for protein-fixed ligand-flexible docking were done using the Lamarckian Genetic algorithm (LGA) method. The positions of the thermal residues in NAT10 (THR117, LYS256, GLN260, ARG286, LYS290, PHE325, ASP388, GLU389, THR412 and LEU465) were identified as binding sites. The best conformations were identified by binding energy and top 20 compounds were selected for further validation.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay

Protein expression was conducted using TNT Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation Systems (L1170, Promega). In short, the NAT10 full length with His tag sequence was cloned into T7 promoter vector and protein expression was conducted without label according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein was then purified using Ni-NTA Agarose (30210, QIAGEN) according to instructions. The ITC experiments were carried out at 25°C on Micro CalPEAQ-ITC system (Malvern MicroCal). The purified protein was loaded into cell and the inhibitor into a syringe. ITC titration was conducted with 19 injections, and 120 s between each injection. Reference cell power was set to 10 μcal/s. Data was analyzed using MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software and KD was calculated.

IC50 measurements

For IC50 measurements, cell lines were treated with vehicle or a range of compounds concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 and 640 μM) for 72 h. Cell viability assays for inhibitors treatment were performed using CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, #G7572) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Dose–response curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (v8) with a log (inhibitor) versus response fitting.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software or R. All data were presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. Statistical details of experiments can be found in figure legends and text. Statistical significance was determined by p < 0.05. In all cases, the p values are represented as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Data was considered as not statistically significant (ns) when p > 0.05.
