Right Atrial Myxoma Presenting as Recurrent Ventricular Tachycardia
Authors: Vasavdatta Sharma, Jaideep Dey, Chayan Vermani, Siddharth Agrawal, Pallavi Punhani, Nikhil Singh, Anil Handoo, Subhash Chandra, Ramji Mehrotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyxomas are the most frequent primary cardiac tumors, with right atrial origin relatively uncommon. Clinical presentation varies with tumor size, mobility, and location.Case SummaryA 34-year-old male patient presented with recurring ventricular tachycardia. Echocardiography demonstrated a large stalked right atrial mass causing right-sided chamber dilatation and severe tricuspid regurgitation. Computed tomography confirmed a calcified right atrial mass. Surgical excision of the lesion along with tricuspid valve annuloplasty was subsequently performed. Histopathology confirmed atrial myxoma with calcification and osseous metaplasia. Postoperative course was uneventful with no arrhythmic complications.DiscussionRight atrial myxomas may present unusually with ventricular arrhythmias. Calcification and osseous metaplasia are uncommon findings in cardiac myxomas. Early surgical resection remains the definitive treatment and confers excellent outcomes.Take-Home MessagesRight atrial myxoma can rarely present with recurrent ventricular tachycardia alongside structural and mechanical complications. Multimodality imaging-guided surgical excision is curative and prevents life-threatening complications.Key Words: cardioversion, echocardiography, imaging, right ventricle, tricuspid valve, valve repair, ventricular tachycardia

Cardiac myxomas (CM) are the most common benign tumors of the heart in adults. Around 75% originate in the left atrium and 20% in the right atrium (RA), with the rest arising at other sites.1 CM may present with a myriad of clinical presentations based on location, morphology, and tendency to produce systemic symptoms. Patients can be asymptomatic and may be incidentally diagnosed during cardiac imaging. Most symptomatic patients present with obstructive or embolic symptoms.2 Arrhythmic presentations of CM are mostly with atrial fibrillation.3 Ventricular arrhythmia (VA) as a presentation of atrial myxoma is uncommon and warrants comprehensive assessment along with urgent surgical intervention to prevent morbidity and mortality.4,5

History of Presentation

A 34-year-old man with no previous cardiac ailments presented to the hospital with a 1-day history of sudden-onset nonexertional palpitations and nausea. There was no history of chest pain, giddiness, or syncope. He had presented to another health care facility previously, where he was diagnosed with ventricular tachycardia (VT) and was electrically cardioverted. However, his palpitations recurred within half an hour and persisted till current admission.

At presentation, the patient was conscious, alert, and afebrile, with a heart rate of 180 beats/min, blood pressure 90/60 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 96% on room air. The electrocardiogram showed a wide complex tachycardia suggestive of VT with site of origin from the right superior free wall of the right ventricular outflow tract (Figure 1A). He was electrically cardioverted, and sinus rhythm was achieved. However, he developed yet another episode of VT within 1 hour, which required further electrical therapy and antiarrhythmics, with achievement of normal sinus rhythm (Figure 1B).

Twelve-Lead ECG of the Patient

ECG = electrocardiogram.

Past Medical History

The patient was hypertensive since the last 2 years for which he was taking tablet telmisartan 40 mg once daily. No previous febrile illness or viral prodrome was noted in the recent past. He had no history of substance abuse or right heart instrumentation. There was no significant family history of cardiovascular disease.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a polymorphic echogenic stalked mass in the RA (66 × 61 mm) with attachment to the RA free wall, 28 mm superior to the lateral tricuspid annulus (Figures 2A and 2B, Video 1). The mass had dense superficial calcification. The RA, right ventricle (RV), and tricuspid valve (TV) annulus were significantly dilated (Figure 2C). Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) was noted with noncoaptation of TV leaflets (Figure 2D, Video 2). The mean diastolic pressure gradient across the TV was 4 mm Hg. RV systolic function was normal. The inferior vena cava was 28 mm in diameter with blunted respiratory variation. Estimated RV systolic pressure was 32 mm Hg. A D-shaped left ventricular cavity was noted with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% (Video 3). Other cardiac valves were normal in morphology and function. A provisional diagnosis of an RA neoplastic mass (myxoma) vs a calcified thrombus was considered.

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) revealed a large, irregular heterogeneous, hyperdense lesion in the RA with significant streak artifacts around it, suggestive of a calcified mass lesion (Figure 3). Disproportionate dilatation of the RA and RV was also noted. There was no evidence of pulmonary embolism or obstructive coronary artery disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Myxoma, calcified thrombus, primary/metastatic malignant lesion, or infective endocarditis were considered the initial possibilities in the patient.

Management

In view of the clinical presentation and imaging findings, surgical resection of the lesion was advised after thorough discussion with the patient and family. Under cardiopulmonary bypass, the patient underwent successful excision of the RA mass. Intraoperatively, the mass appeared triangular in shape with stalked attachment to the RA free wall. Surface convolutions and dense calcification gave a hard stone-like consistency to the mass (Figure 4A). TV ring annuloplasty (30-mm device) was also performed simultaneously.

Gross Appearance of the Resected Mass

Immediate postresection image showing the hard mass with surface irregularities (A); heterogeneous components of the mass with soft gelatinous tissue underneath superficial calcification; the stalk (orange arrow) appears relatively noncalcified (B); multiple gray-white to gray-yellow soft to hard tissues and calcification (orange arrow) associated with the lesion (C and D).

On pathologic examination, the resected mass revealed friable, soft, and gelatinous tissue underneath superficial calcification (Figure 4). On microscopy, findings were suggestive of RA myxoma with osseous metaplasia and calcification (Figure 5).

Histopathologic Examination of the Mass

Multiple irregular fragments displaying abundant hypocellular pale eosinophilic to myxoid stroma with focal hemorrhagic and degenerative areas (H&E, ×40) (A); hypocellular myxoid lesion comprising pale eosinophilic to basophilic myxoid matrix and patchy fibrous septa. Scattered stellate to polygonal cells seen with indistinct margins and round- to oval-shaped nuclei embedded within the stroma. No significant nuclear atypia/mitotic activity/necrosis noted (H&E, ×100) (C). H&E = hematoxylin and eosin.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful with extubation on the second postoperative day. No VA events were noted during the recuperative phase (Supplemental Figures 1 and 2). Postoperative TTE revealed a tricuspid annuloplasty ring in situ (mean diastolic pressure gradient 4 mm Hg across the TV) and moderate to severe TR (RV systolic pressure of 26 mm Hg). The RA (63 mm) and RV (basal 38 mm and mid 31 mm) had reduced in dimension. The patient was discharged on the sixth postoperative day in a stable condition on oral vitamin K antagonist, aspirin, furosemide, and spironolactone. At 6 weeks of follow-up, the patient continues to be asymptomatic, with no fresh complaints of palpitations or right-sided heart failure (RHF) symptoms developing.

Discussion

The characteristic clinical triad in CM consists of obstructive symptoms, cardioembolic features, and systemic manifestations.1 RA myxomas usually present with symptoms related to RHF, pulmonary embolization, syncope, or TV inflow obstruction. VT as the presenting feature of myxoma is exceedingly rare.6 In our patient, recurrent hemodynamically unstable VT was the initial presentation. VT could have resulted from the mechanical impact of the tumor mass on the TV and annulus, compounded by severe TR and right-sided chamber dilatation. The superior TV annulus is in close proximity to the free wall of the right ventricular outflow tract; thus, pathologic processes affecting this site have an effect on both structures. CM are associated with secretion of inflammatory cytokines, which may have created a proarrhythmic milieu. Despite an exact cause-effect relationship not being conclusively established, resolution of VT episodes postoperatively strongly points toward CM as the contributory cause of arrhythmia in our patient.

Severe TR and right-sided chamber dilatation are a direct consequence of tumor impingement on the TV apparatus, leading to malcoaptation and distortion of annular geometry.7 Dynamic obstruction to the TV orifice may also produce features of tricuspid stenosis. Many such patients present with RHF; hence, screening for right heart mass lesions must be performed in these cases.

Surgical excision removed the primary etiology of valvular malcoaptation in our case. An adequately undersized TV annuloplasty ring was implanted to ensure valvular coaptation and prevent the development of iatrogenic tricuspid stenosis. Because of the patient's young age and structurally normal TV leaflets, valve repair was prioritized over replacement. With a gradual reduction in right heart dimensions and optimum medical therapy, it is anticipated that TR severity will reduce. A small reduction in TR severity in the immediate postoperative period was noted with a reduction in RA and RV dimensions.

Calcification occurs in 20% of CM, whereas ossification is rarely seen in only 8% of the cases; the presence of both in our patient may signify a long duration of tumor existence.8 Because of the overlying shell-like calcification, the probability of embolic manifestations from the tumor surface could have been mitigated.

Cardiac tumors very rarely present with VA.6,9 In the pediatric age group, fibroma most commonly presents with VA, which can be incessant and life threatening (30% cases), followed by rhabdomyoma, myxoma, and Purkinje cell tumors.6 In adults, benign tumors such as myxoma, lipoma, hemangioma, and fibroma may present with VA. Primary malignant tumors (sarcoma and lymphoma) and secondary malignant tumors (lung, breast, and melanoma) may cause VA as part of the neoplastic involvement or secondary to chemotherapy-induced arrhythmogenesis.10 Arrhythmia mechanism mainly involves macro–re-entry at the tumor location interspersed with healthy myocardial tissue and triggered automaticity.6 Therapeutic options include complete/partial surgical resection, antiarrhythmic drug therapy, and implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation in selected cases.

Echocardiography remains the first-line imaging modality for the diagnosis of CM because it provides rapid assessment of tumor morphology, chamber involvement, and associated hemodynamic consequences. TTE as compared with transesophageal echocardiography has a sensitivity of 95% vs 100% for myxoma detection and 64.5% vs 95.2% for delineating the tumor attachment site, respectively.11 Despite the high sensitivity of echocardiography in detection of CM, diagnostic dilemma may persist regarding etiology of an intracardiac mass (tumor/thrombus/normal structural variant) and its embolic potential. Newer techniques using tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) offer greater insights into mass characterization and mobility assessment.12 TDI of pathologic structures such as thrombi, tumors, and vegetations demonstrate a discordant, asynchronous movement (out of phase from the adjoining healthy cardiac tissue), not related to the cardiac cycle. In a prospective study of 72 coronary artery disease patients with left ventricular apical thrombi, a peak antegrade velocity of the mobile portion of a thrombus of ≥10 cm/s on TDI was associated with an increased risk of embolization/death at 1-year follow-up.12 This suggests an important role of TDI in diagnosis as well as prognostication during evaluation of intracardiac masses.

On CT, the CM appears hypoattenuated compared with the surrounding blood pool with a heterogeneous appearance. Postcontrast enhancement is mild in comparison with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).1 However, CT is highly sensitive to calcification within the tumor.11 MRI is the ideal diagnostic imaging test in view of enhanced tumor imaging and tissue characterization with contrast. It also provides information on the functional impact of the mass on adjoining structures and hemodynamics using cine and phase contrast imaging sequences.1

Definitive management of CM is prompt surgical excision to prevent embolization, progressive heart failure, refractory arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death. Because of the clinical presentation and imaging findings, we opted for immediate surgery in our case. Sporadic CM has a very low rate of recurrence (1%-3%) postresection.13 Because of the noncalcified stalk, complete resection of the mass with adequate margins was possible in our patient, leading to a satisfactory surgical outcome.

Conclusions

CM may arise from various sites, with the left atrial origin being most commonly seen. Right atrial myxomas present with features of right-sided heart failure or embolic manifestations. Although supraventricular arrhythmias may be noted in patients with atrial myxomas, rare presentations with VA may occur. A multipronged imaging strategy involving echocardiography, CT, and MRI helps in early diagnosis and tumor characterization. Prompt surgical excision is the treatment of choice and is associated with reduction in morbidity and mortality.
