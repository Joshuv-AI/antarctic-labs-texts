Intraoperative measurement of hinge reaction force using strain gauge and predictability of postoperative hinge fracture in open-door cervical laminoplasty: a prospective study with mid-term follow-up
Authors: Shigeo Ueda, Nobuaki Naito, Hokuto Yamashita, Jiro Ohara, Atsuhiko Toyoshima, Miyuki Fukuda, Nobuhiro Sasaki, Masayuki Kuroda, Minoru Hoshimaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpen-door cervical laminoplasty relies on greenstick fracture at the hinge side to maintain laminar elevation. Highlight box

Key findings

• Hinge reaction force in open-door cervical laminoplasty can be quantitatively measured using a strain gauge system (mean 0.70 kgf). Reduced force significantly predicts early hinge fracture (area under the curve =0.798, cutoff 0.59 kgf). At mid-term follow-up, 62% of early fractures healed spontaneously, and neither Japanese Orthopaedic Association recovery rate nor postoperative pain visual analog scale scores differed between fracture and non-fracture groups.

What is known and what is new?

• Hinge fracture is a recognized complication of open-door cervical laminoplasty, but its prediction has relied entirely on subjective tactile assessment. This study is the first to quantitatively measure hinge reaction force, establishing objective numerical benchmarks and a predictive cutoff that transforms an experience-dependent assessment into a measurable parameter.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• As foundational biomechanical research, this study provides the essential reference data for developing simplified clinical assessment devices. For example, a leaf-spring Penfield-type checker calibrated to the 0.59 kgf threshold could enable passive, zero-complexity hinge quality assessment without electronic instrumentation. The force benchmarks also inform force-guided selective fixation strategies (stand-alone hydroxyapatite spacer vs. supplementary miniplate), simulation-based surgical training, and technique-independent quality standards applicable across all laminoplasty methods.

Introduction

Background

Open-door cervical laminoplasty, first described by Hirabayashi in 1978, has become a standard surgical procedure for cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) (1-3). The technique involves complete osteotomy on one side (open side) and creation of a greenstick fracture on the contralateral side (hinge side), allowing the lamina to be elevated like a door to decompress the spinal cord.

Various modifications including autologous bone grafting, ceramic spacers, and titanium miniplate fixation have been developed to prevent lamina reclosure (4-6). While these modifications have reduced reclosure rates, they add surgical complexity and cost. Among these techniques, stand-alone hydroxyapatite (HA) spacer laminoplasty, which relies solely on hinge reaction force to secure the spacer without metallic fixation, remains a widely performed approach in East Asia. This technique also provides an ideal biomechanical model for studying hinge reaction force, because the greenstick fracture is the sole stabilizer and no metallic fixation confounds the force-fracture relationship. The force thresholds identified in this model are potentially applicable to all laminoplasty techniques, including plate-augmented procedures, where hinge quality remains a determinant of long-term stability. Creating an adequate hinge with intact inner cortex and sufficient reaction force remains a critical surgical goal.

Rationale and knowledge gap

The reaction force generated by the greenstick fracture at the hinge provides the clamping force that secures HA spacers in position. However, this force has traditionally been assessed subjectively based on surgeon experience and tactile feedback. Expressions such as “appropriate resistance” or “good springback” require extensive training to master and inevitably lead to inter-surgeon variability. Furthermore, the natural history and healing potential of hinge fractures have not been well documented (7).

Objective

The purposes of this study were to: (I) quantify hinge reaction force using a strain gauge system; (II) identify factors correlated with hinge reaction force; (III) evaluate its predictive value for postoperative hinge fracture; and (IV) investigate mid-term outcomes including bone healing of fractured hinges. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0105/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This prospective observational study included 50 consecutive patients who underwent open-door cervical laminoplasty with stand-alone HA spacers performed by a single surgeon (S.U.) at our Shin-ai Kai Spine Center between November 2015 and June 2017. Mean follow-up period was 2.5 years.

The cohort comprised 35 males and 15 females with a mean age of 68.9 years (range, 46–84 years). Diagnoses included CSM in 31 patients (62%) and OPLL in 19 patients (38%). Mean body mass index (BMI) was 23.2 kg/m2 (range, 16.6–32.5 kg/m2). A total of 173 laminae were treated: C3 (n=18), C4 (n=49), C5 (n=50), C6 (n=50), and C7 (n=6) (Table 1).

Surgical technique

A minimally invasive technique developed at our center was employed. Through a 3-cm midline skin incision, the posterior cervical muscles were split in the midline to expose the laminae. A high-speed diamond burr was used to create a full-thickness trough on the open side and a partial-thickness trough on the hinge side. The laminae were gently elevated to create a greenstick fracture, and custom HA spacers (8–14 mm width; mean 9.4 mm) (8-10) were wedged into position using only hinge reaction force without additional fixation. The 3 cm skin incision provides access to the laminae via a muscle-splitting approach through the posterior midline raphe; the subperiosteal exposure extends beyond the skin edges through self-retaining retractors (see Figure S1 for intraoperative photographs). Postoperatively, NSAIDs (loxoprofen 60 mg three times daily) were routinely administered for 3 days; no perioperative corticosteroids were used.

Strain gauge measurement system

A custom Penfield dissector was equipped with a strain gauge sensor (Figure 1). The sensor interface (KYOWA PCD-400A) and dedicated software (KYOWA PCAS-100A) enabled real-time force measurement. Because metal strain gauges are susceptible to temperature-induced drift, calibration was performed before each surgery using standard weights (50, 100, 250, 500, and 1,000 g) applied to the instrument tip to generate a calibration curve. The calibration demonstrated excellent linearity (R2=0.9998), ensuring measurement accuracy under operating room conditions. Using this instrument, hinge reaction force was measured intraoperatively at two time points. First, during laminar elevation, the instrument tip was placed beneath the elevated lamina to record the initial reaction force generated by the greenstick fracture mechanism. Second, after HA spacer insertion, a lifting force was applied to the lamina, and the residual hinge reaction force securing the spacer was recorded. All measurements were performed in real-time, allowing immediate assessment of hinge quality during the surgical procedure. Each measurement was performed as a single determination per lamina. Repeated measurements were deliberately avoided because iterative manipulation of the hinge risks progressive weakening of the greenstick fracture mechanism, potentially creating a floppy hinge that compromises spacer retention.

Imaging evaluation

All patients underwent computed tomography (CT) imaging at postoperative day 1 and at delayed phase (mean 2.5 years). The following parameters were measured: lamina thickness (mean 5.2 mm), lamina length (mean 13.9 mm), opening angle (mean 20.5°), and presence of hinge fracture defined as discontinuity of the inner cortex. Laminar subsidence into the spinal canal was also evaluated. Bone healing was defined as restoration of inner cortex continuity. All CT images were independently evaluated by two surgeons (S.U. and M.F.) who were blinded to the intraoperative force measurements. Inter-observer agreement for hinge fracture classification was excellent (Cohen’s κ=0.81).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and Python statsmodels library. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for continuous variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the predictive accuracy and optimal cutoff value for hinge fracture. To account for potential within-patient correlation arising from multiple laminae analyzed per patient, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with exchangeable correlation structure and robust standard errors were used as a sensitivity analysis. Both univariate and multivariate GEE models adjusting for lamina thickness, spacer size, and cervical level were performed. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Katano Hospital (approval No. 2022-012), and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Results

Hinge reaction force measurements

Mean hinge reaction force during laminar elevation varied by cervical level: C3, 1.41 kgf; C4, 1.35 kgf; C5, 1.18 kgf; C6, 1.23 kgf; and C7, 1.23 kgf. After HA spacer insertion, mean forces decreased to: C3, 0.79 kgf; C4, 0.81 kgf; C5, 0.63 kgf; C6, 0.65 kgf; and C7, 0.61 kgf. The overall mean was 0.70 kgf (6.87 N; range, 0.13–1.48 kgf; median, 0.67 kgf; IQR, 0.49–0.91) (Table 2).

Factors correlated with hinge reaction force

Correlation analysis revealed that hinge reaction force was positively correlated with lamina thickness (r=0.275, P<0.001) and negatively correlated with spacer size (r=−0.264, P<0.001). No significant correlation was found with lamina length (r=−0.058, P=0.45) or opening angle (r=−0.116, P=0.13). Furthermore, opening angle was not significantly associated with hinge fracture occurrence (fracture group: 21.5±4.1° vs. non-fracture group: 20.3±6.0°, P=0.36).

Hinge fracture at postoperative day 1

Hinge fracture at postoperative day 1 occurred in 24 of 173 laminae (13.9%). The incidence by cervical level was: C3, 5.6% (1/18); C4, 4.1% (2/49); C5, 18.0% (9/50); C6, 20.0% (10/50); and C7, 33.3% (2/6). The fracture group showed significantly lower hinge reaction force [0.46±0.29 kgf (4.51±2.85 N)] compared to the non-fracture group [0.74±0.26 kgf (7.26±2.55 N), P<0.001] (Table 2). Fracture rates varied by spacer size: 8 mm, 4/52 (7.7%); 10 mm, 20/118 (16.9%); 12 mm, 0/2 (0.0%); although this trend did not reach statistical significance (8 vs. 10 mm, P=0.17). No cases of postoperative C5 palsy occurred in this cohort (0/50 patients).

ROC analysis for fracture prediction

ROC analysis revealed that hinge reaction force had good predictive accuracy for postoperative hinge fracture with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.798. As an exploratory analysis to establish initial thresholds for this novel measurement, the optimal cutoff value was 0.59 kgf (5.79 N) (sensitivity 83.3%, specificity 69.1%), suggesting that laminae with hinge reaction force below this threshold are at significantly increased risk of fracture (Figure 2). At this cutoff, the positive predictive value (PPV) was 30.3% and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 96.3%, with a positive likelihood ratio of 2.70 and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.24. A sensitivity analysis excluding C7 laminae (n=6) yielded a virtually identical AUC of 0.797 (C3–C6 only, n=167), confirming that the small C7 sample did not substantially influence the overall predictive model.

Sensitivity analysis using GEE

GEE analysis accounting for within-patient clustering confirmed the significant association between hinge reaction force and fracture risk [odds ratio (OR) =0.002, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.000–0.188, P=0.007]. The results remained significant after adjustment for lamina thickness, spacer size, and cervical level (adjusted OR =0.001, 95% CI: 0.000–0.178, P=0.008). These findings were robust across different correlation structures (exchangeable and independence), demonstrating that the observed association is not attributable to within-patient correlation.

Mid-term outcomes: bone healing and fracture evolution

Delayed phase CT (mean 2.5 years; range, 2.0–3.3 years) was available for 159 of 173 laminae (91.9%); 14 laminae in 4 patients were lost to follow-up (1 patient transferred to another institution, 3 patients did not return for scheduled imaging). Of the 24 laminae with early hinge fracture, 3 were among these patients; thus, 21 early fractures were evaluable at delayed phase. The overall hinge fracture rate decreased from 13.9% at postoperative day 1 to 8.2% (13/159) at delayed phase. Of the 21 laminae with evaluable early hinge fractures, 13 (61.9%) demonstrated bone healing with restored inner cortex continuity, while 8 (38.1%) showed persistent fracture (non-union, including 1 pseudarthrosis) (Figure 3).

Five laminae (3.1%) developed delayed hinge fracture (intact at day 1 but fractured at delayed phase). These delayed fractures showed no significant difference in hinge reaction force compared to the intact group (0.84±0.18 vs. 0.75±0.27 kgf, P=0.85).

Laminar subsidence

No lamina in this series demonstrated subsidence of 1 mm or greater into the spinal canal at any time point during follow-up, including the 24 laminae with early hinge fracture. Despite the occurrence of hinge fractures, all spacers remained securely in position, and all patients maintained adequate canal expansion without clinically significant laminar collapse.

Clinical outcomes

Preoperative Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score improved significantly from 12.9±1.4 to 15.5±0.6 at 12 months postoperatively (P<0.001), with a mean Hirabayashi recovery rate of 62.2%±14.2%. When comparing patients with at least one hinge fracture (n=17) to those without (n=33), no significant differences were observed in postoperative JOA score (15.5±0.6 vs. 15.5±0.6, P=0.69) or recovery rate (63.5%±13.4% vs. 61.5%±14.8%, P=0.63). Similarly, postoperative neck pain visual analog scale (VAS) scores did not differ between fracture and non-fracture groups (2.3±2.7 vs. 2.0±2.4, P=0.83), nor did arm pain VAS scores (0.8±1.6 vs. 1.5±2.0, P=0.056).

Discussion

Key findings

This study represents the first quantitative measurement of hinge reaction force in open-door cervical laminoplasty with mid-term follow-up data on bone healing. Using a strain gauge-equipped instrument, we demonstrated that hinge reaction force can be reliably measured [mean 0.70 kgf (6.87 N)], that reduced force is significantly correlated with early hinge fracture (P<0.001), and that the majority of early fractures (62%) heal by mid-term follow-up. Importantly, no significant differences in JOA recovery rate were observed between patients with and without hinge fractures (63.5% vs. 61.5%, P=0.63), confirming that early hinge fractures do not adversely affect neurological recovery when managed with properly wedged HA spacers. Furthermore, postoperative neck and arm pain VAS scores showed no significant differences between groups, providing additional evidence that hinge fractures do not result in clinically meaningful pain outcomes. The ROC analysis revealed good predictive accuracy (AUC =0.798) with an optimal cutoff of 0.59 kgf.

The absence of significant JOA differences between fracture and non-fracture groups warrants careful interpretation. This finding reflects the benign short-term course of hinge fractures when adequate spacer support is maintained, but does not diminish the clinical rationale for fracture prevention. Hinge fracture with subsequent laminar subsidence is a well-documented cause of delayed radiculopathy from foraminal stenosis, recurrent myelopathy from progressive canal re-narrowing, postoperative axial neck pain, and the need for revision surgery (5,11,12). The 38% non-union rate observed in this series represents a population at potential long-term risk for these complications. Furthermore, the absence of clinically significant subsidence in our cohort may itself reflect the protective effect of adequate hinge reaction force and proper spacer wedging. Therefore, the clinical value of intraoperative force measurement lies not in predicting short-term JOA outcomes, but in providing an objective quality assurance parameter that may reduce the incidence of mechanically compromised hinges and their long-term sequelae. The distinction between short-term neurological outcomes and long-term mechanical integrity is critical: the favorable 12-month JOA results in the fracture group reflect the buffering capacity of properly wedged HA spacers, whereas the 38% persistent non-union rate warrants further investigation in longer-term studies to determine its clinical significance.

Limitations

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, consistent surgical technique by a single surgeon, comprehensive imaging follow-up, and novel quantitative approach to an important but previously unquantified surgical parameter. The mid-term follow-up data on bone healing provides clinically valuable information regarding the natural history of hinge fractures.

This study has several limitations. First, as a proof-of-concept study, a formal sample size calculation was not performed; all consecutive patients during the study period were enrolled. Second, the measurement system requires specialized equipment. Third, neurological outcomes were assessed only at 12 months; longer-term outcome studies are warranted. Fourth, GEE sensitivity analysis confirmed robustness despite within-patient clustering (P=0.007), though ROC analysis did not incorporate clustered data methods. Fifth, the delay between data collection [2015–2017] and publication was necessary to accumulate follow-up data. Sixth, test-retest reliability could not be assessed because repeated hinge manipulation causes progressive weakening; instrument-level accuracy was confirmed by excellent calibration linearity (R2=0.9998), and cadaveric validation studies are warranted. Seventh, this proof-of-concept study establishes measurement feasibility rather than demonstrating clinical outcome improvement; prospective intervention studies are warranted.

Comparison with similar research

Previous studies have reported hinge fracture rates ranging from several percent to 38% (7,11,13). Wong et al. [2024] recently reported that intraoperative hinge fracture in double-door laminoplasty was significantly associated with postoperative cervical kyphosis, further highlighting the clinical importance of hinge integrity (14). Our day-1 fracture rate of 13.9% falls within this range. However, no prior study has quantified the mechanical force at the hinge or correlated it with fracture risk. The 62% bone healing rate we observed, consistent with healing rates reported in recent literature (15-17), provides reassurance that early hinge fractures do not necessarily predict poor outcomes, particularly when adequate spacer support is maintained.

The absence of significant differences in JOA recovery rate between fracture and non-fracture groups (63.5% vs. 61.5%, P=0.63), together with comparable postoperative neck and arm pain VAS scores, provides direct clinical evidence supporting the safety of our stand-alone HA spacer technique. This finding, combined with the absence of clinically significant laminar subsidence, suggests that early hinge fractures, when managed with properly wedged HA spacers, do not adversely affect neurological recovery. Notably, titanium miniplate fixation has been proposed as a solution to prevent laminar reclosure and hinge fracture (18,19); however, recent cost-effectiveness analysis has demonstrated that HA spacer laminoplasty achieves comparable clinical outcomes at significantly lower cost than plate-augmented techniques (20). Our approach of quantifying hinge quality at the time of surgery offers a third strategy: rather than adding fixation hardware universally, the force measurement identifies the subset of laminae that may benefit from supplementary fixation, potentially optimizing both clinical outcomes and resource utilization.

Explanations of findings

We identified lamina thickness as a positive predictor of hinge reaction force (r=0.275, P<0.001). Thicker laminae provide greater structural integrity for the greenstick fracture mechanism, generating higher reaction forces. Conversely, spacer size showed a negative correlation (r=−0.264, P<0.001), likely because larger spacers require greater laminar elevation, which may stress the hinge and reduce its effective spring-back force. In contrast, opening angle showed no significant correlation with either hinge reaction force (r=−0.116, P=0.13) or hinge fracture (P=0.36). This finding suggests that the degree of laminar elevation does not independently predict hinge integrity, and that hinge reaction force serves as a more direct and reliable indicator of the greenstick fracture mechanism’s quality.

C7 demonstrated the highest fracture rate (33.3%) in this series; however, this observation is exploratory given the small sample size (n=6). Despite having the thickest laminae (6.6 mm), C7 may be paradoxically susceptible to fracture because the thick outer cortex tends to impinge on the contralateral bone edge during laminar elevation, generating excessive force that disrupts the inner cortex. For C7 laminoplasty, surgical modifications such as deeper trough creation may warrant further investigation.

Implications and actions needed

Hinge fracture with subsequent laminar subsidence can lead to radiculopathy from foraminal stenosis, recurrence of myelopathy from spinal canal re-narrowing, and ultimately revision surgery (5,11,12). Although no clinically significant laminar subsidence was observed in this series despite the 13.9% early fracture rate, optimizing hinge quality at the time of surgery remains prudent. The absence of subsidence likely reflects the protective effect of adequate hinge reaction force and proper spacer wedging, even when the inner cortex fractures.

The quantitative measurement method established in this study offers an objective numerical target for surgeons. The mean hinge reaction force of 0.70 kgf (6.87 N) provides a benchmark, and the cutoff of 0.59 kgf can serve as an early warning system for hinge compromise. Real-time intraoperative monitoring enables recognition of inadequate hinge creation, allowing surgeons to implement countermeasures before completing the procedure. The clinical significance of our cutoff value lies primarily in its high NPV (96.3%) rather than its PPV (30.3%). This asymmetry is characteristic of a risk stratification tool rather than a diagnostic test; the cutoff is designed to identify at-risk laminae requiring further intraoperative attention rather than to definitively predict fracture. When the measured hinge reaction force exceeds 0.59 kgf, the surgeon can proceed with confidence that the stand-alone HA spacer technique will provide adequate fixation. Conversely, when the force falls below this threshold, we propose a stepwise clinical algorithm: (I) reassess and deepen the hinge-side trough to optimize the greenstick fracture mechanism; (II) if the force remains insufficient after trough revision, consider using a smaller spacer to reduce hinge stress; and (III) if adequate force cannot be achieved, supplementary fixation with a titanium miniplate or suture anchor should be considered. Critically, inadequate hinge reaction force does not always manifest as visible laminar collapse or subsidence during surgery. A hinge may appear visually intact yet generate insufficient clamping force to secure the spacer long-term—a condition undetectable by tactile assessment alone. In such cases, a simplified force checker (such as the leaf-spring Penfield device described above) could objectively identify this occult insufficiency, and plate-and-screw fixation systems would provide more reliable laminar stabilization than stand-alone spacer techniques. In this context, hinge reaction force functions not merely as a quality assurance parameter but as an intraoperative decision-support indicator that directly guides the choice of fixation strategy for each individual lamina.

This study represents a foundational biomechanical investigation that, for the first time, quantifies the greenstick fracture reaction force in cervical laminoplasty. The strain gauge system used in this study is a research instrument, not intended for routine clinical application. Rather, the established force benchmarks and predictive thresholds provide essential reference data for translational developments in several domains.

First, force measurement enables objective, data-driven feedback for surgical trainees learning hinge creation technique, replacing subjective assessment with quantitative benchmarks that can accelerate the learning curve for this technically demanding procedure. The quantitative force data may also inform the development of simulation-based training models with objective performance targets.

Second, the measurement principle established here—strain gauge quantification of bone-implant interface forces—builds on earlier work applying intraoperative biomechanical measurement to spinal surgery (21,22). This approach may be applicable to other spinal procedures where greenstick fracture or controlled osteotomy plays a critical role, such as posterior cervical foraminotomy or thoracic laminoplasty.

Third, the quantitative thresholds established in this study provide the design specifications for simplified, clinically practical assessment devices. For example, a Penfield-type dissector incorporating a leaf spring with a known spring constant calibrated to the 0.59 kgf threshold could serve as a passive, single-use-ready checker: visible deformation of the spring tip during hinge elevation would immediately indicate inadequate reaction force without requiring electronic instrumentation, calibration, or additional operative time. Such a device would translate the biomechanical knowledge generated by this research into a zero-complexity intraoperative tool accessible to any surgical center.

Fourth, future studies integrating hinge reaction force with patient-specific covariates (lamina thickness, cervical level, spacer size) into a multivariate prediction model could yield individualized risk scores for clinical decision-making.

The present study establishes baseline parameters and validates the measurement concept; subsequent prospective studies examining whether intraoperative intervention based on force measurement improves clinical outcomes represent the logical next step in this research trajectory. Future studies should also investigate the correlation between residual lamina thickness after trough creation and hinge reaction force, which could provide guidance for optimal trough depth to prevent early hinge fracture.

Conclusions

This study provides the first quantitative characterization of hinge reaction force in open-door cervical laminoplasty, establishing a mean value of 0.70 kgf (6.87 N) and an optimal predictive cutoff of 0.59 kgf (AUC =0.798). Importantly, 62% of early hinge fractures demonstrated bone healing at mid-term follow-up, and this complication did not compromise clinical outcomes (JOA recovery rate: P=0.63; pain VAS: P=0.83). The established force benchmarks and predictive threshold provide foundational biomechanical data for developing simplified clinical assessment devices and evidence-based surgical training standards. Future studies should investigate whether real-time force monitoring can reduce hinge fracture rates through intraoperative technique modification.
