Enigmatic Roles of Complement Anaphylatoxin Signaling in Health and Disease
Authors: Anthony Shadid, Kathryn D Hok, Aleksey Y Domozhirov, Tingting Weng-Mills, Marie-Françoise Doursout, Nirmal K Banda, Marcos I Restrepo, Pooja Shivshankar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplement anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a are potent immunomodulators whose impact extends well beyond their traditional roles in innate immunity. Acting through G protein-coupled receptors C3aR, C5aR1, and C5aR2, these peptides take part in coordinating immune cell recruitment, vascular tone, and tissue remodeling. Yet their functions are deeply context-dependent: while they play essential roles in microbial clearance and immune coordination, their overactivation contributes to immunopathology in a wide range of diseases. The anaphylatoxins play key roles in early pathogen containment but can also drive cytokine storm and tissue damage, as in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and bacterial sepsis. In autoimmune conditions, the anaphylatoxins promote leukocyte infiltration and complement-mediated tissue injury. In chronic diseases, they contribute to fibrosis in diabetic kidney disease and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and anaphylatoxins disrupt neurovascular integrity in neurodegenerative diseases. In cancer, C3a and C5a shape the tumor microenvironment by facilitating immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis. As complement-targeted therapies gain momentum in clinical settings—particularly in the treatment of genetic disorders, such as paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, more recently COVID-19, and cancer—a deeper mechanistic understanding of C3a and C5a signaling is imperative as we advance closer toward precision medicine, and this review aims to inform future approaches for therapeutic complement modulation.Keywords: Complement system proteins, Complement activation, Alternative complement pathway, Anaphylatoxins, Immunologic factors

INTRODUCTION

The complement system is a central pillar of innate immunity, comprised of a cascade of serum proteins (C1–C9) that function in microbial clearance, immune complex handling, and tissue homeostasis. Activated through the classical, lectin, or alternative pathways, they cause the release of cleavage products such as C3a and C5a, potent pro-inflammatory peptides known as anaphylatoxins, and the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC). Soluble and membrane-bound complement regulators, including factor H, CD46, CD55, and CD59, are required to prevent damage to host tissues (1,2).

Within complement’s effector arms, C3a and C5a have the distinct ability to have rapid and broad immunological effects. These low-molecular-weight peptides act on G protein-coupled receptors—C3aR, C5aR1, and the non-canonical C5aR2—to promote chemotaxis, vascular permeability, cytokine release, and interactions with adaptive immunity (Fig. 1) (3). These receptors possess complex biology: while activation of C5aR1 typically provokes robust inflammatory responses, C5aR2 is contextually regulated to assume regulatory or counter-regulatory roles (3,4).

The anaphylatoxins are now understood to be more than mere inflammatory triggers. They operate with a highly situational effect, contingent upon the type of activating stimulus, the receptor utilized, tissue-specific factors, and even the circadian rhythm. In infectious contexts, C3a and C5a drive microbial clearance and coordination of innate and adaptive immunity (2,5). Excessive activation, however, enhances inflammation, promotes thrombosis, and drives immunopathology as evident in critical coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), wherein C5aR1 signaling augments the generation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and tissue damage (6). On the other hand, the same receptor guards against bacterial infections like Listeria monocytogenes, by limiting type I IFN production and preventing systemic immune dysregulation (7). Complement signaling duality is also demonstrated in autoimmune diseases, such that dysregulated anaphylatoxin receptor activity could tip the immune system towards chronic self-reactivity. C5aR1 and C3aR signaling have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of a range of autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA), with human study evidence and experimental model evidence pointing to them being the initiating causes of immune misfiring early in disease processes (8,9).

Recent findings have further complicated host defense and homeostatic regulation. The anaphylatoxins are now shown to function intracellularly, modulating autophagy, metabolism, and T cell differentiation—an evolving paradigm known as the “complosome” (10,11). Moreover, systemic cues such as circadian rhythms dynamically modulate complement function, providing a temporal complexity to these molecules previously evolved spatial regulation (12,13). These findings point to the pleiotropic and paradoxical functions of complement anaphylatoxins. They are essential sentinels of immune defense and tissue homeostasis but become disease agents when dysregulated. In this review, we discuss the multifaceted and sometimes contradictory roles of C3a and C5a signaling in health and disease. We begin with their functions in infectious and autoimmune disease, then explore their roles in chronic inflammatory and metabolic conditions, neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, genetic disorders, and circadian rhythm biology, and conclude with a survey of therapeutic strategies for modulating these pathways. We aim to illuminate the enigmatic roles of complement anaphylatoxins across biological systems by analyzing experimental and clinical research evidence.

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN INFECTIOUS DISEASE

Viral infections

Complement activation is a central component of the host antiviral response, but its overactivation—particularly through the generation of C3a and C5a—has emerged as a driver of viral immunopathology. In infections caused by influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and SARS-CoV-2, dysregulated anaphylatoxin signaling contributes to cytokine storm, vascular injury, and tissue destruction, often amplifying disease severity rather than containing the pathogen.

In COVID-19, abundant clinical evidence links complement activation to poor outcomes. Elevated plasma levels of C5a and its receptor C5aR1 have been consistently associated with disease severity, respiratory compromise, and mortality (14,15,16). In hospitalized patients with COVID-19, levels of C5a are significantly elevated in plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and correlate with neutrophilic infiltration, cytokine burden, and progression to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). C5aR1 is increased on neutrophils and monocytes, and its engagement leads to the generation of potent IL-6, TNF-α, and CCL2 (15,17). Exaggerated complement activation in these patients is not limited to terminal pathway activation; C3a and upstream components like C1r and C3 have also been implicated (18). Plasma proteomics and single-cell RNA-sequencing studies also support complement cascade systemic and local activation during severe disease, with strong transcriptional signatures for C3 and C5 in circulating and bronchoalveolar immune cells (18,19). Mechanistically, this hyperactivation is partly driven by the virus itself. Activation of complement also occurs intrinsically within epithelial cells: Yan et al. (19) demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 infection induces JAK1/2-mediated overexpression of C3, C5, and their receptors in lung epithelium to form a self-amplifying inflammatory loop.

Beyond the immune cell compartment, SARS-CoV-2 can also activate complement on endothelial surfaces. Perico et al. (20) demonstrated in vitro that free spike protein triggers complement activation and endothelial cell stimulation to elicit platelet aggregation and microvascular inflammation, and the resulting endothelial dysfunction is partially mediated by C3a–C3aR signaling, contributing to barrier disruption and thrombo-inflammation (21). These findings are reinforced by broader research on thrombo-inflammatory synergy, where complement is seen to interact with coagulation cascades to promote systemic injury (17,22). Posch et al. (23) demonstrated that C5aR1 signaling directly impairs epithelial integrity in airway cells infected with SARS-CoV-2, suggesting that interfering with non-immune cells could also be therapeutically significant. Therapeutically, complement blockade with Eculizumab (aka Soliris®) (anti-C5 Ab) or AMY-101 (C3 inhibitor) has shown early promise in halting inflammation, normalizing levels of lymphocytes, and improving oxygenation (24). These findings also confirm the central position of the C5a–C5aR1 axis in cytokine storm and lung injury pathogenesis in COVID-19.

Influenza infection offers a complementary perspective. In human and animal models, C5a levels correlate with the recruitment of neutrophils and lung injury. In clinical influenza, activation of C5 exacerbates pulmonary inflammation and epithelial damage. Moreover, several highly pathogenic influenza strains involve C5a in promoting acute lung injury and vascular permeability (25,26). Viruses may also utilize the complement to their advantage. Viral proteins may engage host complement regulators like CD46 or factor H, allowing pathogens to inhibit complement-mediated elimination (27). Additionally, Ab-dependent enhancement can worsen the severity of the disease through Fc receptor–mediated complement activation, and non-neutralizing Abs can facilitate C3a and C5a production, leading to a harmful state of hyperinflammation (28).

Experimental models of viral complement activation

Experimental animal models provide mechanistic explanations for clinical observations, with additional specific discrimination between the roles of C3a and C5a. In H5N1 and H7N9 influenza disease models, inhibition of C5aR1 or C3aR decreases neutrophilic influx, cytokine storms, and lung pathology. C3aR blockade protects pulmonary epithelial barriers, while C5aR1 inhibition decreases TNF-α and IL-6, showing distinct but overlapping roles for each receptor. (29,30). These effects were consistent across avian and seasonal strains, reinforcing their applicability.

In SARS-CoV infection, C3a-mediated inflammation has also been shown to play a role in pathology independent of viral replication. In C3-deficient mice, infection induced significantly less pulmonary edema, cytokines, and immune cell recruitment despite equivalent virus titers (31). These results suggest that SARS-CoV-induced damage to tissues is not due to the direct cytopathic effect of the virus, but due to complement activation of the immune response. While C5aR1 induced NET formation, causing alveolar damage and cytokine production in SARS-CoV-2 infected mice, inhibition by C5aR noncompetitive allosteric inhibitor, DF2593A decreased NET burden and pulmonary inflammation (6). Complement activation in such models is spatially confined to the lung parenchyma, as Szachowicz et al. (32) show, supporting the hypothesis that local, not systemic, complement dysregulation produces lung damage. Interestingly, the complement can also drive viral pathogenesis outside the lung. For instance, C3a and C5a signaling promote liver damage in murine models of viral hepatitis and D-galactosamine/LPS-induced acute liver failure—pathways that potentially may be involved in multi-organ COVID-19. Blockade of either axis suppressed Kupffer cell activation, reduced hemorrhage, and improved survival (33,34).

In MERS-CoV, C5aR1 induces macrophage pyroptosis by activating NLRP3 inflammasome (35). Blocking the axis reduced IL-1β production and caspase-1 cleavage in THP-1 cells. Also, it safeguarded mice against inflammation in the lungs and spleen (35,36). These effects were also correlated with reduced viral titers, suggesting that C5a can paradoxically support viral persistence by increasing tissue injury. Furthermore, C3aR plays a role in Th2-mediated airway hyperreactivity in models of allergy—pathways also utilized by viral infections like respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), to induce mucus overproduction and epithelial remodeling (37,38). Mice deficient in C3aR had reduced goblet cell metaplasia and accelerated recovery, suggesting that C3aR signaling contributes to acute damage and chronic airway remodeling. These findings have direct relevance in pediatric populations, as RSV is a major cause of bronchiolitis.

Of course, the complement is not always harmful. O'Brien et al. (39) showed that mice lacking C3 had increased mortality following H1N1 and H5N1 infections due to impaired early viral clearance. These findings reflect the biphasic nature of the complement system: while overactivation leads to inflammation, baseline activation is essential for maximal viral defense. In summary, C3a and C5a play dual roles in viral infections: they are critical early mediators of immune defense but can drive severe immunopathology when overactivated. Across all the different viral pathogens, including influenza, SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV, evidence consistently shows that C3a–C3aR and C5a–C5aR1 signaling pathways are involved in tissue injury, organ dysfunction, and adverse clinical outcomes. Targeted inhibition of these 2 axes holds therapeutic promise for mitigating immune-mediated damage while preserving essential antiviral functions.

Bacterial infections

C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins play significant roles in antibacterial host defense. Signaling through their cognate receptors C3aR, C5aR1, and C5aR2, these molecules trigger neutrophil recruitment, cytokine release, and pathogen destruction (40,41). Moreover, C3a and its cleavage product have been shown to exert direct antimicrobial effects, functioning as nonlytic bactericidal peptides that disrupt bacterial membranes, independent of opsonophagocytosis (42). However, like in viral infections, their hyper-activation—or aberrant signaling—can make the protective immune response pathogenic, leading to inflammatory overdrive, tissue injury, and bacterial immune evasion (43). In bacterial pneumonia, clinical findings have shown elevated circulating levels of C3a are correlated with severity and poor outcome, suggesting that complement activation is implicated in the generation of pulmonary inflammation and injury (44). In earlier human studies, sepsis-induced ARDS patients had markedly elevated plasma C5a, validating its status as a mediator of lung injury. While C5a/C5aR1 signaling axis is accountable for neutrophil recruitment, acute lung injury and systemic sepsis, pathogens like Haemophilus influenzae evade this response by producing complement-inhibitory proteins that reduce production of C5a and neutrophil activation in the airway, allowing for chronic colonization (45). These persistent infections may contribute to fibrotic lung remodeling, with H. influenzae implicated in the pathogenesis of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis through chronic immune activation and evasion mechanisms (46).

Animal models

In Pseudomonas aeruginosa-infected mice, C3aR deletion enhanced bacterial clearance and reduced lung injury, suggesting that C3aR may be pathogenic in certain pulmonary infections (47). In contrast, Chlamydia psittaci-infected C3-deficient mice had elevated bacterial burden and more severe lung pathology, affirming C3’s protective role in controlling intracellular respiratory pathogens (48). Studies with Listeria monocytogenes have described how complement interacts with cytosolic sensing of the pathogen. Mice deficient in C5aR1 exhibited increased mortality and levels of type I IFN—a cytokine known to worsen Listeria infection—demonstrating a protective, anti-IFN role for C5aR1 (7). In contrast, C5aR2-deficient mice fared better, suggesting C5aR2 signaling in this setting is detrimental (41). Mechanistic studies revealed that C5a and C3a suppress IFN-β production by inhibiting the STING–IRF3 pathway, a cytosolic DNA-sensing cascade that drives type I IFN responses (40). These findings confirm that IFN-I interferes with bacterial clearance in Listeria infection (49). C5aR1 deficiency in mouse models of Staphylococcus aureus infection led to impaired neutrophil recruitment and delayed bacterial clearance, highlighting the role of C5aR1 in initial immune defense. C5aR2 deletion enhanced the killing of bacteria but caused overactive inflammation (50). These observations highlight the receptor-specific functions of C5a signaling in infection management and immune regulation. In models of ascending urinary tract infections, C5aR1-deficient mice exhibited reduced bacterial loads in the kidney and bladder and reduced tissue damage. The receptor enhanced uropathogenic Escherichia coli adhesion and disruption of epithelium, supporting a role for C5aR1 in facilitating infection rather than controlling it (51,52).

Within the central nervous system (CNS), complement activation serves a dual purpose—preventing bacterial entry but inducing inflammation and disrupting the blood-brain barrier. In experimental meningococcal infection, C5aR1 exacerbated disease severity, while C3aR deficiency led to impaired bacterial control, indicating a protective role for C3a in this context (53). In humans, excessive complement activity in bacterial meningitis has been linked to poor neurologic outcomes, fueling interest in C5-targeted therapies (54). In experimental meningococcal sepsis, C5aR1-deficient mice were protected from overwhelming inflammation and organ injury, whereas C3aR deficiency impaired bacterial control (53). These findings are consistent with human data for bacterial meningitis, where hyperactivation of the complement system is associated with increased neurologic injury and motivates consideration of targeted inhibition (54).

Nonetheless, bacteria have evolved diverse strategies to evade complement-mediated lysis, including recruitment of host regulators (e.g., factor H, C4BP), release of proteases that cleave C3 or C5, and expression of surface molecules that block convertase assembly (55). These mechanisms not only inhibit opsonization and lysis but also suppress the formation of anaphylatoxins, dampening neutrophilic activity and allowing chronic colonization on mucosal surfaces (56). Paradoxically, complement activation in some situations directly promotes bacterial pathogenesis. Anaphylatoxin-mediated inflammation can impair barrier function, promote bacterial dissemination, and exacerbate septic organ dysfunction (57).

Fungal infections

The complement system is a component of innate immunity that engages fungal pathogens through multifaceted mechanisms. The anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a are potent inflammatory mediators that help coordinate early immune responses to fungal invasion. However, this system is also a target for immune evasion by fungi and, under certain conditions, can contribute to host tissue damage.

Fungal pathogens activate the complement system via classical, lectin, and alternative pathways, leading to opsonization, chemotaxis, and cell lysis. Complement-derived C3a and C5a enhance neutrophil recruitment, cytokine release, and phagocytosis (58). Locally produced complement proteins in the lung play an immunomodulatory role, with C5aR1 signaling implicated in antifungal immunity and Th2-mediated inflammation in allergic asthma. Long pentraxins such as pentraxin-3 (PTX3) synergize with complement by enhancing C1q binding and activation of the classical pathway by Aspergillus fumigatus, leading to augmented deposition of C3 and clearance of fungi (59). In addition to their immunoregulatory roles, C3a and derived peptides directly disrupt fungal membranes and exert fungicidal activity independent of leukocytes (60).

Inflammatory cytokine responses are also enhanced by complement. C3 opsonization is necessary for TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 production by Candida albicans-stimulated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (61). Platelet-dependent complement activation was observed due to A. fumigatus galactosaminogalactan, linking fungal infection and thrombosis (62). In mucosal surfaces, C5a initiates calcium mobilization in conjunctival epithelial cells through transient receptor potential channel signaling, suggesting a connection between complement and epithelial sensory function (63). C5a is also a second signal activating neutrophils for efficient fungal killing in blood, supplementing responses otherwise deficient with fungal recognition alone (64). Intracellular interaction of C5a with Dectin-1 in macrophages promotes the production of ROS and enhances fungal clearance (65). In whole blood models, inhibition of the alternative or terminal pathways reduces cytokine release in response to fungal Ags, underscoring the immunomodulatory role of complement beyond opsonization (66).

Animal models

Animal studies have provided essential mechanistic insights into the complement’s role in fungal immunity. C3 and C3aR are required for allergic sensitization to A. fumigatus in the presence of chitin, pointing to a role in Th2 priming during fungal asthma (67). In systemic candidiasis, C5aR1 licenses phagocytes for fungal killing through metabolic reprogramming and ROS generation, facilitating sterilizing immunity (68). Complement deficiency models further underscore its importance. C3-deficient mice show impaired fungal clearance and elevated susceptibility (69). Real-time imaging has demonstrated that C5aR is necessary for coordinated neutrophil responses to Cryptococcus neoformans (70). In A. fumigatus infection, the absence of C5aR1 impairs neutrophil recruitment and fungal killing, leading to higher mortality (71). Together, these studies illustrate how anaphylatoxin signaling contributes to both protective and potentially pathogenic responses and underscore the need for developing targeted therapies.

Fungi have evolved various mechanisms to evade complement activity. A. fumigatus secretes proteases such as Alp1 that hydrolyze C3, C4, and C5, interrupting all 3 pathways of activation and impairing neutrophil recruitment (72). Similarly, C. albicans secretes Pra1 that binds to and inhibits C3 and its derivatives, disrupting opsonization and activation of immune cells (73). Fungal surfaces can also recruit host complement regulators to shut off activation. Factor H and FHL-1 binding by A. fumigatus conidia block C3b deposition and convertase formation (74), while C. albicans encode proteins such as Gpm1 to inhibit alternative pathway activation (75). Some fungal components activate and resist complement. Melanin from C. neoformans triggers the alternative pathway but inhibits terminal complex formation, inducing a pro-inflammatory response instead of causing lysis (76). During infections in the brain, local complement production by glial cells enhances antifungal defense in the absence of systemic circulation, providing a line of innate defense in immune-privileged tissues (77).

Clinical and translational implications

Although essential for defense, excessive activation of the complement leads to immunopathology. C5a has been implicated in contributing to cytokine storm-like responses in systemic fungal infections (78). Long-term complement inhibition with eculizumab is associated with a raised risk of invasive fungal infections, highlighting the protective role of the terminal pathway (79). Serum concentrations of C5a have proved prognostically valuable for invasive candidiasis, offering a biomarker for the stratification of disease severity in immunocompromised hosts (80). Genome-wide clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats screening has revealed that C3aR activity is pivotal in macrophage fungal capture, facilitating cytoskeletal rearrangement and efficient phagocytosis (81). C5aR1 signaling has been proposed as a therapeutic target with dual potential: enhancing clearance or, if overactive, contributing to tissue damage (68).

Parasitic Infections

The classical and lectin pathways of the complement cascade directly counter many parasitic infections. Complement opsonizes parasitic membranes and forms the MAC, promoting Ab production and immunological memory. While the downstream complement anaphylatoxin C5a triggers inflammation crucial for host defense, it can also enhance the pathologic consequences of parasitic infections. For instance, in experimental cutaneous leishmaniasis, it has been demonstrated that C5a/C5aR1 signaling contributes to neutrophil recruitment into secondary lymphoid tissues via L-selectin and integrin-dependent extravasation through draining lymph node vasculature (82). In the context of helminth infections, C5a also plays a protective role; a murine model of Nippostrongylus brasiliensis infection showed that C5a signaling is essential for early eosinophil and neutrophil recruitment to the skin following larval challenge, as demonstrated using the C5a receptor antagonist PMX53. These findings underscore the importance of C5a-mediated leukocyte recruitment in mounting an effective immune response to nematodes (83). Nevertheless, numerous parasitic nematodes secrete proteolytic carboxypeptidases that degrade and inactivate C3a and C5a, thereby skewing the host immune response and resulting in near-complete abolition of the chemotactic immune cell infiltration (84).

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN AUTOIMMUNE DISEASE

The complement anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a have also been shown to be potent immunomodulators in autoimmune disease, participating in the acute inflammation and chronicity and organ specificity of immune-mediated injury. In SLE, increased plasma levels of C3a and C5a have long been associated with disease exacerbation and immune complex deposition. Early accounts established a routine association of low CH50 and C3/C4 levels with heightened clinical activity, and later work confirmed that surges in C3a and C5a correlate with disease flare-ups, particularly lupus nephritis (85). More recent evidence indicates C3aR expression is upregulated in glomeruli of patients with active nephritis, particularly on podocytes and endothelial cells, suggesting that local C3a–C3aR signaling may contribute directly to renal injury (86).

Histopathologic, serologic, and urinary data position the anaphylatoxins as central amplifiers of inflammation and tissue remodeling in lupus. Both C3a and C5a are chemoattractants and activators of neutrophils and monocytes, while also upregulating adhesion molecules and promoting endothelial activation (87). C5aR1, in particular, has been implicated in recruiting inflammatory leukocytes and forming NETs within glomeruli (88). These insights have fueled interest in complement-based therapeutics. Whereas belimumab, a B-cell activating factor-targeting mAb, is not itself a direct complement inhibitor, its therapeutic effect in SLE has been shown to correlate with reductions in complement-activation markers, including C3a and C5a, positioning these fragments as downstream indicators of treatment response (89). Moreover, new approaches using an anti-C5aR1 Ab–protamine–C5 small interfering RNA (siRNA) conjugate was effective in reducing arthritis disease activity by 83% along with marked decrease in C3 deposition, and infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages in the joints of mice with collagen Ab-induced arthritis (90). These data demonstrated the efficacy of conjugated therapy with anti-C5 siRNAs and anti-C5aR1 Ab arthritis bearing mice and projects potential clinical outcome in RA patients.

Interestingly, broader overviews of complement in RA note that disease severity may reflect levels of complement activation and the decreased efficacy of regulatory mechanisms such as factor H, CD59, and other decay-accelerating proteins (91). Activation of the complement in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) also appears to be pathogenic. C3a and C5a levels are higher in primary APS patients, particularly in thrombosis and triple Ab-positive neutrophils (92), suggesting that anaphylatoxins may be responsible for endothelial activation and hypercoagulability, highlighting the proposition that blockade of the complement could be an adjuvant to anticoagulation in severe APS.

Recent studies have illuminated a cell-intrinsic role for anaphylatoxins in human adaptive immunity. In T cells, C3 is intracellularly cleaved by cathepsin L to yield C3a, which stimulates C3aR1 in an autocrine fashion. This process is pivotal to human Th1 differentiation and depends upon CD46 costimulation, revealing a noncanonical complement system acting within lymphocytes (93). These findings extend the range of C3a/C5a beyond circulating effectors to intracellular signaling molecules that determine the fate of immune cells. Together, these studies demonstrate that complement anaphylatoxins are not merely byproducts of activation but are active participants in shaping autoimmune inflammation. Whether through direct effects on resident tissue cells, recruitment and activation of immune effectors, or modulation of adaptive responses within lymphoid organs, the C3a and C5a signaling axes have a central and multifaceted role in autoimmunity. These clinical and translational observations explain pathogenesis and provide novel targets for therapeutic intervention.

Animal models

Experimental models have been instrumental in defining the mechanistic roles of complement anaphylatoxins in autoimmune disease. Genetic deletion of C3aR or pharmacologic blockade of C5aR1 significantly prevented proteinuria, glomerular leukocyte infiltration, and immune complex deposition in MRL/lpr mice, demonstrating that receptor engagement is critical for disease development in the kidney (94,95). These findings confirm that anaphylatoxins mediate their effects locally within end-organ tissues and identify their receptors as targets for therapy. In anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) glomerulonephritis, C5aR1 knockout (KO) mice have preserved renal function, reduced neutrophil activation, and polarization of the immune response towards Tregs (96). C5aR1’s role in promoting disease is not limited to innate immunity; signaling of C5aR1 on dendritic cells was shown to be necessary for local Ag presentation and costimulation, supporting tissue-destructive responses by MPO-specific CD4+ T cells (97). Transferring these T cells into immunodeficient hosts causes crescentic glomerulonephritis, further supporting a model in which complement activates autoreactive T cells and enhances tissue-specific inflammation (Fig. 2) (98).

In a model of epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, examining the effector phases of autoimmune skin disease, elimination of C5aR2—a receptor formerly considered a decoy—led to reduced neutrophil recruitment and disease severity, in part mediated by increased expression of the inhibitory FcγRIIb on neutrophils (99). Thus, anaphylatoxin activity in tissue microenvironments—not just in the circulation—can shape disease outcomes.

A unifying concept among models is cell-intrinsic generation and response to anaphylatoxins in adaptive immune cells. Within germinal centers, signaling via C3aR and C5aR1 on B cells has been shown to regulate affinity maturation, class switch recombination, and plasma cell differentiation through mTOR and CD40 pathway regulation (100). Mice lacking both receptors have defective IgG responses and germinal center organization, revealing a critical role for autocrine complement in humoral immunity (101). Similar findings have been reported in T cell-mediated models of autoimmune uveitis, where C3aR and C5aR were required for complete polarization of Th1 and Th17 cells and induction of retinal inflammation (102). These effects extend to models of Ab-mediated autoimmunity. In a mouse model of type II hypersensitivity, T cell-derived C3a and C5a were required for enabling effector responses via Fcγ receptor activation on myeloid cells (103). Together, these studies establish that complement anaphylatoxins do not merely orchestrate acute inflammation—they are key molecular bridges between innate and adaptive immunity, modulating T cell differentiation, B cell maturation, and end-organ injury in diverse autoimmune contexts.

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN CHRONIC INFLAMMATORY AND METABOLIC DISEASES

Lung disease

The lung is particularly vulnerable to the effects of complement anaphylatoxins, due to its constant exposure to inhaled pathogens, allergens, and toxins. Dysregulated complement activation, primarily through the C3a and C5a pathways, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several chronic inflammatory lung diseases, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and pulmonary hypertension (Figs. 2 and 3).

In acute severe asthma, C3a plasma levels were elevated and correlated with disease severity (104). Additionally, lung tissue from patients who died of lethal asthma exhibited considerable upregulation of C3aR and C5aR, particularly in airway epithelial cells and immune cells (105). The overexpression of these receptors indicates the likely involvement of anaphylatoxins in the pathogenesis of severe, refractory asthma. In contrast, the complement regulator CD46 induces autophagy in bronchial epithelial cells, protecting them from oxidative stress-induced apoptosis, a process relevant in both normal and asthmatic airways (106). In addition, human lung tissue from patients with allergic inflammation displays enhanced production of C3a and C5a, which further supports their roles in eosinophil infiltration and Th2 immune response activation (107).

Complement activation also plays a role in COPD. Serum levels of C3a, C4a, and C5a are elevated in stable COPD and asthma patients compared to controls and correlate with disease severity (108). During acute exacerbations, the level of C5a further rises, implicating its role as both a biomarker and a mediator of acute flares (109). Elevated C5a in COPD patients’ serum and BALF was also strongly associated with inflammation markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and IL-8 (110). Overall, these findings suggest that C5a is a key link between complement activation and the chronic systemic and airway inflammation characteristic of COPD.

In IPF, early and terminal complement pathways appear to contribute to disease pathogenesis. Deposition of the MAC (C5b-9) has been reported in fibrotic lung tissue, implicating terminal complement activation in epithelial injury and fibrogenesis (111). Complement anaphylatoxin receptors are also upregulated in IPF lungs, where activation of C3aR and C5aR promotes fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, most likely via TGF-β-dependent mechanisms (112). Persistent microbial infections like H. influenzae have also been implicated in IPF, with chronic complement activation and immune evasion potentially acting as drivers of ongoing epithelial injury and fibrotic remodeling (46). In pulmonary hypertension, complement activation is partly driven through immunoglobulin-mediated mechanisms. This activation plays a role in vascular remodeling through C3aR and C5aR signaling in animal models and human tissues (113,114).

Animal models

In asthma, C3aR KO mice exhibit significantly reduced airway hyperresponsiveness, eosinophilic inflammation, and Th2 cytokine generation, confirming the role of the C3a-C3aR axis in allergic airway inflammation (37,115). Similarly, mice lacking C5aR show enhanced Th2 responses, indicating a more intricate regulatory role of C5a, which may suppress rather than enhance components of allergic inflammation (116). At the same time, expression studies found C3aR and C5aR on bronchial epithelial cells and smooth muscle cells in models of asthma and sepsis in mice, suggesting direct anaphylatoxin actions on lung tissue (117). In mice exposed to hyperoxic conditions, C5aR blockade can significantly reduce leukocyte infiltration, cytokine release, and tissue damage, suggesting a role for C5aR in the pathophysiology of sterile lung injury (118). Receptor expression mapping in rat and murine models has also corroborated the upregulation of C3aR and C5aR in inflamed lung tissue, particularly within epithelial and immune cell subsets (119). These experimental studies emphasize the context-dependent, occasionally opposing effects of C3a and C5a in lung disease. Altogether, they establish complement anaphylatoxins as biomarkers and therapeutic targets across the spectrum of chronic lung diseases.

Kidney disease

The kidney is especially susceptible to complement-mediated injury due to its high vascular permeability, dense capillary networks, and continual exposure to circulating immune complexes. Complement anaphylatoxins, notably C3a and C5a, are increasingly known to play pivotal roles in governing the pathogenesis and progression of many kidney diseases, from autoimmune glomerulopathies to diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and chronic interstitial nephritis. Their role extends beyond innate immune surveillance, contributing to fibrosis, podocyte injury, and microvascular dysfunction via direct and indirect mechanisms.

Upregulation of anaphylatoxin receptors, in renal tubular and glomerular cells, is observed to augment inflammatory signaling and increase renal injury. C5a, for example, has been linked to inducing thrombosis in patients undergoing hemodialysis (120), whereas C3a/C5a-induced inflammation has been proposed as a driver of chronic kidney disease-associated immune activation (121). DKD has been a particularly fertile ground for deciphering complement-mediated injury. Several studies report increased C3a/C5a levels in DKD patients, correlating with disease severity (122). Intracellular complement system activation and engagement with metabolic and fibrotic pathways are emerging as essential aspects of DKD pathophysiology that further emphasize therapeutic potential of C5aR inhibition and modulation of downstream metabolic signaling (123). In autoimmune nephropathies, Gao et al. (124) demonstrated that C3a/C3aR activation promotes podocyte injury in primary membranous nephropathy, once again establishing the link between complement and glomerular damage. Similarly, Perkovic et al. (125) reported successful alternative pathway blockade with iptacopan (brand name Fabhalta®), an oral factor B inhibitor, in IgA nephropathy—a significant translational advancement. Fibrosis and complement-mediated tissue remodeling are discussed in overall reviews of renal disease progression (126).

Animal models

In diabetic nephropathy murine models, C5a was shown to drive mitochondrial damage in renal cells, leading to energetic breakdown and tissue damage (127). C3a and C5a differentially regulated tubulointerstitial damage in complement-induced nephropathy (128), while in ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) models, these anaphylatoxins exacerbated damage and triggered fibrosis (129,130). Furthermore, Poppelaars et al. (131) showed C5aR2 deficiency diminished I/R injury, whereas Li et al. (132) found that C3aR and C5aR antagonists diminished endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. It was demonstrated that C3aR inhibition saved podocytes in models of diabetic nephropathy, and that CRP suppressed C3a/C3aR-dependent autophagy in DKD (133,134). Other models highlight the inflammatory and fibrotic cascade triggered by anaphylatoxins. C3a triggered epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in proteinuric nephropathy (135), while macrophage-derived C3 promoted IL-17A–mediated fibrosis (136). Complement activation was also shown to mediate renal fibrosis through Akt signaling and EndoMT (137). Earlier studies linked complement component C3 to acid-induced chronic tubulointerstitial disease and validated the fibrogenic role of C5 in tubulointerstitial fibrosis (138). Lastly, in autoimmune-prone MRL/lpr mice, C5aR deficiency reduced T cell-mediated renal injury, validating the role of complement in activating the adaptive immune response in lupus nephritis (139).

Gastrointestinal (GI) tract

Complement activation is increasingly recognized as a central player in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel diseases, including ulcerative colitis (UC). C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins contribute to mucosal inflammation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and immune cell recruitment in the gut. Histopathological studies of UC patients have revealed C3b and terminal complement complex deposition on colonic epithelium, thus directly implicating complement in tissue injury (140). Translational research also supports this notion: intestinal epithelial cells are not merely bystanders but active producers of complement proteins. Inflammatory activation induces colonic epithelial cells to amplify the generation of C3, suggesting that complement from epithelial cells can augment local inflammation (141). Mechanistic in vitro experiments have shown that C3a primes human colonic epithelial cells through Gαi signaling cascades to release pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-8 (142). Together, these findings imply that complement responds to and sustains mucosal inflammation via autocrine and paracrine loops with persistent cytokine signaling in ulcerative colitis. These phenomena are not limited to the colon: studies of cholestatic liver injury also highlight complement’s role in macrophage recruitment and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (143), suggesting a systemic role in mucosal organ inflammation.

Animal models

Murine models of colitis provide direct evidence of anaphylatoxin-mediated pathology. In C3aR-deficient mice with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis, there is significantly decreased colonic inflammation, highlighting the receptor’s role in immune cell infiltration and cytokine production (144). Johswich et al. (145) further demonstrated that deficiency in C5aR reduced the severity of both acute and chronic DSS colitis models, with marked decreases in neutrophil accumulation and tissue damage.

The complement system appears to have a dual function in the gut, driving inflammation while also participating in mucosal repair. In C3-deficient mice, Schepp-Berglind et al. (146) observed protection against acute DSS-induced injury but also noted that its total absence may be unfavorable to certain healing processes. Targeting specific pathways may thus be better than general inhibition. Elvington et al. (147) showed that modulation of the alternative complement pathway using complement receptor (CR) 2-fH in chronic DSS colitis improved clinical outcomes and reduced histological damage, supporting a strategy of selective pathway regulation. Complement’s action is not confined to the colon. In a mouse model of cholestatic liver injury, complement inhibition reduced macrophage infiltration and cytokine production, again illustrating the role of complement anaphylatoxins in regulating mucosal immune responses across GI tissue (143).

Skin

Complement activation plays a pathogenic role across multiple inflammatory skin diseases, including psoriasis, urticaria, bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus vulgaris, lupus-associated skin lesions, and hidradenitis suppurativa. Locally generated and serum-derived anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a recruit neutrophils and other immune cells, trigger mast cell degranulation, and stimulate keratinocytes to release pro-inflammatory mediators, driving epidermal hyperplasia and barrier disruption. Keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and infiltrating leukocytes can all produce complement components, sustaining inflammation within the skin microenvironment (148). Given these mechanisms, blockade of C3aR or C5aR represents a promising therapeutic strategy for complement-driven dermatoses.

Animal models

Experimental psoriasis suggested a protective role of C3a–C3aR signaling, in which C3aR activation on human keratinocytes inhibited proliferation and decreased expression of psoriatic epidermis markers keratin 6, 16, and 17 (149). In allergic and atopic dermatitis models, anaphylatoxin signaling plays an amplifying role. Mice with defective C3a receptors exhibited reduced sensitization of Th2 and inflammation in allergic contact dermatitis, implicating a role for C3aR in the priming of the adaptive immune system (150). Moreover, a lack of the C5a decoy receptor, C5L2 (aka C5aR2), led to exaggerated inflammation, indicating that endogenous buffering of C5a is vital for immune homeostasis (151). It was also demonstrated that C5aR activation in atopic dermatitis in mice promoted epidermal hyperplasia and infiltration of mast cells, and inhibition of this axis alleviated disease severity (152). Together, these mechanistic studies support a model wherein C3a and C5a drive inflammation by activating keratinocytes, recruiting immune cells, and amplifying cytokines. At the same time, dysregulation or loss of receptor control can propel the skin into chronic disease states.

Musculoskeletal (osteoarthritis)

Beyond their classical roles in immune surveillance, complement anaphylatoxins are progressively implicated as active participants in osteoarthritis (OA) pathophysiology. Both C3a and C5a and their respective receptors are expressed in synovial tissues and cartilage, contributing to local inflammation, chondrocyte hypertrophy, and matrix degradation (153). These mediators amplify the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, accelerating cartilage breakdown.

Recent mechanistic studies implicate C3aR and C5aR1 signaling in the mediation of chondrocyte calcification, a hallmark of late-stage OA. In vitro and ex vivo studies show that complement activation promotes mineral deposition and phenotypic changes in chondrocytes consistent with disease progression (154). Genetic and pharmacologic blockades of the complement cascade in OA animal models have been shown to mitigate joint pathology, confirming a central role for complement and its associated anaphylatoxins in OA pathology (155). These observations highlight the therapeutic potential of C3a/C5a signaling as a disease-modifying strategy in OA.

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN CARDIOVASCULAR AND METABOLIC ADVERSITIES

Complement activation is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to the chronic low-grade inflammation that underpins cardiometabolic disorders. Broadly, C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins exert chemoattractant and immunomodulatory effects across adipose tissue, liver, vasculature, and heart, suggestive of their central role in so-called metaflammation—a chronic inflammatory response to metabolic maladaptation (156) In humans, C3a and C5a concentrations are linked to insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia, emphasizing the dual role of complement as cardiometabolic stress sensor and amplifier (Fig. 4) (157,158). Evidence from patient populations suggests a pathogenic role for complement in diabetes and its vascular complications. Elevated levels of C3a have been observed in states of insulin resistance, while downstream effects on glucose metabolism, lipid control, and inflammatory processes within adipose tissue become more pronounced with complement dysregulation. In the eye, diabetic retinopathy has been associated with complement component upregulation within the retinal microvasculature, positioning anaphylatoxins as potential mediators for tissue damage in end-organ disease (159).

C5aR1 signaling in metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases. Under infectious conditions, such as P. aeruginosa that forms biofilms in CF lungs, leads to binding of C3 and subsequent generation of C5a leads to neutrophil flux that exacerbates chronic inflammation. In Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, GPI deficiency caused by somatic mutations in PIGA (which encodes phosphatidylinositol N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase subunit A) results in deficiency of GPI-anchored complement regulator, CD55 and CD59 glycoproteins. The functional loss of these regulators in PNH erythrocytes leads to sustained generation of C5a and C5aR1-mediated proinflammatory signaling that may contribute to red cell lysis, thrombosis, and increased risk of mortality. Both microglia and astrocytes constitutively express C5aR1. In ALS, reactive astrocytes with upregulated C5aR1 signaling promote neuroinflammation via the key chemokine CXCL1-mediated neutrophil chemotaxis. Upregulation of C5aR1 signaling is evident in β-amyloid plaque-associated microglia that promotes neuroinflammatory response and aberrant synaptic pruning in AD. C5a–C5aR1 signaling mediated endothelial-B/T cell interaction and activation contribute to vascular remodeling in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. C5a–C5aR1-mediated skewing of macrophages to proinflammatory M1 phenotype increases inflammatory cytokines IL-6, MCP-1, and TNF-α production resulting in adipose tissue inflammation and insulin resistance. C5a–C5aR1-mediated impaired endothelial function also promotes systemic hypertension via release of endothelin-1, a potent vasoconstrictor (Created in http://Biorender.com).

Within the vasculature, complement activity is evident in both the development and progression of atherosclerosis. C3a and C5a receptors have been identified on human atherosclerotic plaques and coronary endothelial cells (160), and C5a in particular has been proposed as a biomarker of cardiovascular risk (161). In coronary artery disease patients, C5a receptor expression on circulating platelets is elevated (162), and in those with heart failure, plasma levels of C3a predict clinical outcomes independently of conventional risk factors (163). Human research also shows that C3 and its active fragment C3a may be differentially linked with cardiometabolic traits like atherosclerosis and lipid metabolism (157). Complement C5a receptors C5aR1 and C5aR2 signaling is also implicated in vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, with C5a driving activation of vascular endothelium in patients with atherosclerosis (164,165,166). Consistent with these findings, an inverse correlation has also been reported between plasma C5a and the burden of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (LDL) bound to circulating platelets, indicating a potential compensatory regulatory loop (167). Mechanistically, C3a and C5a stimulate vascular endothelial cell migration and modulate B- and T-cell activation, suggesting direct contributions to immune remodeling within the vessel wall (168). Activation of the complement system and C5a signaling in stenotic aortic valves contributes to sustained chronic inflammation (169).

Besides atherosclerosis and diabetes, complement plays a role in uric acid crystal-induced inflammation, a hallmark characteristic of gout. C5a enhances IL-1β production by monocytes exposed to monosodium urate, promoting a cascade of neutrophilic inflammation (170). Pivotal research also demonstrated that urate crystals directly initiate complement activation in serum through the classical pathway with subsequent anaphylatoxin release. Natural Abs and CRP have together been shown to drive C3a and C5a production in response to urate crystals, solidifying the link between innate opsonins and complement activation in the context of sterile inflammation (171).

Animal models

In a rat model of diet-induced obesity, antagonism of both C3aR and C5aR improved metabolic dysfunction, prevented macrophage recruitment, and improved glucose tolerance (172). These findings are complemented by experiments in mice showing that C5aR promoted adipose inflammation and insulin resistance, while C3aR is implicated in macrophage-mediated adipose tissue remodeling (173,174). In models of atherosclerosis, C5aR signaling promoted angiotensin II–mediated cardiac remodeling and hypertrophy (175), whereas C5a directly induced plaque disruption, resulting in accelerated atherothrombosis in apolipoprotein E-deficient mice (176). C3aR also enhanced platelet activation and thrombus formation in mice (177). The role of anaphylatoxins in blood pressure control was examined in murine models of hypertension. Mice deficient in C5aR2 are protected against angiotensin II-induced hypertension and exhibit reduced cardiac fibrosis and kidney injury (178). Models of gout also demonstrate the role of complement signaling in sterile inflammation. C5aR1 is required for neutrophil recruitment and IL-1β production in urate crystal-induced peritonitis, and neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicles were shown to resolve inflammation by interfering with C5a-mediated inflammasome priming (179,180). Mouse models were also used to investigate the role of complement in lipid metabolism. Barbu et al. (181) have characterized the control of lipid profiles and hepatic fat storage by the complement factor C3 in mice, offering a mechanistic link between immune signaling and metabolic homeostasis. Finally, the binding of C3a to oxidized LDL and apoptotic cells, both major constituents of plaque microenvironments, has been further confirmed using molecular assays and mouse models (182).

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES

The activation of the complement system is progressively recognized as a key mediator of tissue remodeling and neuroinflammation in a multitude of neurodegenerative diseases. Among its various effectors, the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a have emerged to play a role in triggering microglial activation, blood-brain barrier disruption, synaptic degeneration, and neuronal death (Fig. 4).

In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), elevated levels of C3a and C5a, along with their receptors, have been consistently observed in patient tissues. Elevated levels of serum C5a and the terminal complement complex C5b-9 have also been shown in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) patients, suggesting that systemic complement dysregulation may extend beyond AD (183). Activation of complement is also seen in multiple sclerosis (MS), where C3aR and C5aR1 expressions have been correlated with cortical grey matter lesions and focal brain inflammation (184). In addition, complement-induced endothelial barrier dysfunction and vascular inflammation have been linked to C3aR signaling in aging populations, supporting a role for complement in age-related neurovascular decline (185). This expands the understanding of how systemic immune activation may exacerbate or initiate complement cascades in the brain.

The complement components C1q, C3a, and C5a are involved in regulating glial cell activation, synaptic reorganization, and neuronal survival. Specifically, the role of complement in AD pathogenesis has been identified with emphasis on complement activation’s downstream consequences to synapse loss and amplification of neuroinflammation (186). Therapeutic targeting of complement components such as C1q, C3aR, and C5aR1 is proposed as disease-modifying interventions in AD neuromuscular and demyelinating illnesses, noting the growing clinical interest in complement inhibitors in neurologic illness like myasthenia gravis and MS (187).

Functional studies of human tissues and cells have offered mechanistic clues. In patients with AD, C5a has been shown to cause neuroinflammation by enhancing microglial reactivity to amyloid-β, increasing the release of pro-inflammatory mediators (188). C5aR1 is induced in ALS motor neurons as well, once again linking this axis to long-term neuroinflammatory disease (189). Parkinson’s disease, and dementia with Lewy bodies showed increased C3 breakdown product iC3b deposition in the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra with the severity of the disease, whereas decreased iC3b deposition in neuromelanin-containing catecholaminergic neurons was associated with neuroprotective effects of normal aging (190). Together, these human and in vitro findings reinforce that anaphylatoxin signaling is a shared mechanism underpinning several distinct neurodegenerative pathologies.

Animal models

In models of AD, pharmacologic inhibition of C5aR1 or gene deletion of C5aR1 reduced microglial activation and cognitive deficits, proposing a pathogenic role for C5a signaling in synapse damage and neuroinflammation (191,192). Furthermore, modulation of C3aR has been shown to regulate glial function as indicated by the neuroprotective effects of TLQP-21 via C3aR1 signaling (193). Mouse models have also revealed that C3a and C5a are involved in synaptic pruning and cognitive dysfunction in amyloidosis and tauopathy (194,195,196). In tauopathy models, C3aR deficiency reduced tau protein burden and restored immune homeostasis (197), while in amyloid models, lack of C3aR signaling remedied metabolic dysfunction and enhanced microglial activation (198).

Paradoxically, neuroprotective effects of C5a were shown to inhibit caspase-3 activity through MAP kinase signaling and reduce glutamate-induced excitotoxicity in vitro and in vivo models (199,200). C5a also enhanced GluR2 receptor expression, offering protection against neuronal injury (200). In vitro models show that C3a promotes migration and differentiation of neural progenitor cells (201), while mice without C3aR exhibit abnormal brain morphology and hyperactivity (202). Further linking complement to neurovascular dysfunction, C3aR activation was shown to promote white matter injury following cerebral hypoperfusion (203).

Complement also appears to be a key conduit linking peripheral immune stimuli to central pathology. In a mouse model, the outer membrane vesicles of Helicobacter pylori have been found to activate the C3–C3aR axis and cause neuroinflammation mimicking AD pathology, showing a mechanistic link between gut microbiota and brain dysfunction (204). In Parkinson’s disease models, astrocyte-neuron communication through the C3a–C3aR pathway has been implicated in neurodegeneration (205,206). These studies demonstrate that complement-driven signaling loops involving astrocytes and microglia can propagate neuroinflammation and neuronal loss. ALS models have also implicated C5aR signaling in motor neuron degeneration. In rat models, C5aR blockade prolonged survival and reduced inflammation (191), and in genetically modified NFL−/− mice, there was a significant upregulation of C5aR1 in vulnerable neurons (189). Pain hypersensitivity and neuroinflammatory responses have also been studied through the lens of complement. In spinal cord models, C5a was shown to activate microglia and contribute to chronic pain behaviors (207). These findings extend the impact of complement signaling beyond neurodegeneration to broader neuroimmune processes. Collectively, these models illuminate the pleiotropic roles of C3a and C5a in the CNS—regulating inflammation, neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and neurovascular homeostasis. Whether acting as drivers of pathology or transient protectors, anaphylatoxins offer compelling targets for precise therapeutic modulation in neurodegenerative diseases.

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN CANCER

While historically understood as constituents of innate immune defense, C3a and C5a also shape the tumor microenvironment in ways that favor oncogenesis, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and metastasis. The dual role of complement in cancer has long been documented, where the activation of classical pathways results in immunosurveillance, while downstream C3a and C5a often support tumor-promoting processes (208). These context-dependent effects are further observed in studies demonstrating how C3a and C5a recruit immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Tregs (209). Mechanistic insights into these processes have shown that activation of C3aR and C5aR1 on immune and stromal cells drives tumor proliferation, angiogenesis via VEGF pathways, and immune evasion through PD-L1 overexpression (210,211). The tumor-promoting effects of complement anaphylatoxins (CAs) also include re-shaping the cytokine landscape to favor immune tolerance (212). These findings have cast C3a and C5a firmly within the context of cancer pathogenesis effectors alongside the traditional oncogenic pathways. This shift in paradigm has led to the reframing of complement as an “unwelcome” but integral part of tumor biology, highlighting the need for targeted inhibition of complement effectors (213,214).

Lung cancer

Lung cancer, particularly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), is characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment where complement effectors are increasingly engaged. CD88 (C5aR1) overexpression in NSCLC tumors has been associated with vascular invasion and poor prognosis, suggesting its role as both biomarker and effector (215). C3a plays a role in modulation of macrophage polarization to a pro-tumor phenotype and promotion of angiogenesis (216). Autocrine stimulation of complement has been identified as a mechanism whereby lung cancer cells release C5a, promoting their invasiveness independent of canonical pathways (217). Complement activity is also of therapeutic importance. Cetuximab, a monoclonal anti-epidermal growth factor receptor Ab used in NSCLC, relies partially on complement activation for its effect; inhibition of complement reduces its antitumor efficacy (218).

In murine models, C5a supports tumor growth by promoting MDSC infiltration and suppressing cytotoxic lymphocytes (219). C3aR signaling has been shown to skew CD4+ T cell differentiation toward immunosuppressive phenotypes, limiting antitumor immunity (220). Concurrent PD-1 and C5aR1 inhibition increases in vivo tumor elimination (221). In models of lung cancer bone metastasis, C5aR1 blockade reduces metastatic burden by inhibiting CXCL16-mediated signaling (222,223). C5a also directly promotes squamous carcinogenesis and inhibits T cell-mediated responses to chemotherapy in mouse models, suggesting a broader role for complement in epithelial tumor immune evasion (224).

Kidney cancer

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), particularly the clear cell subtype, exhibits significant immune modulation within the tumor microenvironment, and emerging evidence implicates complement anaphylatoxins in its pathogenesis and progression. Clinical data have shown that elevated serum C5a levels are associated with poor prognosis, including reduced overall survival and increased tumor stage, pointing to C5a as both prognostic marker and potential driver of disease (225).

Complement activation also appears to crosstalk with known oncogenic and inflammatory mediators. For example, overexpression of MUC1 in RCC tumors has been shown to promote complement activation and reorganize immune infiltration by augmenting neutrophils and M2-like macrophages that provide a tumor-promoting immune microenvironment (226). Likewise, PTX3 overexpression in RCC also mediates complement function and is associated with increased immunosuppressive cytokine production and adverse clinical outcomes (227). In broader mechanistic analyses, the complement system is emerging as a critical player in RCC, with C3a and C5a driving both inflammation and immune escape. These insights underscore the dual role of complement in RCC pathobiology and identify it as a therapeutic target (228).

Animal models further substantiate this role. Expression of C5aR1 in RCC models increases pro-tumor signaling and is linked to immune evasion mechanisms, while inhibition of complement restores cytotoxic immune function and slows tumor progression (229). Together, these data support an emerging model in which complement anaphylatoxins play a central role in shaping RCC progression through both immune regulation and stromal remodeling.

Breast cancer

Persistent activation of C3a and C5a receptors signaling has been reported on the surface of breast tumor cells, suggesting that dysregulated complement activation may be a feature of breast cancer biology. Higher concentrations of serum C3a and C5a in breast cancer patients have also been correlated with more aggressive tumor features, indicating that they may be potential biomarkers (230). Mechanistically, C5a has been shown to promote the growth of breast cancer cells through activating Akt signaling and eliciting RGC-32, a cell cycle advancement and tumorigenesis gene (231). Such observations position the C5a-C5aR1 pathway as a powerful proliferative stimulus in the breast tumor microenvironment. Moreover, C5a fusion to human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted mAbs enhances the cytotoxic effect against HER2-positive breast cancer cells in vitro, indicating its capability to enhance immunotherapeutic approaches (232).

Preclinical models also support the immunosuppressive role of complement in breast cancer. In a murine mammary tumor model, tumor-bearing mice exhibit altered monocyte function early in disease progression, reflecting complement-mediated reprogramming of innate immune cells to a tolerogenic phenotype (233). These findings emphasize that complement not only influences tumor cell proliferation but also helps shape the systemic immune response in ways that support tumor progression.

GI and colorectal cancers

Elevated systemic complement activation has been observed in patients with colorectal cancer, where increased serum levels of C3a may serve as a non-invasive diagnostic biomarker (234). Similarly, in esophageal cancer, C3a has been proposed as a potential early detection marker based on its elevated expression in affected patients (235). Mechanistic studies support a direct tumor-promoting role for C3a and C5a in GI cancers. Pathologic expression of C5aR1 in gastric cancer cells enhances motility and invasiveness by activating downstream signaling pathways, including ERK1/2 (236). C5aR1 expression is also linked to vascular invasion and poor patient prognosis in clinical specimens, again pointing to its potential prognostic significance (237). Besides these receptor-level effects, complement dysregulation also impinges on broader oncogenic cascades of signaling. For instance, C3 overexpression has been shown to activate the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in gastric cancer cells, promoting proliferation and tumorigenesis (238). The C5a–C5aR1 axis has also been involved in metabolic reprogramming in gastric tumors, particularly through iron metabolism pathways, contributing to tumor aggressiveness (239).

At the tissue level, bioinformatic analyses have associated complement function with immune infiltration and poorer prognosis in gastric and colon cancer, suggesting that anaphylatoxins can facilitate immune evasion and stromal remodeling (240). A recent meta-analysis further reinforces the clinical relevance of complement components in colorectal cancer, with several complement factors associated with elevated disease risk (241). In murine models of colon cancer, C5a has been shown to promote hepatic metastases by attracting inflammatory monocytes via monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1) signaling. Thus, complement activation may not only promote primary tumor growth but also facilitate metastatic colonization in distant organs such as the liver (242).

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

Complement activation plays a critical role in shaping the invasive and proliferative behavior of HCC cells. C3aR and C5aR1 signaling has been implicated in tumor progression through the modulation of important pathways such as ERK and EMT. Similarly, exposure to aristolochic acid I has been shown to promote migration and invasion of HCC cells through stimulation of the C3a/C3aR pathway, identifying complement signaling as a downstream mediator of environmental toxin-induced oncogenic behavior (243). Therefore, elevated serum C3a has been postulated as a biomarker for hepatitis C virus-related HCC, suggesting systemic complement activation may reflect tumor burden or progression (244). Cell-based models have observed downregulation of either C3aR or C5aR1 suppress proliferation and EMT, further validating the function of anaphylatoxins in regulating tumor growth and metastatic capability (245). Invasion through ERK1/2 signaling is also enhanced by C5aR1 activity in HCC cells, further implicating complement in intracellular signaling pathways that govern tumor aggressiveness (246).

Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is characterized by aggressive growth, early metastasis, and massive immune evasion. Emerging evidence implicates C3a and its receptor C3aR in modulating PDAC progression and therapy resistance. Clinical analysis has revealed enhanced levels of C3aR expression within tumor tissue, with association to EMT and poor prognostic outcome (247). In vitro studies have demonstrated that C3a–C3aR interaction triggers ERK signaling, promoting EMT and invasiveness of PDAC cells (248). C3a/C3aR activity has also been involved in reduced gemcitabine-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy sensitivity, highlighting the potential of complement inhibition to improve treatment (249).

Ovarian cancer

The complement system is also an important regulator of the ovarian tumor microenvironment, with a special interest in the roles played by C5a and its receptor C5aR1. Transcriptomic expression analysis of ovarian clear cell carcinoma revealed overexpression of complement-related pathways, including those involving C3a and C5a, implicating these effectors in tumor development and immune modulation (250). Functional enrichment analyses suggest that complement activation in ovarian cancer is associated with immunosuppressive signaling and increased inflammatory responses. In vivo studies have validated the tumor-promoting activities of C5a. Genetic KO and pharmacologic blockade of C5aR1 in mice caused reduced endothelial cell activation and impaired neovascularization, suggesting a role for C5a in driving angiogenesis (251). Other studies have shown that complement blockade enhances anti-tumor T cell responses, reinforcing the notion that CAs contribute to immune evasion in ovarian cancer (252).

Prostate cancer

Although research on complement anaphylatoxins in prostate cancer remains limited, emerging data suggest that these effectors contribute to a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment and disease progression. C3a, for instance, has been implicated in long-standing inflammation of the prostate, which may act as a precursor to malignancy. Elevated levels of C3a were observed in benign prostatic hyperplasia patients, suggesting that complement activation may have a greater role to play in prostate pathology (253). Pentraxin-3, a key regulator of complement activation, was found to be upregulated in prostate cancer tissue, where it altered local C3/C5 convertase activity and correlated with increased leukocyte recruitment (254). This highlights the role of endogenous regulators in dictating complement-driven inflammation within prostate tumors. These mechanisms, although still under-characterized in prostate cancer specifically, may offer future therapeutic targets as complement-directed interventions are refined (255).

Leukemia and lymphoma

Complement anaphylatoxins have been implicated in shaping hematologic malignancies, particularly leukemia and lymphoma, through their influence on immune cell behavior, bone marrow dynamics, and cytokine signaling. These roles span both early disease development and progression. Clinical evidence supports the involvement of complement effectors in disease progression and outcome. Overexpression of C3aR1 was observed in patients with acute myeloid leukemia, and higher levels of expression were linked with poorer short-term survival rates (256). Similarly, complement activation has also been linked with increased motility of leukemic cells, mediated in part by downregulation of the protective enzyme HO-1, suggesting a role in disease dissemination and aggressiveness (257).

Animal models have helped further elucidate the dualistic role of complement in hematologic malignancies. In one study, C5a was shown to exert opposing roles depending on tumor context: while it could promote tumor-supportive inflammation, C5a signaling also had tumor-suppressive effects in certain microenvironments, reflecting the complex interplay between immune activation and tolerance in leukemia and lymphoma (258). Altogether, these findings support a model in which complement anaphylatoxins contribute to leukemia and lymphoma pathogenesis by enhancing migratory potential, altering immune cell crosstalk, and influencing microenvironmental cues within hematopoietic tissues.

Brain cancers

Analysis of human glioblastomas revealed enhanced expression of C3a and its receptor C3aR, which was strongly correlated with key pro-tumorigenic pathways like TGF-β and VEGF signaling (259). Such findings support the role of complement in facilitating angiogenesis and immune evasion in the glioma microenvironment. Beyond tumor biology, complement activity may also contribute to neurological complications caused by brain cancer treatment. In a murine model of cranial radiation, pharmacologic blockade of C5aR1 reversed cognitive deficits, suggesting that complement signaling worsens radiation-induced neuroinflammation and neural dysfunction (260). Overall, these findings demonstrate dual roles for anaphylatoxins in glioma pathogenesis and therapy-evoked neurotoxicity.

Melanoma

In melanoma, the complement system has been shown to modulate immune evasion and progression, largely through the actions of anaphylatoxin receptors C3aR and C5aR1. Expression of these receptors on tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells is associated with altered inflammatory signaling and reduced anti-tumor immunity (261). These effects may contribute to melanoma’s resistance to immune surveillance and its capacity to metastasize. Experimental models reinforce these findings. In murine models, activation of C3aR was shown to promote melanoma tumorigenesis by impairing neutrophil infiltration and suppressing CD4+ T cell responses—2 key arms of anti-tumor immunity (262). These studies suggest that complement blockade may enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapies by reversing immune suppression within the melanoma microenvironment.

Osteosarcoma

In osteosarcoma, complement activation has been linked to pro-tumorigenic processes such as angiogenesis. In vitro experiments using osteosarcoma cell lines demonstrate that stimulation of the complement cascade enhances the production of angiogenic growth factors, thereby supporting a vascularized tumor microenvironment conducive to cancer progression (263). These findings suggest that anaphylatoxin signaling may facilitate tumor expansion by promoting neovascularization, although further in vivo validation is needed.

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN GENETIC DISORDERS

Sickle cell disease (SCD)

SCD is an autosomal recessive monogenic hemoglobinopathy that has, in the recent past, been causally linked to dysregulated complement activation as an inflammatory and vaso-occlusive pathogenic mechanism. Of the complement effectors, C5a has been recognized as a key mediator of endothelial injury, leukocyte recruitment, and microvascular stasis. Clinical research has illustrated enhanced complement activation in vaso-occlusive crises among children. Plasma concentrations of C5a and other components of the complement system are elevated in such environments and are associated with acute signs, implying a mechanistic correlation between anaphylatoxin signaling and the severity of the disease (264). Complement dysregulation has also been found to advance endothelial activation, neutrophil adhesion, and oxidative stress—all disease hallmarks of SCD. C5a-C5aR1 signaling is particularly efficacious in potentiating inflammatory cascades and vascular injury (265).

This role is also supported by evidence from animal models. In murine models of SCD, inhibition of C5 activation reduces complement deposition, inflammation, and microvascular stasis significantly (266). Complement blockade with MASP-2 and MASP-3 inhibitors to lectin pathway upstream of C5a generation also improves vascular function (267). Collectively, these studies point to complement anaphylatoxins as mechanistic drivers and potential therapeutic targets in SCD.

Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS)

aHUS is a rare, genetically driven thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by uncontrolled complement activation, particularly of the alternative pathway. Complement anaphylatoxins, especially C5a, are increasingly recognized as key contributors to the endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis seen in this disease. In vitro studies on human endothelial cells demonstrate that C5a–C5aR interaction promotes complement amplification and thrombosis, supporting its role in vascular injury (268). Clinical investigations have shown that complement C5 is essential for the development of aHUS, and blocking C5 activity mitigates disease onset and progression (269). Although the MAC is a principal effector in thrombotic microangiopathy, recent data suggest that C5a also contributes to immune cell recruitment and inflammation in human aHUS pathology (270).

In murine models bearing aHUS-associated factor H mutations, C5aR signaling has been shown to drive renal thrombotic microangiopathy, independently of MAC formation. Targeting C5aR in these models significantly reduced microvascular injury, reinforcing its role as a pathogenic mediator (271). Additionally, overlap with Shiga toxin-associated HUS has been noted, with complement activation, including generation of C3a and C5a, implicated in endothelial dysfunction and thrombosis (272). These findings highlight C5a and its receptor as potential therapeutic targets in complement-mediated hemolytic syndromes.

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH)

Deficiency of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored complement regulators (CD55 and CD59) on cells of PNH leads to uncontrolled activation of the complement system, resulting in both intravascular and extravascular hemolysis. Intravascular hemolysis is mediated by terminal complement complex (C5b-9), whereas upstream activation at the C3 level leads to opsonization of erythrocytes by fragments of C3 and ultimately to extravascular hemolysis via phagocytosis by monocytes and macrophages. Lin et al. (273) provided direct experimental evidence that C3dg, a breakdown product of C3b, facilitates erythrophagocytosis of PNH erythrocytes by interacting with CR3 on monocytes, highlighting the importance of C3 activation products in extravascular hemolysis in C5 inhibitor-treated patients. Risitano et al. (274) also demonstrated that inhibition of C3 activation by compstatin analogs prevents both hemolysis and C3 fragment deposition on PNH erythrocytes, further supporting the central role of C3 activation products in the pathophysiology of PNH.

While collectively these studies outline the pathogenic role of C3 activation products (especially C3b, iC3b, and C3dg) and the consequent effects of C5 cleavage (e.g., C5b-9 formation), few studies in the literature directly investigate the specific role of the soluble anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a in PNH. Current evidence supports the hypothesis that clinical manifestations of PNH, hemolysis and thrombosis, are mediated by cell-associated fragments of complement and membrane attack complex, and not by the anaphylatoxins themselves. However, the generation of C3a and C5a is an inevitable consequence of complement activation in PNH, and their pro-inflammatory and chemotactic properties may contribute to disease manifestations, although this has not been directly dissected in the current studies.

Cystic fibrosis (CF)

Clinical evidence has demonstrated augmented levels of C3a and C5a in CF BALF, with close correlations to markers of severity and lung function decline (275,276). Ex vivo CF respiratory secretion characterization revealed spontaneous and inducible activation of complement resulting in robust production of C5a and neutrophil chemotaxis. This hyperactive inflammatory cycle is additionally maintained by microbial colonization; for example, P. aeruginosa adaptation in CF airways has been shown to enhance C5a production and drive neutrophil recruitment. In addition, the 2 major exoproteases, alkaline protease A and elastase B of P. aeruginosa, contributed to C3 cleavage and further activation of complement cascade (277). Together, these findings position complement anaphylatoxins as disease severity biomarkers and as mechanistic drivers of chronic inflammation in CF.

Hereditary angioedema (HAE)

In HAE, there is dysregulation of the complement leading to acute localized edema due to increased vascular permeability. Longitudinal HAE attack profiling has shown dynamic variations in concentrations of C3a and C5a, with elevations closely correlating with symptom onset and severity (278). These findings suggest an active role for anaphylatoxins in the inflammatory cascade during HAE attacks and validate them as markers of disease activity.

C3 glomerulopathy (C3G)

C3G is a rare renal disease characterized by the presence of chronic complement activation, predominantly at the level of the C3 and C5 convertases. Pathogenesis is mediated through anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a through systemic as well as renal inflammatory mechanisms. Clinical and translational studies have established the occurrence of higher levels of C3a and C5a in C3G patients corresponding to disease severity as well as kidney impairment (279). Autoantibodies of C3G might modulate the activity of C3 and C5 convertases differentially, which may influence the balance of production of C3a and C5a and their downstream effects (280). In other patient populations, nephritic factor-induced hyperactivation of C5 increases production of C5a and inflammatory injury (281). In murine models, inhibition of C5 was found to inhibit renal failure and mortality, confirming a causal involvement of C5a and C5aR-initiated inflammation in disease progression in C3G (282). These mechanistic findings align with broader conceptual frameworks outlined in comprehensive reviews of C3G pathophysiology, where targeting C5a signaling is highlighted as a therapeutic strategy (283).

Complement component deficiencies (e.g., C3, C5 deficiency)

Studies of individuals with genetic deficiencies in core complement components provide insight into the physiological roles of anaphylatoxins in human inflammation. In C3- or C5-deficient individuals, systemic responses to inflammatory challenges are severely compromised, highlighting the pivotal roles of C3a and C5a in coordinating cytokine release, leukocyte migration, and vascular activation (284). These natural “experiments” highlight the indispensable contributions of complement anaphylatoxins to immune homeostasis, and the consequences of their absence.

COMPLEMENT ANAPHYLATOXINS IN CIRCADIAN RHYTHM SIGNALING

Mounting evidence implicates circadian regulation of the complement system, such as anaphylatoxin generation and activity. In healthy individuals, circulating C5a levels exhibit diurnal variation, with variations in both immune activity and concentration throughout the day. These oscillations control monocyte responses to C5a, implying temporal gating of anaphylatoxin signaling (13). Circadian regulation of complement gene expression has been observed in many immune tissues, and fluctuation in C3, C5, and receptor transcripts corresponds to well-established peaks of inflammation (285,286). Clock genes can synchronize cytokine response and leukocyte migration with complement activation such that the immune system can prepare for and regulate inflammation (12).

In disease models, the timing of complement activity appears to influence pathology. In pulmonary hypertension, circadian disruption promotes complement-mediated vascular injury, with C5a playing a major role as a rhythmic immune-endothelial interaction mediator (114). Animal models support this notion: time-of-day modulation of innate immunity alters susceptibility to infection, most likely by virtue of circadian regulation of such elements as C3a and C5a (287). Taken together, these findings reveal that complement anaphylatoxin signaling is not only a powerful effector of inflammation, but also an alarm system linked to the body’s internal clock, raising new considerations for time-based complement therapeutic strategies.

COMPLEMENT THERAPEUTICS

The complement system has been a rich source of highly promising therapeutic targets, including the inflammatory mediators C3a and C5a, also known as anaphylatoxins. These cleavage products of complement activation are not just concerned with immune cell recruitment and cytokine release but also play pivotal roles in tissue remodeling, vascular permeability, and disease pathology. Therapeutic modulation of anaphylatoxin signaling has advanced from experimental concepts to clinical application, with several agents being implemented or under investigation for a myriad of conditions (288,289,290).

Clinically approved therapies and established efficacy

Eculizumab, a mAb that inhibits C5 cleavage and thereby prevents the formation of the anaphylatoxin C5a and the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), represents the first major therapeutic breakthrough targeting the complement cascade. Initially developed for PNH, eculizumab has demonstrated long-term efficacy in the attenuation of hemolysis, transfusion dependence, and thromboembolic risk (291,292). It has also been beneficial in aHUS, in which renal recovery is enhanced by its early administration (293). Furthermore, long-term safety data from global pharmacovigilance registries support its tolerability across diverse patient populations (79).

Complement inhibition has also become a growing force in acute inflammatory syndromes. Anti-C5a Ab vilobelimab was granted emergency Food and Drug Administration approval for use in hospitalized COVID-19 patients based on results from phase 3 trials of reduced mortality in ventilated, critically ill patients (294,295). Meanwhile, the oral C5aR1 antagonist avacopan has been shown to be effective in anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis, allowing for steroid-sparing therapies and sustained long-term disease control (296). These results highlight the therapeutic potential for selective inhibition of anaphylatoxin activity.

THERAPEUTIC APPLICATIONS ACROSS DISEASE DOMAINS

Hematologic and renal disorders

Dysregulation of the complement pathway underlies numerous hematologic and renal disorders. Both clinical observations and mechanistic studies in PNH and aHUS support a central role for C5a-mediated endothelium and coagulation pathway activation (268,274). Complement inhibition was likewise promising in IgA nephropathy and kidney transplant recipients at risk for recurrence of aHUS, with prophylactic eculizumab reducing disease recurrence and preserving renal function (297,298). Mechanistically, C5a also facilitates hematopoietic stem cell exit from the marrow niche, revealing additional roles in bone marrow physiology and potential off-target effects (299).

Viral infections and acute inflammation

In sepsis and COVID-19, C5a overproduction causes neutrophil dysfunction, vascular leak, and cytokine storm. Selective inhibition of the receptor C5aR1 or C5a can be employed to counteract immune paralysis and improve survival, as seen in the PANAMO trial and other models (300). This supports the use of anaphylatoxin blockade in acute respiratory distress syndromes and hyperinflammatory viral syndromes.

Neuroinflammatory and degenerative diseases

Anaphylatoxins also participate in the pathophysiology of neurodegenerative diseases. In AD models, C5a blockade reduces neuroinflammation and improves cognition (301). Broader reviews affirm the therapeutic potential of complement inhibitors in CNS diseases such as MS and stroke, where complement-mediated inflammation may accelerate neurodegeneration (302).

Diabetic and metabolic kidney disease

Complement activation is increasingly recognized in DKD, with C3aR and C5aR1 upregulated in glomerular and tubulointerstitial compartments. Inhibition of C3aR prevents podocyte injury in experimental models (133), while dual receptor blockade reduces inflammatory signaling and fibrosis (303). This supports therapeutic targeting of anaphylatoxins as a disease-modifying therapy in DKD.

Cancer immunomodulation and therapeutic resistance

C5a and C3a advance tumor growth by recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, tumor angiogenesis, and resistance to therapy. In NSCLC, radiotherapy efficacy is enhanced by C5a blockade to improve immune cell infiltration (304). In pancreatic cancer, C3a or C5a blockade enhances chemotherapy and stimulates NK cell activation (305). Likewise, dual C3aR/C5aR blockade suppresses HCC development through inhibition of EMT and tumor growth (245). These results position complement anaphylatoxins as central regulators of the tumor microenvironment and resistance to treatment.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND EMERGING STRATEGIES

Beyond mAbs, next-generation complement-targeted therapeutics are rapidly emerging, including siRNAs, gene editing technologies, fusion proteins, bispecific Abs, nanobodies, complement inhibitor-loaded nanoparticles, aptamers, proteolysis-targeting chimeras, and engineered cell therapies. Structural insight into C3aR and C5aR1 signaling has revealed distinct downstream pathways amenable to biased antagonism or selective modulation (306). Therapeutic vaccines against C5a (e.g., in Alzheimer’s) and small peptide inhibitors of upstream C3 activation (e.g., for PNH) present alternative options to current Ab therapy. Looking ahead, further research should explore the precision targeting of receptor subtypes such as C5aR1 and C5aR2 to optimize therapeutic efficacy while preserving immune function.

CONCLUSIONS

The complement anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a, long regarded as simple inflammatory byproducts, have emerged as critical and context-dependent regulators of immunity, tissue homeostasis, and disease. Their signaling through C3aR, C5aR1, and C5aR2 orchestrates a wide variety of biological responses, from defense against microbes to pathological inflammation, emphasizing the dual nature of their function. Across numerous systems, these small peptides operate at the intersection of innate and adaptive immunity, regulating leukocyte recruitment, cytokine production, barrier function, and even cellular metabolism.

This review has outlined how C3a and C5a signaling exhibits pleiotropic and often paradoxical effects based on local tissue context, disease phase, and the balance of receptor engagement. In infection, these anaphylatoxins are critical for pathogen clearance but are also capable of driving immunopathology when overactivated. In autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases, they are not only implicated in acute inflammatory cascades but also in facilitating long-term immune dysfunction and tissue remodeling. Their role in cancer has revealed a pro-tumorigenic dimension, promoting immune evasion and stromal crosstalk. In neurodegenerative and metabolic diseases, they modulate glial activity, synaptic plasticity, and metabolic homeostasis—reinforcing their reach beyond classical immunology.

Therapeutic targeting of the complement anaphylatoxins is no longer speculative. With the approval of complement inhibitors for diseases such as aHUS and PNH, and with the increasing interest in C5aR1 targeting in sepsis, COVID-19, and autoimmune disease, the landscape is poised for broader clinical translation. The task, however, remains to divorce protective from pathogenic signaling and to implement strategies that modulate complement with precision as opposed to brute force.

Looking ahead, additional investigation into receptor-specific effects, circadian influences, and intracellular signaling mechanisms—the so-called complosome—will be critical. Furthermore, approaches that integrate spatial and temporal complement activation dynamics will help elucidate when and how these pathways tip the scales between health and disease as we march toward the era of precision medicine. In sum, the story of complement anaphylatoxins is one of complexity and contradiction. As both sentinels and saboteurs, C3a and C5a occupy a unique position in human biology, capable of both defending the host and contributing to its demise. Understanding and harnessing this duality may unravel new therapeutic frontiers in infectious, inflammatory, metabolic, neurodegenerative, and oncologic diseases.
